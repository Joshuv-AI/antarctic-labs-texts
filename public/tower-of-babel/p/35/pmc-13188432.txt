A comprehensive analysis of the use of nucleoside analogues in RNA therapeutics
Authors: Alisa A Kukushkina-Zvezdova, Anna A Zherebtsova, Darya V Telegina, Ivan A Skvortsov, Roman A Ivanov, Vasiliy V Reshetnikov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundmRNA therapeutics represent a promising frontier in the development of vaccines, anti-cancer treatments, and gene replacement therapies. The mRNA platform facilitates the rapid design of effective and safe candidates. However, native mRNA molecules are inherently characterized by limited stability and high reactogenicity, which may compromise their in vivo efficacy. The incorporation of modified nucleoside analogues can address these challenges by extending the mRNA half-life and subsequently enhancing protein expression.Main bodyDespite the discovery and widespread adoption of pseudouridine (Ψ) and N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ) in mRNA therapeutics, these modifications have not fully addressed all inherent limitations of mRNA-based platforms. Consequently, identifying novel nucleoside analogues, their combinations, and alternative therapeutic strategies remains a critical area of research. This review provides a comprehensive overview of how commonly used nucleoside analogues influence mRNA stability, translation, and immunogenicity, while also discussing their natural distribution patterns in native mRNA. Furthermore, we examine experimental in vitro and in vivo data regarding the application of non-canonical nucleosides in cell and animal models and evaluate the future prospects of modified mRNA-based therapies.ConclusionIn conclusion, this review provides an extensive analysis of the properties of modified nucleosides employed in mRNA formulations and characterizes their impact on the overall efficacy of mRNA-based therapeutics in detail.Keywords: mRNA therapeutics, Nucleoside analogues, mRNA stability, mRNA reactogenicity

Introduction

Therapeutics based on in vitro transcribed (IVT) mRNA demonstrated significant efficacy and safety during the COVID-19 pandemic. Currently, dozens of mRNA-based vaccines, anti-cancer agents, and gene therapies are undergoing clinical trials [1–3]. Along with the four conventional nucleosides, modified analogues are frequently employed for IVT mRNA synthesis. A major breakthrough occurred in 2005, when the incorporation of pseudouridine (Ψ) was found to significantly mitigate reactogenicity and enhance the efficacy of mRNA-based platforms [4].

To date, over 170 natural modifications have been identified in non-coding RNAs, including ribosomal, transfer, and small nuclear RNAs [5–7]. Furthermore, nucleoside modifications — whether enzymatically incorporated or resulting from non-enzymatic damage, such as alkylation or oxidation — can occur in protein-coding RNA, although their prevalence in mRNA is substantially lower [8–10]. More than 20 distinct modified nucleosides have been identified in natural mRNA (Fig. 1), thirteen of which are generated enzymatically (e.g., N6-methyladenosine (m6A), Ψ, dihydrouridine, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), N1-methyladenosine (m1A), N7-methylguanosine (m7G), 2’O-methyl modifications (2’O-Me/Nm: Cm, Am, Gm, Um), 5-methylcytidine (m5C), 5-hydroxymethylcytidine (hm5C), and inosine (I)). These modifications constitute the epitranscriptomic landscape across all cellular RNA types [5, 11–14]. Among these, m6A, I, and Ψ are the most prevalent, occurring at frequencies 10–100 times higher than other modifications [6]. In contrast, seven other mRNA modifications, such as O6-methylguanosine, O4-methyluridine, m1A, N3-methyladenosine, N3-methylcytidine, N1-methylguanosine, m7G, 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanosine, and 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroadenosine, result from oxidation, alkylation, or UV radiation and are less frequent [15]. Damage most commonly affects the N- or O-groups within the purine or pyrimidine rings. Although the first mRNA modifications were discovered over 50 years ago [16], their systematic investigation began only recently, which explains why the functional roles of many modifications remain partially elucidated. Nevertheless, extensive evidence indicates that modified nucleosides critically influence organismal development, RNA structure, stability, splicing, and translation [17–23]. Aberrant modification patterns, often resulting from enzymatic dysfunction, are linked to neurological pathologies, oncogenesis, and metabolic disorders [24–26].

In addition to endogenous modifications, synthetic analogues such as m1Ψ, 2-pyridone, and zebularine are widely used in therapeutic mRNA. These non-natural analogues stabilize mRNA and suppress innate immune activation, thereby enhancing therapeutic potency [27–29]. Additionally, halogenated pyrimidine nucleosides have recently been shown to increase target protein expression by 5–10 fold [30], while phosphorothioate modifications upstream of the start codon may facilitate ribosomal complex assembly [31].

In this review, we provide a comprehensive analysis of eight modified nucleoside analogues with high therapeutic potential: m6A, m5C, hm5C, ac4C, Ψ, m1Ψ, 5-methoxyuridine (5moU), and Nm. We evaluate their impact on the translation, stability, secondary structure, and reactogenicity of in vitro transcribed mRNA.

The influence of modified nucleoside analogues on mRNA stability

The efficient translation of a target protein depends significantly on the half-life of the mRNA transcript. mRNA modifications can modulate transcript stability through several mechanisms, including altering the RNA secondary structure, recruiting specific RNA-binding proteins, and — in the case of 2’O-Me modifications — shielding the mRNA from nucleophilic attack on the 2’OH group. Moreover, the incorporation of modified nucleoside analogues during IVT reduces the formation of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) by-products, which may alleviate the reactogenicity of mRNA-based therapeutics.

The dual role of m6A: context-specific stability and the impact of reader proteins

m6A is the most prevalent non-canonical mRNA modification identified across prokaryotes, eukaryotes, and viral RNAs [32–35]. Although discovered over 50 years ago, intensive research into m6A was catalyzed by the identification of m6A-specific demethylases (e.g., FTO and ALKBH5), which established mRNA modifications as reversible and regulated epitranscriptomic marks [16, 36–41]. In natural mRNA, m6A accounts for 0.1–0.5% of all adenosines, typically averaging 3–5 modifications per transcript.

Currently, the influence of m6A on mRNA stability is more extensively characterized than that of other nucleoside analogues. Traditionally, the half-life of m6A-methylated transcripts has been considered reduced relative to non-methylated RNA. This destabilization is primarily mediated by the YTHDF2 reader protein, which translocates modified mRNA to P-bodies or stress granules [42] and recruits the CNOT/CCR4 complex for deadenylation and subsequent degradation [43, 44].

However, recent evidence highlights a more nuanced «dual role» for this modification. For instance, in HEK293T cells under hypoxia, elevated m6A levels have been shown to enhance mRNA stability and sustain translational efficiency [45]. Furthermore, the depletion of m6A in ASB2 and RARA transcripts reportedly leads to a decrease in their half-life [46]. This functional paradox underscores that m6A-mediated regulation is a context-specific process, fundamentally shaped by the interplay between competing reader proteins. These conflicting observations may stem from differences in experimental settings - such as comparing steady-state conditions with cellular hypoxia - as well as variations between cell lines as well as differences among cell lines that may possess distinct sets and expression profiles of RNA-binding proteins. Under hypoxic stress, the re-localization of m6A sites can fundamentally alter the recruitment of YTH-family proteins or facilitate the binding of alternative RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) that promote transcript longevity, and these proteins’ expression can be activated only under certain stressful conditions, in this case, hypoxia. Notably, the stabilization of ASB2 and RARA transcripts appears to be an exception to the broader trend of m6A-mediated decay; these specific mRNAs remain largely unaffected by YTHDF1/2 binding, suggesting the existence of yet-unidentified stabilizing RBPs, and the potential impact of the mRNA’s secondary structure must also be taken into account, which may disrupt mRNA recognition by destabilizing proteins. The discovery and structural characterization of such novel readers will be crucial for a comprehensive understanding of how m6A dictates the mRNA life cycle.

Indeed, several stabilizing factors have already been identified. Specifically, members of the IGF2BP family (IGF2BP1, 2, and 3) have been shown to enhance the stability of m6A-containing transcripts [47]. This stabilization is achieved through the recruitment of effectors, such as HuR, which shield the bound mRNA from nucleolytic degradation. Although both YTHDF2 and IGF2BPs recognize the same consensus motif (RRACH, where R = A/G, H = A/C/U), their distinct expression profiles and cellular localizations imply that the net effect of m6A on transcript turnover is a product of the cellular environment and the relative abundance of specific «readers» and «erasers». Beyond IGF2BPs and HuR, other proteins including YTHDC2, G3BP1, and PRRC2A have also been implicated in the stabilization of m6A-modified mRNAs [48–52].

This regulatory paradigm extends to plant biology, where the interaction between m6A and the ECT2 protein (recognizing the URUAW motif) is essential for the stability of transcripts governing trichome morphogenesis in Arabidopsis [53, 54]. Conversely, the demethylase ALKBH10B acts as a negative regulator of stability by removing marks from key developmental transcripts, thereby modulating their half-life. This effect is presumably attributed to the removal of m6A sites that would otherwise recruit destabilizing reader proteins [55]. Such a distinction further underscores the critical importance of the interplay between modified mRNA and its binding partners: the stability of a transcript is primarily dictated by the specific regulatory proteins recruited, which can either extend the molecular lifespan or trigger premature degradation.

Beyond protein-mediated regulation, m6A directly influences mRNA stability by altering its secondary structure. The addition of a methyl group to the N6 position of adenosine introduces stereochemical hindrance, which destabilizes the A = U base pair and leads to the localized melting of RNA duplexes [56, 57]. Such «m6A-switches» can increase the accessibility of the RNA backbone to both protective proteins and degrading enzymes, adding a biophysical layer to the epitranscriptomic control of mRNA stability.

Stability control by modified cytidines: m5C, hm5C, and ac4C

Unlike the inherently reversible m6A modification, modified cytidines (m5C, hm5C, and ac4C) function as critical determinants of structural integrity and immune evasion. While m5C and ac4C primarily enhance the thermodynamic stability of the RNA duplex and promote the assembly of protective protein scaffolds, their oxidative derivative, hm5C, introduces an additional layer of regulatory complexity. Although hm5C contributes to structural rigidity in vitro, its in vivo role is characterized by a more nuanced impact on transcript turnover, often serving as a metabolic intermediate that can influence cellular decay pathways. Consequently, the strategic incorporation and precise balancing of these modified cytidines serve as a sophisticated approach to circumvent nucleolytic degradation and maximize the temporal window of therapeutic expression.

The structural and regulatory roles of m5C

As another prevalent mRNA modification, 5-methylcytidine (m5C) significantly influences transcript stability. In tRNA and rRNA, m5C modulates aminoacylation, structural integrity, and codon recognition [58–61]. This modification has been identified across diverse taxa, including viruses, prokaryotes, archaea, and eukaryotes [37, 62], as well as in various non-coding RNA species [63]. Beyond its role in mRNA stability, m5C regulates splicing, nuclear export, DNA damage repair, and critical cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, stem cell development, and differentiation [17, 64–68]. Although m5C was discovered in mRNA concurrently with m6A, high-resolution studies employing bisulfite sequencing to identify specific m5C sites have only recently emerged [63, 69]. Early estimates of high m5C content in mRNA were subsequently found to be overstated due to contamination with tRNA and rRNA [70].

In contrast to m6A, m5C is generally considered a stabilizing mark [71]. However, its impact on mRNA stability remains less characterized due to its lower abundance; m5C frequency is 3–10 times lower than that of m6A, accounting for 0.03–0.1% of all cytosines [72]. The formation of m5C is primarily mediated by the methyltransferase NSUN2, which catalyzes methylation at the C5 position [73]. This modification is recognized by specific reader proteins, notably ALYREF and YBX1 [17, 64]. YBX1 binds to m5C-modified RNA through π-π interactions between the target RNA and two tryptophan residues (Trp45 and Trp65) within its cold-shock domain [17]. Furthermore, YBX1 recruits stabilizing factors such as PABPC1, which binds the poly(A) tail, or HuR, thereby enhancing the longevity of the m5C-modified transcript [17, 74].

Beyond protein recruitment, m5C provides direct biophysical stabilization by enhancing nucleobase stacking and increasing the thermostability of hydrogen bonding with guanine [75, 76], These effects facilitate rRNA folding and positively regulate ribosomal function [77]. Large-scale analysis of approximately 12,000 mRNAs in Arabidopsis has confirmed that m5C-modified transcripts exhibit greater stability than their non-methylated counterparts, a finding consistent with other recent studies [78–80].

hm5C: impact on RNA structure and metabolic stability

5-hydroxymethylcytidine (hm5C) is a modification present in both DNA and RNA, formed via m5C oxidation by TET (Ten-Eleven Translocation) proteins. Unlike the directly reversible m6A, the m5C mark undergoes a distinct metabolic trajectory, being sequentially converted into hm5C, 5-formylcytidine (f5C), and 5-carboxylcytidine (ca5C) [81].

Evidence regarding the impact of hm5C on RNA stability remains sparse; however, recent studies suggest that TET-mediated hm5C incorporation reduces mRNA stability [82]. Additionally, hm5C may indirectly affect transcript longevity by modulating splicing efficiency, as certain spliced isoforms exhibit shorter half-lives [82, 83]. Furthermore, the TET2–Upf1 complex has been shown to negatively regulate mRNA stability [84].

Biophysical evaluations of hm5C suggest that the incorporation of this single modification generally results in thermal stabilization and lower melting temperatures compared to unmodified oligonucleotides. This effect may be attributed to spatial repulsion within specific motifs, such as the sarcin/ricin loop, highlighting the substantial influence of sequence context in both RNA and DNA [85, 86]. X-ray crystallography data indicate that the 5-hydroxymethyl group does not significantly alter the global RNA structure, although it increases the N4–O6 hydrogen bond length (3.4 Å vs. 2.95 Å) in RNA, an effect not observed in DNA. This disparity may also be sequence-dependent [85, 87]. However, these in vitro findings cannot be directly extrapolated to in vivo systems due to confounding factors such as RNA length, the absence of regulatory protein interactions, and differences in experimental media composition.

ac4C: N4-acetylation as a potent stabilizer

This nucleoside analogue is the only known natural modification formed by the addition of an acetyl group at the N4 position of cytosine. While prevalent in non-coding RNA, ac4C has recently been identified in the mRNA of yeast, humans, hyperthermophilic archaea, and viruses [88–90], accounting for approximately 0.11% of all cytidines [85]. The NAT10 enzyme is responsible for ac4C incorporation in both yeast and humans. NAT10-mediated acetylation typically promotes mRNA stabilization; conversely, NAT10 inhibition decreases ac4C levels, thereby reducing mRNA stability and translational efficiency [88, 89, 91, 92]. Consequently, ac4C-enriched mRNAs generally exhibit extended half-lives compared to their unmodified counterparts [88, 93, 94].

It has been proposed that ac4C enhances translational efficiency by stabilizing the mRNA secondary structure [88]. However, interpreting these data is complicated by the fact that NAT10 also catalyzes ac4C incorporation into tRNA and rRNA [95]. The stabilizing effect of ac4C may stem from its ability to strengthen base pairing with guanosine in double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) structures [96], as the hydrogen becomes more accessible for hydrogen bond formation [97, 98]. In plants, even a partial reduction in ac4C content in specific target transcripts significantly accelerates their degradation, further supporting its role in maintaining mRNA stability. Nevertheless, some studies have reported instances where reduced ac4C levels were associated with increased transcript half-life, while other transcripts in the same experiment lost stability [99]. The molecular mechanisms underlying these divergent effects likely involve specific reader proteins that recognize ac4C and context-dependently modulate the mRNA life cycle. The observation that only a portion of the transcripts exhibited this contradictory effect implies the presence of a shared characteristic; for instance, this modification may alter the secondary structure, enhancing mRNA accessibility to nucleases, or the mRNA may possess sequences that facilitate premature degradation, such as AU-rich elements.

Uridine derivatives: the gold standard for therapeutic mRNA stability

The strategic replacement of uridine with its modified analogues represents a definitive milestone in RNA therapeutics, pivotal to the clinical success of the first approved mRNA vaccines, Comirnaty (Pfizer/BioNTech) and Spikevax (Moderna). Unmodified exogenous mRNA is readily detected by innate cellular sensors, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), triggering inflammatory responses that prematurely curtail translation and compromise transcript stability. The incorporation of uridine derivatives effectively masks the mRNA from these immune-sensing pathways while significantly enhancing its structural integrity and functional longevity.

Ψ: prevalence and structural impact

Pseudouridine is among the most abundant RNA modifications, identified in rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, and mRNA [6, 100, 101], with a prevalence in mRNA comparable to that of m6A (0.2–0.6%) [20, 102]. The formation of Ψ is an irreversible process catalyzed by pseudouridine synthases (PUS) via two distinct pathways: a protein-only mechanism mediated by PUS enzymes and an RNA-dependent mechanism involving box H/ACA ncRNAs and the Cbf5/dyskerin complex [103].

The physicochemical properties of Ψ differ from those of uridine due to a more rigid phosphodiester backbone and an additional hydrogen bond donor (N1-H), which enhances the stability of Ψ–A base pairing [104]. For instance, Ψ stabilizes tRNA tertiary structure, particularly under thermal stress. The conversion of uridine to Ψ frequently occurs at sites critical for intermolecular RNA–RNA and RNA–protein interactions, such as the peptidyl transferase center and spliceosomal complexes [101, 105, 106].

However, the impact of Ψ on mRNA stability remains a subject of ongoing debate. In yeast, PUS7p deficiency has been shown to reduce the stability of Ψ-containing transcripts during heat shock [100]. Conversely, in Toxoplasma gondii, PUS1 dysfunction reportedly increases mRNA half-life, suggesting a destabilizing effect [107]. These variations likely stem from differences in the organisms studied, specific mRNA species, or experimental conditions. Notably, the destabilization observed in T. gondii was relatively marginal. In mammalian cells, in vitro synthesized Ψ-containing mRNA exhibits enhanced stability compared to unmodified counterparts [108].

Biophysically, Ψ acts as a «universal base» capable of stabilizing RNA duplexes by forming Ψ–A, Ψ–G, Ψ–U, and Ψ–C pairs more effectively than uridine [109]. Thermodynamic studies indicate that these stabilizing effects are more pronounced within the context of complex RNA structures, suggesting that enhanced stability arises from mechanisms beyond simple canonical hydrogen bonding, such as improved base stacking [109, 110].

m1Ψ: enhanced stacking and therapeutic efficacy

Thermal denaturation studies confirm that m1Ψ-containing duplexes exhibit superior stability compared to those containing Ψ [110]. Unlike Ψ, m1Ψ lacks the additional N1 hydrogen donor, which renders it a less «universal» base. Nevertheless, it exerts a more potent stabilizing effect on double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) through robust base-stacking and enhanced base-pairing interactions. Specifically, m1Ψ forms more stable pairs with adenine regardless of the sequence context and improves interactions with G, U, and C compared to both U and Ψ [111].

While N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ) is present in archaeal and eukaryotic tRNA and rRNA, it has not been identified in native mammalian mRNA [112, 113]. However, its incorporation during IVT does not suppress protein synthesis, facilitating its widespread use in SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccines [28]. In cellular environments, m1Ψ can result from Ψ methylation at the N1 position, catalyzed by the EMG1 protein [114].

Nevertheless, it exerts a more potent stabilizing effect on double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) through robust base-stacking and enhanced base-pairing interactions. Specifically, m1Ψ forms more stable pairs with adenine regardless of the sequence context and improves interactions with G, U, and C compared to both U and Ψ [115].

Increased structural stability generally extends mRNA half-life, and stabilizing ORFs can significantly enhance protein expression [111, 116]. These findings elucidate the observed positive effects of m1Ψ on mRNA longevity and translation [117]. Ribosomal loading negatively correlates with predicted mean free energy. This trend is more pronounced for m1Ψ than for uridine. This aligns with evidence that highly structured 5’UTRs can suppress translation, whereas the presence of m1Ψ within the ORF increases transcript lifespan [111, 118]. Cell culture experiments demonstrate that mRNA containing m1Ψ — either alone or in combination with m5C — is more stable than transcripts containing Ψ or the m5C + Ψ combination [119]. Consequently, m1Ψ is firmly associated with superior stability achieved through enhanced mRNA structuring. This attribute, combined with high translational efficiency, has established m1Ψ as the preferred analogue for clinical mRNA platforms.

5moU: an emerging alternative

5-methoxyuridine is a modification identified in bacterial tRNAs (e.g., Escherichia coli and other Gram-positive bacteria) [120]. The 5-O-CH3 group can form a single hydrogen bond with the adjacent 5’-phosphate group, thereby stabilizing the local ribose-phosphate backbone conformation and potentially facilitating base-pair recognition during protein synthesis [121]. Although 5moU is unlikely to significantly alter the overall thermodynamic stability of U-pairing, studies utilizing eGFP mRNA have demonstrated that its contribution to mRNA stability exceeds even that of m1Ψ and Ψ [122]. This suggests that 5moU may provide a superior architectural toolkit for designing next-generation, high-persistence therapeutic RNA platforms.

2’-O-methylation (Nm): a shield for the RNA backbone

Nucleosides featuring a methylated ribose at the 2’ position (Nm) were discovered over 50 years ago, yet their diverse regulatory roles remain an active area of investigation. This modification is highly conserved, occurring in the tRNA and rRNA of bacteria and archaea, as well as in both coding and non-coding RNAs of eukaryotes [123–129], and it is added to nucleotides by either methyltransferases or fibrillarin using snRNAs [130, 131]. In higher eukaryotes, Nm is a hallmark of the mRNA 5’-cap structure; the first transcribed nucleotide typically undergoes ribose methylation to form a Cap 1 structure, while the second nucleotide may be similarly modified to form Cap 2. These cap modifications are essential for ensuring mRNA stability and protecting the transcript from premature degradation [132].

Unlike m6A, m5C, and other base analogues, no specific reader proteins have been identified for Nm to date. In human mRNA, Nm occurs across all four standard nucleosides (Am, Gm, Um, and Cm), as well as in the modified m6Am [133]. Quantitatively, Um is the most prevalent (0.15%), while Am is found less frequently (0.012%) [127, 134]. From a biophysical perspective, Nm dramatically enhances RNA stability by rendering the phosphodiester backbone less susceptible to alkaline hydrolysis, enzymatic cleavage, and nucleolytic attack [135–137].

Since methylation occurs on the ribose moiety, it does not directly interfere with base pairing; however, it significantly modulates RNA dynamics, thereby influencing both stability and translation efficiency [138]. Specifically, 2’-O-methylation stabilizes helical regions [139, 140] and shifts the sugar pucker equilibrium toward the С3’-endo conformation, which increases the overall rigidity of RNA duplexes [141, 142]. Furthermore, Nm at the 3’ terminus provides a critical defense for microRNAs, piRNAs, and siRNAs by blocking 3’–5’ exonuclease activity [143, 144]. While internal Nm modifications have been associated with the enhanced stability of specific transcripts, such as Pxdn mRNA, the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated [128]. Interestingly, the density of 2’-O-ribose methylation in bacterial and archaeal rRNA increases with rising environmental temperatures, underscoring its fundamental role in stabilizing RNA under extreme thermal conditions [60, 145–147].

The strategic combination of these modifications allows for the precise engineering of mRNA transcripts with optimized stability, reduced immunogenicity, and sustained translational activity. Understanding the synergy between these biophysical and protein-mediated mechanisms is crucial for the development of the next generation of high-persistence RNA therapeutics and vaccines.

Spatial distribution and the impact of modified nucleoside analogues on mRNA translation

While the previous sections focused on the structural and metabolic stability of modified mRNA, their functional impact extends to the regulation of protein synthesis. The distribution of modified nucleosides in natural mRNA is non-random and follows specific spatial and contextual dependencies. Consequently, each modified analogue exhibits a unique distribution pattern along the mRNA transcript [148]. The presence of these analogues significantly influences not only the stability of the nucleic acid molecule but also its translation through several key mechanisms: altering the mRNA secondary structure, recruiting proteins that modulate translational efficiency, and affecting the frequency of decoding errors.

This section provides a concise overview of how the incorporation of non-canonical nucleosides into mRNA molecules affects the translation efficiency of encoded proteins. m6A

In mammalian mRNA, m6A exhibits a highly conserved distribution pattern, typically enriched within the final exon of the coding sequence (CDS) and the proximal region of the 3’UTR [149]. Comprehensive mapping in murine cells [41], revealed that the majority of m6A sites (50.9%) reside within the CDS, while 41.9% are located in UTRs. Although only 2% were identified in introns, this value is likely an underestimation due to the lack of pre-mRNA enrichment in standard sequencing libraries. Within the UTRs, a striking 93% of m6A modifications are concentrated in the 3’UTR, particularly near the stop codon, with density declining towards the distal 3’ end. Conversely, m6A density remains low in the 5’UTR and the initial portion of the CDS, with a steady increase toward the terminal region. These significant differences may arise from the presence of regions within 3’UTRs that exhibit reduced structural rigidity relative to 5’UTRs, where crucial elements for ribosome binding and translation initiation are situated, thereby rendering 3’UTRs more susceptible to the activity of methyltransferases that facilitate the formation of m6A. Furthermore, sequences enriched with motifs favorable for methylation are more prevalent in the 3’ UTR. Moreover, 5’UTRs exhibit greater evolutionary conservation, as minor alterations can profoundly influence translation initiation, whereas 3’UTRs are marked by considerable variability, particularly in the accumulation of modifications.

Other studies [40, 150] have highlighted additional nuances, such as m6A enrichment at the beginning of the 5’UTR, often associated with the presence of m6Am at the first transcribed position near the cap. In HeLa cells, the distribution follows a characteristic trend: a gradual increase in the 5’UTR, a plateau across the CDS, and a peak immediately preceding the stop codon [150]. Furthermore, m6A residues can form clusters, as demonstrated in mature bovine prolactin mRNA, where a 108-nucleotide region in the 3’UTR contains multiple methylated sites [151, 152]. In HEK293T cells and mammalian brain tissues, approximately 65% of m6A sites are localized in the CDS, with prominent enrichment at the stop codon [95]. Notably, the topographical peaks of m6A and m6Am do not correlate, suggesting distinct tissue-specific regulatory roles [153]. Additionally, 77%–80% of m6A-containing transcripts possess at least one miRNA binding site, with miRNA targets often clustering around m6A peaks. The GGACH consensus motif frequently exhibits reverse complementarity to the seed regions of various miRNAs, indicating a functional interplay between epitranscriptomic marks and RNA interference [153].

Modulation of translation initiation and elongation

m6A-binding proteins significantly enhance translational efficiency and can facilitate cap-independent initiation [154–157]. A single m6A residue within the 5’UTR is sufficient to induce direct binding to eIF3, thereby recruiting the 43 S pre-initiation complex in the absence of the canonical cap-binding factor eIF4E [158].

However, the impact of m6A on translation elongation is more complex and appears to be position-dependent. In vitro experiments demonstrate that m6A within a codon can impede elongation, with the most pronounced reduction in peptide formation occurring when the modification is at the first position (yielding only 25% of the rate seen in unmodified mRNA) [22, 159]. Modifications at the third (wobble) position exert a minimal effect (up to 80% efficiency), likely due to the inherent flexibility of base pairing in this position. The sequence context further modulates this hindrance; for instance, replacing adjacent A-U pairs with G-C pairs in glutamine (CAG) or proline (CCA) codons can alleviate the slowing effect of m6A [159].

This inhibitory effect on elongation has been observed in both bacterial and eukaryotic systems, though it is most pronounced in E. coli cell-free systems, where m6A destabilizes the A-U base pairs in the decoding center and disrupts GTP hydrolysis by EF-Tu [159, 160]. While global m6A content exceeding 5% significantly suppresses overall translation [108], the modification does not compromise translational fidelity or lead to amino acid misincorporation [22, 134].

Context-specific enhancements

Interestingly, m6A can also serve as a translational enhancer depending on its location. In Zea mays (corn), m6A near the start codon positively influences translation, likely by destabilizing and unwinding inhibitory secondary structures [161]. Similarly, in Triticum aestivum (wheat), while methylation in the CDS and stop codons generally suppresses translation, m6A within the 5’UTR and proximal to the start codon enhances efficiency by facilitating ribosome passage through otherwise highly structured regions [162].

m5C

The reported distribution of m5C sites across mRNA regions varies significantly between studies, largely due to the inherent limitations of different detection technologies. While some analyses of murine and human cell lines suggest that m5C is more prevalent in UTRs than CDS [163], a growing body of evidence indicates a predominant enrichment within the CDS — specifically immediately downstream of the start codon — with the lowest density observed in the 5’UTR [64, 74, 164–167]. These divergent findings likely stem from technical artifacts; for instance, bisulfite conversion is prone to false positives, whereas nanopore sequencing remains susceptible to stochastic errors [78, 168]. Despite these challenges, a consensus pattern has emerged where m5C distribution closely correlates with the frequency of NSUN methyltransferase motifs [169, 170]. Furthermore, m5C sites are categorized into Type I (adjacent to downstream G-rich triplets, typically found in 5’ stem-loops) and Type II (adjacent to UCCA motifs, located within loop structures), which are modified by NSUN2 and NSUN6, respectively [169–171]. Ultimately, the m5C landscape is highly dynamic, shaped by species-specific factors, tissue type, developmental stage, and cellular environment [64, 165, 171–173].

Mechanisms of translational regulation

The influence of m5C on protein expression remains a subject of considerable debate, with reported effects ranging from robust enhancement to significant inhibition [163, 174, 175]. In bacterial cell-free systems, the incorporation of m5C into reporter mRNA resulted in a ~ 40% reduction in peptide yield. Notably, m5C also compromised translational fidelity, increasing amino acid misincorporation rates by 50- to 500-fold (e.g., the Cm5CC codon erroneously directing the substitution of proline with leucine or isoleucine) [22]. It should be noted that the data regarding translation errors were derived from a prokaryotic system, in which the elongation mechanisms differ from those in eukaryotic systems.

The functional outcome of m5C appears to be critically dependent on its position within the transcript. In some contexts, CDS-localized m5C negatively regulates translation, while modifications in the 5’ or 3’UTRs exert negligible effects [163]. Conversely, other reports suggest that while m5C in the CDS and 5’UTR is inhibitory, its presence in the 3’UTR can positively modulate translation [176]. Specific examples further highlight this site-specificity: NSUN2-mediated methylation of p27 mRNA at cytidine 64 in the 5’UTR suppresses translation [174], whereas methylation of IL17A (CDS), CDK1 (3’UTR), and SHC (5’UTR) promotes protein upregulation [175, 177, 178].

Synergistic effects and reader-mediated control

The impact of m5C on translation may also be modulated by its interplay with other epitranscriptomic marks. For instance, the co-occurrence of m5C and m6A in the 3’UTR has been shown to synergistically enhance the expression of p21 during oxidative stress-induced senescence [178], as well as upregulate Sox9 [179].

As a primary m5C «writer», NSUN2 plays a central role in orchestrating eukaryotic translation rates [175, 180]. In native transcripts, m5C content often inversely correlates with ribosome occupancy, suggesting a repressive function. Moreover, the enrichment of m5C at the first and second positions of specific codons encoding five different amino acids points toward a highly specialized regulatory mechanism mediated by reader proteins [176].

hm5C

The mapping of hm5C in Drosophila cells by Delatte et al. [181] revealed a distinct distribution pattern, with approximately 48% of sites localized in the CDS, 19% in the 5’UTR, 16% in the 3’UTR, and 17% in introns. A hallmark of these hm5C peaks is the enrichment of a UC-rich motif containing UCCUC repeats, present in 64% of identified sites. Notably, the spatial landscapes of m5C and hm5C do not overlap, suggesting that these two modifications serve divergent biological functions [181].

In mammalian systems, hm5C levels exhibit marked spatial heterogeneity, as demonstrated in the mouse brain [182]: High hm5C concentrations were identified in the brainstem, hippocampus, and cerebellum, while the hypothalamus and amygdala showed moderate levels, and the cerebral cortex displayed the lowest density. Furthermore, hm5C abundance appears to be clinically relevant; for instance, a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease showed significantly reduced hm5C levels in the hippocampus, substantia nigra, and striatum [182] However, a significant caveat to these findings is that the analysis was performed on the total RNA pool rather than the purified mRNA fraction, which precludes definitive conclusions regarding the specific role of hm5C within the messenger RNA landscape.

The influence of hm5C on the translation of native and in vitro synthesized mRNA is still not very well understood. The only currently available data is obtained from full-transcriptome mapping of hm5C in D. melanogaster, which revealed over 3000 putative hm5C sites in mRNA, with this modification predominantly present in actively translated transcripts. This suggests that hm5C may promote mRNA translation [181].

ac4C

It has been shown in mice that the characteristic distribution of the ac4C modification is enrichment in the CDS segments, predominantly at the beginning [183]. In rats, the modification is most frequently identified in the CDS and 3’UTR, with a distinct peak in the vicinity of the stop codon. Notably, in models of cancer-induced bone pain, a significant shift toward exonic enrichment has been observed, indicating that the ac4C landscape is highly dynamic and responsive to physiological stress [184]. In another study on rats, ac4C distribution was uniformly high throughout the entire length of the CDS compared to the UTRs [185]. In HeLa cells, while ac4C is uniformly distributed throughout the CDS, there is a pronounced clustering of acetylation sites near the start codon in the 5’UTR and the stop codon in the 3’UTR [186]. Such differences in the distribution of modification can be explained by a significant difference in the studied models and the associated altered expression profile of m5C writer proteins.

Since ac4C is primarily found in the coding regions, more detailed studies have been conducted on the distribution of this modification. Detailed studies in rice (Oryza sativa) have revealed that ac4C sites are non-randomly distributed among codons. Approximately 71.4% of ac4C modifications are concentrated in codons for seven specific amino acids: alanine (17.0%), proline (12.2%), threonine (11.1%), serine (10.5%), arginine (8.6%), leucine (7.1%), and glutamine (4.9%). Furthermore, ac4C is most prevalent at the third (wobble) position (42.3%) and least common at the first position (21.5%), a pattern that significantly diverges from the distribution of Nm and m5C. This specific positioning is thought to stabilize Watson–Crick base pairing and enhance interactions with cognate tRNAs, thereby optimizing translational efficiency [187].

Dual role in translational regulation: initiation and elongation

The functional impact of ac4C on translation is strictly position-dependent. Generally, the presence of ac4C within the CDS is associated with enhanced translational output. Conversely, ac4C in the 5’UTR often suppresses translation initiation by promoting the formation of inhibitory secondary structures that impede ribosome scanning. However, this inhibitory role remains a subject of debate, as some in vitro evidence suggests that ac4C does not significantly affect the assembly of the 48 S pre-initiation complex [88, 186, 188, 189].

A critical inhibitory site has been identified at the − 1 position of the Kozak sequence. Thus, ac4C disrupts a vital hydrogen bond between the ribose 2’-OH and the hypermodified t6A(N6-threonylcarbamoyladenosine) in the anticodon loop of tRNAiMet, thereby suppressing initiation. Interestingly, the regulatory effect of ac4C near the start codon depends on its strength: acetylation inhibits protein synthesis near a strong AUG but facilitates initiation near a weak start codon [186].

Synergistic and repressive dynamics

The pro-translational effect of ac4C is most pronounced when it occupies the third position of the codon, where it facilitates tRNA decoding and can increase protein synthesis by up to tenfold. While acetylation at the first and second positions also promotes translation by stabilizing Watson–Crick pairing, the overall impact on the transcript is a balance of competing forces [88]. Experiments using luciferase and GFP reporter mRNAs demonstrate this dual nature: an initial delay in translation initiation (mediated by 5’UTR acetylation) is often followed by robust activation (driven by CDS acetylation) [186]. Notably, the repressive effect of ac4C within the 5’UTR can be dominant, potentially negating the stimulatory influence of CDS acetylation and resulting in lower overall protein expression compared to unmodified transcripts. Eliminating cytidines from the 5’UTR has been shown to alleviate this early suppression, confirming that the inhibitory phase is at least partially dependent on 5’UTR-localized ac4C [186, 190].

Ψ

In yeast, mammals, T. gondii, and Arabidopsis, the topographic landscape of Ψ in mRNA is characterized by a higher occurrence within the 5’UTR and CDS, contrasted by a decreased frequency in the 3’UTR [102, 107, 191]. Notably, pseudouridylation shows a distinct positional bias: the probability of modification is significantly higher at the first position of a codon compared to the second or third positions [102, 107, 192]. In yeast and human transcripts, the most frequently modified codons are those for valine (GUA) and phenylalanine (UUU and UUC) [102, 191].

An intriguing aspect of Ψ biogenesis is its regulatory crosstalk with other epitranscriptomic marks. Specifically, TRUB1-mediated pseudouridylation has been shown to facilitate the subsequent deposition of m6A sites, whereas Ψ formation catalyzed by other writer proteins can conversely inhibit m6A incorporation [193].

Enhancement of translational output and structural basis

The pro-translational effects of Ψ were first characterized by Karikó et al., who demonstrated that Ψ-containing mRNA significantly outperforms unmodified transcripts in terms of protein expression [108, 194]. This enhancement is intrinsically linked to the unique chemical properties of pseudouridine. Unlike uridine, Ψ possesses an additional hydrogen bond donor (N1–H), which rigidifies the phosphodiester backbone and enhances base-stacking interactions. These structural elements are thought to be primarily responsible for bypassing innate immune sensors and stabilizing the mRNA secondary structure, thereby facilitating more efficient ribosome recruitment and processivity [101, 195].

Impact on translational fidelity and elongation

Despite its stimulatory effects on total protein yield, the presence of Ψ can compromise translational fidelity and alter elongation kinetics. Replacing uridine with Ψ in stop codons has been shown to suppress translation termination both in S. cerevisiae and cell-free systems. This phenomenon allows ΨAA and ΨAG to encode serine or threonine, while ΨGA can direct the incorporation of phenylalanine or tyrosine [196]. This readthrough is likely facilitated by specific Ψ-Ψ interactions within the codon-anticodon pair [197].

Furthermore, complete substitution of U with Ψ can lead to increased rates of amino acid misincorporation. In both reconstructed bacterial systems and human cells, luciferase translated from Ψ-modified mRNA exhibited a 20-fold higher error rate compared to uridine-containing controls [198]. However, under non-stress conditions, these substitutions occurred in only 1.5% of cases and were largely neutral missense mutations, suggesting that Ψ does not typically result in dysfunctional protein aggregates in the absence of cellular stress. Notably, the accuracy of T7 RNA polymerase is also lower during the incorporation of Ψ compared to m1Ψ or U, which may further contribute to downstream amino acid substitutions [199].

Position-dependent effects and elongation kinetics

The frequency of amino acid substitutions is highly sensitive to the sequence context and the position of Ψ within the codon. For example, Ψ at the first position (ΨUU) causes 3.5 times more substitutions (Phe to Val, Leu, or Ile) than Ψ at the second position (UΨU) [198]. Biophysical data indicate that Ψ generally reduces the protein synthesis rate by lowering the observed rate constant (kobs) and hindering EF-Tu-mediated GTP hydrolysis (kGPT) [198, 200]. Experimental evidence shows that even a single U→Ψ substitution in a phenylalanine codon can reduce local protein expression by nearly twofold, while the triple substitution (ΨΨΨ) can suppress it by threefold [22, 180].

m1Ψ and 5moU

Since m1Ψ and 5moU are absent in native mammalian mRNA, there is currently no endogenous data regarding their spatial distribution across transcript regions. However, their strategic incorporation during IVT has yielded significant therapeutic advantages. mRNA containing m1Ψ can elevate target protein expression by up to 10-fold compared to unmodified transcripts [117, 119, 201]. This enhancement is primarily attributed to the ability of m1Ψ to facilitate ribosome recruitment and promote polysome formation while simultaneously protecting mRNA from degradation through structural stabilization [117, 202]. In various cell lines, the use of m1Ψ, either alone or in combination with m5C, has been shown to outperform both Ψ and the m1Ψ/m5C combination in stimulating the translation of reporter proteins like firefly luciferase [119].

Despite these benefits, the impact of m1Ψ on translation is not uniformly positive. In reconstructed bacterial cell-free systems, m1Ψ-modified mRNA is associated with a decreased rate of peptide bond formation. Interestingly, this inhibitory effect is less pronounced or absent in eukaryotic systems [22, 180, 198, 203], likely due to the inherently lower translation elongation rates in eukaryotes, which may mitigate susceptibility to m1Ψ-mediated interference. Furthermore, evidence suggests that m1Ψ maintains a higher degree of translational fidelity than Ψ. While Ψ significantly increases the risk of amino acid substitutions, m1Ψ-modified mRNA often results in substitution rates comparable to unmodified controls [203].

Positional effects on decoding and frameshifting

The influence of m1Ψ on decoding kinetics is highly dependent on its position within the codon. In phenylalanine codons (UUU), m1Ψ at the first or second positions generally does not alter the rate of amino acid incorporation, whereas a substitution at the wobble position (UUm1Ψ) can induce a twofold increase in the observed kobs. Crucially, unlike Ψ, m1Ψ does not appear to compromise translation termination at stop codons. However, position-specific effects remain complex: for instance, Um1ΨU can decrease kobs for leucine and isoleucine while significantly increasing it for serine. In eukaryotic cells, the overall amino acid substitution rate for m1Ψ-modified luciferase was reported at approximately 0.8%, with phenylalanine being replaced by serine, isoleucine, or leucine — a rate slightly higher than that of unmodified mRNA but varying across specific codons [200].

A critical and recently identified undesired effect of m1Ψ is its propensity to induce + 1 ribosomal frameshifting. The presence of m1Ψ in mRNA vaccines has been shown to trigger the production of off-target proteins, potentially leading to unintended immune responses against these frameshifted products [204]. This discovery highlights the need for careful sequence optimization to minimize recoding events in m1Ψ-based therapeutics.

5moU: a specialized regulator

This nucleoside analog has demonstrated a dual capacity to both inhibit and facilitate translation depending on the experimental context [198, 205]. In certain scenarios, the introduction of 5moU, particularly in combination with m5C, significantly reduces protein synthesis [204]. However, other studies suggest that 5moU can serve as a potent alternative to m1Ψ by further reducing immunogenicity while maintaining sufficient translational output. The specific impact of 5moU appears to be highly sensitive to the sequence architecture and the target cell type, representing an emerging frontier in the fine-tuning of therapeutic mRNA platforms.

2’O-Me/Nm

The distribution of 2’-O-methylation (Nm) in mRNA is characterized by a distinctive enrichment within the CDS, specifically in proximity to splicing sites. In contrast, Nm frequency is lower in the 3’UTR and reaches its nadir in the 5’UTR [127, 206, 207]. While the relative abundance of specific modified nucleosides (Am, Gm, Cm, Um) varies across model organisms, a pronounced tendency has been observed for Nm to occupy the first position of codons within the CDS, occurring approximately twice as frequently as in the second or third positions.

The most frequently modified codons in the CDS include those for cysteine (UGU, UGC), valine (GUC, GUU, GUA, GUG), and leucine (GUG). Sequence motif analysis has identified both extended and short consensus sequences flanking Nm sites. In human cell lines (HeLa and HEK293T), Um appears to be the prevalent modification, often found within GUmC, CUmC and UUmC motifs [208]. Conversely, Nm sites regulated by fibrillarin are predominantly represented by Gm at the first codon position, frequently embedded in GUGmU, CUGmC and UGmU contexts. Recent methodological advances have further expanded this landscape, revealing a prevalence of Cm among newly identified Nm sites, whereas previously characterized sites were dominated by Am and Gm [209].

Mechanisms of ribosomal stalling and decoding interference

As previously noted, 2’-O-methylation occurs both in the cap-proximal regions and throughout the CDS, exerting profound effects on translation by altering mRNA interactions with tRNA and the ribosome. Early studies using bacterial cell-free systems demonstrated that the presence of a single Nm analog significantly reduces translational efficiency. The magnitude of this inhibition is highly position-dependent: peptide synthesis is reduced by 10–15% when Nm is at the first position, 90% at the second, and 50% at the third [22]. This inhibitory effect stems from ribosome halting when the modified codon occupies the ribosomal A-site which is consistent with the increased frequency of Nm occurrence in the first position of the codon, where this modification has the least inhibitory effect. Mechanistically, the 2’-O-methyl group does not create insurmountable steric barriers to codon–anticodon duplex formation; rather, it disrupts the canonical interaction between the mRNA–tRNA helix and the ribosomal monitoring bases. The steric mismatch induced by the Nm group compromises the correct stereochemistry required for the activation of the EF-Tu GTPase. This delay reduces the probability of successful GTP hydrolysis and subsequent tRNA accommodation. While Nm does not inhibit the initial stages of tRNA binding to the A-site, it fundamentally hinders the decoding process and leads to the excessive rejection of cognate aminoacylated tRNAs [159, 210].

Regulatory implications in protein synthesis

Interestingly, Nm modifications are frequently identified in mRNAs characterized by high baseline translational efficiency. This suggests that Nm serves as a critical regulatory «brake», preventing excessive protein expression and ensuring precise control over protein synthesis, degradation, and nuclear export. By fine-tuning the rate of elongation, 2’-O-methylation provides an additional layer of epitranscriptomic quality control, balancing the structural integrity of the transcript with the functional demands of the cellular proteome.

The impact of modified nucleosides on mRNA immunogenicity and their use in mRNA-based therapy

The efficacy and prospects of using mRNA vaccines for the treatment of various diseases depend, among other factors, on the immunological properties of the formulation, specifically its immunogenicity and reactogenicity. Since there is no absolute consensus on the use of these terms in the literature, in this review, we define immunogenicity as the ability of an mRNA therapeutic to trigger a target-specific humoral immune response, and reactogenicity as the activation of intracellular innate immune sensing systems.

The innate immune system provides the first line of defense against pathogens and utilizes pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) as well as endogenous ligands released in response to cellular damage (DAMPs). The most critical class of ligands for innate immune receptors consists of nucleic acids: exogenous DNA, RNA released during viral infection, and double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), which serves as an intermediate in viral RNA-dependent replication [211]. PRRs are capable of detecting «foreign» (exogenous) RNA within the diverse pool of the cell’s endogenous RNAs, which are ubiquitously present in the cytoplasm, nucleus, and other cellular compartments [212].

Upon entry into the cell, foreign bacterial, viral, or mitochondrial RNA activates the innate immune response via several molecular cascades. The most extensively studied signaling pathway is triggered by the activation of PRRs, specifically Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs) [213, 214]. Within the latter family, two primary receptors are of paramount importance: RIG-I, which recognizes 5’-triphosphorylated or diphosphorylated single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) and acts as the main cytosolic RNA sensor [215, 216], and MDA5, which recognizes viral RNA replicating in the cytoplasm [217, 218]. MDA5, similar to RIG-I, is also capable of recognizing dsRNA; however, long dsRNA serves as the optimal MDA5 ligand, whereas RIG-I predominantly detects short dsRNA [219]. The presence of a 5’-cap structure on native mRNAs masks these molecules, preventing their recognition by RIG-I or MDA5 [220]. Another member of this family, LGP2 (Laboratory of Genetics and Physiology 2), possesses a domain structure analogous to that of RIG-I and MDA5 but has received comparatively less attention [221]. A critical step in stimulating protective immune and inflammatory responses is the precise recognition of the nucleic acid ligand, the mechanisms of which warrant detailed examination.

The RIG-I (Retinoic Acid Inducible Gene I) receptor is a large multimeric protein. Its distinctive structural feature is the presence of tandem amino-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domains (CARDs), which mediate signal transduction through downstream signaling cascades. RIG-I also features an atypical RNA-dependent ATPase motor domain (helicase domain), consisting of two RecA domains (Hel1 and Hel2) and an alpha-helical insertion domain (Hel2i), connected by a V-shaped pincer motif, and a carboxy-terminal domain (CTD) [216]. In its inactivated state, RIG-I sequesters the CARDs against the Hel2i surface, thereby locking the protein in an autoinhibitory conformation. It has been established that RIG-I interactions with endogenous RNA result in the formation of transient, low-affinity complexes. These complexes are unable to stimulate sustained CARD release and trigger subsequent activation. However, interaction with viral RNA induces large-scale conformational changes, leading to the partial unfolding of the Hel2 autoinhibitory loop and firm anchoring of the RNA within the RIG-I receptor. This, in turn, triggers the release of the CARD domains and stimulates a pro-inflammatory and antiviral immune response [216].

The RIG-I signaling pathway is activated by short RNA duplexes (at least 10 nucleotides in length) and blunt-ended double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) bearing a 5’-triphosphate (5’-ppp) moiety, a feature commonly found in pathogenic RNAs. In contrast, structural elements such as 5’ or 3’ overhangs on dsRNAs, 5’ capping (specifically m7G capping), 2’-O-methylation, and the presence of G-U wobble pairs, G-A pairs, or other mismatches prevent the binding of endogenous RNAs to RIG-I, thereby averting the activation of the innate immune response [222]. Although primarily localized in the cytoplasm, RIG-I is also sequestered in the nucleus, mitochondria, microsomes, and mitochondria-associated membranes [223]. Upon interaction with «foreign» RNA and the subsequent release of its CARD domains, the RIG-I signaling cascade is initiated, ultimately leading to the upregulated production of Type I interferons (IFNs) and various pro-inflammatory cytokines.

As previously noted, MDA5 is capable of identifying dsRNA and exhibits significant structural homology with the RIG-I receptor. It has been established that long dsRNAs (> 0.5 kb) serve as the primary ligands for MDA5, with the length of the dsRNA being a critical factor in triggering downstream cascades. Increasing the dsRNA length (from 1 to 7 kb) has been shown to progressively enhance the IFN-mediated response through MDA5 activation [219]. However, the precise length-dependent mechanism of MDA5 activation remains to be fully elucidated [224]. LGP2, the third member of the RLR family, is characterized by the absence of a CARD domain and functions as a key regulator of both RIG-I and MDA5 signaling [225].

Other essential nucleic acid sensors include Toll-like receptors (TLRs) sequestered within intracellular compartments such as the endoplasmic reticulum, endosomes, lysosomes, and endolysosomes (specifically TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9). All TLRs, whether localized on the plasma membrane or within intracellular vesicles, share a conserved structural organization: an N-terminal domain containing leucine-rich repeats (LRRs), a single transmembrane (TM) helix, and a C-terminal cytoplasmic Toll-interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domain. The specificity of ligand recognition is governed by structural variations in the LRR domains. To interact with their respective PAMPs or DAMPs, TLRs undergo homo- or heterodimerization, often involving co-receptors or accessory molecules [226]. The structural nuances of these TLRs and the specificities of their ligand recognition are examined in detail below.

TLR3 is a well-established and extensively studied Toll-like receptor for nucleic acids, specifically double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) and its synthetic analogue, polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly I: C). It recognizes dsRNA molecules longer than 40–50 bp, whether of exogenous origin or formed as a result of the lysis or necrosis of virus-infected cells. dsRNA ligands bind to two distinct regions of the TLR3 ectodomain: one at the C-terminus (LRR19–LRR21) and the other near the N-terminus (LRR-NT and LRR1–LRR3) [227]. Within the cell, TLR3 exists as a monomer and a membrane-bound receptor; however, binding to dsRNA induces the formation of a signaling complex consisting of one dsRNA molecule and two TLR3 molecules. While the C-terminal dimerization site is critical for dsRNA binding and subsequent signaling, TLR3 activation also requires an acidic pH to remain functional [228].

Other nucleic acid sensors in this family, such as TLR7 and TLR8, possess a similar structural organization and recognize related ligands. These include viral or bacterial single-stranded RNAs (ssRNAs) rich in guanine (G) or uracil (U), double-stranded siRNAs, microRNAs, and endogenous RNAs released from necrotic cells, as well as nucleosides, nucleoside analogues (e.g., loxoribine), and oligonucleotides within endosomal compartments [229]. Like other TLRs, these receptors exist in an inactive monomeric state, with dimerization occurring only upon ligand binding. Two distinct binding sites have been identified: Site 1, which is highly conserved and recognizes specific nucleosides (guanosine or deoxyguanosine for TLR7; uridine for TLR8); and Site 2, which is less conserved and binds to short RNA fragments, recognizing primarily uridine for TLR7 and guanosine for TLR8. Site 2 synergisticallly enhances the ligand-binding affinity of Site 1 [227]. Oligoribonucleotides may also increase the affinity of TLR7/8 for nucleosides. Similarly, TLR9 serves as a sensor for single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) and utilizes two binding sites that cooperate to promote TLR9 dimerization and activation. The first site recognizes ssDNA containing unmethylated CpG motifs, which are rare in mammalian genomes but prevalent in viral and bacterial DNA, while the second site binds to DNA fragments containing cytosine [230].

Following ligand binding and dimerization, TLRs initiate various signaling cascades via the MyD88-dependent and/or TRIF-dependent pathways. All TLRs, with the exception of TLR3, primarily utilize the MyD88-dependent pathway, which leads to the activation of the key transcription factor NF-κB and the subsequent upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6. Rapid activation of NF-κB promotes immediate inflammatory responses, which are crucial for the initial phase of pathogen defense [231]. In contrast, ligand binding to TLR3 and TLR4 receptors leads to the activation of interferon-regulatory factors (IRFs), particularly IRF3, resulting in the production of Type I interferons (IFN-α and IFN-β) necessary for antiviral defense [232].

While dsRNA activates TLR3 [233, 234] and ssRNA activates TLR7 [235], RNA oligonucleotides with phosphorothioate internucleotide bonds serve as ligands for TLR8 [236]. Thus, foreign RNA is capable of activating TLR3, TLR7, and TLR8. Thus, foreign RNA is capable of activating TLR3, TLR7, and TLR8. However, recent research indicates that the incorporation of modified nucleosides (e.g., m5C, m6A, 5-methyluridine (m5U), 2-thiouridine (s2U), or Ψ) into mRNA significantly reduces or entirely prevents TLR activation [4]. This modification strategy decreases the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and activation markers, such as IFN-α and IFN-β, thereby minimizing the reactogenicity of mRNA-based therapeutics.

Protein kinase R (PKR) can also be activated in response to the presence of foreign RNA within the cell. Although PKR is not a canonical pattern recognition receptor and does not directly induce antiviral or pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades, it suppresses the translation of both viral and host mRNAs, leading to global translation inhibition. Various RNA modifications, such as the m7G cap analogue, m6A, A-to-I editing, and several others, have been shown to suppress PKR activation [237–239].

Another mechanism underlying the cellular response to foreign RNA is mediated by 2’,5’-oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS), which binds to dsRNA to form 2’,5’-oligoadenylate (2–5 A). This molecule serves as a secondary mediator for RNase L activation, inducing its dimerization or oligomerization. Activated RNase L subsequently degrades cellular RNA, leading to translation termination [240, 241]. mRNA modifications are capable of inhibiting OAS activation and the subsequent degradation of RNA by RNase L [242].

In general, the presence of modified nucleosides in RNA significantly reduces the activation of the innate immune response upon cellular entry by modulating the recognition of foreign agents by PRRs. Figure 3 shows a general diagram of the influence of various modified nucleosides on RIG-I-dependent and TLRdependent signaling pathways. For instance, mammalian mRNA, rRNA, and tRNA possess a unique post-transcriptional pattern characterized by a significantly higher diversity of modified nucleoside analogues compared to bacterial RNA; consequently, they do not trigger an immune response upon entry into the cell [243]. Modified nucleosides have also been identified in many viral RNAs; notably, modified nucleosides were found to be more common in viral mRNAs than in cellular mRNAs [244], a feature that allows viruses to evade the degradation of their genetic material [245].

The employment of modified nucleosides in the development of mRNA vaccines improves their stability and facilitates their «masking» upon cell entry. This significantly reduces their immunogenicity, thereby extending their pharmacological half-life (Fig. 3). However, a decrease in immunogenicity is not the sole factor governing the inclusion of specific analogues in mRNA formulations. In addition to modulating immunogenicity, these analogues also regulate the stability and translation (including both its efficiency and fidelity) of mRNA molecules, factors that can critically affect the efficacy of the therapeutic. Currently, researchers are primarily focused on protein expression indicators when selecting modified nucleosides; as a result, the most widely utilized analogues for mRNA therapy remain Ψ and m1Ψ.

Below, we examine in detail how specific modified nucleosides in mRNA modulate the immune response. We further review studies characterizing the therapeutic potential of less-studied modifications-including m6A, m5C, hm5C, ac4C, 5moU and Nm — in the context of mRNA encoding reporter proteins, alongside clinical and preclinical trials of therapeutics incorporating these analogs. Notably, as some of these analogues are utilized far less frequently in mRNA therapy than others, their biological effects, including potential adverse outcomes, remain poorly characterized. To date, no comprehensive toxicity studies have been conducted for m6A, hm5C, ac4C and Nm. Consequently, the safety profile and scalability of these specific analogues in mRNA-based formulations cannot yet be definitively assessed.

m6A

The m6A modification serves as a molecular label for the innate recognition of self and non-self RNA by cells. Studying the effects of introducing m6A into mRNA on its interaction with the RIG-I receptor showed that the binding constant of RNA containing 100% m6A was six times higher than that of unmodified mRNA, indicating reduced RNA affinity for the receptor. Notably, in the case of the Ψ modification, the binding constant was only 1.4 times higher. In Huh7 cells, the binding efficiency between m6A-modified mRNA and RIG-I was 40% lower compared to unmodified mRNA. Like most chemical modifications, such as Ψ and m5C, m6A in mRNA did not induce IFN-β secretion, in contrast to unmodified RNA [217]. Therefore, it can be concluded that the weak interaction between RNAs containing m6A and RIG-I alleviates the immune response [217, 246]. Furthermore, m6A prevents the binding of in vitro transcribed RNA to RIG-I [217]. This hypothesis is also supported by studies on the immune response to cellular entry of the recombinant HMPV virus deficient in m6A. The interaction of viral RNA lacking m6A with the cell significantly induced RIG-I receptor expression and increased its RNA binding affinity, resulting in an increase in Type I IFN secretion [245]. It should be noted that this mechanism has been demonstrated in both cell lines and animal models.

The ability of m6A introduced into RNA to reduce cytokine and chemokine production during the development of the immune response has been demonstrated in numerous studies. In an extensive study focused on the immune response to mRNA containing m6A, researchers used HEK293 cell lines expressing human TLR3, TLR7, or TLR8 as an in vitro model [4]. The presence of 25% m6A in mRNA reduced IL-8 expression by 90%, 8%, and 60% in HEK293-TLR3, HEK293-TLR7, and HEK293-TLR8, respectively, compared to unmodified mRNA. In monocyte-derived dendritic cells (MDDCs), lipofection with 25% m6A mRNA reduced the surface expression of CD80, CD83, CD86, and MHC class II activation markers. Simultaneously, an increase in m6A content in mRNA proportionally inhibited the ability of the RNA to induce TNF-α synthesis in MDDCs. Introducing 0.2–0.4% m6A (corresponding to approximately 3 to 6 modified nucleosides per 1571-nucleotide transcript) was sufficient to inhibit cytokine secretion, while 1.7–3.2% m6A suppressed TNF-α expression by 50% [4]. Incorporating 100% m6A into mRNA did not affect IFN-β secretion or MDA5 ATPase activity in HEK293 cells, unlike unmodified mRNA [247]. However, in dendritic cells derived from human peripheral blood, m6A-modified mRNA exerted an immunostimulatory effect comparable to that of unmodified mRNA [4].

The immune response can also be affected by the method of intracellular delivery. Endosomal delivery of m6A led to a more pronounced induction of immune response reactions, increasing TNF-α and IL-6 secretion by 2- and 9-fold, respectively, 6 hours after transfection. However, 24 h after transfection with m6A-modified mRNA, IL-6 secretion was less pronounced (1.3-fold), while TNF-α induction was comparable to that of unmodified mRNA [248].

Incorporating m6A into circular RNA (circRNA) suppresses the recognition of circRNA by the innate immune system. The immunogenicity of modified circRNA encoding GFP and containing 1% and 5% m6A was also studied in vivo in a murine model. Mice were vaccinated with a prime injection of modified or canonical circRNA-GFP and a booster injection after 5 weeks. Transfection with unmodified circRNA induced the expression of innate immune response genes, such as RIG-I, MDA5, OAS, OASL, and PKR. Incorporating 5% m6A into circRNA resulted in a twofold decrease in the expression of these genes, while introducing 1% m6A reduced the response of activated CD8 + T cells by 75% and antibody titers by 50% compared to canonical circRNA-GFP. These data indicate that circRNA containing 1% m1A in vivo elicited a weaker immune response than unmodified circRNA [249].

Thus, m6A reduces RNA reactogenicity by suppressing its binding to innate immune receptors (RIG-I, TLR3, TLR7, TLR8) that recognize exogenous RNA. However, at high concentrations, introducing this modification into both linear and circular mRNAs can negatively affect translational efficiency. For instance, in rabbit reticulocyte lysates (RRL), 100% m6A repressed translation, whereas in HEK293 and primary mouse dendritic cells, incorporating 5% m6A did not lead to any significant change in target protein expression compared to unmodified mRNA [108]. Furthermore, 100% m6A reduced NanoLuc reporter protein expression by 98% and 99% compared to unmodified RNA when using endosomal and non-endosomal RNA delivery into RAW 264.7 cells [248].

Overall, the greatest potential has been shown for m6A introduced into circular RNAs: a low content of incorporated modified analogues (1–5%) efficiently mimics endogenous patterns, reducing reactogenicity without significantly affecting translation levels. Incorporating m6A into RNA preparations has great potential and is currently used in both reporter mRNAs and mRNA therapeutics (Table 1). However, m6A is rarely used in mRNA therapeutics. One example of such use is the RNA vaccines developed for gene replacement therapy for anemia [253]. The study compared the effects of 5% m6A-circRNA and 100% Ψ-mRNA encoding human erythropoietin (hEpo). Plasma hEpo levels were assessed using ELISA on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7 post-injection, and the percentage of reticulocytes was counted on day 7. On day 1, plasma hEpo levels after m6A-circRNA administration were 2 times lower than after Ψ-mRNA administration. However, hEpo expression in the Ψ-mRNA group declined rapidly within 2 days, whereas in the m6A-circRNA group, it remained stable for at least 96 h post-injection, peaking at 72 h and reaching levels fivefold higher than those in the Ψ-mRNA group. Furthermore, only those mice receiving a single dose of hEpo-circRNA showed a 40% increase in reticulocyte production [253].

Notably, the most effective approach for reporter mRNA and gene replacement therapy was the combined use of m6A with other nucleoside analogues, which will be discussed below.

m5C

As with m6A modification, incorporating m5C into RNA reduces its reactogenicity. Lipofection of HEK293 cells with m5C-modified RNA decreased TLR3 and TLR8 activation levels, albeit to a lesser extent than m6A. However, as assessed by IL-8 expression, m5C reduced TLR7 activation as effectively as m6A. Furthermore, m5C decreased TNF-α and IL-12 secretion, as well as the expression of CD80, CD83, CD86, and MHC class II on the surface of MDDCs. The inhibitory effect on TNF-α secretion was dependent on the percentage of modified bases in the RNA [4]. Reduced reactogenicity of m6A-modified mRNA and self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) was also demonstrated in 293-ISRE, CHME-ISRE, and THP-1 Dual cell lines, which express luciferase upon activation of innate immune receptors [254]. Immune response activation was further estimated using PBMCs; in these cells, m5C-modified mRNA and saRNA caused a less pronounced increase in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IP-10 and IFN-α2 compared to unmodified mRNA [254].

The efficacy of innate immune receptor suppression was affected not only by m5C content but also by its combination with other nucleoside analogues. Lipofection of SiHa cells with a combination of m5C and m1Ψ introduced into different mRNA regions significantly reduced IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α expression compared to unmodified RNA. This effect was more pronounced when m5C was located in the 5’UTR-CDS and m1Ψ was restricted to the 3’UTR, rather than when m5C was located only in the CDS and m1Ψ in the UTRs [255]. In RAW264.7 cells, mRNAs containing m5C or a combination of m5C + Ψ inhibited the secretion of IFN-β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β more efficiently than both unmodified mRNA and 2sU-modified mRNA, with the effect being dose-dependent [256]. The combined use of 25% m5C and 25% 2sU during RNA synthesis reduced the activation of TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and RIG-I after PBMC transfection. Moreover, administering the mRNA-megafectin complex to mice reduced serum levels of IFN-γ, IL-12, and IFN-α compared to mRNA containing 50% 2sU or unmodified mRNA [257]. Studies on PolyU/UC RNA showed that while m5C incorporation did not alter RIG-I binding, the modified RNA failed to induce the conformational changes necessary to trigger receptor activation [217].

Thus, the experimental data indicate that introducing m5C into RNA generally alleviates its reactogenicity. However, one study [258] reported contradictory results: lipofection of whole human blood samples with m5C-modified mRNA increased TNF-α and IL-6 secretion, an effect that persisted regardless of whether chromatographic purification was used to remove mRNA impurities.

Studies involving cell-free systems and cell cultures demonstrate that the complete or partial substitution of cytidines with 5-methylcytidines stimulates target protein expression (Table 2). In HEK293 cells, the presence of m5C in mRNAs encoding FLuc and RLuc slightly increased luciferase expression; notably, the translation rate of m5C-modified RLuc mRNA was ~ 3.5 times higher than that of unmodified mRNA. Similar results were obtained in primary mouse dendritic cells (DCs), where luciferase expression increased 2.2-fold [108]. Another study using primary human neonatal epidermal keratinocytes showed that complete substitution of cytidines with m5C in GFP transcripts resulted in a 2.5-fold increase in cell viability and a 3.5-fold increase in ectopic reporter protein expression [68]. However, in an in vivo study where mice received intramuscular injections of LNPs containing m5C-modified saRNA encoding a modified luciferase reporter (CBR2opt), m5C incorporation did not significantly affect CBR2opt expression compared to unmodified saRNA [259].

m5C is routinely used in reporter mRNAs and various mRNA therapeutics targeting a wide range of molecules (Table 3). For instance, one such vaccine utilized m5C-modified saRNA expressing the receptor-binding domain of the SARS-CoV-2 S protein (S-RBD). Incorporating m5C into LNP-saRNA did not impair its immunogenicity and promoted humoral immunity in BALB/c mice [254]. Other in vivo studies revealed significant protective effects of m5C-modified saRNA vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 [261]. Healthy mice received prime and boost intramuscular injections (21 days apart) of 10 ng or 1000 ng of either unmodified or m5C-modified saRNA. By day 35, following a lethal intranasal challenge with SARS-CoV-2, all mice vaccinated with 1000 ng of either saRNA variant survived with minimal weight loss. However, at the 10 ng dose, survival rates were 45% for unmodified saRNA versus 75% for m5C-saRNA. Twenty-four hours post-injection, mice vaccinated with 1000 ng of m5C-saRNA exhibited 50% lower serum IFN-α1 levels compared to those receiving unmodified saRNA. While no significant differences in S-specific IgG titers were observed at the 1000 ng dose, the 10 ng dose of m5C-saRNA elicited 5-fold higher IgG titers than the unmodified version [261].

Beyond murine studies, m5C-modified saRNA is employed in the VLPCOV-02 booster vaccine, currently in clinical trials. Phase I results demonstrated a favorable safety profile. Most adverse events (AEs) were mild to moderate, occurring more frequently in the non-elderly (55.6%) than the elderly (44.4%) population, with frequency increasing dose-dependently. Serious systemic events occurred in less than 16.7% of participants, primarily in the 15 µg cohort. Participants vaccinated with 1, 3, 7.5, or 15 µg of 5% m5C-saRNA showed approximately 3-fold higher IgG titers by day 28 compared to baseline, though clinical efficacy was not reported [266].

The m5C modification is also utilized in therapeutics such as follistatin mRNA for restoring muscle mass. В mice, a single administration of m5C-modified mRNA increased serum follistatin levels 24-fold after 8 h, returning to baseline by 96 h. Consequently, a regimen of 16 injections over 8 weeks was implemented. Twenty-four hours after the final injection, serum follistatin was 1.8 times higher than in untreated controls. The wet weight of calf and triceps muscles increased by 9.6% and 23.9%, respectively, confirming therapeutic efficacy. No toxicity was observed, and there was no significant elevation in pro-inflammatory genes (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1b) or liver markers (CRP and APC) compared to controls [262].

In conclusion, data from reporter mRNAs and mRNA-based therapeutics suggest that, in most cases, introducing m5C into mRNA promotes target protein expression and suppresses innate immune activation.

hm5C

Similarly to m5C, the introduction of hm5C into mRNA alleviates reactogenicity by suppressing the induction of IFN-α1 and IFN-β1. Specifically, PBMC transfection with hm5C-modified LNP-saRNA resulted in 8.5- and 3-fold lower expression of these cytokines, respectively, compared to unmodified RNA [261]. In Huh7 cells, hm5C-modified mRNA led to a 6-fold decrease in IFN-β secretion; however, this reduction was less pronounced than that observed for mRNA containing m6A, m5C, Ψ, or m1Ψ [217]. Conversely, transfection of whole human blood with hm5C-modified Cas9 mRNA induced a slight increase in IL-12 levels and a significant elevation in TNF-α and IL-6 secretion, regardless of whether chromatographic purification was performed [258].

Another study [261] evaluated the impact of hm5C on saRNA efficacy, finding that its incorporation increased target protein expression more than 100-fold relative to unmodified saRNA. Furthermore, hm5C incorporation during IVT significantly suppressed dsRNA formation. In HEK293T cells, hm5C-modified saRNA encoding the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein provided approximately 2-fold higher expression than unmodified saRNA, whereas m1Ψ incorporation suppressed protein expression. Similarly, in C2C12 cells, hm5C-modified saRNA yielded 2- and ~ 8-fold higher protein expression than unmodified saRNA and m1Ψ-modified mRNA, respectively. Comparable results were observed for saRNA encoding influenza hemagglutinin [261].

The effect of hm5C on translation efficiency varied depending on the co-incorporated nucleoside analogues and the incubation temperature in RRL. This variability may be attributed to alterations in the mRNA secondary structure, which subsequently affect interactions with the ribosomal apparatus [122]. At 37 °C, the translation efficiency of FLuc mRNA modified with hm5C, hm5C + 5moU, and hm5C + m1Ψ reached 0.3, 0.5, and 0.9, respectively, while the hm5C + m5U combination was 2-fold more efficient than unmodified mRNA. In contrast, at 30 °C, the translation efficiencies were 0.25, 0.3, and 0.65 for the same combinations, while hm5C-modified mRNA alone was 4.7 times more efficient than its unmodified counterpart. Transfection of Hep3B and THP-1 cells with FLuc and eGFP mRNA revealed that the hm5C + m5U combination outperformed unmodified RNA only in THP-1 cells (increasing FLuc and eGFP intensities by 4-fold and 1.3-fold, respectively). However, these modifications were significantly less effective than m1Ψ or Ψ, which enhanced signal intensity in THP-1 by 15–40 times for FLuc and 1.5–3.1 times for eGFP [122].

In conclusion, hm5C-modified mRNA promotes protein translation (up to 100-fold) compared to unmodified mRNA, and its combination with other analogues represents a promising approach for RNA therapeutics. Nevertheless, current data are insufficient to draw a definitive conclusion regarding the overall impact of hm5C on mRNA reactogenicity.

ac4C

Evidence regarding the role of ac4C modification in modulating the RNA-mediated immune response remains scarce. It has been shown that TNF-α secretion by MoDCs following the introduction of mRNA harboring ac4C is less pronounced compared to unmodified mRNA, and in some cases, comparable to background levels. Similar results were obtained for IL-1β, IL-10, and other cytokines. Analysis of secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase activity in HEK293T cells demonstrated that N4-acetylation significantly affects the NF-κB pathway and alleviates PKR activation, although it does not reduce TLR3 activation compared to cytidine [190]. In general, incorporating ac4C into in vitro transcribed mRNA reduced eGFP expression compared to unmodified RNA in the presence of a cap structure. However, in the case of uncapped mRNA, ac4C exhibited high efficiency, suggesting that this modification may trigger cap-independent translation initiation [190]. Conversely, another study reported that the presence of ac4C in luciferase mRNA elevated reporter protein expression approximately 12-fold [88].

To sum up, ac4C holds promise for use in mRNA therapeutics, as it facilitates target protein translation and does not appear to induce innate immune activation. However, further research is needed to confirm this assumption.

Ψ

One common approach for mRNA modification is the complete substitution of uridine with pseudouridine, which is sometimes combined with the substitution of cytidine with 5-methylcytidine [268]. The Ψ modification has been shown to modulate both the immunogenicity and reactogenicity of RNA therapeutics.

In HEK293 cells, the presence of Ψ in transfected dsRNA did not reduce the activation of the MDA5 receptor, as MDA5 binds to the dsRNA backbone and does not interact directly with the bases [269].

RNAs harboring Ψ, m6A, or s2U only slightly activated 2’–5’-oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) and failed to trigger the production of functionally active 2–5 A, thereby inhibiting RNA cleavage by RNase L. Furthermore, mRNA containing Ψ suppressed OAS activation, likely due to a reduction in the dsRNA ratio within the preparation rather than a direct impact on OAS binding [242].

Following the lipofection of dendritic cells, Ψ-modified mRNA was found to activate TNF-α, IL-12, and IFN-α expression less significantly than unmodified mRNA, an effect that was further enhanced by combining Ψ with m5C. The inhibition of TNF-α expression was dependent on the relative Ψ content in the mRNA [4]. These findings were confirmed in murine experiments: intravenous administration of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) containing Ψ-modified mRNA significantly reduced TNF-α and IFN-α expression compared to unmodified mRNA [108]. Moreover, transfection of monocytes and pDCs with Ψ-modified mRNA decreased IFN-α expression [270]. Furthermore, introducing Ψ into mRNA led to less pronounced PKR activation; however, this effect was highly sequence-dependent [271]. Transfection of HEK293T cells with Ψ-modified mRNA suppressed the interaction between mRNA and PKR, reduced PKR phosphorylation, and decreased the levels of phosphorylated eIF2α, which normally inhibits translation [272]. The alleviated immunogenicity and reactogenicity of Ψ-modified mRNA are thought to be related to the reduced content of dsRNA formed during IVT [273]. This is supported by the fact that the delivery of mRNA containing Ψ or m5C + Ψ — purified from dsRNA and other contaminants using HPLC — to dendritic cells did not trigger Type I IFN or TNF-α expression, nor did it significantly induce genes associated with RNA sensor activation.

It should be noted, however, that according to a study [268], which scrutinized a large panel of cytokines, chemokines, and various immune cell populations, LNPs containing Ψ-modified mRNA did not reduce immunogenicity during in vivo experiments in mice. Currently, Ψ is primarily used for modifying reporter proteins and RNA therapeutics, often in combination with other modifications described below.

m1Ψ

m1Ψ has been shown to significantly reduce the reactogenicity of modified RNA via three primary mechanisms: (1) the use of m1Ψ during IVT reduces the dsRNA content in the preparation [247]; (2) m1Ψ alters the RNA structure, hindering its recognition by innate immune receptors such as TLR3 and RIG-I [119, 217, 274]; (3) m1Ψ disrupts interactions with receptors that recognize ssRNA, such as TLR7 [275]. Furthermore, mRNA containing m1Ψ does not induce PKR and eIF2α phosphorylation in HeLa lysates, thereby avoiding translation inhibition In 2015, the complete substitution of uridine with m1Ψ was found to reduce mRNA immunogenicity more significantly than Ψ [119]. Incorporating m1Ψ into mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 prevented the activation of immune sensors responding to foreign RNA, mitigating the pro-inflammatory response and ensuring efficient translation of the target antigen [28, 276]. Notably, the Curevac mRNA vaccine (CVnCoV), which also encodes the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and utilizes a similar LNP-based delivery approach but lacks m1Ψ, proved less effective [277].

In human PBMCs, protamine nanoparticles containing m1Ψ-modified mRNA did not induce IFN-α or TNF-α secretion, unlike unmodified mRNA [278]. Lipofection of A549 cells with mRNA containing m1Ψ or a combination of m1Ψ + m5C led to a less pronounced increase in IFN-β and CCL5/RANTES production, with levels remaining comparable to the negative control [119]. According to [279], m1Ψ reduced TNF-α and IL-6 levels more efficiently than Ψ, though to a lesser extent than 5moU. Conversely, another study [271], showed that adding m1Ψ-modified mRNA to whole human blood samples did not stimulate IL-12 secretion but did upregulate TNF-α and IL-6, regardless of whether chromatographic purification was performed. However, these effects were not observed in mice. Murine models demonstrated that LNPs containing purified m1Ψ-modified mRNA significantly reduced IL-6, IFN-α, and TNF-α levels in blood plasma compared to unmodified mRNA [280].

In summary, accumulated evidence indicates that introducing m1Ψ into RNA reduces its reactogenicity. Similar to Ψ, it is frequently used in RNA therapeutics, often in combination with other modifications.

5moU

The introduction of the 5moU modification resulted in a lower dsRNA content in mRNA samples after IVT compared to Ψ, m1Ψ, and m5C. It also completely prevented the induction of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-β in macrophages, even following transfection with high doses of mRNA without chromatographic purification [279, 281]. In contrast to Ψ and m1Ψ, lipofection of bone marrow stem cells with 5moU-modified mRNA did not induce IFN-β or IL-6 secretion, nor did it trigger caspase activation [282]. Furthermore, 5moU only slightly affected cellular metabolic activity and did not cause cellular senescence. It also failed to trigger the migration of CD8 + and CD4 + T cells, while only slightly inducing the migration of CD19 + B cells. Notably, the effects of this modified mRNA on immunogenicity were independent of the nucleotide sequence [282], and transfection did not alter the cell secretome [283]. Even in the absence of HPLC purification, 5moU-containing mRNA did not induce pronounced IL-12 secretion. However, TNF-α and IL-6 levels in whole human blood remained high compared to those induced by Ψ-modified mRNA, regardless of whether HPLC purification was performed [258]. Conversely, in murine models, intravenous infusion of 5moU-mRNA resulted in serum levels of IL-12, TNF-α, and IL-6 that were similar to the negative control [258].

Comparing the efficiencies of modified linear mRNA and unmodified circRNA demonstrated that treatment of HEK293 cells with purified, LNP-encapsulated hEpo-circRNA exhibited stable expression comparable to 5moU-mRNA. The efficiencies of mRNA modified with either 5moU or m1Ψ were similar [252]. Like 5moU-mRNA, unmodified circRNA did not induce RIG-I, TLR3, TLR7, or TLR8 activation, nor did it stimulate IFN-β1 expression. In mice, following local administration of LNP-RNA to visceral fat tissue, serum levels of hEpo encoded by circRNA were two-fold lower than those from 5moU-mRNA. After injection into fat tissue, serum hEpo degradation was slower for LNP-circRNA than for LNP-5moU-mRNA; however, degradation rates were similar following intravenous injection. Neither RNA type elevated cytokine levels or caused local induction of RIG-I, TNF-α, or IL-6 [252].

In summary, the introduction of 5moU into mRNA effectively suppresses reactogenicity. Consequently, it is routinely used for reporter mRNAs (Table 5) and a wide variety of RNA therapeutics (Table 6). For instance, FLuc mRNA modified with 5moU induced luciferase expression more than 700-fold compared to plasmid DNA [284]. Similar potent effects have been demonstrated for numerous 5moU-modified RNA therapeutics, regardless of the administration route [285–288].

The research examined how different delivery systems (LNPs or polyplex nanomicelles) and administration routes (intramuscular or intravenous) of 5moU-modified FLuc mRNA affected reporter signaling, toxicity, muscle damage, and post-injection pain. The results revealed that the FLuc signal was detectable and remained stable for two weeks following intravenous injection. In contrast, luciferase expression from mRNA delivered by LNPs or nanomicelles via intramuscular injection was lower than that observed with intravenous administration. Histological analysis showed extensive interstitial edema and inflammatory infiltration in mice receiving intramuscular LNP injections. Furthermore, cytokine expression analysis revealed that LNP administration drastically elevated IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels, whereas nanomicelles did not. Pain assessment indicated that intramuscular LNP injection was the most traumatizing administration route, while mice receiving nanomicelles experienced almost no discomfort [289].

APE-LNPs containing 5moU-modified FLuc mRNA with C4 to C9 side chains efficiently delivered mRNA to the lungs, spleen, and liver, with organ selectivity depending on the side-chain length [287]. The efficient delivery of 5moU-modified Nanoluc mRNA has also been demonstrated in pigs: the mRNA was detected in the liver, stomach, small and large intestines, spleen, lungs, uterus, red bone marrow, and blood cells, with no signs of hepatotoxicity [288]. In another study, 5moU-modified GFP mRNA was encapsulated into LNPs bearing surface peptides that bind photoreceptors and was subretinally injected into the eyes of Rhesus macaques [290]. Autofluorescent fundus imaging demonstrated GFP expression, with protein synthesis detected in the photoreceptors, retinal pigment epithelium, and Müller glia. These results indicate that the introduction of 5moU into mRNA improved both tissue delivery and target protein production.

However, it is worth noting that the transfection of Hep3B and THP-1 cells with 5moU-modified FLuc and eGFP mRNA was less efficient. FLuc expression was 10- and 2-fold lower, while eGFP expression was 1.5- and 1.75-fold lower compared to unmodified RNA. Moreover, the introduction of m1Ψ and Ψ into THP-1 cells yielded significantly better results, increasing FLuc luminescence 15–40 times and eGFP fluorescence 1.5–3.1 times [122].

The use of 5moU for RNA vaccine development has yielded promising results, particularly in anti-tumor therapy. Mice with OV8-mCherry ovarian tumors were treated with T-sgPLK1-cLNPs conjugated with anti-hEGFR antibodies. This treatment resulted in approximately 82% editing of the PLK1 locus in tumor cells, inhibited tumor growth, and increased viability by ~ 80% [293]. Similarly, a single intracerebral injection of CRISPR-LNPs targeting PLK1 (sgPLK1-cLNP) enabled in vivo editing of 70% of genes in aggressive orthotopic glioblastoma cells, induced tumor cell apoptosis, inhibited tumor growth by 50%, and increased survival by 30% [293]. Pronounced tumor growth suppression was also observed in the MC38-OVA murine colon carcinoma model. Treatment with 5moU-bearing mOVA/CpG-NPs increased median survival from 11 days (control) to 17 days, with full tumor regression and long-term survival observed in 50% of the mice [300]. Utilizing nanoparticles containing the same mRNA-OVA vaccine to treat B16-F10-OVA murine melanoma in combination with adjuvants led to a statistically significant reduction in tumor load and completely prevented tumor growth for over a week. The combined use of the mRNA vaccine with CpG and Poly(I: C) adjuvants increased median survival from 21 days in the control group to 35 and 33 days in the experimental groups, respectively. In mice receiving the OVA mRNA NPs alone, 8.9% of circulating CD8 + T cells were OVA-specific; this proportion was further elevated by CpG and Poly(I: C) adjuvants to 31.0% and 45.1%, respectively [300].

Similar anti-tumor activity of 5moU-containing mRNA has been observed in other melanoma models. In the B16-OVA murine melanoma model, a single vaccination with OVA mRNA-LNPs was shown to significantly increase survival compared to a control group injected with PBS. Nearly 50% of the animals receiving mRNA-LNPs survived for more than 40 days, with 3 out of 11 mice remaining tumor-free for 60 days [299]. Treatment of B16F10 melanoma with TRP2 mRNA-LNPs also significantly delayed tumor growth and increased the viability of treated mice, with over 60% surviving beyond 40 days [299]. In mice vaccinated with A18 LNPs, secondary tumor growth was also suppressed, further confirming the potent anti-tumor effects of these mRNA vaccines. Notably, the vaccination did not induce systemic toxicity [299].

In another study, dendritic cells (DCs) transfected ex vivo with 5moU-modified OVA mRNA were injected into mice bearing melanoma tumors. Administering even small quantities of these modified mRNA-LNPs was shown to significantly upregulate IL-12, which suppresses tumor growth by activating the cellular immune response [298]. These effects were found to be dose-dependent. During in vivo experiments, WT BL6 mice received three intravenous injections of either transfected or non-transfected cells, followed by B16-OVA melanoma cell implantation. While the control group developed tumors within 7 days, the group receiving transfected cells showed the first signs of tumor formation only on day 14. Furthermore, animals treated with 5moU-modified OVA mRNA exhibited pronounced tumor growth inhibition and increased survival [298].

5moU-modified mRNA has also been utilized for the in vivo generation of CAR-T cells, both with and without adjuvants. The group receiving mRNA-CAR-T plus CpG exhibited more pronounced tumor growth inhibition and improved survival compared to those receiving naked mRNA-CAR-T + CpG or mRNA-CAR-T without CpG. While serum from mice immunized with mRNA-CAR-T contained only trace amounts of OVA-specific IgG, a robust OVA-specific T-cell response was registered [303]. This vaccine proved highly efficient in the A20-OVA murine lymphoma model for both large (> 100 mm3) and medium-sized (50–100 mm3) tumors; after three injections of 3 µg OVA mRNA supplemented with 5 µg CpG, 8 out of 10 mice with medium-sized tumors showed complete regression and survival exceeding 75 days. In the group with large tumors, significant growth inhibition was observed, and 40% of the treated animals were completely cured [303].

The use of 5moU-modified mRNA has also demonstrated high efficacy in treating genetic disorders. One study focused on phenylketonuria (PKU) treatment by substituting a single nucleotide in the phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) gene using SaKKH-CBE3 mRNA. Mice were injected with LNPs containing 5moU-modified mRNA encoding the base editor SaKKH-CBE3, alongside SaCas9 sgRNA featuring thioate and Nm modifications. Chemically modified sgRNA was shown to provide 3-fold more efficient editing than unmodified sgRNA both in vitro and in vivo. A single 3 mg/kg injection restored the correct sequence of the target PAH region in 5.5% of cases; after a second administration one week later, this parameter reached 10.8%, with an editing frequency of 21% in isolated hepatocytes. Moreover, blood L-phenylalanine levels dropped below the therapeutic threshold of 360 µM/L after the second injection. Changes in fur color, a phenotypic marker associated with PAH activity, further confirmed the efficacy of this 5moU-mRNA therapeutic [291].

In the work devoted to the development of a base editing system and its verification on the model of tyrosinemia I with FAH protein deficiency, the incorporation of 5moU into ABE mRNA and guide RNA resulted in stable protein expression in HEK293T cells and increased editing efficiency compared to unmodified mRNA [292]. Non-viral LNP-mediated delivery of 5moU-modified ABE mRNA and guide RNA successfully corrected the Fah point mutation in the hepatocytes of mice with tyrosinemia I. The efficacy of this mRNA therapy was comparable to that of pDNA-based therapy. RT-PCR analysis of murine liver tissue confirmed that the majority of FAH mRNA was correctly spliced [292].

Furthermore, the 5moU modification is frequently employed in developing novel delivery systems for RNA therapeutics to enhance stability and reduce reactogenicity. It has been shown that 5moU, similarly to m1Ψ, exerts a stabilizing effect on mRNA secondary structure and base-pairing thermodynamics [111].

For instance, 5moU-modified Cas9 mRNA was used to design novel lipid particles for targeted in vivo drug delivery to tumor cells [292] and 5moU-modified hEpo mRNA served as a reporter gene to test a new LNP formulation method in mice [294]. In both cases, the 5moU modification increased animal survival. Additionally, 5moU was utilized to enhance mRNA stability in studies focused on the thermostability and composition of novel LNPs [301]. The modification was also used to evaluate the efficiency of polysaccharide nanoparticles for cellular mRNA delivery. Utilizing these nanoparticles with 5moU-modified OVA mRNA (encoding the SIINFEKL antigenic peptide) increased the proportion of CD8a + IFN-γ + T cells to approximately 16.0% (compared to ~ 5.27% for naked mRNA) and yielded a 3-fold increase in IgG titers. Furthermore, a prime-boost strategy significantly enhanced OVA-specific IgG production compared to a single administration (by approximately 3 times) [302].

The systemic administration of biodegradable LNPs containing mRNA encoding h-α-Gal A, in which uridine was completely replaced by 5-methoxyuridine, was evaluated in α-Gal-deficient mice (a model for Fabry disease) [295]. Following a single administration of the therapeutic, the h-α-Gal A enzyme produced from the exogenous mRNA exhibited a longer duration of action and an extended half-life in both plasma and tissues compared to control groups. The highest h-α-Gal A activity was observed in the liver, spleen, and plasma, where it increased more than 8-fold, while activity in the heart and kidneys increased 2- to 3-fold 24 h post-injection. Stable enzyme activity was maintained in the liver, heart, and kidney tissues for up to 160 h. Moreover, the enzyme activity demonstrated a clear dose-dependent effect. Notably, repeated mRNA administration in both Fabry disease mice and non-human primates did not elicit the formation of antibodies against h-α-Gal A, indicating improved immune tolerance toward the human protein produced in the liver [295].

Preclinical studies demonstrate the high efficacy of 5moU-modified mRNA vaccines for the treatment of methylmalonic acidemia (MMA). MMA is a rare, inherited metabolic disorder characterized by a deficiency in methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MUT), a mitochondrial enzyme that mediates the final stage of oxidation for valine, isoleucine, and odd-chain fatty acids. Systemic administration of a single dose of 5moU-modified hMUT mRNA provided rapid and sustained hMUT enzymatic activity for one week and significantly reduced the biochemical abnormalities typical of the disease in vivo. Multiple administrations of hMUT mRNA did not elicit changes in hepatotoxicity indicators or inflammatory markers (IL-6, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1β). Following five doses of mRNA, all treated mice survived for six weeks, in contrast to untreated animals, which died during the third week. Given the low therapeutic doses (0.1–0.5 mg/kg), rapid onset of action, sustained efficacy, and the feasibility of repeated administration without triggering hepatotoxicity, inflammation, or an immune response, hMUT mRNA-based therapy represents a versatile and promising option for MMA treatment [296].

The effectiveness of LNPs containing modified mRNA was also demonstrated in a model of acute intermittent porphyria (AIP), caused by haploinsufficiency of porphobilinogen deaminase (PBGD), the third enzyme in the heme biosynthesis pathway [297]. A single administration of hPBGD mRNA featuring 100% 5moU substitution efficiently induced PBGD expression in the liver of wild-type mice, reaching a plateau after 10 h and remaining detectable for 10 days. Notably, hPBGD expression was detected in more than 90% of hepatocytes. Administration of 5moU-modified mRNA to rabbits and non-human primates at the same dosage as in mice led to a 75–80% increase in endogenous PBGD activity. High therapeutic efficacy and biosafety (evidenced by the absence of hepatotoxicity and anti-PBGD antibodies) of 5moU-modified hPBGD mRNA were demonstrated across three animal species (mice, rabbits, and primates). The authors suggest that this mRNA therapeutic could address both sporadic and recurrent clinical manifestations of acute intermittent porphyria [297].

In summary, the nucleoside analogue 5moU is successfully employed in multiple types of mRNA therapy and demonstrates high efficiency across various delivery platforms, making it a highly promising tool for the development of mRNA therapeutics.

2’O-Me/Nm

Numerous studies have shown that incorporating 2’O-Me (Nm) modifications into RNA significantly alleviates the reactogenicity of therapeutics, predominantly by reducing TLRmediated recognition. For instance, 2’O-methylated RNA in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) does not activate TLR7 or induce IFN-α expression. In human monocytes, which do not express TLR7, 2’O-methylated RNA also significantly reduces TNF-α and IL-12p40 expression, likely due to the absence of TLR8 activation. In PBMCs, 2’O-methylated RNA does not trigger significant IL-6 or TNF-α secretion [304].

The administration of LNPs containing a mixture of unmodified and Nm-modified RNA into PBMCs and Flt3L-DCs resulted in less pronounced upregulation of IFN-α and IL-6, indicating the inhibition of TLR7 and TLR8 activation [305]. In vivo experiments involving the simultaneous administration of LNPs containing immunostimulatory RNA and Nm-modified RNA prevented the induction of IFN-α and IL-6, reducing their concentrations to negative control levels. Furthermore, synthetic siRNAs with multiple 2’O-methylations did not trigger TLR7- or TLR8-dependent cytokine secretion [306–308]. Notably, while 2’O-methylated RNA binds more strongly to TLR7 than unmodified RNA [308]; this interaction does not trigger receptor activation.

Conservative 2’O-methylation of G18 in tRNATyr has been shown to avoid TLR7 activation [309–311]. Similarly, a single 2’O-methylation at position 1490 of a synthetic RNA sequence derived from 18 S rRNA prevented IFN-α secretion in Flt3L-DCs. However, it did not affect IL-6 secretion, suggesting suppressed TLR7 activation in contrast to TLR8 [312].

Experiments on dendritic cells yielded similar results [313]. Nm-modified RNA did not trigger TLR7 activation (as assessed by IFN-α and IL-6 expression), though the efficiency of TLR7 inhibition was influenced by the position of the 2’O-methylated nucleoside. The ability of Nm-modified RNA to act as a TLR8 agonist varied significantly depending on the sequence context. Additionally, RNA containing Um was shown to be incapable of binding to RNase L [314].

The impact of 2’O-methylation on translation efficiency was also evaluated in vitro [267]. RNA modified with 2’O-Me at both termini exhibited a 4-fold higher translation efficiency compared to unmodified RNA; conversely, introducing Nm into CDS abolished protein expression. Adding a poly(A) tail containing 20 Am (2’O-methyladenosine) residues to mRNA with 2’F-nucleosides in the CDS significantly stimulated protein expression compared to unmodified mRNA.

Methyltransferase inhibition in viruses often leads to reduced pathogenicity and lower infection rates while boosting the host immune response. However, it remains difficult to determine whether this effect stems from the absence of cap methylation or the lack of Nm within the internal mRNA body. Coronaviruses lacking 2’O-methyltransferase activity [315] induced significantly higher Type I IFN levels without pathogenic effects in wild-type mice; however, their replication and spread were restored in mice deficient in TLR7 and MDA5. This suggests that mRNA 2’O-methylation protects viral RNA from recognition by MDA5 and prevents MDA5-mediated Type I IFN production. Similarly, demethylated HIV-1 RNA has been shown to upregulate Type I IFN in dendritic cells via MDA5 activation [316].

The combined use of Nm with 2’F-, 2’O-methoxy modifications, or phosphorothioate linkages reduced protein expression in cell lysates, yet such modified mRNA was still five times more efficient than unmodified RNA. In HeLa cells, mRNA containing all four modifications provided the highest target protein expression compared to natural RNA, likely due to the increased stability afforded by non-canonical analogues with substitutions at the 2’ position of the ribose. Furthermore, modifying the poly(A) region with Nm analogues stimulated translation more effectively than incorporating Nm into the 5’UTR [267]. These data imply that Nm can be successfully utilized in mRNA therapeutics to enhance stability and translation efficiency, provided the analogue is located within non-coding regions.

The combined use of modified nucleosides in mRNA therapy

The most effective therapeutic approach often involves the combined use of modified nucleosides in mRNA vaccines and reporter proteins. In vivo studies have demonstrated that improved efficacy is predominantly achieved by combining m5C with uridine modifications such as Ψ, m1Ψ, or s2U. For instance, the combination of m5C and Ψ in mRNA was shown to elevate FLuc reporter expression 60-fold in hPSCs and 100-fold in murine models 24 h post-transfection compared to unmodified mRNA. This higher translation efficiency also extended the duration of the luciferase signal by 48 h in vitro and 24 h in vivo compared to unmodified mRNA [260]. However, it is noteworthy that other studies have reported lower translation efficiencies for mRNA modified with Ψ + m5C or m1Ψ + m5C combinations compared to those modified with Ψ or m1Ψ alone. For example, when LNPs containing FLuc-encoding mRNA were administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously to BALB/c mice, the translation efficiency of dual-modified mRNAs (m5C + Ψ and m5C + m1Ψ) was lower than that of mRNAs containing only a single modification type. The most pronounced differences in bioluminescence intensity were observed two days post-intramuscular injection, where FLuc expression from m5C + Ψ-modified mRNA was 10-fold lower than that of mRNA modified with either Ψ or m1Ψ alone [119].

The combination of m5C with other modifications remains a promising strategy for RNA vaccines and therapeutics. For example, m5C + m1Ψ and m5C + Ψ combinations were employed in tropoelastin replacement therapy to restore skin affected by genetic disorders or physical damage [263]. Experimental data indicated that these effects may be sequence-dependent. The development of tropoelastin gene therapy involved sequence optimization, resulting in several variants: mRNA_3 (codon adaptation index (CAI) = 0.8010, GC-content = 72.2%), mRNA_14 (CAI = 0.7200, GC-content = 62.2%), and the native sequence (CAI = 0.6947, GC-content = 64.3%). mRNA_14 modified with m5C and m1Ψ exhibited a 12% reduction in tropoelastin expression and a 14% decrease in cell viability 48 h post-transfection compared to mRNA_14 modified with m1Ψ alone. In contrast, incorporating m5C and m1Ψ into the second-best transcript, mRNA_3, increased tropoelastin expression by 63% and cell viability by 14% compared to mRNA_3 modified with m1Ψ. Conversely, the same dual modification in the native mRNA sequence reduced tropoelastin expression by 40% and cell viability by 10% relative to the optimized mRNA_14.

The m5C + Ψ combination was also utilized in an anti-tumor mRNA vaccine encoding the TRP2 gene for the treatment of B16F10 murine melanoma. A single injection of the modified mRNA induced stable TRP2 expression in the lymph nodes for five days and elicited a more pronounced CTL response (64%) compared to native mRNA (43%). Animals vaccinated with modified TRP2 mRNA demonstrated an antigen-specific T-cell response comparable to mice receiving a TRP2 peptide-based vaccine. Twelve days after the injection of m5C + Ψ-mRNA-LCP, tumor volume was reduced by 80% and 90% compared to canonical mRNA-LCP and non-vaccinated animals, respectively. However, no significant differences in the humoral immune response were observed between groups immunized with modified or unmodified mRNA, with both displaying similarly high antibody titers [264].

A study on malaria vaccination [265] focused on the effects of an mRNA vaccine containing a combination of m5C and Ψ encapsulated in various LNPs and encoding the malaria cell-traversal protein for ookinetes and sporozoites (CelTOS). The administration of m5C + Ψ mRNA-LNP1 yielded antibody titers similar to those elicited by canonical mRNA-LNP1. Two weeks after the third immunization, mice were challenged with Plasmodium falciparum expressing CelTOS. Animals in all groups, including the control, developed malaria, indicating that the mRNA vaccination induced an adaptive immune response rather than full protective immunity.

Another common combination of modifications used in mRNA therapeutics is 25% m5C and 25% s2U. Incorporating this hybrid modification into mRNA encoding mouse erythropoietin (mEpo) proved highly effective in vivo [257]. Fourteen days after intramuscular administration to BALB/c mice, mEpo levels were 4.8- and 4.4-fold higher than those in untreated mice or mice receiving unmodified mEpo mRNA, respectively. By day 28, the hematocrit of mice receiving the dual-modified mEpo mRNA (25% m5C + 25% s2U) rose to 64.2 ± 5.9%, which was significantly higher than that of mice receiving unmodified mRNA (54.2 ± 0.5%), modified mRNA containing 50% s2U (56%), or untreated animals (51.5 ± 1%). Cytokine analysis performed 8 h post-injection showed that the modified mRNA maintained the secretion of IFN-γ, IFN-α, and IL-12 at levels comparable to the untreated control group.

The combination of 25% m5C + 25% s2U was also utilized for gene replacement therapy in mice with congenital surfactant protein B (SP-B) deficiency. Cytokine analysis, performed 8 h after administering the modified mRNA to mice with induced SP-B expression, demonstrated a 2-fold and 6-fold reduction in IFN-γ and IL-12 secretion, respectively, compared to unmodified mRNA. Treatment with modified SP-B mRNA, administered twice weekly for four weeks, relieved respiratory distress and increased the mean lifespan to 26.2 ± 2.7 days, compared to only 3.7 ± 0.3 days in the control group receiving eGFP-Luc mRNA. Mice treated with SP-B mRNA expressed 71.2 ± 8.3% of the SP-B protein relative to wild-type animals. Additionally, SP-B mRNA restored lung expansion and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid to levels observed in healthy controls [257].

Another study focused on the development of a universal influenza vaccine scrutinized the therapeutic potential of mRNA containing either 100% Ψ or a combination of 20% m6A and 100% Ψ. Two weeks after the booster injection, the specific IgG titer was 2.5-fold higher than that of the negative control; by week five, the registered specific IgG titers in mice immunized with different mRNA variants were comparable [251]. The same combination of modified nucleosides (20% m6A + 100% Ψ) also proved highly efficient in mRNA encoding GFP. Fluorescence detection in transfected HEK293 cells demonstrated that this combination yielded 1.3 times more GFP-positive cells than Ψ alone and 300 times more than unmodified mRNA [251].

Employing combinations of modified nucleosides in mRNA-based platforms allows for prolonged target protein expression, stabilized mRNA translation, and significant potential for treating a diverse range of diseases. However, fundamental issues surrounding their comparative efficacy and long-term safety remain incompletely understood and continue to be a subject of active scientific debate.

Synthesis of mRNA with region-specific modifications

In addition to classical methods of incorporating modified nucleoside analogues where standard nucleosides are replaced throughout the entire molecule (complete substitution) or at random sites (partial substitution) — novel approaches for site-specific introduction of analogues have emerged. For instance, current RNA ligation protocols enable the synthesis of mRNA with regional modifications localized specifically within the 5’UTR, CDS, or 3’UTR (Fig. 4). While various chemical ligation methods are available, they are typically limited to short fragments and are often inefficient for long-range RNA ligation or result in the formation of non-canonical (unnatural) linkages within the mRNA [317–319]. DNAzymes (e.g., 9DB1 and SC9) can successfully catalyze the formation of native phosphodiester bonds between short nucleic acid fragments (< 100 nt); however, their application in producing full-length mRNA has not been extensively studied and would likely require the development of highly sequence-specific DNAzymes [320–322]. Nevertheless, adapting DNAzymes for the large-scale production of regionally modified mRNA vaccines could potentially offer a cost-effective and straightforward alternative to current enzymatic methods.

Overview of the existing protocols for ligating RNA fragments for the synthesis of regionally modified mRNA: enzymatic ligation through the use of RNA or DNA ligases and DNAzyme-catalyzed ligation

Enzymatic ligation is also feasible through the use of RNA or DNA ligases [323]. Specifically, the ligation of RNA on a DNA splint using T4 RNA Ligase 2 is the most promising enzymatic method for synthesizing regionally modified mRNA. This approach, already widely utilized in laboratory settings, allows for the efficient assembly of long RNA fragments with high yields and minimal byproduct formation, while exhibiting high tolerance toward various modifications [317, 324, 325]. For example, mRNA variants constructed using this method — carrying m1Ψ or m5C in distinct regions such as the 5’UTR, CDS, or 3’UTR — have already been evaluated. Interestingly, the optimal localization of different analogues varied across mRNA regions to maximize protein translatability. This positional effect may be attributed to the influence of specific modifications on the translational machinery, the stability of secondary structures, and interactions with translation-regulatory proteins [255].

In the future, it may also become possible to introduce site-specific modifications into targeted mRNA regions using «writer» proteins. This approach could enhance molecular stability without negatively impacting translation efficiency. Furthermore, with the advancement of bioinformatics tools, it will be feasible to predict the impact of minor nucleosides on mRNA secondary structure. Such predictive capabilities will help avoid the formation of inhibitory structures, such as hairpins that terminate translation, and assist in the in silico design of optimal primary mRNA sequences to maximize protein production. Currently, numerous algorithms are available to optimize mRNA secondary structure for enhanced stability and translatability. These tools account for the secondary structures of UTRs while maintaining a critical balance between codon composition and overall molecular stability.

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

The efficacy of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic unequivocally demonstrated the potential of in vitro synthesized mRNA for drug development, facilitating the transition of mRNA therapeutics from basic research to clinical applications. Recent preclinical and clinical studies indicate that mRNA-based products represent an effective and safe alternative for the prevention and treatment of infectious, genetic, and oncological diseases, as well as for applications in regenerative medicine [326–331]. Furthermore, the development and production of mRNA therapeutics are markedly more rapid and straightforward than those of recombinant proteins or viral-based products, providing this platform with a competitive advantage over other therapeutic modalities [332].

Nucleoside modifications address the primary challenges associated with in vitro synthesized mRNA. These modifications enhance transcript stability [108], diminish reactogenicity by obstructing mRNA recognition by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [4, 333, 334], and increase the expression of target proteins [108, 256]. However, comprehensive research on the specific effects of various nucleoside analogs remains limited. Variations in experimental methodologies — including the presence or absence of RNA purification steps, specific RNA sequence attributes, and the characteristics of tested cell lines — hinder the precise determination of the role of each analog. This variability makes it difficult to establish definitive causal relationships between specific modifications and observed translational outcomes or immunogenicity.

The utilization of nucleoside analogs may also influence the concentration of IVT byproducts [269] and the ensuing reactogenicity of the drug, particularly if the target mRNA remains contaminated with these impurities. Furthermore, the effects of analogs may be contingent upon the nucleoside composition of a specific mRNA, the presence of binding sites for particular RBPs, and the expression levels of these proteins within specific cell types. All of these variables can significantly influence the overall efficacy of modified mRNA therapeutics (Fig. 5).

Consequently, thorough and integrated studies are required to systematically evaluate the effects of nucleoside analogs. This effort can be significantly bolstered by the advancement of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven tools. For instance, the LinearDesign algorithm was developed to optimize mRNA sequences by enhancing codon usage and structural stability [335]. Currently, such tools are predominantly applicable to canonical mRNA nucleosides; however, expanding these AI models to include modified analogs represents a critical frontier in the in silico design of next-generation mRNA therapeutics.

The various nucleoside analogues reviewed herein demonstrate significant potential for clinical application, as primary safety concerns regarding these compounds have been largely addressed. These analogues are either already in widespread clinical use (e.g., N1-methylpseudouridine) or occur naturally in cellular RNAs. Nevertheless, many analogues including m6A, hm5C, and Nm - remain understudied, with limited examples of their application in mRNA-based therapeutics in animal models. Currently, the use of m6A is of significant interest for circRNA-based therapeutics, while hm5C shows promise for saRNA-based platforms. Among all recognized modifications, 5-methylcytidine and uridine analogues, such as pseudouridine, N1-methylpseudouridine, and 5-methoxyuridine, exhibit the highest in vivo potential. Notably, N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ) remains the only analogue utilized in approved products, such as the COVID-19 vaccines Comirnaty® and Spikevax™ [28]. This analogue is also being evaluated in a range of investigational mRNA vaccines targeting Chikungunya, rabies, and influenza A viruses, several of which are being developed by companies such as Moderna [336]. Beyond infectious diseases, m1Ψ is employed in mRNA therapies for cardiovascular, oncological, and hereditary diseases [337–340], currently at various stages of clinical trials [341–343].

Other nucleoside analogues are also entering clinical evaluation; for instance, the saRNA booster vaccine VLPCOV-02 against SARS-CoV-2 incorporates 5% m5C [266]. Furthermore, a substantial number of therapeutics featuring less common modifications are in the pre-clinical stage. For example, a 5moU-containing mRNA therapeutic for Fabry disease has reached preclinical trials in non-human primates [295]. Additionally, studies on the combined use of 25% m5C and 25% s2U for treating lung diseases, such as cystic fibrosis and congenital surfactant protein B deficiency have demonstrated high efficacy and reduced immunogenicity in murine models [257, 344].

The optimizations required for mRNA vaccine development differ fundamentally from those used in gene replacement therapy. mRNA vaccines necessitate only minimal quantities of the target antigen, as the immune system can substantially amplify the signal via cellular and antibody-mediated immunity [345]. Moreover, moderate mRNA reactogenicity often induced by residual dsRNA — can serve as an intrinsic adjuvant, thereby augmenting immunogenicity [346]. Conversely, gene replacement formulations demand a high degree of target protein biosynthesis and minimal reactogenicity to ensure safety and therapeutic longevity [345, 346].

In conclusion, the field of RNA therapeutics stands at the precipice of a new era in biomedicine. Meticulous investigation into the biochemical properties of both canonical and minor nucleosides will remain the cornerstone of future innovation (Fig. 6). As our understanding deepens and AI-driven sequence design tools mature, we anticipate a paradigm shift toward highly personalized and rapidly deployable treatments. This evolution will not only expand the therapeutic arsenal against infectious, oncological, and genetic diseases but will fundamentally redefine the speed and precision with which we address human pathology.
