TAF15::ZNF384 Mixed Phenotype Acute Leukemia With Complex Karyotype: Case Report With B‐ALL Induction, Blinatumomab Bridging to Allogeneic Transplant and Literature Review
Authors: Sarah Blain, Brianna Empringham, Talal Mahdi, Benjamin D. Hedley, Jean McGowan‐Jordan, Ruth Padmore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCD10, CD19 and MPO in one flow cytometry tube facilitated distinguishing B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia with dim expression of MPO versus B/myeloid mixed phenotype acute leukemia. B‐ALL chemotherapy followed by blinatumomab bridging to allogeneic stem cell transplantation was successful in our patient.

Introduction

ZNF384 (zinc finger) gene fusions occur with a variety of fusion partners, which may form a distinct group of acute leukemias and can present as either precursor B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) or B/myeloid mixed phenotype acute leukemia (B/myeloid MPAL) [1, 2, 3]. B‐ALL and MPAL with ZNF384 gene fusion share a similar transcriptional profile, suggesting they form a biological continuum [4]. This case report and review of the literature of TAF15::ZNF384 acute leukemias highlight important aspects in the diagnosis and management of this category of acute leukemia.

Case History/Examination

A 15‐year‐old female presented with fever, cough, and abdominal pain. Laboratory investigations showed marked hyperleukocytosis with a WBC of 320 × 109/L with 91% blasts (Figure 1A), anemia (hemoglobin of 60 g/L), and thrombocytopenia (platelets of 35 × 109/L).

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Bone marrow aspirate showed an extensive blast infiltrate that replaced normal trilineage hematopoiesis. The cerebrospinal fluid was negative for blasts. The blasts were negative for Sudan Black staining of peripheral blood and marrow. Bone marrow cytogenetics (Figure 2, 46,XX,add(12)(p13),‐17,add(20)(q11.2‐q13.1),+mar [3]/46,idem,der(16)t(1;16)(q21;q12‐13) [1]/46,XX [5]) revealed a complex karyotype with loss of one normal chromosome 17, an additional marker chromosome of undetermined origin, and uncharacterized abnormalities of chromosome 12 short arm and chromosome 20 long arm in the main abnormal clone; this clone evolved to include the original abnormalities along with an unbalanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 16, resulting in monosomy for part of the chromosome 16 long arm and trisomy for part of the chromosome 1 long arm.

Cytogenetic karyotyping of bone marrow aspirate. Green arrow add(12)(p13). Red arrow der(16)t(1;16)(q21;q12‐13). Fine black arrows ‐17 and abnormal 20q.

Flow cytometry immunophenotyping was performed and interpreted using the 2006 Bethesda International Consensus Recommendations for immunophenotypic analysis [5], and 88% blasts were identified in the bone marrow, positive for CD34 and for B‐cell markers CD19 (99% positive), cytoplasmic CD22, cytoplasmic CD79a, and HLA‐DR, and negative for CD20 (Figure 1B–F). The blasts were negative for T‐cell markers surface CD3, cytoplasmic CD3, CD5, CD7, and CD56. The blasts showed dim TdT positivity. A subset (20% of blasts) showed CD10 positivity. The blasts were dim positive with myeloid markers CD13 and CD33, and cytoplasmic myeloperoxidase (MPO) showed partial positivity (28%), with isotype control and normal lymphocytes available for MPO positive threshold determination. The remainder of the myeloid markers were negative, including CD123, CD4, CD11c, CD14, CD15, CD64, CD65, and CD117 (see Table S1 for full flow cytometry diagnostic tube work‐up). Initial diagnostic impression was that of B‐ALL with MPO expression [6]. Another flow cytometry panel was performed, with CD19, CD10, and MPO in the same tube. It revealed a distinct CD19+CD10‐MPO+ blast population, representing 28% of total leukocytes (Figure 1G–I). The CD10 vs. MPO dot plot showed that the blast cells were heterogeneously separated into three further subpopulations: part 1 was dim MPO but negative for CD10, part 2 was negative for both MPO and CD10, and part 3 was CD10 positive but negative for MPO. This specific flow pattern [7] favored the diagnosis of B/myeloid MPAL. This immunophenotype, in addition to the dim expression of CD10 and aberrant expression of myeloid markers CD13 and CD33, was suggestive of a leukemia harboring a ZNF384 gene fusion. The marrow sample was sent for ZNF384 gene fusion testing, which revealed TAF15::ZNF384 fusion by RNA sequencing. The uncharacterized abnormality of chromosome 12p was consistent with a complex rearrangement between chromosome 12p and chromosome 17 (rather than a simple t(12;17)), resulting in TAF15::ZNF384 fusion. Additional molecular testing identified a frameshift truncating mutation in exon 11 of KMT2D. The patient's diagnosis was updated to B/myeloid MPAL with ZNF384 gene fusion.

The patient was treated with four drug induction (dexamethasone, peg‐asparaginase, vincristine, and daunorubicin). Bone marrow aspirate at the end of induction showed residual disease with 6% leukemic blasts by flow cytometry minimal/measurable residual disease (MRD) based detection technique. The patient continued consolidation as per the very high‐risk arm of AALL1131 (cytarabine, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, 6‐mercaptopurine). Marrow examination at mid‐consolidation showed ongoing refractory disease with 5% leukemic blasts. There were significant considerations around next steps including acute myeloid leukemia (AML) directed chemotherapy (high‐dose cytarabine based regimens), further ALL directed therapy (high‐dose methotrexate), and immunotherapy. We proceeded with single therapy blinatumomab with close monitoring for phenotype switch, which has been reported to occur with higher frequency in ZNF384 gene fusion leukemias [1]. Bone marrow aspirate on day 14 of blinatumomab was negative by flow MRD (with lower detection limit of < 0.01%). The patient underwent allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

She is currently in remission at 3.5 years follow‐up. The patient tolerated treatment very well with no unexpected complications. She does have long‐term complications secondary to transplant: she is on hormone replacement for primary ovarian failure and has an asymptomatic decrease in DLCO on surveillance pulmonary function tests.

Of the six patients with individual details who relapsed, 4 out of 6 (67%) died of their leukemia. None of the four deceased patients received an allogeneic transplant. One of the two patients surviving the relapse received an allogeneic transplant; the other was treated with chemotherapy only.

Of the 22 patients alive and without relapse, (19 with B‐ALL and 3 with B/myeloid MPAL), 5 out of 22 (23%) received allogeneic transplant, including 4 with B‐ALL and 1 with B/myeloid MPAL. All 6 (100%) of the patients (5 without relapse, 1 with relapse) who received allogeneic transplant were in remission after the transplant.

A total of seven case reports of B/myeloid MPAL with TAF15::ZNF384 have been reported, including the current case report. The older (74 years old) patient died of sepsis during induction chemotherapy. One patient was diagnosed as AML, but we reclassified him as B/myeloid MPAL since he fulfilled the WHO criteria for this diagnosis (see Table S2). He relapsed and died. Another patient relapsed but regained remission after allogeneic transplant. One patient is in remission after ALL initial therapy. Our patient is in remission after ALL‐directed chemotherapy, followed by blinatumomab and allogeneic transplant. One patient was reported without clinical follow‐up.

The mean age at diagnosis for acute leukemias with TAF15::ZNF384 was 20 years (with a wide range of 1 ½–74). All but 5 of the cases were younger than 35 years of age; see age distribution in Figure S1. The WBC was reported in 35/70 cases in our literature review, with the mean WBC 52.8 × 109/L (range 2.6–320 × 109/L). WBC > 100 × 109/L at presentation was observed in 6/34 cases, most commonly observed in either B/myeloid MPAL (2/6, 33%) or in B‐ALL cases with age ≤ 5 years (2/6, 33%). Blast count at diagnosis was high, averaging 81.6%, range 50%–95%. In the 16 cases where it was reported, CNS was usually negative at diagnosis (14/16, 87.5%) or with CNS 2 (2/16, 12.5%). Flow cytometry immunophenotyping generally reported bright CD19, with variable typically dim CD10 and with aberrant expression of CD13 and/or CD33.

In the six cases of B/myeloid MPAL with cytogenetic information reported, all six had additional cytogenetic abnormalities in addition to abnormalities involving chromosomes 12 and 17.

In B‐ALL cases with reported karyotypes, cytogenetic abnormalities in addition to t(12;17) were noted at diagnosis in 1/5 (20%) cases that relapsed and 5/16 (31%) cases which did not relapse. Additional cytogenetic abnormalities were noted in 2/5 (40%) of B‐ALL at the time of relapse. In cases of TAF15::ZNF384 B‐ALL reported as part of a case series, 7/13 (54%) demonstrated t(12;17) by classic cytogenetic karyotyping, whereas 6/13 (46%) did not, with TAF15::ZNF384 in these cases identified only by retrospective molecular analysis.

Molecular Origin of TAF15::ZNF483

TAF15::ZNF384 is a rare gene fusion in acute leukemia, characterized as involving ZNF384 (CIZ, Cas‐interacting zinc finger) and TAF15 (TATA box binding protein associated factor) genes in 2002 [22]. The TAF15 gene is part of the human FET gene family ( FUS, EWSR1, TAF15) and encodes an RNA binding protein. Genes of the FET family, including TAF15, have oncogenic properties when the FET protein fuses with a DNA binding domain of a transcription factor protein. The ZNF384 gene encodes a C2H2 (Cys2‐His2) zinc finger protein that is responsible for transcription factors involved in bone metabolism and spermatogenesis. More than 10 fusion partners have been described in the literature involving ZNF384, most frequently EP300, followed by TCF3, TAF15, and CREBBP [23]. ZNF384 fusions have been identified in approximately 5%–10% of B‐ALL [1] and more commonly in B/myeloid MPAL. The identification of the ZNF384 gene fusion might be denominator and method dependent, identified in 14% of all cases of pediatric B/myeloid MPAL by RT‐PCR for three fusion partners [16] and in up to 48% of pediatric B/myeloid MPAL by multiple molecular methods, where KMT2Ar, BCR‐ABLr, and MPAL NOS had been excluded [4].

Flow Cytometry Immunophenotypic Diagnostic Challenges of B/Myeloid MPALs With a Focus on MPO Expression

No clinically validated, prospective standardized approach has been established for the diagnostics of MPALs. Kovach et al. [7] have published a tentative standardized semiquantitative universally applicable flow cytometric criteria for immunophenotypic lineage assignment and isolated MPO approach in the diagnosis of MPAL; however, this was a retrospective study based on central review by expert consensus of two hematopathologists. On the other hand, the EuroFlow consortium has developed the 8‐color Acute Leukemia Orientation Tube, which includes CD45 in PO, CD34 in PerCPCy5.5, CD19 in PE‐Cy7, CD7 in APC, CD3 in APC‐H7, cyCD3 in PB, MPO in FITC and cyCD79a in PE standardized through specific sample processing, instrument set‐up and compensation settings [24]. However, many flow cytometry laboratories use broader or differing screening panels for new leukemias. Our case report highlights the challenges of interpreting MPO positivity. There is no current consensus for the percentage cutoff for MPO positivity for the diagnosis of MPAL; the WHO suggests that MPO intensity on blasts should in part exceed 50% of the mature neutrophil level [2]. The unit by which we quantify the intensity of a CD marker for a given cell population by flow cytometry is traditionally the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Although the MFI is enumerated as a linear value, flow cytometry data is visualized in logarithmic space distribution plotted on a combined logarithmic‐linear scale on the 2 × 2 dot plots (see Figure 1 for illustrative example). For analytic purposes, data is best visualized in a logarithmic space to allow optimal data distribution and visualization. In general, we assume flow cytometry specialists apply the WHO criteria by making sure that the linear MFI value of the aberrant blast population is at least half of the linear MFI value of the mature neutrophils. However, a significant limitation of the WHO diagnostic criteria for MPAL is that it does not specify which unit to use to assess the intensity of expression of the antigens on the cells (MFI, or other) nor if this criteria should be applied to the linear MFI value of cell populations, or simply visually on logarithmic data representation of the cell populations.

The EuroFlow consortium has published a standardized immunophenotypic analysis of myeloperoxidase (MPO) in acute leukemia, with MPO positivity defined as ≥ 20% positivity on the leukemic cells, using benign lymphocytes to set the negative internal control [25]. Our laboratory does not use the EuroFlow MPO protocols. The International Consensus Classification recommends that care be taken where less than 10% MPO positivity is noted by flow cytometry [3]. In our patient's case, MPO positivity by flow immunophenotyping was shown to be approximately 28% of the leukemic blast cells. CD10 negativity was determined as an MFI expression of less than 101 on the CD10 logarithmic scale (CD10/MPO dot plot). We defined the MPO negativity of the CD10+ blasts subset based on their comparison to the CD10‐MPO+ counterpart. We determined that any blast cells that express an MPO level stronger than the CD10+MPO‐ blasts were positive for MPO (see Figure 1G–I), where all blasts (orange population) shown are CD19+.

There remain many MPO‐positive B‐ALL cases which are not MPALs, characterized by dim uniform MPO positivity by flow cytometry as the only expression of myeloid differentiation in otherwise typical B‐ALL cases with a single uniform blast population; this may constitute nonspecific MPO positivity and not warrant MPAL designation [6]. Our case emphasized the importance of showing two distinct CD19+ populations, one positive with CD10 and the other positive with MPO, enabling interpretation as B/myeloid MPAL rather than B‐ALL with isolated expression of MPO.

Cytogenetics and FISH in the Diagnosis of Acute Leukemia With ZNF384 Gene Fusion

ZNF384 rearranged leukemia may be difficult to recognize by classical cytogenetic karyotyping as both ZNF384 and many of its translocation partners are telomeric in location [1]. Optical genome mapping shows promise for routine genomic profiling of acute leukemias, with a recent study demonstrating the ability to detect TCF3::ZNF384 gene fusion [26]. Our case report of B/myeloid MPAL showed a complex karyotype might raise the possibility of acute myeloid leukemia–myelodysplasia related [27], but the identification of ZNF384 gene fusion superseded this diagnostic classification. For immediate treatment purposes, our case was compatible with B/myeloid MPAL with immunophenotypic lymphoid‐lineage predominance [28]. This is supported by the favorable response of our patient to B‐lineage directed therapy, and also by the unfavorable outcome of the one case report of TAF15::ZNF384 gene fusion acute leukemia interpreted as acute myeloid leukemia treated with conventional chemotherapy, with death from the acute leukemia occurring eight months after diagnosis [11].

An approach utilizing the ZNF384 characteristic flow immunophenotype has been applied as a screening tool for subsequent FISH identification of ZNF384 fusion in B‐ALL [29]. Using a combination of FISH and molecular methods, up to one third of B‐ALL‐other (i.e., negative for Down syndrome, ETV6::RUNX1, BCR::ABL, TCF3::HLF, TCF3::PBX1, high hyperdiploidy, near haploidy, low hypodiploidy, KMT2A rearrangements, and iAMP21‐ALL) can be categorized into genetic subgroups, which include those with ZNF384 gene fusions [23].

Summary of the Outcomes of ZNF384 Acute Leukemias From Literature Review

Our literature review confirms that most acute leukemias with TAF15::ZNF384 gene fusion occur in children and adolescents and young adults (AYA) [30], (see Figure S1). B‐ALL with TAF15::ZNF384 gene fusion has been characterized as having higher WBC and younger age at presentation than other ZNF384 gene fusions [21], and our literature review confirms this. Additional cytogenetic abnormalities were detected at diagnosis in all B/myeloid MPAL with TAF15::ZNF384 gene fusion, where karyotype was reported (6 of 6 cases) (Table S2).

Clinical outcomes for acute leukemia with ZNF384 gene fusion are variable, from good to poor. In B‐ALL with a variety of different ZNF384 gene fusions identified at diagnosis, the outcome may be influenced by the ZNF384 fusion partner. The partner EP300 shows superior event‐free survival compared with other fusion partners, including TAF15 [30]. Relapse occurred very early (less than 1 year) in both cases of relapsed B/myeloid MPAL with TAF15::ZNF384 [10, 11]. As summarized in Table 1, B‐ALL with TAF15::ZNF384 gene fusion showed earlier relapse in one case at 2.2 years from diagnosis [13] and later in two cases at 4.5 years [12] and 5.7 years [15]. B‐ALL with ZNF384 gene fusion may be prone to late relapse; for example, the retrospective study by Qin et al. [31] with only two patients out of 47 relapsing following allogeneic stem cell transplant, and they did so after 5 years of follow‐up, 5.6 and 5.8 years after allogeneic stem cell transplant. Qin et al. [31] also showed better prognosis of B‐ALL with ZNF384 gene fusion with allogeneic stem cell transplant. Shi et al. [32] showed significantly improved survival of adult patients with ZNF384 gene fusion B‐ALL treated with ASCT, who had less than 2.5‐log reduction of ZNF384 fusion transcript levels after consolidation but before ASCT.

B‐Cell Targeted Therapy in the Treatment of ZNF384 Acute Leukemias

Blinatumomab has been described as effective therapy as a bridge to allogeneic transplant in pediatric B/myeloid MPAL [33]. The patient in our case report was given blinatumomab for persistent disease as a bridge to transplant. CAR T‐cell therapy has also been used successfully as bridging therapy to allogeneic transplant in two patients with EP300::ZNF384 positive B‐ALL [34]. Importantly, acute leukemias with ZNF384 gene fusions have the risk for lineage switch. ZNF384 acute leukemias at relapse exhibit a propensity for lineage switching from B‐cell to myeloid [13], and may show monocytic switch during induction chemotherapy [35]. This illustrates the plasticity of these leukemias. It is believed that the risk of myeloid switch is a consequence of selection pressure from B‐cell directed chemotherapy, allowing emergence, or clonal evolution of a myeloid clone that was below the detection limit of our current diagnostics tools at diagnosis. Emergence of acute myeloid leukemic blast cells after CD19 directed therapy, such as blinatumomab [36] or CAR T‐cells [37] have been described in acute leukemia with ZNF384 gene fusion.

Future Therapeutic Avenues for ZNF384 Acute Leukemias

B‐ALL with ZNF384 gene fusion has been associated with increased expression of FLT3 protein, with one study showing the highest FLT3 expression in B‐ALL with ZNF384 where TAF15 was the fusion partner [21]. Interestingly, the FLT3 protein expression is increased in B‐ALL with ZNF384 despite the fact that FLT3 gene mutations are detected in only a minority of acute leukemias with ZNF384 gene fusion (14%) [30]. The overexpression of FLT3 protein is observed across all acute leukemias with ZNF384 gene fusion, and may direct future therapeutic options with specific FLT3 inhibitors such as gilteritinib [38].

Conclusion

Acute leukemias with TAF15::ZNF384 gene fusion are mostly present in children and AYA. Those manifesting as B/myeloid MPAL may show early relapse, and those manifesting as B‐ALL may show late relapse and may be prone to lineage switch. Our case report demonstrated that flow immunophenotyping using CD19/CD10/MPO in the same tube may assist in showing distinct blast populations, enabling categorization as B/myeloid MPAL. Dim CD10 expression, along with CD13 and/or CD33 positivity, should prompt testing for ZNF384 gene fusion. In our patient, bridging to allogeneic stem cell transplant with blinatumomab has resulted in continued complete remission, currently at 3.5‐year follow‐up. We also advocate for the creation of an international MPAL registry database, including integral diagnostics information, clinical treatment, and long‐term follow‐up. Such information would provide a firm starting point for much‐needed prospective clinical trials with attention to the defined genomic subset of MPAL, such as those with ZNF384 gene fusion [27].
