Angiogenic behavior of human umbilical vein endothelial cells in hydrogel containing lyophilized powder of decellularized adipose tissue from high-fat diet-fed or exercise-trained rats
Authors: Seita Osawa, Yoshifumi Tsuchiya, Tetsuya Izawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity often leads to impaired angiogenesis through extracellular matrix (ECM) reorganization, whereas exercise training has beneficial effects on ECM remodeling and capillary density in adipose tissues. However, the relationship between angiogenesis and ECM changes due to high-fat diet (HFD) intake and exercise training remains unclear.PurposeTo evaluate in vitro tube-like formation of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) using a three-dimensional hydrogel containing decellularized adipose tissue (DAT) powder derived from the adipose tissues of HFD-fed or exercise-trained (EX) rats.MethodsDAT was prepared from the inguinal and epididymal adipose tissues of rats in four intervention groups: normal diet (ND)-fed sedentary, HFD-fed sedentary, ND-fed EX, or HFD-fed EX groups. DAT was incorporated into a three-dimensional hydrogel, and proteomic analysis of DAT was performed. Tube-like network formation was evaluated in HUVECs cultured in DAT hydrogel.ResultsSeveral angiogenic marker proteins were identified using immunoblotting. Inguinal DAT hydrogel from the HFD-EX group exhibited greater development of network connectivity, branching, and length of HUVECs compared to the other groups. Similar phenomena were observed in epididymal DAT hydrogels from the HFD groups compared to those from the ND groups. HUVEC tube-like network formation was greater in the epididymal than in the inguinal hydrogels, with enhanced protein expression of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2, focal adhesion kinase, and vascular endothelial cadherin. Exploratory proteomic analysis implied fat depot- and intervention-dependent differences in DAT composition.ConclusionHUVEC angiogenic behavior may be sensitive to HFD- and exercise training-associated changes in DAT composition in a fat depot-dependent manner.Keywords: Decellularized adipose tissue, HUVECs, Angiogenesis, Extracellular matrix, Inguinal adipose tissue, Epididymal adipose tissue

Introduction

Adipose tissue expansion largely depends on adipocyte hypertrophy, hyperplasia, or both. In the former process, mature resident cells expand to increase energy storage capacity, resulting in augmented adipose tissue mass. The latter process involves the multiplication of small adipocytes differentiated from precursor cells, resulting in increased adipose tissue mass. Hypertrophied adipocytes have been highly correlated with pathological adipocyte phenotypes and adverse metabolic outcomes associated with obesity [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In contrast, adipose tissue, which is composed of a large number of relatively small adipocytes, is resistant to obesity-related metabolic effects [1,3,5]. Therefore, healthy adipose tissue expansion in which metabolic homeostasis is maintained requires appropriate regulation of adipocyte size and number. Capillary angiogenesis is also required to ensure adipocyte function and adipogenesis [[6], [7], [8]]. Thus, adequate initiation and maintenance of capillary angiogenesis are important orchestrated responses for healthy adipose tissue expansion without disturbing metabolic homeostasis.

Early vascular changes at the onset of overnutrition exacerbate adipose tissue dysfunction [9]. The rapid expansion of hypoxic adipose tissue during obesity, coupled with inflammation and fibrosis, promotes endothelial dysfunction and reduces vascular density [10,11]. A recent study showed that obesity induces micro- and macrovascular remodeling in visceral white adipose tissue through vascular morphogenesis and extracellular matrix (ECM) reorganization, whereas subcutaneous white adipose tissue shows increased expression of factors involved in angiogenesis, collagen biosynthesis, ECM organization, and integrin interactions [11]. Thus, ECM reorganization is involved in the mechanisms underlying micro- and macrovascular remodeling in obesity. Considering angiogenesis involves endothelial cell proliferation and migration into the ECM [12,13], it is important to clarify the relationship between obesity-induced ECM changes in a fat depot-specific manner and endothelial angiogenic capacity.

In the context of obesity-induced ECM changes, previous studies have shown that in hypertrophied adipose tissue, the expression of genes encoding ECM proteins, such as collagen and matrix metalloproteinases, significantly increases, leading to collagen accumulation and tissue fibrosis [[14], [15], [16]]. In contrast, exercise training, which effectively increases adipose tissue angiogenesis and capillary density [8,17,18], has beneficial effects on ECM remodeling in visceral adipose tissue by suppressing high-fat diet (HFD)-induced collagen deposition and fibroblast activation [[19], [20], [21]]. However, endothelial cell behavior in an ECM microenvironment altered by obesity and exercise training remains unclear.

To address this knowledge gap, we evaluated the in vitro tube-formation ability of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in a three-dimensional hydrogel containing the lyophilized powder of decellularized adipose tissue (DAT) prepared from the inguinal and epididymal adipose tissue of HFD-fed or exercise-trained (EX) rats. DAT hydrogels have been shown to be repurposed as scaffolds that possess ECM properties [22]. Moreover, considering the possible mechanisms underlying the differences in vascular development between subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissues may include differences in the relative abundance of the five functionally distinct endothelial cell clusters within the adipose tissue [23], as well as significant cellular heterogeneity [24,25], this study investigated the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs to exclude the influence of these inherent characteristics in native endothelial cells. Thus, utilizing HUVECs is helpful in clarifying the effects of ECM composition changes on angiogenesis within adipose tissue.

Materials and methods

Animal care and exercise training program

Four-week-old male Wistar rats (total n = 20; SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) were housed in an enclosure maintained at 23°C with a 12 h light/dark cycle. All rats were given 7 days to acclimatize to their new environment. At age 5 weeks, the rats were provided with a normal diet (ND) containing 9% energy from fat (MF, Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) or a HFD containing 60% energy from saturated fat (D12492, Research Diets, Inc., New Brunswick, NJ, USA). The rats were randomly assigned to ND-fed sedentary control (ND-SED, n = 5), HFD-fed sedentary control (HFD-SED, n = 5), ND-fed exercise-trained (ND-EX, n = 5), or HFD-fed exercise-trained (HFD-EX, n = 5) groups for 9 weeks. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Rats in the EX group were subjected to running on a treadmill at a 5° incline 5 days per week for 9 weeks [17,26]. At the end of the study, the EX rats were sacrificed at least 36 h after the last exercise session. Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (15 mg/100 g body weight; Kyoritsu Seiyaku, Tokyo, Japan). Blood samples were collected at the time of sacrifice, and plasma triglyceride concentrations were measured using a triglyceride assay kit (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Inguinal and epididymal adipose tissues were rapidly removed and processed for analysis. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Doshisha University (approval codes: A23055 and A25054) and performed in accordance with the “Guiding Principles for the Care and Use of Animals in the Field of Physiological Sciences” of the Physiological Society of Japan.

Preparation of DAT and DAT hydrogel

DAT was prepared from fat pads in each group (n = 5 in each group) according to a previously published protocol [27,28]. Adipose tissues were frozen/thawed three times for 1 h each, followed by washing with deionized water for 48 h, 0.5 M and 1 M sodium chloride solution for 4 h each, deionized water overnight, 0.25% trypsin for 2 h, isopropanol for 48 h, deionized water overnight, 1% Triton X-100 for 48 h, and deionized water for 48 h (Fig. 1A). Thereafter, DAT was lyophilized for 48 h and cryo-milled into a powder. The DAT hydrogel was prepared as follows: first, pre-hydrogel was prepared by digesting 10 mg of the DAT powder from each individual rat-derived DAT sample in 1 mL of pepsin solution (10 mg pepsin dissolved in 1 mL 0.05 N HCl) for 48 h at 4°C. After digestion, the pre-hydrogel solution was neutralized on ice by adding 0.1 N NaOH and 10× PBS until the pH reached 7.4. For gelation, the neutralized DAT pre-hydrogel was mixed with the TrueGel3D crosslinking system—composed of slow-gelling thiolated dextran (polymer component) and cell-degradable crosslinker (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, St. Louis, MO)—according to the manufacturer's instructions and allowed to polymerize at 37°C for 25 min. DAT hydrogels were prepared from inguinal and epididymal adipose tissues from each intervention group (n = 5 in each group): ND-SED, ND-EX, HFD-SED, and HFD-EX DAT. The DAT hydrogels were prepared uniformly in terms of the quantity of DAT powder, pepsin digestion conditions, pH neutralization, crosslinking system, polymerization temperature, and polymerization time.

HUVEC culture

HUVECs were obtained from Lonza (Basel, Switzerland). The cells were cultured in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2) BulletKit® (Lonza) according to the manufacturer's instructions and maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells at passages 3 and 4 were used in this study.

HUVEC 3D angiogenesis assay and cell viability assessment

HUVECs were suspended in EGM-2 medium and added to the DAT hydrogel at a density of 45,000 cells per gel (Fig. 1B). The cell-hydrogel mixture was supplemented with 0.5 mM TrueGel3D RGD integrin adhesion peptide (TRUERGD; Sigma-Aldrich, Merck), dispensed into 96-well plates and polymerized at 37°C for 25 min, after which EGM-2 medium was added to overlay the gels. Constructs were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and the medium was replaced after 1 h. Thereafter, the medium was changed to EGM-2 BulletKit® supplemented with 10 ng/mL human VEGF165 recombinant (Qkine, Cambridge, UK) to initiate angiogenic stimulation. Tube-like network formation was assessed 2 days after the initiation of angiogenic stimulation by staining with 3 μM Calcein AM (AAT Bioquest, Pleasanton, CA) at 37°C for 60 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800, Keyence, Tokyo, Japan) and angiogenic parameters related to tube formation were quantitatively analyzed using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin in ImageJ (version 1.54p, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and BZ-X800 Analyzer software (Keyence). Angiogenic parameters of the analyzed tube-forming elements included the number of junctions, segments, meshes, etc. to quantitatively evaluate the vessel-like network organization. At the same time, cell viability within the hydrogel was evaluated by propidium iodide (PI; AAT Bioquest) staining (1.5 μM) for 15 min according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For tube-like network formation analysis, 16 image fields were acquired from each rat-derived DAT hydrogel, and the values from these fields were averaged to generate one value per biological replicate. Image acquisition and quantification were not performed in a blinded manner; however, the same imaging conditions and analysis parameters were applied across all groups.

Immunoblotting analysis

For immunoblotting analysis, HUVECs were cultured in four DAT hydrogels per individual rat-derived DAT sample, and the cells recovered from these four gels were pooled to obtain sufficient protein for one biological replicate. Cells embedded in the hydrogel were then released using TrueGel3D Enzymatic Cell Recovery Solution according to the manufacturer's instructions to dissociate the hydrogel matrix and collect the cells. The recovered cells were washed and lysed in ice-cold EzRIPA lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 1% NP-40, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], 0.5% deoxycholic acid, and 150 mM sodium chloride) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan). The lysates were centrifuged twice for 20 min at 14,000 × g and 4°C. Immunoblotting of the clear supernatant was performed according to a standard protocol developed in our laboratory [26,29]. The same amounts of protein were separated by 6.0–13.5% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (ATTO), and blocked for 5 min with Bullet Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). To evaluate angiogenesis-, adhesion-, and mechanotransduction-related protein expression in HUVECs cultured in inguinal- and epididymal-derived DAT hydrogels, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against B-cell/CLL lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin), cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31), alpha smooth muscle actin (αSMA), integrin α6, integrin β1, Talin-1, Vinculin, phosphorylated and total focal adhesion kinase (p-FAK and t-FAK, respectively), phosphorylated and total proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase Src (p-Src and t-Src, respectively), phosphorylated and total extracellular signal-regulated kinase (p-ERK and t-ERK, respectively), phosphorylated and total AKT (p-AKT and t-AKT, respectively), phosphorylated mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 and 2 (p-mTORC1 and p-mTORC2, respectively), total mammalian target of rapamycin (t-mTOR), active and total β-catenin, and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), as listed in Supplementary Table S1, at 4°C overnight. Membranes were then labeled for 60 min with 1:2500 dilutions of anti-rabbit, anti-mouse, and anti-goat immunoglobulin G (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK). Bands were visualized using an ECL Prime system (GE Healthcare) and quantified using a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). The expression levels of total, phosphorylated, and active target proteins were normalized to GAPDH. To evaluate phosphorylation or activation status, phosphorylated and active protein levels were further normalized to their corresponding total protein levels and expressed as phosphorylated-to-total or active-to-total protein ratios, respectively.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) protein analysis in lyophilized DAT powder

Pooled samples of lyophilized DAT powder from three randomly selected individual rats in each intervention group were prepared to ensure equal volumes in each experimental group. The pooled sample compensated for lower-volume samples and mitigated the effects of biological sample variation; however, the inability to perform statistical tests comparing the abundance of individual proteins is a limitation. The samples were reconstituted in 2 mL of lysis buffer (7 M urea, 0.1% NP-40, 500 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer) and disrupted using a pestle. After 10 min of ultrasonic bath treatment, the tissue was centrifuged and the supernatant was collected as the extracted sample. A portion of the extracted sample was subjected to trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation and the resulting precipitate was dissolved in 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate solution containing 0.1% SDS. A portion of the TCA precipitated sample was subjected to 5–20% SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions at a constant voltage of 300 V. Subsequently, One-Step Ruby staining (APRO, Tokushima, Japan) was performed. The reconstituted sample was reduced and cysteine blocked. Trypsin was added and incubated at 37°C for 20 h. The recovered samples were desalted, concentrated, and subjected to peptide quantification (Supplementary Table S2). Based on the peptide quantification results, a 10 μg equivalent sample was labeled using the TMTpro™ 18plex Label Reagent Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), fractionated into six fractions using a cation-exchange cartridge system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and desalted. The resulting solution was subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis using Vanquish Neo (Thermo Fisher Scientific), performed by Pharma Foods International Co., Ltd. (Kyoto, Japan); the on-site protocol was followed for all analyses.

Proteomic data processing and bioinformatic analysis

MS/MS spectra acquired by LC-MS/MS were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer 3.0.1.27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein identification was performed using the SequestHT search engine against the UniProtKB Rattus norvegicus database downloaded on February 15, 2024, containing 92,958 sequences.

For proteomic analysis of DAT, relative abundance values of the identified proteins were used for downstream analyses to explore overall features of DAT composition and to identify differentially expressed proteins between the groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize overall differences in the proteomic profiles of epididymal and inguinal DATs among the intervention groups. Comparisons between the groups were performed based on fold change (FC), using thresholds of >1.5 or < −1.5 log2 FC. Venn diagrams were generated from the differentially abundant protein lists to visualize overlaps between the groups, and heatmaps were subsequently created for visualization. To evaluate the functional characteristics of the extracted proteins, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID bioinformatics resources. UniProt accession numbers were used as input IDs for DAVID. GO analysis was performed for the Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function categories, and enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were selected using a nominal p value threshold of <0.05. In addition, STRING/Cytoscape network analysis was performed based on publicly available databases using relative abundance values of the identified proteins.

Statistical analysis

Experimental groups consisted of n = 5 biological replicates. The sample size was not determined by statistical methods, but our sample size was consistent with that reported in a previous rodent study [30].

Statistical analyses and graphical representations of the data were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between the two experimental groups were performed using a two-tailed Student's t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test, unless otherwise stated. Details of the statistical analyses for individual experiments are provided in the corresponding figure legends. For image-based analyses, each DAT hydrogel prepared from an individual rat contributed one data point to the statistical analysis. When multiple image fields were acquired from the same DAT hydrogel, the values from these fields were averaged to obtain one value for that hydrogel. Thus, the experimental unit was each individual rat-derived DAT hydrogel, not each image field, thereby avoiding pseudoreplication. Comparisons involving two independent variables were performed using two-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. Significance was set at p < 0.05. As described earlier, proteomic analysis was performed using pooled samples, and therefore statistical analysis of individual protein abundances was not conducted.

Results

Changes in body mass, adipose tissue mass, and blood triglyceride concentrations with exercise and HFD intake

Total body, inguinal adipose tissue, and epididymal adipose tissue mass were higher in HFD-SED rats than in ND-SED rats, whereas they were lower in ND-EX and HFD-EX rats compared with ND-SED and HFD-SED rats (Fig. 2A–C). Similarly, blood triglyceride concentrations were higher in HFD-SED rats than in ND-SED rats, and lower in ND-EX and HFD-EX rats compared with their SED counterparts (Fig. 2D). These results adequately represent the well-known effects of HFD intake and exercise training on the parameters measured in this study.

Viability of HUVECs in DAT hydrogel

HUVEC death after 48 h culture in hydrogel was assessed by PI staining. Compared with HUVECs cultured in the hydrogel without DAT, the PI fluorescence intensity was significantly lower in HUVECs cultured in all DAT hydrogels prepared from each intervention group. In contrast, no significant differences were observed among the DAT hydrogel groups (Fig. 3A and B). Western blot analysis revealed no significant differences among the DAT hydrogel groups in the protein expression levels of Bax or Bcl-2, or in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio (Fig. 3C), indicating that all DAT hydrogels maintained low levels of HUVEC death.

Fig. 4A shows representative images of the inguinal-derived DAT hydrogels. HUVECs cultured in DAT hydrogel from the HFD-EX group exhibited higher numbers of nodes, junctions, segments, and branches— network connectivity indicators—than those cultured in DAT hydrogel from the ND-SED, ND-EX, and HFD-SED groups (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the total length, segment length, and branch length—network morphology indicators— were significantly longer in the HUVECs cultured in DAT hydrogel from the HFD-EX than in those cultured in DAT hydrogel from the other groups (Fig. 4C). The number of meshes—a network maturation indicator—was significantly higher in the HUVECs cultured in HFD-EX DAT hydrogel than in those cultured in HFD-SED DAT hydrogel (Fig. 4D). Thus, in the inguinal-derived DAT hydrogels, HFD-EX potentiated most of the indicators related to vascular network formation in HUVECs compared to under the other experimental conditions.

Fig. 4E shows representative images in the epididymal-derived DAT hydrogel. HUVECs cultured in HFD-SED or HFD-EX DAT hydrogels exhibited lower number of extremities compared with those cultured in ND-SED or ND-EX DAT hydrogels. In contrast, number of nodes, junctions, and segments were higher in HUVECs cultured in HFD-SED or HFD-EX DAT hydrogels compared with those cultured in ND-SED or ND-EX DAT hydrogels (Fig. 4F). Network morphology indicators other than total length, such as total segment and total branch lengths, were significantly greater in the HUVECs cultured in HFD-SED or HFD-EX DAT hydrogels compared with those cultured in ND-SED or ND-EX DAT hydrogels, respectively (Fig. 4G). The network maturation indicators number of and mean meshes were greater in the HUVECs cultured in HFD-SED or HFD-EX DAT hydrogels compared with those cultured in ND-SED or ND-EX DAT hydrogels, respectively (Fig. 4H). Thus, in the epididymal-derived DAT hydrogels, the HFD condition potentiated most of the indicators related to vascular network formation in HUVECs compared to the ND condition.

Assessment of three-dimensional characteristics of tube-like formation

The angiogenic structure of HUVECs in DAT hydrogel was evaluated three-dimensionally using Calcein AM staining (Fig. 5). Fig. 5A shows representative images obtained by maximum intensity projection of HUVECs cultured in inguinal-derived DAT hydrogels. No significant difference was observed in the mean Feret's diameter (Z-axis) of HUVECs cultured in inguinal-derived DAT hydrogel between the groups (Fig. 5B); the size distribution of Feret's diameter was analyzed as a histogram (Fig. 5C). All groups exhibited a peak Feret's diameter around 50–70 μm (Supplementary Fig. S1A). When comparing groups within the 50–60 μm range, the HUVECs cultured in DAT hydrogel from the ND-EX group exhibited higher values compared with those cultured in DAT hydrogel from the ND-SED, HFD-SED, and HFD-EX groups. When comparing groups within the 60–70 μm range, the HUVECs cultured in DAT hydrogel from the two EX groups exhibited higher values compared with those cultured in DAT hydrogel from the two SED groups. In contrast, within the 10–20 μm range, the HUVECs cultured in DAT hydrogel from the HFD-SED and HFD-EX groups exhibited higher values than those cultured in DAT hydrogel from the ND-SED and ND-EX groups; however, no significant differences were observed among other Feret's diameter ranges (Fig. 5D).

Expression of angiogenesis-related markers and adhesion- and mechanotransduction-related factors in HUVECs cultured in DAT hydrogel

Vascular network formation is regulated through endothelial cell adhesion to the ECM, in which integrin β1 and α6β1 contribute to endothelial cell adhesion, migration, and sprouting [31,32]. Adhesion-dependent signals are transmitted through the FAK/Src pathway via focal adhesion complexes containing Talin-1 and Vinculin and are linked to downstream signaling factors, such as ERK, AKT/mTOR, and β-catenin, which regulate endothelial cell survival, stretching, lumen formation, and angiogenesis [[33], [34], [35]]. Therefore, we assessed the protein expression levels of angiogenesis-, adhesion-, and mechanotransduction-related markers in HUVECs cultured in inguinal- and epididymal-derived DAT hydrogel.

Protein expression levels of VEGFR2 and VE-cadherin were significantly higher in HUVECs cultured in epididymal DAT hydrogel than in those cultured in inguinal DAT hydrogel across all intervention groups (Fig. 7A). Protein expression levels were unaffected by HFD and EX interventions in HUVECs cultured in either hydrogel. No significant difference in CD31 and α-SMA protein expression in HUVECs was found between the intervention groups or culture conditions.

Regarding the expression levels of cell adhesion and cytoskeleton-related proteins, no significant difference in integrin α6, integrin β1, Talin-1, and Vinculin expression was found between HUVECs cultured in inguinal DAT hydrogel and those cultured in epididymal DAT hydrogel (Fig. 7B). Discussion

HUVECs seeded on the basement membrane are capable of forming vessel-like structures in vitro, thus mimicking angiogenesis [36,37]. The composition of the basement membrane affects the efficacy of HUVEC tube formation, and various types of basement membranes can be used, including collagen, Matrigel, and other hydrogel matrices [37,38]. In the present study, DAT was physically processed into powder and mixed with a hydrogel to form a 3D DAT hydrogel as a scaffold for HUVECs. This DAT hydrogel, lacking a 3D ECM structure, was composed only of ECM components; however, it successfully induced HUVECs to form tube-like structures while maintaining low levels of cell death. The DAT powder hydrogel fabricated in this study was highly compatible with HUVEC viability and demonstrated sufficient interaction ability with HUVECs to induce vascular-like network formation.

Our findings showed that tube-like network formation in HUVECs responded more strongly to epididymal than to inguinal DAT hydrogels. Considering DAT hydrogels contain ECM-derived powder with most of its physical structures destroyed, this result highlights that well-known anatomical differences in adipose tissue vascular development are largely dependent on differences in ECM composition between subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue. ECM is composed of collagen (types I, III, IV, VI, and VII), glycosaminoglycans, laminin, and fibronectin [39,40] —these components were confirmed by proteomic analysis in the present study. These ECM components act as signals that promote angiogenesis in vascular endothelial cells. Integrins that read the ECM information interact with growth factor receptors, including VEGFR2, fibroblast growth factor receptor, and platelet-derived growth factor receptor β, and amplify the tyrosine kinase signaling of these receptors via an outside-in pathway involving the FAK/Src–phosphoinositide 3-kinase–AKT–mTORC1 axis, thereby inducing endothelial cell survival, migration, and branching [20,39,40]. Of these signaling molecules, FAK plays a central role in integrin-dependent adhesion signaling by linking VEGFR2-related signaling with VE-cadherin-mediated endothelial cell adhesion regulation [20,[39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. According to this scenario, it is possible that the increased tube-like network formation of HUVECs in epididymal DAT hydrogel compared with those in inguinal DAT hydrogel may be related to the significant increase in VEGFR2 and FAK protein abundance, along with a VEGFR2-induced increase in VE-cadherin [45]— a major component of endothelial adhesion junctions. Nonetheless, the phosphorylation state of signaling molecules remained unchanged.

Interestingly, the integration of our proteomic data with public multi-omics databases predicted possible interactions of t-FAK, VEGFR2, and VE-cadherin with collagen. Supporting this prediction, proteomic analysis clearly showed that Col4a4 and Sel1l were particularly high in epididymal DAT compared to inguinal DAT. Col4a4 is a constituent of type IV collagen and is involved in basement membrane construction and adhesion scaffold formation in endothelial cells [46,47]. Furthermore, Sel1l is involved in collagen turnover and extracellular collagen clearance [48] and has been associated with vascular homeostasis and angiogenesis [49,50]. In zebrafish, sel1l knockdown caused abnormalities in angiogenesis and circulatory dynamics [49]. In glioblastoma, SEL1L was highly expressed in endothelial cells and pericytes of proliferative tumor blood vessels, suggesting its involvement in tumor neovascularization [50]. Although it should be noted that proteomics performed on pooled samples do not provide statistically supported interpretations, it may be speculated that the collagen properties of epididymal DAT-containing ECM are more favorable for the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs compared with inguinal DAT.

Contrary to expectations, an effect of exercise training and HFD on the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs was found only in inguinal DAT hydrogel from the HFD-EX group and epididymal DAT hydrogels from the HFD-fed groups. These intervention-dependent differences were not accompanied by changes in the angiogenic proteins tested. Considering the improvements in vascular integrity do not always require the recruitment or alteration of novel intracellular signaling molecules [51,52], it may be assumed that changes in DAT composition in the hydrogel are associated with the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs. Given this assumption, at least two possibilities are suggested: first, DAT, which enhanced tube-like network formation in HUVECs, may contain substances that commonly change regardless of fat-depot differences; second, the molecules that respond specifically to each intervention may differ between inguinal and epididymal DATs. Integrated analysis of exploratory proteomic data may indicate high protein abundance of Serpina3m as a candidate for the former possibility, and high Col3a1 expression in inguinal DAT and decreased Tpm4 expression in epididymal DAT as a candidate for the latter. However, the lack of direct data revealing the role of these proteins in promoting HUVEC angiogenesis precludes mechanistic insight in this study. Nonetheless, several previous studies have offered insight on the relationship between these three proteins and angiogenesis. Serpina3m belongs to the serpin superfamily and is orthologous to human SERPINA3. Some members of the serpin family have been reported to be involved in angiogenesis and ECM remodeling [[50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Col3a1 has been implicated in endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and vascular-like structure formation [55], whereas Tpm4 and other tropomyosin family members are involved in actin cytoskeleton stabilization, cell adhesion, and cell migration [[56], [57], [58], [59]]. In the present study, GO and KEGG analyses suggested pathways related to cytoskeletal and ECM remodeling were enriched in inguinal HFD-EX DAT, whereas pathways related to cytoskeletal remodeling were enriched in epididymal DAT from the HFD-fed groups. Further research into the fat depot- and intervention-dependent effects of Serpina3m, Col3a1, and Tpm4 in the ECM on controlling tube-like formation in HUVECs would deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying ECM involvement in angiogenesis.

Our in vitro model using the DAT hydrogel does not represent in vivo angiogenesis; the ECM exhibits a more complicated microenvironment in vivo. In vivo, reactive oxygen species and inflammation due to macrophage infiltration increase along with adipose tissue expansion, consequently disrupting angiogenesis in adipose tissue [[60], [61], [62], [63]]. In contrast, exercise training can result in both the inhibition of reactive oxygen species production and macrophage infiltration [64]. These in vivo effects could not be reproduced in the DAT hydrogel system used to evaluate the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs in the current study. Furthermore, the HUVECs used in the present study possess fundamentally different characteristics to those of adipose tissue endothelial cells and do not adequately reflect the inherent heterogeneity of vascular endothelial cells within adipose tissue in vivo [24,[65], [66], [67]]. These differences in the microenvironment between in vivo ECM and DAT hydrogels, as well as the differences between HUVECs and native adipose tissue endothelial cells, likely explain, in part, why we were unable to fully reproduce the reported obesity-induced ECM changes [[14], [15], [16]], exercise training-induced angiogenesis, and increased capillary density in adipose tissue [8,17,18]. Thus, we were unable to fully reproduce the reported effects of obesity and exercise training on adipose tissue angiogenesis in this study may be the difference between our experimental systems: in vivo and in vitro using the DAT hydrogel.

In addition to the limited interpretation mentioned above, this study had other limitations. First, multiple physical and biochemical properties of decellularized ECM hydrogels, such as stiffness, viscoelasticity, fiber structure, porosity, pore size, degradability, residual lipid/DNA content, and ligand density, were not directly evaluated and might have affected the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs [[68], [69], [70], [71], [72]]. In future, it will be necessary to further evaluate the influence of physical and biochemical changes of DAT hydrogel on the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs. It would be helpful to quantitatively evaluate the angiogenic potential of HUVECs using a reconstitution system that allows stepwise manipulation of the mechanical properties and structural factors of the DAT hydrogel. Second, our findings are limited to male rats. There are intrinsic sex differences in angiogenesis and sex-specific gene expression and behavior of endothelial cells [[73], [74], [75], [76]], including HUVECs [[77], [78], [79]], have been reported. However, elucidating this issue in angiogenesis was beyond the scope of this study. Further research is required to verify the possibility that sex differences exist in the ECM, consequently contributing to known sex differences in angiogenesis.

Conclusion

Our findings showed that HUVECs formed tube-like networks in hydrogels containing DAT from rats exposed to HFD, EX, or both. The results indicated that tube-like formation of HUVECs was significantly enhanced in inguinal HFD-EX DAT and epididymal HFD DAT. Furthermore, epididymal DAT hydrogels resulted in greater tube-like formation of HUVECs compared with inguinal DAT hydrogels. Thus, the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs was altered in response to different DAT compositions, which was altered by HFD feeding alone or combined with exercise training in an anatomical site-specific manner. Although we were unable to provide mechanistic insight into the relationship between ECM and angiogenic behavior of HUVECs, to our knowledge, these morphological results regarding the angiogenic behavior of HUVECs in DAT hydrogel comprising ECM components are the first to be reported.
