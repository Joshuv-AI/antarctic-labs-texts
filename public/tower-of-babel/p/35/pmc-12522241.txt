Mechanism of metabolic resistance in Anopheles coluzzii to insecticides in two urban areas of southern Benin

Background

Malaria is still a significant public health issue in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for nearly 90% of malaria-related deaths worldwide [1]. As a West African country south of the Sahara, Benin is no exception. In 2021, there were approximately 2,656,855 cases of malaria in Benin. The incidence rate was 21.2%, resulting in 1914 deaths [2]. Malaria is transmitted by species of the Anopheles gambiae complex, mainly Anopheles coluzzii and An. gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.) [3, 4]. Anopheles coluzzii thrives in poorly urbanized, built-up areas in Africa [5]. It is the predominant species (> 98%) in southern Benin, particularly in Cotonou, Sô-Ava and Porto-Novo, where the constant presence of water provides breeding sites, facilitating rapid mosquito reproduction [6, 7]. The current strategy for combating malaria relies mainly on the use of insecticides through two major interventions: the distribution of long-lasting insecticidal mosquito nets and indoor residual spraying [8–11]. This vector control approach remains the first line of malaria prevention in most strategies implemented by the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP).

The insecticides used to treat these mosquito nets and for indoor spraying include organochlorines, organophosphates, carbamates, synthetic pyrethroids, and benzylureas [12, 13]. Although pyrethroids have long been the only insecticides recommended for impregnating mosquito nets, new generations of mosquito nets combining several classes of insecticides, such as pyrethroids combined with pyriproxyfen or chlorfenapyr, are now being deployed to overcome vector resistance [14]. In mosquitoes, two major mechanisms are involved in insecticide resistance: mutations affecting the target site, such as those in the voltage-gated sodium channel gene responsible for resistance to pyrethroids, and metabolic resistance, which is linked to the overexpression of detoxification enzymes, such as monooxygenases with oxidase function, esterases, and glutathione S-transferases [15]. Resistance to pyrethroids and dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) in malaria vectors has emerged and spread rapidly in several regions of Africa, particularly in Benin, and poses a major threat to the effectiveness of vector control tools [15]. In Benin, the mechanisms of target site mutation and the overexpression of metabolic enzymes are the most frequently studied in mosquitoes, particularly in An. gambiae sensu lato (s.l.). However, since the identification of An. coluzzii within An. gambiae s.l., data on the distribution of metabolic resistance and the ecological factors responsible for its emergence are lacking for this species. The present study was conducted in this context.

This study aimed to determine the metabolic resistance mechanisms of local An. coluzzii populations to pyrethroids and carbamates. These results will help refine the malaria control strategies of the Benin NMCP in the major cities of southern Benin.

Methods

Study area

This study was carried out in two locations, the municipalities of Porto Novo and Cotonou (Fig. 1).

Map of Benin showing the locations of the mosquito collection sites

Porto-Novo municipality

Porto-Novo, the administrative capital of Benin, is located in the Oueme department in the southern part of the country, near the Atlantic coast. The city is situated at coordinates of 6° 0′ N, 3° 30′ E, approximately 13 kms from the Atlantic Ocean, and is separated by a lagoon. It stands at an altitude of approximately 245 m and covers an area of 52 square kilometres. Porto-Novo lies 30 kms east of Cotonou, the economic capital of Benin. As of the 2015 General Population and Housing Census (RPHG 4), the city had a population of approximately 223,552 inhabitants [16].

Porto-Novo has a humid subequatorial climate characterized by two rainy seasons, with annual rainfall ranging from 1100 mm to 1300 mm, and two dry seasons. The temperature in the region varies between 21.9 °C and 32.8 °C. The soil types in the area include ferrallitic plateau soils, sandy soils at the base of slopes bordering marshy lowlands, and clayey hydromorphic soils rich in organic matter, which are found in flood-prone areas. These conditions are favourable for the proliferation of disease vectors [17]. Within this municipality, the localities of Akron, Tokpota, and Djègan Daho were selected because of the widespread, unregulated use of various insecticides by local farmers in vegetable and cereal-growing areas (Fig. 1). Farmers in these areas often disregard proper insecticide handling and recommended dosages [18]. Additionally, these areas have seen significant use of long-lasting insecticide networks (LLINs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), and other vector control measures.

Cotonou municipality

Cotonou, Benin’s economic capital, covers 79 km2 (0.07% of the territory) and represents the department of Littoral, the smallest of Benin’s twelve departments. Situated at 6°20 N and 2°20 E, Cotonou is bordered by Lake Nokoue to the north, the Atlantic Ocean to the south, Seme-Kpodji (Department of Oueme) to the east, and Abomey-Calavi to the west. Cotonou is characterized by alluvial sands with a maximum elevation of approximately five metres. Its landscape features longitudinal depressions parallel to the coast and low-lying areas carved by rainwater runoff, which connect to Lake Nokoué [16]. Cotonou experiences an equatorial climate, with two rainy seasons alternating with two dry seasons. The temperatures ranged from 18 to 35 °C. Although the city has no rivers, Lake Nokoué (85 km2), lagoons, and several low-lying areas serve as water reservoirs. The littoral area primarily consists of sandy, acidic soils. According to the RGPH4 census of May 2013, the population was 679,012 inhabitants [16]. The localities of Agla, Yenawa, Houeyiho, Ladji, Suru Lere and Avotrou were selected for larval collection in Cotonou (Fig. 1). These sites were chosen because of the uncontrolled use of insecticides by farmers in market gardens and cereal fields, often ignoring recommended dosages [18], along with the significant use of LLINs, IRS, and other interventions.

Larval collection of Anopheles gambiae s.l.

The study took place from May to August 2021. Larvae and pupae of An. gambiae s.l. were collected from natural breeding sites located in areas where vegetables and cereals are grown. The collection method consisted of scooping larvae and pupae from the surface via a ladle. These breeding sites were permanent, somewhat turbid, and sun-exposed. After collection, the larvae were filtered and stored in labelled jars on the basis of locality and then transported to the insectarium at the Centre de Recherche Entomologique de Cotonou (CREC). The larvae were reared under standard insectarium conditions (26 °C–30 °C, 60%–90% relative humidity) at the CREC.

Rearing of Anopheles larvae and pupae

In the insectarium, An. gambiae larvae were sorted and separated according to their larval developmental stage to prevent cannibalism. They were then divided into batches of 50 per water tank to optimize growth [19]. The larvae were fed cat food (5 g/500 ml of water for 80 Anopheles larvae), which is high in protein and minerals. Each tank was covered with mosquito netting and kept in a room where the relative humidity varied between 70 and 80% and where the temperature was between 25 °C and 30 °C. A photoperiod was maintained via incandescent lamps that were on from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. Pupae were collected and transferred to cubic cages (30 cm per side) to allow adult mosquitoes to emerge and reduce losses. The CREC insectarium has 2 compartments: one for the wild adult room and one for susceptible adults. Adults that emerged from field-collected larvae were placed in cages and fed a 10% sucrose solution. Female adults aged 3–5 days were harvested, euthanized, and stored in Eppendorf tubes at −80 °C for testing for detoxification enzymes.

Susceptibility testing of collected Anopheles to various insecticides

Susceptibility tests were conducted by WHO test tubes according to the protocol established by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. The tests were carried out with nonblood-fed An. gambiae s.l. females aged 3–5 days. These female mosquitoes were exposed to insecticide-impregnated papers containing pyrethroids (0.05% deltamethrin, 0.75% permethrin) and carbamates (0.1% bendiocarb). The diagnostic doses used were based on WHO recommendations. For each insecticide, 20–25 female mosquitoes were introduced into each test tube lined with insecticide-impregnated paper. Four tubes were used for each type of insecticide. Control tests were conducted using two additional tubes with paper treated only with solvent (insecticide-free). After an hour of exposure, the mosquitoes were transferred to observation tubes containing untreated paper and given access to a 10% honey solution. The observation period lasted 24 h. After this period, mortality rates were calculated for each insecticide, and the resistance status of the mosquito vectors from each locality was determined.

Detoxification enzyme activity in Anopheles coluzzii populations from Porto-Novo and Cotonou

To assess metabolic resistance, mosquitoes that had been exposed to insecticides and those that had not been exposed, which were used as controls, were stored at −80 °C. Each mosquito was individually crushed with 200 μl of distilled water in Eppendorf tubes on ice to prevent enzymatic degradation. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min at room temperature. The supernatant was collected for detoxification enzyme analysis after identification of An. coluzzii. The debris remaining in the tubes was used to extract DNA to confirm the species by PCR. Only samples from female An. coluzzii were used for biochemical tests to measure detoxification enzyme activity.

Molecular identification of Anopheles coluzzii species in Porto-Novo and Cotonou

To identify An. coluzzii species in Porto-Novo and Cotonou, molecular characterization was performed by PCR, following the protocol of Santolamazza et al. [20]. Two primers (R6 and F6) with the following sequences were used to identify the An. gambiae species complex by PCR:

Detection of detoxification enzymes in the wild An. coluzzii population

The supernatants of crushed An. coluzzii mosquitoes from each site, which were obtained after centrifugation and collected in 96-well plates, were assayed for enzyme activity according to the protocol of Hemingway et al. [21]. This study focused on the activities of glutathione S-transferase (GST), esterase and oxidase because the overexpression of these enzymes is associated with insecticide resistance [22]. The enzyme activity was normalized to the total protein content and compared with that of susceptible An. coluzzii N’gousso strain. Using a multichannel pipette, the supernatant samples were transferred in duplicate to new microplates for detoxification enzyme assays. The following assays were performed:

Oxidases

After 20 µl of mosquito homogenate from two replicates was added to each well, 80 µl of 0.0625 M potassium phosphate buffer (KHPO4) at pH = 7.2 was added, followed by the addition of 200 µl of 0.25 M tetramethylbenzidine solution (TMBZ) at pH = 5.0 and the addition of 25 µl of 3% hydrogen peroxide solution to each well. The plate was incubated for 1 h, and the absorbance at 630 nm was read via a spectrophotometer.

Nonspecific esterases

Two replicates of 10 µl of mosquito homogenate were added to each well of the plate, and 90 µl of 1% Triton phosphate saline (PBS) buffer (pH = 6.5) was added. After mixing, the plate was left for 10 min at 25 °C or room temperature. Next, 100 µl of a solution consisting of 0.3 M alpha-naphthyl acetate (or beta-naphthyl acetate) and 2.5 µl of Triton PBS (pH = 6.5) were added, followed by the addition of 7 µl of water. The plate was incubated for 30 min, after which 100 µl of Garnett Salt (FGBC) solution was added. After a further 10-min incubation at 25 °C or room temperature, the absorbance was read at 550 nm.

Glutathione S-transferase

Two replicates of 10 µl of mosquito meal were added to each well of the plate, followed by the addition of 200 µl of a solution of reduced glutathione (GSH) and 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB). After incubation at 25 °C, kinetic spectrophotometry was performed at 340 nm for 5 min.

Total protein

Two replicates of 10 µl of ground material were added to each well of the plate, to which we added 200 µl of a solution consisting of Coomassie and protein assay reagents. After incubation for 5 min at 25 °C or room temperature, the endpoint was read at 590 nm by a spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis

The resistance status of the tested mosquito populations was determined according to WHO criteria [23]. For the WHO tube tests, the resistance status is determined as follows:

Mortality after 24 h ≥ 98%: the An. gambiae s.l. population is considered susceptible.

Mortality after 24 h ≥ 98%: the An. gambiae s.l. population is considered susceptible.

Mortality between 90 and 97% after 24 h: the An. gambiae s.l. population is suspected to be resistant;

Mortality between 90 and 97% after 24 h: the An. gambiae s.l. population is suspected to be resistant;

Mortality after 24 h < 90%: the An. gambiae s.l. population is considered resistant.

Mortality after 24 h < 90%: the An. gambiae s.l. population is considered resistant.

No mortality was recorded in the control group, so Abbott’s formula was not required to adjust the mortality rates. The chi-square test for comparing proportions was performed by R software (version 3.6.3) to compare the proportions of An. coluzzii and An. gambiae s.s. found in Cotonou and Porto Novo after PCR series testing. Optical density (OD) values were recorded and used to create an Excel database. This method was used to calculate the activity levels of different detoxification enzymes (GST, oxidase, and esterase), which were normalized to the total protein content. To better assess the distribution of metabolic resistance enzymes to insecticides in mosquitoes, an analytical approach based on comparing expression levels by region was used. GraphPad Prism 8 software was used to calculate p values and generate graphs.

Results

Susceptibility of Anopheles gambiae s.l. to insecticides

Susceptibility tests revealed that An. gambiae s.l. females from Cotonou were resistant to deltamethrin, permethrin and bendiocarb. The average mortality rates were 25%, 14.77% and 73.56%, respectively (Fig. 2). Similarly, in Porto-Novo, An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes developed resistance to deltamethrin and permethrin, with average mortality rates of 21.73% and 24.21%, respectively. However, these populations showed suspected resistance to bendiocarb, with 96.77% mortality (Fig. 2). Overall, resistance to permethrin was observed in both Porto-Novo and Cotonou. Similarly, there was resistance to deltamethrin, with statistically similar rates in both municipalities. No mortality was observed in the controls.

Identification of Anopheles coluzzii

The results of the PCR analysis of the 160 samples revealed a statistically significant difference between the proportions of An. coluzzii and An. gambiae in each locality, with a p value less than 0.05. An. coluzzii was the predominant species in both Porto-Novo (62.5%) and Cotonou (75%) (Table 1).

Expression of detoxification enzymes in Anopheles coluzzii populations

Figure 3 illustrates that An. coluzzii populations from Cotonou presented significantly greater oxidase activity than did the susceptible reference An. coluzzii N’gousso strain (p < 0.0001). However, no significant difference in oxidase activity was detected in the Porto-Novo mosquito population compared to the susceptible An. coluzzii N’gousso strain (p = 0.0958) (Fig. 3A). Glutathione S-transferase (GST) activity was detected in all An. coluzzii populations from the study areas. The GST activity was significantly elevated in both the Cotonou (p < 0.0001) and Porto-Novo (p = 0.0015) populations compared with the susceptible An. coluzzii N’gousso strain (Fig. 3B). The results also indicate the presence of esterase activity in An. coluzzii populations (Fig. 3C and D). In Cotonou, esterase activity was relatively low. However, significantly higher α- and β-esterase activities were detected in An. coluzzii population from Porto-Novo compared with the reference strain (p < 0.0001). In contrast, no statistically significant difference was detected in the expression of esterases (α- and β-esterases) in An. coluzzii mosquitoes from Cotonou compared with the susceptible strain An. coluzzii N’gousso (p = 0.2120).

Discussion

The main vector of malaria in tropical and subtropical regions, An. gambiae s.l., has developed significant resistance to certain insecticides in Cotonou and Porto-Novo. This resistance is primarily associated with the diagnostic doses of permethrin and deltamethrin used in these areas. Suspected resistance to bendiocarb was also observed in both cities. These results are consistent with previous work carried out in Kandi, Malanville, and Parakou by Kpanou et al. [24], who reported resistance in An. gambiae s.l. to permethrin and deltamethrin, as well as presumed resistance to bendiocarb. The results obtained are similar to those of Sagbohan et al. [25], who reported high resistance of An. gambiae to permethrin and deltamethrin in southern Benin. The observed resistance to pyrethroids in Cotonou and Porto-Novo aligns with previous studies in Benin, which have demonstrated that An. gambiae s.l. is resistant to pyrethroids [22, 26, 27]. This resistance of An. gambiae to pyrethroids and carbamates in the study areas is likely due to the excessive use of pesticides by farmers, without adherence to recommended dosages [18]. The use of insecticides in vegetable and cereal farming in Africa to control crop pests creates natural selection pressure on resistant insects. This selection pressure not only affects agricultural pests but also impacts malaria vectors [28]. During these farming practices, pesticide runoff comes into contact with mosquito breeding sites. Kpanou et al. [24] confirmed this natural selection for insecticide resistance in An. gambiae s.l. populations through the use of LLIN and mosquito insecticides. Yadouleton et al. [29] reported that the majority of pesticides used in agriculture are pyrethroids. These compounds have long been the only insecticides recommended by the WHO for impregnating mosquito nets because of their rapid action, excito-repellent effects, low required dosage, and good tolerability in humans [30]. However, owing to vector resistance to pyrethroids, the WHO now recommends the use of mosquito nets impregnated with two active ingredients: pyrethroids and either chlorfenapyr or PBO [31]. This distribution is likely linked to several ecological factors, including the type of larval habitat (e.g., permanent or semipermanent water bodies that are often polluted or artificial) and the physical and chemical properties of the habitat (e.g., pH, salinity, ambient temperature, and dissolved oxygen) [32]. These conditions create a favorable environment for An. coluzzii, a hydrophilic and anthropophilic species well adapted to urban and peri-urban environments [33].

These results confirm previous studies that reported An. coluzzii as predominant (> 98%) in southern Benin [3, 6, 7, 34]. These results are consistent with those of Koukpo et al. [35], who reported An. coluzzii as the dominant species in southern Benin. Biochemical data revealed an overproduction of mixed function oxidases (MFOs) in An. coluzzii populations in Cotonou. This overexpression is significant because MFOs play a key role in detoxifying pyrethroids in insects [36, 37]. The high expression of these enzymes is likely responsible for the elevated resistance to pyrethroids observed in malaria vectors in both study locations. This can be attributed to the widespread use of insecticides in agriculture [38, 39]. Furthermore, the study revealed the overexpression of the enzyme glutathione-S-transferase (GST) in wild An. coluzzii populations from both Cotonou and Porto-Novo. GST overproduction has been linked to resistance in An. gambiae s.l. to pyrethroids by either binding the insecticide and blocking its action [40] or protecting the mosquito through other biochemical mechanisms against oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation induced by pyrethroid exposure [21, 41]. Biochemical analyses and synergistic studies with metabolic inhibitors have shown that high levels of GST activity, with predominant peroxidase activity, confer resistance to both pyrethroids, whereas esterases contribute to resistance to permethrin [41]. Thus, detoxification enzymes (e.g., GST) may increase phenotypic resistance by broadening the spectrum of insecticide resistance mechanisms [42]. Additionally, the results revealed high esterase activity in the wild An. coluzzii population in Porto-Novo, with lower enzymatic activity in Cotonou. This elevated esterase activity reflects a detoxification process in An. coluzzii against carbamate insecticides. These findings are consistent with studies by Etang et al. [43]. in Cameroon and Matowo et al. [44]. in Tanzania. This may explain the reduced sensitivity of mosquitoes in the study areas to bendiocarb [21]. The high expression of detoxification enzymes calls for renewed efforts in vector control. The development of new approaches, such as exploring the epigenetic regulation of genes encoding these enzymes, could offer solutions for more effective insecticides in the future.

Conclusion

This study reveals metabolic resistance mechanisms to pyrethroids and carbamates in An. coluzzii populations in the Porto-Novo and Cotonou municipalities. This study confirms the involvement of detoxification enzymes in increased tolerance to these insecticides. These results provide crucial data to inform National Malaria Control Program strategies for managing resistance. Given the constant evolution of resistance, establishing a system of continuous entomological surveillance is imperative to anticipate the adaptive dynamics of vectors and preserve the effectiveness of control tools. Thus, this study serves as an essential scientific foundation for supporting sustainable malaria control policies in Benin.
