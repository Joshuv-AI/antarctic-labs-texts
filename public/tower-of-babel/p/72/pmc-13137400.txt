What Deprescribing Information Should Be Communicated on Discharge and How? A Qualitative Study of Pharmacists' Perspectives on Deprescribing at Transitions of Care
Authors: Katherine Desforges, Aili V. Langford, Aisling M. McEvoy, Majd Abu Al Shieh, Justin P. Turner, Michael Dooley, Cristina P. Roman, Emily Reeve
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveHospitalisation provides an opportunity to deprescribe (withdraw inappropriate medications), however, safe and effective deprescribing relies on good communication between relevant stakeholders. The aim of this study was to explore the perspectives of Australian hospital and community pharmacists on communicating information about deprescribing at transitions of care and their role in the deprescribing process.MethodsA diverse group of hospital and community pharmacists was purposively recruited. Data were collected via individual semi‐structured interviews and focus groups that were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim using NVivo. Each transcript was thematically analysed using an inductive coding approach, performed by two independent coders.ResultsThirty‐two pharmacists were recruited between June and August 2023. Seventeen participants worked in hospitals, 10 in community pharmacies, and five across both settings. Four themes with 12 subthemes were identified, pertaining to (i) how pharmacists communicate about deprescribing on hospital discharge, (ii) how pharmacists collaborate with other healthcare professionals and patients to optimise deprescribing, (iii) how the role of the pharmacist in deprescribing at transitions of care can be optimised, and (iv) how pharmacists feel about their current and future role in deprescribing.ConclusionsChallenges in communicating deprescribing recommendations and ensuring continuity of care when patients transition between care settings were identified. Development of new tools (e.g., templates and guidance on how and what should be communicated) and processes to support communication at the transition of care from hospital to the community are needed to support deprescribing activities.

Introduction

Potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) are those that have an unfavourable balance of benefits and harms [1]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis showed a pooled prevalence of PIM use of 36.7% in older adults around the world, and this prevalence has increased in the past two decades [2]. The use of PIMs is associated with many risks, such as increased incidence of adverse drug events and emergency department visits [3, 4, 5]. PIMs' use associated with polypharmacy is common in hospitalised patients, particularly older adults, and may even increase during hospital stays [6, 7, 8].

Deprescribing is the process of withdrawal of inappropriate medications, supervised by a clinician with the goal of reducing polypharmacy and improving outcomes [9, 10]. The benefits of deprescribing range from decreasing the number of PIMs (and therefore pill burden, costs and risk of adverse drug reactions) to a potential reduction in mortality [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. The hospital inpatient environment is potentially a good setting to initiate deprescribing, for reasons such as the opportunity to reassess medications on admission (e.g., in the context of changed goals of care) with time to engage in discussions with inpatients, access to clinical resources and capacity for close monitoring [16]. Additionally, it has been shown that nearly 90% of older hospitalised patients are open to having a medication deprescribed [17, 18, 19]. However, barriers to deprescribing in the inpatient environment include the acute care context (i.e., the need to focus on the acute problem), short lengths of stays, difficulties accessing information needed to inform deprescribing and lack of confidence in the transition of care process [16]. Fear of consequences (mainly adverse drug withdrawal events) and therapeutic inertia have also been reported as limitations to implementing deprescribing strategies in hospital [20].

The transfer of medication information during transitions of care has been identified by the World Health Organization Global Patient Safety Challenge ‘Medication Without Harm’ as one of three priority areas to reduce medication‐related harm [21]. Recent studies have synthesised evidence on deprescribing at transitions of care, highlighting multiple implementation challenges, including fragmented care processes, unclear responsibility and poor communication between healthcare providers across care settings [22, 23]. Studies exploring the transfer of deprescribing information at discharge have focussed mainly on physicians [24, 25]. However, community and hospital pharmacists play an important role in medication management across care settings. Pharmacist‐led deprescribing initiatives have demonstrated positive outcomes including reductions in emergency department visits, hospital readmissions and the number of medications prescribed [26, 27, 28]. Pharmacists' scope of practice, including prescribing, and remuneration models are expanding around the world, including in Australia, with evidence to support hospital pharmacist prescribing as part of a collaborative approach with medical staff [29, 30].

The aim of this study was to explore key elements and communication strategies when transferring information about deprescribing at hospital discharge from the perspective of pharmacists who work in hospital or community settings. A secondary aim was to explore Australian pharmacists' perspectives about their role in the deprescribing process and, specifically, how they would feel about initiating deprescribing (within the context of expanded prescribing rights). For the purpose of this manuscript, we refer to this as pharmacist‐initiated deprescribing.

Methods

This study was approved by Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (project ID 38434). Methods are reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Appendix S1) [31].

Participants and Recruitment

Eligible participants were registered pharmacists practicing in Australia, either currently or in the past 2 years. Participants could practice in hospital (e.g., patient wards, the emergency department, outpatient or hospital in the home services) or community settings (e.g., community pharmacies, General Practice clinics, or conducting Home Medicine Reviews (HMRs) or Residential aged care Medication Management Reviews (RMMRs)). Participants were recruited using a mix of convenience and purposive sampling. Advertising through relevant professional pharmacy organisations, social media, personal contacts of investigators and snowballing were used. All participants were asked to complete an expression of interest form via the REDCap platform to determine their eligibility to participate. This was also used to inform purposive sampling to ensure diversity and representation across the sample based on gender, years of experience and practice setting (community or hospital setting, and metropolitan or rural/remote setting).

Procedure and Data Collection

This qualitative study employed both one‐on‐one interviews and focus groups. Eligible participants known to be working in the same setting were invited to participate in a focus group where practical; other participants were contacted to schedule an individual interview. A semi‐structured interview guide, tailored to participants' practice setting (hospital or community; Appendix S2) was developed by the investigators, including pharmacists with experience in both settings. The guide focussed on deprescribing at hospital discharge for patients returning home or transitioning to residential aged care and was informed by the Communication‐Human Information Processing (C‐HIP) model and the Expanded Chronic Care Model [32, 33]. Pilot‐testing was conducted with two volunteers (one pharmacist working in a community pharmacy and one with experience in a hospital setting), which helped refine the questions. Their data were not included in the study analysis. The interviews were conducted by one researcher (KD, female, geriatric pharmacist, B. Pharm., M. Sc., BCGP), who was supervised for the first two interviews by researchers with experience in qualitative research (ER and AVL). The focus group was conducted by one experienced researcher (ER, female, pharmacist, BPharm(Hons) PhD). For a minority of participants, there was an established collegial relationship with the interviewer (as personal networks were used as a recruitment strategy). Implied consent was used for all participants. Interviews and focus groups were conducted via video conference (Zoom) or in person within Monash University. Repeat interviews were not carried out and field notes were not taken. Following interviews and focus groups, participants were asked to complete a demographic data questionnaire on the REDCap platform. Information concerning participants' age, qualifications, state or territory of practice, supervision of students and whether they worked in a private or public setting was obtained. No participants dropped out. Interviews and focus groups were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim using the NVivo software voice to text function. All transcripts were checked for accuracy by one of two researchers (KD or MAAS). Data collection ended after data saturation was achieved, defined as no new relevant themes or subthemes identified from two consecutive interviews with pharmacists from the same practice setting. Participants were offered a $A30 gift card as honorarium.

Analysis

The software program NVivo (QSR International Pty Ltd., version 14) was used to code interview transcripts using inductive thematic analysis. The coding framework was iteratively refined through a process of group discussion between the coders and the other members of the research team. The first 22 transcripts were coded in duplicate by two independent researchers (KD and either AMM or MAAS). The final 10 transcripts were coded to the codebook by one researcher (KD) and checked by another member of the research team (AVL). For all transcripts, any differences in coding were discussed and resolved between the researchers. Neither transcript checking nor participant validation of findings were conducted.

Thirty‐two participants (17 hospital pharmacists, 10 community pharmacists and five pharmacists practicing across both hospital and community settings (mixed practice)) were recruited. Individual interviews (n = 26) and one focus group (n = 6) took place between June and August 2023. Table 1 presents the participants' characteristics. The length of each interview ranged from 30 to 60 minutes. Four themes and 12 subthemes were identified (Figure 1). Theme 1: Communication on discharge

Pharmacists detailed their role in the transition of care process and talked about how and to whom information is transferred upon discharge from hospital. This theme also captured how the communication process could be optimised to support deprescribing.

Pharmacists' role in transition of care

Participants described various roles for hospital pharmacists at transitions of care. This included preparing discharge medication lists with additional information, such as recommendations for deprescribing, which, in some electronic health record (EHR) systems, could be pushed into the discharge summary. Participants also described liaising with other healthcare practitioners, including those in the community, and providing verbal and written counselling to patients about medication changes. Community pharmacists described reiterating medication changes to patients based on information provided in hospital discharge summaries, which follow a broadly consistent structure across Australian hospitals, although the format and level of detail may vary by institution.

Documentation on discharge

When asked to consider an ideal scenario to support communication upon discharge, participants were split between wanting a structured deprescribing template and a dedicated section in the discharge summary allowing free‐text entry. Key elements deemed necessary for inclusion in a template were the rationale for why the medication was deprescribed, steps already completed, proposed tapering schedule and recommended monitoring, all as specific as possible. Regardless of format, participants highlighted the importance of a deprescribing‐dedicated section being consistently located and easily identifiable for rapid reference, while also allowing flexibility for customisation.

Information to other healthcare professionals

Participants stated that usually only General Practitioners (GPs) and residential aged care facilities received a copy of discharge summaries and medication lists. However, they believed that every healthcare professional involved in the patient's care should receive a summary of the changes made during their hospital stay, including community pharmacists. When deprescribing discussions were initiated in hospital but not implemented, participants from other healthcare settings indicated they would appreciate receiving this information for future reference.

Communication channels

Participants stated that discharge information is usually sent to a patient's GP via fax or electronic transfer, as it was seen as more efficient for busy healthcare professionals. Discharge documents were also reported to sometimes be printed and given to the patient to bring to their GP. Interactions between hospital and community pharmacists were most commonly reported when patients using dose administration aids (e.g., blister packs such as Webster‐paks) were discharged or when clarification about new or changed medications was required. Such interactions were reported to be routinely conducted via fax, email or phone calls. Community pharmacists valued knowing a specific hospital contact person for follow‐up and most believed that contact should happen as soon as possible after discharge. However, some hospital pharmacists suggested that an individual, such as a GP liaison, should be available to respond to post‐discharge queries. Overall, participants highlighted that current communication channels are limited in their capacity to support effective communication about deprescribing across transitions of care.

Theme 2: Collaborating on deprescribing with healthcare professionals and patients

Participants reported on how the multidisciplinary team, specifically the medical team, and patients react to deprescribing suggestions, and their influence in the process.

Working with other healthcare professionals

Participants highlighted the importance of communication and collaboration with pharmacists across settings during deprescribing. The receptiveness of medical teams to pharmacists' deprescribing suggestions was reported to largely depend on their relationships and level of trust. Participants reported that some doctors appreciate the support they offer and the shared responsibility for deprescribing decisions, particularly given time constraints and limited knowledge of why medications were initiated or how to deprescribe them.

Many hospital and community pharmacy participants believed that medical colleagues would be supportive of pharmacists initiating deprescribing, provided they were kept informed. In most cases, participants felt that doctors would not oppose deprescribing suggestions being transferred to and implemented by community pharmacists. However, a few community pharmacists expressed that some GPs would be hesitant to relinquish responsibility for deprescribing decisions, preferring to be closely involved in their patients' care.

Working with patients

Participants mentioned that patients' opinions on pharmacist involvement in deprescribing, now and in the future, varied depending on the therapeutic relationship. When the burden of current medications and the benefits of deprescribing were explained, participants believed that most patients would be agreeable to deprescribing. However, it was stated that many would want their doctor's input and approval. Participants also suggested that education for doctors and patients about pharmacists' roles in deprescribing could lessen the fear associated with it.

Theme 3: Future optimisation of pharmacists' roles in deprescribing at transitions of care

This theme captured hospital pharmacists' views on the role of community pharmacists in future pharmacist‐initiated deprescribing, and vice versa. It also reflects the general opinion of participants regarding community pharmacists continuing or changing the deprescribing plan initiated in hospital.

General opinion

Participants from all settings believed that colleagues from other work environments should be allowed to implement deprescribing. Hospital‐based participants thought that community pharmacists would require more time and access to information and support before initiating deprescribing. Many viewed an accreditation process as necessary to ensuring competence, particularly in the context of future independent prescribing rights to initiate tapering regimens. Many hospital pharmacists mentioned that HMR and RMMR pharmacists might be more equipped to implement deprescribing in their practice than those working in community pharmacies, due to increased time, experience and confidence. Some community‐based participants believed that hospital pharmacists could start the discussion about deprescribing but should not necessarily start the process as they may lack access to a full patient history during admission.

Continuing deprescribing initiated in hospital/changing the plan

With respect to implementing deprescribing plans initiated in hospital, most participants agreed that pharmacists across settings have the necessary knowledge and skills. Regarding changing the plan, participants believed that community pharmacists should be enabled to make changes when clinically necessary, in line with their professional responsibilities and informed by previous experiences. This view was attributed to community pharmacists' ongoing relationships with patients.

Theme 4: Current and future deprescribing in practice

This theme captured participants' current roles and contributions to the deprescribing process, and how they envision pharmacist‐initiated deprescribing in the future (e.g., in the context of expanded deprescribing rights).

Contributions to deprescribing

Hospital pharmacists and community pharmacists who conducted HMRs or RMMRs reported being highly involved in the deprescribing process, mostly through recommending medications suitable for deprescribing and providing advice to healthcare professionals and patients regarding rates of tapering and monitoring parameters. In contrast, participants working exclusively in community pharmacies felt that they did not actively recommend deprescribing, stating that they felt uncomfortable intervening in what they, and patients, consider to be the doctors' responsibility.

Attitudes

Hospital pharmacists reported being mostly confident and highly motivated in their abilities to deprescribe. Enablers included greater years of experience and training. Despite reporting that they had little current input into deprescribing, community pharmacists still described being comfortable initiating conversations with prescribers and patients about the appropriateness of medications if they had concerns. Pharmacists from all settings stated that they would be comfortable initiating the deprescribing process in the future, if it was done in conjunction with the medical team and if they had access to pertinent medical information.

Drug classes

Participants reported that the most common drug classes deprescribed were those considered to be high risk because of their side effects profile, such as antipsychotics and opioids. Similarly, medicines without a current indication, or ones that can be stopped with limited weaning and monitoring, such as statins and vitamins, were reported to be prioritised for deprescribing.

In the context of future pharmacist‐initiated deprescribing, most participants indicated a preference for reviewing the entire medication profile. Others described a more incremental approach, identifying specific medications based on a condition and gradually expanding to all medications. Hospital pharmacists believed that community pharmacists could initially focus on preventative medicines, medications that require close follow‐up when being deprescribed (such as antihypertensives and antidepressants), and medications with which they have experience in deprescribing. On the other hand, community pharmacists felt that hospital pharmacists were better positioned to review the entire medication profile, given their access to patients' medical histories and their ability to collaborate with the medical team.

Logistics of deprescribing

Participants described several logistical barriers and enablers for deprescribing across care settings.

All pharmacists interviewed believed that their work setting was a good place for deprescribing. However, participants described workplace distractions (e.g., phone calls, discussions with colleagues, competing priorities) as barriers to engaging in deprescribing activities. Hospital pharmacists mentioned workload and reduced lengths of stay as the main barriers to deprescribing. Participants who reported being actively involved in deprescribing activities explained being able to manage workplace distractions and allocate dedicated time to this activity.

As enablers, some participants mentioned using tools to inform when and how to deprescribe. These included the Tasmanian deprescribing guidelines [34], and the deprescribing.org and Canadian Medication Appropriateness and Deprescribing Network tools and resources [35, 36, 37]. Community pharmacists described limited access to resources in their setting (e.g., nursing support for blood pressure monitoring), whereas some hospitals had implemented liaison services to support monitoring activities following discharge.

Finally, when reflecting on the potential for pharmacists to be granted specific deprescribing rights in future, participants from all settings identified specific and mandatory training/credentials, guidelines and frameworks, and dedicated time as enablers. Remuneration, mainly in the form of funding for a specific deprescribing role, was also mentioned. Hospital pharmacists additionally expressed that receiving feedback on patient outcomes following discharge would support reflection and improvement of future practice.

Discussion

This qualitative study identified four themes and 12 subthemes regarding communication of information about deprescribing at transitions of care and initiation of deprescribing from the perspectives of Australian pharmacists. Pharmacists reported having a role in communication at transitions of care and emphasised the need for effective transfer of specific and detailed information. However, they highlighted that within current systems there is no single ideal communication channel. The value of the therapeutic relationship between healthcare professionals and patients and/or their caregivers was identified as crucial to initiate deprescribing. Finally, when asked to reflect on a future of expanded pharmacist‐initiated deprescribing, pharmacists from all settings described being comfortable under certain conditions.

Insufficient communication at transitions of care can lead to significant patient harm, and errors in medication documentation can lead to adverse drug events [38, 39]. Pharmacist involvement in the documentation process on discharge improves medication documentation and reduces errors [40, 41, 42]. Insufficient communication about deprescribing at transitions of care can lead to harm through restarting of inappropriate medications, incomplete taper, abrupt cessation or too rapid taper [43, 44]. Using the EHR has been reported by pharmacists as their preferred method of transferring and receiving information at transitions of care [45, 46], but participants in our study reported that phone calls are still needed at times. Many EHR systems used in clinical practice are currently set to work independently, with limited communication pathways available across other systems (within the hospital and between hospital and community), and some functionalities (such as requests to discontinue a medication to be sent to community pharmacies) not readily accessible [47]. Those limitations and logistical barriers identified in this and previous studies present challenges to effectively communicating at transitions of care [46, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52].

Similar to our study, previous qualitative research with GPs reported that specific and detailed information in the discharge summary is essential to continue and monitor the deprescribing process initiated during a hospital admission [25, 53]. Participants in our study supported the idea of a template but highlighted the need for flexibility. Key elements to be included in the template are the reason for deprescribing, what steps were already completed, proposed tapering schedule and recommended monitoring. These key elements are the same as those requested by GPs in previous studies [25, 46, 53].

While it is clear from this and other studies that pharmacists have a key role in supporting deprescribing in practice [28, 54, 55, 56, 57], legislation and health system policies internationally are generally not explicit about pharmacists' rights and responsibilities to initiate deprescribing. In some countries where pharmacists have prescribing rights, they are able to initiate deprescribing, provide prescriptions required for tapering and conduct appropriate monitoring within their scope of practice [58, 59, 60]. In others, legislation and funding have been implemented to support pharmacists expanding their scope of practice to specifically include deprescribing. For example, in the province of Quebec, Canada, the clinical act of deprescribing has been included in the pharmacy legislation since 2022 [61]. Under specific circumstances, it can be remunerated by the provincial health insurance agency. However, in Australia and other countries where pharmacist prescribing rights may be more limited, the legal ability for pharmacists to initiate deprescribing may be unclear. For non‐prescription medications, pharmacists are able to directly advise patients to deprescribe, as this is within the current scope of practice and legislative rights of pharmacists [62, 63]. In Australia, changes are currently underway to expand prescribing rights for community pharmacists, and partnered pharmacist medication charting (PPMC) exists within many hospital systems with federal government support to expand this model nationally [64, 65]. PPMC is a recognised model of care that has been shown to reduce medication errors and length of stay in hospitals [29, 30]. Whether it would be considered within hospital and community pharmacists' scope of practice to initiate deprescribing of prescription medications where no new prescriptions are required, for example, advising changing from regular to as‐needed use or where no tapering is needed, requires further consideration. However, pharmacists from all settings in our study emphasised the necessity of close collaboration with doctors to support safe and effective deprescribing. Also, similar to what was found in this study, direct forms of communication between healthcare providers to discuss and transfer deprescribing suggestions and trust built between other healthcare providers and pharmacists were considered facilitators to deprescribing [66, 67, 68]. Finally, participants in our study mentioned patients' and/or their caregivers' trust as paramount to effective deprescribing; this resonates with a study that showed that more than half of patients would be comfortable with their pharmacist being involved in the process [69].

Our study has several strengths including an adequate sample size with saturation achieved in both the hospital and community pharmacist cohorts, representation of pharmacists from various settings and the involvement of multiple team members in analysis [70]. However, there are some important limitations. First, our sample had fewer male than female participants (19% vs. 81%); nevertheless, this somewhat reflects the gender breakdown of pharmacists in Australia [71]. Second, due to our recruitment methods, we likely attracted participants who had an interest in deprescribing, which could have biased our findings. Finally, our findings are limited to Australia and may not be generalisable to other countries.

Further research is needed to determine how existing EHR systems can be best utilised for communicating deprescribing medication changes at transitions of care. Our participants suggested adjustments to improve current practices in transferring deprescribing suggestions to the community, such as being easily identifiable within the information transferred on discharge and being sent to all healthcare professionals involved in the patient's care. This was consistent with recent research in Quebec, Canada, to develop guiding principles for a medication discharge plan [46].

Conclusions

This study identified many challenges in communicating about deprescribing when patients transition between care settings. Development and implementation of electronic communication tools (e.g., templates and guidance on how and what should be communicated), tailored to specific contexts and practice settings, alongside standardised processes to support communication and collaboration at transitions of care, are needed to support deprescribing success. Further research is needed into how the skills and expertise of pharmacists can be best used to optimise deprescribing in practice and enhance patient safety during transitions of care.
