Real-world outcomes of faricimab in treatment-naïve diabetic macular edema: The Middle East perspective
Authors: Abdulrahman F. AlBloushi, Ahmed M. Bedda, Ahmed Habib, Anant Pai, Ahmed Souka, Faisal Alkathery, Yasmine Bedda, Eman Samir, Heba Abdelaziz, Hashem A. Abu Serhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:Diabetic macular edema (DME) is a major cause of visual impairment in patients with diabetic retinopathy. While intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) monotherapy has been considered standard of care, many patients have suboptimal treatment responses. Faricimab, which targets angiopoietin-2 and VEGF-A, has shown efficacy and tolerability in randomized controlled trials (RCTs). As RCT results are often challenging to replicate in real-world practice, this study assessed the impact of faricimab on DME outcomes in Middle Eastern real-world settings.METHODS:Treatment-naïve patients with DME receiving faricimab between 2023 and 2025 with ≥6 months follow-up were examined at five sites in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar. Following loading doses, patients were treated with either pro re nata or treat-and-extend regimens. Outcomes included visual acuity (VA), retinal thickness, number of injections, and adverse events (AEs). Data were analyzed separately by site.RESULTS:Eighty-eight eyes were treated across sites, with mean follow-up ranging from 7.6 to 12.5 months. Mean number of faricimab injections ranged from 3.0 to 4.2. Improvements from baseline in VA (mean logarithm of the Minimal Angle of Resolution reductions between −0.16 and −0.49) and retinal thickness (mean reductions between −50.6 μm and −194.0 μm) were observed with faricimab treatment at all sites, regardless of differences in treatment protocol or observation time points. No AEs of intra-ocular inflammation or endophthalmitis were observed.CONCLUSION:The findings suggest faricimab conferred visual and anatomical improvements in a heterogeneous group of patients with DME in real-world Middle East settings. Further real-world studies are essential to confirm long-term safety and effectiveness in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic macular edema (DME) is a common complication of diabetes mellitus characterized by the accumulation of fluid in the macula.[1] It is a major cause of visual impairment,[1] with global prevalence expected to increase to around 24 million affected individuals by 2030.[2] Within the Middle East, the prevalence of DME among individuals with diabetes mellitus (primarily Type 2 diabetes mellitus) is reported as 5.7% in Saudi Arabia and 11.5% in Egypt.[34] DME pathogenesis involves the damage of blood vessels in the retina due to chronic high blood sugar levels, leading to a breakdown of the blood–retinal barrier, causing leakage of fluid, proteins, and lipids into the retinal tissue, and resulting in retinal thickness and swelling.[15] There are several risk factors for developing DME, including poor glycemic control, hypertension, longer diabetes duration, and genetic predisposition.[1] The pathogenesis of DME is multifactorial, whereby vascular leakage and instability are synergistically driven by both angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).[156] If untreated, irreversible damage to retinal nerve cells and permanent vision loss may occur.[1]

Intravitreal anti-VEGF monotherapies have historically been considered the standard of care in the treatment of DME.[278] However, a considerable portion of patients have a suboptimal response to anti-VEGF therapy, even with monthly injections for up to 2 years.[7] Additionally, several studies have demonstrated that the efficacy of anti-VEGF therapies seen in clinical trials is not achieved nor maintained in real-world clinical practice.[89] This discrepancy may stem from various factors, including inadequate treatment frequency in clinical practice, inability to replicate complex treatment protocols, and diversity among patients in clinical presentation and social determinants of health.[810]

The requirement for frequent injections with anti-VEGFs leads to a significant burden for patients, carers, and healthcare systems, with negative impacts on patient and carer quality of life, decreased work productivity, and economic burden due to frequent clinic visits and healthcare resource use.[711] Therefore, there is a need for longer-acting, more efficacious, and durable therapies to help alleviate this burden.

Faricimab is the first bispecific antibody approved for intraocular use,[12] targeting both Ang-2 and VEGF-A.[13] By targeting multiple pathways, faricimab has been shown to be an effective, durable, and well-tolerated treatment option for treating DME in the YOSEMITE (NCT03622580)[14] and RHINE (NCT03622593)[15] phase three randomized controlled trials (RCTs).[8] In these trials, faricimab achieved comparable visual acuity (VA) gains, decreased the total number of injections required, and reduced retinal fluid and central retinal thickness compared with aflibercept 2 mg over 2 years.[8] In addition, faricimab treatment enabled extension of the interval between successive injections, demonstrating treatment durability over 2 years.[8]

It is important to consider real-world data alongside data from RCTs, given that vision outcomes in the real world often do not reflect those observed in clinical trials. There is currently a paucity of real-world evidence on faricimab in treatment-naïve patients with DME in the Middle East. This study presents the first real-world observations of faricimab use for treatment-naïve patients with DME in clinical practice at sites in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar, describing how faricimab treatment is being implemented in clinical practice in the Middle East, and whether its effectiveness in real-world practice reflects the efficacy seen in clinical trials.

METHODS

Patients

This retrospective, observational study included treatment-naïve patients with DME treated with faricimab at five secondary and tertiary centers in three countries in the Middle East, including Egypt (Al Mashreq Eye Center, Cairo; Alexandria University, Alexandria; and iCare Eye Center, Alexandria), Qatar (Hamad Medical Corporation, Doha) and Saudi Arabia (King Khalid University Hospital Dallah Hospital, Riyadh). Data from each site are reported separately.

Eligible patients had Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes mellitus, had no previous anti-VEGF treatment for DME, and had ≥6 months of follow-up. Patients at Alexandria University were also required to have a central macular thickness of ≥350 μm for inclusion. At all sites, patients were excluded if they had an active infection or a previous allergy to faricimab. Patients were additionally excluded at all sites if they had other conditions, such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration, and all sites, if they had prior corticosteroid intravitreal injections.

Faricimab treatment was administered between 2023 and 2025 with treatment periods of up to 14 months at one site and approximately 24 months at the remaining four sites.

Study design

Current guidelines in the Middle East recommend that faricimab (6 mg, 0.05 mL solution) is administered every 4 weeks (Q4W) for the first four doses.[1617] Treatment can then be individualized based on physician’s assessment, using a treat-and-extend (T&E) approach following assessment of the individual patient’s anatomic and visual outcomes, with the dosing interval extended to up to every 16 weeks (Q16W).[18]

Egypt

At all sites in Egypt (iCare Eye Center, Alexandria University, and the Al Mashreq Eye Center), the majority of patients received three loading injections. Following loading doses, a pro re nata (PRN) regimen was employed at each site, whereby patients were treated at the discretion of the clinician.

Qatar

At the Hamad Medical Corporation, three loading doses were administered; follow-up was then conducted at Week 16, followed by a T&E regimen involving treatment interval extension depending on disease activity and clinician discretion.

Saudi Arabia

At Dallah Hospital, the treatment protocol specified a minimum of four loading doses, though not all patients received all four doses. Thereafter, a T&E regimen was employed, also consisting of treatment interval extension depending on disease activity and clinician decision.

Outcomes

Key outcomes collected included faricimab treatment patterns (number of loading and PRN/T&E doses received over a given treatment period), VA gain (measured using logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution [logMAR] scores), mean change in retinal thickness (measured by central subfield thickness [CST]; optical coherence tomography [OCT] instruments used included the Cirrus Zeiss and Heidelberg Spectralis), and adverse events (AEs). AEs were defined as unfavorable events in health, including both ocular and non-ocular events. Ocular AEs included, but were not limited to, injection-related AEs, intra-ocular inflammation, retinal detachment, endophthalmitis, vitreous hemorrhage, retinal artery occlusion, or significant and rapid worsening of vision or anatomy. At most sites, intra-ocular inflammation was assessed based on routine clinical examination including slit-lamp evaluation of anterior chamber cells and flare; follow-up assessment period varied between sites, and earlier assessment was performed if patients reported any symptoms of inflammation. The Hamad Medical Corporation and iCare Eye Center also used fundus assessment including vitreous and retina examination, fundus photography, or OCT to assess intra-ocular inflammation. At the Al Mashreq Eye Center, intra-ocular inflammation was only assessed further if complications were recorded.

At all sites, non-ocular AEs included arterial thromboembolic complications (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke, or other clinically significant vascular events). The iCare Eye Center also monitored for transient ischemic attack, systemic hypertension exacerbation, and hospitalization for cardiovascular causes in follow-up records.

Statistical analysis

Data were aggregated and analyzed by site individually at the cohort level, and summarized using descriptive statistics. Statistical comparisons across study sites were not feasible due to cross-site variability, and as such, results for each site’s cohort are reported separately. Data were managed using Microsoft Excel at all sites; data from Dallah Hospital and Hamad Medical Corporation were additionally analyzed using SPSS® (IBM Inc. Chicago, Illinois, USA).

Data are presented using means and standard deviation (SD), or ranges (minimum–maximum), where available.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. For each site, the study was locally registered as a retrospective audit where patient identifiable data were omitted; ethical approval was not required at four sites. The protocol for the Hamad Medical Corporation was approved by the institutional review board (MRC-01-25-783). At Dallah Hospital, the Hamad Medical Corporation, and Alexandria University, written informed consent was not sought because the study involved retrospective review of existing clinical records without direct patient intervention or identifiable patient information, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. At the Al Mashreq Eye Center and iCare Eye Center, participants gave informed consent to participate in the study before taking part.

RESULTS

Patient disposition and baseline characteristics

A total of 88 eyes were treated across the five sites, with a range of 9–26 eyes (from 7–16 patients) treated per site. The mean patient age at baseline ranged from 51.4 to 64.3 years; a similar proportion of eyes were from female versus male patients in each site’s cohort, with 47.1% to 61.5% of eyes being from male patients [Table 2]. The majority of patients had Type 2 diabetes mellitus, including 100% of eyes from sites in Egypt and Saudi Arabia, and 92.3% of eyes from Qatar. At baseline, mean logMAR score (SD) for VA ranged between sites from 0.43 (0.19) to 1.11 (0.47) and mean retinal thickness (CST) ranged from 367.1 (108.6) to 499.3 (126.2) μm. No patients were lost to follow-up at the iCare Eye Center, Alexandria University, and Dallah Hospital. Patients who were lost to follow-up at the Hamad Medical Corporation and Al Mashreq Eye Center were excluded from the analysis.

Key outcomes

Number of faricimab doses received

Across sites, the mean follow-up duration ranged from 7.6 to 12.5 months. The mean number of faricimab doses received per eye was 3.0–3.1 at most sites, apart from Alexandria University and Hamad Medical Corporation; these sites reported a mean of 4.2 and 3.5 doses per eye over mean follow-up periods of 12.5 and 9.4 months, respectively.

At Dallah Hospital and Hamad Medical Corporation, which employed a T&E regimen, the majority of patients only received faricimab loading doses and did not have any T&E doses within the follow-up period [Figure 1a and b].

At Dallah Hospital, patients received 1–4 loading doses of faricimab. Over a period of 6–12 months, 12/21 (57.1%) eyes only received faricimab loading doses and no further T&E doses [Figure 1a]. A PRN regimen was employed at all sites in Egypt [Figure 1c and d]. At the Al Mashreq Eye Center, the majority of eyes (12/15 [80.0%]) only received two or three loading doses over a treatment period of 6–18 months and did not receive any further PRN doses. The remaining three eyes received one, two, or five PRN doses after three loading doses, over 24 months [Figure 1c]. At Alexandria University, 10/17 (58.8%) patients received faricimab loading doses only (3–4 doses) over a treatment period of 7–26 months. The remaining seven eyes additionally received 2–4 PRN doses [Figure 1d]. Over a period of 6–13 months at the iCare Eye Center, 8/9 (88.9%) eyes received 1–3 loading doses only; the remaining eye received three loading doses and three PRN doses over 10 months [Figure 1e].

Visual acuity gain

VA gains, seen as reductions in logMAR score from baseline after faricimab treatment, were observed at all study sites regardless of the different treatment protocols used [Table 3]. After a mean of 3.0 injections, mean reductions in logMAR (SD) of −0.16 (0.25) and −0.22 (0.14) were observed at Dallah Hospital and the iCare Eye Center, respectively. At the Al Mashreq Eye Center, the mean change in logMAR (SD) was −0.26 (0.29) after a mean of 3.1 injections. A mean reduction in logMAR (SD) of −0.25 (0.25) was observed at the Hamad Medical Corporation after a mean of 3.5 injections, and Alexandria University reported a mean change in logMAR (SD) of −0.49 (0.45) after a mean of 4.2 injections.

Retinal thickness

A reduction in retinal thickness (CST) from baseline was observed with faricimab treatment at all sites [Table 3]. For sites with a mean number of 3.0 injections per eye, mean change in CST (SD) was −55.3 (114.2) μm (from 367.1 μm at baseline) at Dallah Hospital, −126.3 (139.7) μm (from 460.7 μm at baseline) at the Al Mashreq Eye Center and −130.0 (76.8) μm (from 403.7 μm at baseline) at the iCare Eye Center. Mean change in CST (SD) was −188.6 (176.8) μm (from 484.4 μm at baseline) after a mean of 4.2 injections at Alexandria University, and −194.0 (130.6) μm (from 499.3 μm at baseline) after a mean of 3.5 injections at the Hamad Medical Corporation.

Adverse events

No AEs were reported at any of the study sites. This included no ocular AEs of interest, namely injection-related AEs, intra-ocular inflammation, retinal detachment, endophthalmitis, vitreous hemorrhage, retinal artery occlusion, or significant and rapid worsening of vision or anatomy. No non-ocular AEs, including no systemic thromboembolic events, were observed.

DISCUSSION

This study assessed real-world outcomes of treatment-naïve patients with DME receiving faricimab in clinical practice in the Middle East. The objectives were to assess the effectiveness and safety of faricimab and to provide insights into the treatment’s implementation in underrepresented real-world settings, where patients are likely to represent heterogeneous patient populations not captured by RCTs. Treatment protocols employed at the included study sites differed in T&E versus PRN regimen use, number of loading doses, and length of follow-up. Improvements from baseline in VA were observed at all included sites, with mean logMAR reductions ranging from −0.16 to −0.49. Reductions in retinal thickness were also observed, with mean reductions in CST ranging from −126.3 μm to −194.0 μm at four sites, despite differences in treatment protocols and the length of follow-up. While improvements in retinal thickness were observed at Dallah Hospital, the mean CST reduction of −55.3 μm was comparatively modest. This may be attributable to the chronicity of DME and late presentation of DME in most patients from this site. Altogether, the results demonstrate the effectiveness of faricimab in reducing disease activity, including improvement of visual and anatomical outcomes, in real-world clinical practice in the Middle East. No AEs were reported at any site during this study, including the ocular AEs of interest. While this should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size, retrospective nature of data collection, and the possibility of different AE reporting protocols across sites, these results support the favorable safety profile of faricimab and mirror findings from the wider literature, including in clinical trials and other real-world studies from different regions.[819202122]

In our study, different treatment protocols were followed across sites, with heterogeneous periods of follow-up. Furthermore, at clinician discretion, most eyes received fewer than the recommended four loading doses.[1617] This reflects real-world clinical practice where recommended protocols are not always adhered to, compared to RCTs where protocols are rigid and closely monitored. Moreover, many patients did not receive additional faricimab doses after the loading phase. While this resulted from adherence issues in some instances at the Dallah Hospital and Al Mashreq Center, the real-world clinical observations of the investigators at all five sites indicated that most patients stopped treatment after demonstrating clinically satisfactory improvements in VA and/or retinal thickness as judged by the treating physician, precluding the need for further injections. Our results are the first to demonstrate the real-world effectiveness of faricimab for treatment-naïve patients with DME across multiple sites in the Middle East, and align closely with other published real-world data for faricimab, including a short-term, single-site real-world study in a similar patient group in Abu Dhabi.[23] Furthermore, published data from various regions worldwide, including real-world studies of faricimab in clinical practice in India, the US, the UK, and Italy, reported similar VA gains and anatomical improvements.[24252627] For example, in a retrospective observational study conducted at four tertiary eye care centers in India, the mean change in best-corrected VA (logMAR score) was −0.21 after 6 months of faricimab treatment in 16 treatment-naïve eyes, and central macular thickness reduced by −121.0 μm at 6 months (from 424.8 [SD: 38.6] μm at baseline), similar to results observed in our study.[27] The present study, which included only treatment-naïve patients, also builds on previous evidence showing the effectiveness of faricimab for patients in the Middle East with DME who are not treatment-naïve: a prior study among 100 aflibercept-refractory patients in the United Arab Emirates who switched to faricimab treatment demonstrated a meaningful improvement in VA (+5 Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study letters) and reduction in CST (−67.9 μm) after 6 months of follow-up.[28]

Although longer follow-up is required to establish whether the durability of faricimab’s efficacy seen in clinical trials is fully reflected in real-world settings, the results demonstrate similar trends for improved outcomes with faricimab treatment as seen through to 2 years in the YOSEMITE and RHINE RCTs.[8] Real-world evidence is essential for assessing treatment effectiveness within actual clinical practice, which often does not correspond with observations from RCTs. Given that real-world outcomes often differ from those seen in RCTs, the effectiveness of faricimab generally observed in these five real-world patient cohorts is particularly encouraging.[29] While further real-world studies carried out in other regions have noted the general consistency of trends across real-world faricimab treatment settings,[203031] more data are required to establish the applicability of these findings beyond the Middle East.

Strengths/limitations

The main strength of this study is that the data are more representative of real-world clinical practice compared with RCTs, with detailed site-level outcomes demonstrating the effectiveness of faricimab in cohorts from Egypt, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia. However, several study design limitations inherent to real-world studies impact our ability to draw robust comparisons and conclusions from the data. Sample sizes were small at each site, limiting power for statistical testing and limiting the interpretation of effectiveness and safety outcome data. Additionally, the variation in patient populations, treatment regimens (e.g. T&E versus PRN, and variations of these protocols), inclusion and exclusion criteria, outcome assessment methods, and durations of follow-up impacts the interpretation of these outcomes, as well as the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy and durability of faricimab treatment at these sites. Data were collected retrospectively, which could introduce selection bias and reduce outcome validity. Altogether, these factors also limit our ability to confidently generalize these findings to broader Middle Eastern populations.

Due to the small sample sizes, retrospective data collection, and differences in AE reporting protocols between sites (including AE assessment timepoints), the ability to reliably detect uncommon, minor, transient, or delayed complications is limited in this study. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of safety, and larger prospective studies with standardized AE monitoring are warranted.

Finally, due to data heterogeneity, it was not possible to effectively obtain pooled estimates, compare outcomes, or perform any statistical comparisons across sites, meaning the statistical significance of the observed changes could not be assessed. As a result, only a narrative synthesis of observations across sites is presented.

CONCLUSION

These first real-world data for faricimab in treatment-naïve patients with DME across multiple sites in the Middle East demonstrate the benefits of faricimab in this disease, reflecting observations from other regions as well as RCTs. These results highlight the importance of real-world data, providing insight into clinical practice which cannot be obtained from RCTs, particularly for aspects such as variations in treatment protocols. Further real-world studies across varied geographies are needed to reinforce the effectiveness and safety of faricimab in clinical practice in larger cohorts, and to assess whether outcomes are maintained in the long-term.
