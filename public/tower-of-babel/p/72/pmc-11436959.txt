Defect related anomalous mobility of small polarons in dielectric oxides at the example of congruent lithium niobate
Authors: Anton Pfannstiel, Mirco Imlau, Marco Bazzan, Laura Vittadello
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Polarons play a major role in the description of optical, electrical and dielectrical properties of several ferroelectric oxides. The motion of those particles occurs by elementary hops among the material lattice sites. In order to compute macroscopic transport parameters such as charge mobility, normal (i.e. Fickian) diffusion laws are generally assumed. In this paper we show that when defect states able to trap the polarons for long times are considered, significant deviations from the normal diffusion behaviour arise. As an example of this behavior, we consider here the case of lithium niobate (LN). This can be considered as a prototypical system, having a rich landscape of interacting polaron types and for which a significant wealth of information is available in literature. Our analysis considers the case of a stoichiometric, defect-free lithium niobate containing a certain concentration of small electron polarons hopping on regular Nb sites, and compares it to the material in congruent composition, which is generally found in real-life applications and which is characterized by a large concentration of antisite NbLi defects. While in the first case the charge carriers are free polarons hopping on a regular Nb sublattice, in the second case a fraction of polarons is trapped on antisite defects. Thus, in the congruent material, a range of different hopping possibilities arises, depending on the type of starting and destination sites. We develop a formalism encompassing all these microscopic processes in the framework of a switching diffusion model which can be well approximated by a mobile–immobile transport model providing explicit expressions for the polaron mobility. Finally, starting from the Marcus–Holstein’s model for the polaron hopping frequency we verify by means of a Monte Carlo approach the diffusion/mobility of the different polarons species showing that, while free polarons obey the laws for normal diffusion as expected, bound polarons follow an anomalous diffusion behaviour and that in the case of the congruent crystal where mixed free and bound polaron transport is involved, our expressions indeed provide a satisfactory description.

Introduction

In the last decades it became apparent that the charge transport in a number of polar materials must be understood in terms of polaron motion. These are quasi-particles made up of an electrical charge that, by interaction with the crystalline environment, is able to distort the local lattice creating a local potential well: as a net result, the particle becomes self-localized1. Polarons play a major role when it comes to the interpretation of the optical, electrical or dielectrical properties2 of important technological materials such as LiNbO33,4, KNbO35,6, LiTaO37,8, BaTiO39,10, PbTiO311, r-TiO212,13, CeO214,15 and solid solutions of the above like LiNb1−xTaxO316 or KTa1−xNbxO317. In these polar materials, the electron–phonon coupling is so strong that the exceeding charge is localized on a single lattice site, i.e. only small strong—coupling polarons are formed. These polarons can move in the lattice via random hopping mechanisms in response to the lattice thermal motion, as described by the Marcus–Holstein theory18. In standard formulations used to compute the drift mobility of the polaronic carriers, it is assumed that the latter hop on a regular lattice of spacing d between nearest-neighboring sites. Thus the mobility of the polarons follows the familiar law \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu \propto ed^{2}w/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$$\end{document}μ∝ed2w/(kBT)19,20.

However, the Marcus–Holstein model forecasts an exponential distance-dependent hopping frequency: depending on the characteristic hopping length, the hopping process may involve also far away hopping sites at a distance bigger than d. Even more daunting is the fact that real polar materials are often characterized by the presence of a considerable amount of point defects in the lattice. Due to extra Coulomb interaction, they can act as privileged localization centers for the polarons. When sitting on those sites, the hopping frequency at a given temperature may differ of several orders of magnitude with respect to free polarons moving on regular sites. Thus different types of polarons may exist in a given material and their interplay can result in a rich and complex behaviour, like the formation of polarons bound to defects (bound polarons), polaron complexes made up of a regular and a bound polaron (bipolarons)21,22 or a hole polaron and an electron polaron (self-trapped excitons)23. In recent works4,24–26 it has been shown that the relative weight of the different microscopic hopping processes is strongly dependent on the temperature and on the defect concentration.

The goal of the present work is to provide a novel formulation for the calculation of the polaron mobility taking into account the complications induced by the presence of defects and of the different types of polarons arising from them. This new formulation is based on the fractional differential approach which revealed already successful in the description of the semiconductor transport properties27,28. As a case study, we will consider LiNbO3 (LN). This is a technologically important material widely used in a great variety of applications ranging from electro-optic modulators, waveguides29, nonlinear optical devices, photorefractive holography and, more recently, for novel applications such as the manipulation of small organic30 and inorganic objects31, as substrate for photorefractive tweezers32 and as a platform for microfluidic chips33–36. The structure of stoichiometric LN is constituted by a quasi-cubic Nb sublattice intercalated by Li and O atoms. In an ideal defect-free material (stoichiometric LN, sLN) polarons can only form at the regular Nb sites \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Nb}}_{\mathrm{Nb}}^{5+}$$\end{document}NbNb5+ ions and are indicated as F-polarons. However, LN is normally produced at the congruent composition (cLN) which is characterized by a high concentration (typically, up to 1 mol%) of substitutional “antisite” defects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Nb}}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{5+}$$\end{document}NbLi5+37. These defects constitute the preferential sites for the formation of polarons indicated as bound polarons (B-polarons). While further polaron types and localization sites exist, they contribute to a lesser extent to the transport of electrons and are thus omitted in this work. In LN the relevant microscopic parameters necessary to compute the Marcus–Holstein hopping frequency are known from previous studies3,4,25, making it an excellent test case. Our approach can anyway be adapted to different materials.

Bound polaron hopping

Congruent lithium niobate is the most commonly encountered composition of this material, since it is the energetically most stable and the one that is most easily grown. It contains about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[{\text{Nb}}_{\mathrm{Li}}] = 19.09 \times 10^{25}\,{\text{m}}^{-3}$$\end{document}[NbLi]=19.09×1025m-3 antisite defects37. Those antisites are localization centers for B-polaron formation. As it was reported in several recent papers4,25,26, for sufficiently low temperatures and congruent composition, the main channel for polaron transport is direct hopping among the antisites which can be viewed collectively as the sites of a disordered lattice. Due to this increased disorder level and to the exponential dependence of the hopping frequency, the Central Limit Theorem is no longer applicable to the distribution of the waiting times and the normal diffusion theory breaks down. The theoretical framework to deal with this problem is the Continuous Time Random Walk (CTRW) model38 in which a particle, after hopping to a given site, waits a certain time before moving again. Due to the use of the Marcus–Holstein hopping frequency (see Eq. 1a) we are in a situation in which the characteristic waiting time diverges while the jump length variance remains finite. For this situation, Fick’s second law (Eq. 2) is replaced by a more general equation governing the diffusion. Free and bound mixed hopping

The most general situation, which is also the one most often encountered in experiments at room temperature with congruent LN is the one where both bound and free polarons contribute to the transport. In this case two more elementary hopping processes besides FF and BB hopping are involved in the transport, i.e. BF and FB. The idea here is to relate the two transport modes detailed in “Free polaron hopping” and “Bound polaron hopping” sections via some coupled transport models.

Mobile–immobile model

The previous treatment allows for an exact numerical solution of the problem, but does not have an analytical solution. Since the hopping frequency of bound polarons for all the processes considered here is several orders of magnitude smaller than the one of free polarons, we expect that a good approximation of the previous situation may be provided by a mobile–immobile diffusion model (MIM)44 which on the contrary is analytically solvable. This is known in the semiconductor physics or in amorphous solids as multi-trapping model45,46 whose connection with the MIM model has been extensively discussed47,48. Monte Carlo simulations of polaron hopping transport in LN

Monte Carlo (MC) simulations have proven themselves a valuable tool in modelling polaron transport in LN4,25. A dedicated Monte Carlo algorithm is developed to study the material transport properties using the same methodology detailed in the cited papers. The code generates a stoichiometric LiNbO3 structure with periodic boundary conditions and subsequently a certain number of NbLi antisites is randomly placed in the structure in the appropriate lattice sites, according to the crystal composition.

The hopping frequencies for a given initial site i towards a destination f included in a suitable volume around the starting site are computed by Eq. (1a) using the values reported in Table 13,4,25. The final destination is chosen via the Gillespie algorithm. The time needed to perform the hop is then recorded and the process is repeated until a given time has elapsed. A new polaron is then created and the whole simulation iterated until a satisfactory statistics has been achieved. For each run, the program records the final polaron positions with respect to its original site, the number of different sites encountered during its walk and the time needed to reach the final site. All the information is then analyzed to compute the variance and the mean displacement of the final polaron distribution as a function of time. Congruent LN

In this section, the real-life case of a cLN crystal is considered. As detailed in the “Introduction”, this material is characterized by a high concentration of intrinsic defects, namely Li vacancies (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{Li}}$$\end{document}VLi) and Nb antisites (NbLi). In this case polarons do not only hop among alike sites, but conversion processes where a free polaron is captured by an antisite defect becoming a bound one and vice-versa are present, mixing up the transport processes described in previous sections. The relative weight of these processes depends not only on the sample composition, but also on temperature4 as the activation energies for the various processes are different, as per Eq. (1). Two situations may be considered depending whether the initial delta-like polaron distribution is assumed to be in the free polaron state or in the bound one. This is very much dependent on the experimental conditions and on the time—scale of the experiment one is looking at. In the following both situations are simulated and discussed focusing the attention to the case of a congruent LN crystal with an antisite concentration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[{\text{Nb}}_{\mathrm{Li}}]=1.9 \times 10^{20}\, {\text{cm}}^{-3}$$\end{document}[NbLi]=1.9×1020cm-3 at room temperature.

Discussion

In the case of defect-free lithium niobate the polaron diffusion and mobility are normal, as expected from theoretical models and verified by Monte-Carlo simulations. The polaron mobility is thermally activated with an energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{\text{FF}} = 0.27$$\end{document}UFF=0.27 eV and has a value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _\mathrm{FF} = 7.3 \times 10^{-6} {\mathrm{cm}}^2$$\end{document}μFF=7.3×10-6cm2/(Vs) at room temperature, corresponding to a diffusion coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_\mathrm{FF} = 1.89 \times 10^{9} \,{\text{\AA }}^{2}$$\end{document}DFF=1.89×109Å2/s. When the more realistic situation of a non-stoichiometric lithium niobate sample is considered, the phenomenology becomes significantly richer. The system can be well described by a switching diffusion model, embodied by Eqs. (12b) which can in principle be solved numerically, but for which the rate constants and the anomalous coefficients parameters are difficult to compute analytically. Our MC simulations provide for these quantities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 0.72 \pm 0.01$$\end{document}α=0.72±0.01 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{D}}_{\mathrm{BB}} = (7.1 \pm 0.4) \times 10^{3} \, \text{\AA }^{2}/{\mathrm{s}}^{\alpha }$$\end{document}DBB=(7.1±0.4)×103Å2/sα. The value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α indicates that the bound polarons are sub-diffusing. Simulations at different temperatures, not discussed here, confirm that this value is temperature independent, as expected from the fact that in Eq. (1a) the spatial and temperature dependencies are factorized. Instead, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α is determined by the average distance between the antisite defects and on the orbital parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{\mathrm{BB}}$$\end{document}aBB. While the second parameter is an intrinsic characteristic of the material which is dictated by the localization of the electron wavefunction at a polaronic site, the distance between antisites is ultimately determined by the defect concentration. Thus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α is directly affected by the material stoichiometry, and our result is specific for the often encountered congruent composition. Other stoichiometries would have a different \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α value, but this investigation is considered beyond the scope of the present paper. However we showed that for a LN sample in standard conditions, i.e at room temperature and congruent composition (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${[\text{Nb}}_{\mathrm{Li}}] = 1.9 \times 10^{20} {\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}$$\end{document}[NbLi]=1.9×1020cm-3) the influence of direct \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{B} \rightarrow \mathrm{B}$$\end{document}B→B hops is generally small, therefore the system can be described with a good accuracy in terms of a mobile–immobile model for which the polaron diffusion and mobility are described by Eqs. (15a), (15b) and (16). These equations show that the polarons behave differently depending on the experimental situation and on the time scale considered, as detailed below. If the reduction degree is not too high, charge transport can be attributed to small bound polarons55 which are much more abundant in the lattice than free polarons. Conclusions

The motion of polarons, described by a random hopping process among the sites of a polar crystal is the underlying physical process establishing the electrical properties of the material, which are of paramount importance for several applications. In this work the role of defects on polaron diffusion and mobility has been explored theoretically for the specific case of lithium niobate. We have shown that the presence of defects requires a significant revision of the theoretical framework needed to describe the polaron motion and obtained realistic estimates of the physical quantities necessary to quantitatively describe the process. In particular, the presence of intrinsic defects, as those encountered in the commonly used LN with congruent composition, introduces a time-dependent diffusion and mobility. Depending on the initial sample condition and on the time scale of the experiment one is interested in, several regimes have been evidenced, with the defects affecting the transport behavior to increasing extents. Our theoretical models, supported by MC simulations based on the Marcus–Holstein hopping frequency, are able to describe quantitatively the situation under consideration, providing both formal expressions and numerical parameters for the case of congruent LN at room temperature. In particular, the Mobile - Immobile description provides reliable and simple analytical expressions for diffusion and mobility in a time range from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-8}$$\end{document}10-8 s up to the seconds and beyond.

These expressions should replace the ones used in general formulations for diffusion and mobility whenever non-stoichiometric LN is considered. Our formalism can be readily extended to other temperatures, provided that the rate constants entering Eq. (12b) are known. Furthermore, our approach may be extended to include additional defects and or dopants with the capability to affect the polaron hopping process, such as Fe traps in the case of Fe-doped lithium niobate or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {Ta_{Nb}}$$\end{document}TaNb substitutional defects as in the mixed lithium niobate—tantalate system.
