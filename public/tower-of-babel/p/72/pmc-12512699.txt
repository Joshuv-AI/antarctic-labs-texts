Total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis versus subtotal colectomy with cecal-rectal anastomosis for slow transit constipation: protocol for a multicenter randomized controlled trial (STOPS trial)

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConstipation, a prevalent gastrointestinal disorder, affects 10%–15% of adults in the USA and approximately 8.2% of China’s general population. Slow transit constipation (STC), accounting for 15%–42% of constipation cases, is defined by impaired colonic motility. For patients refractory to conservative treatment who experience chronic, intractable symptoms and diminished quality of life, surgical intervention becomes the treatment of last resort. The primary surgical approach for STC has traditionally been total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis (TC-IRA). However, over the past two decades, subtotal colectomy with cecorectal anastomosis (SC-CRA) has gained increasing attention among clinicians due to its potential to reduce postoperative diarrhea. Despite this advantage, SC-CRA is also associated with concerns regarding an increased risk of recurrent constipation. The debate surrounding which approach is superior persists. This study aims to resolve this controversy through a comparative analysis of TC-IRA and SC-CRA, evaluating their therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles in refractory STC.MethodsThe STOPS trial is a prospective, randomized, multicenter-controlled clinical trial to compare the effectiveness of TC-IRA versus SC-CRA in treating patients with STC. After screening for eligibility and obtaining informed consent, a total of 252 adult patients will be randomized into two groups in a 1:1 ratio after assuring that none of the exclusion criteria is present. The primary outcome is the Wexner Constipation Score. The secondary outcomes include operative time, conversion rates, blood loss, short- and long-term complications, bowel movements, abdominal pain, bloating, straining, enema use, laxative use, Wexner Incontinence Score, gastrointestinal quality of life index (GIQLI), 36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), and satisfaction. Outcomes will be assessed at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months postoperatively.DiscussionThis trial will be the first and largest randomized clinical trial to assess whether TC-IRA or SC-CRA should be the standardized surgical treatment for STC in terms of WCS outcomes. We hope that its findings will not only demonstrate the value of the surgical procedure in the treatment of STC, but also provide more reliable clinical evidence for further standardization of STC surgical treatment.Trial registrationSTOPS Trial: A Multicentre Prospective Randomised Clinical Trial Comparing Total Colectomy with Ileorectal Anastomosis Versus Subtotal Colectomy with Cecal-rectal Anastomosis for Slow Transit Constipation (http://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT05352074). Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Constipation affects 10%–15% of adults globally and 8.2% of China’s population [1, 2]. Slow transit constipation (STC), accounting for 15%–42% of cases, involves impaired colonic motility despite a morphologically normal colon and rectum [3]. Surgical intervention is considered for patients unresponsive to conservative therapies [4].

The surgical management of constipation has evolved significantly since the first documented colectomy efficacy in 1908 [5]. Over time, multiple types of colectomy have emerged: total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis(TC-IRA) [6], subtotal colectomy with cecorectal anastomosis(SC-CRA) [7], colonic exclusion [8], antegrade enemas (the Malone procedure) [9], modified Duhamel surgery [10], and permanent ileostomy [11]. Among these interventions, the main surgical procedures for STC are TC-IRA and SC-CRA [12].

TC-IRA, which involves complete resection of the colon with terminal ileum-to-proximal rectal anastomosis, remains the most widely adopted surgical approach for STC [12]. By removing the slow-transit colon, the transportation time of intestinal contents is shortened, which leads to more liquid feces filling the rectum and results in significant relief of constipation-related symptoms [13]. Although most patients showed immediate constipation symptoms relief following the TC-IRA procedure, an increased incidence of diarrhea, abdominal pain, fecal incontinence, and intestinal obstruction was observed postoperatively [14].

Over the past two decades, SC-CRA has garnered significant clinical interest due to its potential to reduce postoperative diarrhea [12]. SC-CRA employs the direct anastomosis of the cecum with the rectal stump, thus preserving the ileocecal valve functionality [7]. With the retention of the ileocecal valve, the theoretical advantages of control in ileum content into the large bowel and preservation of absorptive functions (bile, vitamin B12 and electrolytes) may contribute to relieve diarrhea [15]. However, potential drawbacks, such as a notable incidence of postoperative constipation recurrence, cannot be overlooked when considering SC-CRA as a treatment option [3, 5, 16].

While TC-IRA remains the primary surgical approach, SC-CRA has emerged as an alternative to reduce postoperative diarrhea. However, concerns about constipation recurrence persist [12, 14]. Reviewing the literature, most studies on TC-IRA and SC-CRA are small-scale and retrospective [6, 12, 15, 17]. This trial aims to establish the optimal surgical approach for STC.

Objectives {7}

The current study is to evaluate whether SC-CRA is non-inferior to TC-IRA in treating STC, as measured by postoperative defecatory symptoms and quality of life.

Trial design {8}

The STOPS trial is a multicentre, prospective, randomized, controlled clinical trial conducted across 14 tertiary hospitals in China. Randomization (1:1 ratio) will be stratified by center using permuted blocks. The study has been approved by ethics committees and registered at clinicaltrials.gov (Registration number: NCT05352074). The protocol for this trial is reported based on the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 Checklist [18]: defining standard protocol items for clinical trials. The eligible patients will be randomly divided into two groups, and participants will receive a 1-, 3-, 6-, 12-, 24-, and 36-month follow-up after surgery. Outcomes will be assessed at baseline, during the treatment, and at the end of the follow-up. Figure 1 illustrates the flow diagram of the trial.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

The most widely used surgical procedure for slow-transit constipation (STC) in current clinical practice is total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis (TC-IRA), which serves as an appropriate control group. By comparing subtotal colectomy (STC) with the current mainstream total colectomy approach, this study aims to determine whether subtotal colectomy represents a more suitable surgical intervention for slow-transit constipation.

Intervention description {11a}

Standard perioperative management in both groups

The perioperative management implemented based on enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol used in the clinic. Bowel preparation will be conducted by polyethylene glycol for all patients on the day before surgery according to local standards. Early mobilization and early oral liquid intake after surgery will be performed by postoperative care. Daily ward rounds with clinical assessment by surgeons will be routinely included in postoperative clinical management, and routine blood tests will be usually performed on postoperative days 1, 3, and 5 and thereafter on demand based on the clinical course.

Standard surgical technique performed in both groups

The patients will be placed in the modified lithotomy position under general anesthesia. The primary trocar with a 12 mm port will be inserted to gain access to the peritoneal cavity and establish the pneumoperitoneum below the umbilicus for the 30° laparoscope. Two 12 mm ports will be placed in the right iliac fossa and in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen. The other 5 mm ones will be located in the right upper quadrant and left lower quadrant. Mobilization of colonic segments with harmonic system will be started at the ileocecal junction, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and transverse colon. Next, the descending colon, splenic flexure, and sigmoid colon will be mobilized, and the omentum will be preserved carefully. Finally, the upper part of the rectum will be mobilized and transected by a 60-mm linear stapler (below the level of the sacral promontory).

Experimental intervention (SC-CRA)

Following complete colonic mobilization with preservation of the ileocolic vascular pedicle and its branches, the surgical specimen was extracted by extending the right lower quadrant trocar incision to 4–5 cm. After insertion of the anvil from a 29-mm circular stapler through the ascending colon resection margin, a resection about 3 cm distal to the ileocecal junction will be conducted. The cecum was then positioned in the pelvis without rotational torsion, and an antiperistaltic cecorectal anastomosis was created between cecal fundus (after appendectomy) and the rectal stump (Fig. 2A). The anastomosis was performed via transanal insertion of the circular stapler to ensure tension-free, contamination-controlled reconstruction. Finally, a closed suction drain was placed in the rectouterine pouch (Douglas pouch), and all abdominal incisions were closed in a layered fashion.

A Resection area (the shadow) of Control group (TC-IRA). B Resection area (the shadow) of Experimental group (SC-CRA)

Control intervention (TC-IRA)

Following complete colonic mobilization without preservation of the ileocolic vascular pedicle, the surgical specimen was extracted by extending the right lower quadrant trocar incision to approximately 4–5 cm. A resection of ileum, 2–3 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction, will be conducted by stapler (Fig. 2B). The anvil of a 29-mm circular stapler was inserted into the proximal ileal lumen and repositioned intra-abdominally. Ileorectal anastomosis was performed by transanal insertion of the circular stapler, aiming to achieve a tension-free, contamination-minimized reconstruction. Finally, a closed suction drain was placed in the rectouterine pouch (Douglas pouch), and all abdominal incisions were closed in layers.

Sample size {14}

Based on discussions with the expert panel and data from published literature, the WCS at 12 months postoperatively for SC-CRA is 4.55 ± 2.04, and for TC-IRA it is 3.99 ± 1.92 [17]. The sample size is calculated according to the non-inferiority study. PASS.15 software is used to compute the sample size with a bilateral test of equivalence. Using α = 0.025, β = 0.2, and Δ = 0.15, each group requires 107 patients, resulting in a total of 214 patients needed for the study. Estimating a 15% dropout rate, a minimum recruitment of 252 subjects is required.

Recruitment {15}

Patients with STC scheduled for colectomy at each trial center will be assessed for eligibility according to the following rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria, and informed about the trial in detail. The principal investigators at each center will obtain informed consent from eligible patients. All patients will voluntarily agree to participate and sign an informed consent document before randomization.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

The allocation sequence will be generated by an independent trial statistician using computerized permuted block randomization with a fixed block size of 4. This process will be implemented through the Statistical Analysis System software (Version 9.2, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), ensuring reproducibility and auditability. Stratification by participating surgical center maintains procedural equity across study sites through predetermined randomization strata.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

A centralized interactive web-response system (IWRS) serves as the allocation concealment mechanism. The IWRS platform automatically executes group assignments only after baseline data verification, preventing prior access to allocation sequences. This real-time validation process ensures irreversible assignment concealment until intervention initiation.

Implementation {16c}

Following informed consent acquisition, study coordinators at each participating site utilize an Interactive Web Response System (IWRS) to obtain randomization assignments and subsequently allocate patients to either the control or intervention arm according to the concealed allocation scheme implemented through this centralized platform, a methodology specifically designed to prevent clinical investigator influence on group assignment while ensuring allocation concealment.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

For obvious reasons, surgeons or patients cannot be blinded to the assigned treatment. Moreover, all outcomes assessed by blinded data collectors were quality of life or functional outcomes, that is, patient-reported outcomes. Therefore, blinding of data collectors or outcome assessors was not reasonable. Outcome statistical analysis will be conducted independently by blinded statisticians.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Not necessary, as there is no blinding.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

The following baseline and demographic parameters will be collected on all patients: age (years), gender (female/male), body mass index (kg/m2), duration of disease (years), previous abdominal surgery, American Society of Anesthesiologists class, bowel movements (frequency/week), abdominal pain, bloating, straining, laxative use, enema use, diarrhea occurrence, and prior diagnostic studies (colonic transit test/colonoscopy/defecography/barium enema/anorectal manometry). Intraoperatively, the following data will be gathered: operation time (min), blood loss (mL), intraoperative blood transfusion (yes/no and number of packed red blood cells), reconstruction technique, and intraoperative complications. Longitudinal data collection for all primary and secondary endpoints will be conducted through a standardized assessment protocol at seven pre-specified timepoints: preoperatively, followed by systematic postoperative evaluations at months 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36, ensuring comprehensive temporal coverage of clinical outcomes throughout the 3-year follow-up period.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

To mitigate the risk of data loss, a dedicated study coordinator has been designated to conduct regular follow-ups through telephone calls, text messages, or alternative communication channels, while simultaneously monitoring participant compliance and providing ongoing support throughout the research period.

Data management {19}

The electronic case report form (eCRF) will be designed to collect all clinical observations of each patient. The investigators are responsible for the completeness, validity, and reliability of data and complete the eCRF as soon as possible after information is collected. An explanation should be given for all missing data. The completed eCRF will be reviewed and signed online by the investigator. At the end of the trial, the electronic data will be locked. All paper documents and electronic data will be retained for 5 years after publication and then destroyed.

To ensure the quality of the trial, a standard operation process, including information on recruitment, randomization, perioperative management, surgical technique, eCRF, the assessment of outcomes, data management, and adverse serious events, will be unified and trained through a multicenter web internal meeting for the consistency of results. Data monitoring will be undertaken periodically by the data monitoring committee (DMC) to identify missing data and potential erroneous data. Data issues will be identified and handled by the management group. In addition, meetings will be held every three months to report on the progress of the trial and each amendment of the protocol conforms to the Good Clinical Practice principles and maintains the ethical standards of the randomized controlled trial.

Confidentiality {27}

Patient identifiable information will be strictly accessible to authorized study physicians, while all collected data will be de-identified and securely stored in password-protected encrypted storage systems with access restricted to authorized study personnel.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

There will be no biological specimens collected in the trial.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Data will be represented as the mean ± SD or median, and analyzed using a parametrical test (Student’s t-test) or non-parametrical test (Wilcoxon rank sum test). Categorical variables will be compared using the χ2 test and reported as proportions (percentage).

The homogeneity of the treatment groups will be described by comparison of the demographic data and the baseline values. Statistical analysis in this trial will be conducted by the Department of Epidemiology affiliated with Army Medical University in China. SPSS 23.0 will be used for data analysis. A two-sided P < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Interim analyses {21b}

No interim analyses are pre-planned.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

Since some patients with STC concomitantly present mild-to-moderate rectal mucosal prolapse (RMP), we will stratify patients into subgroups based on the presence or absence of concomitant mild-to-moderate RMP. This stratification will be applied to both primary and secondary outcome measures to evaluate its impact on surgical therapeutic effects.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

All enrolled patients that underwent one of the two interventions under investigation will be assessed using an intention-to-treat analysis in each group. However, patients that did not undergo surgery at all or a completely different procedure will be excluded from the analysis. Per-protocol analysis will be performed as a secondary analysis. Missing values will be imputed through the multiple imputation method.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data and statistical code {31c}

The full protocol, de-identified datasets, and statistical code may be made available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and appropriate resources after the final publication of results.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5d}

The trial steering committee retains ultimate authority for scientific governance and administrative oversight of the trial. This multidisciplinary body, comprising sponsor representatives, clinical experts, biostatisticians, independent adjudicators, and participating principal investigators (PIs), oversees all aspects of trial design, implementation, analytical methodology, and data interpretation. Its constitution aligns with International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) E6(R2) guidelines for clinical trial oversight.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

The Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) will comprise an independent statistician and domain-specific experts external to the research team, who will conduct periodic data reviews to ensure the reliability of experimental outcomes.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

The standardized Clavien-Dindo classification of surgical complications is applied as a simple, widely adopted, and practical tool to assess and report postoperative complications in general surgery [23]. Adverse events are represented by complications of grades 1–2 according to the Clavien-Dindo classification, whereas grades 3–5 represent serious adverse events. The coordinators are responsible for documenting all adverse events on the Case Report Form (CRF), including information such as the time of occurrence, severity, duration, measurement, management, and outcome. In case of a serious adverse event, the necessary action will be taken immediately for the patient’s safety, and the serious adverse event should be submitted to the Ethics Committees Office and the Department of General Surgery of Army Medical Center within 24 h by the clinician.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

The steering committee will convene annually, the Project Management Committee monthly, and the Data & Safety Monitoring Board quarterly.

Discussion

STC, characterized by colonic slow transit, is one of the most common functional gastrointestinal disorders [3]. Although most patients are treated with conservative medical methods, a minority of patients with intractable STC who have failed long-term non-operative attempts may require surgical interventions [24]. Currently, the most popular surgical procedures for STC include TC-IRA and SC-CRA; however, no standard surgical procedure for STC has been established [12].

TC-IRA is considered to be an effective procedure and widely recommended by many colorectal surgeons [12]. The patients undergoing TC-IRA for STC experience immediate relief of constipation symptoms [25]. Nevertheless, a significant proportion of patients complain about diarrhea (approximately 30%), small intestinal obstruction (10%), and incontinence (20.4%) with detrimental effects on the postoperative quality of life [26–28]. To avoid the consequent postoperative constipations, Sarli et al. reported that the SC-CRA procedure, which preserves the function of the ileocecal junction, may be a better option [7]. Based on the vital role of the terminal ileum in the absorption of electrolytes, bile salts, and vitamin B12, and preventing the backflow by the ileocecal valve, SC-CRA is associated with several homeostatic and metabolic benefits, which improve the diarrhea and incontinence [29].

The unknown etiology of STC, coupled with the absence of specific tests to precisely identify the abnormal colonic segments preoperatively, contributes significantly to the heterogeneity in surgical approaches for STC. As a consequence, we believe that a multicenter randomized controlled clinical trial that assesses both surgeries for STC is needed to identify the standardization of STC surgical treatment.

However, there are still limitations in this trial that deserve particular attention. First, the primary outcome measure of WCS is a subjective rating scale score and therefore does not provide objective evidence to directly support the effectiveness of a particular treatment. Secondly, the BMI of Asian patients is lower than that of Western patients, which may limit the generalizability in this trial.

In conclusion, it will be the first and largest randomized clinical trial to assess whether TC-IRA or SC-CRA should be the standardized surgical treatment for STC in terms of WCS outcomes. We hope that its findings will not only demonstrate the value of the surgical procedure in the treatment of STC, but also provide more reliable clinical evidence for further standardization of STC surgical treatment.
