Second-Level Appeals of Denied Anticancer Medication Claims in Medicare Part D
Authors: Youngmin Kwon, Kenji V. Aoki, Shelley A. Jazowski, Xin Hu, Lindsay M. Sabik, Stacie B. Dusetzina, K. Robin Yabroff, Zhiyuan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat is the rationale behind denials of anticancer medications in Medicare Part D, and what factors drive successful appeals?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 4952 second-level appeals for denied anticancer medications submitted to a Medicare-contracted reviewer, 1 in 5 denials were reversed, with a higher rate of successful appeals for on-label indications.MeaningA considerable number of successful second-level appeals suggests potentially inappropriate utilization management decisions by Part D plans.

Introduction

Insurance coverage denials are commonplace in the US health care system due to its reliance on private managed care organizations to administer health services.1 Although private insurers employ utilization management (UM) tools—such as prior authorization (PA) and formulary restrictions—with the purported goal of facilitating high-value care, these tools have been criticized for imposing administrative obstacles to necessary care.2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9 While growing research has quantified the frequency of denials and their variation across payers and services,3,4,6,10,11 there is only limited research on the specific reasons behind denials, complicating efforts to evaluate the appropriateness and quality of UM decisions. Prior work has shown a high prevalence of claims reversed after further review,12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19 suggesting that managed care plans often make inconsistent and flawed coverage decisions.4,13

The burden of UM is particularly salient in oncology, where UM is disproportionately applied to control access to many high-cost services and treatments.3,4 For anticancer treatments, UM may lead to care delays and discontinuation and financial toxicity2,8,20—all risk factors for adverse clinical outcomes.21,22,23,24 While UM has been ubiquitous for anticancer medications due to their high costs, it has been especially prominent in Medicare Part D because these medications belong to a protected drug class for which broad coverage is required, leaving UM as the primary tool for gatekeeping utilization.2,9 As such, denials of anticancer medications provide an important setting for investigating the quality of coverage denials.

In this study, we analyzed second-level appeals decisions for denied anticancer medications in Medicare Part D. Although they comprise only a subset of all denials (not all denials are appealed) and are not meant to be representative,13 they contain granular decision summaries that can be leveraged to enrich our understanding of the rationale behind denials, especially for claims and beneficiaries that have experienced persistent coverage barriers. To process these summaries, we used a commercially available large language model to perform natural language processing (NLP) and examined the reasons and justifications for denials, as well as the factors associated with appeal outcomes.

Methods

Data and Study Population

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of second-level appeals for denied Part D claims for anticancer medications.25 The Part D appeals process includes 5 levels of review by several entities. Following unfavorable initial coverage determinations by Part D sponsors, beneficiaries may request a redetermination from the plan sponsor (first-level appeal). An unfavorable first-level review can be appealed to a Medicare-approved Qualified Independent Contractor (QIC), which reconsiders the plan sponsor’s decision (see eMethods in Supplement 1 for more details).26 Second-level appeals constitute only a small subset of initial denials. For example, a 2019 report by the Office of Inspector General noted 2.8 million denials in Part D, a quarter of which (n = 744 987) were appealed back to the Part D insurer; of the 201 397 appeals that failed at the first level, 34 697 (17%) were escalated to the second level.13 However, second-level decisions by a QIC carry analytical significance because they provide a more objective signal of the appropriateness of the sponsor’s initial coverage determinations, and, importantly, each decision includes a high-level summary that provides helpful context of the case.

Among the appeals summaries from 2020 to 2024 made publicly available by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, our sample included denied claims for oncology conditions and medications that treat cancer and its treatment-related symptoms, as listed in the Cancer Medications Enquiry Database (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).27 This cross-sectional study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center’s institutional review board because it analyzed publicly available, deidentified data; in accordance with 45 CFR §46, informed consent was waived. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline and the Chatbot Assessment Reporting Tool (CHART) reporting guideline.

Data Extraction Using NLP

We conducted NLP using OpenAI’s large LLM model (ChatGPT, version 5 mini) and its application programming interface to process and classify the appeals decision texts based on standardized, predefined prompts (eTables 2-4 and eMethods in Supplement 1 detail our prompt engineering and query strategy).28,29 ChatGPT is pretrained on open-source web content and datasets, and its neural network is also continuously reinforced with real-time human feedback.30,31 No additional training, validation, or fine-tuning datasets were created for the study, and the existing model parameters were not modified.

We report the aggregated information from the NLP output. First, we characterized reasons for initial denial by Part D plans, which included (1) drugs administratively excluded from Part D benefits and coverage (eg, drugs covered under Part B, reimbursement related issues, and out-of-network coverage), (2) drugs not meeting medically accepted indication (eg, off-label use), (3) requests for formulary exceptions, (4) requests for tiering exceptions, and (5) failure to meet prior authorization coverage criteria (ie, typically on-label therapies that require further documentation of patient treatment candidacy).

Second, we examined the second-level appeals outcome (favorable vs unfavorable review). We interpreted favorable reviews as suggesting potentially inappropriate denials by Part D plans that were subsequently overturned by a QIC. Among unfavorable reviews, we further distinguished whether a QIC’s decision was based on clerical issues (eg, insufficient or inconsistent documentation) vs nonclerical issues (eg, a clinical review of available information).

We analyzed several secondary outcomes among appeals involving redeterminations of medically acceptable indications, which comprised the majority of the sample. First, we examined the source used to determine medically acceptable indications: Medicare-approved drug compendia (summarizing citations from clinical guidelines and peer-reviewed literature32), external citations included separately in appeals, or both. For external citations, we characterized the source journal (reported only for favorable appeals) and the reasons for rejection by QICs (reported only for unfavorable appeals).

Covariates

Covariates included cancer type, treatment type, and decision year. We categorized treatment types using the following Cancer Medications Enquiry Database categories: conventional systemic therapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, hormone therapy, and ancillary therapy (eg, symptom management). Given that medication costs are likely correlated with more aggressive UM, we also characterized the median of average per-claim spending for each medication, as reported in the 2023 Part D spending dashboard.33

Statistical Analysis

We used summary statistics (frequency and percentages) to describe key dimensions of second-level appeals. We also conducted a multivariable linear regression analysis to identify factors associated with the probability of a favorable appeal, which controlled for cancer and treatment type, per-claim drug spending, reasons for initial denial, and decision year. We report both regression-adjusted estimated probabilities (estimated for each level of a covariate, while holding all other variables at their mean values via the delta method) and mean marginal effects (95% CIs) that quantify differences in these probabilities. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Studio, version 3.81 (SAS Institute Inc) and Stata, version 14 (StataCorp LLC). Data were analyzed from March 13, 2025, to May 12, 2026.

To assess the validity of the NLP-based extraction, in sensitivity analyses, we reviewed a random 5% sample of appeals, manually recoded 2 key variables (reasons for initial denial and appeal outcomes), and calculated interrater reliability between NLP-derived and manually coded values using Cohen κ statistic. We also explored the sensitivity of our main finding to the use of the recoded values. Furthermore, we replicated the multivariable regression analysis, excluding 2020 data due to concerns about COVID-19–related disruptions to UM processes.

Results

Appeals Characteristics

Among the 4952 second-level appeals from 2020 to 2024 (Table 1), most were for drugs initially denied for non–medically accepted indications (off-label use: n = 3410 [68.9%]), followed by appeals for formulary exceptions (n = 545 [11.0%]), redetermination of PA coverage criteria (n = 463 [9.3%]), drugs excluded from the Part D benefit (n = 300 [6.1%]), and tiering exceptions (n = 234 [4.7%]). The most common cancer sites represented in appeals were hematological cancers (n = 1155 [23.3%]), prostate cancer (n = 876 [17.7%]), cancers of the digestive system (including colorectal cancer; n = 650 [13.1%]), breast cancer (n = 613 [12.4%]), and lung, bronchus, and respiratory cancers (n = 430 [8.7%]). Most appeals were for targeted (n = 2889 [58.3%]) or hormonal therapy (n = 959 [19.4%]). Appeals for non–medically accepted indications or formulary exceptions were more likely to request coverage of drugs with higher median per-claim spending; conversely, appeals for drugs excluded from the Part D benefit or tiering exceptions were likely to have lower spending.

Appeals Outcome

More than 19.5% (95% CI, 18.4%-20.6%) of second-level appeals received favorable reviews (ie, initial denial by Part D plan was subsequently overturned by a QIC). In addition, 55.2% (95% CI, 53.8%-56.6%) and 25.3% (95% CI, 24.1%-26.5%) failed appeals due to clerical and nonclerical issues, respectively (Figure 1).

Bar Graph Showing Medicare Part D Anticancer Medication Appeals Outcome by Reasons for Initial Denial

The distribution of appeals outcomes by reasons for initial denial and appeal is displayed. A favorable review indicates that a Medicare-contracted reviewer overturned the Part D plan sponsor’s initial decision to deny the appeals (ie, potentially inappropriate denials). We separately identified appeals that received unfavorable reviews due to clerical issues (such as inadequate documentation) and those that received unfavorable reviews despite adequate documentation (not due to clerical issues). PA indicates prior authorization.

Appeals outcomes were heterogeneous by reasons for initial denial. Appeals requesting coverage of drugs excluded from Part D and tiering exceptions almost universally failed (unfavorable review: 89.7% [95% CI, 85.8%-93.6%] and 97.0% [95% CI, 95.1%-98.9%], respectively). Drugs initially denied for failing to meet PA coverage criteria had the highest rate of favorable reviews (61.1% [95% CI, 56.7%-65.6%]). Requests for formulary exceptions were often denied, with only 20.2% (95% CI, 17.0%-23.3%) receiving favorable reviews. Among appeals for drugs initially denied for non–medically accepted indications, 16.5% (95% CI, 15.2%-17.8%) received favorable reviews. They also featured a high rate of unfavorable reviews due to clerical issues (72.6% [95% CI, 71.1%-74.1%), with a QIC often noting failure to include supporting citations from peer-reviewed literature. These patterns were also largely reflected in the multivariable model results (Table 2).

Determination of Medically Accepted Indications

Nearly all appeals for redetermination of medically accepted indications relied on the US Food and Drug Administration indication and/or the Medicare compendium for adjudication (Figure 2). Among favorable reviews, more than 87.9% (95% CI, 86.1%-89.6%) were found to have supporting citations in compendium as per a QIC, and 15.2% (95% CI, 13.3%-17.0%) also included an external citation that was deemed acceptable (top cited journals were Journal of Clinical Oncology, Lancet, and New England Journal of Medicine) (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Furthermore, 8.4% (95% CI, 6.7%-10.0%) of favorable reviews were evaluated based on both the compendium and external citations. In contrast, only 3.3% (95% CI, 2.5%-4.0%) of unfavorable reviews included external citations.

Among unfavorable reviews that included external citations, common reasons why citations were deemed insufficient to demonstrate medically acceptable use by QICs were the following: not representative of the requested drug’s use or the patient’s cancer condition (n = 33 [35.1%]), investigational data (eg, early phase or nonhuman trials; n = 21 [22.3%]), and abstracts only (n = 19 [20.2%]) or abstracts not from an approved journal (n = 18 [19.1%]) (Figure 3).

Sensitivity Analysis

Outcomes derived by the NLP model vs manual coding were largely consistent; the κ statistic was 0.81 (95% CI, 0.74-0.88) for initial reasons for denials and 0.84 (95% CI, 0.78-0.90) for appeals outcome (values >0.7-0.8 are considered acceptable34,35). We observed similar patterns of appeal outcomes across appeal types when using manually coded values (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). The results were robust when excluding 2020 data (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of second-level appeals of denied anticancer medications in Medicare Part D, more than one-fifth of appeals were successfully overturned after reconsideration by a Medicare-contracted reviewer, suggesting a considerable number of potentially inappropriate denials by Part D plans. However, the success of appeals varied by the types of initial denial. Concerningly, the proportion of favorable reviews was highest for drugs likely prescribed on label but initially failing to meet PA coverage criteria (61.1%), highlighting substantial coverage barriers even for clinically appropriate medications. Conversely, the majority of appeals were for off-label or non–medically accepted indications (68.9%), but most denials were upheld, and many cited inadequate supporting documentation (72.6%). This could reflect patterns of care for more complex cases (eg, rarer or treatment-resistant cancers) that may not have a clearly established standard of care, thereby raising concerns about the value of the prescribed medications.

Our findings advance the burgeoning literature on coverage denials in several ways.3,4,6,8,11,12,13,16,36,37,38 First, using NLP, we elucidated the rationale behind denials in the decision summaries, rather than simply quantifying their frequencies. Such information can inform the assessment of whether UM is serving its ostensible goal of facilitating high-value care. Second, we focused on anticancer medications, which are disproportionately subjected to UM by Part D plans.9 Third, we investigated the Medicare appeals process—a critical recourse for beneficiaries seeking coverage after repeated denials by Part D plans38—and identified factors that may contribute to successful appeals. The observed number of unfavorable reviews due to clerical issues (even at the second level) suggests challenges in navigating coverage rules and the appeals process.

We propose several considerations for UM reforms. Our findings support efforts to expedite the review of medications prescribed for US Food and Drug Administration–approved indications. Many appeals in this category involved denials of targeted therapies, for which preapproval criteria often require documentation of genetic and biomarker profiles to assess on-label prescribing. Requiring evidence of on-label prescribing represents a potential target for streamlining the UM process and reducing administrative burden. One way to achieve this would be through an audit-based approach that oversees prescribing practices after medications are dispensed.39 This approach would shift the burden of UM away from prescribers and patients toward payers who may be better resourced and positioned to monitor utilization. Widespread adoption of electronic UM processes (integrated with electronic health records) may facilitate alternative approaches to streamlining approval of on-label prescriptions.40

UM may, instead, be more appropriate for regulating the coverage of off-label therapies, which are an essential tool in oncologic care41,42,43 but raise legitimate concerns about safety and effectiveness.41,44 The Part D program relies on clinical compendia, such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Drugs and Biologics Compendium,45,46 to adjudicate medically acceptable off-label use, but several inefficiencies persist in determining what constitutes acceptable off-label use. For instance, there may be potential disagreements in recommendations across compendia—reflecting variation in included indications, the quality and synthesis of evidence45,46—and delays in listings due to rapidly evolving advances in research of off-label use.45 This lack of uniformity may be problematic because an indication failing to receive recommendations in any one compendium may be grounds for denying coverage.47 Furthermore, the financial costs of subscribing to compendia (and peer-reviewed journals) may lead to inequities in coverage decisions,46,48 in which resource-constrained prescribers may be less able to submit high-quality supporting evidence. To address these drawbacks, further efforts to scrutinize the evidentiary standards underpinning clinical compendia and to improve compendia’s accessibility are warranted.45,46 Conversely, the fact that nearly all favorable reviews (87.9%) of off-label prescribing were for indications supported by a compendium or peer-reviewed evidence may indicate potential errors by Part D plans in their initial determinations of medically acceptable indications. This underscores the need for a thorough review of formularies to ensure concordance with evidence-based care.

The remaining appeals were for drugs statutorily excluded from Part D coverage, suggesting that improving awareness of coverage rules may help reduce unnecessary claims and appeals. One source of confusion regarding the coverage of anticancer medications in Part D may arise because some medications have overlapping coverage between Medicare Part B (covering clinician-administered drugs) and Part D (covering prescription drugs).49,50 These medications include certain oral drugs with direct equivalents to clinician-administered formulations (eg, capecitabine) or home infusions. Correct billing practices at the point of care could have obviated coverage issues affecting these drugs. Moreover, we documented that appeals seeking tiering exceptions often failed (89.7%). Although tiering exceptions are designed to allow beneficiaries to obtain drugs at a lower cost sharing,51 anticancer medications are often placed on specialty tiers, which preclude tiering exceptions. Although these appeals may have been initiated as a last resort to access unaffordable medications, their high rate of rejection suggests that beneficiaries may have borne the burden of the appeals process52 without recourse.

Our results have implications for efforts to streamline the UM process.53,54 Notably, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services have proposed a rule on PA requirements for prescription drugs,55 expanding on a rule that revamped PA for nondrug services in Medicare Advantage and other plans.53 These rules aim to improve the transparency and administrative efficiency of utilization reviews through provisions such as real-time information exchange between health care professionals and payers via electronic systems, public reporting of denial metrics, and a shortened claims review timeline.53 Although much needed, they may not resolve the substantive issues behind denials of specialty drugs that involve specific assessments of medical necessity. Other efforts have focused on tools such as “gold carding,” which waives PA for prescribers with a history of high preapproval rates.56 However, these tools may introduce unintended consequences for cancer care quality,57 as they may penalize clinicians treating complex cancers that are more likely to benefit from nonstandard treatments. It may also exacerbate the uneven burden of PA, where practices with fewer resources dedicated to the appeals process may be more easily targeted as “poor” compliers.58 These points highlight the importance of considering the nuances of various clinical contexts (across different therapeutic conditions and care settings), rather than pursuing a “one-size-fits-all” approach to reforming UM.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, second-level appeals are not representative of all initially denied claims. This selection may have oversampled cases with greater ambiguity regarding medical necessity for denied medications or beneficiaries or prescribers who were able and willing to undergo the appeals process. Second, the use of a pretrained large language model introduces potential biases inherited from its training data and variability in text interpretation. To reduce measurement errors, standardized predefined prompts were applied consistently across all appeal decision texts. In manually reviewing 5% of the data for consistency, we found a high level of congruence between NLP-extracted and manually coded outcomes, and the qualitative conclusions were similar across the 2 approaches. Third, given that we focus on appeals for anticancer medications in Part D, additional analyses of denials in other therapeutic contexts and among other payers can facilitate more generalizable insights. Similarly, future research should investigate how the correlates of appeals outcomes and denials may impact patient-level outcomes to further clarify the clinical appropriateness of UM processes.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of second-level appeals for anticancer medications in Medicare Part D, we observed that one-fifth of appealed first-level denials were overturned and that appeal outcomes varied by the nature of the denials. The preponderance of unfavorable reviews stemming from clerical issues highlighted that appeals were often adjudicated on nonclinical grounds. These findings echo calls to address the administrative burden of UM to ensure it acts as a targeted tool for promoting high-value care rather than putting up administrative obstacles to necessary care.
