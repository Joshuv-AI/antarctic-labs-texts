Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa), the leading cause of cancer-associated death, threatens human life [1, 2]. Given the role of androgen receptor in PCa progression, AR signaling blockade acts as the mainstay of systematic therapy for PCa [3, 4]. However, there is a subgroup of PCa that harbors TP53, a well-known tumor suppressor, mutations [5, 6]. Notably, this subclass of PCa has been reported to exhibit a more aggressive and multiple drug-resistant phenotypes [7–9]. Unfortunately, although efforts to explore the potential targets to improve the outcome of these aggressive subtypes have made marginal progress in recent years [10–12].

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, the most abundant modification in eukaryotic mRNA, is mainly catalyzed by a complex consisted of multiple proteins, with methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) serving as the core catalytic component [13, 14]. METTL3 has been reported to play a vital role in promoting tumorigenesis and drug resistance in numerous cancer types [15–19]. METTL3 is also reported to be aberrantly upregulated in PCa [20]. Notably, targeting METTL3 has been reported to suppress PCa progression and overcome the drug resistance [21, 22]. However, the role of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutations is totally unknown.

Here, we analyze the transcriptome signature of METTL3 complex and employ the novel inhibitor of METTL3 to validate the role of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutation and decipher the potential mechanism of how METTL3 contributes to the malignancy of PCa harboring TP53 mutations.

Materials and methods

Landscape of genetic mutations

RNA-sequencing expression (level 3) profiles, genetic mutation and corresponding clinical information for 498 were downloaded from the TCGA dataset(https://portal.gdc.com). The data of mutations were downloaded and visualized using the maftools package in R software. Genes with higher mutational frequency detected of 498 patients in histogram was showed.

Identification of diffentially expressed genes in TP53-mutated PCa compared to that in TP53-wildtype PCa

RNA-sequencing expression (level 3) profiles and corresponding clinical information for PCaTP53Mut (n = 53) and PCaTP53WT (n = 437) were downloaded from the TCGA dataset(https://portal.gdc.com). Using the limma package in the R software to study the differentially expressed mRNA. “Adjusted P < 0.05 and Log2 (Fold Change) > 0.7 or Log2(Fold Change) < − 0.7” were defined as the threshold for the differential expression of mRNAs.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis

To further confirm the underlying function of potential targets, the data were analyzed by functional enrichment. Gene Ontology (GO) is a widely-used tool for annotating genes with functions, especially molecular function (MF), biological pathways (BP), and cellular components (CC). ClusterProfiler package (version: 3.18.0) in R was employed to analyze the GO function of potential targets. The R software ggplot2 package was used to draw boxplot; the R software pheatmap package was used to draw heatmap.

The expression distribution of m6A in PCaTP53Mut and PCaTP53WT tissues and normal tissues

RNA-sequencing expression (level 3) profiles and corresponding clinical information for PCaTP53Mut (n = 53) and PCaTP53WT (n = 437) were downloaded from the TCGA dataset (https://portal.gdc.com). The m6A-related genes are derived from Juan Xu’s research on the molecular characterization and clinical significance of m6A modulators across 33 cancer types. All analysis methods and R packages were implemented by R foundation (version 4.0.3) the software packages were ggplot2 and pheatmap.

METTL3 expression in PCaTP53Mut and PCaTP53WT tissues and normal tissues

RNA-sequencing expression (level 3) profiles and corresponding clinical information for PCaTP53Mut (n = 53) and PCaTP53WT (n = 437) were downloaded from the TCGA dataset (https://portal.gdc.com). Statistic alanalyses were performed using R software v4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cell culture

Human gastric cancer cell lines, LNCaP and PC-3, were purchased from ATCC and were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (EallBio, # 03.U16001). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a saturated humidity atmosphere containing 95% air and 5% CO2.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were dispensed into 96-well plates after UZH1a (MCE, #HY-134673 A) and incubated with the medium containing 10% Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (ApexBio, #K1018) for 1 hat 37 °C. The OD450 nm value was measured using a microplate reader.

Growth curve

Cells were dispensed into 96-well plates (3000 cells per well) and were treated with DMSO or UZH1a. Cell proliferation was analyzed by Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent at the indicated time points for 2 h of incubation at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at OD 450 nm, and growth curves were generated.

Colony formation assay

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well. Following a 14-day UZH1a treatment, the colonies were stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, #C0121) and analyzed by Image J software.

METTL3 correlated genes inPCaTP53Mut and PCaTP53WT tissues

Based on the median expression, we divided PCaTP53Mut samples into METTL3High and METTL3Low groups. 36 samples were included in METTL3High and 21 samples were included in METTL3High. Similar to this principle, we divided PCaTP53WT samples into METTL3High and METTL3Low groups. Here, 212samples were included in METTL3High and 225 samples were included in METTL3High in PCaWT. Followed differentially expressed genes and GO analysis were conducted as described formerly.

Immunoblotting

For immunoblotting analyses, cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, #P0013C). Using the BCA kit (Yeasen, #20201ES86) following the manufacturer’s instructions, the protein concentration of each sample was determined. Subsequently, equal amounts of protein extracts were separated via electrophoresis on the appropriate Tris-Glycine gel (Yeasen, #36252ES10) and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, #IPVH00010). The primary antibodies listed below were used to probe the membrane, followed by secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP. The resulting immunoblot bands were visualized using an ECL Kit (E412-02, Vazyme, China).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test while differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Genomic mutation profiling in PCa

To decipher the mutational landscape of PCa, we assessed TCGA database and identified that TP53, SPOP, FOXA1, KMT2D, MUC16, SYNE1, SPTA1, KMT2C and LRP1B were the top 10 cumulative mutated genes in PCa (Fig. 1A). Noticeably, C > T was found to be the most common single nucleotide mutation (SNV) in PCa (Fig. 1B). Base alterations in individual sample of the top 10 mutated genes in PCa were summarized and this summarization indicated that the occurrence of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) was much more frequently than insertion (INS) and deletion (DEL) (Fig. 1C–D). Collectively, above analyses are consistent with previous studies, which indicated this dataset is suitable for exploring the putative role of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutation.

Transcriptome signature in PCaTP53mut compared to PCaTP53WT

We next turned to explore the differentially expressed genes in PCa harboring TP53 mutation in comparison with the TP53-wildtype PCa. A total of 205differentially expressed genes were identified (Fig. 2A). Among these differentially expressed genes, 117 were upregulated and 88 were drastically downregulated (Fig. 2B). Pathway analysis of these upregulated genes enriched “extracellular structure organization”, “extracellular matrix organization” and “cell junction assembly” among the most significant categories (Fig. 2C), which consistent with the strong metastatic characteristic of PCa harboring TP53 mutation. In addition, “Negative regulation of growth” was the most significantly enriched when conducting the GO analysis based on these downregulated genes (Fig. 2D). These transcriptome alteration in PCa harboring TP53 mutations consistent with its clinical features, suggesting using these transcriptome signatures is suitable for the further exploration of the expression signature of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutations.

Expression analysis of METTL3 complex in TP53 wildtype and mutated PCa

Based on above analyses, we further explored the expression signature of METTL3 complex members in PCa harboring TP53 mutations. As shown in Fig. 3A, consistent to previous reported, the most members in METTL3 complex were upregulated in PCa samples. Intriguingly, the expression level of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutation was found to be much higher than that in TP53 wildtype PCa (Fig. 3B). Given the crucial role of METTL3 in promoting tumor progression, we hypothesize that PCa harboring TP53 mutation may rely on METTL3 to maintain its malignant phenotype.

METTL3 is a crucial therapeutic target for PCaTP53mut

Fortunately, UZH1a as a novel inhibitor for METTL3 has been developed recently which provides a new choice for cancer treatment [23, 24]. We therefore assessed the IC50 of UZH1a in LNCaP and PC-3, two PCa cell lines harboring TP53 mutations [25] (Fig. 4A–B). Intriguingly, LNCaP was more sensitive to METTL3 inhibitor than PC-3, indicating that METTL3 inhibitor may be more effective in cell with TP53 mutation like Y234H missense mutation than that with TP53 frameshift deletion. Furthermore, we found that pharmaceutical blockade of METTL3 drastically inhibited the proliferation of PCa harboring TP53 mutations (Fig. 4C–D). In addition, long-term clonogenic assay further confirm the anti-growth function of UZH1a (Fig. 4E–F). Collectively, these data suggest that the catalytic activity of METTL3 is essential for maintaining the malignant proliferation of PCa harboring TP53 mutations.

METTL3-mediated activation of MAPK signaling contribute to its pro-tumor role in PCaTP53mut

The pro-proliferation role of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutations inspired us to further explore the underlying mechanism on how METTL3 exerting its functions. According to the expression levels of METTL3, we divided the PCa harboring TP53 mutations into METTL3High and METTL3Low (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, the majority (over 98%) of differently expressed genes in METTL3High group were identified to be upregulated (Fig. 5B). We individually conducted KEGG and GO analyses based these upregulated genes. KEGG reveals that “cGMP-PKG signaling pathway” was one of the most enriched categories in METTL3High from the PCa harboring TP53 mutations (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, “Regulation of GTPase activity” was found to be most significantly enriched GO pathways in METTL3High from the PCa harboring TP53 mutations (Fig. 5D). These two pathways are well-established upstream of MAPK signaling, which is known to promote proliferation across human cancers [26, 27]. Hence, we hypothesized that METTL3 overexpression in PCa harboring TP53 mutations may activate MAPK signaling to maintain its malignant proliferation. Consistent to our hypothesis, UZH1a treatment led to a dramatical repression of ERK and MEK activation (Fig. 5E). Except the knowing upregulated upstream of MAPK, we also notice that IGF1R, a well-established gene directly activating MAPK signaling, was more significantly positively correlated with METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutations (R = 0.36) than that in TP53-wildtype PCa (R = 0.26) (Fig. 5F–G). Of note, in prostate cancer patients with low METTL3 expression and TP53 mutation, the expression level of IGF1R shows no significant difference compared with that in normal tissues (Fig. 5H). However, once the expression of METTL3 increased, the expression level of IGF1R were upregulated significantly (Fig. 5H). These findings demonstrate that METTL3 activates MAPK signaling via activating its multiple upstream signalings. Taken together, our data elucidate that PCa harboring TP53 mutation may rely on METTL3 to keep the sustained activation of MAPK signaling and thereby maintain its malignant phenotype.

Discussion

The role of METTL3 in promoting tumor progression across human cancers has been widely investigated. But, the expression features of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutations are totally unknown. Here, we found that METTL3 expression levels in PCa harboring TP53 mutations were significantly higher than that in tissues derived from PCaTP53 WT and normal.

TP53 mutations confers the features of rapid proliferation and multiple drugs resistance to various cancers including prostate cancer [6, 28]. Cancer cells harbor mutations in anticancer genes which make their survival relied on other genes [29, 30]. Aberrant METTL3 expression has been reported to sustain the rapid growth of human cancers [16, 31–33]. Meanwhile, a novel inhibitor for METTL3 has been developed recently, which shad a light for the novel treatment for human cancers [17, 34]. Given the expression feature of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutations, we therefore hypothesize that METTL3 may be involved in the maintaining of malignant phenotype of PCa with TP53 mutations. Consistent with our hypothesis, our data revealed that pharmaceutical blockade of METTL3 drastically suppressed TP53-mutated cells proliferation, suggesting METTL3 plays a pivotal rolein sustaining malignant proliferation of PCa harboring TP53 mutations. And, targeting METTL3 might be an effective treatment for PCa harboring TP53 mutations. Intriguingly, the binding motifs of TP53 failed to be found in the promoter region of METTL3 and no related studies demonstrate the potential TP53-mediated regulation of METTL3. We hypothesize that the aberrant upregulation of METTL3 in PCa harboring TP53 mutations may result from the potential adaptive molecular alteration.

The mechanism by which METTL3 promotes tumor progression has been more widely reported [22]. However, how METTL3 exerts its tumor-promoting function in PCa harboring TP53 mutations is not yet reported. Here, we found that METTL3 in TP53-mutated PCa specifically activated MAPK signaling cascade to sustain the rapid growth of TP53-mutated PCa. Intriguingly, our analyses revealed that the correlated genes of METTL3 in TP53-wildtype and -mutated PCa were distinctly different. This result suggests a change in preference for METTL3-regulated substrates in PCa harboring TP53 mutations. It indicated an interesting field of research on which molecules mediate above mentioned process.

Unfortunately, due to some limitations, we failed to perform the in vivo study to further support the clinical implication of METTL3 inhibitor in treating PCa harboring TP53 mutations. And, the molecular mechanism by which METTL3 selectively activates the MAPK signaling pathway has also not been clearly elucidated in this study.

In summary, our data achieved the characterization of METTL3 expression in prostate cancers carrying TP53 mutations and demonstrated that METTL3 was crucial for the maintaining of rapid growth of TP53-mutated PCa. We offered a new potential therapeutic strategy for PCa carrying TP53 mutations from targeting METTL3.

Conclusion

METTL3 sustains the malignant proliferation of PCa carrying TP53 mutations via activating MAPK signaling cascade. And, pharmaceutical blockade of METTL3 serves as a potent therapeutic strategy for TP53-mutated PCa.
