Real-Time Intravascular Ultrasound-Guided Percutaneous Coronary Intervention for Spontaneous Coronary Artery Dissection

History of Presentation

A 49-year-old woman presented with a 1-week history of central burning pain in the chest that began while performing a home workout. Over the course of the week, the pain had progressively worsened, occurring with mild exertion such as walking upstairs. Her past medical history included gastroesophageal reflux disease managed with omeprazole 20 mg once daily. She was not taking any other regular medications, including hormonal contraceptives, at the time of presentation. The symptoms were initially attributed to gastroesophageal reflux disease, and the dose of omeprazole was increased to 40 mg once daily without relief. She is a nonsmoker with minimal alcohol consumption and has no traditional cardiovascular risk factors.

Investigations

On presentation to the emergency department, the patient was hemodynamically stable with a blood pressure of 140/80 mm Hg, heart rate of 106 beats/min, and oxygen saturation of 100% on room air. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. Initial electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with T-wave inversions in leads V3 and V4 (Figure 1). Complete blood count, urea, and electrolytes were normal. Serial high-sensitivity troponin I levels were normal (<4 ng/L), and D-dimer was negative (<150 ng/mL). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular and right ventricular size and systolic function with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%, no regional wall motion abnormalities, and no valvular pathology. Unstable angina was diagnosed, and aspirin 75 mg once daily and ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily were initiated. Coronary computed tomography (CT) angiography revealed severe luminal narrowing of a dominant proximal right coronary artery (RCA) in the absence of appreciable atherosclerotic disease elsewhere, most likely consistent with spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) (Figure 2).

Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) demonstrated severe luminal narrowing of a dominant proximal right coronary artery, suggestive of spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD). The imaging features supporting SCAD rather than atherosclerotic narrowing include a smooth, tapered luminal narrowing without eccentric calcification or discrete atheromatous plaque and the complete absence of atheromatous disease elsewhere in the coronary tree.

Management

Invasive coronary angiography was subsequently undertaken from the right radial artery using a 6-F sheath. The left coronary system was engaged first with a 6-F JL3.5 diagnostic catheter and showed normal left anterior descending (LAD) and left circumflex arteries. The RCA was then engaged with a 6-F JR4.0 guide catheter. Initial angiography demonstrated a dominant RCA with subtotal occlusion of the proximal segment and TIMI flow grade 2 (Video 1), consistent with the coronary CT angiography findings of type 3 SCAD, given the absence of coronary atheroma elsewhere (Figure 3).

Invasive Coronary Angiography Showing Normal Left Anterior Descending and Left Circumflex Arteries With No Atheroma

Coronary angiography of the right coronary artery (RCA) demonstrates subtotal occlusion of the proximal RCA with TIMI flow grade 2, consistent with type 3 spontaneous coronary artery dissection. LCA = left coronary artery.

We decided to perform percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for the SCAD in view of its proximal location, causing flow limitation and ongoing chest pain symptoms. A Runthrough (Terumo) workhorse guidewire was advanced into the conus branch, and an intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) catheter (OptiCross HD 60 MHz; Boston Scientific) was tracked over this wire into the conus to obtain a real-time, 360-degree cross-sectional view of the proximal RCA from the side branch and delineate the true and false lumens of the SCAD (Figure 4, Video 2). A second Runthrough workhorse guidewire was then advanced into the RCA true lumen under continuous IVUS guidance from the side branch, avoiding false lumen entry and minimizing the risk of dissection propagation (Figure 5, Video 3). The IVUS catheter was subsequently advanced over the second guidewire to confirm the true lumen wire position within the RCA (Figure 6). Direct stenting was deliberately performed without balloon pre-dilation to mitigate the risk of "toothpasting" and propagation of the intramural hematoma. A 3.5 × 24 mm BioMatrix (Biosensors International) drug-eluting stent was deployed at 8 atm from the proximal RCA to the ostium, deliberately crossing the conus branch and extending to the aorto-ostium of the RCA to prevent proximal propagation of the intramural hematoma into the aortic sinus, followed by post-dilation with a 3.5-mm noncompliant balloon. Coronary angiography following stent deployment showed TIMI flow grade 3 in a dominant RCA (Video 4).

Of note, the 6-F guide catheter could not accommodate both the IVUS catheter and the stent delivery system simultaneously. Real-time IVUS guidance (RTIG) in this case was therefore used solely to facilitate true lumen wiring of the proximal RCA and was not employed during stent deployment to optimize stent position at the aorto-ostium. Final IVUS assessment confirmed covering of the ostium, and good stent expansion and apposition with a minimum stent area >8 mm2 and no distal propagation of the hematoma (Figure 7, Video 5).

Follow-Up

The patient was discharged in a stable condition on dual antiplatelet therapy and bisoprolol 2.5 mg once daily, given the emerging evidence of beta blockers in reducing SCAD recurrence. She was referred for outpatient follow-up for symptom assessment and imaging screening for fibromuscular dysplasia and evidence of SCAD elsewhere.

Discussion

SCAD is an increasingly recognized cause of acute coronary syndrome, particularly among younger and middle-aged women without traditional cardiovascular risk factors. The condition is characterized by the formation of an intramural hematoma or intimal tear within the coronary arterial wall, leading to compression of the true lumen and impaired coronary blood flow. SCAD most commonly affects the LAD artery and its branches, followed by the LCX and RCA and their respective branches.1 Some investigators propose that disruption of the vasa vasorum within the vessel wall is the origin to the intramural hematoma or false lumen. In most cases, however, the mechanism is an intimal disruption that allows blood to enter and propagate along the arterial wall. Predisposing conditions include fibromuscular dysplasia, hormonal influences, pregnancy, connective tissue disorders, and extreme physical or emotional stress.2

Diagnosis of SCAD is often challenging, particularly in patients with atypical symptoms or nondiagnostic biomarkers, as demonstrated in this case. Coronary angiography remains the first-line diagnostic modality, although angiographic findings may be subtle or mimic atherosclerotic disease.

Coronary CT angiography has an emerging role in the diagnosis of SCAD, being most valuable in cases with atypical clinical presentation. Several case series have shown good sensitivity for detecting SCAD at least in the proximal vessels, with potentially more specificity than invasive coronary angiography.3

Saw4 classified SCAD into 4 types. Type 1 manifests as a radiolucent flap and linear double lumen with contrast hang. Type 2 manifests as long diffuse and smooth stenosis in mid-distal segments. Our case resembles type 3, which manifests as a focal stenosis mimicking a focal atherosclerotic lesion and requires confirmation by intracoronary imaging. Type 4 is defined as a total vessel occlusion most likely distally. Adjunctive intracoronary imaging with IVUS or optical coherence tomography is frequently required to confirm the diagnosis by demonstrating intramural hematoma, intimal disruption, and true-false lumen separation. Specific caution is warranted with intravascular imaging in SCAD because of high risks of manipulating the dissected coronary artery, particularly with contrast injections required for optical coherence tomography and the potential for hydraulic spread of dissection. IVUS is therefore the preferred imaging modality in SCAD because of its ability to provide real-time imaging without contrast injection.5

Current consensus and observational data support conservative management as the preferred strategy in clinically stable patients, as spontaneous healing of the vessel occurs in the majority of cases.5 However, revascularization is indicated in high-risk situations, including ongoing or recurrent ischemia, hemodynamic instability, ventricular arrhythmia, left main involvement, or persistent TIMI grade flow limitation.6 Our case required intervention due to ongoing symptoms with limited coronary flow. PCI in SCAD is technically challenging because of the fragile vessel wall, the risk of dissection propagation, and the possibility of wiring the false lumen, with reported PCI failure rates higher than in atherosclerotic lesions.7 This mandates the use of intravascular imaging to guide the procedure if technically visible.

The concept of RTIG has recently emerged as a valuable technique in complex PCI scenarios. Harding et al8 first described the use of real-time IVUS guidance for accurate ostial stent placement. Subsequently, Fairley et al9 published a recent article expanding RTIG for both aorto-ostial and side-branch ostial lesions. The technique involves advancing the IVUS catheter into a side branch to obtain real-time cross-sectional views of the main vessel, enabling precise stent positioning. Similarly, Leung et al10 described a “floating IVUS” technique for aorto-ostial stenting, in which the IVUS catheter is positioned in the aortic cusp to delineate the aorto-ostial junction and guide stent deployment under real-time imaging. Beneduce et al11 further demonstrated the utility of real-time IVUS guidance for ultra-low contrast complex bifurcation PCI, highlighting the role of this approach in minimizing contrast use. In the context of iatrogenic dissection, Sharma et al12 reported the use of an IVUS-guided parallel wire technique to rescue catheter-induced spiral dissections, in which IVUS from a false lumen wire was used to guide a second wire into the true lumen, facilitating successful stenting.

More recently, the specific concept of live ultrasound-guided wiring has been described in the setting of coronary dissection. Ho et al13 reported real-time IVUS-guided wiring in SCAD, demonstrating the feasibility of this approach for reliable true lumen identification in angiographically ambiguous cases. Mohan Singh Sindhi et al14 similarly described live IVUS-guided wiring during PCI for an LAD-diagonal bifurcation lesion (Medina 1,1,0) when the wire was repeatedly biased into the diagonal branch, thereby underlining the broader applicability of the technique beyond SCAD. Additionally, Fairley et al15 proposed a contemporary algorithm to guide PCI in high-risk SCAD, integrating intracoronary imaging-guided wiring with tailored lesion preparation strategies.

Building on this emerging experience, our case illustrates the specific application of live side-branch IVUS guidance from the conus branch for safe guidewire advancement during PCI of proximal RCA SCAD. Although the existing literature has demonstrated the utility of RTIG in atherosclerotic ostial and bifurcation lesions, our case extends the live IVUS-guided wiring concept, using a side-branch imaging window. The primary challenge in SCAD is identifying the true lumen in the presence of an intramural hematoma that compresses and distorts the vessel architecture. By positioning the IVUS catheter in the conus branch, we obtained continuous, real-time, 360-degree cross-sectional imaging of the proximal RCA without advancing it through the dissected segment, thereby avoiding the risk of hematoma propagation.

Lesion preparation in SCAD warrants specific consideration. Balloon pre-dilation carries a theoretical risk of "toothpasting" the intramural hematoma, with potential propagation both proximally and distally. For this reason, direct stenting was deliberately performed in the present case. Nevertheless, contemporary literature supports the selective use of cutting or scoring balloons to decompress the hematoma via controlled fenestration of the intimal flap before stent deployment, as incorporated into the high-risk SCAD algorithm proposed by Fairley et al.15 In addition, the use of low-tip-load guidewires, such as SUOH 03 (Asahi Intecc), is recommended to reduce the risk of inadvertent hematoma propagation.14 In our case, a standard workhorse wire (Runthrough) was successfully directed into the true lumen.

Conclusions

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of SCAD in a middle-aged woman presenting with atypical chest pain and its successful management with PCI. PCI for SCAD carries a significantly higher risk of iatrogenic dissection propagation and technical failure than atherosclerotic PCI. The side-branch IVUS technique used in this case may therefore be considered a valuable procedural adjunct in the interventional management of SCAD, where accurate true lumen identification is important for procedural safety.

•SCAD must be considered in patients with limited risk factors and angiographically normal coronary arteries (eg, young, female, nonsmoker) presenting with acute coronary syndrome in absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors.
