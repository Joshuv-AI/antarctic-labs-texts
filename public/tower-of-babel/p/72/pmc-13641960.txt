Quantitative analysis of cervical lymphadenopathy associated with periodontitis using diffusion-weighted imaging
Authors: Hirotaka Muraoka, Hiroshi Yamamoto, Kohei Otsuka, Tomohiro Komatsu, Kotaro Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn this study, we aimed to assess cervical lymphadenopathy associated with periodontitis using diffusion-weighted imaging.Materials and MethodsThe MRI data of patients examined at our dentistry hospital between April 2017 and March 2018 were retrospectively analyzed. The primary predictor was disease status (presence of periodontitis), and the primary outcomes were the mean size and ADC values of cervical LNs. Age and sex were included as additional variables. Statistical evaluation comprised the Mann–Whitney U test, Spearman’s correlation coefficient, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsRecords from 51 patients (aged 31-77 years) and 194 lymph nodes were reviewed. Short-axis diameter was significantly larger and ADC values were significantly higher in the periodontitis group than in the non-periodontitis group (P<0.001). ROC curve analysis demonstrated good discriminative performance, with AUC values of 0.87-0.93 for short-axis diameter and 0.89-0.92 for ADC. Optimal cutoffs were 4.26 mm (level IB) and 5.50 mm (level IIA) for short-axis diameter, and 0.89 × 10−3 mm2/s (level IB) and 0.85 × 10−3 mm2/s (level IIA) for ADC.ConclusionOur findings suggest that LN size and ADC values are associated with periodontitis-related lymphadenopathy and may serve as quantitative indicators of inflammatory changes. However, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory, and further prospective studies with external validation are required before clinical implementation.Keywords: Periodontitis, Lymphadenopathy, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Diffusion Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Introduction

Several lymph nodes (LNs) are distributed throughout the cervical region. Among them, the submandibular, submental, internal jugular, and spinal accessory nodes are particularly relevant to the oral region. The submandibular nodes are situated in the submandibular triangle, lateral to the anterior belly of the digastric muscle, and near the submandibular gland. In contrast, the internal jugular nodes are located adjacent to the internal jugular vein.1 Lymphadenopathy due to inflammatory conditions such as bacterial, viral, or granulomatous infections involves increases in lymphocytes and macrophages in response to antigenic stimulation.2 In metastatic disease, LNs are infiltrated by proliferating neoplastic cells that disrupt the normal nodal architecture.3,4 Lymphadenopathy is associated with a broad spectrum of conditions, including infectious and malignant disorders.

Radiological imaging is essential for evaluating the anatomical distribution and imaging characteristics of cervical LNs. To detect cervical node enlargement, morphological imaging techniques such as ultrasonography (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are employed. Among these, MRI is particularly valuable for assessing lymphatic disorders.

Short tau inversion recovery (STIR) is a rapid and sensitive MRI sequence commonly used to evaluate cervical LNs.5 In addition, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) allows quantitative assessment of water molecule motion and diffusion, facilitating the detection of subtle tissue abnormalities. The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) value derived from DWI aids in differentiating the etiology of cervical lymphadenopathy.6,7 Notably, distinct ADC profiles have been reported for conditions such as metastasis, sarcoidosis, and cat scratch disease.6,8,9

Imaging-based assessment of lymphadenopathy plays a key role in guiding treatment strategies and predicting clinical outcomes.10 However, no studies have quantitatively examined the LN changes associated with periodontitis, a common oral condition, using DWI in MRI.

This study focused on the diagnostic significance of LN size and ADC measurements in the quantitative assessment of cervical lymphadenopathy among patients with periodontitis.

Materials and Methods

Study design

We designed and conducted a retrospective cohort study, which was approved by Nihon University Ethics Committee (EC15-12-009-1). The requirement to obtain written informed consent was waived for this retrospective study. All procedures followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects.

We examined 51 patients (19 men, 32 women; age range, 31–77 years; mean age, 55.31 years) and 194 cervical lymph nodes. All patients underwent MRI at our hospital between April 2017 and March 2018 for clinical indications unrelated to periodontal evaluation, such as assessment of temporomandibular joint disorders or jaw lesions. Periodontal status was determined retrospectively based on dental records and radiographic findings. Patients were excluded if MRI revealed severe susceptibility artifacts or if they had conditions known to affect cervical lymph nodes, including advanced apical periodontitis, other odontogenic infections, acute upper respiratory tract infection, tonsillitis, sinusitis, sialadenitis, chronic systemic inflammatory diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis or tuberculosis), hematological disorders, lymphoma, metastatic cancer, or a history of head and neck malignancy.

The diagnosis of Stage III periodontitis followed the 2018 American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) and European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) classification systems, based on radiographic bone loss extending to the middle third of the root or beyond (≥33% of root length) in conjunction with the presence of bleeding on probing (Fig. 1).11 Patients diagnosed with Stage I or Stage II periodontitis were excluded to ensure clear separation between groups. Accordingly, 21 patients (7 men, 14 women; mean age, 57.81 years) were classified into the Stage III periodontitis group, and 30 patients (12 men, 18 women; mean age, 56.23 years) without radiographic evidence of pathologic alveolar bone loss or clinical findings consistent with periodontitis were assigned to the non-periodontitis group.

Image acquisition

Panoramic imaging was conducted using a digital system (Veraviewepocs; J. Morita Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) with exposure settings varying from 1 to 10 mA and peak kilovoltage between 60 and 80 kV. MRI examinations utilized a 1.5-T superconducting scanner (Intera Achieva® 1.5 T Nova; Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands) paired with a five-channel phased-array coil. For STIR sequences, a spin-echo protocol was applied with parameters set as follows: a repetition time (TR) of 2500 ms, echo time (TE) of 60 ms, and inversion time (TI) of 180 ms; the slice thickness was 6.0 mm; matrix size was 320 × 256; field of view (FOV) measured 230 × 195.5 mm; and number of acquisitions was one. DWI was performed with parameters including a TR/TE of 5100/70 ms, slice thickness of 6.0 mm, matrix of 256 × 256, FOV of 250 × 250 mm, an interslice gap of 1.4 mm, and an acquisition duration of 3 min 29 s. The b-values used were 0 and 1000 s/mm2. ADC maps were produced directly on the MRI console.

Cervical LNs were classified into two anatomical groups; submandibular nodes and superior internal jugular nodes based on their location according to Rouvière’s anatomical criteria12 and the level classification system designed by Som et al.13

Level IB: The anatomical location of the submandibular nodes is above the hyoid bone, below the mylohyoid muscle, and anterior to the posterior margin of the submandibular gland.

Level IB: The anatomical location of the submandibular nodes is above the hyoid bone, below the mylohyoid muscle, and anterior to the posterior margin of the submandibular gland.

Level IIA: Extending from the skull base to the lower border of the hyoid bone are the superior internal jugular nodes. These nodes are located posterior to the submandibular gland and anterior to the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. They may lie anterior, medial, lateral, or posterior to the internal jugular vein.

Level IIA: Extending from the skull base to the lower border of the hyoid bone are the superior internal jugular nodes. These nodes are located posterior to the submandibular gland and anterior to the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. They may lie anterior, medial, lateral, or posterior to the internal jugular vein.

The short-axis diameters of the LNs were measured on axial STIR images. ADC values were obtained by manually placing regions of interest (ROIs) over the LNs (Fig. 1). ROI placement was guided by the corresponding STIR images to ensure accurate anatomical localization. Care was taken to avoid the nodal margins in order to minimize partial volume effects. Measurements were independently performed and recorded by two oral radiology specialists (KI: 13 years of experience; HM: 12 years of experience).

Data analysis

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (2,1) values were classified into categories indicating the level of agreement: values from 0 to 0.2 represented slight agreement; 0.21 to 0.39 minimal; 0.40 to 0.59 weak; 0.60 to 0.79 moderate; 0.80 to 0.90 strong; and values exceeding 0.90 indicated perfect concordance. To account for potential within-patient clustering effects, LN size and ADC values were averaged for each patient, and all subsequent analyses were conducted at the patient level. Differences between the periodontitis and non-periodontitis groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, with mean LN size and mean ADC value per patient as outcome variables. Disease condition functioned as the independent variable, with LN size and ADC values as dependent variables. Age and sex distributions were also compared between groups.

Diagnostic accuracy for identifying periodontitis-related lymphadenopathy based on LN size and ADC measurements was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with calculation of the area under the curve (AUC). All statistical computations were carried out using SPSS software (ver. 28.0; IBM Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and R statistical environment (ver. 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Significance was set at 5% (P<0.05).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that patients with periodontitis had significantly larger LNs and higher ADC values than those in the healthy control group.

Periodontitis is a long-lasting inflammatory disorder that impacts many people, resulting in the deterioration of tissues supporting the teeth and ultimately causing tooth loss.14 Moreover, it is acknowledged as a contributing risk factor for several systemic diseases including stroke, coronary artery disease, and peripheral arterial disease.15,16 Chronic periodontitis promotes systemic inflammation, with moderate to severe cases being associated with elevated inflammatory marker levels.17,18 Additionally, periodontal pathogens including Tannerella forsythia, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Prevotella intermedia can translocate to submandibular LNs adjacent to the oral cavity.19

The lymphatic system consists of lymphatic vessels and lymphoid tissues. These vessels are responsible for transporting leaked fluid, proteins, and cells back into the bloodstream, and they play crucial roles in immune cell movement, immune defense mechanisms, and the absorption of dietary fats.20 Lymphatic drainage from periodontal tissues flows into lymph nodes located in the anterior and posterior cervical triangles. Specifically, lymph from the labial and lingual gingiva of the mandibular incisor area drains into level IA nodes. The palatal gingiva of the maxilla drains to the deep level IIA nodes, while lymph from the buccal gingiva of the maxilla and the buccal and lingual gingiva of the mandibular premolar and molar regions drains into level IB nodes.13

Viral and bacterial infections are the most common causes of lymphadenopathy; however, cervical lymphadenopathy can also arise from malignancies, including Hodgkin’s and non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas. For precise evaluation of lymph nodes, imaging techniques such as US, CT, and MRI are frequently employed.21,22 DWI provides important supplementary information to standard MRI sequences in the assessment of lesions in the head and neck region. ADC values derived from DWI provide tissue characterization at the microscopic level, offering information beyond that available from standard T1- or T2-weighted spin-echo and STIR imaging.8 Previous studies have shown that ADC values differ significantly between benign and malignant lesions, with malignant tissues generally exhibiting reduced ADC values.23,24 Several other studies have also supported the clinical value of ADC measurements in diagnosing cervical lymphadenopathy.9,25,26 However, reports specifically evaluating ADC values in LNs affected by periodontitis using DWI remain limited.

In this study, the mean short-axis LN diameters measured in the periodontitis group were significantly larger relative to those in the non-periodontitis group, with values of 5.18 ± 0.93 mm (level IB) and 5.59 ± 0.91 mm (level IIA) in the periodontitis group versus 3.8 ± 0.72 mm (level IB) and 4.52 ± 0.8 mm (level IIA) in the non-periodontitis group. Similarly, the mean ADC values of LNs in the periodontitis group were significantly higher than those in the non-periodontitis group (Non-periodontitis: level IB, 0.76 ± 0.1 × 10−3 mm2/s; level IIA, 0.78 ± 0.1 × 10−3 mm2/s; Periodontitis: level IB, 0.98 ± 0.17 × 10−3 mm2/s; level IIA, 0.97 ± 0.14 × 10−3 mm2/s).

Eida et al. reported optimal short-axis diameter thresholds of 8.0 mm and 9.0 mm for metastatic LNs at levels I and II, respectively.25 A diagnosis of reactive lymphadenopathy due to bacterial infection is usually made if the short-axis diameter does not surpass these cutoff values. The LNs affected by periodontitis in this study were larger than those in the non-periodontitis group but remained below these metastatic size cutoffs, suggesting reactive lymphadenopathy associated with periodontitis. This result aligns with those of prior studies showing that ADC values in reactive lymphadenopathy are generally elevated, at around 1.24 × 10−3 mm2/s.9

Typically, tissues with high cellularity like malignant tumors show reduced ADC values, while non-neoplastic conditions such as edema, inflammation, fibrosis, and necrosis tend to have elevated ADC values because of their lower cellular density.10

Despite these supportive findings, the ROC-derived cutoff values identified in this study should be interpreted cautiously. LN size and ADC values reflect nonspecific inflammatory or reactive changes rather than disease-specific pathology. Moreover, the proposed thresholds are protocol-dependent, given the use of 1.5-T MRI with thick-slice diffusion-weighted imaging and vendor-generated ADC maps. Because external validation was not performed, the generalizability of these cutoff values remains uncertain. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than immediately applicable in routine clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. Owing to its retrospective design, detailed quantitative periodontal parameters, such as full-mouth probing pocket depth measurements and comprehensive bleeding-on-probing records, were not consistently available for analysis. In addition, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Although patients with known major diseases affecting cervical lymph nodes were excluded, undocumented or subclinical inflammatory conditions of the head and neck region may have influenced lymph node size and ADC values. Furthermore, because MRI examinations were performed for various clinical indications rather than specifically for periodontal assessment, selection bias may have been introduced. Finally, ADC measurements were unavailable in some patients because of image distortion caused by susceptibility artifacts.

In conclusion, LNs in the periodontitis group were significantly larger and had higher ADC values than those in the normal group. The results indicate that quantitative assessment using DWI in MRI may provide additional insight into cervical lymph node changes associated with periodontitis, although further validation is necessary before clinical implementation.
