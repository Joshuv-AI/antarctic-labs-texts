Amyloidosis for the Gastroenterologist: A Comprehensive Systematic Review of Diagnosis and Management of Gastrointestinal Manifestations
Authors: Myo Jin Tang, Theng Yi Tay, Simon Gibbs, Ola Niewiadomski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAmyloidosis is a rare, multisystem disorder characterized by extracellular deposition of misfolded protein fibrils. Gastrointestinal (GI) amyloidosis is uncommon but under‐recognized due to nonspecific symptoms, but early diagnosis is critical to improve quality of life and prognosis. This comprehensive systematic review aimed to summarize current literature on GI and hepatic manifestations of amyloidosis, highlighting diagnostic and management strategies across the major subtypes of amyloidosis, in particular AL (immunoglobulin light chain), ATTR (transthyretin), and AA (serum amyloid A) amyloidosis.MethodsA systematic search of MEDLINE, Embase, and PubMed conducted for studies published between January 2014 and December 2024 identified 77 studies meeting inclusion criteria.ResultsGI symptoms occur in approximately 1 in 6 patients with amyloidosis, but symptoms correlate poorly with gastrointestinal deposition. Current literature suggests that duodenal biopsy is the most reliable diagnostic site. Fibroscan shows promise in detecting hepatic amyloid involvement. Management is subtype specific. However, supportive care, particularly nutritional intervention, is essential across all subtypes.ConclusionsGI amyloidosis presents diversely with nonspecific symptoms. Standardized diagnostic algorithms, targeted therapies, and nutritional support can lead to improved outcomes and enhance quality of life.

Introduction

Amyloidosis is a rare disorder caused by protein misfolding into insoluble fibrils that deposit extracellularly [1]. Amyloidosis is an umbrella term encompassing multiple subtypes, each defined by distinct pathological protein fibrils involved. Each subtype is designated “A” followed by the misfolded protein (e.g., “L” in AL amyloidosis for immunoglobulin light chains).

The combination of organ systems affected varies among individuals, although certain patterns have been observed depending on the type of amyloid involved [1]. Both gastrointestinal (GI) amyloidosis and systemic amyloidosis are commonly underdiagnosed. A 2015 survey of 533 patients found 37.1% were diagnosed more than a year after symptom onset [2]. The nonspecific nature of GI symptoms often delays recognition, although early intervention improves quality of life [3].

This review aims to provide an overview of the current literature describing the gastrointestinal manifestations of amyloidosis as well as relevant diagnostic and management options of the most common amyloidosis subtypes—immunoglobulin light chain amyloidosis (AL), transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR), and serum amyloid A amyloidosis (AA) [4]. ATTR amyloidosis can be further categorized into hereditary/variant (ATTRv) or wild‐type (ATTRwt) form. Notably, GI bleeding and hepatic involvement are more common in AL and AA amyloidosis and are infrequently observed in ATTR amyloidosis. Furthermore, GI symptoms and autonomic neuropathy are characteristic of ATTRv, while ATTRwt typically presents with cardiac involvement and rarely involves the GI tract. Search Strategy

A systematic search of Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, and PubMed was conducted using the terms “amyloidosis,” “gastrointestinal,” “upper gastrointestinal tract,” “lower gastrointestinal tract,” “hepatic,” “liver,” and “nutrition” as MeSH terms and text words. Additional references were identified through manual searches of relevant articles and reviews. The search was limited to English‐language studies published between January 2014 and December 2024. Exclusion criteria included duplicates, nonhuman studies, participants under 18, abstracts only, and unpublished data.

Results

The search yielded 1880 articles. After applying exclusion criteria, 138 studies were assessed, and 77 were included in the review (Figure 1). The included studies comprised: basic science study (n = 1), diagnostic accuracy study (n = 1), cross‐sectional studies (n = 2), case reports (n = 15), case series (n = 6), retrospective cohort studies (n = 14), prospective cohort studies (n = 4), narrative reviews (n = 24), randomized controlled trials (n = 2), clinical trials (n = 3), and guidelines/consensus statements (n = 5). Given the heterogeneity of study designs, no formal quality appraisal tool was applied. Findings were interpreted in the context of each study's design and methodological robustness. GI Amyloidosis: Pathophysiology and Clinical Features

The gastrointestinal symptoms of amyloidosis arise from the accumulation of amyloid protein fibrils in the GI tract, disrupting normal bowel function [6]. A study of 729 amyloidosis patients at a single amyloidosis center found GI involvement in 24.8% of AL, 34.5% of AA, 15.9% of ATTRv, and 3.8% of ATTRwt amyloidosis patients [7].

Most cases of GI amyloidosis (79%) occur as part of systemic disease, where amyloid precursor proteins are synthesized in distant organs [8]. Systemic amyloidosis is commonly associated with plasma cell dyscrasias, chronic inflammatory conditions, dialysis, or hereditary disorders, with the GI tract being one of several organs affected by amyloid deposition.

Less commonly (21%), amyloid deposition is localized, meaning precursor protein synthesis, amyloid fibril deposition, and clinical manifestations are confined to the GI tract [8, 9]. True localized GI amyloidosis carries an excellent prognosis, with no progression to systemic disease [8, 10]. However, patients presenting with GI symptoms alone should undergo thorough screening for systemic amyloidosis. This includes evaluating serum protein electrophoresis, serum free light chain concentrations, urine protein electrophoresis, cardiac biomarkers, inflammatory markers, and bone scintigraphy to detect cardiac uptake characteristic of ATTR amyloidosis. Additionally, symptoms of peripheral and autonomic neuropathy should be carefully assessed [8].

Different amyloid subtypes exhibit distinct patterns of deposition both along the proximal–distal axis (horizontal distribution) and within different layers of the intestinal wall (vertical distribution).

Regardless of subtype, amyloid is most frequently detected in duodenal biopsies. AL amyloidosis is the most prevalent subtype (66.5%), followed by ATTR amyloidosis (15.7%). Deposition primarily occurs in the submucosa, followed by the muscularis mucosae and lamina propria [11]. While this pattern is consistent across AL, ATTR, and AA amyloidosis, each subtype has unique preferential sites of deposition. In ATTRv amyloidosis, for example, vascular deposition predominates, while AA amyloidosis shows more interstitial involvement [11].

Autonomic neuropathy, particularly in ATTRv amyloidosis, disrupts the enteric plexus, leading to loss of inhibitory mechanisms and upregulation of excitatory neurons and subsequently dysmotility symptoms [12, 13].

Another factor contributing to dysmotility is the loss of interstitial cells of Cajal, which play a crucial role in regulating GI motility [14]. In the lower GI tract and colon, amyloid infiltration of the myenteric plexus is a frequent cause of colonic motility disorders. Emerging evidence also suggests that ATTR amyloidosis may affect neuroendocrine cells in the GI tract.

Hepatic amyloidosis is often incidental but may present with portal inflammation, fibrosis, or bile duct injury [15]. In rare cases, amyloidosis can present as severe acute liver failure [16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Gastrointestinal Manifestations

A single‐center study conducted from 2008 to 2017 by Yen et al. retrospectively estimates that at least one in six patients with amyloidosis experience GI symptoms [21]. However, autonomic neuropathy and cardiac cachexia can mimic GI symptoms [8]. A biopsy of the GI tract is required to confirm a diagnosis of true GI amyloidosis [22].

Symptoms vary but commonly include weight loss (40%–43%), diarrhea (27–29%), and abdominal pain (28%). Other notable features include macroglossia (10%–20%), GI bleeding (16%–36%), early satiety (19%–33%), dysphagia, heartburn, and constipation [8, 21, 23]. Variations in symptom prevalence across studies are expected due to small cohort sizes. GI symptoms can be categorized based on their underlying pathophysiology [21].

Malabsorption and Nutrition

The mechanisms underlying malabsorption in amyloidosis remain poorly understood. Fewer than 5% of patients with systemic amyloidosis present with malabsorptive symptoms [24]. Proposed mechanisms include muscular infiltration leading to myopathy, autonomic neuropathy of the enteric nervous system, bacterial overgrowth due to dysmotility, protein‐losing enteropathy, and ischemia from vascular deposits [9]. The most common resulting symptoms from malabsorption are weight loss (100%) and diarrhea (95%). Occasionally, some individuals can present with steatorrhea (< 5%). Predominant physical findings are weight loss and hypotension or orthostatic changes [24]. Malabsorption can also lead to vitamin deficiencies, particularly in fat‐soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K) and vitamin B12, which can cause paraesthesia and peripheral sensory loss [25].

Malnutrition is common at diagnosis. A prospective study of newly diagnosed systemic AL amyloidosis patients found that 66% met criteria for significant malnutrition (Patient‐Generated Subjective Global Assessment [PG‐SGA] score ≥ 4) [26]. Another study reported that nearly 25% of patients failed to meet their estimated caloric needs, and 45% had midarm muscle circumference (MAMC) scores indicating reduced muscle protein stores, correlating with lower quality‐of‐life scores (SF‐36 survey) [27]. Nutritional status is an independent predictor of mortality, and appropriate management is associated with improved survival and quality of life [28, 29, 30].

GI and Hepatic Hemorrhage

GI bleeding results from mucosal erosion and vascular amyloid deposition, leading to fragility and spontaneous hemorrhage [31]. Intestinal bleeding may occur because of submucosal or polypoid amyloid lesions, bowel ischemia, or infarction [32], while gastric bleeding may result from submucosal hematomas or Dieulafoy‐like ulcers [33, 34]. GI bleeding is less common but remains a major presentation of localized GI amyloidosis and is more frequently associated with AL and AA amyloidosis subtypes [4, 8, 9, 21].

Hepatic amyloidosis can lead to life‐threatening complications such as hepatic bleeding, hematoma formation, or, in rare cases, hepatic rupture [35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. These complications are more commonly seen in AL and AA amyloidosis, due to amyloid deposition in the liver parenchyma or blood vessel walls, resulting in vascular fragility compounded by coagulation abnormalities [37]. Acquired factor X deficiency, pathognomonic for AL amyloidosis, should be considered in cases of massive hemorrhage [42, 43].

Protein‐Losing Enteropathy

Protein‐losing enteropathy (PLE) results from excessive loss of proteins into the GI tract [44]. In amyloidosis, it is thought to arise from increased permeability of the vasculature, especially the capillaries [45]. Mucosal inflammation, particularly in AA amyloidosis, may further exacerbate protein loss [9]. Patients with PLE experience hypoalbuminemia‐related symptoms, including peripheral edema, abdominal distension, dyspnea, and diarrhea [44].

Gastrointestinal Dysmotility

Amyloid deposition in the enteric and autonomic nerves of the GI tract can lead to significant dysmotility, with considerable heterogeneity in clinical presentation depending on the affected region and whether transit is accelerated or delayed [46].

In the esophagus, dysmotility may manifest as heartburn, dysphagia, or chest pain. In some cases, the clinical picture resembles achalasia, characterized by aperistalsis of the midesophagus and a “bird‐beak” appearance of the lower esophageal sphincter, which may respond to pneumatic dilatation [47]. Conversely, some patients exhibit reduced lower esophageal sphincter pressure [24].

Delayed gastric emptying, more frequently observed in ATTRv amyloidosis, often presents with nausea, vomiting, and early satiety [46]. Similar symptoms may also arise from gastric outlet obstruction secondary to submucosal masses, large polyps, or antral narrowing [48].

Diarrhea can result from rapid intestinal transit or bacterial overgrowth, leading to secondary malabsorption of proteins, bile acids, and fats, with consequent steatorrhea and weight loss [49]. Constipation, more commonly associated with ATTRv amyloidosis, is attributed to prolonged colonic transit time and loss of spontaneous rectal contractions due to amyloid infiltration of the intestinal muscle layer or enteric nervous system [50].

Pseudo‐obstruction, which can affect the small bowel, colon, or both, presents with obstructive symptoms and radiographic findings of bowel dilation and abnormal luminal gas accumulation. In AL amyloidosis, pseudo‐obstruction is typically caused by amyloid deposition in the intestinal smooth muscle and is associated with a poor prognosis and high mortality [51]. In contrast, in AA amyloidosis, it is often subacute or self‐limiting, potentially due to differences in amyloid deposition patterns, particularly involvement of the myenteric plexus [51].

Relationship Between Symptoms and Amyloid Type

AL amyloidosis commonly presents with obstructive symptoms, while AA amyloidosis more often causes diarrhea and malabsorption [52]. However, symptom profiles overlap, making a diagnostic biopsy with immunohistochemistry (staining for kappa, lambda, transthyretin, and AA) and mass spectrometry essential for accurate subtype classification [8]. This is especially important in ATTR amyloidosis, where there is significant risk of misdiagnosis as AL amyloidosis due to the frequent detection of monoclonal protein in the blood (~25% of patients with the ATTR subtype) [6].

Hepatic Involvement

Hepatic involvement is common in systemic amyloidosis, with amyloid deposition in approximately 70% of patients (AL: 60%–90%, AA: 50%–60%) [15]. However, overt clinical manifestations are uncommon. When present, symptoms such as weight loss, fatigue, and abdominal pain are generally mild [53]. Hepatomegaly (81%) and elevated ALP (86%) are common due to sinusoidal infiltration, although vascular involvement may also contribute [54]. Hepatic congestion secondary to cardiac failure, particularly in AL and ATTR amyloidosis, can further complicate the clinical picture [53]. Fibroscan shows promise in detecting hepatic amyloid infiltration, particularly in AL amyloidosis, although further validation is needed [55].

Diagnostic Process

Diagnosing amyloidosis requires a high index of suspicion, as symptoms are often nonspecific. A definitive diagnosis of GI or hepatic involvement necessitates both symptomatology and biopsy [56]. Endoscopic biopsies from prior investigations may contain amyloid deposits upon re‐evaluation. If GI biopsies are negative despite strong clinical suspicion, alternative biopsy sites should be considered. Systemic amyloidosis can often be diagnosed via less invasive methods, such as abdominal fat pad aspiration or labial salivary gland biopsy (particularly in ATTRv amyloidosis) [57]. In patients with plasma cell dyscrasia detected via serum free light chains or electrophoresis, bone marrow biopsy is used as a screening tool but remains negative in ~30% of AL and 85% of ATTR amyloidosis cases [1].

Amyloid deposits stain eosinophilic salmon‐pink with Congo red and exhibit apple‐green birefringence under polarized light, a hallmark feature [58]. Biopsy specimens must undergo amyloid typing using immunohistochemistry (staining for transthyretin, kappa, lambda, and AA), genetic testing, or, more recently, laser microdissection with mass spectrometry [6]. Accurate subtyping is crucial, as treatment and prognosis differ significantly. Importantly, suspicion of amyloidosis must be explicitly communicated to the pathologist, as specific staining and processing must be requested [22]. Referral to an amyloidosis center is recommended in uncertain cases.

Endoscopic Findings

Endoscopy is frequently performed in patients presenting with GI symptoms and allows for simultaneous biopsy acquisition [59]. However, symptoms do not always correlate with macroscopic endoscopic findings. Said et al. found no consistent endoscopic patterns associated with amyloid type in their cohort of 79 patients [6], emphasizing the need for biopsy even in normal‐appearing mucosa.

Mouth and Esophagus

Macroglossia is the most common oral manifestation of amyloidosis, although it remains relatively rare and is primarily seen in AL amyloidosis [9]. Esophageal involvement is uncommon, with most reports limited to case series and case reports. Endoscopic findings may include a fine granular mucosal pattern, esophagitis, or luminal narrowing [60].

Stomach

The stomach is the second most affected site after the small intestine, particularly in AL amyloidosis [6, 60]. Endoscopic abnormalities include mucosal erosions, erythema, ulcers, and tumor‐like lesions [60]. These lesions may resemble submucosal edema with ulceration or mimic gastric malignancies, causing luminal narrowing [61].

Small Intestine

The small intestine is the most frequently involved site in systemic amyloidosis [11]. Some studies report duodenal biopsy positivity in up to 100% of patients, although others estimate a diagnostic yield of 50%–70% [8]. AL amyloidosis is often characterized by polypoid protrusions and thickened duodenal folds, while AA amyloidosis more commonly presents with fine granular lesions and polypoid protrusions [62]. Additional findings include mucosal elevations, thickened folds, submucosal tumor‐like lesions, ulcerations, erosions, submucosal hematomas, and scalloping [63]. However, these features are not consistently present in all cases [61].

Colon and Rectum

Rectal biopsy was historically a sensitive diagnostic method, with AL amyloidosis showing up to 85% positivity. However, ATTR amyloidosis is detected in only 15% of cases [57]. Deep rectal biopsies are required to examine submucosal vessels for amyloid deposition. Endoscopic findings in the colon and rectum are variable, and amyloid deposits may be present despite normal‐appearing mucosa [6]. Some studies have described fine granular changes, erosions, and ulcerations, while other studies have reported vascular abnormalities, erythema, edema, and submucosal hematomas [61].

Biopsy Site

Duodenal biopsy provides the highest diagnostic yield [61]. Additionally, biopsy site selection may be influenced by amyloid subtype. A retrospective study by Freudenthaler et al. suggests that upper GI biopsies are more diagnostic for AL (kappa) and AA amyloidosis, while lower GI biopsies favor AL (lambda) and ATTR amyloidosis [11]. Thus, a combination of duodenal and rectal biopsies is recommended, with a minimum of three samples per site to optimize diagnostic accuracy [57]. Endoscopy with biopsy is valuable in excluding amyloidosis as a cause of unexplained GI symptoms. However, symptoms alone do not always indicate pathological GI amyloid deposition. Yen et al. found that half of the patients with systemic amyloidosis and GI symptoms lacked biopsy‐proven amyloid infiltration [21], possibly due to autonomic neuropathy rather than direct amyloid involvement. Given that most diagnosed patients are older [8], the risks of biopsy, including perforation and bleeding—especially in cases with extensive vascular amyloid deposition—must be carefully considered [61].

For hepatic amyloidosis, liver biopsy may confirm amyloid infiltration, but a transjugular approach is recommended to mitigate the risk of life‐threatening hemorrhage [15].

Although biopsy of the clinically involved organ is the most sensitive method for diagnosing amyloidosis, surrogate sites may be used when biopsy is contraindicated or unrevealing despite high clinical suspicion. These include abdominal subcutaneous fat aspiration for systemic amyloidosis, bone marrow biopsy for suspected systemic AL amyloidosis, and minor salivary gland biopsy for AA or AL amyloidosis [57].

Despite the high sensitivity and specificity of Congo red staining, false negatives can occur due to factors such as amyloid burden, deposit age, section thickness, tissue fixation, or staining technique [64]. Digitally reinforced hematoxylin–eosin polarization (DRHEP) is a novel technique combining routine light microscopy with digital photography to detect weak birefringence that may be missed under conventional microscopy [65, 66]. Although currently limited to renal biopsies, its applicability in GI amyloidosis is under investigation [67].

Gastric Emptying Studies

Gastric emptying scintigraphy is sometimes used to assess delayed gastric emptying in amyloidosis‐associated dysmotility. A Swedish study by Wixner et al. examined the relationship between gastric emptying and autonomic dysfunction in ATTRv amyloidosis. Although no strong correlation was found between gastric emptying abnormalities and autonomic neuropathy or symptom severity, delayed gastric emptying was highly prevalent in this cohort [68].

Other Relevant Investigations

Laboratory Testing

Serological tests lack specificity for diagnosing GI amyloidosis. Elevated alkaline phosphatase is the most common abnormality [1]. Although less commonly detected, increased serum bilirubin suggests cholestasis and portends a poor prognosis, particularly when bilirubin exceeds 1.5 times the upper limit of normal, indicating extensive hepatic involvement [69]. Higher fecal calprotectin levels in ATTRv amyloidosis hint at an inflammatory component but lack specificity [70]. Hypoalbuminemia may result from protein‐losing enteropathy or renal proteinuria. Anemia may stem from GI hemorrhage, multiple myeloma, renal dysfunction, or coagulation abnormalities [24, 47].

Imaging

Imaging modalities used to assess abdominal amyloidosis include ultrasonography, barium studies, and computed tomography (CT) [71]. While CT findings are often nonspecific, the small bowel has the highest rate of radiologic abnormalities, with bowel wall thickening being the most common finding. Additional findings include bowel dilatation, mesenteric infiltration, and adenopathy [63]. Although less frequently used today, barium studies may show focal or symmetrical thickening of bowel folds [52].

Hepatic Involvement

Imaging plays a key role in evaluating liver function test abnormalities. The most characteristic CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings include hepatomegaly and periportal involvement [71, 72]. In AL amyloidosis, hepatic involvement is defined as total liver span > 15 cm in the absence of heart failure or alkaline phosphatase > 1.5 times the institutional upper limit of normal [73]. However, the specificity of these criteria has recently been questioned [74]. Fibroscan is another noninvasive radiographic modality that can be used to show increased stiffness of the liver secondary to amyloid deposition [75, 76]. A prospective study by Loustaud‐Ratti et al. found significantly higher median liver stiffness in patients with AL amyloidosis compared to controls (27.4 kPa vs. 4.8 kPa, p < 0.0001) [55], suggesting its potential role as an adjunctive tool in assessing hepatic involvement, particularly given the invasiveness of liver biopsy. Although liver biopsy provides a definitive diagnosis, it is generally not recommended and should be approached with caution due to the risk of bleeding. It should only be considered when an alternative diagnosis is suspected [77]. The Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score may help predict prognosis in patients with AL and ATTR amyloidosis [78].

Management

The treatment of systemic amyloidosis involves suppressing amyloid fibril protein precursor synthesis and supporting affected organs. Suppression strategies vary by amyloid subtype. Although not a guarantee, effective treatment of systemic amyloidosis, regression of GI amyloid, and symptoms may occur, though not always. Further accumulation of amyloid, however, almost always leads to deterioration in organ function. Currently, no targeted therapies exist to directly address amyloid deposits in the GI tract.

The management of AL amyloidosis focuses on suppressing the production of monoclonal immunoglobulin light chains through the eradication of malignant plasma cells [67, 73]. First‐line therapy for fit patients eligible for disease‐directed therapy is Dara+CyBorD (daratumumab, cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone) as initial induction therapy [22, 79]. Further management—either autologous stem cell transplantation or continued single‐agent daratumumab—depends on the patient's hematologic response [80]. The role of doxycycline as adjuvant therapy has become controversial based on recent studies [81, 82]. Emerging therapeutic agents include CAEL‐101, NEOD001, and birtamimab [83, 84, 85].

The treatment of ATTR amyloidosis aims to prevent amyloid fibril formation by either stabilizing the transthyretin (TTR) tetramer or suppressing its production. TTR stabilizers (tafamidis, diflunisal) prevent amyloid formation, while RNA‐targeted therapies (patisiran, vutrisiran, inotersen, eplontersen) suppress hepatic TTR production [65, 86]. Fibril disruptors (doxycycline, EGCG) show promise as adjunct therapies; however, there is low evidence of efficacy [87, 88]. Treatment selection is guided by clinical presentation [89]. For hereditary ATTR amyloidosis (ATTRv), orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT) may be considered to remove the source of mutant TTR, functioning as a form of “surgical gene therapy” [90]. However, OLT has not been shown to improve gastric emptying or nutritional symptoms and has largely been replaced by pharmaceutical management [90, 91]. Emerging therapies include monoclonal antibodies targeting deposited TTR amyloid and CRISPR‐Cas9–based gene editing [92, 93].

The treatment of AA amyloidosis focuses on controlling the underlying inflammatory or infectious condition to reduce serum amyloid A (SAA) protein levels and prevent further amyloid deposition [9, 94]. Disease‐specific therapies include immunosuppressive agents (e.g., tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors for rheumatoid arthritis) and antibiotics for chronic infections. Currently, no therapies exist to prevent SAA fibril formation or remove existing amyloid deposits.

Supportive care, often overlooked, is crucial in managing symptoms and correcting nutritional deficiencies [9].

Upper GI Symptoms

Upper gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, and early satiety) are common dysmotility‐related symptoms and challenging to manage. In patients with symptomatic gastroparesis, the focus is mostly on antiemetics and prokinetic agents [95]. Notably, symptom severity does not correlate with the degree of gastroparesis. Most studies on gastroparesis have focused on AL and ATTRv amyloidosis, with limited case reports on AA amyloidosis. In AL amyloidosis, domperidone (up to 20 mg every 6 h before meals) and metoclopramide are recommended [73, 96, 97]. In ATTRv amyloidosis, erythromycin (50–250 mg two to three times daily) or domperidone (10 mg twice daily, 5 min before meals) is preferred [3, 98]. Lower doses of erythromycin are found to be as effective as the usual dose of 250 mg TDS [98]. Furthermore, there is a rapid decline in response and an increase in side effects associated with higher doses of erythromycin [65]. For prolonged treatment, domperidone is recommended long‐term, while metoclopramide (IM or IV) may be used for acute attacks or recurrent vomiting [25, 98]. Though not explicitly studied in amyloidosis, novel prokinetic agents such as prucalopride, itopride, levosulpride, and cisapride may be reasonable alternatives due to their similarities in mechanism to diabetic enteropathy [25, 69, 95, 98, 99]. Small, frequent meals are better tolerated than large, solid meals. Hypercaloric liquid formulations should be used in patients with poor caloric intake. For those with heartburn, proton pump inhibitors in standard doses are recommended [25].

Diarrhea

Diarrhea is a common but difficult‐to‐treat symptom due to its multiple potential etiologies. In AL amyloidosis, a stepwise approach is recommended, beginning with antimotility agents such as loperamide (up to 32 mg daily), codeine (up to 30 mg twice daily), and diphenoxylate [73, 97]. For severe cases, octreotide may be beneficial in an outpatient setting [73, 96]. In ATTR amyloidosis, octreotide may also improve diarrhea, particularly in ATTRv amyloidosis, where the underlying mechanism is thought to involve a reduction in somatostatin‐secreting neuroendocrine cells in the gut [100]. A trial of 3–5 days should be instituted with close monitoring of bowel actions to confirm response, as a proportion of patients will not respond. In ATTRv amyloidosis, racecadotril (300 mg daily) may be considered as an alternative to loperamide [101]. In AA amyloidosis, corticosteroids have been shown to suppress the AA precursor protein SAA while also reducing inflammation and edema, thereby improving diarrhea in patients with chronic autoimmune inflammatory diseases. Case reports suggest that corticosteroids combined with octreotide can be effective in treating severe diarrhea and PLE [102]. If octreotide is considered, a gentle up titration from 50 mcg twice daily to a maximum of 100 mcg three times daily is recommended [98]. For bile acid malabsorption, cholestyramine (4 g before meals, twice to four times daily) can be used in conjunction with a fat‐restricted diet [103].

Constipation

In the management of constipation, osmotic laxatives, particularly polyethylene glycol (macrogel, up to 13–80 mg daily), are the most effective first‐line option [3]. Lactulose (up to 15–60 mL daily), though associated with greater side effects, may serve as an alternative [104]. If symptoms persist, picosulfate (10–20 drops daily) can be considered [98]. In ATTRv amyloidosis, dietary modifications and increased fiber intake are often insufficient [25] Lubiprostone, prucalopride, and linaclotide, though not specifically studied in AL or AA amyloidosis, are reasonable options in refractory cases [25]. Prucalopride, a 5HT4 receptor agonist, is particularly recommended as it may enhance both upper and lower gastrointestinal motility [104].

Pseudo‐Obstruction

Pseudo‐obstruction in amyloidosis is associated with poor prognosis. In AA amyloidosis, it typically presents acutely or subacutely and is usually self‐limiting, responding well to bowel rest and total parenteral nutrition (TPN) [51]. In AL amyloidosis, the course is more chronic [51]. Cisapride (up to 10–20 mg QID) has been considered but remains limited in use [105]. Alternatively, prucalopride (2–4 mg daily) or pyridostigmine can be considered. Avoid other pro‐kinetic agents due to side effects and limited benefit. Surgery is not recommended, and TPN should be initiated to allow for bowel rest [95].

Small Intestine Bowel Overgrowth

Although treatment for SIBO in amyloidosis has not been extensively studied, management typically involves rotating courses of antibiotics every 14–21 days, depending on symptom recurrence, followed by probiotic supplementation. Diagnosis can be made using a hydrogen/methane breath test before initiating treatment; however, because of its limited sensitivity, a trial of antibiotics may be warranted based on clinical suspicion [106]. Treatment should be approached with caution, as there is a moderate risk of gut microbiota disruption and antibiotic resistance. While not specifically studied in amyloidosis, commonly used antibiotics include ciprofloxacin (500 mg twice daily), tetracycline (100 mg daily), and metronidazole (500 mg twice daily) [104, 107]. For long‐term management, rifaximin (550 mg twice daily) is preferred due to its minimal systemic absorption [108]. In acute cases, neostigmine may be considered [69]. Vitamin B12 supplementation is recommended, as SIBO impairs intrinsic factor function, leading to B12 deficiency [25].

GI Hemorrhage

In amyloidosis‐related gastrointestinal hemorrhage, embolization is often the first‐line approach for hemostasis, diverging from conventional endoscopic techniques typically used for gastrointestinal bleeding. Micro‐coil embolization has been employed with some success in cases of lower gastrointestinal bleeding, although there is limited evidence supporting its effectiveness in amyloidosis [109]. Embolization may be less effective in cases of diffuse amyloid deposition in the gastrointestinal tract [110]. Endoscopic vessel ligation is another technique that has been utilized, but its efficacy in amyloidosis remains unclear [67]. Conventional endoscopic interventions such as adrenaline injection, thermal coagulation, and clips have not been clearly demonstrated to be beneficial, likely due to the increased vascular fragility and diffuse nature of amyloid involvement [111, 112]. If no contraindications exist, antifibrinolytic agents such as tranexamic acid or epsilon aminocaproic acid may be considered [113].

Management of hepatic hemorrhage focuses on blood product resuscitation and hemorrhage control. Selective embolization is preferred as the initial intervention [35, 37] However, escalation to surgical management, including exploratory laparotomy, liver lobectomy, or hepatic artery ligation, may be necessary in certain cases [35, 37] Additionally, correction of any underlying coagulopathy is essential.

Nutritional Support

Nutritional status significantly impacts quality of life and survival, necessitating early assessment and intervention [114]. Caccialanza et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial evaluating the effects of personalized nutritional counseling on various nutritional parameters. Their findings demonstrated that, compared to the control group—which experienced a mean weight loss of 2.1 kg (95% CI, 0.2 to 4.1; p = 0.003)—the group receiving nutritional counseling largely maintained their initial weight, with a mean loss of only 0.6 kg (95% CI, −1.0 to 2.1; p = 0.214). Additional parameters, including caloric intake and MAMC, also favored the nutritional counseling group [28]. However, it is important to acknowledge the challenges in establishing a direct causal relationship between nutritional counseling and weight change, given the influence of confounding factors such as chemotherapy regimens in patients with AL amyloidosis.

Despite these limitations, dietary modification and nutritional supplementation remain crucial because of the high prevalence of malnutrition in amyloidosis. In cases where oral intake remains inadequate, parenteral nutrition may be necessary, particularly in patients with gastrointestinal pseudo‐obstruction [115]. In ATTRv amyloidosis, parenteral nutrition has been shown to alleviate gastrointestinal symptoms and improve nutritional status, although further studies are needed to confirm these findings [115].

Strengths and Limitations

This comprehensive review offers a clinically focused overview of gastrointestinal and hepatic manifestations across all major amyloidosis subtypes. Current literature is limited by significant heterogeneity in study design and quality, with much of the available data derived from case reports or retrospective cohort studies. Prospective, multicentered studies with standardized outcome measures are lacking. Future research should prioritize larger‐scale prospective cohort studies. Key areas where evidence remains limited include the utility of Fibroscan for hepatic amyloidosis, the impact of nutritional interventions, and the prevalence and management of gastrointestinal dysmotility in systemic amyloidosis.

Conclusions

Although gastrointestinal amyloidosis remains rare, its incidence has risen in recent years due to increased awareness and earlier diagnosis. Given the nonspecific and varied presentations, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion to avoid delays in diagnosis, which can significantly impact prognosis. Gastroenterologists should be vigilant, as gastrointestinal symptoms in amyloidosis often mimic other disorders. The development of standardized diagnostic and management guidelines would be invaluable in improving both mortality and, more importantly, quality of life for affected patients.
