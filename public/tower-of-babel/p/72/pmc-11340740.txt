Yellow fever vaccine coverage and associated factors among under-five children in Kenya: Data from Kenyan Demographic and Health Survey 2022
Authors: Mohammed Seid Ali, Enyew Getaneh Mekonen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYellow fever is a vaccine preventable hemorrhagic disease that leads to morbidity and mortality in the affected individuals. The only options for preventing and controlling its spread are through vaccination. Therefore, this study was conducted to estimate yellow fever vaccination coverage and associated factors among under-five children in Kenya. The total weighted samples of 2,844 children aged under-five were included in this study. The data were taken from the Kenyan Demographic and Health Survey 2022. In the multivariable analysis, the adjusted odds ratio with a 95% CI was used to declare significant associations of yellow fever vaccine. The yellow fever vaccine coverage among children aged under-five in Kenya was 18.50%. The significant factors associated with yellow fever vaccine coverage were: the age of the child older than 24 months (AOR = 1.7; 95% CI (1.17–2.58)); higher odds of yellow fever vaccination coverage was observed among older children, place of residence (AOR = 1.76; 95% CI (1.04–2.97)); higher odds was observed among urban residents, maternal education; primary education (AOR = 1.99; 95% CI (1.04–2.97)), secondary education (AOR = 2.85; 95% CI (1.41–5.76)), mothers who attended primary or secondary education have higher odds of yellow fever vaccination coverage, wealth index (AOR = 2.38; 95% CI (1.15–4.91)); higher odds of vaccination coverage was observed among poor households. Yellow fever vaccine coverage among under-five children in Kenya was low and has become an important public health concern. Policymakers and other stakeholders are recommended to focus on vaccination programs to prevent yellow fever disease.KEYWORDS: Yellow fever, vaccination, coverage, children, Kenya

Introduction

Yellow fever is an acute viral vaccine preventable hemorrhagic disease in tropical regions of Africa and Latin America. It is caused by flavivirus which is transmitted by the bite mosquito.1 Yellow fever causes over 81,000 deaths globally each year, about 89% of all deaths accounts in Africa. This disease affects the economy and health care systems worldwide. The virus is endemic in tropical areas of Africa and Latin America, affecting over 900 million people. About 200,000 cases and 30,000 deaths occurred annually, particularly in Africa.2,3 The case fatality rate in Africa ranges from 15% to 50%.4 The disease can be confirmed through, serological testing for yellow fever virus or isolation of the virus from blood samples.5 The significance of morbidity and mortality of yellow fever is high in tropical regions, including Kenya.6 Yellow fever infection in humans can lead to hemorrhagic fever and is fatal in. After an initial phase with flu-like symptoms, about 12% of infected patients progress to a more severe form of the disease. This severe form is characterized by hemorrhagic symptoms, jaundice, and eventually shock and multisystem organ failure. The case-fatality rate for severe yellow fever cases ranges from 30% to 60%.5,7

The clinical impact of yellow fever disease in humans shows a wide range of asymptomatic infection, mild illness with flu-like manifestations, and severe disease including hemorrhage and death.8 The management of yellow fever infection is mainly supportive care. Yellow fever is preventable through vaccination. A single dose of the yellow fever vaccine provides life-long protection. Vaccination is crucial to reduce yellow fever risk and mortality.9

Its prevention and control is through an effective vaccination covering all individuals including children older than 9 months.10,11 The most recent yellow fever outbreak occurred in March 2022, a higher case number recorded in the African region, particularly in Kenya. This outbreak was significantly associated with vaccination.12 In addition, the incidence of yellow fever outbreaks in Kenya is rising at an alarming rate and has become a public health concern, requiring immediate intervention. In East Africa, yellow fever disease has been causing epidemics, with high morbidity and mortality, especially in various Kenyan regions.13

Many organizations, like the World Health Organization (WHO), UNICEF, and other health partners, began executing strategies to prevent the recurrence of yellow fever disease in various countries. These organizations designed the Eliminate Yellow Fever Epidemics strategy and aimed at preventing future outbreaks by addressing the vaccine against yellow fever for over 1 billion individuals.14

Although the yellow fever disease is prevalent in tropical regions, there are very limited studies about vaccination coverage, particularly for children. In nine countries of the WHO African region, Kenya reported yellow fever cases with a high risk of death, outbreaks, and a history of transmission.15 Thus, it shows the need for studies about the vaccination coverage among children, which are among the most vulnerable populations. The mechanisms of yellow fever disease are poorly understood and require attention in modern clinical research. Since there is no specific management and treatment of patients with the disease, it is extremely problematic; the emphasis should be given on preventative measures and vaccination.16

However, there is no curative treatment for yellow fever disease, the only options for preventing and controlling its spread are vaccination and ensuring preventive measures.17 Hence, the status of yellow fever vaccination coverage should be known to provide the vaccination and to strengthen efforts toward the prevention of yellow fever. The exact coverage of yellow fever vaccine is not known in the regions and the evidence of vaccination coverage among the pediatric population is very limited. Probability-based studies such as cluster surveys can estimate yellow fever vaccination coverage even if the size of the target population is not known.18 It is important to conduct yellow fever vaccine coverage studies to identify the gaps and design future vaccination strategies in the affected regions. Therefore, the primary objective of the study was to estimate the yellow fever vaccination coverage among less than 5-years old children in Kenya and to identify factors associated with this coverage using comprehensive data from the 2022 Kenyan Demographic and Health Survey (KDHS). This study will help to recommend possible actions, to guide future vaccination efforts and contribute to the control of yellow fever disease in the affected regions.

Methods

Study design and setting

A national-based cross-sectional study was conducted from KDHS 2022 data. The KDHS was carried out in 2022 and encompassed both urban and rural areas. Kenya is located in East Africa and borders Uganda to the west, Tanzania to the south, Sudan and Ethiopia to the north, and Somalia and the Indian Ocean to the east. The Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS) and other stakeholders worked together to implement the 2022 KDHS. This is the seventh KDHS implemented in the country. Data collection was conducted between February 17th and July 31st, 2022.

Data source, extraction, sampling procedure, and study participants

The KDHS was the seventh to be carried out in Kenya, following similar surveys conducted in 1989, 1993, 1998, 2003, 2008–09, and 2014. The survey aimed to provide up-to-date information on socio-economic, demographic, nutrition, and health indicators to plan, monitor, and evaluate various health programs and policies. The Kenya Household Master Sample Frame (K-HMSF) was used to draw the sample for the 2022 KDHS, which included 129,067 enumeration areas. To create the HMSF 10,000 enumeration areas with probabilities proportional to size were chosen. Subsample one of the K-HMSF was used to draw the 2022 KDHS sample. The clusters were developed through a process of household listing and geo-referencing. Kenya is divided into 47 counties under the devolved system of government established by the Constitution of Kenya in 2010. The 2022 KDHS sampled a total of 42,022 households. Interviews were conducted only in the pre-selected households and clusters; no replacement of the pre-selected units was allowed during the survey data collection stages

Study variables

The outcome variable in this study was yellow fever vaccine coverage, which was determined by the KDHS question “yellow fever vaccine” uptake of children. The response was dichotomized as “Yes” if a child had vaccinated yellow fever vaccine and “No” if a child did not vaccinate yellow fever vaccine within the study period. The independent variables included in the study were age of child, sex of child, maternal age, marital status, place of residence, educational status, religion, watching television, listening to radio, wealth status, occupational status, mode of transportation to health facility, preceding birth interval, place of delivery, number of ANC visits, visited by Community Health Workers (CHW), and service/information provided by CHWs.

Data management and analysis

To restore the survey’s representativeness, the sample weights were applied to compensate for the unequal probability of selection between the strata. The STATA version 17 software was used to conduct descriptive statistics and logistic regression analysis. A binary logistic regression model was fitted and the model fitness was tested by Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit. Multivariable logistic analysis was employed to identify the associated factors associated with yellow fever vaccine coverage. Multivariable logistic regression allows us to explore how various factors affect vaccination coverage while adjusting for potential confounding factors. The influencer factors were reported after controlling potential confounders and reported using adjusted odds ratio (AOR). Both bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression were conducted. Variables with a p-value <.2 in the bivariable analysis were eligible for multivariable analysis. In the multivariable analysis, an AOR with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was reported, and variables with a p-value <.05 were declared to be statistically significant associated factors with vaccination coverage of yellow fever.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the participants

A total of 2,844 children aged less than 5 years were included in this study. Among the total, about 51.41% of the children were males, and more than half (53.16%) of the children were in the age group of less than 24 months. About 61.18% of the mothers’ age was less than 30 years old and 58.68% of the children were lived in a rural residence. Regarding maternal educational status, about 28.20% of the mothers had not attended any formal education. Among the total households, the majorities (71.45%) of the mothers were married and about 52.25% of the mothers were unemployed (Table 1).

Yellow fever vaccination coverage

In this study, the yellow fever vaccine coverage was low. Only 18.50% (95% CI (17.11% 19.96%)) of the children aged under-five have received yellow fever vaccine in the country during the 2022 national survey (Figure 1).

Multivariable regression analysis of the factors associated with fever vaccine coverage

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was carried out to identify factors affecting yellow fever vaccine coverage among under-five children and presented with AOR and 95% CI. The significant factors associated with fever vaccine coverage were: the age of the child greater than 24 months (AOR = 1.7; 95% CI (1.17–2.58)), those children whose age greater than 24 months were 1.7 times more likely to be vaccinated as compared to their counter parts, place of residence (AOR = 1.76; 95% CI (1.04–2.97)), children living in urban areas were 1.76 times more likely to be vaccinated with yellow fever vaccine as compared to those children from rural areas, maternal education; primary education (AOR = 1.99; 95% CI (1.04–2.97)), secondary education (AOR = 2.85; 95% CI (1.41–5.76)), mothers who attended primary or secondary education have higher odds of yellow fever vaccination coverage for their children as compared to uneducated mothers, wealth index (AOR = 2.38; 95% CI (1.15–4.91)), children from poorer households were 2.38 times more likely to be vaccinated with yellow fever vaccine as compared to children from richer households (Table 3).

Discussion

This study assessed the yellow fever vaccine coverage and the associated factors among under-five children in Kenya from the most recent data of Kenyan Demographic and Health Survey 2022. Assessing yellow fever vaccination status determines the country’s overall economic and health care systems to design preventive strategies. The vaccination coverage was affected by the socioeconomic and demographic variables of the participants in the country. The WHO and other organizations designed strategies to eliminate yellow fever by 2026. So, investigating vaccination coverage and associated factors is crucial to achieving this goal. It is important to include vaccination as the main intervention strategies to control and prevent yellow fever diseases as well as epidemics.

In this study, the main findings showed that yellow fever vaccine coverage among under-five children was 18.50% (95% CI (17.11%−19.96%)). Regarding the regions in the country, the yellow fever vaccination coverage was slightly higher in West Poko, Baringo, and Turkana regions as compared to other regions of Kenya. The vaccination coverage in Kenya was low and below the WHO recommendations. This low coverage of yellow fever vaccination highlights the urgent need for public health strategies of improving vaccination accessibility to the community, particularly to the affected populations. In order to prevent the occurrence of epidemic and outbreaks of yellow fever, the main preventive measure, vaccination, should be implemented. This study showed that the low coverage of vaccination was affected by place of residence, age of child, maternal educational level, and wealth index of the participants. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommends the vaccination of yellow fever as the main prevention mechanism for the disease. Routine child immunization against yellow fever is an important part of the yellow fever control strategy. Although controlling the vector for yellow fever is one of the preventive measures but vaccination against yellow fever is the single most effective strategy to control the disease.19–21

Yellow fever vaccine coverage was lower in this study than a study conducted in Brazil. This might be due to the variation in the health care services between Kenya and Brazil. It might also be due to the fact that in Brazil, the accessibility of the vaccine can be better than in Kenya. Both countries are at high risk of yellow fever disease because they are located in tropical regions that are prevalent with mosquito vectors as well as the yellow fever virus.22 The vaccination coverage in Kenya is also lower than the yellow fever vaccination coverage in Ghana. At the national level, the vaccination coverage for yellow fever in Ghana is 88%.23 This might be the WHO and ACIP recommendations for yellow fever vaccination, which is more practiced in Ghana than Kenya. Generally, the yellow fever vaccination coverage in Kenya was lower than Ghana and Brazil. The possible explanation for this difference might be due to the fact that variations in the accessibility of health care infrastructure, vaccine accessibility, and public health policies. This is because of those countries with higher vaccination coverage might be due to better accessibility of vaccination and health care services. Further studies are needed to identify the possible reasons for variations in vaccination coverage rates across different countries.24 Great emphasis on vaccination coverage and accessibility should be given to East African Countries, particularly, Kenya where yellow fever is epidemic.25

The sex of the child has no influence on yellow fever vaccination coverage in our study, but the age of the child can influence. The age of the child was one of the factors affecting the coverage of the yellow fever vaccine in this study. Those children whose age was older than 24 months were 1.7 times more likely to be vaccinated as compared to younger children. However, we did not get supporting evidence, and the reason is not clear. Possibly, this might be due to mothers who had children age older than 24 months can access better health care services because, during the breastfeeding period, the child can walk and eat independently from their mother. This maternal freedom might access vaccination services for their child as compared to a breastfeeding child. It is recommended that future researchers clarify it in detail. The demographic factors, like maternal employment and religion, had no significant association with yellow fever vaccination coverage in this study. Even though religion is a significant factor, it is a non-modifiable factor; everyone cannot be obligated to change their religion.

Another significant factor associated with yellow fever vaccination was maternal educational status. Mothers who attended primary or secondary education have higher odds of yellow fever vaccination coverage for their children as compared to uneducated mothers. This finding was supported by other study.26–28 This might be due to the fact that educated mothers more likely to have a positive attitude toward child vaccination against yellow fever; because mothers who attend primary or secondary education can read and write health related written materials, have better access and understanding toward media exposure about health care services, including child immunization, and also have better health-related information, including the need for health care visits, as compared to uneducated mothers.

In this study, the wealth index of the participants was also a significant factor associated with vaccination coverage. Children from a family with low socio-economic backgrounds were 2.38 times more likely to be vaccinated with the yellow fever vaccine as compared to their counterparts from higher socio-economic backgrounds. This finding was supported by other studies conducted in Togo.29 This might be due to the fact that disease exposure is more common in poorer living standards. The health-related organizations, including non-governmental organizations, focused on the affected individuals, and community health workers also focused on poorer households for primary health care services including vaccination. So, the vaccination coverage might be higher as compared to richer households. On the contrary, the richer households might have good living standards like adequate nutrition boosting immunity, good hygiene, and sanitation, and during illness, they go to private health institutions for better health care. But yellow fever vaccination is given by governmental or non-governmental organization for yellow fever-exposed population.30,31

The yellow fever vaccination was negatively associated with rural residents. Children living in urban areas were 1.76 times more likely to be vaccinated with the yellow fever vaccine as compared to those from rural areas. This finding was supported by other studies.32,33 This might be due to the stock of vaccine is limited and the population in rural areas are larger than the urban areas. In addition, the accessibility of health care services is limited in rural areas compared to urban areas due to lack of adequate transportation and other infrastructure to seek vaccination and related health care services. The transmission rate of yellow fever is also frequent in rural and forest areas.34 It is important to emphasize preparing vaccination campaigns to improve vaccination coverage in rural populations, particularly for susceptible individuals like under-five children. Further studies are required to enhance a comprehensive understanding on prevention strategies of yellow fever and identify unanswered gaps. Future researchers are recommended to investigating barriers to vaccination uptake and evaluating the effectiveness of different intervention strategies in increasing vaccine coverage in the affected tropical regions, including Kenya.

Limitation of the study

This study was based on secondary data, the vaccination status was recorded from self-reported or vaccination cards. The self-reported vaccination status might be subjected to recall bias.

Interpretation and implications

The low coverage of yellow fever vaccination in this study indicates the need for effective strategies to address vaccination against this fatal disease. Yellow fever vaccination has become public health concern. The low vaccination coverage might lead to epidemic and outbreak occurrence. Missing or delay in vaccination against yellow fever potentially place children and their communities at high risk of serious infectious hemorrhagic disease. Every individual in the tropical regions is at risk of developing yellow fever infections. This disease has no effective treatment other than vaccination. Yellow fever vaccination has a great impact on the health of children as well as for prevention and control of the diseases. The disease has its own impact on the country’s health care system and economic status, health care costs for treatment and vaccination. Yellow fever illness can affect the economic productivity of one’s country. The vaccination process and healthcare costs require financial support from the government to save the life of the population. This disease requires targeted interventions to reduce the burden and its health impacts on the population. The health system and policy in the country as well as the region better to emphasize on the vaccination against yellow fever, particularly vulnerable populations like children. The responsible body in the country should focus on improving strategies of vaccine accessibility and community mobilization.

Conclusion

Yellow fever vaccine coverage among under-five children in Kenya was low and has become an important public health concern that requires immediate attention. The significant factors associated with yellow fever vaccine coverage among under-five children in Kenya were the age of child; higher odds of yellow fever vaccine coverage was observed among children aged greater than 24 months, place of residence; higher odds was observed among rural areas, maternal education; higher odds of vaccination coverage was observed among educated mothers, wealth index; higher odds of vaccination coverage were observed among poorer households. It is recommended to the health policymakers and other stakeholders, such as governmental and non-governmental organizations of the country, should focus on improving yellow fever vaccination programs to prevent yellow fever disease, with special emphasis on the above determinant factors that are an important input to developing strategies for improving the accessibility of vaccination services in the country.
