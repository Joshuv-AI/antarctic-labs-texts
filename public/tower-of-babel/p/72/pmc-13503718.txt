A multimodal vision-language model for comprehensive dental diagnosis and enhanced clinical practice
Authors: Zijie Meng, Jin Hao, Xiwei Dai, Yang Feng, Jiaxiang Liu, Bin Feng, Huikai Wu, Xiaotang Gai, Hengchuan Zhu, Tianxiang Hu, Yangyang Wu, Hongxia Xu, Jin Li, Jun Xiao, Xiaoqiang Liu, Joey Tianyi Zhou, Fudong Zhu, Zhihe Zhao, Bing Fang, Lunguo Xia, Jimeng Sun, Jian Wu, Zuozhu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral diseases affect billions of people, yet specialist dental expertise remains unevenly distributed, and diagnosis often requires synthesis across diverse imaging modalities. Existing artificial intelligence systems mostly address isolated tasks, limiting their applicability in comprehensive dental assessment. Here we introduce DentVLM, a dental vision-language model that jointly interprets images and text, supports expert-level oral disease diagnosis across seven dental imaging modalities and 36 tasks. Developed using 110,447 images and 2.46 million bilingual visual question-answer pairs, DentVLM outperforms leading proprietary, open-source and domain-specific medical models on internal and external tests. In a study of 32 participants, DentVLM surpasses junior readers, matches intermediate general practitioners and approaches senior specialists. In collaborative workflows, it raises junior and intermediate readers toward specialist-level performance and reduces diagnostic time for all readers by 15.0-37.0%. These results establish DentVLM as a clinical decision support tool for reducing specialist care gaps and broadening access to high-quality dental expertise.

Introduction

Oral diseases constitute a formidable public health challenge, affecting approximately 3.5 billion people globally, with a particularly acute burden in low- and middle-income countries1–3. The escalating prevalence of conditions such as dental caries, periodontal disease, and malocclusion, coupled with a critical shortage of dental professionals, has created a widening gap between healthcare demand and capacity4–7. This disparity often results in delayed diagnoses and suboptimal management, underscoring the urgent need for scalable, automated solutions to augment clinical decision-making and democratize access to specialized care8,9.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising tool to address these systemic inefficiencies10–14. To date, the application of AI in dentistry has been predominantly driven by deep neural networks15,16 tailored for specific, narrow tasks, such as diagnosis of conditions on panoramic radiographs17–21, tooth segmentation on Cone-Beam Computed Tomography22–31 (CBCT), tooth alignment in intraoral scans32–36, or landmark localization for cephalometric analysis37–39. While these task-specific models have achieved impressive accuracy within their defined scopes, they largely operate in fragmented silos. In contrast, real-world clinical practice is inherently multimodal and integrative, requiring practitioners to synthesize heterogeneous information, ranging from intraoral photographs and various radiographic modalities to patient history, to form a comprehensive diagnosis40. Furthermore, traditional deep learning models often provide diagnostic outputs without transparent reasoning. This lack of interpretability remains a significant barrier to clinical trust and adoption, as clinicians require not just a prediction, but a clinically sound rationale to support their decision-making.

Recent breakthroughs in foundation models (FMs)41–43 and vision-language models (VLMs)44–46 offer a new paradigm for reshaping healthcare delivery through advanced multimodal understanding and reasoning capabilities. While existing medical FMs excel at extracting latent representations from 2D/3D images for downstream diagnosis tasks, they often lack the inherent interpretability and interactive capabilities essential for clinical deployment. Recent general-purpose and medical VLMs address these limitations by seamlessly integrating visual and textual data. This integration facilitates instruction-following across diverse diagnosis tasks, enhances generalization in open-ended question-answering, and supports fluid interaction via natural language explanations47–51. However, current VLMs falter when handling the nuanced, fine-grained visual features unique to dental pathologies52. Furthermore, they lack the domain-specific knowledge required to distinguish active disease processes and historical treatments53. Consequently, effective adaptation necessitates efforts on domain-specific instruction data curation, effective multimodal dental image-text alignment, and the embedding of clinical reasoning pathways54–56.

To bridge this gap, we developed the dental vision-language model (DentVLM), a versatile VLM engineered to perform 36 clinical tasks across seven distinct 2D oral imaging modalities, including panoramic X-rays, lateral cephalograms, and five views of intraoral photographs. By leveraging a novel two-stage progressive training strategy on a massive-scale bilingual dataset, we seek to equip the model not only with high diagnostic precision but also with the explainable, expert-level rationales. Through comprehensive benchmarking, DentVLM exhibited statistically significant superiority, outperforming 18 leading models (including GPT-4o44) by an average margin of 23.8%. Moreover, rigorous clinical validation confirmed that the model surpasses junior students, matches intermediate practitioners, and approaches senior specialists; crucially, as a collaborative assistant, it effectively bridges expertise gaps and accelerates diagnostic workflows by 15.0–37.0%. Finally, DentVLM demonstrated robust generalizability across external global datasets (Dentex57, TDD58) and out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks, validating its practical utility as a scalable solution for diverse healthcare settings.

Results

Overview of DentVLM

Problem formulation

We developed DentVLM as a specialized medical VLM designed to diagnose 36 distinct dental conditions across seven imaging modalities (Fig. 1a). Its analytical capabilities extend to both radiographic inputs, specifically lateral (LAT) and panoramic (PAN) X-rays typically captured by Pano-Ceph X-ray Units, and a comprehensive suite of intraoral photographs, including left (INL), right (INR), frontal (INF), upper arch (UPP), and lower arch (LOW) views (Table S1, Note S1). The diagnostic process follows a Visual Question Answering (VQA) paradigm. Specifically, for an input pair consisting of an image It and a question Qt corresponding to task t, denoted as (It, Qt), DentVLM generates a comprehensive output comprising a diagnostic answer At, a detailed rationale Rt, and an optional anatomical location Lt (Fig. 1a, Table S6, Note S1). While the majority of these tasks are framed as multi-class classification, we incorporated two complex multi-label tasks for malocclusion to generate exhaustive diagnoses of facial profiles or intricate occlusal types (Fig. 1a, Tables S1–S5, Note S1).

Dataset construction and statistics

To support this broad diagnostic scope, we constructed a massive-scale, bilingual dataset (Fig. 1b). The primary training corpus comprises 110,447 images sourced from hospitals and clinics across 31 provinces in China (2017–2024), covering a cohort of 20,741 patients. Leveraging these images and their corresponding diagnostic records, we constructed a large-scale Chinese (ZH) VQA dataset with a predefined list of questions (Table S7), comprising 733,338 pairs for oral disease and treatment identification, and 495,704 pairs for malocclusion. For precise localization of tooth-related pathologies, the dental arch was segmented into six distinct regions. A subset of 6298 images was chosen to generate diagnostic rationales utilizing GPT-4o44 (Table S8), leading to 44,308 VQA pairs with rationales. To enhance the dataset’s linguistic diversity, we translated the Chinese VQA pairs into English (EN), where we employed an expert-calibrated medical lexicon to guide the translation (Fig. 1b, Table S9). Our efforts yielded a large-scale dataset of 1.47 million bilingual VQA pairs for oral diseases and treatment identification, and 0.99 million pairs for malocclusion. We employed this pipeline to construct the test set and the OOD global benchmarks57,58, where both the test set and part of the training set underwent further calibration by 34 senior experts, as detailed in the “Methods.”

We also curated a separate retrospective clinical study set (1946 patients, 2642 images, 3105 VQA pairs) to assess real-world utility. The ground truth for this set was established through an expert consensus protocol: labels were derived from independent annotations by 4 senior experts (all holding PhD/DMD and senior professional titles from top stomatological hospitals in China with >20 years of experience), followed by confidence filtering and majority voting (Fig. S1, Note S2). This process yielded a high inter-rater agreement of 86.7%, with 94.0% of the ground truth labels derived from candidate annotations of all four experts.

We provided comprehensive statistics across all dataset partitions, including the number of patients, images, and VQA pairs (Fig. 2a, b). Model architecture and training

DentVLM is designed to enable expert-level understanding and reasoning across multimodal dental images. By leveraging the Qwen2-VL46 foundation, it employs a specialized vision encoder that processes images at dynamic resolutions. This capability is critical for dental diagnosis, as it preserves fine-grained features that are often compromised by standard image resizing (Fig. S4). Technically, for an input pair consisting of a visual image (I) and a textual question (Q), the model encodes I into patched visual embeddings and concatenates them with the token embeddings of Q. This fused multimodal representation is subsequently processed by a large language model (LLM) decoder to generate diagnostic outputs in an autoregressive manner.

To adapt this generalist architecture to clinical dentistry, we introduced a two-stage progressive training strategy (Fig. 2c). The first stage, vision-language alignment, utilizes a massive dataset of simple VQA triplets \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{D}}}}}_{1}=\{(I,Q,A)\}$$\end{document}D1={(I,Q,A)} to rapidly align visual features with dental concepts. The second stage, diagnosis instruction tuning, fine-tunes the model on a meticulously curated, high-quality dataset of rational VQA \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{D}}}}}_{2}=\{(I,Q,A,R,L)\}$$\end{document}D2={(I,Q,A,R,L)}. By integrating chain-of-thought guidance and structured location-aware supervision, this stage transforms DentVLM from a simple classifier into an interpretable clinical assistant capable of generating not only diagnostic answers A but also detailed rationales R and anatomical locations L.

Evaluation protocols

We conducted a multi-dimensional evaluation to validate DentVLM’s capabilities, including baseline benchmarking, a stratified clinical reader study, and extensive tests for global generalizability and practical utility. The evaluation metrics are detailed in the “Methods,” where Accuracy (%), Hit-rate (%), and Intersection over Union (IoU) were employed to evaluate multi-class (Tables S2–S4), multi-label (Table S5), and localization (Tables S2 and S3) tasks, respectively.

For baseline benchmarking, we compared DentVLM against 18 state-of-the-art models, spanning proprietary models (GPT-4o44 and the Gemini series45), open-source models (DeepSeek-VL259, Gemma3-4B60, InternVL-2.5-8B61, LLaVA-OneVision-7B62, Llama-3.2-11B63, Qwen2-VL-7B/72B46, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B/72B64), and domain-specific medical multimodal large language models (MLLMs) (LLaVA-Med-v1.554, HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B/34B55, and RadFM56).

For clinical validation, we orchestrated a reader study with 32 participants stratified into three expertise groups (junior, intermediate, and senior). The protocol evaluated quantitative diagnostic accuracy (with and without AI assistance), efficiency gains, and qualitative scores of model-generated responses across six dimensions65–67.

For generalizability and utility, we extended the evaluation to OOD global multi-center datasets57,58 and unseen diagnostic tasks. This was complemented by ablation studies on training dynamics and inference stability, alongside simulations of three real-world deployment scenarios to demonstrate practical applicability.

Diagnosis comparison of DentVLM and baselines

We compared the diagnostic capability of DentVLM against 18 state-of-the-art models. Overall, DentVLM exhibited statistically significant superiority, surpassing all baselines in bilingual scenarios (Fig. 3a–d, Table S10). Quantitatively, our model outperformed the leading baselines (i.e., GPT-4o44, DeepSeek-VL259, Qwen2.5-VL64) by an average margin of 23.8% (P < 0.001). It achieved robust consistency, with an average accuracy of 78.8% (95% CI: 75.3–82.4%) for oral diseases and treatment identification, and 77.3% (95% CI: 73.1–81.4%) for malocclusion tasks. Beyond diagnostic classification, DentVLM demonstrated superior capabilities in fine-grained visual localization, which is crucial for underpinning diagnostic credibility. When benchmarked against four strong baselines (Fig. 3e, Table S11), DentVLM achieved a mean IoU score of 39.4% (95% CI: 36.4–42.4%), significantly surpassing the top-performing proprietary model, GPT-4o44, by 5.8% (P < 0.001).

To assess decision reliability, we analyzed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and confusion matrices averaged over 32 binary diagnostic tasks (Fig. 3f, Table S12). Due to the inaccessibility of token probabilities for proprietary systems, ROC analysis was limited to open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL64 and Qwen2-VL46). DentVLM demonstrated superior discriminative ability, exceeding these baselines in area under the curve (AUC) by 0.291 (P < 0.001) and 0.338 (P < 0.001), respectively. Moreover, DentVLM yielded the highest true prediction rate (0.776, 95% CI: 0.769–0.783) and the lowest error rate (0.224, 95% CI: 0.217–0.231). Conversely, baselines revealed distinct biases: Qwen2.5-VL skewed toward false positives (FPR of 0.692, 95% CI: 0.680–0.705), while Qwen2-VL favored false negatives (FNR of 0.714, 95% CI: 0.705–0.723).

Finally, a granular task-by-task comparison confirmed DentVLM’s dominance in 34 of the 36 bilingual tasks (Fig. 3g, Tables S13–S16), with exceptions only in midline deviation and sagittal relationship classification, where performance slightly trailed GPT-4o44 and Gemma360. Notably, DentVLM maintained cross-lingual stability (average discrepancy <0.1%), whereas general medical MLLMs showed severe degradation in Chinese contexts (e.g., RadFM56 dropped to an average of 38.5%, P < 0.001). This underscores the critical value of our domain-specific and bilingual training strategy.

Distinct from the data annotators, we recruited 32 human readers stratified into three expertise levels: a junior group (7 dental students), an intermediate group (13 general practitioners), and a senior group (12 orthodontic specialists). We first evaluated the standalone diagnostic accuracy of DentVLM against human readers (Figs. 4b–d and S6). Overall, DentVLM achieved an average accuracy of 77.6% (95% CI: 76.2–79.1%). This performance significantly outperformed the junior group by a margin of 9.1% (P < 0.001), performed on par with the intermediate group (75.9%, 95% CI: 74.4–77.5%), and closely approached the level of senior specialists (80.3%, 95% CI: 78.8–81.7%). A key finding emerged in task-specific analyses: for general oral disease and treatment identification, DentVLM (78.3%, 95% CI: 76.2–80.4%) surpassed all human cohorts, even marginally outperforming senior specialists (77.2%, 95% CI: 75.0–79.4%). In complex malocclusion scenarios, while DentVLM (77.1%, 95% CI: 75.0–79.1%) consistently outperformed junior readers by an average of 8.9% (P < 0.001) and matched the intermediate group (79.3%, 95% CI: 77.2–81.3%), it slightly trailed senior specialists by 6.0% (P < 0.001). These results indicate that DentVLM exceeds specialist capabilities in general screening tasks while maintaining competent, intermediate-level performance in highly specialized diagnostics.

We further investigated the value of human-AI collaboration under two model-assisted diagnosis modes: providing predicted answers (DentVLM-A) and providing answers with rationales (DentVLM-R). Overall, DentVLM assistance proved transformative, effectively bridging the experience gap for the junior group (yielding an improvement of 10.5%, P < 0.001) and significantly boosting both intermediate and senior groups (Figs. 4b–d and S7). In general diagnostic tasks, model assistance propelled junior and intermediate readers to levels exceeding those of unaided senior specialists. In complex malocclusion tasks, the utility of assistance exhibited a stratified pattern: junior readers benefited universally (average gain of 9.6%, P < 0.001); intermediate practitioners benefited most from the explanatory rationales in DentVLM-R (accuracy gain of 3.5%, P < 0.005); whereas senior experts primarily benefited from the rapid answers in DentVLM-A (gain of 2.6%, P < 0.05).

Beyond diagnostic accuracy, efficiency, and qualitative trust are pivotal for clinical adoption. DentVLM significantly accelerated workflows, with its standalone diagnostic time constituting only 22.8% (P < 0.001) of that required by the fastest senior dentists (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, both assistance modes profoundly improved the efficiency for all human groups. Finally, a qualitative evaluation by a panel of 25 experienced readers confirmed high ratings across dimensions of accuracy, correctness, completeness, fairness, faithfulness, and acceptability65–67, demonstrating strong professional acceptance of DentVLM’s clinical reasoning capability (Fig. 4f, Tables S17 and S18).

Model generalizability and ablation analysis

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of DentVLM’s robustness, covering global multi-center generalization, zero-shot inference, and ablation studies.

We first assessed the model’s generalization capability on global multi-center test sets (Fig. 5a). Leveraging the Dentex57 (Türkiye) and TDD58 (USA) datasets, processed to support bilingual evaluation across four diagnostic tasks (i.e., caries, impacted teeth, apical periodontitis, and residual root), we observed DentVLM’s exceptional robustness. It significantly outperformed the nearest competitors (i.e., Qwen2-VL46, DeepSeek-VL259), achieving average accuracy margins of 15.7% on Dentex and 15.4% on TDD. In addition, we probed the model’s zero-shot capacity on OOD diagnosis tasks entirely absent from the training stage (Fig. 5b). When tested on four novel conditions: bone islands, embedded teeth, cysts, and veneers (Table S19), DentVLM achieved average accuracies ranging from 52.0% to 64.4% without any direct supervision. While clinical deployment for these results requires further optimization, these findings highlight the model’s emergent ability to extrapolate foundational dental knowledge to novel pathologies, laying the groundwork for open-world dental AI.

We also performed systematic ablation studies across training and data configurations (Note S5). Regarding model architecture, the framework proved architecture-agnostic, integrating seamlessly with various base models and outperforming GPT-4o by over 25% across all variants (Fig. 5c). Ablation results validated the superiority of our progressive two-stage training pipeline and the full-parameter fine-tuning over Low-Rank Adaptation68 (Fig. S9a–d). We also showed that scaling to larger datasets (retraining on 1%, 10%, and 50% subsets) were indispensable for resolving complex tasks (e.g., malocclusion) and achieving Chinese language proficiency (Fig. 5d). Moreover, models trained on a single domain or language failed to match the unified framework, confirming the necessity of a mixed-task, bilingual training strategy (Fig. S9e, f).

Finally, fine-grained inference analysis confirmed DentVLM’s operational readiness. The model delivered rapid processing speeds, approximately 1.55 s on server-grade GPUs (NVIDIA A800-80G) and 3.21 s on consumer-grade hardware (NVIDIA RTX 3090), while maintaining stability across diverse resolutions and prompt variations (Fig. S9g–i). Although fine-grained tasks with subtle visual cues (e.g., calculus) present ongoing challenges, DentVLM maintains strong average bilingual consistency across seven imaging modalities, validating its potential for efficient, real-world deployment (Figs. 5e and S10).

First, we considered the increasingly prevalent scenario where oral images are captured by edge devices, such as smartphones or portable equipment, which are ideal for population oral health management (Fig. 6a). We deployed DentVLM to generate diagnosis results across all oral diseases (N = 7) related to intraoral images (Fig. 1a). A comprehensive list of diagnosed diseases is then aggregated using either majority voting or matching voting (Note S6). On a held-out set of 61 patients, DentVLM achieved a diagnosis matching score of 93.9% for health management using matching voting, representing the upper bound of the model’s capability in predicting patient-level disease lists. With majority voting, a diagnosis matching score of 78.2% was achieved, providing more convincing and reliable diagnostic outcomes (Fig. 6c).

Second, we applied DentVLM to simulate real-world orthodontic diagnosis scenarios within a hospital setting (Fig. 6b). We collected medical images captured using professional equipment across all seven modalities and queried DentVLM to obtain a comprehensive list of diagnosed malocclusion diseases. Results from an external test set involving 69 patients showed that DentVLM achieved a diagnosis matching score of 88.1% with matching voting and 85.3% with majority voting (Fig. 6c). Such performance shows promise for streamlining diagnostic workflows and reducing the rate of missed diagnoses.

Finally, we explored integrating a reasoning model, DeepSeek-R169, with DentVLM, constructing a preliminary multi-agent collaborative framework to provide more interpretable outputs and user-friendly interactions (Fig. 6d). We sampled 50 data items from the clinical study set, comprising 50 images covering all modalities and 31 tasks. Each image was initially processed by DentVLM to generate diagnoses and rationales, which were then provided to DeepSeek-R1 for enhanced clarity and multiple rounds of interaction (Table S20). Three professional dentists, each with over 8 years of experience, were invited to evaluate these interactions across seven distinct dimensions (Fig. 6e). All dimensions received scores above 3, with fairness, acceptability, and readability approaching 4, indicating the strong practical utility of DentVLM (Figs. S11–S13).

Discussion

The inherent complexity of oral healthcare necessitates a paradigm shift from fragmented, single-task AI tools toward holistic, patient-centered VLMs. Despite rapid advances in medical AI, clinical deployment in dentistry has been constrained by models that lack multimodal integration and interpretability. To bridge this gap, we developed DentVLM, a unified VLM capable of comprehensive analysis across 36 clinical tasks. By seamlessly synthesizing information from seven distinct imaging modalities, DentVLM not only standardizes diagnostic pathways but also establishes a reproducible framework for developing specialty-specific models in data-scarce medical domains.

A core technical innovation of DentVLM is its two-stage progressive training strategy, which optimizes the use of large-scale, mixed-quality medical datasets for unified VLM development (Figs. 2c and S4). The first stage addresses the scarcity of image-caption corpora by utilizing 2.37 million simple VQA pairs for fast dental alignment. Through full parameter updates of the vision-language backbone, the model acquires a robust foundational understanding of dental features to directly generate diagnostic answers. The second stage refines this foundation using a high-quality dataset of 88,616 instances augmented with expert rationales. By fine-tuning on these logically clear reasoning traces, DentVLM transcends simple classification to provide transparent, well-founded decision-making processes indispensable for clinical practice. Comprehensive ablation studies confirm the necessity of this two-stage approach, demonstrating superior performance compared to using either stage in isolation (Fig. S9a). These results underscore the synergistic contributions of a large-scale alignment corpus and high-quality, rationale-enhanced data, offering a resource-efficient blueprint for future VLM development in medicine.

The translational potential of DentVLM was validated through a stratified multi-reader study. Results revealed a performance gradient mirroring human expertise: DentVLM surpassed dental students, matched general practitioners, and closely approached senior specialists. Crucially, when integrated into the clinical workflow, DentVLM served as an effective “force multiplier,” elevating the diagnostic accuracy of students and generalists to near-specialist levels while reducing diagnostic time by 15.0–37.0%. This suggests that DentVLM is particularly well-suited to mitigate the expertise gap in primary care settings. However, granular task-level analysis in malocclusion diagnosis indicated that the model’s advantage narrowed: while it still outperformed intermediate practitioners in 8 out of 19 tasks, it surpassed senior specialists in only 3 out of 19 tasks (Fig. S6b). This disparity underscores specific limitations for DentVLM in complex orthodontic applications, a domain that demands extended specialized training and nuanced 3D spatial reasoning, often difficult to capture fully in 2D modalities.

Beyond diagnostic accuracy, we dissected the nuances of human-AI interaction. While model assistance improved consistency metrics across all groups (Fig. S5e, f), interaction patterns diverged by expertise. For malocclusion-related tasks, junior readers relied on the model to bridge knowledge gaps, showing similar benefits from both direct answers (DentVLM-A, 77.2%) and rationales (DentVLM-R, 77.0%). Intermediate practitioners, however, benefited significantly more from rationales (82.1% by DentVLM-R vs. 79.8% by DentVLM-A), indicating a need to integrate model reasoning for selective adoption (Fig. S7c, d). Conversely, senior specialists utilized outputs primarily as safety checks, with rationales showing less impact on “easy” cases (Fig. S8a, b). This suggests that while rationales improve interpretability, they may introduce cognitive load or bias in expert workflows. Future implementations should therefore explore adaptive rationale delivery based on task complexity and user expertise. Complementary case studies confirmed the consistency between qualitative human evaluation and quantitative accuracy, attributing errors to subtle or atypical pathologies that highlight targets for future refinement (Figs. S11 and S12).

Furthermore, DentVLM showed robust generalizability and could be readily integrated into clinical workflows. By extending our evaluation to Dentex57 (Türkiye) and TDD58 (USA), we confirmed that DentVLM significantly outperforms leading proprietary (e.g., GPT-4o) and open-source (e.g., DeepSeek-VL2, QwenVL series) models on OOD benchmarks. These findings provide compelling evidence that the model has learned fundamental dental representations that transcend variations in ethnicity, imaging equipment, and clinical protocols. Furthermore, by explicitly distinguishing “Treatment Identification” (e.g., recognizing existing crowns or implants) from active “Oral Disease Diagnosis” in our task taxonomy, we ensured the model aligns with the logical flow of real-world clinical assessment, where charting dental history is a prerequisite for diagnosis.

Despite promising results, several limitations delineate the path for future research. First, while we have validated the model on 2D imaging, the integration of 3D modalities such as CBCT and intraoral scans remains a frontier that would unlock capabilities in diagnosis and treatment planning. Second, although DentVLM approaches specialist-level performance, it has not yet consistently surpassed human experts in complex cases. Future iterations could incorporate both dental knowledge and reinforcement learning from human feedback70 to provide more reliable reasoning traces. Finally, while our retrospective and OOD validations are robust, large-scale and multi-center prospective clinical trials are essential to fully assess the model’s impact on longitudinal patient outcomes and ensure equitable deployment.

In conclusion, DentVLM demonstrates the transformative potential of multimodal VLMs in dentistry. Through a rigorous combination of systematic data curation, advanced alignment strategies, stringent multi-population validation, and a stratified multi-reader clinical study, we have established a robust, interpretable, and generalizable dental assistant. This work can serve various clinical expertise levels and healthcare settings, from specialized clinics to resource-limited regions. These compelling findings highlight promising new directions for developing VLMs in medical domains characterized by integrated imaging and intricate clinical workflows, with the ultimate goal of enhancing patient care.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of Sichuan University (approval number: WCHSIRB-D-2021-331) and Shanghai Jiao Tong University (approval number: SH9H-2020-TK400-1). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients (or their legal guardians) for the use of their clinical data, including radiographic and intraoral images, for research and for publication. All data were de-identified to remove non-diagnostic personal information (e.g., names, ID numbers) prior to analysis, in strict compliance with data privacy regulations.

Training dataset construction

Based on the formulated problem (Fig. 1a, Note S1), we curated a large-scale dataset comprising 20,741 patients, 110,447 images, and 2,458,084 bilingual VQA pairs to train DentVLM (Fig. 1b). The patients were sourced from hospitals and dental clinics across 31 provinces in China between 2017 and 2024. The images cover seven modalities: LAT (13,628, 12.3%), PAN (18,237, 16.5%), INL (15,818, 14.3%), INR (15,539, 14.1%), INF (16,234, 14.7%), UPP (15,638, 14.2%), and LOW (15,353, 13.9%). Collectively, this diverse dataset supports 17 tasks related to oral disease and treatment identification (Tables S2 and S3) and 19 tasks related to malocclusion (Tables S4 and S5).

Preprocessing and construction for tasks of oral diseases and treatment identification

We collected 93,500 images, including PAN and five types of intraoral images, each of which was annotated with bounding boxes for every disease and tooth region, as well as FDI notations. Using these spatial annotations, we directly determined the image-specific diagnosis: if a bounding box for a given disease was present, the task was mapped to “yes”; otherwise “no.” Moreover, we calculated the distance between the bounding boxes and the tooth, and constructed the disease region localization based on the tooth-region mapping (Table S6). In particular, the teeth were split into upper and lower parts in the vertical direction based on their respective jaw planes, and into right posterior, anterior, and left posterior in the horizontal direction based on their distance from the midline. Subsequently, based on the image-specific diagnosis, we randomly sampled questions for every task to construct 733,338 VQA pairs (I, Q, A) in Chinese (ZH) (Table S7). To ensure the quality of the source data used for guiding the rationale generation, we invited 6 oral specialists (PhD/DMD, >10 years of clinical experience) from a top stomatology hospital in Zhejiang Province to further correct image-specific diagnosis and disease region localization from 2865 images (Note S2). Then, we instructed GPT-4o44 (i.e., gpt-4o-2024-08-06) to annotate reasoning traces based on the provided images, questions, answers, and potential existing locations with predefined prompts (Table S8). We manually reviewed 5% of the rationales to ensure fluency and logic. Finally, we obtained 26,397 rationale VQA pairs (I, Q, A, R, L) for 2865 images and 706,941 simple VQA pairs (I, Q, A) for the remaining 90,635 images.

We translated the original ZH dataset to English (EN) by dividing samples into two groups based on the presence of a rationale. For samples without rationale, where the questions originated from a predefined list and the answers fell within a fixed range, we employed an end-to-end dictionary-based approach that covered all possible phrasings for direct matching and translation (Tables S2–S7). In contrast, for VQA pairs with rationale, we used the GPT-4o-mini44 (i.e., gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18) to guide the rationale translation with elaborate prompts (Table S9), while the questions and answers were still translated via the same direct mapping strategy. To ensure terminological consistency and professional accuracy, we implemented a constraint-based approach for rationale translation: specific medical terms were dynamically extracted from the source text and used to construct a reference constraint based on our expert-validated terminology dictionary (Tables S2–S7), which the model was required to follow during translation. Then we concatenated the translated answer with the rationale to construct the corresponding EN response. Ultimately, we randomly sampled some data items for manual review to ensure the accuracy, fluency, and naturalness of the translations, and generated 1.47 million bilingual VQA pairs for oral disease and treatment identification.

Preprocessing and construction for tasks of malocclusion

We collected 90,471 images from 14,420 patients, encompassing all the 7 modalities and diagnosis reports for each individual, which includes specific manifestations related to malocclusion tasks. We first established modality-specific diagnosis results based on the image-task mapping, which was predefined by three senior dentists (Fig. 1a). Next, we randomly sampled questions to construct 495,704 VQA pairs (I, Q, A) in ZH (Table S7). However, considering the potential misalignment of information when directly mapping the malocclusion issues from the diagnostic report to specific images (e.g. the dental crowding of a patient may occur only in the upper arch while the lower arch remains healthy), we further invited 28 orthodontic specialists (all hold DMD or higher and specialized in malocclusion treatment over 8 years) to correct 17,911 VQA pairs from 557 patients’ 3433 images (Note S2). The rationales were annotated for these pairs by GPT-4o44 (i.e., gpt-4o-2024-08-06) based on the provided images, questions, and answers with predefined prompts (Table S8). We also manually reviewed 5% of the rationales to ensure fluency and logic. Finally, we obtained 17,911 rationale VQA pairs (I, Q, A, R) for further corrected 3433 images, and 477,793 VQA pairs (I, Q, A) for 87,038 images. We translated them into EN with a similar procedure described in oral diseases and treatment identification tasks, and generated 0.99 million pairs.

Test set construction

For oral diseases and treatment identification-related tasks, we collected 1220 images along with dentist-annotated locations, which include 532 panoramic radiographs and 688 intraoral photographs. Based on its data acquisition process, each panoramic image corresponds to a unique patient, while intraoral images typically cover five modalities per patient. For malocclusion-related tasks, we gathered another 1475 images from 241 patients, each containing all seven modalities along with corresponding malocclusion diagnosis reports. Then, with the same data preprocessing pipeline of training set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{D}}}}}_{2}$$\end{document}D2 (Fig. 1b), we derived the diagnosis and region localization for each image. To ensure data fidelity, we implemented the same domain-specific calibration with the expert panel utilized for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\mathcal{D}}}}}_{2}$$\end{document}D2, engaging 6 oral specialists (>10 years clinical experience) for oral disease and treatment tasks and 28 orthodontic specialists (>8 years experience) for malocclusion data (Note S2). Finally, according to the predefined question lists (Table S7) and expert-calibrated medical lexicon-based translation process, we constructed bilingual VQA pairs (I, Q, A) without rationales, including 19,600 pairs for oral diseases and treatment identification, and 15,342 pairs for malocclusion.

Clinical study set construction

In order to construct the clinical study set, we included a total of 2095 patients to obtain medical images through various imaging devices. We sampled 3011 images to create 3600 VQA pairs covering all 36 tasks, ensuring that reliable conclusions could be drawn for each task. As for the ground truth labeling, we invited four senior expert dentists from orthodontics or oral medicine specialties (all from a top-tier stomatological hospital in China, holding PhD/DMD degrees and senior professional titles, with an average of over 20 years of clinical experience) to annotate each VQA pair (Fig. S1, Note S2). Specifically, each expert was required to provide a diagnostic answer, their confidence (low, medium, or high), and a complexity rating (easy, moderate, or difficult) for each case based on the current image and task. We rigorously excluded annotations with low confidence. Then, we conducted a majority vote on the remaining annotations to determine the final answer. In cases of a tie or if the selected answer indicated “The answer is indeterminate from current imaging,” the data item was directly removed. Finally, we combined the annotations corresponding to the final answer, selecting the highest confidence and the most difficult complexity as supplementary annotations. Through this meticulously designed annotation and filtering process, we retained 3105 VQA pairs (I, Q, A) from 2642 images of 1946 patients. Among these cases, 94.0% of cases had valid annotations (in high or medium confidence) from all four experts, and fewer than 1% retained valid annotations from only two experts. Additionally, 91.6% of cases were supported by more than half of the experts, and the mean inter-rater agreement under majority voting reached 86.7%.

Out-of-distribution (OOD) dataset construction

To rigorously evaluate the model’s generalizability across diverse populations and imaging equipment, we curated two publicly available, peer-reviewed external testing datasets from distinct geographic regions: (1) Dentex (Türkiye): Sourced from the Dental Enumeration and Tooth Pathosis Detection Benchmark for Panoramic X-ray57, this dataset consists of panoramic radiographs collected from three hospitals in Türkiye. We utilized the official test split containing 242 images from patients aged 12 and older. The ground truth, covering caries, impacted teeth, and apical periodontitis, was originally annotated by dental students and verified by experts. We standardized these labels into our VQA format, generating 1936 bilingual VQA pairs. (2) TDD (USA): Derived from the Tufts Dental Database58, this dataset comprises panoramic radiographs from the Tufts University School of Dental Medicine. We selected 203 images that included detailed expert-annotated diagnostic reports. Using DeepSeek-R169 for information extraction followed by manual expert verification, we structured the diagnostic labels for impacted teeth, apical periodontitis, and residual roots, resulting in 1218 bilingual VQA pairs. Both datasets underwent the same translation and quality control pipeline as our test set to ensure comparability.

Details of DentVLM architecture

We adopted the general-purpose Qwen2-VL-7B46 as the base model and fine-tuned it on oral healthcare applications (Fig. 2c). Unlike the conventional architecture of MLLM71, which comprises a vision encoder, a connector, and an LLM decoder, we removed the connector in DentVLM. Instead, it employs multimodal rotational position embedding to unify the encoding of both visual and textual inputs (Fig. S4). DentVLM’s vision encoder is a Vision Transformer72 (ViT) with 675M parameters, a depth of 32 layers, a patch size of 14, and a hidden size of 3584, whose initial weights were imported from Qwen2-VL-7B46. It supports dynamic resolution encoding73 of images to minimize feature degradation caused by image resizing. Following the vision blocks, a patch merger, implemented as a simple MLP layer, compresses each group of adjacent 2 × 2 tokens into a single token, thus reducing resource overhead. The LLM decoder is a 7B-parameter language model from the Qwen274 families, with a depth of 28 layers and a hidden size of 3584. It supports a maximum context length of 32,768 tokens and can process concatenated token sequences of image and text, generating output text in an autoregressive manner.

Two-stage training pipeline of DentVLM

Downstream evaluation details

To evaluate the capabilities of DentVLM, we curated a bilingual test set consisting of 910 patients, 2695 images, and 34,942 VQA pairs, covering all 36 tasks. Statistical analysis

In the overall performance comparison of DentVLM and baselines, as well as across various diagnostic scenarios in the clinical study, we conducted t-tests to determine whether the differences observed were statistically significant. The null hypothesis posits no discernible difference between two competitors, and P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. Additionally, we calculated the 95% confidence interval (CI) to assess the uncertainty around the mean Accuracy or the Hit-rate. We first computed the sample mean, followed by the standard error of the mean, which was derived from the sample’s standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. The margin of error was then determined by multiplying the standard error by the critical value from the Student’s t-distribution corresponding to a 95% confidence (i.e., 1.96). This margin was finally added to and subtracted from the sample mean to establish the upper and lower bounds of 95% CI.

Diagnosis comparison with baseline models

First, DentVLM was evaluated on the test set by comparing against commonly used proprietary general-purpose MLLMs (i.e., GPT-4o-2024-11-2044, GPT-4o-mini-2024-07-1844, Gemini-2.5-Flash-04-1745, Gemini-2.0-Flash45, and Gemini-2.0-Flash-Lite45), open-source MLLMs (i.e., DeepSeek-VL259, Gemma3-4B60, InternVL-2.5-8B61, LLaVA-OneVision-7B62, Llama-3.2-11B63, Qwen2-VL-7B/72B46, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B/72B64), as well as domain-specific medical MLLMs (i.e., LLaVA-Med-v1.554, HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B/34B55, and RadFM56) (Fig. 3a–d, g, Tables S13–S16). For the proprietary models, testing was conducted through their respective official API services. The open-source models were evaluated locally by their released weights. We adopted a two-step inference strategy for these baselines. The original image and question were first input into the model to obtain an initial response. Subsequently, a task-specific prompt was used to guide the model to extract the final answer from the response for evaluation. The prompt was customized based on the language of the question and the expected format of the task’s answer (Table S10). For DentVLM, we directly input the image and the corresponding question to prompt the model to generate the final answer along with its rationale. We utilized the vLLM76 library to accelerate inference, with a batch size of 1. The minimum and maximum pixel values were set to 4 × 28 × 28 and 8192 × 28 × 28, respectively. The model was deployed independently on a single NVIDIA A800-80G GPU, supporting a maximum input token length of 16,384 and a maximum output length of 512 tokens. The temperature was set to 0.1.

Location

We compared DentVLM with GPT-4o-2024-11-2044, DeepSeek-VL259, Qwen2-VL-7B46, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B64 for localization (Fig. 3e). Following the same two-stage inference strategy as in previous experiments, we guided the baseline models to output combinations of the nine predefined location descriptors to compute the final IoU scores (Note S1). In detail, we first used the original image along with the bilingual prompts to query the MLLMs. Next, based on the initial response, we further guided the MLLMs to extract the final answer using the corresponding instruction (Table S11). For DentVLM, these descriptors were directly extracted from the generated rationale using an exact match strategy. The IoUs were computed on the 5749 cases in which DentVLM correctly diagnosed the disease or identified the treatment, and the condition was indeed present.

ROC curves and confusion matrices

To evaluate diagnostic discrimination, we calculated ROC curves (Fig. 3f) and confusion matrices based on analysis (Table S12) for 32 binary tasks (Tables S2–S4). Unlike traditional deep learning models (e.g., ToothNet22) or standard FMs (e.g., Panderm43), which output class-specific probabilities directly, VLMs operate by predicting tokens from a vast vocabulary (e.g., 152,064 tokens for our base model) in an autoregressive manner. Therefore, to construct ROC curves for DentVLM, we first identified the token position of the answer and then extracted its generative probability. Additionally, due to the inaccessibility of generative logits in closed-source commercial APIs, comparative analysis was restricted to open-source models, Qwen2-VL-7B46 and Qwen2.5-VL-7B64.

Clinical study setup

We designed a multi-center reader study involving 32 participants from 10 regions in China to assess DentVLM’s clinical utility (Fig. 4a). They were kept entirely independent of the data annotators and recruited solely for clinical evaluation. To establish a rigorous expertise hierarchy, participants were stratified into three groups based on professional qualification: (1) Junior Group (n = 7): Comprising 7 final-year dental students, who possess foundational knowledge but minimal independent clinical experience, serving as a baseline for non-specialist performance. (2) Intermediate Group (n = 13): Consisting of general practitioners holding a DMD degree or equivalent but without postgraduate specialty training, representing competent primary care practitioners. And all of them are working as general practitioners at private clinics or hospitals in rural regions in China; (3) Senior Group (n = 12): Composed entirely of orthodontic specialists with postgraduate specialty and residency training. All of them are working at top stomatological hospitals in China, and hold academic appointments (e.g., Associate Professors or Lecturers) at top dental schools.

To ensure the quality of results and reduce the influence of subjective or accidental factors from individual readers, we divided the entire dataset into two parts and employed a carefully designed data allocation strategy to assess both self- and group-consistency for readers (Fig. S5a–d, Note S4). The first part Didp, contains a total of 2817 VQA pairs, which were evenly assigned to each reader and could be used to evaluate their self-consistency. The second part Dgv consists of four subsets, each containing 72 data items labeled as Dgv{i}(i = 1, 2, 3, 4), which were used to validate the consistency of clinical results in different readers’ groups.

The study was conducted on a custom web-based platform and consisted of three phases: (1) Independent Diagnosis: participants diagnosed cases solely based on clinical images and questions; (2) AI-Assisted Diagnosis: participants re-evaluated cases with assistance from DentVLM, provided either as direct answers (DentVLM-A) or answers with reasoning path (DentVLM-R); (3) Qualitative Human Evaluation: participants rated the quality of DentVLM’s responses with “Accuracy” dimension for the final answer, and five dimensions for the rationale, including Correctness, Completeness, Fairness, Faithfulness, and Acceptability65–67. Specifically, we established a discrete 4-point scale for the “Accuracy” dimension: 0 (Cannot judge from current image), 1 (Incorrect), 2 (Partially Correct), and 3 (Correct). For multi-class tasks, dentists assigned a score of 1 or 3 based on the accuracy of the result. For multi-label tasks, where the model may only distinguish part of all existing diseases, readers would give a score of 2. Moreover, if the dentist believed the result could not be judged based on the current image, they gave a score of 0. The scores for the rationale evaluation dimensions all range from 1 to 5, with 1 for poor, 2 for fair, 3 for moderate, 4 for good, and 5 for excellent. And they respectively emphasize whether the rationale: (1) aligns with diagnostic logic and guidelines; (2) integrates multiple perspectives and information without being one-sided; (3) contains no evident biases or safety concerns; (4) avoids fabrications, misleading the user, or nonsensical statements; and (5) the overall acceptance level of the reasoning content.

Generalization over OOD tasks

To evaluate the zero-shot generalization ability of DentVLM, we mined our collected data to identify four tasks that were not present in our training set: bone islands, embedded tooth, cyst, and veneer (Table S19). With the same construction pipeline as the test set, we obtained 774, 366, 328, and 74 instances for these four tasks, respectively. And we adopted the zero-shot inference, where DentVLM generates the final responses only with images and questions.

Generalization over base models

We evaluated the adaptability of our training pipeline with additional base models, such as Qwen2-VL-2B46 and Gemma3-4B60. Compared with the original base model Qwen2-VL-7B, Qwen2-VL-2B has a similar architecture but a different scale, where the hidden sizes are both 1536 in the vision encoder and the LLM decoder. Gemma3-4B follows the general three-component architecture of MLLM, which includes a vision encoder77, a connector, and an LLM decoder. The vision encoder is a ViT to extract visual features with approximately 417M parameters, a depth of 27 layers, a patch size of 14, a hidden size of 1152, and a fixed input image resolution of 896 × 896. The connector is a simple projection layer that maps visual features into the latent space of the language model. The LLM decoder has a depth of 34 layers and a hidden size of 2560, which supports a maximum context length of 128K tokens. We trained all three models under the same hardware and software environments, using the same training data and hyperparameter configuration, and then performed inference on the test set with the same settings.

Inference across different hardware

We also examined DentVLM’s inference latency across different GPU hardware using the clinical study set (Fig. S9g). We deployed the DentVLM on a single GPU and set the batch size to 1 to closely mimic a real diagnostic process, where questions were answered sequentially, although larger batch sizes can significantly reduce total processing time. Furthermore, we optimized this process using the vLLM76 library and employed the same parameter configurations (i.e., threshold of pixel values, input token length, output length, and temperature) for all evaluations.

Practical utility for DentVLM in real-world demonstrations

We explored the potential real-world applications of DentVLM, such as health management at home (Fig. 6a), intelligent diagnosis at the hospital (Fig. 6b), and a multi-agent collaborative framework (Fig. 6d).

Health management at home

Users capture five intraoral images at home using edge devices (e.g., smartphones, portable oral imaging devices). Then, for each intraoral image and its corresponding oral diseases, we construct VQA pairs based on the image task mapping and predefined question templates (Fig. 1a, Table S7). Subsequently, DentVLM’s responses for each image are consolidated to generate a list that contains all image-specific disease diagnosis results. These lists are aggregated using either majority voting or matching voting to determine the potential disease list for the current patient, thereby supporting personalized remote preliminary screening (Note S6).

Intelligent diagnosis at hospital

Patients are captured with seven clinical oral images at the hospital using professional equipment. For each modality and its corresponding malocclusion tasks, DentVLM generates diagnostic results based on constructed VQA pairs similar to the health management scenario. The diagnosis lists are aggregated via the same voting strategies to produce a symptom list, which was used for dentists’ proofreading, thereby facilitating the construction of an intelligent, efficient, and clinically feasible assisted diagnosis workflow for healthcare institutions.

Evaluation details for health management and intelligent diagnosis

We collected 305 intraoral images from 61 patients captured via edge devices, with 5 images per patient. We invited 3 expert dentists with more than 8 years of experience to annotate their manifestations on seven oral disease tasks (i.e., caries, periodontal disease, wedge-shaped defects, demineralization, plaque, tooth wear, and calculus). For malocclusion tasks, we selected 69 patients from the test set with 7 modalities with high annotation quality. Multi-agent collaborative framework

Witnessing the outstanding performance of current reasoning models across various real-world scenarios, we sought to explore integrating a reasoning model with DentVLM, thereby constructing a multi-agent collaborative framework to provide a more user-friendly interaction experience. For each input image and question, DentVLM first performs reasoning and produces an initial response. This response is then passed to the selected DeepSeek-R169 reasoning model, which refines it into a more professional reply by incorporating the historical conversation context, the user’s query, and the initial answer (Fig. 6d). We dynamically select the language of the prompt for DeepSeek-R1 based on whether the user query is Chinese or English (Table S20).

Evaluation details for the multi-agent collaborative framework

To further validate the effectiveness of this framework, we sampled 50 VQA pairs from the clinical study set, consisting of 50 images and 31 tasks covering all modalities. Using this data, we interacted with the developed framework to generate responses spanning multiple rounds of dialog: 5 samples with one interaction round, 37 samples with two rounds, and 8 samples with three rounds. Three professional dentists with more than 8 years of experience were invited to evaluate the interaction records on seven dimensions: correctness, completeness, fairness, faithfulness, acceptability, readability, and coherence, using a score ranging from 1 to 5, with 1 for poor, 2 for fair, 3 for moderate, 4 for good, and 5 for excellent. The first five dimensions share similar meanings with those used in the qualitative evaluation of DentVLM-generated rationales in the clinical study. The readability refers to whether the generated content is easy to read and understand, while coherence assesses the contextual consistency and logical flow throughout the multi-turn dialogs.

Computing hardware and software

Codes for the entire work were implemented in Python (v3.10.16). For the two-stage training and various downstream evaluations, we used a server with 8 × 80 GB NVIDIA A800-80G GPUs and Ubuntu 20.04 OS. The training process is configured on the 8-GPU single-node by DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 using LLaMA-Factory78 (https://github.com/hiyouga/LLaMA-Factory) with PyTorch (v2.4.0, CUDA 12.4) and Transformers (v4.45.2). The evaluation of DentVLM is deployed on a single GPU with vLLM (v0.6.3.post1) and vLLM-flash-attn (v2.6.1). For proprietary MLLMs, we employed GPT by OpenAI Batch API (https://platform.openai.com) and Gemini by Google Developer API (https://ai.google.dev). For Gemma3, InternVL-2.5, LLaVA-OneVision, Llama-3.2, Qwen2-VL, and Qwen2.5-VL, we deployed them in consistent ways with DentVLM. For other models, we implemented them by modification based on their official inference ways: DeepSeek-VL2 (https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-VL2), LLaVA-Med (https://github.com/microsoft/LLaVA-Med), HuatuoGPT-Vision (https://github.com/FreedomIn-telligence/HuatuoGPT-Vision), and RadFM (https://github.com/chaoyi-wu/RadFM). Additionally, all web-based platforms developed in this work utilized Flask as the backend framework, MySQL for database management, Docker and Docker Compose for containerization and orchestration, along with Nginx as a reverse proxy server.
