Safety of Intravenous Cangrelor Versus Dual Oral Antiplatelet Loading Therapy in Endovascular Treatment of Tandem Lesions: An Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Aaron Rodriguez‐Calienes, Marion Oliver, Ameer E. Hassan, Juan Vivanco‐Suarez, Afshin A. Divani, Marc Ribo, Nils Petersen, Michael Abraham, Johanna Fifi, Waldo R. Guerrero, Amer M. Malik, James E. Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProcedural intravenous cangrelor has been proposed as an effective platelet inhibition strategy for stenting in acute ischemic stroke. We aimed to compare the safety profile of low‐dose intravenous cangrelor versus dual oral antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) loading in patients with acute cervical tandem lesions.MethodsWe retrospectively identified cases from an international multicenter cohort who underwent intraprocedural administration of intravenous cangrelor (15 μg/kg followed by an infusion of 2 μg/kg per min) or DAPT loading during acute tandem lesions intervention. Safety outcomes included rates of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, parenchymal hematoma type 2, petechial hemorrhage, and in‐stent thrombosis. Inverse probability of treatment weighting matching was used to reduce confounding.ResultsFrom 691 patients, we included 195 patients, 30 of whom received intravenous cangrelor and 165 DAPT. The DAPT regimens were aspirin+clopidogrel (93.3%) or aspirin+ticagrelor (6.6%). After inverse probability of treatment weighting, the patients treated with cangrelor were not at greater odds of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (odds ratio [OR], 1.30 [95% CI, 0.09–17.3]; P=0.837), symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage–parenchymal hematoma type 2 (OR, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.05–4.98]; P=0.589), or petechial hemorrhage (OR, 1.11 [95% CI, 0.38–3.28]; P=0.836). Similarly, the rate of in‐stent thrombosis was not significantly different between the 2 groups (1.8% versus 0%; P=0.911).ConclusionCangrelor at the half dose of the myocardial infarction protocol showed a similar safety profile compared with the commonly used DAPT loading protocols in patients with acute tandem lesions. Further studies with larger samples are warranted to elucidate the safety of antiplatelet therapy in tandem lesions.

Intraprocedural antiplatelet therapy (APT) is critical for the use of acute carotid artery stenting (CAS) during mechanical thrombectomy (MT) in the presence or absence of concomitant tandem lesions (TLs). 1 , 2 To date, there is limited evidence of an optimal antiplatelet inhibition regimen. Coadministration of dual oral antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and an oral P2Y12 receptor inhibitor is the most common APT approach for planned CAS. 3 On the other hand, the use of DAPT in the acute phase may be challenging. First, it requires emergency placement of a nasogastric tube to rapidly administer the initial oral loading dose to prevent the risk of aspiration. 4 Additionally, clopidogrel is affected by underlying comorbidities and cytochrome P450 genetic polymorphisms interact with other medications, and thus, the effect of APT may take up to 4 hours. 5

Prasugrel and ticagrelor are newer oral APT agents, with a relatively slow onset (15–30 minutes) and offset of action (1–9 days). 5 Recently, low‐dose intravenous cangrelor has been proposed as a valuable therapeutic option in the management of procedural platelet inhibition for neuroendovascular procedures due to its unique pharmacokinetics, demonstrating fast and reliable onset of action (2 minutes), and reversible effects (1 hour). 6 However, its use in TLs and acute stroke is limited. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Herein, we aimed to compare the safety of low‐dose intravenous cangrelor versus DAPT in patients with acute TLs during MT.

Methods

This study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. 12 The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

We used a pooled, multicenter cohort registry for the study. Patient eligibility and methods of the collaboration have been reported previously. 13 Briefly, the study included adult patients with TL treated with MT within 24 hours after stroke onset from 16 stroke centers (15 hospitals in the United States and 1 in Spain) between January 2015 and December 2020. TL was defined as an intracranial large‐vessel occlusion (petrous, sigmoid, or terminus segment of the internal carotid artery (ICA) or M1 or proximal M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery) with concomitant extracranial ICA stenosis ≥50% according to the NASCET (North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial). 14 All patients received MT for the intracranial occlusion with cervical ICA lesion treatment. Cervical ICA lesion treatment consisted of CAS with and without angioplasty or angioplasty alone. The endovascular and medical therapeutic interventions were performed according to the protocol of each institution and at the discretion of the interventionalists.

We identified cases of procedural intravenous cangrelor administration or DAPT loading dose with no additional intravenous APTs during an acute TL intervention. Patients were divided into 2 groups: (1) low dose intravenous cangrelor at half the dose of the myocardial infarction protocol (bolus of 15 µg/kg administered over 2 minutes, followed by an infusion rate of 2 µg/kg/min), and (2) DAPT loading dose (combinations: aspirin [325 mg]+clopidogrel [300–600 mg] or aspirin [325 mg]+ticagrelor [180 mg]). The rationale for using a low dose of cangrelor was based on previous reports of its favorable efficacy and safety in neuroendovascular procedures 9 , 15 and the increased rates of postprocedural hemorrhage observed with the full dose. 7 , 8 , 10 Maintenance doses for DAPT were initiated after initial computed tomography was obtained. Details on APT are available in Table S1.

Institutional review board approval was obtained from all sites.

Data Collection

The variables included research center, demographics (age, sex, race), comorbidities (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, smoking history, previous stroke, coronary artery disease), prior use of antiplatelets, transfer from outside institution, glucose levels at admission, baseline modified Rankin scale (mRS), admission National Institute of Health Stroke Scalescore, Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score, cause of cervical ICA lesion, location of intracranial occlusion, complete occlusion of cervical ICA, use of intravenous thrombolysis, use of general anesthesia, first‐line MT technique (aspiration device, stent retriever, or combined), use of intra‐arterial tissue plasminogen activator, use of intraprocedural heparin, and cervical ICA stenting.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary study outcomes were (1) symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) defined by the ECASS‐3 (European Collaborative Acute Stroke Study) criteria, 16 (2) a composite of sICH and parenchymal hematoma type 2 (PH2) [sICH‐PH2], and (3) petechial hemorrhage (hemorrhagic infarction types 1 and 2), according to the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification. 17 All the outcomes were assessed in the follow‐up computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging at 24±12 hours. We also assessed the rate of hemorrhagic transformation of the ischemic infarct categorized as no hemorrhage, hemorrhagic infarction type 1– hemorrhagic infarction type 2– parenchymal hematoma type 1, and sICH‐PH2.

Additional outcomes of interest included successful reperfusion (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction 2b–3), complete reperfusion (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction 3), discharge mRS 0 to 2, 90‐day mRS 0 to 2, intraprocedural in‐stent thrombosis (occurrence of thrombosis within the stent during endovascular procedure), and in‐hospital and 90‐day mortality rate.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline patient characteristics were presented using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and median (interquartile range) and mean±SD for continuous variables. For the univariable analysis, we used the analysis of variance test for continuous variables and chi‐square test for categorical variables, as needed.

Clinically relevant variables for the outcomes of interest were considered for adjustment. Missing data for these variables were handled through a multiple imputation by chained equations method if missing observations accounted for <20% of total observations. 18 Otherwise, they were not included in the analysis. Quality of imputation was assessed graphically through a density plot and a strip plot for original and imputed observations.

To compare the outcomes between groups, we performed matching using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to generate pseudo‐populations stratified by treatment. IPTW was selected on the basis of variable balance, preservation of intravenous cangrelor cases, and reduced bias in small sample sizes. 19 Propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model with the following covariates: admission National Institute of Health Stroke Scale score, Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score, use of intravenous thrombolysis, and use of intraprocedural heparin. These covariates were measured before the assignment of treatment. They are also known to be associated with our primary study outcomes and were selected a priori on the basis of previous literature 13 , 20 and expert knowledge on the topic. Variables that were related only to the exposure but not to the outcome were not selected. 21 Absolute standardized mean differences <0.1 were taken to imply the absence of substantial imbalance between the weighted treatment groups. 22 Baseline patient characteristics were compared again after IPTW. For each continuous variable, analysis of variance was used, with weights coming from IPTW. For each categorical variable, a weighted logistic regression analysis was used. 23

To estimate the effect of cangrelor versus DAPT on the efficacy and safety outcomes, we used a survey‐weighted generalized linear model. All the statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Of the 691 patients from the pooled data set, 195 were included, 30 of whom received intravenous cangrelor (used at 4 sites) and 165 DAPT (used at 16 sites) (Figure S1). Demographics data, baseline characteristics, and hemorrhage rates are presented in Table 1 and (Figure 1A). The DAPT regimens were as follows: aspirin+clopidogrel (93.3%) or aspirin+ticagrelor (6.6%). After IPTW adjustment, there were no differences among the variables included in the IPTW (Figure S2).

The results for the additional outcomes are presented in Table 2. The odds of complete reperfusion (56.7% versus 46.1%; OR, 1.53 [95% CI, 0.67–3.5]; P=0.310) were similar between groups. In‐stent thrombosis was presented by 3 patients (1.8%) in the DAPT group during hospitalization and none in the cangrelor group. Patients treated with cangrelor showed a trend toward higher odds of achieving a discharge with a mRS 0 to 2 (40% versus 26.7%; OR, 2.42 [95% CI, 1–5.78]; P=0.046). Finally, no differences were observed between the 2 groups for 90‐day mRS 0 to 2 (55.6% versus 52.1%; OR, 1.68 [95% CI, 0.70–4]; P=0.236), in‐hospital mortality rate (6.7% versus 6.7%; OR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.16–4.71]; P=0.826), or 90‐day mortality rate (11.1% versus 13%; OR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.20–2.94]; P=0.702).

Discussion

We found that patients with acute TLs during MT who received intraprocedural intravenous cangrelor at the half dose of the myocardial infarction protocol had similar rates of sICH, sICH‐PH2, and petechial hemorrhage compared with patients who received intraprocedural DAPT loading doses. Furthermore, both groups had similar rates of complete reperfusion, 90‐day mRS 0 to 2, and in‐hospital and 90‐day mortality rate. To date, this is the first report comparing intravenous cangrelor with DAPT loading therapy in the setting of emergency CAS for TLs during MT.

According to observational cohort studies, 1 , 24 acute CAS for TLs is associated with higher recanalization rates and better clinical outcomes compared with angioplasty alone. Nevertheless, it has been associated with higher rates of sICH and a small risk of in‐stent thrombosis. 25 , 26 Thus, APT selection with a high safety profile and fast action onset is critical. Recently, the use of intraprocedural single‐antiplatelet regimens has been preferred over DAPT loading therapy. A recent guideline suggests that for patients undergoing CAS to treat acute TLs during MT, it may be reasonable to administer a loading dose of intravenous or oral glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors or a P2Y12 inhibitor followed by maintenance intravenous infusion or oral dosing. 27 However, this recommendation was based solely on the results from one retrospective study. 20 Similarly, a Delphi consensus recommended the use of a single‐antiplatelet regimen with intravenous aspirin or intravenous glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors as a first‐line approach for CAS in the setting of endovascular treatment. 28

Initial small case series have reported the safety profile of intravenous cangrelor when used in various acute ischemic stroke cases. 6 Salahuddin et al 11 described lower rates of sICH using intravenous cangrelor (0%; 0/12 patients) at half the cardiac dose compared with DAPT (13%; 3/23 patients) in a mixed population of anterior and posterior circulation stroke requiring acute cervical or intracranial stenting. However, the small sample size may not render the results generalizable to other institutions. Our multicenter study further expands the available evidence and suggests that intravenous cangrelor is associated with a similar safety profile as DAPT for acute treatment of anterior circulation TLs in which stenting is performed. In addition to its safety profile, the short half‐life of intravenous cangrelor (3–6 minutes) differentiates it from other APTs in the context of clinical worsening related to ICH. Furthermore, the immediate cangrelor suspension can rapidly normalize platelet function with the aim of limiting ICH extension (if it occurs) or systemic hemorrhage and performing rapid and safe surgical treatment if necessary. 29

An association has been reported between “aggressive” intraoperative APT (intravenous glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors or P2Y12 inhibitors) and carotid stent patency on day 1 after acute TL stenting. 20 The rapid onset and direct effect of cangrelor without requiring a transformation to active metabolites represent ideal features to immediately prevent platelet aggregation after CAS placement. Small case series examining the use of intravenous cangrelor for stenting in acute ischemic stroke have reported 0% rates of in‐stent thrombosis. 7 , 9 , 11 , 30 Similarly, we observed that none of our patients treated with intravenous cangrelor presented in‐stent thrombosis.

The efficacy outcomes were also reassuring in patients treated with cangrelor. We observed a trend toward better functional outcomes with cangrelor considering the significant higher rate of discharge with mRS 0 to 2 and the nonsignificant higher rate of 90‐day mRS 0 to 2 in patients who received intraprocedural intravenous cangrelor. Furthermore, despite no significant differences, the rates of both successful and complete reperfusion were higher in the cangrelor group compared with DAPT. These findings are in line with previous literature reporting better functional outcomes with cangrelor compared with other intravenous APTs in emergency stenting of the ICA. 31

There are several limitations to our study. First, we included a small sample size of patients treated with cangrelor. Second, because of the retrospective nature of the data, a potential selection bias might have affected our findings. Similarly, the decision to use cangrelor or DAPT was dependent on the institutional protocol of each center, which means a substantial risk of selection bias. In addition, despite applying similar protocols, both endovascular strategy and intraprocedural APT are subject to substantial variations. Third, the outcomes were self‐adjudicated by independent investigators at each center without external control or core imaging laboratory adjudication. Fourth, the atrial fibrillation rate was low (9.8%) in our population; therefore, our findings may not apply to patients with atrial fibrillation treated with oral anticoagulants. Fifth, we did not collect the exact time of administration of the APTs. This could produce bias in our results, as delays might increase the risk of thrombosis, which might explain some of the presented findings. Finally, relevant safety outcomes, such as stent patency at follow‐up and extracranial hemorrhagic events, were not available.

In conclusion, cangrelor at half the dose of the myocardial infarction dose protocol showed a similar safety profile compared with the commonly used DAPT loading protocols in patients with acute TLs. Further studies with larger samples are warranted to elucidate the safety of APT in TLs.
