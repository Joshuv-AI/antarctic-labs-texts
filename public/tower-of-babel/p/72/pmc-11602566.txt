CXCR4 orchestrates the TOX-programmed exhausted phenotype of CD8+ T cells via JAK2/STAT3 pathway
Authors: Canhui Cao, Miaochun Xu, Ye Wei, Ting Peng, Shitong Lin, Xiaojie Liu, Yashi Xu, Tian Chu, Shiyi Liu, Ping Wu, Bai Hu, Wencheng Ding, Li Li, Ding Ma, Peng Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryEvidence from clinical trials suggests that CXCR4 antagonists enhance immunotherapy effectiveness in several cancers. However, the specific mechanisms through which CXCR4 contributes to immune cell phenotypes are not fully understood. Here, we employed single-cell transcriptomic analysis and identified CXCR4 as a marker gene in T cells, with CD8+PD-1high exhausted T (Tex) cells exhibiting high CXCR4 expression. By blocking CXCR4, the Tex phenotype was attenuated in vivo. Mechanistically, CXCR4-blocking T cells mitigated the Tex phenotype by regulating the JAK2-STAT3 pathway. Single-cell RNA/TCR/ATAC-seq confirmed that Cxcr4-deficient CD8+ T cells epigenetically mitigated the transition from functional to exhausted phenotypes. Notably, clinical sample analysis revealed that CXCR4+CD8+ T cells showed higher expression in patients with a non-complete pathological response. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the mechanism by which CXCR4 orchestrates CD8+ Tex cells and provide a rationale for combining CXCR4 antagonists with immunotherapy in clinical trials.Keywords: CXCR4, T cell exhaustion, CD8 T cells, JAK2-STAT3 pathway, PD-1, single-cell RNA-seq, single-cell ATAC-seq, tumor microenvironment, immunotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade

Introduction

In cancer or chronic viral infections, T cells exposed to cognate antigens for extended periods often experience attenuation of their effector functions, leading to a deviation from their progression into functional effector (Teff) or memory (Tmem) T cells, known as T cell exhaustion (Tex) or dysfunction.1,2,3,4 Although CD4+ Tex and CD8+ Tex cells share a core transcriptional signature, they exhibit distinct expression patterns for exhaustion-related molecules and transcription factors.5 Exhaustion in CD8+ Tex cells is marked by epigenetic alterations in gene expression, leading to the upregulation of inhibitory markers,6,7 such as programmed cell death 1 (PD-1), lymphocyte activating 3 (LAG3), T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM3), and T cell immunoglobulin and ITIM domain (TIGIT), which are key targets for tumor immunotherapies.8 CD8+ Tex cells are characterized by metabolic shifts, impaired proliferative potential, reduced survival, and decreased production of cytokines, such as interleukin-2 (IL-2), tumor necrosis factor, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ).5 Progression of the Tex phenotype varies among different cancer types within the complex milieu of the tumor immune microenvironment (TiME).9 Previous studies have demonstrated that exhausted T cells are tightly regulated by transcription factors, such as thymocyte selection-associated high-mobility group box (TOX),10,11,12,13 along with other regulators that shape exhausted states in CD8+ T cells.14,15 Furthermore, TOX has emerged as a pivotal controller of tumor-specific T cell differentiation, and the exhaustion program induced by TOX is also implicated in averting excessive activation of T cells within tumor environments.10

Considering the complex and diverse characteristics of CD8+ Tex cells, standalone immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies may yield limited clinical benefits for certain cancer treatments.3 Thus, concurrently targeting the alternative pathways involved in Tex development within the context of cancer is important for enhancing therapeutic outcomes.16 A growing body of clinical trials investigating C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) have revealed that CXCR4 antagonists improve patient outcomes and enhance immunotherapy effectiveness in several cancer types (Figure S1A; see STAR Methods). Small synthetic molecules that bind to CXCR4 and inhibit its function, such as plerixafor (AMD-3100), motixafortide (BL-8040), and mavorixafor (AMD-070), have been used in combination with molecular-based therapies to enhance disease outcomes and bolster immunotherapy efficacy in metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma (NCT02826486),16 advanced renal cell carcinoma (NCT02923531),17 and solid tumors (NCT02737072) (Table S1; see STAR Methods). Furthermore, the strategic combination of CXCR4 antagonists with ICBs has been incorporated into multi-center clinical trials across various cancer types (Table S1). CXCR4 antagonists have also been integrated into targeted therapies, such as tyrosine kinase receptor inhibitors for metastatic renal cell carcinoma,18 microtubule inhibitors for HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer,19 and proteasome inhibitors for refractory multiple myeloma,20 as well as in combination with anti-CD20 immunotherapy in chronic lymphocytic leukemia.21 CXCR4 inhibitors have been shown to enhance the anti-tumor activity of chemotherapy and radiation therapy in several clinical trials, including NCT01838395,22 NCT01439568,23 NCT02995655,24 and NCT01977677.25 Furthermore, CXCR4 antagonists have been investigated as single anti-tumor agents, showing promise in improving disease outcomes in several clinical trials (Figure S1B; see STAR Methods). Nevertheless, the complex molecular mechanisms by which CXCR4 induces and stabilizes the phenotypic attributes of immune cells within tumors remain unclear.

In this study, we analyzed single-cell transcriptomic data from both normal and tumor tissues to identify CXCR4 expression patterns in T cells. Our analysis revealed pronounced CXCR4 expression in CD8+ PD-1high Tex cells. To elucidate the role of CXCR4 in maintaining the CD8+ Tex phenotypes in tumors, we utilized CXCR4 antagonists and transgenic mouse models (Cxcr4flox/floxLckCre) across various tumor types. Furthermore, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)/T cell receptor sequencing (scTCR-seq) and single-cell assays for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (scATAC/scRNA-seq) to validate the TOX-mediated Tex cell phenotype induced by CXCR4 in vitro and in vivo. Our findings provide insights into the molecular mechanisms by which CXCR4 contributes to the maintenance of the Tex cell phenotype in tumors, thereby offering a rationale for the use of combined strategies involving CXCR4 blockades and ICBs in cancer treatment.

Results

scRNA-seq analysis identified CXCR4 as a marker gene for T cells, with high expression in CD8+PD-1high Tex cells in tumors

To ascertain the CXCR4 expression patterns in normal and tumor tissues, we conducted a comprehensive analysis at multiple levels of resolution. Using the expression matrix from the Human Cell Landscape (HCL) dataset,26 we observed that CXCR4 was highly expressed in immune cell clusters, especially in T cells (c6 and c24) among the 102 cell clusters (Figures S2A–S2C; see STAR Methods). CXCR4 showed high co-expression with T cell markers, including CD3D, CD3E, and CD3G (Figure S2B). Subsequently, we used a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plot to visualize the expression of CXCR4 and T and B cell markers. We found a prominent co-expression pattern of CXCR4 in T cell clusters (Figure S2D). Upon further investigation of CXCR4 expression in normal tissues using data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA),27 we observed high expression in T cell clusters across various organs, including the skin, lung, breast, prostate, and liver (Figure S2E). Moreover, single-cell transcriptomic data from the PanglaoDB dataset28 confirmed the high expression pattern of CXCR4 in T cells. Notably, among the 663 cell clusters identified in 155 samples, 176 annotated T cell clusters displayed high CXCR4 expression (Figure S2F). In addition, CXCR4 was identified to be specifically associated with CD8+ T cells in certain tissues, such as the uterus and cervix (Figure S2G).

Multiple deconvolution-based analyses of gene expression in immune cells across 33 different cancer types sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets29 demonstrated that CXCR4 consistently exhibited higher expression in CD8+ T cells compared to other immune cell types, including natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells (Figures S3A–S3C; see STAR Methods). Comparing expression among T cell subtypes, CXCR4 was found to be more highly expressed in CD8+ T cells than in other subtypes (Figure S3D). Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a significant association between CXCR4 expression and exhausted T cell phenotype across 36 cancer types from TCGA (Figure S4A; see STAR Methods). Multi-color immunohistochemistry (mIHC) analysis of cervical cancer tissues validated the co-expression patterns of CXCR4 in CD8+ T cells (Figure S4B). To further investigate CXCR4 expression in exhausted CD8+ T cells within tumors, we analyzed scRNA-seq data of CD8+ T cells in pan-cancers.30 Integrating data from 21 cancer types, CD8+ T cells were classified into 17 cell clusters from naive CD8+ T (Tn) to Tex cells (Figure S4C; see STAR Methods). Notably, T cell exhausted markers were highly expressed in Tex cell populations (clusters 11–14, Figure S4D). In examining CXCR4 expression across meta-cancer clusters, by either integrating or differentiating the data, CXCR4 was found to be highly expressed in CD8+ PD-1high Tex cells in various tumor types (Figures S4E and S4F). These findings suggest that CXCR4 is a marker gene for T cells, with increased expression in CD8+ PD-1high Tex cells within tumors.

Assessment of CXCR4 expression differences between tumors and normal tissues across TCGA cancer types showed increased CXCR4 expression in tumor samples (Figure S5A; see STAR Methods). Additionally, data from Cancer Clinical Proteomics Research (CPTAC) indicated a significant increase in CXCR4 copy-number variation in various cancer types (Figure S5B; see STAR Methods). At the proteomic level, CXCR4 was upregulated in cancer tissues, including uterine, stomach, and lung squamous cell carcinomas (Figure S5C). Furthermore, we performed mIHC staining in normal and tumor tissues and confirmed the high levels of CXCR4+CD8+ T cell infiltration in the TiME of cervical, ovarian, breast, and endometrial cancers (Figure S5D). These findings suggest a notable infiltration of CXCR4+CD8+ T cells within the TiME in various cancers. Based on the above findings of high CXCR4 expression tumor-infiltrating CD8+ PD-1high Tex, these results suggest that CXCR4 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for tumor immunotherapy.

Blocking CXCR4 exerted anti-tumor effects in syngeneic immunocompetent mice by enhancing the functional T cell phenotype and attenuating Tex phenotype

Next, we assessed the effects of CXCR4 inhibition on xenograft tumors in syngeneic immunocompetent (C57BL/6 or BALB/c), immunodeficient (NOD-SCID), and Cxcr4-transgenic (Cxcr4flox/floxLckCre) mice, respectively (Figure 1A). Notably, plerixafor showed anti-tumor effects in syngeneic immunocompetent mice, which were markedly attenuated in immunodeficient mice (Figures 1B, 1C, S6A, and S6B). Plerixafor also enhanced survival time in immunocompetent tumor-bearing mice but not in immunodeficient tumor-bearing mice with cervical (U14), ovarian (ID8), breast (4T1), and colon (CT26) carcinomas (Figure 1D). Consistently, administration of plerixafor markedly increased tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, predominantly GZMB+CD8+ T cells, and decreased PD-1+TOX+CD8+ T cells in both tumors and ascites (Figures 1E and 1F). These findings collectively suggest that plerixafor exerts its anti-tumor effects, at least in part, by enhancing the recruitment and functional activity of CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment.

To further explore the role of CXCR4 in T cells, we generated CXCR4 conditional-knockout (cKO) transgenic mice (Cxcr4flox/floxLckCre) and subsequently induced xenograft tumors in these mice in a syngeneic immunocompetent environment (Figure S6C; see STAR Methods). The Lck gene, a well-known T cell marker, is commonly used in T cell cKO transgenic mouse models.31 For comparison, we also generated xenograft tumors from control Cxcr4flox/flox mice. mIHC staining in tumors from cKO and control mice revealed that deletion of Cxcr4 in T cells led to a reduction in the exhausted CD8+ T cell phenotype, including reduced expression of exhaustion markers such as PD-1, CTLA4, and TIM-3 in CD8+ T cells (Figure S6D). In addition, receptors of PD-1 in cancer cells decreased following the reduced expression of PD-1 (Figure S6D). Flow cytometry analysis of spleens also demonstrated that Cxcr4 deficiency in T cells enhanced the functional T cell phenotype and decreased Tex phenotype by increasing the activation markers (EOMES, CD107a, IFN-γ, GZMB, CD69, CD71, and CD10332) and reducing the exhausted markers (PD-1 and TIM-3) in vivo (Figures 1G and S7A–S7C). These results suggest that inhibition of CXCR4 contributes to anti-tumor activity by reducing the Tex phenotype and increasing the functional CD8+ T cell phenotype in syngeneic immunocompetent mice.

CXCR4 inhibition promoted anti-PD-1 immunotherapy by reducing the TOX-associated exhausted phenotype in vivo

Prompted by the functional phenotype of T cells, we assessed the impact of CXCR4 and PD-1 blockade on anti-tumor immunity in vivo. We utilized U14 (HPV-deficient), ID8 (STING-deficient), and 4T-1 (ER−, PR−, and HER2−) tumor models in both syngeneic immunocompetent and Cxcr4-cKO mice. Generally, the administration of anti-PD-1 or plerixafor alone had a modest impact on reducing tumor growth (Figures 2A and 2B). However, combining plerixafor with anti-PD-1 significantly enhanced this effect, markedly decreasing tumor growth and extending survival time in mice with xenograft tumors (Figures 2A–2D). Furthermore, improved outcomes from anti-PD-1 immunotherapy were also observed in Cxcr4-cKO mice, including fewer abdominal milia metastases (Figure 2E). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis revealed increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells, characterized by a lower proportion of PD-1+, TOX+, and TOX+PD-1+CD8+ T cells in the groups treated with plerixafor, anti-PD-1, or combination (Figures 2F and S8A). Similarly, anti-PD-1 therapy in the Cxcr4-cKO mice led to more robust infiltration of IFN-γ+CD8+ and GZMB+CD8+ T cells and reduced infiltration of TOX+PD-1+CD8+ cells in tumors (Figure 2G). Upon examining IFN-γ and GZMB in CD8− T cells, we observed that IFN-γ production was increased in CD8− T cells following Cxcr4-cKO in vivo, a phenomenon not seen in the anti-PD-1 treatment groups (Figure S8B). Additionally, GZMB production in CD8− T cells increased after both Cxcr4-cKO and anti-PD-1 treatment, with a significant enhancement in the combination group (Figure S8B). It is possible that CXCR4 knockout in T cells via the LckCre system stimulates the production of IFN-γ and GZMB in both CD8+ and CD8− T cells. These findings suggest that CXCR4 blockade enhances the infiltration and functionality of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells while reducing exhausted CD8+ T cells, thereby promoting anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.

To elucidate alterations in gene expression induced by CXCR4 inhibition in tumors, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on U14 xenograft tumors subjected to various treatments (Figure S8C). Principal-component analysis revealed that the groups treated with plerixafor were closely aligned with those receiving combined therapy (Figure S8D). Certain differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the plerixafor- and combination-treated groups overlapped with those in Tox−/− mice (GEO: GSE131871),11 including Tox, Tox2, Pd-1, and Pd-l1 (Figures 2H and S8E). TOX promotes the development and persistence of Tex cells in the context of tumors and chronic viral infections,10,11,12 which could be a potential target for immunotherapy in malignancies.33,34 In RNA-seq data, Tox and Pd-1 expression were significantly downregulated after CXCR4 inhibition and combination treatment (Figure S8F). Furthermore, significant enrichment in the gene signatures associated with CD8+ Tmem and CD8+ Teff cells was observed in the plerixafor- and combination-treated groups, while gene-expression signatures of CD8+ Tex and PD-1high CD8+ T cells were notably depleted (Figure 2I). Transcriptional analysis of the downregulated DEGs in the CXCR4 inhibition and combination treatment groups revealed enrichment in several key immune-related pathways, including the PD-1, CTLA4, and T cell receptor (TCR) signaling pathways (Figures S8G and S8H). Furthermore, the expression of immune checkpoint ligands associated with Pd-1 (Pd-l1 and Pd-l2) was downregulated following CXCR4 inhibition and combination therapy (Figure S8I). A significant proportion of downregulated genes in the CXCR4-inhibited groups was strongly associated with TOX expression based on scRNA-seq across various cancer types, including TIM-3, CD80, CD86, CTLA4, ADORA2A, and LAG3 (Figure S8J). Collectively, these findings suggest that CXCR4 inhibition enhances the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy by modulating the TOX-associated Tex phenotype in tumors.

CXCR4 inhibition attenuated Tex phenotype and promoted CD8+ T cell activation by mediating abundant CD8+ TCR clonotypes and increasing MHC-I expression

To further investigate the immunopathology of CD8+ T cells in the TiME after CXCR4 inhibition, we conducted 5′ scRNA-seq combined with V(D)J/TCR-seq (scRNA/scTCR-seq) analysis of tumors subjected to different treatments (Figure 3A). Single cells from different treatment groups were prepared, barcoded, and sequenced. Following quality control, normalization, unsupervised graph-based clustering, and UMAP plots (Figure 3B), 62,061 high-quality cell profiles were obtained and grouped by lineage and sample origin, with known expression markers used for their annotation into 11 cell clusters (Figures 3B and S9A–S9D; Table S2). Analysis of cell cluster percentage in the four groups revealed a decrease in epithelium cells but an increase in immune cells (B, CD8+ T, CD4+ T, and dendritic cells) in the inhibitor-treated groups, especially in the combination group (Figures 3C, 3D, and S9E). We further examined CD8+ T cell populations, which were significantly expanded in the combination treatment group (Figure 3E and S9F). The DEGs in the combination group were enriched in the TCR signaling pathway, chemokine signaling pathway, PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in cancer, and the JAK-STAT pathway, with the downregulation of Tex markers (Pd-1, Tim3, Ctla4, and Lag3) (Figures 3F and S9G). Notably, the signatures of naive T cells (Tn, mainly Ccr7, Il7r, and Klf230), effector memory T cells (Tem, Eomes30), tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm, Cd6935), and terminally differentiated effector memory T cells (Temra, mainly Klf2 and Klf330) showed upregulation in the CXCR4 inhibition and combination treatment groups (Figures S9H and S9I).

Unsupervised clustering partitioned CD8+ T cells into four distinct clusters according to the marker genes of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, including naive (Tn), Znf683+ Cxcr6+ Trm, ISG+ (interferon-stimulated genes), and Gzmk+ Tex CD8+ T cells according to canonical markers (Figures 3G and S9J; Table S3). These patterns were regulated by common transcription factors, including STAT1/3, within the TiME (Figures S9K and S9L). The combination treatment group showed a higher proportion of naive CD8+ T cells but a lower proportion of Tex cells (Figure S9M). Primary differentiation for the respective lineages revealed a transitional state from naive to exhausted CD8+ T cells, as shown by the RNA velocities in the UMAP plot (Figure 3G; Table S4). Pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle2 further delineated a developmental transition from naive to exhausted CD8+ T cells (Figure S9N). During this transition, Tex cell molecules (including Tox and Slamf6) showed a significant increase (false discovery rate < 0.01, top ten), whereas naive and Trm cells showed a significant decrease (Figure 3H). Collectively, these findings suggest that inhibition of CXCR4 attenuates the generation of the CD8+ Tex phenotype by fostering key developmental markers of exhaustion and promoting the recruitment of Tn cells in tumors.

To explore the clonotypes of T cells within the TiME, we conducted a comparative analysis of scTCR-seq data from the treatment and control groups. Cells (n = 7,018) with identical CDR3 sequences in both TCRα and TCRβ chains were categorized as the same clonotype. The results indicated a notable increase in T cell diversity within the TiME and a higher number of clonotypes with the same clone size in both the plerixafor and combination treatment groups than in the control group (Figures 4A and 4B). Enrichment of TCR clonotypes in the plerixafor and combination treatment groups predominantly involved TCR α/β-J and TCR α/β-V genes (Figures 4C and S10A–S10C). Clonally expanded T cells were extensively distributed among CD8+ T cells (Figure 4D), with a notable increase in the number of medium and small clone types in the plerixafor and combination treatment groups compared with that in the control group (Figure 4E). Conversely, there was a decrease in the hyperexpanded clone type in the Tex cell population of the combination treatment group (Figure S10D). By comparing the tumor-specific TCRs, we observed that most were found exclusively in the combination group (Figures 4F and S10E). These results showed that CXCR4 inhibition promoted the abundant TCR clonotypes in CD8+ T cells.

Naive CD8+ T cells give rise to cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which promote the effective eradication of viruses and tumors.36 This process was activated by recognizing cognate antigens via TCR and costimulatory molecules (peptide-major histocompatibility complex [MHC] molecules) on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), including dendritic cells, monocytes, macrophages, and B cells.37 We then analyzed MHC-I expression (H2-D, H2-K, H2-M, H2-T, and H2-Q) in dendritic cells, monocytes/macrophages, and B cells. The results showed that MHC-I expression was increased after each inhibitor treatment (Figures 4G and S10F). Moreover, combining plerixafor with anti-PD-1 significantly enhanced the effect on increasing MHC-I expression (Figures 4G and S10F). These results suggested that CXCR4 inhibition promoted the activation of CD8+ T cells by mediating abundant TCR clonotypes in CD8+ T cells and increasing MHC-I expression in APCs within the TiME (Figure S10G), which supports the effective eradication of tumors.

CXCR4 inhibition mitigated the TOX-mediated Tex phenotype by JAK2-STAT3 pathway regulation to reduce p-STAT3 and TOX interactions

Since multiple transcription factors regulated phenotypes of CD8+ Tex, including TOX, NR4A, T-bet, EOMES, NFAT, IRF4, BATF, and OSR2,38,39 we compared the expression change in RNA-seq data after different treatments in vivo. We found that only transcription factor Tox was downregulated in the three drug-treated groups (Figure S11A), which indicates that Tox was the key downstream of CXCR4 in regulating Tex phenotypes. Our data indicated that the associated DEGs showed enrichment in the JAK-STAT pathway after CXCR4 inhibition in scRNA-seq data (Figure S9F). We conducted experiments to block or activate CXCR4 in human Jurkat T cells and ex vivo-cultured mouse T cells (Figure 5A). CXCR4 inhibition using plerixafor led to a reduction in the expression of TOX and PD-1, accompanied by a decrease in phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3 (Figure 5A). Conversely, CXCR4 activation using the CXCR4 agonist NUCC39040 led to the opposite expression patterns (Figure 5A). Importantly, these changes in expression patterns were restored upon inhibition of the JAK2-STAT3 pathway (Figure 5A). Furthermore, the TOX-mediated Tex phenotype in the JAK2-STAT3 pathway was reduced by motixafortide, another CXCR4 antagonist, in both human and mouse T cells (Figure 5B). To determine how CXCR4 inhibition regulates TOX-mediated PD-1 expression, we performed a co-immunoprecipitation assay and confirmed the interaction between p-STAT3 and TOX in human and mouse T cells (Figure 5C). This interaction decreased following Cxcr4 deletion in spleen T cells (Cxcr4flox/flox LckCre), correlating with downregulation of the TOX-associated Tex phenotype and phosphorylation of the JAK2-STAT3 pathway (Figure 5D).

Subsequently, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis to examine the interaction between p-STAT3 and TOX in human and mouse T cells after CXCR4 inhibition. Remarkably, the binding peaks of p-STAT3 and TOX showed similar distributions and a significant reduction following CXCR4 inhibition (Figures 5E, 5F, S11B, and S11C). We also explored whether CXCR4 inhibition affected the distribution of peaks near the immune checkpoint genes (ICGs) (Figure 5G). Clustering analysis of peak distributions near ICGs revealed that clusters 1 and 2 were reduced after CXCR4 inhibition (Figure S11D). These clusters were associated with functions related to leukocyte cell-cell adhesion and the regulation of T cell activation (Figures S11E and S11F). Consistent with the ChIP-seq results, ChIP-PCR analysis further demonstrated the reduced binding of TOX and p-STAT3 to the promoters of Tex-related genes after plerixafor treatment (Figure S11G).

We also investigated whether the therapeutic benefits of adoptive transfer of CD8+ T cells from Cxcr4-cKO mice could be attenuated by JAK2-STAT3 pathway inhibitors. To assess this, we isolated splenic CD8+ T cells from Cxcr4-cKO mice and pretreated them with JAK2-STAT3 pathway inhibitors (SD-1029 or STAT3-IN-9) before intraperitoneal injection into tumor-bearing BALB/c mice (Figure 5H). This intervention resulted in significant tumor progression and a marked decrease in survival compared to mice that received CD8+ T cells from Cxcr4-cKO mice (Figures 5I and 5J). Collectively, these findings support the notion that inhibition of CXCR4 mitigates the TOX-mediated CD8+ Tex phenotype by modulating the JAK2-STAT3 pathway.

Single-cell chromatin accessibility profiles revealed that CXCR4 deficiency epigenetically orchestrated the exhausted and functional CD8+ T cell phenotypes

To assess the impact of CXCR4 deficiency on the chromatin accessibility profiles of CD8+ T cells at single-cell resolution in vivo, we conducted scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq analyses on tumor-bearing Cxcr4-cKO and control mice (Figure 6A). Single-cell and single-nucleus samples were extracted from the spleens of mice 8–9 days after tumor transfer, prepared, barcoded, and sequenced. After quality control, filtering, and normalization, 25,851 single cells were retained for scRNA-seq analysis, and 17,841 single nuclei were retained for scATAC-seq analysis. Cell clusters in the scRNA-seq data were identified using well-established marker genes, resulting in the identification of 14 unique cell clusters (Figures 6B and S12A–S12D; Table S5). B cells were the dominant cell type, with a significant number of T cells in the spleen (Figure S12E), which is consistent with the results of single-cell studies.41 We subsequently merged the scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq datasets to align and corroborate gene expression with chromatin accessibility profiles across the various cell clusters (Table S6). Violin plots illustrated the marker genes associated with the scRNA-seq cell clusters, while ATAC-seq peaks demonstrated distinct chromatin accessibility patterns within the cell clusters (Figures S12C and S12D). The analysis revealed a clear correlation between alterations in chromatin accessibility and corresponding gene transcript expression within the cell clusters. When comparing the cell cluster distribution between the groups, similarities were observed in most cell populations (Figure S12E). However, a notable decrease in erythroid progenitor cells, neutrophils, and mucosal-type mast cells was detected in the cKO group (Figure S12E). Furthermore, Cxcr4 expression in CD8+ T cells was downregulated in the cKO group, and the exhausted phenotypes of CD8+ T cells were validated by mIHC staining in spleens (Figures 6C and 6D).

Next, we reclustered CD8+ T cells (n = 1,915) into eight subtypes based on scRNA-seq. The cell clusters were annotated using well-known markers, including naive CD8+ T cells (Ccr7 and Sell), terminally differentiated effector memory or effector CD8+ T cells (Term, Plek and Gzmm), NK T cells (Klre1 and Klra7), γδ CD8+ T cells (Trdc, Tcrg-c2, and Myb), Tc17 cells (Rora and Zbtb16), ISG+ CD8+ T cells (Isg15 and Ifit1), precursor-exhausted CD8+ T cells (Cd83 and Ctla4), and CD8+ Tex cells (Ctla4, Lag3, and Pd-1) (Figure 6E). Subsequently, we annotated the cell clusters using the scATAC-seq data (Figures S12F and S12G). Comparing the proportion of cell clusters in the two groups, we noted a decrease in exhausted CD8+ T cells accompanied by an increase in Trdc+ CD8+ T cells, particularly γδ T cells (Figure 6F). The top DEGs in exhausted CD8+ T cells were associated with processes related to the regulation of T-helper 1 cell cytokine production and comprehensive IL-17A signaling (Figure S12H). In addition, the top DEGs in γδ T cells were associated with processes related to the IL-23 pathway and the cellular defense response (Figure S12I).

To explore the epigenetic profiles of CD8+ T cells from functional to exhausted phenotypes following Cxcr4 deletion, we analyzed the chromatin accessibility profiles associated with essential marker genes. The results indicated that the peaks in CD8+ T cell were decreased in exhausted markers (Pd-1, Ctla4, Tox, Tim-3, Tigit, and Slamf6) (Figures 6G and S13A) but increased in functional markers (Il-2, Il-13, Il-17b, Il-17d, Il-21, and Eomes) in the Cxcr4-cKO group (Figures 6H and S13B). Of note, these alterations in chromatin peaks were specific to T cells and were not observed in other cell types (Figures 6G and 6H). Furthermore, the exhausted markers were also inhibited in the exhausted CD8+ T cell cluster (Figure S13C), whereas the functional markers were activated in the Trdc+ CD8+ T cell cluster after Cxcr4 deletion (Figure S13D). Following the analysis of chromatin accessibility in CD8+ T cells, we observed that the exhausted markers (such as Tim-3 and Tigit) exhibited lower accessibility, while the functional markers (such as Il13 and Il2) showed stronger accessibility in the Cxcr4-cKO group compared to the control group (Figures 6I and S13E). Importantly, functional genes were also upregulated after plerixafor treatment in syngeneic immunocompetent mice (Figures 6J and S13F). These findings further suggest that CXCR4 deficiency epigenetically orchestrates the exhausted and functional phenotypes of CD8+ T cells.

Clinical applications demonstrated that CXCR4 expression was associated with ICB response status in cancers

To determine the clinical implications of CXCR4 expression in ICB treatments, we performed mIHC staining in surgical samples from cervical cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) with cisplatin plus paclitaxel and combined with anti-PD-1, as part of the NACI clinical trial (NCT04516616).42 The samples were categorized into the complete pathological response (pCR) and non-pCR groups (Figures 7A and S14A; Table S7). TP63, a cancer cell marker gene,43 was highly expressed in cancer cells in the non-pCR group (Figure 7B). Notably, CXCR4+ CD8+ T cells were significantly increased in the non-pCR group, whereas GZMB+ CD8+ T cells were decreased in the non-pCR group (Figure 7C). Surprisingly, the overall infiltration of CD8+ T cells was not significantly different between the two groups (Figure 7D).

We then explored whether CXCR4 expression could serve as an indicator of response to ICB therapy in other tumor types.45,46,47,44 In the melanoma cohort treated with anti-PD-1 therapy using pembrolizumab,47 high CXCR4 expression was observed in the poor remission group, specifically in non-responders with stable disease or progressive disease (Figure 7E). This association was independent of the expression levels of PD-1, CD8A, and CD8B (Figure S14B). Furthermore, CXCR4 expression was downregulated in melanoma after anti-CTLA4 therapy (ipilimumab)45,46 compared with that in the pretreatment groups (Figure 7F). However, the expression levels of PD-1, CTLA-4, CD8A, and CD8B remained unchanged after anti-CTLA4 therapy (Figure S14C). These results suggest that CXCR4 expression is associated with the ICB response status in cervical cancer and melanoma.

Discussion

Recent clinical trials have demonstrated the improved efficacy of anti-tumor immunotherapy and better disease outcomes from the administration of CXCR4 antagonist, both as an independent treatment and in combination with other therapies (Table S1). Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which CXCR4 influences host immunity in these contexts are inadequately understood. The efficacy of CXCR4 antagonists in eliciting anti-tumor activity was exclusively observed in syngeneic immunocompetent mice, highlighting the crucial role of host immunity in the anti-tumor effects of these antagonists. CXCR4 has also been detected in newly generated neurons during embryogenesis and adulthood, playing crucial roles in neuronal guidance.48 In the context of cancer, CXCR4 has emerged as a critical receptor governing communication between tumor cells and the TiME.49 Elevated CXCR4 expression is linked to unfavorable survival prognosis but is also associated with improved responses to ICB therapy in colorectal cancer.50 Furthermore, CXCR4 overexpression is prevalent in a variety of cancers, including kidney, lung, brain, prostate, pancreas, ovarian, and melanoma.51 Our study revealed that tumors exhibited a higher prevalence of CXCR4+CD8+ T cells than normal tissues, and CXCR4 expression was correlated with the exhausted phenotype observed in CD8+ T cells within tumors. These results suggest that CXCR4 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for tumor immunotherapy.

The presence of CD8+ Tex cells is often associated with a poor prognosis and reduced responsiveness to anti-cancer treatments.52 These cells, which include both progenitor and terminally exhausted states, typically display compromised effector functionality and increased expression of inhibitory receptors,10,11,12 leading to the inability of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells to effectively control tumor progression.52 Notably, most T cell therapies, including approaches involving tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes or chimeric antigen receptor-T cell therapy, depend on the cytotoxicity of T cells driven by TCR.53 Our findings indicated that CXCR4 inhibition effectively attenuated the CD8+ Tex phenotype and increased the infiltration of naive CD8+ T cells, with abundance of TCR clonotypes and higher MHC-I expression. Naive CD8+ T cells differentiate into cytotoxic T lymphocytes by recognizing cognate antigens via TCR and costimulatory molecules on APCs,36,37 by which they contribute to CD8+ T cell activation for anti-tumor immune response in the TiME. These observations support the well-established theory that blocking CXCR4 mobilizes functional CD8+ T cells, thereby promoting the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in both human pancreatic cancer54 and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.16 The exhausted states of CD8+ T cells in the TiME are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including the downregulation of the transcription factor TCF1 and the upregulation of TOX.55 Introducing functional CD8+ T cells into tumor-bearing mice results in rapid loss of cytokine production and cytotoxicity, indicating that negative regulatory mechanisms in tumor-bearing hosts can supersede functional programming.56 Of note, tumor-specific T cells may proliferate without the acquisition of effector functions.56 Here, inhibition of CXCR4 in CD8+ T cells was observed to increase functional CD8+ T cells and shift their differentiation state away from exhaustion, which is in line with the downregulation of TOX mediated by the JAK2-STAT3 pathway. These findings are consistent with the involvement of cytoplasmic domains of CXCR4 in JAK2-STAT3 phosphorylation.57

Limitations of the study

This study had several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, although we successfully identified CXCR4 in orchestrating the CD8+ Tex phenotype, we did not fully explore the mechanisms underlying the increased expression of CXCR4 in the TiME. Given the function of CXCR4 as a receptor for CXCL12, a deeper understanding of the roles and mechanisms underlying the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis in tumors is imperative. To improve our mouse model targeted Cxcr4 in CD8+ T cells, a mouse model that targets Cxcr4 in CD8+ T cells (such as C57BL/6J-Tg(Cd8a∗-cre)B8Asin/J) should be utilized to identify the functional phenotype in these cells. Additionally, further studies are needed to explore the roles and mechanisms of Cxcr4 in CD8− T cells. Before translating our findings into clinical applications, further research is required to elucidate how distinct T cell subtypes (Tn, Teff, Trm, Term, and Treg) respond to CXCR4 antagonists in tumor immunotherapy. Moreover, the insights gained by single-cell sequencing provide only a snapshot of the extensive diversity of T cell immunopathology within tumors. Future studies should aim to capture the comprehensive spectrum of TCR clonotypes associated with tumor neoantigens and further explore the role of CD8+ T cell activation after CXCR4 inhibition in vitro and in vivo.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically examined CXCR4 expression patterns in normal and cancer tissues at single-cell resolution, identifying CXCR4 as a significant marker for T cells, with particularly high expression in PD-1highCD8+ Tex cells. Inhibition of CXCR4 by antagonists and transgenic mice enhanced anti-tumor immunotherapy and attenuated the TOX-mediated CD8+ Tex phenotype via modulation of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway. Our findings reveal a mechanism by which CXCR4 orchestrates the exhausted and functional CD8+ T cell phenotypes in cancer, providing strong evidence for incorporating CXCR4 antagonists alongside ICBs in a broader range of cancer-based clinical trials.

Method details

Clinical trials analysis of CXCR4 antagonists

To investigate the mechanisms of CXCR4 antagonists for treating tumors, we explored unveiled, ongoing, or completed clinical trials from ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CXCR4 antagonists and CXCR4 antagonists in combination with other therapies for treating tumors. We listed clinical trials using CXCR4 antagonists combined with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and summarized different kinds of CXCR4 antagonists in clinical trials via the Gantt chart (Table S1; Figure S1).

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of CXCR4 expression

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis in human tissues was performed based on the Human Cell Landscape (HCL, CNP0000325)26 the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, RRID:SCR_006710)27 and the PanglaoDB (RRID:SCR_022580).28 HCL provided over 700 000 cells from more than 50 human tissues, categorized into 102 clusters (Figures S2A–S2C). We used ‘Gene search (Pseudocell)’ to provide a global view of CXCR4 expression on the pseudo-cell level. We used the CELLxGENE tool (RRID:SCR_021059)58 to show CXCR4 expression, scores of T cell makers, and scores of B cell makers in the UMAP plot (Figure S2D). We used a single cell type section from the HPA67 to explore the expression profiles of CXCR4 in normal tissues (Figure S2E). Cell annotation of cell clusters was used as the default setting. We used the search function within PanglaoDB from 1 368 scRNA-seq data to identify the cell types in which CXCR4 is expressed (Figure S2F). Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of CD8+ T cells from pan-cancer was performed based on single-cell data of tumor-infiltrating T cells (GSE156728) (Figure S2G). To further investigate CXCR4 expression in exhausted CD8+ T cells within tumors, we analyzed scRNA-seq data of CD8+ T cells in pan-cancers.30 Integrating data from 21 cancer types, CD8+ T cells were classified into 17 cell clusters from naive CD8+ T (Tn) to Tex cells (Figure S4). CD8+ T cells were annotated according to a previous study.30

Multiple deconvolution-based analysis

Multiple deconvolution-based analyses of CXCR4 expression in immune cells were performed using GEPIA2021 (RRID:SCR_018294).29 We used the sub-expression analysis tool to visualize the CXCR4 expression in each immune cell type, which determines the cell type-level differential expression of CXCR4 in immune cells. Differential expression analysis of gene expression levels was performed using ANOVA. Using the deconvolution strategy, we can unlock valuable cell type information, enabling us to investigate the relationship between cell proportions. Utilizing the deconvolution results, we can compare the proportions of various cell types and calculate and compare CXCR4 expression in bulk samples contributed by these distinct cell types (Figures S3A–S3D). CXCR4 expression and number variation in TCGA cancer types were analyzed from GEPIA and Cancer Clinical Proteomics Research (CPTAC) (Figures S5A–S5C).

In vivo treatment

Cancer cells were transfected with CMV-luciferase-IRES-RFP lentiviral particles obtained from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Transfected cells were subsequently isolated using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). These luciferase-labeled cancer cells were used for live-animal imaging. For in vivo tumor growth experiments, a total of 1✕106 U14 cervical cancer cells, 1✕106 TC-1 HPV-positive cells, 5✕105 4T1 breast cancer cells, 1✕106 ID8 ovarian cancer cells, and 5✕105 MC38 colon carcinoma cells were used. Subsequently, the mice were randomly allocated into different treatment groups and subjected to three cycles of treatment. Plerixafor (MCE, Cat. No. HY-10046), was administered at a dose of 5.2 mg/kg, while anti-PD-1 (BioXCell, Cat. No. BE0146) was administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg. A combination of both drugs was also administered. These agents were intraperitoneally injected thrice per week. A control group was established using 0.9% NaCl and IgG (BioXCell, Cat. No. BE0089). Tumor growth was continuously monitored in live mice through optical imaging of luciferase activity, using the IVIS SPECTRUM system (RRID:SCR_018621, Caliper, Xenogen USA). Additionally, tumor volume measurements were performed manually using calipers every three days, and the volume was calculated using the formula: length × width2 × 0.52. Throughout the experiment, the body weight of each animal was assessed every three days. Drug groups were non-blinded, animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups, and no specific method was used to calculate sample sizes.

Purification of CD8+ T cells

Mouse primary CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of tumor-bearing Cxcr4flox/flox LckCre and Cxcr4flox/flox mice. Single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were obtained by thoroughly mashing the entire spleens through a 100-μm nylon cell strainer (BD Falcon, Cat. No. 352360). To eliminate red blood cells, a hypotonic ACK buffer (Gibco, Cat. No. A1049201) was used for cell lysis. Subsequently, CD8+ T cells were isolated using the EasySep Mouse CD8+ T cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL, Cat. No. 19853). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from blood samples collected with EDTA as an anticoagulant. Separation was achieved by density-gradient centrifugation. T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Cat. No. 11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. A4766801), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Servicebio, Cat. No. G4003), IL-2 (150 U/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. PMC0025), 50 mM of β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, Cat. No.21985023), and 2 mM of GlutaMAX (Gibco, Cat. No. 35050061).68 T cells were maintained in a controlled environment with 20% O2, 5% CO2, and a temperature of 37°C.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Purified total RNA from the tumors was extracted using a total RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, Cat. No. DP419) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer to assess RNA integrity. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Stranded mRNA-seq Library Pre Kit (Vazyme, NR602-02) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. RNA-seq analysis was performed as previous studies.10,11 RNA-seq data from Tox−/− and wild-type mice were downloaded from GEO: GSE131871.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analysis

GSEA analysis (RRID:SCR_003199) was performed according to the previous study.63 In our analysis, we employed the C7: immunological signature gene set from the Human Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, RRID:SCR_016863). These gene sets represent various chemical and genetic perturbations of the immune system, and are generated through manual curation of published studies in both human and mouse immunology. This comprehensive resource provides valuable insights into molecular signatures associated with immunological processes and perturbations. We used the gene signature of “GSE9650 Exhausted Vs. Memory Cd8 T cell_Up” “GSE41867 Day6 Effector Vs. Day30 Exhausted Cd8 Tcell Lcmv Clone13_Down”, and “GSE9650 Exhausted Vs. Memory Cd8 T cell_Up” to calculate enrichment scores.

Western blot

Western blot assays were performed as described in the previous study.69 Briefly, cells were cultured in a 6-well plate, washed twice with PBS, and then lysed on ice for 30 min with pre-chilled RIPA buffer (Servicebio, Cat. No. G2002) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Antgene, Cat. No. ANT072) and 10% phosphatase inhibitor (ApExBIO, Cat. No. K1015). Subsequently, the lysates were sonicated on ice before centrifugation at 12 000 rpm at 4°C. Protein samples were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE gels (BioSci, Cat. No. 8012011), and electro-transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Cytiva Life Sciences, Cat. No. 10600023). After blocking with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (Servicebio, Cat. No. GC305010) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.5% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 9005-64-5) for 1h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with indicated primary antibodies (Table S8) at 4°C overnight, following by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1h at room temperature. The resulting bands were visualized using Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 32209) and the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, RRID:SCR_021693).

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and co-IP

Cells were washed twice with PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min in pre-chilled IP lysis buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and 10% phosphorylase inhibitors. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm at 4°C for 20 min to obtain protein supernatants. Primary antibodies were immobilized onto Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (MCE, Cat. No. HY-K0202) at 4°C for 4h, and protein supernatants were incubated with immobilized antibodies at 4°C overnight. Normal IgG was used as a negative IP control. Magnetic Beads were washed extensively, followed by denatured elution with 1✕SDS-PAGE Loading Buffer at 100°C for 5 min, and then the proteins were used for Western Blotting analysis of target or interacting proteins.

Multi-color immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining

mIHC staining was used to analyze the expression levels of CXCR4+ CD8+ T cells in tumors (Figure S5D). mIHC staining was performed following the previous study.70 Briefly, the seven-color multiplex fluorescence immunohistochemical staining kit (Absin, Cat. No. abs50031). Tumor sections from the FFPE samples were deparaffinized, dehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking with PBS containing 5% goat serum. The sections were then incubated with the primary antibodies (Table S8). Each target was visualized by fluorescently labeled tyramide signal amplification (TSA), including TSA-520, TSA-540, TSA-570, TSA-620, TSA-650, and TSA-700. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (Abbkine, Cat. No. BMD0063) prior to sealing. Fluorescence images were obtained using a PerkinElmer Vectra automated multispectral microscope. Image segmentation tool was used to analyze immune cells with co-expressed markers in mIHC staining via ImageJ (RRlD: SCR 003070).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-qPCR

The ChIP assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using SimpleChip Plus sonication chromatin IP kit (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), Cat. No. 56383) in Jurkat T cells or mouse T cells after treatment with DMSO or plerixafor for 48h, and T cells were isolated from the spleens of tumor-bearing mice as described above. For each ChIP assay, 5 x 106 cells were used. Briefly, the cells were cross-linked with 1% methanol-free formaldehyde and quenched with 125 mM glycine, and the chromatin was sonicated to generate 500-800bp DNA fragments. The DNA-protein supernatant was used for immunoprecipitation by incubating with TOX (CST, Cat. No. 36778) or p-STAT3 (CST, Cat. No. 9145, RRID:AB_2491009). Subsequently, chromatin was eluted and the cross-link was reversed. DNA was isolated and purified for sequencing and quantitative PCR (qPCR). DNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq ChIP library preparation kit (Illumina) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (RRID:SCR_016387). ChIP-seq analysis was performed as described in the previous study.71 We used DeepTools72 to generate peak profile plots. Real-time PCR was used to quantify DNA expression using the target gene primer sequences. Data were normalized to the corresponding DNA input controls. The primer sequences are listed in Table S9. The quality control of the sequencing data is shown in Table S10.

Tissue digestion and flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were excised, cut into small pieces, and digested using a mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. No. 130-096-730) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, on a shaking table for 1h at 37°C, followed by a wash with pre-chilled PBS and filtration and centrifugation steps. For spleen tissues, the procedure involved mechanically dissociating the tissues on a 70 mm filter unit. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK Lysis Buffer (Servicebio, Cat. No. G2015). This was followed by additional filtration and centrifugation. After centrifugation at 500g for 5 min, single immune cells were collected and suspended in staining buffer (BD Biosciences, Cat. No. 4032357, RRID:AB_2869637). Dead cells were excluded using Fixable Viability Stain 700 (BD Biosciences, Cat. No. 564997), followed by incubation with the antibodies in the dark at room temperature for 15 min (Table S8). For intracellular staining of mouse cytokines, immune cells were stimulated with a Leukocyte Activation Cocktail (BD Biosciences, Cat. No. 550583) overnight and then fixed and permeabilized using Fixation Buffer (Biolegend, Cat. No. 420801) and Intracellular Staining Permeabilization Wash Buffer (BioLengend, Cat. No. 421002) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and subjected to surface and intracellular staining using fluorescently labeled antibodies (Table S8) in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Beckman Coulter flow cytometry (Cytoflex LX, RRID:SCR_025067) was used to analyze the components of immune cells in mouse tumor and spleen tissues, and FlowJo V10 (RRID:SCR_008520) software was used to quantify the populations.

Single-cell 5′ and V(D)J sequencing

Single-cell 5′ and V(D)J libraries of the TCR were prepared in accordance with the protocol provided by the 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell Gene Expression platform. In summary, cell suspensions containing 700-1 200 viable cells per microliter (with a viability of at least 85%, as determined by CounterStar) were loaded onto a chromium single cell controller (10× Genomics). This step facilitated the generation of single-cell gel beads in emulsion (GEMs) by employing chromium single-cell V(D)J Reagent Kits. After the cells were captured, they underwent lysis, and the resulting RNAs were barcoded via reverse transcription, all within individual GEMs. Subsequently, each single-cell 5′ and V(D)J library was subjected to sequencing using Illumina NovaSeq 6000, employing 150 paired-end reads for comprehensive analysis. We used GIANA to analyze TCR clustering and multi-disease repertoire classification.73 The quality control of the sequencing data is shown in Table S10.

Preprocessing of single-cell transcriptomic data

Single-cell transcriptomic data were subjected to a comprehensive preprocessing workflow. Initially, cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were identified using UMI-tools74 and then attached to read names to distinguish different cells and RNA molecules. Read adapters were removed using Cutadapt,75 and high-quality reads were mapped to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome. FeatureCount76 was employed to count the reads mapped to each genomic gene, and Samtools77 organized and indexed the BAM files for efficient storage of mapped reads. Subsequently, UMI-tools were used for UMI-corrected molecular counts. Finally, a unified gene expression matrix, encompassing all sequenced samples, was generated using a local Perl script, ensuring the dataset’s readiness for downstream analysis.

Cell type annotation

Cluster biomarkers were discerned using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers procedure,59,78 which employed a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test to pinpoint differentially expressed genes within each cluster. To enhance single-cell annotation, we harnessed the R package SingleR (RRID:SCR_023120, v1.2.2),60 which leverages reference transcriptomic datasets representing pure cell types to independently infer the cell of origin for each individual cell. This annotation process drew upon two scRNA-seq datasets of cervical cancer and the Mouse Cell Atlas as the reference datasets.41 Cell clusters were further refined through manual annotation, involving the examination of classic marker gene expression patterns and cross-referencing with purified bulk RNA-seq datasets. Subtypes cluster annotation of CD8+ T cells was based on markers of a pan-cancer atlas of intratumoral T cells.30 Naive CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Sell, Ccr7, Tcf7, and Lef1. Tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Ldlrad4,30 Phlda1, Prf1,79 and Abcb1b.80 Exhausted CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Ctla4, Pd-1, Tigit, and Tox. Interferon-stimulated genes+ (ISG+) CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Stat1, Ifit1, Isg15, and Isg20. Temra CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Plek and Gzmm. Natural killer T cells highly expressed genes: Klra7 and Klre1. γδ CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Trdc, Tcrg-c1, and Tcrg-c2. Tc17 CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Rora and Zbtb16. The precursor-exhausted CD8+ T cells highly expressed genes: Cd83 and Ctla4.81

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the FindMarkers function,59,78 employing default parameters and normalized gene expression values as input. To calculate the logFC (log-fold change) value for each gene, the average expression value in each group was divided between the two groups and subsequently log-transformed. Genes were considered differentially expressed based on the following criteria: 1) genes needed to be detected in a minimum of 10% of the cells in either of the two groups; 2) the threshold for logFC was set to the default value of 0.25; and 3) the Bonferroni-adjusted p value had to be less than 0.05 for statistical significance.

Single-cell V(D)J data processing

Single-cell V(D)J data were processed using Cell Ranger (RRID:SCR_017344, v7.1.0), specifically version 3.1.0 from 10x Genomics. To identify common clonotypes across samples, paired α and β CDR3 sequences from different groups were merged or pooled together. Cells that exhibited an identical CDR3 sequence for both the α-chain and β-chain were classified as belonging to the same clonotype.

Single-cell trajectory analysis and RNA velocity

In our analysis of the transcriptional and functional trajectories of CD8+ T cell clusters, we used Monocle 2 (RRID:SCR_016339, v2.14.0).61 To establish a consistent pseudotime reference point, Naive CD8+ T cells were designated as the starting point of the trajectory. The Velocyto package (RRID:SCR_018167, v0.17.15) was utilized to demonstrate the direct estimation of RNA velocity,62,82 which represents the time derivative of the gene expression state. This estimation was achieved by discerning unspliced and spliced mRNA molecules, providing valuable insights into the dynamic changes in gene expression over time.

scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq

Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were prepared following the protocols outlined in the Chromium Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3 (10x Genomics) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For the isolation of nuclei required for scATAC-seq, the 10x Genomics Nuclei Isolation protocol (CG000169) was followed. Subsequently, scATAC-seq library construction and sequencing were performed by incubating the prepared nuclei with Tn5 transposase. The nuclei suspension was then loaded into chromium microfluidic chip E, using 10x reagents, and barcoded through a 10x chromium controller (RRID:SCR_019326, 10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). DNA fragments from the barcoded cells were subsequently amplified, and sequencing libraries were constructed employing reagents from a chromium single cell ATAC reagent kit (10x Genomics; PN-1000110, PN-1000156, PN-1000084), following the manufacturer’s instructions as outlined in the chromium single cell ATAC reagent kits user guide (10x Genomics). Post-library construction and preliminary quantification were performed using Qubit 2.0 (RRID:SCR_020553), followed by quality inspection of the Insert DNA of the library using the Fragment Analyzer (FA). Once the Insert size met the expected criteria, qPCR was employed to accurately quantify the effective library concentration at 2 nM. The libraries were pooled and loaded onto an Illumina NovaSeq platform for sequencing. The quality control of the sequencing data is shown in Table S10.

Generation and analysis of single-cell transcriptome

The raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed and aligned to the reference genome using the 10x Genomics Cell Ranger (RRID:SCR_017344, v7.1.0) pipeline, with default parameters. Specifically, this process involves quantification of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) for each gene within individual cell barcodes, resulting in the construction of digital expression matrices. Subsequently, a secondary filtration step was conducted using the Seurat package, wherein genes expressed in more than three cells were considered as 'expressed,' and each cell was required to have a minimum of 200 expressed genes. Additionally, cells that did not meet the quality criteria were filtered out. The Cell Ranger count module processes FASTQ files and performs various tasks, including alignment, filtering, barcode counting, and UMI counting. This module utilized chromium cellular barcodes to generate feature barcode matrices, which were then used in subsequent steps, such as dimensionality reduction, clustering, and gene expression analysis, all with default parameter settings. Normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and differential expression analysis were conducted using the Seurat package. To perform an integrated analysis of the datasets, we applied the Seurat alignment method known as canonical correlation analysis (CCA), which allowed us to identify shared patterns across multiple datasets.

Generation and analysis of scATAC-seq

Raw sequencing reads were first subjected to demultiplexing and alignment to the reference genome using the 10x Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline with default parameters. Subsequent single-cell analyses, unless stated otherwise, were performed using Cell Ranger ATAC (RRID:SCR_021160), Seurat (RRID:SCR_016341, v3.1.4), and Signac (RRID:SCR_021158, v0.2.5).59 In summary, in the context of gene expression data, unique molecule identifiers were counted for each gene and each cell barcode (filtered by CellRanger-arc), resulting in the creation of digital expression matrices. For ATAC data, raw sequencing data were aligned to the reference genome, followed by read filtering, detection of accessible chromatin peaks, dimensionality reduction, cell clustering, and differential accessibility analyses. The Cell Ranger ATAC (RRID:SCR_017344, v7.1.0) count module processed the FASTQ files and performed alignment, filtering, barcode counting, and UMI counting. It utilized chromium cellular barcodes to generate feature barcode matrices, which were subsequently used in dimensionality reduction, clustering, feature linkage, and transcription factor analysis, all following the default parameter settings of the CellRanger-arc. For secondary analysis of ATAC and gene expression data, we used Seurat and Signac. The Signac package was specifically employed for data normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and feature linkage. We employed a data integration algorithm implemented in Seurat3 to identify 'integration anchors,' allowing us to effectively integrate all the samples and mitigate batch effects. Subsequently, we conducted a UMAP dimensional reduction on the scaled data using the RunUMAP function provided by Seurat. To delineate distinct cell populations, we used the FindClusters function in Seurat. Following cluster identification, we further eliminated a few clusters that consisted of only a small number of cells and lacked enrichment of positive marker genes, indicating the potential presence of cell-cell multiplets. Next, we employed the Seurat function FindAllMarkers to identify open chromatin peaks specific to individual cell clusters. Additionally, we calculated differentially accessible peaks using the Seurat function FindMarkers.

Cis-regulatory interaction analysis

We used the Cicero64 tool to analyze the cis-regulatory interactions in genes based on scATAC-seq data. Cicero is an R package designed for the analysis of single-cell chromatin accessibility. It facilitates the prediction of cis-regulatory interactions, including those between enhancers and promoters, by assessing the co-accessibility patterns within the genome. Cicero assigns a "Cicero co-accessibility" score, which falls within the range of −1 to 1, for every pair of accessible peaks within a user-defined genomic distance. Higher co-accessibility scores indicate stronger co-accessibility between the assessed regions, helping identify potential regulatory interactions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For the in vivo xenograft studies in Figures 1A–1D, S6A and S6B, 2A–2E, and 4H–4J, optical in vivo imaging, raw tumor volumes, and tumor weight are shown. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Transgenic luciferase-expressing cells were used for bioluminescence for in vivo detection, and p values were calculated by the Mann-Whitney test in GraphPad Prism. Tumor volume measurements were performed manually using calipers every three days, and the volume was calculated using the formula: length × width2 × 0.52. Throughout the experiment, the body weight of each animal was assessed every three days. Drug groups were non-blinded, and animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups. p values of the tumor volume curve were determined using two-way ANOVA using Geisser-Greenhouse correction. Survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves, and the log rank test to compare survival curves between different treatments for survival. p values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

For flow cytometry analysis of xenograft studies in Figures 1E–1G, 2F–2G, and S8B with different treatment groups. Data are presented as mean ± SD. p values were calculated by unpaired, parametric t test in GraphPad Prism. For mIHC staining in Figure S5D, p values were determined using the Mann-Whitney test in GraphPad Prism. For mIHC staining in Figures 7A–7D from cervical cancer undergoing anti-PD-1 treatment. The expression of p63, percentage of CXCR4+ CD8+ and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells, and number of CD8+ T cells were shown. p values were determined using the Mann-Whitney test in GraphPad Prism. For the expression levels of MHC-I genes in dendritic cells, monocytes/macrophages, and B cells within different treatment groups in Figures 4G, S9H, and S10F. p values were calculated by the Mann-Whitney test in GraphPad Prism. Each group was compared with the control group. For IHC staining in Figure 6J, p values were determined using an unpaired, parametric t test in GraphPad Prism. p values were considered statistically significant if the p value was <0.05. For Figure 7F, the DEGs RNA-seq were calculated by DESeq2 with parameters (|Log2 fold change |> 1 and p < 0.05).

For all other experiments, statistical analysis was indicated in figure legends. p values were considered statistically significant if the p value was <0.05. For all figures, ∗ indicates p < 0.05, ∗∗ indicates p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ indicates p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ indicates p < 0.0001. All data are represented as the mean ± standard derivation (SD) as indicated in the figure legends. All statistical analyses were performed using R-Studio (RRID:SCR_000432, v3.5.2) or GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798, v9.0).
