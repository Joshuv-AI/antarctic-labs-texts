Petromaroside A, an unprecedented natural nucleoside from the sponge Petrosia sp. with potent cytotoxicity and biosynthetic insight
Authors: Phuong Vu Luu, Huong Lien Ton-Nu, Hien Minh Nguyen, Cuong-Quoc Nguyen, Quang Minh Thai, Kuei-Hung Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A chemical investigation of the marine sponge Petrosia sp. led to the isolation of a novel nucleoside, petromaroside A (1), featuring an unprecedented C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose moiety, along with thymidine (2). These structures and their absolute configurations were elucidated by comprehensive spectroscopic analyses and quantum chemical calculations, including NMR, HRESIMS, LC-MS/MS, GIAO-NMR calculations, and DP4+ probability analysis. Notably, this discovery establishes sponge Petrosia sp. as the first known natural source of nucleosides bearing a C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose unit, while thymidine (2) is reported for the first time from the genus Petrosia. A plausible biosynthetic pathway is proposed for petromaroside A (1), in which its formation is suggested to originate from a common precursor derived via condensation between l-aspartate and carbamoyl phosphate. Compound 1 exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against a panel of human cancer cell lines (HeLa, MCF-7, Hep-G2, and HT-29), with IC50 values ranging from 0.5 ± 0.1 to 1.7 ± 0.3 µM, whereas thymidine (2) showed no significant activity. These findings identify petromaroside A as a promising scaffold for future mechanistic, biosynthetic, and synthetic investigations aimed at the development of structurally novel nucleoside-based anticancer agents.

Introduction

Nucleosides are fundamental building blocks of nucleic acids, forming the structural basis of DNA and RNA through the canonical pairing of nucleobases such as adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), and cytosine (C). In these biological systems, nucleosides consist of a nucleobase linked to a pentose sugar, 2′-deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA-whose precise chemical structure governs the stability, conformation, and replication fidelity of genetic material (Fig. 1).1–3 Even subtle modifications to either the nucleobase or the sugar moiety can profoundly alter base pairing, enzymatic recognition, and overall biological function, which underpins the success of many nucleoside-derived therapeutic agents.4–6 While extensive efforts have focused on modifications of nucleobases, structural variations of the sugar unit, particularly deoxyribose-have also proven to be a powerful strategy for tuning biological activity.1–3,7 However, natural nucleosides typically retain a conserved deoxyribose framework, with only limited functionalization observed at peripheral positions. In particular, substitution at the C-5′ position remains rare, and expansion of the deoxyribose scaffold through additional hydroxymethyl functionality has not been documented in naturally occurring systems.3

Marine sponges of the genus Petrosia (Class Demospongiae) are widely distributed in coral reef and rocky habitats and have been recognized as rich sources of structurally diverse secondary metabolites, including polyacetylenes, sterols, peptides, meroterpenoids, alkaloids, saponins, and fatty acids.8–12 Many of these metabolites exhibit a broad spectrum of biological activities, such as cytotoxicity,13–16 antimicrobial,17 antiviral,18,19 antifungal,20 inhibition of protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B,21 and inhibition of acetylcholinesterase.22 As part of our ongoing search for structurally novel and bioactive natural products from Vietnamese marine organisms, we have isolated a novel nucleoside featuring an unprecedented hydroxymethylated deoxyribose moiety, distinguished by the presence of an additional –CH2OH group at C-5′. In this study, we describe the isolation and structural elucidation of this compound using comprehensive spectroscopic analyses and quantum chemical calculations. Notably, this unusual sugar modification represents a previously unreported structural motif in natural nucleosides, expanding the chemical diversity of this important class of biomolecules. Furthermore, the compound exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against in various cancer cell lines, suggesting that modification of the deoxyribose framework may provide new opportunities for the development of nucleoside-based anticancer agents.

Materials and methods

General experimental procedures

Column chromatography was carried out using silica gel 60 (0.040–0.063 mm, Merck). Analytical thin-layer chromatography was performed on precoated silica gel F254 plates (Merck). Detection of spots was achieved under UV irradiation at 254 and 365 nm, or by spraying with a vanillin-10% H2SO4 reagent followed by heating for 3–5 min. NMR data were obtained on a Bruker Avance 600 instrument (600 MHz, BrukerSpin, Germany). High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectra (HRESIMS) were measured on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA) coupled with a Xevo G2-XS QTOF mass spectrometer, with analyses conducted at the Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI, Chuncheon Center). HeLa (CCL-2), MCF-7 (HTB-22), A549 (CCL-185), Hep-G2 (HB-8065), and HT-29 (HTB-38) cell lines were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).

Sponge material

Fresh specimens of the marine sponge Petrosia sp. were collected at depths of 10–15 m in the An Thoi area of Phu Quoc Island, Vietnam, in January 2018. Specimens were identified based on comparative morphological anatomical analysis conducted at the Marine Zoology Laboratory, Institute of Oceanography, Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology, Khanh Hoa, Vietnam. A voucher specimen (SP9-2018) has been deposited at the Organic Chemistry Laboratory, College of Natural Sciences, Can Tho University, Can Tho, Vietnam.

Extraction and isolation

Fresh material of Petrosia sp. (105 g) was extracted with 95% ethanol (EtOH) (2 L × 5) at room temperature. The combined extracts were filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure at 35 °C to yield a crude EtOH extract (5.8 g). This extract was suspended in water and successively partitioned with n-hexane (Hex), dichloromethane (DCM), and ethyl acetate (EtOAc), affording the corresponding fractions along with the remaining aqueous layer (EtOH/H2O). After solvent removal, the residues were obtained as Hex (1.25 g), DCM (0.85 g), EtOAc (1.35 g), and aqueous (EtOH/H2O, 1.79 g) fractions. The DCM-soluble fraction (0.85 g) was subjected to silica gel column chromatography and eluted with a gradient system of Hex : EtOAc (60 : 40 → 0 : 100, v/v) to afford five fractions (Fr.1-Fr.5). Fraction Fr.4 (102.5 mg) was further fractionated by silica gel column chromatography using a gradient of Hex-EtOAc (55 : 45 → 10 : 90, v/v) to yield three subfractions (Sub-Fr.4.1–4.3). Sub-Fr.4.3 (15.5 mg) was purified by column chromatography with CH2Cl2–MeOH (99 : 1, v/v) to afford compounds 1 (3.8 mg), and 2 (5.9 mg) (Fig. 2).

GIAO-NMR calculations

Gauge-Including Atomic Orbital – Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (GIAO-NMR) calculations were carried out for compound 1. For each stereoisomer, an initial conformational analysis was performed using the GMMX algorithm as implemented in GaussView 6.1 (Gaussian Inc., Wallingford, CT, USA). The generated conformers were subsequently optimized, followed by frequency calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level employing the IEFPCM solvation model for DMSO within the Gaussian 16. The absence of imaginary frequencies confirmed that all optimized structures corresponded to true energy minima. Relative Gibbs free energies were used to determine Boltzmann distributions, and conformers contributing less than 2% were omitted from further consideration.23–27 The retained conformers were then used for NMR shielding calculations via the GIAO approach at the mPW1PW91/6-311+G(d,p) level, incorporating the IEFPCM model for DMSO. Chemical shifts (δu) were derived relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS) using the relationship δu = σref – σcalc, where σcalc denotes the Boltzmann-weighted shielding constants of the conformers and σref represents the shielding constant of TMS computed at the same theoretical level.23–27 Finally, the calculated 13C NMR data were evaluated against experimental values for compound 1 by comparison with their corresponding stereoisomers (1a-5′S and 1b-5′R), employing statistical parameters including mean absolute error (MAE), correlation coefficient (R2), and DP4+ probability analysis.27

Cell viability assay

The bioassay protocols and experimental conditions were derived from previously published methods with minor adjustments in the concentration ranges of the tested samples.23–26,28 When cultures reached approximately 80% confluence, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and detached by treatment with 0.25% trypsin-0.02% EDTA (Gibco) at 37 °C for 5 min. The cells were then resuspended and dispensed into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well in 100 µL of complete culture medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator for 24 h to allow cell attachment. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium containing the test compounds, and the cells were further incubated for 72 h under the same conditions. Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated according to the formula:(%) Cell viability = [(Abssample − Absblank)/(Abscontrol − Absblank)] × 100%

All assays were conducted in triplicate (n = 3). Compounds that inhibited cell proliferation by more than 50% were selected for IC50 determination using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2).29–32 5-Fluorouracil was employed as the positive control.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of the in vitro experiments were carried out using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to assess statistical differences among groups, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test for post hoc analysis. A probability value (P) of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Result and discussion

Structure elucidation of the isolated nucleosides

The fresh material of Petrosia sp. was extracted with 95% EtOH at room temperature, and the resulting crude extract was subsequently partitioned with solvents of increasing polarity, including Hex, DCM, and EtOAc. The DCM-fraction was subjected to repeated silica gel column chromatography and further purification using various solvent systems, ultimately leading to the isolation of two nucleoside-type compounds, petromaroside A (1) and thymidine (2).33,34

Compound 1 was obtained as a yellow oil. Its molecular formula was established as C11H16N2O6 based on HRESIMS analysis, which showed a sodium adduct ion at m/z 295.0903 [M + Na]+ (calcd for C11H16N2O6Na, 295.0906), indicating five degrees of unsaturation. The IR spectrum displayed characteristic absorption bands attributable to hydroxy groups (3331 cm−1), conjugated carbonyl moiety (1627 cm−1), and ketone functionalities (1685 cm−1). The NMR data of compound 1 (Table 1) displayed characteristic resonances attributable to a nucleoside framework, closely resembling those of thymidine (2).33,34 Specifically, a downfield exchangeable proton signal at δH 11.24 (1H, brs) was assigned to an amide (-NH) group. In the aromatic/olefinic region, a doublet signal at δH 7.69 (1H, d, J = 1.2 Hz, 4J), together with a methyl-substituted olefin signal at δH 1.77 (3H, d, J = 1.2 Hz, 4J), suggested the presence of a substituted pyrimidine base, consistent with a thymine moiety. The 13C NMR and DEPT spectra revealed 11 carbon signals, including one methyl (δC 12.2), two methylenes (δC 39.4 and 61.3), five methines (δC 70.4, 72.5, 83.8, 87.3, and 136.1), and three quaternary carbons (δC 109.4, 150.5 and 163.7). Among them, two carbonyl carbons at δC 163.7 and 150.5, along with aromatic carbons at δC 136.1 and 109.3, further supported the presence of a thymine unit. In addition, four oxygenated methine carbons at δC 70.4, 72.5, 83.8, and 87.3, together with two methylene carbons at δC 61.3 and 39.4, indicated a highly oxygenated and structurally modified sugar moiety rather than a typical deoxyribose unit in thymidine (2).

Detailed analysis of HSQC, COSY, and HMBC spectra allowed construction of this unusual sugar fragment (Fig. 3). The 1H–1H COSY coupling patterns and chemical shifts of the corresponding protons H-1′ (δH 6.16)/H2-2′ (δH 2.07)/H-3′ (δH 3.76)/H-4′ (δH 4.24)/H-5′ (δH 3.43)/H2-6′ (δH 3.54, 3.59), indicating a highly oxygenated sugar framework. Furthermore, the key HMBC correlations from H-3′ (δH 3.76) to C-1′, C-2′, C-4′, and C-5′, together with those from H-4′ (δH 4.24) to C-1′, C-3′, and C-5′, further confirmed the planar connectivity of the sugar unit. Notably, the presence of an oxygenated methine group at δC 72.5 (H-5′, δH 3.43) and an additional oxymethylene group at δC 61.3 (H2-6′, δH 3.55 and 3.59), suggested an uncommon carbon extension at C-5′, corresponding to a hydroxymethyl-substituted deoxyribose skeleton. Comparison of the NMR data of compound 1 with those of thymidine (2) revealed closely related sugar resonances. In particular, the anomeric proton H-1′ appeared as a doublet of doublets with a relatively large coupling constant (J = 7.6 and 6.2 Hz), thereby supporting a β-d-glycosidic configuration and indicating the same β-oriented sugar framework.33,34 Finally, the anomeric proton H-1′ (δH 6.16) showed HMBC correlation to C-2 (δC 150.5) and C-6 (δC 136.1) of the base, establishing the N-glycosidic linkage.

Key COSY (red bold) and HMBC (blue arrows) correlations observed in petromaroside A (1).

The unusual sugar moiety of compound 1 was further supported by LC-MS/MS fragmentation analysis (Fig. 4). Cleavage of the N-glycosidic bond generated a characteristic fragment ion at m/z 125, corresponding to the thymine base. A fragment ion at m/z 147 (C6H11O4+) was attributed to the modified sugar unit and was consistent with a hydroxymethylated deoxyribose fragment. In addition, the fragment ion at m/z 61 (C3H7O2+) further supported the presence of a terminal –CH2OH substituent. These fragmentation patterns corroborated the NMR-based assignment of a C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose moiety in compound 1.

LC-MS/MS fragmentation pathway of petromaroside A (1).

To determine the absolute configuration at C-5′ of compound 1, GIAO-NMR calculations were performed for the two possible stereoisomers, designated as 1a-5′S and 1b-5′R.23–26 The calculated 13C NMR chemical shifts of both isomers were statistically compared with the experimental data using DP4+ probability analysis. The results clearly supported the 1a-5′S configuration for compound 1 with a DP4+ probability of 100%, whereas isomer 1b-5′R gave a probability of 0% (Fig. 5). In addition, a strong correlation between the calculated and experimental 13C NMR data (R2 = 0.9866) further supported this assignment. Accordingly, the absolute configuration at C-5′ was assigned as S. Collectively, these spectroscopic features demonstrated that compound 1 consists of a thymine unit attached to a structurally modified deoxyribose bearing an additional hydroxymethyl group at C-5′. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first naturally occurring nucleoside featuring a C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose moiety. Therefore, compound 1 was characterized as a novel nucleoside and named petromaroside A (1).

Cytotoxic activity

The cytotoxic activities of compounds 1 and 2 were evaluated against five human cancer cell lines, including HeLa, MCF-7, A549, Hep-G2, and HT-29, using the CCK-8 assay with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) as the positive control (Table 2). Compound 1 exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against HeLa, MCF-7, Hep-G2, and HT-29 cells, with IC50 values ranging from 0.5 ± 0.1 to 1.7 ± 0.3 µM, whereas no significant activity was observed against A549 cells (IC50 > 10 µM). Notably, compound 1 showed stronger cytotoxicity than 5-FU against HeLa, MCF-7, and HT-29 cell lines. In contrast, compound 2 (thymidine) was inactive against all tested cell lines (IC50 > 10 µM), suggesting that the unusual hydroxymethylated sugar moiety in compound 1 plays a crucial role in enhancing cytotoxic activity. The lack of activity observed against A549 cells may be attributed to intrinsic differences in cellular uptake and intracellular response mechanisms among cancer cell lines. In particular, variation in nucleoside transporter expression and efficiency, as well as differences in intracellular metabolic activation or degradation pathways, may lead to reduced accumulation or activation of compound 1 in A549 cells. Furthermore, A549 cells have been reported to exhibit relatively strong drug resistance characteristics and altered apoptosis-related signaling pathways, which may contribute to decreased sensitivity toward nucleoside analogues.35–38 Collectively, these factors may account for the observed cell-line-dependent cytotoxic profile of compound 1.

Structurally, the presence of an additional hydroxymethyl substituent at C-5′ markedly differentiates compound 1 from canonical thymidine analogues. Such modification may alter the physicochemical properties of the nucleoside scaffold, potentially influencing cellular uptake, metabolic stability, or interactions with nucleoside-processing enzymes and biological targets involved in DNA biosynthesis.23 Notably, the additional hydroxymethyl (–CH2OH) group may further enhance hydrogen-bonding capacity, potentially facilitating interactions with intracellular biomacromolecules, including apoptosis-related regulatory proteins.25 Such interactions could plausibly contribute to the modulation of key signaling pathways governing programmed cell death, thereby influencing the observed cell-line-dependent cytotoxicity.25 Given that numerous clinically used anticancer agents are nucleoside analogues, the potent activity of compound 1 highlights the C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose framework as a promising and previously unexplored pharmacophore for anticancer drug discovery. Although the precise mechanism of action remains unclear, the remarkable activity observed for compound 1 warrants further biological investigations, including mechanistic studies and target identification. Moreover, the unprecedented sugar architecture of petromaroside A (1) may inspire future synthetic and medicinal chemistry efforts toward the development of structurally novel nucleoside-based anticancer agents.

Proposed biosynthetic pathway

In nature, thymidine biosynthesis generally proceeds through two distinct pathways, namely the salvage pathway and the de novo pathway.39,40 The salvage pathway enables reutilization of preformed nucleobases and nucleosides through enzymes such as thymidine phosphorylase and thymidine kinase, whereas the de novo pathway constructs the pyrimidine scaffold from simple primary metabolites.40 Based on the structural features of compounds 1 and 2, a plausible biosynthetic pathway for thymidine (2) and petromaroside A (1) is proposed herein through the de novo pyrimidine biosynthetic route (Scheme 1). The biosynthesis is initiated by condensation of l-aspartate and carbamoyl phosphate, generated by carbamoyl phosphate synthase (pyrA).40–42

Proposed biosynthetic pathway for petromaroside A (1) and thymidine (2).

This transformation, catalyzed by aspartate carbamoyltransferase (pyrBI), affords carbamoyl aspartate, which subsequently undergoes cyclization and oxidation to produce uridine monophosphate (UMP) as the common pyrimidine precursor.40–42 UMP is then phosphorylated by UMP kinase (pyrH) to form uridine diphosphate (UDP), followed by reduction by ribonucleotide diphosphate reductase (nrdAB) to generate deoxyuridine diphosphate (dUDP). Subsequent phosphorylation, deamination, and hydrolysis steps involving dCTP deaminase (dcd) and deoxyuridine triphosphatase (dut) furnish dUMP.40,43,44 Finally, thymidylate synthase (thyA) catalyzes transfer of a methylene group from 5,10- methylenetetrahydrofolate to dUMP, affording dTMP,45,46 which is subsequently converted into thymidine (2).

Petromaroside A (1) is proposed to arise from thymidine (2) through a late-stage one-carbon modification at C-5′ of the deoxyribose moiety. Specifically, a thymidylate synthase-like (thyX) or flavin-dependent enzyme may utilize N5,N10-methylenetetrahydrofolate (CH2THF) as a methylene donor to introduce an additional one-carbon unit onto the sugar framework, generating a hydroxymethylated intermediate.47 Subsequent stereoselective oxidation-reduction steps could then furnish the unusual C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose architecture observed in petromaroside A (1).47 This proposed pathway provides a reasonable biosynthetic rationale for the formation of the unprecedented sugar motif and suggests that petromaroside A (1) may originate from post-modification of a canonical thymidine scaffold.

Conclusion

In summary, chemical investigation of the marine sponge Petrosia sp. led to the discovery of petromaroside A (1), a structurally unprecedented nucleoside featuring a rare C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose moiety, together with thymidine (2). Notably, petromaroside A (1) represents the first example of a naturally occurring nucleoside bearing a C-5′ hydroxymethylated deoxyribose unit, thereby expanding the structural diversity of naturally occurring nucleosides. A plausible biosynthetic pathway involving a common pyrimidine precursor derived from condensation of l-aspartate and carbamoyl phosphate was also proposed. Biologically, compound 1 exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against several human cancer cell lines, whereas thymidine (2) was inactive, suggesting that the unusual hydroxymethylated sugar framework plays an important role in enhancing cytotoxicity. These findings highlight petromaroside A as a promising scaffold for future mechanistic, biosynthetic, and synthetic studies toward the development of structurally novel nucleoside-based anticancer agents.
