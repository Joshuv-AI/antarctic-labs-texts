Influence of types of railway traffic on ground borne vibration towards vibration threshold limit
Authors: Mazlan Rafidah, Mohd Khairul Afzan Mohd Lazi, Sitti Asmah Hassan, Khairul Hazman Padil, Norliana Sulaiman, Muhammad Rizal Razman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ground-borne vibration (GBV) presents critical challenges in urban environments when levels exceed residential thresholds. This study investigates the influence of train type, speed, distance, soil conditions, coach length, and ballast and embankment height on GBV. Findings reveal that high-speed trains operating at 80–160 km/h generated the highest PPVs, with most values surpassing the 0.8 mm/s DOE guideline even at 7 m. Commuter trains exhibited intermediate vibration levels, whereas LRT systems produced the lowest PPVs, largely within perceptibility limits. Vibrations attenuated with distance but were amplified in soft soils. The CART regression model identified train speed (100%) and distance (79.7%) as the dominant predictors, with strong predictive accuracy (R2 = 97.94% training; 97.75% testing). These results emphasise the need for effective GBV management strategies in dense urban settings.

Introduction

In-land railways represent one of the most important components of modern transportation networks. Their direct contact with the ground, however, often results in a significant transmission of ground-borne vibration (GBV). These vibrations propagate through soil layers and can be detected in surrounding structures, potentially disturbing residents and contributing to human annoyance in urban communities located near railway lines. The propagation of GBV from in-land railways is influenced by a range of factors, including soil composition, track design, and train speed. Seyedi1 notes that vibrations are often amplified by ground stiffness and the proximity of buildings to railway lines, both of which intensify disturbance for nearby residents. Furthermore, GBV is typically stronger at low frequencies, which are more likely to induce resonance in larger structures, thereby heightening both human perception and annoyance2.

In-land railway systems take multiple forms, each serving distinct functions within national transport infrastructure. High-speed railways, for instance, are designed to connect major cities rapidly and efficiently, while commuter trains provide daily services for individuals travelling from suburban to urban areas3. Light rail transit systems, on the other hand, are more localised, offering shorter-distance services at lower speeds to improve accessibility within densely populated metropolitan zones4. Collectively, these systems form the backbone of sustainable mobility, reducing dependency on private vehicles, alleviating traffic congestion, and lowering carbon emissions. Beyond passenger transport, in-land railways also play a vital role in economic development by facilitating the efficient movement of goods and strengthening regional integration5. Hence, while in-land railways provide essential services that enhance mobility, reduce environmental impact, and support economic integration, their direct contact with the ground makes them a key source of GBV. The intensity of these vibrations depends on soil conditions, track characteristics, and operational factors, with potential consequences for both structures and human comfort.

The establishment of threshold limits for GBV is a crucial aspect of evaluating the effects of vibrations on both residential and industrial environments. These limits serve as protective guidelines, ensuring that the intensity of vibrations remains within acceptable levels to safeguard human health, structural integrity, and industrial operations. The threshold limit for GBV levels is an essential parameter for assessing the impact of vibrations on residential and industrial areas. Raising awareness about railway-induced vibrations is vital for building a sustainable environment, as effective mitigation not only protects community well-being but also ensures resilient and eco-friendly urban development6. Given the higher sensitivity of residential structures and the vulnerability of residents to vibration-induced annoyance, the thresholds applied to residential areas are generally more stringent than those for industrial zones7.

Residential vibration limits are primarily designed to minimise disturbances to sleep and daily comfort, while also reducing the risk of long-term structural damage to buildings. In contrast, industrial thresholds focus more on maintaining the stability of operations and preventing damage to sensitive machinery or equipment. Van Leeuwen and Van Zwienen8 note that perceptible vibration is directly related to the interaction of vibration levels with surfaces. Human perception of vibration begins at approximately 0.1 mm/s, with vibrations between 0.3 and 1 mm/s considered noticeable, and those ranging from 1 to 10 mm/s perceived as very strong. The frequency range of most human sensitivity to vibrations lies between 1 to 80 Hz, although exposure to vibrations below 1 Hz has also been shown to affect psychological well-being. In the context of railway-induced vibrations, these disturbances are often categorised as perceptible vibrations, which contribute to discomfort and annoyance among residents. Smith et al.9 highlighted that horizontal vibrations within the 5–10 Hz frequency range are particularly relevant for assessing railway-induced sleep disturbances, as their disruptive effects intensify with greater amplitude. This suggests that individuals living near railway tracks are especially vulnerable to such impacts. Establishing appropriate GBV thresholds is therefore critical to ensuring adequate protection. As noted by Lazi et al.10, humans can perceive vibrations within specific ranges that are especially disruptive to sleep and more likely to trigger annoyance. These findings reinforce the importance of applying stricter limits in residential areas, where the consequences extend beyond structural safety to include health, well-being, and overall quality of life11,12.

In the United States, the Federal Transit Administration (FTA) provides one of the most comprehensive frameworks for addressing railway-induced vibration. Its Transit Noise and Vibration Impact Assessment Manual sets out assessment methodologies, exposure thresholds, and recommended mitigation measures, specifically tailored for transit systems, including railways13. In the United Kingdom, regulations are jointly overseen by the Department for Transport (DfT) and the Office of Rail and Road (ORR). These institutions establish procedural requirements, permissible thresholds, and mandatory mitigation strategies to minimise vibration impacts on communities. Across continental Europe, countries such as Germany and France have also developed robust regulatory frameworks, often harmonised with European Union directives on environmental noise and vibration14. While the structure of these frameworks is broadly similar, key differences arise in terms of assessment methodologies and limit values. For example, some nations adopt rigid numerical thresholds, whereas others place greater emphasis on subjective criteria, such as human annoyance and perceived well-being15. This demonstrates a dual emphasis within European policy: safeguarding structural integrity and prioritising human comfort. GBVs generated by railway systems are a significant concern in many advanced countries with extensive rail networks. These nations have developed and implemented standards and guidelines to address the impact of vibrations on both human well-being and the built environment12.

In Malaysia, the Department of Environment (DOE) is the principal authority responsible for regulating environmental impacts associated with railway operations, including GBVs. The DOE has developed specific guidelines that define vibration thresholds for a variety of receptors, including residential, commercial, industrial, and sensitive locations such as schools, hospitals, and places of worship16. These measures aim to safeguard the environment, preserve public well-being, and promote sustainable railway operations in Malaysia17. These guidelines categorise vibrations into three types such as continuous, intermittent, and impulsive, with intermittent vibration being the relevant category for railway-induced disturbances. These guidelines provide not only permissible threshold values but also methodologies for evaluating and interpreting vibration data. A key feature of the DOE framework is the use of velocity decibels (VdB), which enables vibration levels to be expressed in a logarithmic scale for comparison with international standards18. Beyond measurement methodology, the DOE framework sets out criteria for evaluating environmental impacts, addressing not only human perception but also potential effects on structures, ecosystems, and natural resources. The DOE collaborates with railway operators and other stakeholders to ensure compliance with vibration regulations, reinforcing the role of governance in promoting sustainable transport operations17.

GBVs are primarily generated through the dynamic interaction between rail tracks and passing trains. Each type of railway traffic such high-speed rail, light rapid train, and commuter trains, exhibits distinct operational characteristics, including weight, speed, and frequency of movement, all of which influence vibration levels. Thompson et al.20 modelled wave fields generated by unit loads at varying speeds (80 m/s, 168 m/s, and 250 m/s), demonstrating how load velocity affects both the amplitude and propagation of vibrations. High-speed trains, due to their elevated velocities, tend to generate higher-frequency vibrations of shorter duration, which can nonetheless be highly perceptible to residents and damaging to nearby structures.

Environmental conditions, particularly soil type, also play a decisive role in vibration propagation. For example, high-speed trains operating on soft soils can generate amplified vibrations compared to those on stiffer foundations. Train design further compounds this effect, as differences in wheel structure, railcar configuration, and track design alter the transmission of vibrations to the ground21,22. Dense traffic patterns and long train formations intensify these impacts, leading to more persistent vibration fields. This study highlights the necessity of establishing context-specific GBV threshold limits that reflect both railway traffic type and environmental conditions. By analysing vibration characteristics associated with high-speed, commuter, and light rapid trains, and by examining their propagation across varying soil conditions, a more comprehensive understanding of vibration impacts can be achieved. The DOE’s framework provides a foundation for regulating railway-induced GBVs in Malaysia, with stricter thresholds for residential areas to minimise human annoyance. Future research directions include the development of advanced predictive models, integration of GBV considerations into urban planning, and the adoption of innovative mitigation technologies such as soil stabilisation. Refining international and national standards based on empirical and modelling evidence will enable transportation authorities and policymakers to optimise railway operations while protecting communities and the environment.

Methodology

The primary aim of this section is to outline the methodological framework adopted to investigate GBV thresholds in relation to different types of railway traffic. Particular emphasis is placed on the influence of train type alongside other variable characterisations, including operational and environmental factors. Establishing a clear methodology is essential for ensuring accuracy in data collection, reliability in analysis, and validity in the research findings. Technical flow study.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The fieldwork was conducted at three sites in Selangor that is Klang, Ampang, and Serdang each with unique soil stratifications, track structures, and residential proximities. These site-specific conditions limit the wider applicability of the findings, as soil stiffness, damping capacity, and layering vary across different regions. The analysis was also restricted to receiver distances of 4–13 m, reflecting near-field residential exposure but constraining insights into attenuation at greater ranges, where geometric spreading and soil damping may play a stronger role. Furthermore, the study focused on three train categories high speed, commuter, and light rapid train each represented by rolling stock, axle configurations, and suspension systems, which restricts direct generalisation to other networks.

Measurement-related constraints should also be noted. Although the MiniSEIS digital seismograph is highly sensitive and internationally compliant, its placement on firm ground near residences may have introduced variability from localised soil heterogeneity or external noise sources such as traffic or construction. Likewise, Doppler radar speed measurements, while generally reliable, are susceptible to angular error, potentially introducing minor discrepancies in the dataset.

Peak particle velocity analysis

The selection of an appropriate indicator is central to the accurate assessment of GBVs generated by railway systems. In this study, Peak Particle Velocity (PPV) was adopted as the primary parameter due to its recognised relevance in both human perception studies and structural safety evaluations34. PPV measures the maximum instantaneous velocity of ground particles during a vibration event and is widely applied in environmental vibration research. It is particularly valued for its strong correlation with human annoyance thresholds as well as with structural damage limits, as defined by international standards such as ISO 2631–2:200335 and BS 7385–2:199336.

Field measurements were undertaken using the MiniSEIS digital seismograph, a portable, high-sensitivity instrument capable of recording ground vibrations in three orthogonal directions: radial (R), transverse (T), and vertical (V). An external microphone was also integrated to capture simultaneous airborne noise, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Ground-borne vibration can be expressed in terms of velocity, displacement, or acceleration, and the MiniSEIS system complies with IEC Publication 184. Additional components, including amplifiers, variable frequency systems, and carrier systems, were employed in accordance with IEC Standard 22216.Fig. 7Three measurement directions for PPV.

Three measurement directions for PPV.

Seismograph deployment and data collection

The MiniSEIS units were installed on firm, level ground at residential receiver sites located along the railway corridor. As illustrated in Fig. 8, a geophone transducer was used to convert ground motion into electrical signals, enabling precise measurement of vibration events. To ensure data integrity, the internal clock of the instrument was synchronised with actual train timetables, allowing vibration signals to be directly linked to specific train pass-by. At each monitoring point, three repeated measurements were collected to minimise uncertainty. The data were subsequently processed using White Seismograph Data Analysis V13 software, enabling detailed investigation of vibration patterns. These measurements allowed for a comprehensive analysis of site-specific attenuation profiles, soil vibration interactions, and the relationship between PPV levels and human annoyance reports.Fig. 8Setup of the MiniSEIS unit at residential receiver sites.

Setup of the MiniSEIS unit at residential receiver sites.

Train speed measurement

Train speed was considered a key variable in the study. Data were collected using a BINAR Doppler radar gun, which records train speeds in kilometres per hour with a margin of error of ± 2 km/h up to 320 km/h. As illustrated in Fig. 9a and b, the schematic and field records demonstrate how train speed was monitored. While head-on measurements would yield the most accurate results, safety considerations and the potential distraction to drivers required positioning the radar at the trackside. The Doppler Effect, which detects frequency shifts in electromagnetic waves, was applied to calculate speed, though angular deviation may have caused minor fluctuations in recorded values37.Fig. 9(a) Schematic diagram of train speed measurement; (b) Field record of train speed.

Distance and ballast measurements

The distance between the railway track and MiniSEIS receiver locations was also measured to investigate its effect on GBV levels. Using both a laser meter and measuring tape, distances were recorded from the track centre to the receiver sites, including points beyond drainage areas (Fig. 10). This dual method ensured accuracy, especially in locations where direct measurement was challenging.Fig. 10Distance measurement from receiver to track centre before and after drainage.

Ballast dimensions, specifically height and width, were also recorded to examine their relationship with vibration propagation. Since wheel–rail interaction serves as the primary vibration source, variations in ballast geometry may influence the extent of vibration transmission through the rail soil system38.

Train types and characteristics

Three categories of trains were selected to represent the diversity of Malaysia’s railway network high-speed, commuter, and light rapid train. The high-speed train considered is the Siemens Desiro ET 425 M, a four-car electric multiple unit (EMU). The commuter is represented by the CRRC Zhuzhou–built KTM Class 92, which operates as a six-car EMU. For the light rapid train, the selected model is the CSR Zhuzhou six-car EMU, commonly used in urban rail systems. Train speed and coach length were identified as primary factors influencing vibration intensity. Longer trains and higher velocities generally generate stronger vibrations, increasing the potential for human annoyance and structural effects. Light dynamic penetrometer JKR probe test: soil analysis

The mechanical properties of soil underlying railway corridors and adjacent residential areas play a critical role in determining the propagation, attenuation, and amplification of GBVs. Parameters such as soil stiffness, density, and undrained shear strength govern the dynamic interaction between the track–subgrade system and surrounding structures, particularly under repeated, high-magnitude loading generated by railway traffic. Variability in these geotechnical properties often leads to significant differences in vibration levels. For instance, softer or moisture-sensitive soils tend to amplify low-frequency vibrations, whereas stiffer soils are generally more effective in attenuating them. To evaluate these characteristics within the study area, the Light Dynamic Penetrometer (LDP) JKR Probe Test, a standardised method under BS 8004:198640 was employed.

The JKR Probe Test involves driving a cone-tipped steel probe vertically into the ground through repeated free-fall impacts of a hammer with fixed weight and drop height. The number of blows, or J-value, required to achieve 300 mm of penetration is recorded (Fig. 12). Higher J-values correspond to denser or stiffer soil conditions, whereas lower values indicate softer and more compressible subgrades.Fig. 12Setup of JKR probe test.

Setup of JKR probe test.

From the field survey, three predominant soil types were identified along the railway corridor. To quantify soil strength, the undrained shear strength (Su) was estimated using the empirical correlation developed by Yusof et al.41, which has been validated for Malaysian soils and is applicable to both JKR and Mackintosh probe results:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{Su}=18{\text{J}}^{0.3}$$\end{document}

Using these values, the allowable bearing capacity of the soils was subsequently estimated by reference to established correlation charts, thereby enabling soil classification in accordance with JKR geotechnical design standards.

In the context of this research, the probe test was not only used for subgrade classification but also as a means of generating critical input parameters for further analysis. Specifically, the data provided a basis for correlating site-specific soil stiffness and strength with the PPV values measured in Section "Peak Particle velocity analysis". Such correlations improve the accuracy of vibration transmission modelling and enable the identification of soil conditions most susceptible to vibration amplification.

Results and discussion

Scatterplot fundamental of rail traffic vibrations on no. of data

The assessment of railway-induced GBV is closely tied to established threshold limits, which determine the extent to which vibrations may cause annoyance or discomfort in residential environments. Figure 13 presents a scatterplot of the recorded vibration data in comparison with the permissible limits outlined by the DOE guidelines. For residential areas, the DOE specifies maximum allowable vibration levels of 0.8–1.6 mm/s during the daytime and 0.4 mm/s at night, thresholds that are consistent with ISO and BS 6472 standards42. These benchmarks provide a basis for evaluating whether railway operations exceed acceptable levels of vibration exposure.Fig. 13Scatterplot overall data.

Scatterplot overall data.

The scatterplot in Fig. 13 consolidates all data collected from the study sites, regardless of train type, speed, or distance from the receiver. Analysis of the results shows that the majority of recorded vibration events exceeded the DOE guideline limits for human annoyance, raising concerns about the implications of long-term railway operations for nearby residents. This observation is reinforced by international standards, which also highlight 0.8 mm/s as a critical threshold beyond which vibrations are likely to cause annoyance. Comparable exceedances were reported by Yuan et al.34, further validating the significance of these findings.

While broadly aligned with European standards such as those adopted in the Netherlands (0.8 mm/s) and Germany (0.4–1.6 mm/s), the DOE thresholds are stricter than those applied by the United States FTA. The FTA sets the nighttime residential limit at 0.254 mm/s, with more lenient values for commercial and industrial zones43. Such variations reflect regional considerations, including differences in geotechnical conditions, population density, and cultural expectations regarding environmental comfort.

The comparative analysis of train types highlights significant differences in vibration behaviour. High-speed trains exhibited the widest range of PPV values, with a considerable number of measurements surpassing residential thresholds. This outcome indicates that the higher operating speeds of high-speed trains generate vibrations strong enough to intrude upon residential comfort, leading to a heightened potential for human annoyance. In contrast, LRT systems generally produced lower PPV values, with most measurements remaining below the 0.8 mm/s limit, suggesting that LRT operations are comparatively less disruptive to nearby communities. Commuter trains displayed a more variable pattern, with several readings exceeding the residential threshold. This highlights the potential for commuter services, particularly in densely populated urban corridors, to generate ground vibrations capable of affecting household comfort and daily activities.

These findings support previous research on railway-induced GBV, which consistently demonstrates that vibration intensity is not only influenced by proximity to the track but also strongly dependent on train type and operational characteristics44.

The scatterplot analysis illustrates that a substantial proportion of vibration levels recorded at the study sites exceeded the DOE thresholds for residential comfort. High-speed trains, due to their greater velocities, were the primary contributors to exceedances, while LRT services showed minimal disturbance potential. Commuter trains produced intermediate outcomes, with several cases surpassing the recommended limits. Collectively, these results emphasise the importance of incorporating train type and operating conditions into GBV management strategies. Establishing context specific thresholds, supported by robust monitoring and predictive modelling, remains essential for protecting residential well-being in areas adjacent to railway corridors.

These observations underscore the critical role of train speed in vibration propagation mechanisms. Track segments where trains accelerate, decelerate, or pass through areas with soil resonance potential are especially vulnerable to exceedances of annoyance thresholds. Such effects are most concerning near residential areas, railway stations, or gradient transitions, where prolonged exposure intensifies human perception of vibration. Zhang et al.48 similarly reported that higher speeds exacerbate dynamic wheel–rail interactions, axle loading, and excitation frequencies, thereby increasing the severity of vibration propagation.

The analysis confirms that train velocity is a dominant determinant of railway-induced GBVs, with exceedances of perceptibility and annoyance thresholds particularly associated with high-speed services. While lower speeds produce moderate vibration levels, velocities above 120 km/h significantly amplify PPV, posing challenges for nearby residential environments. These findings highlight the direct causal relationship between train speed and ground vibration severity, reinforcing the need for speed management strategies as a practical mitigation measure. Operational controls, combined with consideration of soil resonance characteristics, can provide an effective approach for minimising vibration impacts in sensitive zones along railway corridors.

Conclusions

This study investigated the relationship between different train types and their influence on vibration thresholds that contribute to human annoyance from railway-induced GBV. The analysis demonstrated that train speed, source–receiver distance, and subsurface soil conditions are the dominant parameters, while infrastructural factors such as ballast thickness, embankment height, and coach length exert comparatively minor influences. High-speed trains consistently generated the highest PPV values, with most measurements exceeding the DOE guideline of 0.8 mm/s. The strong correlation between velocity and vibration intensity was particularly evident at speeds above 120 km/h, where amplification due to dynamic loading and potential soil resonance became pronounced. Although attenuation was observed with distance, residents within 7 m of the track remained significantly exposed. Ballast thickness and coach length had minimal direct influence on PPV, yet longer train formations operating at higher velocities increased exposure duration, thereby intensifying annoyance. Soft surficial soils, such as those in Serdang, further amplified vibration propagation, while embankments provided only modest mitigation, indicating the need for more advanced geotechnical measures in high-speed rail corridors. Commuter trains exhibited intermediate vibration behaviour, with outcomes strongly influenced by speed and soil–track conditions. Exceedances of the 0.8 mm/s threshold were frequent at operating speeds above 80 km/h, particularly in urban corridors underlain by soft deposits. Dense soils, such as those in Klang, effectively attenuated vibration levels, whereas softer soils amplified transmission. Embankments offered partial damping, though their effect was secondary to dynamic influences. Coach length and ballast thickness were negligible as direct factors but longer trains in urban settings may still extend exposure. In contrast, LRT systems produced the lowest PPV values, with most measurements remaining below perceptibility thresholds. While speed and distance retained predictive importance, their influence was less pronounced due to lighter axle loads and shorter formations. Standard ballast depths proved adequate for vibration mitigation, and attenuation with distance was rapid, with minimal exceedances beyond 10 m. Although some amplification of low-frequency vibrations was noted in soft soils at Ampang, the overall disturbance potential of LRT operations was limited compared to heavier train types.The CART regression model reinforced these findings, ranking train speed (100%) and distance from the receiver (79.7%) as the most influential predictors, while infrastructural parameters contributed comparatively little. The high predictive accuracy of the model R2 = 97.94% for training and 97.75% for testing for data sets confirmed the robustness of machine-learning methods for capturing multivariate effects on GBVs.

Overall, the results confirm that high-speed trains pose the greatest risks to residential well-being, commuter trains exert moderate but context-dependent impacts, and LRT services cause minimal disturbance. Effective mitigation should integrate speed regulation in sensitive areas with soil reinforcement, embankment optimisation, and adequate buffer zones. The establishment of context-specific thresholds, guided by predictive modelling and continuous monitoring, will be essential in protecting residential comfort and ensuring sustainable railway development in urban environments.
