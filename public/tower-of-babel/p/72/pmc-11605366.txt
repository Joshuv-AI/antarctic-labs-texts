S. cerevisiae ERG5DeltaNTH1DeltaAMS1Delta construction enhancing stress tolerance for ethanol production increase in the presence of inhibitors and mechanism analysis based on the comparative transcriptomics
Authors: Peizhou Yang, Zhuanzhuan Lin, Jianchao Chen, Shuying Jiang, Zhi Zheng, Kanglin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaccharomyces cerevisiae is considered the most promising large-scale production strain with ethanol as the main product. The fermentation of Saccharomyces cerevisiae is generally inhibited under various stress conditions. Various inhibitors in the hydrolysate severely inhibit yeast proliferation and yeast accumulation. In this study, S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 were knocked out to improve the yeast stress tolerance by the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats Cas9 (CRISPR-Cas9) technology. The result indicated that the stress tolerance of S. cerevisiae ERG5ΔNTH1ΔAMS1Δ mutant (S. cerevisiae SCENA) was remarkably improved compared with the wild-type strain. The contents of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan in S. cerevisiae SCENA were 1.67, 1.53, and 1.47 folds compared with those in the control. The ethanol concentration in S. cerevisiae SCENA reached 16.5 g/L, which was 1.23 folds compared with the control using rice bran hydrolysate. Further, the transcriptome analysis indicated down-regulated differential expression genes (DEGs) in S. cerevisiae SCENA were mainly from cellular response to glucose, cell periphery, and plasma membrane. Up-regulated DEGs were mainly from spore wall assembly, fungal-type cell wall assembly, and ascospore wall assembly. Thus, S. cerevisiae SCENA could effectively produce ethanol using the fermentation of lignocellulosic hydrolysate in the presence of inhibitors by regulating fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan metabolisms.Keywords: Saccharomyces cerevisiae, CRISPR-Cas9, Stress tolerance, Transcriptome analysis, Ethanol production

Introduction

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a model microorganism widely used in ethanol production and dough fermentation [1]. S. cerevisiae is generally exposed to various environmental stresses including high temperature, high sugar, and high organic acids during the fermentation [2,3]. These external stresses have a significant impact on the cell membranes with the disadvantageous characteristics of cell growth decrease, cell viability reduction, and product formation diminution of S. cerevisiae [4]. The engineering modification, mutagenesis, and breeding pathways have been developed to alleviate the stresses by enhancing yeast stress tolerance performance [5].

The compositions of membranes and the structures in cell walls directly affect the stress tolerance of S. cerevisiae under high-stress conditions [[6], [7], [8]]. The compositions of cell walls maintain the cell vitality and function from the adverse environmental conditions [9]. Sterols, mannan, and trehalose in the cell wall of S. cerevisiae play a critical role in high-stress tolerance under the external inhibitory conditions [10]. The ratio, composition, and structure of sterols affected cell membrane fluidity by the stability regulation of the multi-layer structure in cell membranes [11]. The alteration of sterol compositions rendered the thermotolerance enhancement by adjusting the sterol metabolites of yeast [12]. Mannan (beta-glucans) is a polysaccharide existing in cell wall with antiproliferative, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory functions [13]. High-content mannan strengthened the structural rigidity of cell wall and improved stress tolerance [14,15]. In addition, trehalose is an effective saccharide in the lipid bilayer. Trehalose prevented the phase transition events of cell wall and relieved imbibitional damage when yeast cells were suffered from severe environmental stresses [16].

The contents of sterol, trehalose, and mannan in the cell membrane were affected by the carbon flux and gene metabolism regulation of S. cerevisiae. The gene mutation of C-22 sterol desaturase ERG5 in S. cerevisiae resulted in the excessive accumulation of fecosterol [12,17]. Trehalose degrading enzyme NTH1 and mannan catabolism mannosidase AMS1 catalyze the trehalose degradation and mannan metabolism in the plasma membrane of yeast, respectively [18,19]. However, the effects of the simultaneous deletion of ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 on the fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan contents and yeast stress tolerance have not been investigated so far.

In this study, S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 were knocked out to construct S. cerevisiae ERG5Delta NTH1Delta AMS1Delta mutant using the CRISPR-Case9 approach (Fig. 1). The effect of gene deletion on the cell growth and ethanol production of engineered S. cerevisiae was investigated under stress conditions. In addition, the ethanol production of engineered strain was investigated using rice bran hydrolysate as fermentation substrates. Further, the molecular mechanism of stress tolerance improvement was explored based on the transcriptomics approach.

Note: A indicated that the main stresses of cell membrane in S. cerevisiae were from high temperature, high sugar, and high acid; B indicated that the composition and proportion modification of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan affected the stress tolerance of S. cerevisiae; C represented that the gene deletion of S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 was beneficial for the accumulation of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan according to various metabolic pathways.

Material and methods

Strains, plasmids, and chemicals

S. cerevisiae used for gene deletion for engineering strain construction was haploid. Cas9-NTC vector embracing nourseothricin resistance gene (NTC) and gRNA-trp-HYB plasmid embracing hygromycin B resistance gene (HyB) were obtained from Addgene Company (USA). Weblink http://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/was used to screen the 20-bp gRNA sequences to identify the targeted genes according to the calculated scores. The Taq enzyme of 2 × Phusion master mix for PCR amplification was from NEB Company (USA). The primer synthesis, gene sequencing, library construction, and data treatment for transcriptomics analysis were performed by Sangon Biotech Company (Shanghai, China). All the chemicals with an analytically pure grade were from Sangon Biotech Company (China, Shanghai).

Extracellular gene integration strategy by the LiAc-ssDNA method

The transformation of exogenous genes into S. cerevisiae cell was performed according to the PEG-mediated LiAc-ssDNA method [22]. The transformation was carried out as the parameters of 240 μL PEG 3350 (50 %, v/v), 36 μL LiAc, 50 μL SSDNA (2 mg/mL), 10 μL plasmid, and nuclease-free Water 24 μL. The mixed system was incubated in a 42 °C water bath for 25 min. After a centrifugation of 4500 rpm for 30 s, the cells were collected and suspended with the addition of 700 μL YPD. Then, 20 μL of cell solution was coated on the solid screening plate containing antibiotics and incubated at 30 °C for 60 h.

Engineered S. cerevisiae construction using the CRISPR-Cas9 technology

Cas9-NTC was transformed into S. cerevisiae on a solid screening plate containing 80 μg/mL of antibiotic NTC. Expression vectors for gRNA transcription and insertion DNA were simultaneously transferred into the transformant on the solid plate. The solid screening media contained 80 μg/mL of NTC and 300 μg/mL of HyB. ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1. After the next transformation of gene deletion, the plasmids carrying antibiotics needed to be further lost by the identification on the solid plate containing antibiotics. Each engineered strain was confirmed by DNA amplification and sequencing. The final engineered strain with simultaneous gene deletion of S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 was named S. cerevisiae SCENA.

Stress test of S. cerevisiae SCENA on the solid media

The effect of gene deletion on the stress tolerance of S. cerevisiae SCENA was investigated under the conditions of high temperature, high glucose concentration, and acetic acid. The growth proliferation of colonies was tested on the solid media under the conditions of 42 °C, 5.5 g/L of acetic acid, and 400 g/L of glucose. The YPD solid medium in addition to 5.5 g/L acetic acid and YPD solid medium prepared by 400 g/L glucose were used to test the stress tolerance of yeast. In addition, five dilution gradients ten times were sampled in each set of conditions. The amount of 5 μL of the solution was dropped onto the solid media. After culturing for 48 h at 30 °C, the growth and morphology of colonies were recorded in the presence of glucose and acetic acid. The growth of yeast cells was analyzed after a culture of 48 h under different temperatures.

Effect of stresses on the proliferation of S. cerevisiae SCENA

The effect of various stresses such as 42 °C, 5.5 g/L acetic acid, 400 g/L glucose on the cell proliferation of S. cerevisiae SCENA was further investigated under liquid fermentation conditions. The absorbance value at the wavelength of 600 nm (OD600nm) was measured to represent the growth of yeast cells. The fermentation for cell proliferation was performed using the 250-mL Triangular flask equipped with 100 mL YPD broth. The samples of fermentation broth were suctioned to measure the cell concentration every 6 h during 0–72 h.

Viability determination of S. cerevisiae SCENA under high-stress conditions

Loeffler's methylene blue stain was used to determine yeast cell viability [23]. The cell morphology of S. cerevisiae SCENA was observed using a microscope (OLYMPUS, CX33 type) at a magnification of 400 times. The cell number of yeast was summed in the same visual field. The cell morphology was recorded by photograph with Photo Shot Software. Five samples were taken to investigate the cell morphology. In addition, 20 view fields were observed to calculate the cell viability of yeast in each sample. A concentration of 0.1 % Loeffler's methylene blue solution (w/v) was used to stain yeast cells. Unstained and stained cells after soak represented the living and dead yeast, respectively. The survival percentages of cells were calculated according to the staining data.

Cell morphology detection using a scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The effect of stress on the cell morphology of S. cerevisiae SCENA was investigated using a COXEM scanning electron microscope (Korea Kusaim EM-30AX Plus). After treatment of 5.5 g/L acetic acid for 10 h, the yeast cells were collected after a centrifugation of 5000 rpm for 5 min. After treatment with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde solution (v/v) prepared with a phosphate buffer solution of pH7 at 4 °C for 4 h, the cell was dehydrated with gradient concentrations of 30, 50, 70, 80, 90, and 100 % ethanol (v/v) with each dehydration time of 10 min. The gradient-dehydrated samples were freeze-dried after a low temperature curing and then glued onto sample trays and metal coated. The cell morphology photos were taken using the following parameters of work distance of 10 μm, voltage of 8 kV, emission current of 8 μA, and magnification of 5000 times.

Rice bran pretreatment by enzymatic hydrolysis

Rice bran was pretreated using the alkali pretreatment method for lignin removal [24]. The rice bran was crushed to powder with a diameter of 1–2 mm. The mixture of rice bran powder and 1 mol/L of NaOH solution in a ratio of 1–10 (w/v) was soaked at 30 °C. After treatment for 24 h, the powder was washed to pH 7 and dried by hot air. Then, a complex enzyme containing cellulase (100 000 U/g) and xylanase (50 000 U/g) was added to the solution of rice bran powder in the ratio of 1 ‰ (w/w), incubation at 50 °C for 6 h. The enzymatic hydrolysis of rice bran was performed by the addition of the mixed enzyme adjusted to pH4 using 0.01 M citric acid. The mixed enzyme was composed of 0.5 % glucoamylase (w/v), 1.5 % protease (w/v), and 1.5 % cellulase (w/v).

Ethanol production of yeast using rice bran hydrolysate

The hydrolysate of rice bran was isolated from the solid residue through filtration with a filter cloth with a size of 30 μm diameter. The concentrated rice bran hydrolysate (approximately 10 % of sugar, w/v) was sterilized using a sterilization pot at 120 °C for 20 min. The fermentation broth was prepared with the addition of yeast extract (1 %, w/v) and peptone (2 %, w/v) in the sterilized rice bran hydrolysate. The amount of 5 % S. cerevisiae (v/v) was added into the broth for further fermentation at 30 °C with a shaking speed of 200 rpm.

Determination of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan contents

Dry yeast was prepared through centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min, water washing for 3 times, and drying at 65 °C for 10 h. Total sterols extraction, fecosterol isolation, and content measurement were carried out with slight modifications based on the previous reports [[25], [26], [27]]. The hexane layer was scanned spectrophotometrically between 240 nm and 300 nm with a UV–visible spectrophotometer. According to the characteristic four-peak curve of ergosterol, the content of ergosterol was determined using the previously reported [28].

The trehalose isolation, purification, and determination were carried out with slight modifications [29]. The mannan content was determined by the High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) approach after isolation and purification [30]. The parameters of chromatographic conditions were as follows: Waters Sugar Pak TMI column (6.5 mm × 300 mm), differential refractive index detector (RID), mobile phase of ultrapure water, flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, and column temperature of 80 °C.

Comparative transcriptomics analysis

The transcriptomics analysis of S. cerevisiae SCENA was carried out through the treatment processes of RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, sequencing analysis, and item analysis. The cDNA library was constructed through the operations of double-stranded cDNA synthesis and modification. After library detection and quality control, raw image data files were obtained using Illumina Hiseq™. Trimmomatic for quality shearing was used to obtain accurate and effective data by FastQC. Genes with q < 0.5 and |FoldChange|>2 were defined as significantly different genes. Transcripts Per Million (TPM) was used to estimate the sample expression and differential expression gene sequence analysis (DEGseq). Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were also analyzed based on the gene sequencing.

Determination of glucose and ethanol contents

The concentrations of ethanol and residual sugar during the fermentation were measured according to the HPLC method [20]. The protein in the fermentation broth was removed by adding 50 % of trichloroacetic acid (v/v). After centrifugation, filtration, and dilution 100 times, the concentrations of glucose and ethanol were determined using the HPLC method as the following parameters: mobile phase of 0.01 mol/L H2SO4, column temperature of 50 °C, binary HPLC pump (Waters 1525), refractive index detector (Waters 2410), and chromatographic column (Shodex SH1011) [21].

Data analysis and software treatment

The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.) based on the statistics from three repetitions. The statistical and analytical implementation of data was conducted using OriginPro 2018 Software. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were marked with an asterisk (p < 0.05), two asterisks (p < 0.01), or three asterisks (p < 0.001).

Results

Engineered S. cerevisiae SCENA with ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 deletion

S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 were knocked out using the dual-antibiotic screening strategy by the CRISPR-Cas9 approach. The putative-engineered S. cerevisiae SCENA was constructed on the screening media prepared by NTC and HyB antibiotics after ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 deletion. In the control groups, no yeast colony grew on the solid screening plate. DNA electrophoresis identification indicated that the sizes of appropriately 1500 bp, 800 bp, and 600 bp were similar to the sizes of insertion DNA in AMS1, ERG5, and NTH1 genes. The true transformant S. cerevisiae SCENA was further confirmed through the gene sequencing.

Stress tolerance of S. cerevisiae SCENA

The effect of various stresses on the colony growth of S. cerevisiae SCENA was investigated under the different stress conditions of temperature, glucose, and acetic acid (Fig. 2-A, B, and C). S. cerevisiae SCENA exhibited a stronger proliferation ability than the control. After dilution 105 times, several colonies from S. cerevisiae SCENA still grew with no colony available in the control. The cell concentrations of S. cerevisiae SCENA were significantly decreased in the presence of 42 °C, 5.5 g/L acetic acid, and 400 g/L glucose compared with their lowest parameters under liquid fermentation conditions. Then, the parameters of 42 °C, 5.5 g/L acetic acid, and 400 g/L glucose were used to test the stress tolerance of S. cerevisiae SCENA compared with the control on the solid media (Fig. 2-A, B and C). Thus, the simultaneous deletion of S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 resulted in significant resistance improvement in terms of temperature, acetic acid, and glucose.

Note: A, B, and C represented the colonies of yeast growing on the solid media under 42 °C, 5.5 g/L of acetic acid, and 400 g/L of glucose, respectively. D, E, and F indicated that OD600 nm values of S. cerevisiae under different temperatures, acetic acids, and glucose, respectively.

Stresses affecting the growth of S. cerevisiae SCENA compared with the control

The parameters of 30 °C and 42 °C, 5.5 g/L acetic acid, and 400 g/L glucose were used to measure the cell proliferation of S. cerevisiae SCENA (Fig. 2-D, E, and F). The OD600 nm values of S. cerevisiae SCENA under 42 °C (8.46), 5.5 g/L acetic acid (6.73), and 400 g/L glucose (12.15) were 1.13, 1.18, and 1.07-folds of the control (7.47, 5.68, and 11.39) after fermentation of 72 h. Thus, the stress tolerance of S. cerevisiae SCENA was dramatically improved under these stresses.

Cell viability of S. cerevisiae SCENA in 5.5 g/L acetic acid

The cell viability and membrane morphology of yeast were determined using Loeffler's methylene blue solution staining method and COXEM scanning electron microscope in the presence of 5.5 g/L acetic acid, respectively (Fig. 3). The percentage of stained S. cerevisiae SCENA cells was less than the control (Fig. 3-A, B). Further, the photos taken by scanning electron microscope showed that the cells of the control exhibited irregular wrinkled shapes after acetic acid treatment (Fig. 3-C). The cell morphology of S. cerevisiae SCENA exhibited a uniform elliptical shape with consistent size and shape (Fig. 3-D). The statistical results based on staining indicated that the viability percentage of S. cerevisiae SCENA (83.1 %) was 1.83 folds compared with the control (45.3 %) after treatment for 48 h under the condition of 5.5 g/L acetic acid (Fig. 3-E). Therefore, S. cerevisiae SCENA had high cellular viability without cell membrane deformation in the presence of 5.5 g/L acetic acid.

Effect of gene deletion on fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan contents

The contents of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan in S. cerevisiae SCENA were measured to compare the effect of gene deletion on the individual oligosaccharide accumulation (Fig. 3-F). The contents of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan in S. cerevisiae SCENA (5.41, 6.27, and 88.25 mg/g) were 1.67, 1.53, and 1.47 folds compared with those in the control group (3.23, 4.11, and 60.13 mg/g), respectively. Thus, the deletion of S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 resulted in the content increase of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan.

Glucose consumption and ethanol production of S. cerevisiae SCENA

The cell proliferation, glucose, and ethanol contents were measured to investigate the effect of gene deletion on the fermentation of S. cerevisiae SCENA (Fig. 4). The curve trend of OD600 nm values between S. cerevisiae SCENA and the control remained consistent during fermentation of 72 h (Fig. 4-A). The gene deletion could not affect the cell growth of S. cerevisiae SCENA. In addition, both S. cerevisiae SCENA and the control consumed glucose in fermentation broth with 22 g/L of ethanol concentration after fermentation of 48 h. Almost no difference existed between S. cerevisiae SCENA and the control. Thus, the deletion of three genes did not affect the production of ethanol during the fermentation process (Fig. 4-B).

Ethanol production of S. cerevisiae SCENA using rice bran hydrolysate

The ethanol concentrations of S. cerevisiae SCENA and the control were measured using rice bran hydrolysate as fermentation substrate. Rice bran hydrolysate consisted of 50 g/L of glucose, 23 g/L of xylose, and 27 g/L of other reducing sugars after saccharification (Fig. 4-C). The ethanol concentration of S. cerevisiae SCENA was 16.5 g/L, which was 1.23 folds compared with the control (13.4 g/L) after fermentation for 72 h (Fig. 4-D). S. cerevisiae SCENA produced more ethanol than the control. Thus, engineered S. cerevisiae SCENA after gene deletion could adapt to more complex fermentation conditions in rice bran hydrolysate than the wild-type strain.

Down and up-regulated DEGs analysis based on GO enrichment

The numbers of 8651098595 and 5949425185 bp bases from S. cerevisiae SCENA and the control were obtained to analyze DEGs through data quality control from the raw bases, respectively. S. cerevisiae SCENA had 1729 up-regulated genes and 336 down-regulated genes compared with the control according to the TPM analysis. The number of up-regulated genes far exceeded the down-regulated ones (Fig. 5-A).

Up-regulated and down-regulated genes and metabolites of S. cerevisiae SCENA. Note: A represented up-regulated and down-regulated genes based on DEGs data; B and C represented bubble distribution maps of down-regulated and up-regulated genes based on the GO enrichment, respectively.

The point distributions of down and up-regulated DEGs were drawn based on GO enrichment. According to the analysis of down-regulated DEGs, the high-rich factors mainly were from cellular response to glucose, ADP metabolic process, glycolytic process, pyruvate biosynthesis process, and ATP generation from ADP. The biggest rich factor (0.5) was from cellular response to glucose (Fig. 5-B). The significant numbers were from the cell periphery, oxoacid, plasma membrane, organic acid metabolic process, carbohydrate metabolic process, and monocarboxylic acid metabolic process, in which significant DEGs numbers from the cell periphery and plasma membrane (60 DEGs) was the biggest.

In addition, the GO enrichment of up-regulated DEGs indicated that the rich factors were mainly from DNA replication, spore wall assembly, fungal-type cell wall assembly, ascospore wall assembly, and sister chromatid cohesion. The rich factor exceeding 0.6 was mainly from spore wall assembly and fungal-type cell wall assembly (Fig. 5-C). The big significant up-regulated DEG numbers were from the cell cycle, cell cycle process, chromosome, and the mitotic cell cycle, in which the biggest number was from the cell cycle (250 DEGs).

KEGG analysis based on the transcriptomic DEGs data

KEGG pathway analysis indicated that DEG groups were divided into five categories including cellular processes, environmental information processing, genetic information processing, metabolism, and organic systems (Fig. 6). In cellular processes, cell growth, transport, and catabolism showed significant differences. In the environmental information processing category, membrane transport and signal transformation showed more enriched numbers of significant DEGs. In the genetic information processing category, the significant DEGs were mainly enriched in translation, replication, and repair. In the metabolism category, the significant DEGs were mainly enriched in carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, and glycan biosynthesis. In the organic systems category, endocrine system, aging, and development were the main significant DEGs. Thus, the gene deletion in S. cerevisiae SCENA mainly resulted in the significant metabolism regulation in carbohydrate, energy, lipid, and glycan biosynthesis (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The excessive sugar, organic acids, inhibitors, and temperature generally decreased the production of ethanol and constricted the proliferation of yeast cells [31]. Various approaches including genetic modification, gene deletion, and overexpression were used to improve the ethanol yield and survival rate of yeast. S. cerevisiae ADY2 and PHO13 deletion increased ethanol production in the presence of high-concentration acetic acid [32,33]. S. cerevisiae ELM1, ADE1, ADE13, and ADE17 overexpression resulted in ethanol yield improvement [34,35]. In addition, S. cerevisiae SNF1, ACE2, RCK1, and HSF1 overexpression also increased the production rate of ethanol and cell biomass under high sugar and temperature [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. In this study, S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 genes were simultaneously deleted to regulate the fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan metabolisms for ethanol yield improvement in the presence of inhibitors. S. cerevisiae ERG5delta NTH1delta AMS1delta mutant possessed stronger stress tolerance than the wild-type strain. S. cerevisiae SCENA still maintained the more excellent proliferation efficiency and fermentation performance than the wild-type strain in the presence of inhibitors. Further, the fermentation of rice bran hydrolysate indicated that the ethanol yield of S. cerevisiae SCENA was 23 % higher than that of the control. Therefore, this study developed engineered S. cerevisiae SCENA for ethanol production with high-stress tolerance by increasing the fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan contents in the cell membrane.

The cell concentration of engineered yeast was higher than the wild-type strain in the rice bran hydrolysate medium. Bothe the biomass accumulation and alterations in cellular enhanced resistance mechanisms could attribute to the increase of ethanol content. However, which is main factor to increase the ethanol content still need further be investigated in future research. Although the constructed engineering yeast showed some improvement in temperature tolerance, acetic acid resistance, and high sugar tolerance, the tolerance of the engineering yeast constructed in this study still had shortcomings, mainly reflected in the gap between temperature tolerance and practical application. In addition, the comprehensive stress resistance of the constructed engineering yeast still needed further verification. The main potential challenges in scaling up the engineered strain for industrial use existed in genetic stability with high tolerance, adaptability to comprehensive stress factors, safety assessment of engineered yeast, etc.

The membranes and the structures in cell walls affected the tolerance of S. cerevisiae under high-stress conditions. The sterol, trehalose, and mannan contents in the cell membrane were affected by the gene metabolism regulation of S. cerevisiae. In this study, S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 were simultaneously knocked out to construct S. cerevisiae mutant using the CRISPR-Case9 approach. This study indicated that the three-gene deletion in S. cerevisiae SCENA mainly resulted in the metabolism regulation in carbohydrate, energy, lipid, and glycan biosynthesis. In summary, the improvement of stress resistance in engineering yeast mainly lied in the overall regulation of the yeast intracellular metabolic system.

Conclusions

The compositions of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan in the cell membrane directly affect the rigidity, permeability, and stability of yeast cells. S. cerevisiae SCENA was constructed with S. cerevisiae ERG5, NTH1, and AMS1 deletion using the CRISPR-Cas9 technology. The contents of fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan contents in S. cerevisiae SCENA was higher than the control. S. cerevisiae SCENA possessed more cell viability than the control in the medium containing 5.5 g/L of acetic acid. The ethanol yield of S. cerevisiae SCENA was 1.23 folds compared with the control using rice bran hydrolysate as carbon source. The down-regulated and up-regulated DEGs of S. cerevisiae SCENA were mainly from plasma membrane and spore wall assembly, respectively. Thus, S. cerevisiae SCENA with stress tolerance improvement could directly associate with fecosterol, trehalose, and mannan components on the yeast cell membrane by regulating the metabolism of yeast cell membrane.
