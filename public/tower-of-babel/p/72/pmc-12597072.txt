A case report of Staphylococcus saccharolyticus bloodstream infection in a non-implanted host diagnosed by mNGS: Mechanism and pathogenesis analysis
Authors: Ziwen Song, Tingting Huang, Xi Jiang, Ming Hu, Hui wu, Jiaping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStaphylococcus saccharolyticus is an obligate anaerobic skin commensal that is rarely implicated in bloodstream infections, particularly in immunocompetent individuals without implanted medical devices. Due to its low virulence and fastidious growth requirements, it is often overlooked or dismissed as a contaminant. This report describes a rare case of S. saccharolyticus bacteremia and explores the underlying pathogenic mechanism.Case presentationA 50-year-old immunocompetent female presented with recurrent low-grade fever and oral mucosal ulceration. Pathogen detection was performed using plasma-based metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), which identified S. saccharolyticus at a relative abundance of 15 % (seven species-specific reads). The result was corroborated by anaerobic blood culture. Laboratory assessment revealed severe vitamin D deficiency (25-hydroxyvitamin D: 11.6 ng/mL). Initial treatment with ceftriaxone was ineffective. Following the initiation of intravenous moxifloxacin and oral vitamin D supplementation (cholecalciferol 2000 IU/day), the patient’s fever resolved within five days, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels decreased markedly from 88.3 mg/L to 12.0 mg/L. Mechanistically, the combination of mucosal barrier disruption due to oral ulceration and vitamin D deficiency may have facilitated translocation of the anaerobic pathogen into the bloodstream.ConclusionsThis case underscores the diagnostic value of mNGS in detecting low-biomass anaerobic infections and proposes a synergistic “mucosal barrier disruption–pathogen translocation” model. It also highlights the potential role of host-directed adjunctive therapy in managing bloodstream infections not associated with prosthetic material.Keywords: Staphylococcus saccharolyticus, Bloodstream infection, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Mucosal barrier dysfunction, Vitamin D deficiency

Introduction

In recent years, coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) have been recognized as significant pathogens in catheter-related bloodstream infections, largely due to the widespread use of implantable medical devices [1], [2], [3]. Among these, Staphylococcus saccharolyticus—a strictly anaerobic skin commensal—has rarely been implicated in bloodstream infections, particularly in hosts without implants [4], [5]. Its fastidious growth requirements and low presumed pathogenicity often lead to misclassification as a contaminant, complicating clinical decision-making [5].

Conventional blood culture methods exhibit limited sensitivity in detecting anaerobic organisms, especially in low-biomass infections where oxygen exposure or subculture failure can hinder microbial recovery [6]. In this context, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a promising diagnostic tool, yet its sensitivity for obligate anaerobes remains variable, with recent studies reporting rates between 60 % and 80 % [8]. This highlights the need for case-based evidence to assess its clinical utility in anaerobic bacteremia.

To date, the pathogenesis of S. saccharolyticus infections has been primarily associated with biofilm formation on implanted devices [9]. However, its pathogenic mechanisms in immunocompetent individuals without foreign materials remain poorly understood.

This report describes a rare case of S. saccharolyticus bloodstream infection in an immunocompetent host without implants, in which the pathogen was detected by mNGS and confirmed by anaerobic blood culture. The patient presented with severe vitamin D deficiency and oral mucosal ulcers, suggesting a possible synergistic mechanism of mucosal barrier disruption facilitating pathogen invasion. This case not only illustrates the diagnostic advantage of mNGS in identifying low-biomass anaerobic infections but also proposes a novel model of pathogenesis involving compromised host immunity. By integrating pathogen detection with host immune assessment, this case contributes to a conceptual framework of “molecular diagnostics–mechanistic analysis–targeted intervention,” offering practical insights into the diagnosis and management of rare anaerobic infections in non-device-associated settings.

Case report

Basic information and medical history

A 50-year-old female developed a left buccal mucosal injury after consuming hard food on March 28, 2024. Beginning April 1 (Day 4 post-injury), she experienced low-grade evening fevers (maximum 38.5 °C) and fatigue, without chills or night sweats. She received intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g/day) at an outside hospital from April 1 to 5, with no improvement in temperature. The patient was referred to our hospital on April 6 for further evaluation.

Admission evaluation and initial investigations

Physical examination (April 6, 2024)

Vital signs: Temperature 38.2 °C (at 4:00 PM), heart rate 88 bpm, blood pressure 112/74 mmHg; Oral findings: A 1.2 × 0.5 cm ulcer on the left buccal mucosa with a hyperemic base and no purulent exudate; Other systems: Cardiopulmonary auscultation normal; no hepatosplenomegaly; extremities without clubbing.

Laboratory and imaging results

Complete blood count:WBC: 13.8 × 10⁹/L ↑ (ref: 3.5–9.5 × 10⁹/L);Neutrophils: 87.9 % (absolute count: 12.1 × 10⁹/L ↑);Hemoglobin: 110 g/L ↓;Platelets: 392 × 10⁹/L ↑;Inflammatory markers:CRP: 88.3 mg/L ↑ (ref: <5 mg/L);ESR: 51 mm/h ↑ (ref: 0–20 mm/h);Immune function:Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D]: 11.6 ng/mL ↓ (ref: 30–100 ng/mL);Imaging:Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT: No abscesses or infection foci;Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE): No evidence of endocardial vegetations.

In accordance with Michels et al. [7], the patient’s condition was evaluated for the likelihood of true infection in CoNS-positive cases:

mNGS experimental protocol

Peripheral blood (10 mL) was collected in EDTA tubes. Buffy coat was isolated post-centrifugation, and DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform (paired-end 150 bp), yielding ≥ 20 million reads per sample. Human reads were removed using the hg38 reference genome. Quality-filtered reads were taxonomically classified using Kraken2 (v2.1.2) against the NCBI RefSeq bacterial database (version 2023.09). A species was considered present if ≥ 3 specific k-mer matches were detected.

In negative controls (blank extraction, sequencing, and healthy plasma), Staphylococcus saccharolyticus yielded a maximum of 9 reads (mean abundance 0.07 %), significantly lower than the patient sample (7 reads, 15 % relative abundance; Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.05). No matching sequences were found in the in-house contamination database. Genome coverage of S. saccharolyticus was 12.3 % (of the 2.8 Mbp reference genome), exceeding commonly accepted thresholds for reliable species identification (> 1–5 %). Notably, key virulence genes such as adhesin SasF (GenBank: CP012742.1) were detected with a minimum read depth ≥ 5 ×.

Diagnosis and differential diagnosis

Microbiological evidence

Only the anaerobic blood culture bottles were positive (alarm at 10 h; growth index ≥ 30). Aerobic bottles remained negative, and subculture yielded no growth after 7 days. S. saccharolyticus—a rare anaerobic skin commensal—was implicated. Catheter-related infection was ruled out.

Diagnostic criteria

The diagnosis was based on consistent detection in anaerobic cultures and high-abundance identification of the same organism by mNGS. Contamination was considered unlikely due to:Very low abundance of typical contaminants (e.g., S. epidermidis < 1 %);Concordant results in two independent blood samples.

Final diagnosis

Staphylococcus saccharolyticus bloodstream infection (strict anaerobic bacteremia).

Differential diagnoses considered

Bacterial endocarditis – ruled out by TEE; Intra-abdominal abscess – ruled out by contrast-enhanced CT.

Treatment and clinical outcome

Antibiotic therapy

The patient initially received ceftriaxone (2 g/day IV) and oral levofloxacin (500 mg/day) from April 1 to 6, which led to a modest CRP reduction (88.3 → 76.2 mg/L; 13.7 %). On April 7, the regimen was adjusted to intravenous moxifloxacin (400 mg/day) based on EUCAST recommendations [10]. Fever resolved by Day 3 of new therapy (maximum 37.2°C);Body temperature normalized by Day 5 (36.5–37.0 °C);Inflammatory markers showed significant improvement:CRP: 76.2 → 12.0 mg/L (↓84.3 %);WBC: 12.5 → 6.7 × 10⁹/L (normalized);ESR: 43 → 12 mm/h (↓72.1 %)

Adjunctive therapy

Severe vitamin D deficiency (11.6 ng/mL) was managed with oral cholecalciferol 2000 IU/day, per the 2011 Holick guidelines [11]. Levels increased to:20 ng/mL at 2 weeks;23 ng/mL at 4 weeks

Clinical outcome

After 5 days of moxifloxacin therapy, both CRP and ESR normalized. The patient remained afebrile and clinically stable with no recurrence at 1-month follow-up.

Discussion

Diagnostic utility of mNGS in low-biomass anaerobic infections

Conventional blood culture exhibits limited sensitivity in detecting strict anaerobes such as Staphylococcus saccharolyticus, with reported detection rates ranging from 20 % to 40 % [6]. In the present case, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) successfully identified the pathogen within 48 h. Multiple parameters supported the reliability of this result:Specific reads: 7 (low absolute count);Relative abundance: 15 %, significantly higher than the control maximum of 0.07 % (p < 0.05);Genome coverage: 12.3 %, well above the commonly accepted threshold of 1–5 % for species-level identification [12], [13]; Virulence gene depth: SasF gene covered at ≥ 5 × depth.

Although the absolute read count was low, the substantial genome coverage, high relative abundance, and concurrent positivity in anaerobic culture collectively provided strong diagnostic evidence. Several factors likely contributed to the low sequencing depth, including:Host DNA accounting for over 95 % of circulating nucleic acids [14]; In low-biomass infections, read counts fewer than 10 with relative abundance exceeding 5 %—when concordant with clinical features—are still considered diagnostically meaningful [15].

The detection of S. saccharolyticus virulence factor SasF, a mucosal adhesin, further supports its role in epithelial translocation and invasion [16].

Nonetheless, limitations remain. Host DNA enrichment may artifactually increase background noise, and low read depth restricts comprehensive analysis of resistance or virulence determinants. Future diagnostic strategies should incorporate microbial enrichment techniques or single-cell sequencing approaches to enhance resolution and reduce host interference [17].

A proposed “mucosal barrier disruption–pathogen invasion” synergistic model

Revisiting traditional infection paradigms

Staphylococcus saccharolyticus has traditionally been associated with biofilm-mediated, device-related infections [9]. In contrast, the present case lacked any implanted materials. Instead, two concurrent host factors—mucosal disruption and profound vitamin D deficiency—may have contributed to a plausible pathogenic pathway.

Local barrier disruption: Recurrent oral ulceration likely compromised mucosal integrity, allowing opportunistic translocation of commensal organisms.

Systemic immune susceptibility: Severe vitamin D deficiency (25[OH]D = 11.6 ng/mL) may impair innate defenses through suppression of antimicrobial peptides (e.g., cathelicidin) [18], [19] and reduced epithelial regenerative capacity [20]. Preclinical data indicate that 25(OH)D levels below 20 ng/mL are associated with increased mucosal permeability. Together, these factors could have created a permissive environment for anaerobic invasion, although direct evidence is limited to this single case.

A vicious cycle of pathogen–host interaction

SasF expression has been shown to increase under hypoxic conditions, and the observed sequencing depth (≥ 5 ×) indicates its potential involvement in epithelial adhesion. Concurrent vitamin D deficiency may have exacerbated mucosal vulnerability by disrupting the Th17/Treg immunological balance [15], promoting a pathological cycle of "barrier disruption–infection–immune dysregulation."

Vitamin D supports mucosal immunity through the following mechanisms:(1)Antimicrobial defense: Induction of cathelicidin reduces bacterial adhesion and proliferation;(2)Physical barrier repair: Upregulates tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin) to maintain epithelial integrity;(3)Immune regulation: Maintains Th17/Treg balance and suppresses excessive inflammation [12], [15].

Following supplementation with cholecalciferol (2000 IU/day), the patient’s serum 25(OH)D level increased to 32.5 ng/mL within four weeks. This biochemical recovery coincided with the resolution of oral ulcers and systemic symptoms, suggesting a temporal and possibly causal relationship. The clinical course appears to be consistent with a plausible sequence of events (Vitamin D Deficiency → Barrier Disruption → Infection → Supplementation → Barrier Restoration), which should be interpreted as a working hypothesis pending further validation.

Given that this report describes a single clinical case, the proposed mechanistic interpretation should be regarded as a hypothesis rather than definitive evidence. Further experimental and multi-case validation is warranted to confirm the causal relationship between vitamin D deficiency, mucosal barrier integrity, and anaerobic translocation.

Implications for diagnostic and therapeutic optimization

This case highlights the clinical value of integrated pathogen- and host-directed strategies:

Pathogen-targeted therapy: A marked reduction in CRP (88.3 → 12.0 mg/L; 84.3 %) within five days of moxifloxacin therapy demonstrates rapid antimicrobial efficacy, consistent with EUCAST susceptibility profiles [10].

Host-directed therapy (HDT): Correction of vitamin D deficiency via oral supplementation (2000 IU/day) restored serum levels to 23–32 ng/mL, potentially enhancing mucosal barrier function and reducing infection risk through immunomodulatory pathways.

These findings support a broader diagnostic and therapeutic framework that combines molecular diagnostics with personalized immune modulation.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations must be acknowledged: (1) mNGS findings were not validated using targeted sequencing or culture-based confirmation of virulence gene expression. (2) The causal relationship between mucosal injury and bacteremia remains hypothetical; future studies employing animal models or in vitro barrier systems are needed to confirm this mechanism. (3) The specific molecular pathways linking vitamin D deficiency to barrier dysfunction were inferred from existing literature but were not directly investigated in this case.

Further research should aim to establish a standardized diagnostic pipeline integrating mNGS screening, single-pathogen isolation, and functional validation, along with dynamic modeling of vitamin D–mediated epithelial repair. Such a framework may improve the management of low-biomass infections, particularly in immunocompetent individuals without foreign body involvement.
