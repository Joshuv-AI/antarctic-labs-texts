Machine learning to predict ureteral access sheath placement failure in flexible ureteroscopy: development and prospective validation
Authors: Linjie Li, Shiqi Yang, Zhijie Jiang, Ruixi Feng, Ge Wang, Bingyang Zha, Fei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFailed placement of a Ureteral Access Sheath (UAS) during flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy (FURL) creates significant operative challenges. The objective of this study was to develop a machine learning (ML) model that integrates diverse indicators to predict the likelihood of UAS placement failure.MethodsWe conducted an analysis of clinical records from 1,153 patients diagnosed with upper urinary tract calculi, followed by the enrollment of 265 consecutive patients for independent prospective validation. The predictive model integrated variables including patient medical history, parameters from CT imaging, anesthesia modality, and markers of systemic inflammation. Nine distinct ML algorithms were formulated. The predictive performance of the model was subsequently compared with that of senior urologists in a prospective setting.ResultsThe final dataset comprised retrospective (n = 651) and prospective (n = 265) cohorts, both exhibiting similar failure rates of approximately 30%. Among the tested algorithms, the RF model yielded superior performance, attaining an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.845 in the testing set. During prospective validation, the simplified RF model achieved an AUC of 0.872, significantly surpassing the predictive accuracy of senior urologists. Through SHAP analysis, the mid-ureteral diameter, distal ureteral diameter, neutrophil count, renal parenchyma width, and the long diameter of the calculus were identified as the five most critical predictors. Furthermore, the model exhibited robust stability across various subgroups.ConclusionsWe successfully developed and prospectively validated a robust ML model capable of predicting UAS placement failure. By assimilating clinical, imaging, and inflammatory data, this model demonstrated superior performance compared to clinical experts. It provides a dependable instrument for personalized preoperative risk stratification and surgical strategy planning, accessible via a newly deployed web application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02127-z.Keywords: Machine Learning, Ureteral Access Sheath, Flexible Ureteroscopic Lithotripsy

Introduction

FURL has established itself as a common therapeutic modality for upper urinary tract calculi, frequently utilizing a UAS to facilitate the operation [1–3]. By creating a stable conduit, the UAS alleviates intrarenal pressure, optimizes the visual field, and thereby augments both the safety and efficiency of the surgery [4–7]. Nevertheless, successful UAS placement is not guaranteed, with literature reporting failure rates between 8% and 22% during initial attempts. Such failures not only pose a risk of ureteral injury but often mandate the premature termination of the surgery, necessitating staged procedures. This consequently escalates the financial burden, prolongs treatment duration, and exposes patients to the risks of repeated anesthesia.

To ameliorate these issues, the accurate preoperative prediction of UAS placement difficulty is clinically imperative. While preoperative stenting can enhance placement success, its non-selective application may result in overtreatment for patients who could have undergone a single-stage procedure without it [8, 9]. Accordingly, prior studies have investigated various risk factors. For example, Hu et al. identified male sex, short disease duration, and smaller ipsilateral common iliac artery diameter as associated factors, though the clinical utility of these findings is restricted [10]. Although a predictive model exists, its AUC was limited to 0.789, suggesting potential for enhancement [11]. Similarly, Erdogan et al. assessed pelvic diameters via KUB radiographs but were limited by a small sample size (n = 49) and few radiological indicators, requiring further validation [12].

Furthermore, with the proven safety of local anesthesia for FURL, UAS placement under local anesthesia is gaining prevalence. While data on this specific topic remains sparse, our center has performed numerous such procedures. This approach significantly lowers costs and circumvents general anesthesia risks, benefiting patients intolerant to systemic sedation [13–15]. On the other hand, while the impact of systemic inflammation indices on surgical prognoses is increasingly acknowledged [16, 17], such parameters have largely been overlooked in existing frameworks for forecasting UAS placement.

As precision medicine evolves, ML algorithms provide a sophisticated means to process high-dimensional, complex datasets, enabling the construction of reliable predictive tools [18, 19]. Given that successful UAS placement depends on a multifactorial interplay, single indicators are often insufficient for accurate prediction. Consequently, this study leverages ML techniques to synthesize a broad spectrum of variables—spanning anesthesia type, comprehensive inflammatory profiles, and CT imaging metrics. Our objective is to establish a model that identifies patients prone to placement failure more precisely, thereby empowering clinicians with personalized risk assessments to refine surgical decisions. Following development, this model has been integrated into an online interface to facilitate clinical practice.

Patients and methods

Study patients

We performed a retrospective analysis of 1,153 patients who underwent FURL at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University from June 2022 to January 2024. Eligibility screening was conducted based on specific inclusion and exclusion requirements (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 1).

Study flowchart. This figure illustrates the complete process of this study, from patient screening, data collection and preprocessing, to machine learning model construction, performance evaluation, and final model interpretation

Inclusion criteria encompassed: (1) patients undergoing FURL for upper urinary tract calculi; (2) availability of complete urinary tract CT data; (3) utilization of a UAS during the surgery; and (4) age ≥ 18 years.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of a pre-existing ureteral stent on the operative side; (2) multiple calculi; (3) severe urinary tract infection; (4) urinary tract anatomical anomalies (e.g., duplicated ureter, horseshoe kidney); (5) blurred boundaries between the ureter and surrounding tissues on CT, preventing accurate measurement; and (6) incomplete essential data, such as external CT scans that could not be re-assessed.

To verify the clinical generalizability of our model, an independent prospective validation cohort was established. From February 2025 to November 2025, we consecutively enrolled 265 patients at our center who met the identical inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study variables

We collected comprehensive patient data, including demographics, comorbidities, and medical history. Preoperative evaluations comprised complete blood counts and urinalysis. Anatomical assessments covered stone characteristics, hydronephrosis severity, anesthesia type, and dimensions of the renal parenchyma, aorta, iliac arteries, and ureters.

Several inflammatory markers were derived from blood count parameters, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), among others. The specific formulas for all indices are detailed in the supplementary materials.

Surgical procedure

Upon verification of patient identity, subjects were placed in the lithotomy position. Standard disinfection and draping procedures were followed, with anesthesia administered as either general or topical urethral anesthesia. The surgical technique followed established protocols as previously described [14, 20]. Briefly, a ureteroscope was advanced to the bladder, followed by guidewire insertion into the renal pelvis. After initial ureteral assessment, a UAS was introduced over the guidewire. Laser lithotripsy was performed using a holmium laser, and a ureteral stent was indwelt postoperatively for 2–4 weeks.

Outcome measure

The primary endpoint was the successful placement of an 11/13 Fr UAS. While 10/12 Fr, 11/13 Fr, and 12/14 Fr are common sizes [21], the 10/12 Fr sheath was excluded to avoid compromising irrigation flow. Failure was defined as the inability to advance the 11/13 Fr UAS to the renal pelvis or the calculus location despite successful guidewire placement. To guarantee assessment consistency and accuracy, outcomes were adjudicated by three senior urologists (associate chief physician level or above), each with experience exceeding 600 FURL cases.

Measurement of CT data

While CT urography (CTU) and intravenous urography (IVU) are traditional methods for ureteral assessment, they carry risks of contrast allergy and renal burden, alongside higher costs. Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) offers a safer, cost-effective alternative that sufficiently reflects ureteral morphology. Consequently, this study utilized NCCT scans (1.25 mm slice thickness).

Parameters measured included calculus dimensions, renal parenchyma width, hydronephrosis presence, abdominal aorta diameter, iliac artery diameters, and ureteral diameters at proximal, mid, and distal points. Measurement methodologies adhered to previously validated techniques [10, 22, 23]. Machine learning algorithms

Model development proceeded through feature selection and data preprocessing. Continuous variables underwent Z-score standardization, while categorical variables were one-hot encoded. The retrospective cohort (n = 651) was randomly partitioned into training (n = 455) and testing (n = 196) sets at a 7:3 ratio.

We constructed nine ML models, including Decision Tree (DT) and Random Forest (RF). Hyperparameters were optimized via five-fold cross-validation targeting the AUC metric. Performance was evaluated using accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, and specificity. The Youden index determined the optimal decision threshold. The SHAP algorithm was subsequently applied to the best-performing model to interpret feature contributions and non-linear relationships.

Comparison with clinical experts

For the prospective cohort, two senior urologists independently reviewed preoperative data to provide binary predictions (success/failure) for UAS placement. Discrepancies were resolved by a third senior urologist to finalize the clinical prediction.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses utilized R software (v4.3.1) and SPSS (v26.0). Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s) and compared using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median [Q1, Q3] and analyzed via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical data were reported as frequencies/percentages [n(%)] and compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Subgroup analyses employed DeLong’s test to assess model stability. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In terms of clinical history, patients in the failure cohort exhibited a markedly reduced duration of illness (11 days vs. 20 days) alongside a greater incidence of renal colic (80.10% vs. 72.75%). In contrast, a history of ipsilateral ureteroscopy (4.08% vs. 9.89%) and percutaneous nephrolithotomy (1.02% vs. 5.49%) appeared less frequently among those who experienced placement failure. Analysis of anesthesia modalities revealed that local anesthesia was utilized more often in the failure group (47.45%), whereas general anesthesia was the predominant method in the successful placement group (72.31%).

Radiological and laboratory data revealed that the failure cohort had elevated NPR levels and reduced urinary leukocyte counts. Crucially, anatomical measurements showed significantly smaller calculus long diameters, as well as narrower proximal, mid, and distal ureteral diameters in the failure group (Table 1).

As detailed in Table 2, a comparison of the retrospective and prospective cohorts demonstrated no statistically significant disparities across primary demographic variables (P > 0.05). The failure rate was consistent between the retrospective (30.11%) and prospective (31.70%) groups. Variations in anesthesia type and BMI provided an opportunity to test model generalizability.

Identifying the best-performing algorithm

Utilizing the multimodal dataset comprised of 651 patients, we established nine distinct machine learning models (detailed hyperparameters provided in Supplementary Table 1). Among these, the RF model delivered the most superior outcomes. On the testing dataset, it recorded an AUC of 0.839 (95% CI: 0.775–0.902), with accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity reaching 0.791, 0.763, and 0.803, respectively. The XGBoost model secured the second-best position (AUC: 0.832), whereas the KNN model displayed the least effective performance (AUC: 0.648) (Fig. 4A-C).

Based on these results, the RF algorithm was selected as the foundation for our final predictive model (hyperparameters listed in Supplementary Table 2). This model yielded an AUC of 0.926 within the training cohort and maintained an AUC of 0.839 in the testing cohort (Fig. 4D-E). Furthermore, the confusion matrices verified the model’s robust capacity for discrimination (Fig. 4F-G).

Simplifying the model

To mitigate feature redundancy, we applied SHAP analysis to the initial RF model (Supplementary Fig. 2). Features were recursively eliminated based on importance, and the model was rebuilt. A simplified model utilizing 21 features—including mid-ureteral diameter, distal ureteral diameter, and widest renal parenchyma diameter—was selected. This optimized model achieved AUCs of 0.913 (training) and 0.845 (test) (Fig. 5B-C). Calibration and decision curve analyses confirmed its predictive excellence (Fig. 5D-E).

Prospective validation and comparison with clinicians

The 21-feature model underwent external validation in the independent prospective cohort (n = 265), achieving an impressive AUC of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.824–0.920) (Fig. 6A). In the “Human vs. Machine” comparison, the clinical experts’ prediction point fell below the model’s ROC curve, signifying the superior sensitivity and specificity of the ML model. Confusion matrices (Fig. 6B-C) further highlighted this advantage: the model correctly identified 66 failure cases compared to 52 by clinicians, while maintaining high success prediction accuracy. Calibration and decision curves further attested to the model’s robust prospective performance (Figs. 6D-E).

Interpretation and utilization of the model

SHAP analysis quantified feature impacts for the final model. The top predictors included mid-ureteral diameter, distal ureteral diameter, and calculus long diameter (negatively correlated with failure risk), as well as neutrophils and renal parenchyma width (positively correlated with failure risk) (Fig. 7, Supplementary Fig. 3). A web-based prediction interface (https://lljde.shinyapps.io/web_page_operation/) was developed to facilitate convenient clinical usage (Supplementary Fig. 4).

SHAP interpretability analysis of the Random Forest model. A SHAP summary plot showing the importance ranking of features in the model and their impact on prediction results; B SHAP dependence plots of key predictive factors (mid-ureteral diameter, distal diameter, etc.)

To further investigate the phenomenon of lower urinary leukocytes in the failure group, we selected 216 patients with low urinary leukocyte counts (≤ 13 cells/µL) from the retrospective cohort for subgroup analysis. The final Random Forest model maintained robust predictive performance in this specific subgroup, achieving an AUC of 0.869 (95% CI: 0.819–0.919). Subgroup SHAP analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5) revealed that in the absence of elevated urinary leukocytes, systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., neutrophils, neutrophil percentage, and serum WBC count), anatomical parameters (e.g., mid-, distal, and proximal ureteral diameters), and calculus long diameter remained critical determinants of placement failure.

Discussion

The present research successfully established and verified a robust Random Forest classifier designed to anticipate the likelihood of UAS placement failure during FURL. This predictive tool integrates a broad spectrum of clinical variables, spanning patient demographics, medical history, laboratory indices, and CT imaging characteristics. Upon comparing nine different machine learning approaches, the Random Forest algorithm—subsequent to feature optimization—demonstrated the most effective performance (Test set AUC: 0.845). While the retrospective findings laid the groundwork, the prospective validation yielded an even higher AUC of 0.872, confirming the model’s generalizability and lack of overfitting. The “Human vs. Machine” comparison provided compelling evidence favoring AI assistance, as the model outperformed senior urologists. Subgroup analyses further verified robustness across gender and BMI categories. Crucially, SHAP analysis unveiled complex interactions among predictors, and the model is now publicly accessible via a web application, marking a significant stride toward clinical precision medicine.

A pivotal finding is the critical role of ureteral anatomy. SHAP analysis quantified a potent negative correlation between ureteral diameter and placement failure (Fig. 7B): risk rises sharply as mid- and distal diameters decrease. This aligns with clinical intuition and prior research, such as Fulla et al., who identified ureteral diameter as a predictor of injury [10]. Interestingly, a larger calculus long diameter was associated with successful placement. We hypothesize that large, impacted calculi cause chronic obstruction, inducing passive ureteral dilation and creating favorable conditions for the sheath. Similarly, a smaller renal parenchyma width was linked to lower failure risk, likely due to the same mechanism of long-term obstruction causing hydronephrosis and parenchymal thinning alongside ureteral dilation.

Notably, neutrophils emerged as a vital inflammatory predictor. Although the initial comparison between groups did not yield statistical significance regarding neutrophil levels, the machine learning algorithm detected their substantial impact through the analysis of multivariate interactions. High neutrophil levels, serving as an index of the inflammatory response [24, 25], are likely indicative of a subclinical systemic inflammatory state that results in edema of the ureteral wall and compromised compliance. The observation of a markedly elevated NPR within the failure cohort lends support to this hypothesis. Paradoxically, the failure group exhibited lower counts of urinary leukocytes, implying a distinct separation between the roles of local infection and systemic inflammation—a finding that merits future study. Combined with observations in clinical practice, while the exact mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain uncertain, it provides an interesting avenue for clinical speculation. One plausible hypothesis is that this finding might be related to a severe or complete ureteral obstruction caused by an impacted calculus. In such potential scenarios, even if an upper urinary tract infection is present, purulent urine could be temporarily trapped above the obstruction and unable to flow into the bladder, which might tentatively explain the low or negative leukocyte counts observed in routine urinalysis. In reality, this localized trapped infection not only further exacerbates the edema and stiffness of the ureteral wall but also induces or aggravates systemic inflammation, severely compromising ureteral compliance and thereby hindering the successful placement of the UAS.

To further explore this clinical scenario, we applied our model to the subgroup of patients with low urinary leukocyte counts (≤ 13 cells/µL, n = 216). The model maintained a high AUC of 0.869, and the subgroup SHAP analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5) confirmed that in this context, systemic inflammatory markers and anatomical factors remained the primary predictors of placement failure. Although our current model has successfully incorporated certain spatial parameters, further mining of radiological data is needed. Specifically, future research will involve delineating regions of interest (ROIs) for the kidney, hydronephrosis, ureter, and calculus, as well as incorporating the CT values of the calculus and hydronephrosis, to extract underlying radiomic features. This will further perfect the model and optimize surgical planning.

Additionally, our model uncovered an intricate association regarding the duration of symptoms and the history of renal colic. Although univariate analysis initially suggested that patients with placement failure had a shorter symptom duration, the multivariate interpretation via SHAP clarified that a prolonged duration is actually associated with an increased risk of failure. We postulate that for patients who retain calculi for prolonged periods, latent pathological factors—such as congenital narrowing, ureteral tortuosity, or fibrosis resulting from chronic inflammation—may serve as the primary barriers hindering successful UAS placement [26, 27].

Compared to literature reporting failure rates of 8% to 22% [28], our overall failure rate was higher (30.11%). This is attributed to our inclusion of many local anesthesia cases (where muscle relaxation is absent) and the strict protocol of not using balloon dilation or smaller sheaths upon initial failure, due to DRG-payment constraints and physician-patient relationship factors in China [29–32]. Therefore, the developed model serves as an accurate representation of clinical reality in environments subject to resource limitations. The model performed exceptionally well in the local anesthesia subgroup (AUC: 0.935), highlighting its value in settings without muscle relaxants where accurate prediction of “difficult” ureters is critical.

Additionally, the model highlighted other significant variables. In particular, individuals who had previously undergone ipsilateral ureteroscopy or PCNL demonstrated superior success rates regarding sheath insertion. We hypothesize that prior surgical interventions may induce a passive expansion of the ureter, rendering it more tolerant to subsequent sheath insertion. This contrasts with some existing literature suggesting that prior ureteroscopy might complicate placement due to potential post-procedural stricture formation or stenosis [33]. We also assessed various risk factors previously documented in the literature. While prior research has implicated demographic variables—such as male sex and younger age—as contributors to increased placement difficulty [10, 11, 34], these factors did not achieve statistical significance in our specific cohort. This discrepancy may be attributable to variations in sample size or inherent population heterogeneity, which might have obscured subtler effects in our analysis.

The clinical utility of these results is significant. Utilizing the novel web-based calculator (https://lljde.shinyapps.io/web_page_operation/), urologists are empowered to perform individualized estimations of placement failure risk prior to surgery. For patients identified as high-risk, surgeons can proactively formulate tailored strategies, such as opting for a smaller caliber sheath or planning for a staged procedure. Furthermore, this tool is particularly valuable for improving success rates in FURL performed under local anesthesia, thereby expanding access to less invasive options with faster recovery times.

Despite the promising results, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Although this study included a prospective validation phase, it remains confined to a single-center experience. Constraints regarding resources have precluded a multi-center investigation at this stage. Future research efforts will focus on inter-institutional collaboration to perform multi-center external validation, a crucial step for confirming the model’s generalizability across broader and more diverse patient populations.

Conclusions

In this work, we constructed and confirmed via prospective analysis a robust machine learning classifier based on the Random Forest algorithm, incorporating a diverse array of clinical parameters to forecast the likelihood of unsuccessful sheath insertion during flexible ureteroscopy. The analysis highlighted critical determinants, most notably the dimensions of the ureter and the level of neutrophils, providing new perspectives on the factors complicating sheath access. The web-based risk calculator developed herein offers a user-friendly and efficient approach for tailored operative planning, possessing substantial potential for routine urological practice.
