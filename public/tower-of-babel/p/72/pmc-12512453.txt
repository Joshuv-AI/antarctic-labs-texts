Late-stage hepatoid adenocarcinoma of the pancreatic head with a long survival after surgery: a case report
Authors: Xiao-Yin Yuan, Zheng-Wei He, Awang Danzeng, Pingcuo Ciren, Dong Kuang, Zhen-Hua Yang, Xue-Wei Jiang, Ling Guo, Bin-Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatoid adenocarcinoma of the pancreas is a rare primary malignant tumor. According to few previous reports, with poor prognosis and atypical clinical features, hepatoid adenocarcinoma of the pancreas is easily misdiagnosed. Its pathological examination often shows similar morphological and immunohistochemical features to those of hepatocellular carcinoma. There is also insufficient clinical experience in the diagnosis and treatment of hepatoid adenocarcinoma.Case presentationA 45-year-old Asian woman, initially suspected to have late-stage pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, was admitted to our clinic with abdominal pain and jaundice. The magnetic resonance imaging scan showed a pancreatic head mass with enlarged retroperitoneal and portal lymph nodes. To confirm the diagnosis, a thorough evaluation of her medical history, clinical presentation, imaging findings, and laboratory tests was conducted. An endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy was performed, and the initial biopsy report suggested a neuroendocrine tumor. Based on this finding, a multidisciplinary team discussion was held, and the decision was made to proceed with radical surgery. Postoperative pathological examination unexpectedly revealed hepatoid adenocarcinoma of the pancreas, which led to a revision of the final clinical diagnosis. Following surgery, she received three cycles of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy with a regimen of gemcitabine plus albumin-bound paclitaxel. Postoperative follow-up at 4 months indicated liver metastasis, for which she underwent microwave ablation. Subsequently, she was supplemented with adjuvant therapy combining durvalumab and S-1. At the time of writing, 16 months after her initial clinic visit, the patient remains alive without evidence of abdominal metastasis.ConclusionThis case of hepatoid adenocarcinoma of the pancreas achieved a better prognosis through a comprehensive treatment based on surgery after multidisciplinary team discussion, giving the patient hope for long-term survival. The multidisciplinary team, in the form of multidisciplinary experts coming together to discuss the patient’s condition, changed the patient’s treatment decision, which may be another option when it comes to complex cases.

Background

Hepatoid adenocarcinoma (HAC) is a primary extrahepatic tumor that resembles hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) morphologically and immunohistochemically and often produces alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) [1, 2]. The most common site of occurrence is gastric, followed by the ovaries and lungs; while HAC of the pancreas is very rare, the pathogenesis and epidemiology are unclear [2, 3], and the clinical features and behavior have not been elucidated to date. The prognosis of HAC of the pancreas is poor, and most patients have lymph nodes or liver metastases by the time of diagnosis. Several studies suggest that surgery is preferred in the treatment; however, there is no standard treatment protocol [4–6]. We share the cases of HAC of the pancreas encountered, demonstrating clinical symptoms, imaging presentation, tumor markers, hepatitis markers, histopathology, management, and survival outcome, with the hope of providing experience for our peers.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old Asian female with a body mass index (BMI) of 20.7 presented to our hospital with subxiphoid pain for more than 10 days accompanied by gradual jaundice of the skin. An abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination at her local hospital suggested pancreatic head mass with enlarged lymph nodes in the retroperitoneum. The local hospital thought it was a case of advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), suggesting the patients to receive systemic chemotherapy.

This patient then came to our clinic for further diagnosis and treatment. Relevant laboratory investigations were done which are as follows; total bilirubin (TBIL) 135.3 umol/ml (reference value ≤ 21 umol/ml), direct bilirubin (DBIL) 115.1 umol/ml (reference value ≤ 8 umol/ml), hepatitis B surface antigen positive, hepatitis B virus DNA 4.25 × 102 IU/ml, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) 1.0 ng/ml (reference value ≤ 5 ng/ml), AFP 5229 ng/ml (reference value ≤ 7 ng/ml), and protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II) 176.32 Au/ml (reference value ≤ 40 m Au/ml). The MRI suggested an irregular size of 34 mm × 29 mm mass in the pancreatic head. The diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) showed a high signal with uneven enhancement, dilated intrahepatic bile duct and choledochus, and multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the hilar region and retroperitoneum (Fig. 1). She denied any other medical history. After assessing the patient’s condition, the doctor in charge considered that there was some difficulty in diagnosis and suggested the patient to receive multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion. Our team performed a comprehensive evaluation of the information she provided, including interpretation of imaging data and analysis of laboratory tests. She had an elevated AFP but normal carbohydrate antigen (CA)-19-9 and uncharacteristic appearances on image; extensive hilar and retroperitoneal lymph node metastasis without peripheral vascular and bile duct invasion is not consistent with the highly aggressive features of PDAC. The patient was recommended to receive a puncturing biopsy of the pancreatic mass through endoscopic ultrasound, followed by consideration of surgical resection or other treatment depending on the type of pathology. Because of jaundice and abnormal liver function due to ductal obstruction, the patient received percutaneous transhepatic cholangial drainage (PTCD) treatment at first. Considering that this case might not be a conventional PDAC, the patient underwent an endoscopic ultrasound and biopsy of the pancreas and retroperitoneal lymph nodes. Puncture biopsy pathology suggested a high-grade PNET.

After jaundice subsided and liver function improved, the patient underwent radical pancreaticoduodenectomy plus hepatic hilar lymph node dissection plus parietal aortic lymph node dissection and parietal left renal vein lymph node dissection (Fig. 2). The digestive tract was reconstructed by Chen’s pancreatic-enteric anastomosis [7], and the patient recovered well and discharged 10 days after surgery without postprocedure complications such as infection, pancreatic fistula, or biliary fistula. The postoperative pathology indicated a poorly differentiated HAC producing AFP with cancer metastasis in all dissected regional lymph nodes (Fig. 3). Based on clinicopathologic and immunohistochemical findings, the final diagnosis of this patient was poorly differentiated HAC of the pancreas. Postprocedure, the patient received three cycles of chemotherapy with gemcitabine (1400 mg per cycle) plus albumin paclitaxel (400 mg per cycle). A total of 4 months after the operation, the follow-up examination predicted multiple liver metastases. Microwave ablation of the liver lesions was performed. After the ablation, the patient started to receive immunotherapy durvalumab (1000 mg every 3 weeks). Oral S-1 (50 mg) was administered orally twice a day for 28 consecutive days repeated every 6 weeks. The patient has survived for 16 months without abdominal metastasis since the onset of the disease, and the imaging examination suggested no tumor recurrence in the abdominal cavity.

Discussion and conclusion

Hepato-pancreato-biliary (HPB) is a comprehensive discipline, including the liver, biliary tract, and pancreas, which contains diverse and complex diseases. Most of them, such as tumor diseases, have been ranked as a leading cause of death and a critical barrier to improve life expectancy in many countries of the world [8, 9]. PC is the seventh leading cause of cancer death worldwide [10]. Moreover, the incidence and mortality of part of these diseases are increasing annually. According to the data of a study of 28 European countries, PC will replace breast cancer to be the third leading cause of cancer death by 2025 [11]. HAC of the pancreas seems a very rare tumor, and its diagnosis relies on typical histological features with immunohistochemical presentation. Therefore, the preoperative diagnosis seems difficult. The biological behavior of HAC is still poorly understood. However, most reports suggested an aggressive behavior of early metastasis. Disease aggressiveness and delays in detection contribute to the poor prognosis associated with HAC. With limited experience due to its rarity, the treatment approach has not been standardized. Previous studies showed that radical surgical resection is a key factor in prolonging patient survival [12–15]. The symptoms of HAC depend on the tumor site. Moreover, HAC presents no specific imaging features. Elevated serum AFP is more frequent in HAC compared with common adenocarcinoma, which may help in early detection [16].

Certain studies have indicated that viral hepatitis may not be a significant factor in the pathogenesis of extrahepatic hepatoid carcinoma. We report a distinctive and complex case of a female patient positive for hepatitis B surface antigen, who presented with upper abdominal pain and jaundice but exhibited atypical imaging consistent with pancreatic cancer, thereby necessitating an MDT discussion. Given the rarity of this condition, preoperative imaging often fails to provide an accurate diagnosis. The involvement of an MDT can enhance the precision of diagnosing such diseases, a practice that has been well-documented in existing literature [17–19]. Previous studies have shown that most pancreatic HAC are located in the caudal part of the pancreatic body. In our case, the HAC was located in the head of the pancreas, and lymph node metastasis to the hilar and retroperitoneum had already occurred at the time of diagnosis. This is in line with the highly aggressive character of HAC, which has been reported previously [20, 21]. In this case, the laboratory test showed elevated AFP but normal CA-19-9, which may be because the liver, pancreas, and stomach originate from the primitive embryonic foregut, and these organs may have retained the potential ability to produce AFP [5].

In this case, the patient received pancreaticoduodenectomy under the situation of the disease locally advanced, receiving microwave therapy after liver metastases and postoperative adjuvant durvalumab plus S-1, resulted in a relatively satisfactory survival with high quality of life. Previous studies have shown that radical surgical resection seems a key factor in prolonging patient survival [12–15]. As mentioned before, this patient underwent endoscopic pancreatic biopsy. The pathology suggested a high-grade neuroendocrine tumor. It was reported that pancreatic resection associated with lymphadenectomy represented the gold standard for patients with localized PNET in the presence of symptoms or suspicion of nodal metastases; moreover, surgery was associated with an improved survival in patients with well-differentiated PNET [22]. In addition, several studies based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database have examined potential benefit of resecting primary PNET in patients with distant disease [23–25]. Considering that surgery may benefit the patient, we decided to perform pancreaticoduodenectomy for the patient after MDT discussion. Pancreaticoduodenectomy is the best treatment for malignant tumors of the pancreatic head, in which the most critical step is called pancreatico-enteric anastomosis. The pancreatico-enteric anastomosis was performed with Chen’s U-suture pancreaticojejunostomy, which largely avoided pancreatic fistula. As we expected, the patient did not have any complications such as pancreatic fistula after the procedure, which is in line with previous study [7, 26].

The first-line treatment regimens for pancreatic cancer remain FOLFIRINOX and AG [27]. There was evidence that chemotherapy can benefit HAC sufferers, and cisplatin-based chemotherapy was considered as the most effective first-line systemic therapy for metastatic HAC [28]. Postprocedure, the patient received three cycles of chemotherapy with gemcitabine plus albumin paclitaxel; 4 months after the operation, the patient underwent microwave ablation of multiple liver metastatic lesions. Several studies confirmed that durvalumab plus gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel showed promising efficacy and acceptable safety for ampulla cancer [29–31]. Several reports also found that treatment of S-1 monotherapy resulted in complete remission of lymph node metastases and declining serum AFP levels [16, 32]. After the ablation, the patient started to receive immunotherapy and chemotherapy. The regime was intravenous use of durvalumab plus S-1. At 2 months after receiving microwave and immunotherapy, the serum AFP decreased from 725.5 ng/ml to 45.75 ng/ml. It had been previously reported that in AFP-producing pancreatic tumors, serum AFP levels are critical for the diagnosis and can be a marker for evaluating recurrent disease [33, 34]. The significant decrease in AFP levels following treatment, now stabilized below 20 ng/ml, confirms the efficacy of our therapeutic approach.

This case of HAC of the pancreas achieved a satisfactory prognosis through comprehensive treatment based on surgery after MDT discussion, giving the patient hope for long-term survival. MDT plays an important role in the diagnosis of this complex case. Some degree of response to chemotherapy and long-term survival has also been reported in locally unresectable, metastatic, or recurrent disease. Therefore, this means that aggressive treatment can be ensured even in the case of locally advanced disease, and treatment targeting metastases can also be considered. The application of immunotherapy to HAC is a new attempt. As a case report, the treatment effect is accidental, and more clinical evidence and studies are needed to prove the effectiveness of the treatment. Nevertheless, we hope that our experience with this case will serve as a reference for our peers.
