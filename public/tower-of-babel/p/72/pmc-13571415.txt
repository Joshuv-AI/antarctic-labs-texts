Communicating with children about a parent’s cancer: A document analysis of national cancer organization websites across 13 countries
Authors: Chuanmei Wang, Xiaoyan Huang, Jiefang Xu, Jiajia Qiu, Liying Wang, Xingyi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo synthesize publicly available guidance on communicating with children about a parent’s cancer from official national cancer organization websites.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative document analysis of eligible webpages and PDF resources identified through systematic Google and within-site searches. Thirteen eligible resources, each obtained from a national cancer organization website in a different country, were analyzed using the READ (Ready, Extract, Analyze, Distil) approach and conventional content analysis.ResultsSix themes described why, when, and how to communicate; who should be involved; what information should be discussed; and how emotional support could be provided. The resources generally favored honest, developmentally appropriate communication, preparation for treatment-related and daily-life changes, and involvement of trusted family members and professionals when needed.ConclusionsThe study provides a structured thematic synthesis of publicly available guidance rather than a validated communication framework. All included resources were from Europe, North America, or Oceania, which limits transferability to other sociocultural settings. Further research should culturally adapt and empirically evaluate these strategies across disease stages, child developmental stages, and family structures.

Introduction

Cancer is one of the most common life-threatening illnesses worldwide, with an incidence rate of 196.9 per 100,000 people in 2022.1 Among people aged 20–49 years, the reported incidence rate was 91.9 per 100,000.1 According to the UN World Population Prospects (WPP) 2024, the peak age-specific fertility rate for women is concentrated in the 25- to 29-year age group globally, shifting to ages 30-34 years in many developed regions.1, 2, 3 Consistent with this pattern, data from comparative studies of male fertility show that male fertility schedules are right-shifted, with peak rates typically occurring in the subsequent 5-year age brackets (30–34 and 35–39 years for males).3 This creates an overlap between childbearing ages and rising cancer risk, meaning that a large population of patients may have minor children.2 The psychosocial impact of a cancer diagnosis affects both parents and their children.4 For affected parents, a cancer diagnosis exerts a profound influence on their physical and mental health as well as their family roles.5 Parents with cancer commonly experience substantial emotional distress, treatment-related side effects, and relational challenges, which may impede open and effective parent–child communication.6,7 Beyond parental well-being, a life-threatening cancer diagnosis also compromises the physical, social, and emotional health of their children, whose reactions and needs vary significantly with their developmental stage.8 Adverse outcomes for children of all ages include disrupted daily routines, role changes, emotional and behavioral problems, and impaired social functioning, while minor children, especially those in sensitive developmental periods, are particularly vulnerable to negative emotions such as depression, anxiety, and fear, and may even develop feelings of inferiority.9,10 Existing studies further confirm that disruptions to daily schedules, such as parents being unable to take children to and from school or participate in family activities, can elicit traumatic stress responses and negative emotional states in children.11

Previous studies have suggested that open and honest communication between parents with cancer and their children about the illness could help children better understand it, improve their coping strategies, and assist them in adapting to current situations and changes in family roles.8,12,13 Such communication could also relieve parents of the pressure to keep their diagnosis a secret, help children and parents establish realistic expectations, and strengthen their mutual support and care for each other, thus facilitating the parent–child relationship.8,11 Although open communication is often advocated for its psychological benefits, its effectiveness and appropriateness depend heavily on the child’s age and developmental stage.8 Inappropriate communication may affect a child’s understanding of parental cancer, leading to psychological stress.14 In addition, cultural receptiveness may influence the way in which information is communicated.8,15 In East Asian families, cultural values emphasizing family harmony and protection often lead parents to adopt strategies of protective silence or concealment rather than open communication, intending to shield children from distress.14 However, this approach sometimes backfires, with children experiencing unexplained anxiety, self-blame, and emotional suppression due to the lack of honest, age-appropriate information.14 This creates a critical gap between parental intentions and children’s psychological needs, undermining trust and coping mechanisms within the family.14

Regardless of the cultural context, many children reported that they did not receive enough information to understand their parents’ cancer.14 Parents with cancer reported that they experienced major challenges and emotional distress when sharing information about their illness with their children.16 Most parents found it difficult to decide whether, when, and how to communicate with their minor children about the cancer diagnosis, its progression, and the possibility of death, and they were also worried about the impact of inappropriate communication on their children.17 They desired guidance on parent–child communication about parental cancer, but they may not have had access to such information due to the limited access to reliable, easily identifiable resources.14 Furthermore, many health care professionals may not have received training in instructing patients to communicate with their children, meaning that they may be unable to provide appropriate guidance and support.18

Existing evidence indicates that the content and focus of parent–child illness communication differ markedly across disease stages.19 Communication in the context of terminal cancer involves distinct themes, including disclosure of poor prognosis and impending death, shifted communicative responsibilities, and inter-parental disagreements.20, 21, 22, 23 In contrast, during the non-terminal phase, communication with minor children tends to focus primarily on disclosing the diagnosis itself, with limited discussion of prognosis or the possibility of death.24 Communication during the non-terminal phase is therefore increasingly relevant to families affected by cancer.25 Therefore, this study aimed to summarize the communication strategies for parents with non-terminal cancer and their minor children from official websites of national cancer associations, which are publicly accessible and authoritative sources of information, in order to provide appropriate guidance for health care professionals and families affected by cancer.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative design and the document analysis method to collect textual data on communication strategies between patients with cancer and their children, and to analyze it using the conventional content analysis approach to provide a reference for constructing parent–child communication strategies in clinical practice. The READ (Ready, Extract, Analyze, Distil) approach, which includes (1) preparing your materials, (2) extracting data, (3) analyzing data, and (4) distilling your findings, was used to provide practical guidance on maximizing the value of documentary data and ensuring rigor in document analysis.26 Data sufficiency was assessed during analysis to evaluate the rigor and completeness of the analytical process.27,28 In practice, this was confirmed by (1) analyzing all the eligible websites identified through the systematic search, and (2) observing that the coding of subsequent websites did not yield any new codes or thematic categories. In this study, “minors” were operationally defined as individuals aged 0–17 years, consistent with the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child.29 Additionally, this study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR), a comprehensive checklist designed to ensure the quality and transparency of reporting in qualitative studies.30

Source preparation

The website searches and data collection were conducted between January 1 and December 31, 2025.

Search strategy and source identification

A list of countries was compiled based on the directories of the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC). Broad search terms combining the name of each country with phrases such as “cancer society” or “cancer association” were systematically used to identify relevant websites through Google searches. For eligible materials from international cancer association websites in languages other than English, machine translation was used as a preliminary comprehension aid for initial screening. All relevant content was fully translated into English and Chinese using Google Translate, then back-translated using DeepL, and checked by research team members proficient in both English and Chinese to ensure accuracy prior to formal qualitative content analysis. Titles of the included materials originally in languages other than English were likewise translated into English by the research team and are presented in English in the reference list and tables.

An on-site search strategy was conducted within the identified official websites of national cancer associations. The strategy was built upon the key conceptual triad of “parental cancer,” “communication,” and “children.” To construct the search terms, we employed a hybrid approach, first identifying relevant Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) as the conceptual anchor for each domain: “Neoplasms” combined with “Parents” for parental cancer; “Communication” and “Disclosure” for communication; and “Child” and “Minors” for children. To ensure comprehensive retrieval within website search engines, these controlled vocabulary terms were then expanded with an extensive list of common free-text synonyms and variations (e.g., for parental cancer: “parent with cancer,” “mother with cancer”; for communication: “talk,” “discussion”; for children: “kids,” “adolescents”). These terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) to form flexible search queries (e.g., “parental cancer AND disclosure AND children”) to locate relevant content.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Websites were included if they met the following criteria: (1) they were the official websites of recognized cancer associations, and (2) they focused on communication issues between parents with cancer and their children. Websites were excluded if they involved: (1) content primarily concerning childhood cancers or cancers in other family members; (2) communication related to the terminal stage of cancer; (3) adult children; or (4) content presented in a language that could not be translated and understood for analysis, despite the use of standard machine translation tools (e.g., Google Translate) for preliminary screening.

Website screening and selection process

The screening was conducted independently by two researchers (C.W. and J.X.), both of whom had professional backgrounds in oncology and had received systematic training in qualitative research methods through a certified university course. The researchers first verified the authenticity of each site as an official association. They then independently screened all potentially relevant websites against the eligibility criteria by reviewing the homepages. All discrepancies were discussed through deliberation and checked by the corresponding author (X.H.) until full consensus was reached. Finally, this systematic search yielded 13 relevant websites addressing communication between parents with cancer and their minor children. Data analysis

The conventional content analysis method was adopted to analyze the data.31 The first author repeatedly read the textual data, allowing themes to emerge directly from the data without imposing any preconceived categories or theoretical frameworks. The process began with repeated readings of all the documents to gain a holistic understanding, followed by a detailed, word-by-word examination to derive initial codes. These codes, which were often taken directly from the documents, captured the key components of the communication strategies. The researcher then gradually grouped codes that shared semantic or conceptual similarities into meaningful subcategories. These subcategories were then further refined and organized into a smaller set of broader categories to form a structured set of communication themes. The analysis continued throughout this process, during which data sufficiency was assessed to evaluate the rigor and completeness of the analytical process. To ensure credibility, the data analysis was conducted by the first author (C.W.) and then reviewed by another author (J.X.). If the two authors failed to reach a consensus, the corresponding author (X.H.) was involved, or a discussion was held within the research team.

Synthesis of findings

Given the inconsistencies across most documents in how they address the disclosure of diagnoses and related issues to children, a structured thematic synthesis of these findings was developed.

Why do we need to talk?

Drivers of disclosure: prioritizing children’s growth and right to information

Some websites43,44 noted that disclosure of a cancer diagnosis was driven by considerations for children’s growth and rights. As reported by a relevant website,43 the primary incentive for disclosing a cancer diagnosis to children was to respect their right to information. “Children have the right to know about any important matters that affect their family such as a cancer diagnosis.”43 Meanwhile, other key drivers of parental disclosure identified by the websites included the conviction that disclosing such information could help children grasp family situations and improve their coping abilities.44 “For children with a mother or father diagnosed with cancer, they need information to understand what is happening and how to adapt to the new living situation of having cancer in the family.”44

Rationales for non-disclosure: safeguarding children’s emotional well-being

In contrast, some websites32,34,35,40, 41, 42 summarized the main rationales for non-disclosure, which centered on concerns about the emotional burden and distress caused by disclosure. However, these websites32,34,35,40, 41, 42 cautioned that non-disclosure could trigger unregulated reactions in children, including imagining scenarios worse than reality and developing more passive feelings. “Children can usually perceive that others are acting differently and sense that something is wrong. If they believe important information is being hidden from them, they may feel confused and frightened. Some children will even go out of their way to listen in unnoticed. When they overhear these conversations, they are likely to become more anxious, or even more confused and frightened.”32

When do we need to talk?

Conditions for appropriate timing

Some websites32,35,37,42,43 indicated that there is not always a clearly defined or universally appropriate time to disclose the diagnosis. “There is rarely a single best moment, which often leaves parents feeling confused about when to tell their child.”42 However, one website43 delineated specific communication timings for the distinct phases before and after a diagnosis. “During diagnostic waiting periods, progressive information disclosure is recommended, which involves truthfully conveying available information in stages while acknowledging current uncertainties.”43

Although most websites did not specify the exact timing of communication, some websites32,42 mentioned that essential conditions for an appropriate communication context, such as a minimally distracting, private, and time-sufficient environment, as well as the emotional stability of the caregivers, are crucial factors. If caregivers are not yet emotionally prepared, it may be advisable to postpone the conversation until they have achieved greater composure.

Times and circumstances to avoid

Some websites33,39 provided guidance on negative circumstances, including transitional periods in daily routines (e.g., after meals or before bedtime, when attention is fragmented) and immediately before significant events in the child’s life (e.g., exams or competitions, which are inherently stressful). As one website39 indicated: “Avoid having the conversation immediately before bedtime. If you wish or need to have a serious talk as an adult, make sure you create an appropriate atmosphere for the discussion.” Children need enough time and mental space to process information.33 However, one website33 noted that prompt notification is required in specific situations where a child witnesses an adult’s emotional distress, exhibits behavioral changes after overhearing conversations, or experiences persistent psychological distress (e.g., regressive behaviors, separation anxiety).

How can we talk?

Honest communication

Honest communication, which can improve children’s accurate understanding of parental illness progression and significantly enhance emotional security by establishing reliable trust relationships, was highly recommended by several relevant websites.32,33,35,37, 38, 39 As described on one website,33 “Honesty is most important. Tell the truth without downplaying or exaggerating. Acknowledge the difficulties while avoiding catastrophic statements—authenticity is crucial.” With regard to the boundaries and extent of honest disclosure for children, a relevant website33 pointed out that “When telling children the truth, you should neither overstate nor downplay it. Acknowledging the difficulties involved in having this conversation, while avoiding deliberate exaggeration, is essential to being honest.”33

Age-appropriate language and tools

The use of gradual, age-appropriate language and communication tools was recommended across several included websites.32,33,35,36,42 Several websites32,36,44 emphasized the importance of using understandable language, yet provided only vague assertions regarding concrete communication strategies. Apart from one website,33 which focused on the themes of “cancer” and “changes” and provided sample scripts for talking about cancer with children of different ages, few offered age-specific communication scripts. For younger children, one may say, “You know Mum hasn’t been feeling well lately. Today, the physicians told us the test results show she has cancer. The good news is that she has a very high chance of getting better.” For infants, toddlers, and preschoolers, one may say, “Mummy is sick and needs to go to hospital to get better. You can visit her soon.”33 Some websites32,35,36,44 also mentioned that children at different developmental stages demonstrate distinct levels of disease comprehension. Adolescents possess adult-like abstract reasoning but often face various emotional challenges when coping with illness-related distress.35

Relevant websites33,36,43,44 summarized several tools that can help children develop an understanding of key illness and treatment concepts, as well as facilitate emotional expression. One website42 regarded symbolic play (e.g., storytelling, therapeutic games) and art therapy as especially appropriate communication tools for young children. Some websites42,43 suggested that authoritative treatment manuals or professional medical websites should be made available to adolescents, respecting their autonomy and encouraging them to seek information independently. Other innovative communication methods were also noted on included websites as valuable in clinical practice, including therapeutic games, virtual reality simulations of medical settings, hospital visit experiences, and emotion journals.33 As described on one website,33 “The ‘Pop the Cancer Bubbles’ game is a creative therapeutic activity designed to help children comprehend cancer treatment through interactive play. In this game, parents and children blow bubbles together, with children encouraged to pop them by jumping, clapping, or stomping. Parents can use this metaphor to explain: ‘Chemotherapy and radiation therapy work similarly to eliminate bad cells (cancer cells).’”33

Multi-child/single-parent family situations

Most websites32,33,35, 36, 37,39,42 emphasized that distinct communication strategies could be adopted for multi-child and single-parent families. As one website37 pointed out, “When there is a substantial age gap between children (e.g., a 3-year-old and a 13-year-old), a differentiated communication model should be adopted, providing more detailed medical explanations to older children and using simplified, developmentally appropriate language for younger ones.” These strategies are grounded in the core principle of informational transparency, whereby all children, even if not engaged simultaneously, must be fully informed and included in communications.39

For single-parent families, children’s demand for information about their parents' health status generally remains unchanged, irrespective of their living arrangements following parental divorce or separation.39 Acknowledging the current limitations of the situation while providing consistent reassurance is essential during communication about cancer.35 “You must explain why this is the case and emphasize that he or she will be informed when you know more, just as the other children will be informed.”35

Who needs to be involved?

Some websites32,33,35 indicated that people who possess a deep understanding of the child’s personality, communication preferences, and emotional needs are considered the most appropriate participants. Parents and other trusted family members were highly recommended by some websites.32,33,35 Additionally, other trusted social support network members, such as teachers, mentors, or coaches, were considered advisable, given that ongoing support from routine interactions can promote children’s emotional well-being.33

As mentioned by some websites,32,33 children’s autonomy should be respected in their decision to inform others about their parent’s illness. One website32 outlined specific scenarios to guide children in addressing potential inquiries about their parents' illness. For peer inquiries: “Perhaps you could ask the teacher or school nurse,” or “Thank you for your concern, but it’s difficult to discuss this at school,” or “I don’t know the answer to that question.” For adult inquiries: “Thank you for your concern, but I’m not sure how to answer,” or “You might want to ask Mom or Dad (or another adult family member),” or “I don’t know the answer to that question.”32 In summary, the support network may include parents, trusted family members, school personnel, and health care professionals, according to the child’s and family’s preferences.

What should we talk about?

Disease-related knowledge

Most websites32,33,35,36,39,41, 42, 43 suggested that basic disease-related information should be provided to children, addressing common questions such as what cancer is and whether it is contagious. Some websites described cancer as a disease. For example, one website defined cancer as “a condition where a group of abnormal cells grow uncontrollably and crowd out normal cells.”32 Basic concepts including types of cancer, recurrence, and classification were also discussed.35 The specific name of the disease, by using precise terms such as “leukemia,” was recommended by some websites.36,41, 42, 43 “If a 5-year-old first hears about a ‘blood disease’ and then suddenly learns it’s leukemia, they may think the situation has worsened or that their parents hid the truth. In reality, don’t be afraid to use the name of the disease. We tend to describe illness as ‘being sick,’ but children worry they might catch it too.”42

Some websites33,39,41 summarized the possible cognitive biases that cause children, due to a lack of knowledge about cancer, to link illness to their own actions. As one child33 described his feelings, “When Mom asked me to get my toys, I got angry with her, and then she got sick. Maybe I made her sick.” One website33 suggested providing clear explanations when discussing conventional cancer treatments. “Chemotherapy is like a special medicine that kills bad cells, but it can also make people feel very tired,” or “Radiation therapy is like an X-ray. It doesn’t hurt, and it’s safe to touch me.”33

Treatment-related side effects

As summarized by several relevant websites,32,33,36,43 it is critical to disclose potential treatment-related side effects to children in advance, as this helps them adapt. Common post-treatment physical changes, including alterations in appearance, persistent fatigue, limited mobility, and visible surgical scars or dressings, were summarized by some relevant websites.32,36 One website33 indicated that children’s reactions are largely shaped by parents' responses to post-treatment physical changes, advising parents to first clarify the changes with a straightforward statement, like “Mom’s hair will fall out,” followed by corresponding reassurance or a concrete plan, e.g., “She will start wearing a wig, or we will go buy some new hats.”33

Hereditary cancer risk

Some websites33,38,43 addressed hereditary cancer risk and emphasized the importance of seeking professional guidance on risk assessment. One website38 suggested that parents should clarify the genetic susceptibility to cancer using evidence-based data in order to alleviate anxiety. According to this resource, only 5% of cancers are associated with inherited genetic susceptibility, including certain breast cancers (8%), colon cancers (5%), and prostate cancers (10%); it advises that individual risk can be assessed with a geneticist and encourages parents to raise any concerns with their oncologist.38 On the other hand, maintaining a balance between providing accurate information and offering emotional reassurance is crucial.33 For adolescents with more mature cognitive abilities, it is appropriate to explain that “Certain cancers do have a genetic predisposition, but most cases are not inherited. We can assess the related risks together when you reach adulthood.”33

Daily life concerns

When children ask questions such as “Who will take care of me?”, many websites32,35,36,38,43 emphasized the explanation of potential adjustments to everyday life and provided concrete clarification when changes occur in daily routines. “Starting next week, you will need to take the bus home from school by yourself,” or “Tomorrow you and your sister will go to the neighbor’s house so that I can accompany Dad to the doctor.”32 Time frames and analogies can also be used to explain medical arrangements, for example, “Chemotherapy takes about as long as watching two movies.”43

Opportunities for supportive participation

As noted on some websites,32,36 when addressing children’s inquiries such as “What can I do for Mom?”, it is important to strike a balance between encouraging engagement and avoiding excessive pressure, in order to foster a sense of achievement in children and prevent undue psychological strain. Decision-making processes and assigning household tasks within children’s capabilities were recommended by relevant websites.32,36 “Children can be encouraged to engage more extensively in family-related tasks, like ‘Could you help Mom tidy up the room?’ or ‘Would you wash this carrot for Mom?’”36 Several engaging ways, such as sharing photos from outpatient visits, making video calls from waiting rooms, or arranging appropriate hospital visits, were also recommended.32

How do we provide emotional support?

Emotional management methods

Various websites32,33,35,39,40,43 emphasized that emotional management encompasses daily regulation of parents’ own emotions and their responses and guidance to their children, particularly during the periods before and after treatment, with expert support sought when necessary.

In terms of children, some websites33,35,39,40,43 reported that parents’ recognition and understanding of their children’s emotional reactions, allowing full expression, are crucial to cultivating their capacity for emotional regulation. As mentioned on one website,39 “Silence stems from the child’s sense of insecurity. For example, because the father’s or mother’s illness triggers feelings in them that they were previously unaware of.” A relevant website33 recommended some methods of emotional management, such as providing children with regular updates on treatment progress to reduce anxiety caused by a lack of information. Additionally, assisting children in recognizing and naming their own emotions can also foster more mature emotion-regulation skills.40 In certain cases, both parents may need to go to a major hospital, which may lead to diminished attention to their children. One website35 offered practical suggestions: “For example, have the child draw pictures or take photos to send to you. If the children can visit you, they can bring cards or photos from home, flowers picked from the garden, or a toy to ‘take care of’ you in the hospital.”35 During treatment, parental fatigue and energy fluctuations may also cause diminished attention to children. Thus, some websites32,33,35 emphasized the importance of maintaining the family’s daily routines.

Some emotional tools were also noted as helpful for parents to ease emotional tension.33 One website33 offered methods such as creating an “emotional thermometer.” “The physical and emotional well-being of patients with cancer may vary during and after treatment. Sometimes it’s hard to let your family know how you’re feeling, and they might find it difficult to bring up the topic. An emotional thermometer could be helpful. This simple tool visually displays daily emotions. You can create one yourself, have your children help make it, or find many templates online—just search for ‘emotional thermometer.’ Select the emotions you want to include and add a movable pointer to indicate different feelings. Place the emotional thermometer where everyone can see it, like on the fridge or a bulletin board.”33

Professional and social support

Professional and social support was mentioned by some websites.32,39,43 In certain cases, when children find it difficult to communicate directly with their parents, professional and social support from relatives, friends, school counselors, and community counselors is recommended.43 In other cases, when parents face cancer-related challenges, support from diverse sources such as grandparents, friends, neighbors, teachers, psycho-oncologists, psychologists, caregivers, physicians, and pastors is essential.32,39

Discussion

Main findings

By analyzing the content of official websites of national cancer associations across multiple countries, this study summarized the communication strategies comprising six core themes for parents with non-terminal cancer and their children.

The theme “Why do we need to talk?”32,34,35,40, 41, 42, 43, 44 revealed that disease-related information should be disclosed in consideration of children’s growth and rights. A study by Chinese scholars14 indicated that Chinese mothers who took the initiative to disclose the truth about their cancer regarded the illness as an opportunity for growth and development, using it to encourage their children to study harder. Mothers in Japan and South Korea also expressed similar views.45,46 While there were some inconsistencies in the motivation for disclosure across the existing findings,43, 44, 45, 46 a key shared observation was that facilitating children’s healthy development was a primary concern for parents, regardless of whether they reside in an Eastern or Western country. This similarity may reflect a shared parental concern across cultural contexts for safeguarding children’s physical and psychological well-being. Current research has predominantly focused on the maternal perspective, neglecting the roles of other key stakeholders, including fathers, children themselves, health care professionals, and school teachers, in illness disclosure and parent–child communication. Future studies may adopt a family systems theory framework to explore the alignment of parental disclosure motivations, children’s needs and experiences regarding disclosure, and the influence of external family support (e.g., from health care and school settings) on disclosure decisions. Crucially, this exploration should explicitly examine the dyadic relationship and communication patterns between parents themselves, as consensus or conflict within the parental subsystem may lead to effective or dysfunctional communication with their children. This would establish a more comprehensive research framework for family communication.

Among these strategies, the sub-theme “Honest communication” was emphasized frequently by relevant websites32,33,35,37, 38, 39 which is consistent with existing literature.46,47 This may be because most of the websites included in this study originate from Western countries. However, disease disclosure in Eastern cultures tends to emphasize the notion of “benevolent concealment,” which is intended to protect children from the adverse emotional impact of parental illness.48 Indeed, due to the differences in perceptions of illness and death between Eastern and Western cultures, culture may serve as a key factor influencing communication.15 In Western countries, where individual autonomy is highly valued, patients and their children are generally recognized as having the right to obtain direct access to disease-related information. From the perspective of the included websites, an open and direct communication style is commonly adopted in parent–child communication about the illness in Western countries. By contrast, Eastern countries are deeply influenced by the Confucian concept of family-centered values.49 Mori50 further noted that, in Asian cultural contexts, patients tend to prioritize family harmony over making independent personal decisions, and disease disclosure is often conducted in a more indirect or reserved manner to avoid conflict and psychological distress. Based on the reviewed literature, children’s right to receive developmentally appropriate information may be given less priority, with more implicit and indirect expressions adopted during the disclosure process. Existing research15,50 has also documented cultural differences in disease-related communication between Western and Eastern countries, which manifest in the objectives, scope, communication participants, and the division of family responsibilities involved in disease-related communication. In general, the communication strategies identified in this study require further investigation and validation. Current strategies may be suitable for Western contexts but not necessarily for Eastern ones; thus, it is necessary to develop a tailored communication strategy framework adapted to Eastern cultures.

Another sub-theme identified was “Age-appropriate language and tools”.32,33,35,36,42, 43, 44 A possible explanation is that children at different cognitive developmental stages display disparities in their cognitive development and emotional regulation capacity.51 This observation may directly address one concern regarding the substantial differences in receptiveness, comprehension, and communication skills between younger (e.g., preschool) and older (e.g., 17-year-old) minors. According to Piaget’s cognitive development theory,51 children’s psychological development is a continuous process of organizing and reorganizing intrinsic cognitive structures. This process progresses sequentially through four stages: the sensorimotor stage (0–2 years), the preoperational stage (2–7 years), the concrete operational stage (7–12 years), and the formal operational stage (12 years and above).51 Each stage is characterized by distinct cognitive features, and while the sequential order of these stages is fixed, their onset may be advanced or delayed, with overlap between adjacent stages.51 Among the included websites, only one operationalized this developmental perspective by proposing stage-specific, age-appropriate communication strategies regarding language use and tools. In contrast, several websites32,36,42,43 merely emphasized the importance of “using understandable language,” but offered only vague guidance on its concrete, developmentally staged application. The imbalance between the growing demand for age-appropriate communication guidance and the lack of targeted staged guidance indicates room for further exploration, especially for minors with substantial developmental differences. Future studies may focus on exploring age-appropriate communication strategies tailored to children’s varying levels of cognitive development and emotional regulation; for instance, strategies may involve providing young children (0–7 years) with concrete, visual aids, and offering adolescents (12–17 years) opportunities for abstract dialog. Beyond these approaches, future studies could further explore combining personalized disease information delivery with the communication framework, as existing findings have shown that other factors, such as children’s age, sex, personality, and academic performance, also influence parents' decisions regarding the content and method of parent–child disease communication.52, 53, 54 This integration should take into account children’s cognitive developmental stages, emotional regulation abilities, how they receive and interpret information, preferences regarding disease-related information, and individual personality traits. Furthermore, socio-cultural context should also be considered, including varying legal definitions of the “age of majority” across countries, as well as familial communication patterns and cultural norms. Researchers can also develop age-appropriate resources such as plain-language educational books, parent–child communication guidebooks, and multimedia promotional materials to facilitate structured emotional assessment and support. These resources could be disseminated via multiple channels such as hospital outpatient departments and official platforms, enabling families to acquire practical communication guidance and feasible tools for reference. The health care system may benefit from multidisciplinary expertise, such as that of pediatric clinicians, psychotherapists, and social workers, to form a specialist family communication support team. This team could provide personalized communication consultation and guidance for families in which a parent has cancer, as well as monitor and address emotional distress in daily interactions.

The sub-theme “Multi-child/single-parent family situations”32,33,35, 36, 37,39,42 highlighted the need for tailored communication strategies. In multi-child families, differentiated strategies based on children’s cognitive and emotional levels are critical. However, parental anxiety or protective inclinations may lead to inconsistent or even contradictory disclosures across siblings, potentially triggering confusion and interpersonal tension among children.55 Future research could further explore feasible coordinated communication approaches that respect individual developmental differences while ensuring uniform and consistent messaging to all children within the family. Single parents tend to inform their children more promptly, possibly due to concerns about disease unpredictability and the absence of a co-parent.55 However, without spousal support, they may experience greater emotional distress following disclosure and find it difficult to sustain responses to their children’s follow-up questions.55 Notably, existing literature and online guidance provide limited practical insights into how single parents can effectively recognize and address their children’s informational and emotional needs under sustained high-stress conditions. Further empirical studies specifically recruiting single-parent families affected by cancer are needed. Communication in the non-terminal phase tends to be confined primarily to discussions of the diagnosis and adaptation to daily changes.13,24 By contrast, in the terminal phase, communication involves a distinct set of challenges, including disclosing the imminence of death, managing shifts in communication responsibility, and resolving parental disagreements.20, 21, 22, 23 Future longitudinal research is therefore recommended to track families from diagnosis and treatment through the non-terminal stage and into terminal care, so as to summarize the dynamic evolution of communication strategies, stakeholder networks, and approaches to emotional support, thereby offering empirical references for constructing a staged and targeted family communication support framework.

Regarding the theme “What should we talk about?”, most websites32,33,35,36,38,39,41, 42, 43 focused on disease-related knowledge, treatment-related side effects, hereditary cancer risk, and daily life concerns. This is consistent with Fisher’s52 research, which found that mothers and daughters frequently discussed topics such as physical changes after treatment, disease risk, and prevention strategies, including genetic risks and screening behaviors, to promote daughters' self-protection and healthy behaviors.52,56 Existing websites and studies have explored the content of parent–child disease communication from both the parental perspective19,32,42,43 and the child perspective.32,33,35,36,39,41, 42, 43 However, the degree to which the communication content actively chosen by parents matches children’s actual information needs remains unclear. Future studies could use a paired design to collect parents’ communication content preferences alongside children’s information needs. They could then analyze where these align and diverge, pinpointing key mismatches between what parents choose to share and what children actually want to know. This would allow targeted communication strategies to be developed to narrow such differences, making parent–child disease communication more effective. Notably, the theme “Who needs to be involved?” was described in less detail than “How can we talk?” and “How do we provide emotional support?”. The resources that addressed this theme consistently indicated that communication may involve parents, trusted family members, school personnel, and health care professionals, depending on the child's and family's preferences. The relatively brief presentation of this theme reflects the limited range and specificity of the guidance available in the included resources.27,28 In contrast, themes such as “How can we talk?” and “How do we provide emotional support?” exhibited substantial semantic diversity due to individual differences among families, children, and illness conditions, thus requiring more data to fully capture their complex connotations. This observation aligns with the principles of data saturation and sufficiency in qualitative content analysis.

Implications for nursing practice and research

Emphasis should be placed on the practical application of this strategy. While respecting the cultural backgrounds of different countries, the focus of communication strategies should be adjusted in light of local characteristics. It is also essential to collect information on the actual strategies and needs of local parents with cancer, minor children of different ages, and health care professionals in illness communication. This information can then be used to develop contextually appropriate communication strategies. In addition, future research should conduct stratified analyses based on disease trajectory (e.g., recurrence, terminal stage), children’s developmental stages (e.g., age and cognitive development levels of minor children), and family structures (e.g., single-parent, multi-child families) to provide differentiated communication guidance. Building on this, clinical practice could promote the localization and stratified development of standardized communication tools. By combining local culture with the cognitive characteristics, communication patterns, and needs of different disease stages and children’s age groups, appropriate communication guidelines and other tools could be developed and integrated into the health education support system. Simultaneously, strengthening relevant training for health care professionals may help establish a more adaptable and targeted clinical communication support system, ultimately safeguarding the parent–child relationship in families in which a parent has cancer and promoting the physical and mental health of minor children.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, although we made deliberate efforts to search for relevant resources from Asia and other non-Western countries, the scarcity of structured, publicly accessible guidelines on parent–child communication within these regions resulted in their under-representation. Therefore, the cultural generalizability of the findings may be limited, particularly for East Asian contexts. Future studies are therefore needed to examine parent–child cancer communication in these settings. Such research could aim to synthesize Western evidence with East Asian cultural values (e.g., benevolent concealment) to generate specific recommendations. Second, this analysis was limited to official website content, which tends to be general and subject to change. Many parents, however, may seek cancer-related information and support from social media platforms (e.g., TikTok, Facebook, Instagram) rather than official websites. Future research could integrate social media sources, local organizations, peer-reviewed literature, guidelines, and expert consensus for a more comprehensive understanding. Third, although this analysis identified six core themes related to parent–child communication strategies, a simple thematic summary may not fully reveal the interrelationships among these components. Therefore, a conceptual model of parent–child cancer communication could be developed in future research to provide a more structured theoretical framework for clinical nursing practice and health education. Fourth, the translation and interpretation of non-English website content (e.g., Dutch, Czech, Danish, French, German, and Icelandic) present a potential limitation. While we employed standard machine translation tools (Google Translate, DeepL) followed by verification by research team members proficient in English and Chinese, these materials were not reviewed by native speakers or professional translators. Although steps were taken to ensure accuracy, some fine distinctions in language or cultural context may have been lost, which could affect interpretation. Finally, although we defined “minors” as 0–17 years to align with international standards, this broad definition masks significant developmental differences (e.g., between preschoolers and adolescents). Future research could adopt more age-stratified designs to validate the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

This document analysis provides a structured thematic synthesis of guidance from official national cancer organization websites on communicating with minors about a parent's non-terminal cancer. Communication should be tailored to the disease trajectory, the child's developmental stage, and family structure. Because all included resources originated in Europe, North America, or Oceania, the findings should be applied cautiously in other cultural and health care contexts. Further research should culturally adapt and empirically evaluate these strategies across diverse settings.
