Regional Trends in the Opioid Epidemic Using Safety Signals of Drug Abuse and Dependence: A Disproportionality Analysis Using VigiBase
Authors: Bohyun Suh, Hyung Suk Oh, Ju-Young Shin, Ju Hwan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe opioid epidemic, once a major concern in the United States, has escalated worldwide through three waves driven by prescription and synthetic opioids like oxycodone and fentanyl. Regional patterns of misuse and dependence remain unclear due to data heterogeneity. To address this, we compared adverse events reporting for these opioids using VigiBase.MethodsWe analyzed “drug abuse and dependence” adverse events using VigiBase, a global pharmacovigilance database containing more than 40 million Individual Case Safety Reports (ICSRs). A regional comparison of opioid-related adverse event reporting was performed, with oxycodone and fentanyl as the primary drugs and morphine and tramadol as comparators. Adverse events were defined by Standardized MedDRA Query (SMQ) criteria and cases were stratified by three periods (1990–2009, 2010–2012, 2013–2024) and regions (Americas, Western Pacific, Europe). Opioid-related death was additionally assessed as a secondary outcome. Disproportionality analysis was conducted to identify safety signals, using the reporting odds ratio (ROR). Signals were considered significant if ROR > 2, χ2 > 4, and more than three reports were observed.ResultsOxycodone-related reports peaked in 2005 (n = 8395), 2011 (n = 10,444), and 2021 (n = 45,025), while fentanyl peaked in 2011 (n = 11,451) and 2017 (n = 32,704). The safety signals for oxycodone were found across all predefined periods in the Americas (1990–2009: ROR 4.40, n = 9102; 2010–2012: ROR 2.13, n = 2299; 2013–2024: ROR 5.20, n = 108,068), and in two periods in the Western Pacific (2010–2012: ROR 6.53, n = 81; 2013–2024: ROR 5.94, n = 1066). No signals were detected for oxycodone in Europe, and fentanyl did not meet signal criteria in any region or period in the primary analyses.ConclusionsOxycodone showed consistent safety signals in the Americas and Western Pacific. The absence of signals for fentanyl and in Europe may reflect under-reporting, highlighting the need for improved surveillance of illicit opioid use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40801-026-00550-0.

Key Points

This study found strong safety signals for oxycodone misuse and dependence across all predefined periods in the Americas and during the second and third periods in the Western Pacific.In contrast, fentanyl did not show consistent signals for abuse and dependence across regions, and no clear oxycodone signals were observed in Europe, which may reflect under-reporting, regional differences in reporting practices, and study design features (e.g., exclusion of overdose deaths).

Introduction

The opioid epidemic is no longer a regional issue, and poses a threat to global public health burden. United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) monitoring has documented the global expansion and diversification of synthetic opioids, underscoring the global significance of the opioid epidemic [1]. To date, three distinct waves have been described based on the clustering of opioid overdose deaths, driven by the uses of prescription oxycodone (the first wave), illicit heroin (the second wave) and synthetic opioid such as fentanyl (the third wave), respectively [2]. However, the timing of these waves may vary across countries (e.g., a delayed third wave in Canada) [3, 4]. In the United states (US), although state policies aimed at preventing opioid abuse have reduced abuse of prescription opioids, they have coincided with increased access to illicit opioids, highlighting the need for practical policies [5]. This coincided with shifts in the illicit supply, including increased availability of illicitly manufactured fentanyl and fentanyl-adulterated or counterfeit products. According to the US National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), opioid overdose deaths in 2022 were approximately 1.6 times higher than in 2019, and continue to rise with the illicit production of synthetic opioids such as fentanyl [6]. Additionally, the cost of opioid-related hospital admissions in the US was estimated to be US$11.8 billion in 2016 and US$12.9 billion in 2018; this estimate only accounts for hospital admissions, and the total costs associated with opioid use are likely much higher [7].

Opioid epidemics have been extensively documented in the regions outside of the Americas. A retrospective ecological study using multi-source databases of Europe reported a growing number of opioid-related harm across the European region, and the burden in the United Kingdom was comparable to that reported in the US [8]. According to the data from the International Narcotics Control Board, the national average per capita consumption of opioids in 2015–2017 was the highest in Germany, followed by Iceland, the US, Canada, and Austria [9]. In the Asia-Pacific region, opioid overdose rates remain lower compared with the US; however, with increasing dependency [10–12], there is a potential risk of rising opioid overdose deaths. The market for illicit opioids already exists, and restricting prescription opioids is likely to increase illicit access as people seek substitutes [13].

While it is clear that the epidemic is a global crisis, the trend or clustering of opioid abuse and dependence is likely to vary by region. However, generating comparative data on the regional level remains challenging due to limited access to relevant data in each region and heterogeneity in the data measurement and outcome reporting. A recent study attempted to overcome these limitations using a global pharmacovigilance database to identify regions with opioid abuse and dependence among 131 countries registered in the database; the study found the highest reported risk of drug abuse and dependence in Australia, Cananda, France, Germany, the United Kingdom and the US [14]. However, it still remains unknown whether the trends of epidemics vary by region. We selected oxycodone and fentanyl as the sentinel opioids representing prescription and synthetic opioids, respectively. In this regard, we compared temporal and regional reporting of opioid abuse and dependence-related adverse events (AEs) for oxycodone and fentanyl in VigiBase using standard disproportionality-based signal detection [15]. We aimed to evaluate whether regional reporting trends for oxycodone and fentanyl vary over time, reflecting the evolving opioid epidemic.

Methods

Date Source

VigiBase is the World Health Organization (WHO) global pharmacovigilance database managed by the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC), containing > 40 million individual case safety reports (ICSRs) submitted by national pharmacovigilance centers participating in the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring since 1967 [16]. Reporting volume varies over time and across countries because submissions depend on national systems, regulatory requirements, and pharmacovigilance capacity [16–18]. ICSRs are compiled through national processes from multiple channels (healthcare professionals, consumers, and marketing authorization holders) and include structured patient, report, drug, and AE information coded using MedDRA (version 27.1) and WHODrug [16]. UMC performs automated quality checks and provides guidance/caveats on key limitations (e.g., under-reporting, heterogeneity, and potential duplication), and data completeness/quality has been evaluated in methodological studies [17–19]. In this study, datasets were merged using “UMCReportID”.

This study was approved by Sungkyunkwan University Institutional Review Board (IRB No. SKKU 2025-05-051), and informed consent was waived.

Study Design and Data Extraction

We conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance study using VigiBase to compare AE patterns between waves of the opioid epidemic and to analyze regional differences in AEs. The extracted data were ICSRs and opioid–AE pairs recorded in VigiBase from January 1990 to December 2024. Oxycodone and fentanyl were selected as the primary drugs of interest because they serve as the sentinel opioids representing prescription and synthetic opioids, respectively, and have been central to shifts in opioid-related harms. Morphine and tramadol, commonly used opioid analgesics across multiple regions, were chosen as comparator drugs to provide a clinically relevant reference within the opioid class. We excluded hydromorphone and hydrocodone as Region of Americas accounted for > 90% of its use, methadone and buprenorphine were excluded as they are used to treat opioid use disorder, and codeine was excluded as it is also used to treat a wider indication in combination with other drugs. In addition, we excluded suspected duplicate reports (identified using the automated vigiMatch algorithm [20]), events with onset before drug administration, opioid–opioid drug pairs, overdose deaths, and cases where AEs were not reported (Fig. 1). “Opioid–opioid drug pairs” are opioid–AE pairs from reports listing more than one opioid as a suspected or concomitant drug, which could obscure attribution to a single opioid.

Study flow chart of analysis. ADRs adverse drug reactions, ICSRs individual case safety reports

We defined “drug abuse and dependence” as the AE of interest (primary outcome) because it directly reflects the core public health phenomenon underlying the opioid epidemic, non-medical use and development of dependence, and is well suited for comparing regional and temporal patterns in spontaneous reporting data [14]. We used the narrow Standardized MedDRA (Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities) Query (SMQ) to define this outcome [21] (Supplementary Table 1, see electronic supplementary material [ESM]). To assess severe opioid-related harms, we evaluated opioid-related death as a secondary outcome, defined as fatal reports (seriousness: death) listing an opioid as a suspected/interacting drug and coded to the MedDRA narrow SMQs “Acute central respiratory depression” or “Overdose” [14, 22] (Supplementary Table 1, ESM). Our analysis was carried out for segmented time periods aligned with commonly cited inflection points in North American overdose mortality. The opioid epidemic was divided into three periods as follows: (1) 1990–2009; (2) 2010–2012; (3) 2013–2024. Moreover, the analyses were conducted separately for three regions, which were the Americas, Western Pacific, and Europe. This study was conducted according to the READUS-PV guideline [23].

Statistical Analysis

Characteristics of the ICSRs of study opioids were summarized using frequencies and percentages. To identify opioid epidemic in the study data, we conducted a disproportionality analysis, which is a data mining technique widely used for signal detection in spontaneous reporting databases where exposure denominators are unavailable [15]. A safety signal reflects disproportionate reporting of a drug–event pair relative to a comparator and does not imply causality. We used these signals to examine whether opioid abuse reporting patterns vary over time and across regions.

To compare reporting signals, we calculated the reporting odds ratio (ROR) for “drug abuse and dependence” AEs for the drug of interest compared with the comparator within each wave and region [24]. The ROR was calculated as the odds of reporting the AE of interest for the drug of interest divided by the corresponding odds for the comparator; a ROR > 1 indicates higher reporting for the drug of interest. The chi-squared test was applied to the same 2 × 2 table to evaluate whether reporting differed between groups, and tables were reviewed to ensure the chi-squared approximation was appropriate. We defined a meaningful signal a priori using commonly applied disproportionality screening thresholds (report counts > 3, χ2 > 4 and ROR > 2) to reduce spurious findings from sparse data [25–27]. Morphine and tramadol were combined as an opioid-class comparator to provide a stable reference distribution. Safety signals for oxycodone and fentanyl were evaluated by periods and by regions, yielding nine disproportionality analyses per study drug (3 regions, 3 periods each). All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS (Release SAS 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R (R version 4.4.2), with forest plots generated using the R package forestplot.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

Among a total of 1,442,509 ICSRs and 5,211,369 opioid–AE pairs recorded in VigiBase between January 1990 and December 2024, a total of 1,201,241 ICSRs, and 3,281,812 opioid–AE pairs were analyzed in this study (Fig. 1). The most frequent exclusion in the study flow was opioid–opioid drug pairs (n = 173,036) (Fig. 1). The Americas accounted for the highest number of ICSRs with 575,500 reports (47.9%), followed by the Western Pacific with 408,602 (34.0%), and Europe with 179,984 (15.0%). Oxycodone accounted for 27.5% (n = 330,638) and fentanyl for 22.0% (n = 263,908) of the ICSRs. Among the ICSRs in this study, 57.4% (n = 689,608) were female and 53.1% (n = 636,769) were ≥45 years old. Most of the ICSRs were reported by healthcare professionals and spontaneous reports, accounting for 97.9% (n = 1,176,507) and 87.3% (n = 1,048,951) of the total, respectively (Table 1).

Reporting Trends of Opioids

Based on the predefined segmented time periods, ICSRs for oxycodone peaked in 2005 (n = 8395) during 1990–2010, in 2011 (n = 10,444) during 2010–2012, and in 2021 (n = 45,025) during 2013–2024. For fentanyl, peaks were observed during 2010–2012 and 2013–2024 periods, with the highest peak occurring in 2017 (n = 32,704) during 2013–2024 and a smaller peak in 2011 (n = 11,451) during 2010–2012 (Fig. 2). The reporting trends by region showed that ICSRs for oxycodone were more prevalent, compared with fentanyl, across the 2010–2012 and 2013–2024 periods in the Americas and Europe, whereas ICSRs of fentanyl predominated across all waves in the Western Pacific (Supplementary Fig. 1, ESM). Disproportionality Analysis by Regions and Periods

In the Americas, oxycodone showed “drug abuse and dependence” safety signals across all three periods (1990–2009: ROR 4.40, No. of cases (n) = 9102; 2010–2012: ROR 2.13, n = 2299; 2013–2024: ROR 5.20, n = 108,068). In the Western Pacific, oxycodone signals were observed in two periods (2010–2012: ROR 6.53, n = 81; 2013–2024: ROR 5.94, n = 1066). In Europe, oxycodone did not meet signal criteria in any period (1990–2009: ROR 1.72, n = 3; 2010–2012: ROR 1.24, n = 43; 2013–2024: ROR 0.63, n = 308) (Fig. 4). Fentanyl did not meet signal criteria across regions or periods (Fig. 4).

As a secondary outcome, opioid-related causes of death showed significant oxycodone signals in the Americas (1990–2009: ROR 2.62, n = 3564; 2013–2024: ROR 2.78, n = 12,861) and in the Western Pacific during 2013–2024 (ROR 2.85, n = 62) (Supplementary Table 2, ESM). No death-related signals were observed in Europe, and fentanyl did not meet signal criteria across regions or periods (Supplementary Table 2, ESM).

In subgroup analyses by age and sex, the overall patterns were largely consistent with the main findings (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, ESM). By age, fentanyl met signal criteria in the Americas during 2013–2024 for both ≥ 45 years (ROR 3.39, n = 6051) and < 45 years (ROR 3.02, n = 2155), and among those aged ≥ 45 years in the Western Pacific during 2010–2012 (ROR 2.03, n = 21) (Supplementary Table 3, ESM). In Europe, oxycodone met signal criteria during 1990–2009 among individuals aged < 45 years (ROR 2.21, n = 32) (Supplementary Table 3, ESM). By sex, findings in males and females were generally similar to the main results; however, among females in the Americas, oxycodone did not meet signal criteria during 2010–2012 (Supplementary Table 4, ESM).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first safety signal detection study examining multiple region-periods of the opioid epidemic. This study utilized VigiBase, the WHO’s global pharmacovigilance database based on voluntary AE reporting, to identify signals of opioid abuse and dependence from January 1990 to December 2024 through disproportionality analysis. We conducted a safety signal detection analysis for specific opioids across different regions—namely, the Americas, Europe, and the Western Pacific—during predefined periods. Notably, we found a strong safety signal for oxycodone across all periods in the Americas and during the second and third periods in the Western Pacific region. Supporting these findings, secondary outcome analyses for opioid-related death also showed significant oxycodone signals in the Americas and the Western Pacific, suggesting that periods/regions with elevated misuse- and dependence-related reporting also showed disproportionate reporting of fatal events consistent with acute opioid toxicity. Because VigiBase is a spontaneous reporting database, these findings indicate reporting patterns rather than incidence or causality. In contrast, fentanyl did not show consistent safety signals across regions in the primary analysis of the overall population. Moreover, no clear safety signals were observed for oxycodone or fentanyl in Europe.

According to CDC reports, opioid overdose death in the US—including those from prescription opioids, heroin, and synthetic opioids like fentanyl—increased from 21,089 cases in 2010 to 81,806 cases in 2022, and continues to rise [6]. A previous FAERS-based study found fentanyl (42%) and oxycodone (35%) to be the most reported opioids, with fentanyl-related reports peaking in 2017 and oxycodone in 2013, consistent with our findings [28]. A VigiBase-based study also identified the US and Canada as the most strongly associated with opioid abuse, with oxycodone and fentanyl accounting for most AEs [14]. While these studies primarily described absolute reporting volume or overall frequency, our analysis further compared abuse and dependence reporting for specific opioids against an opioid-class comparator across regions and periods, highlighting relative disproportionality signals. Opioid consumption in North America nearly doubled between 2001 and 2013, accounting for more than half of global use, whereas increases in low-income regions were minimal [29]. Although consumption declined slightly between 2015 and 2019, North America remained the highest-consuming region [30]. These findings support our observation that oxycodone produced consistent safety signals across all predefined periods in North America. In contrast, the absence of a safety signal for fentanyl may reflect the study’s exclusion of overdose deaths and the underreporting of illicit opioid-related AEs, which may also contribute to the lack of fentanyl signals for death-related terms.

Although opioid use may be less widespread in the Western Pacific compared with the Americas, our analysis supports concerns about a potential opioid epidemic in this region. In Asia, opioid analgesic consumption increased steadily from 5.87 to 7.47 milligram morphine equivalent per 1000 inhabitants per day (MME per 1000/day) between 2015 and 2019, with national healthcare systems, regulations, cultural attitudes, and cancer mortality influencing usage patterns [30]. In Australia, about 16% of the population uses opioids annually, with rising prescriptions for strong opioids among non-cancer patients [31]. Australia was also identified as a high-risk country for the inflow of illegally manufactured potent synthetic opioids, with projections estimating 1500–5700 deaths over 5 years if an outbreak occurs [13]. Several Southeast Asian countries have shown a significant rise in strong opioid use over the past decade, particularly for fentanyl and oxycodone [32], and increasing prescription rates in South Korea call for proactive monitoring [10, 33]. Taken together, these prior studies suggest that even in the Western Pacific region, strong opioids such as oxycodone and fentanyl may play an increasing role in the Western Pacific through prescribed use and potentially through illicit channels. Consistent with this concern, our study detected oxycodone abuse and dependence signals in the Western Pacific during the second and third periods, underscoring the need for strengthened prescribing oversight and pharmacovigilance-based monitoring in the region. Moreover, oxycodone also showed a death-related term signal in the Western Pacific in 2013–2024.

The risks associated with the opioid epidemic have been well documented in Europe. While prescription opioid use varies across countries, overall trends show an increase. Scotland and Ireland have experienced the most severe opioid-related harms, with Scotland recording higher opioid overdose mortality rates than the US between 2010 and 2018 [8]. Unlike the US, Europe has seen lower rates of prescription opioid abuse, with a more localized crisis. A cross-national study (2015–2017) identified Germany, Iceland, the US, and Canada as having the highest per capita opioid consumption, with about half of the top 21 opioid-consuming countries located in Europe [9]. In the Netherlands, the number of opioid prescriptions nearly doubled between 2008 and 2017, with oxycodone prescriptions increasing four-fold [34]. In Germany, opioid prescriptions steadily rose, especially among chronic non-cancer pain patients, with oxycodone and fentanyl most commonly prescribed [35]. France also reported a marked rise in strong opioid use, alongside increases in opioid-related hospitalizations and deaths mainly linked to oxycodone and fentanyl [36, 37]. However, heroin remains the dominant illicit opioid in Europe, unlike North America. According to the European Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction, heroin continues to account for the majority of opioid treatment admissions and fatal overdoses, although synthetic opioids like fentanyl have become more prevalent in some countries [38, 39]. The absence of a safety signal in Europe in our study may reflect the predominance of heroin-related harms in this region rather than an absence of opioid-related risk. Similarly, no clear death-related term signals were observed in Europe in the secondary analyses. While Europe has not experienced an opioid epidemic comparable to that of North America, rising concerns about synthetic opioid inflow and increasing non-medical opioid use highlight the need for continued surveillance.

The previous opioid epidemics in the US presented in three waves: prescription opioids, heroin, and then fentanyl [2]. More recently, a “fourth wave” has been proposed in the literature, characterized by increasing co-use of fentanyl with stimulants such as cocaine and methamphetamine [40]. Deaths involving both substances have risen markedly over the past decade, and patterns of stimulant involvement appear to vary by region within the US [40, 41]. This co-use may be partly driven by supply factors, such as fentanyl being mixed into other drugs, and may be more common in populations with limited access to healthcare and greater social vulnerability [42, 43]. Given rising stimulant-involved harms and limited treatment options, continued monitoring is needed, and future studies could examine whether reporting ratios for stimulants show emerging signals in recent years.

Several limitations of our study warrant cautious interpretation of the findings. First, as it relies on spontaneous reporting data, there is potential for under-reporting and reporting bias, limiting causal inference. Second, our results may differ slightly from previous reports that include fatal outcomes because overdose deaths were not included as AEs of interest; moreover, illicit opioid-related events may be particularly under-reported. Third, because morphine and tramadol were used as an opioid-class comparator and their use and reporting may also have increased over time, the resulting reporting ratios could have been attenuated, potentially diluting signals for the study opioids. Fourth, incomplete patient data, including age and sex, may introduce selection bias and limit subgroup analyses. Fifth, as the VigiBase extract was provided at the regional level, we were unable to examine country-level heterogeneity, and variation in prescribing practices, regulatory environments, and healthcare systems may have affected reporting patterns. Sixth, the focus on only two opioids excludes the broader impact of other opioids and does not capture emerging signals associated with the fourth wave, which should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

This study, using extensive pharmacovigilance data, found that signals of oxycodone abuse and dependence were detected across three periods in the Americas and two periods in the Western Pacific. Notably, the strong signals for oxycodone abuse and dependence observed in the Western Pacific suggest a significant potential risk for an emerging opioid epidemic in this area. In contrast, the lack of robust fentanyl-related signals may indicate underreporting of illicit opioid misuse, underscoring the urgent need for further research and targeted interventions addressing illicit opioid use.
