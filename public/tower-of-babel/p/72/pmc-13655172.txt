A chromosome-level genome assembly of SAGS Anisodus tanguticus (Maxim.) Pascher (Solanaceae) from the Tibetan region of Sichuan, China
Authors: Gang Liu, Dan Su, Yutong Liu, Zhan Shi, Shenzhong Gao, Xin He, Shiyin Zhang, Heng Deng, Jingjing Xiao, Yinan Yao, Peng Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anisodus tanguticus (Maxim.) Pascher (A. tanguticus), a cold-tolerant perennial herb in the Solanaceae family, is distributed across China’s Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and extends to Nepal, Bhutan, Sikkim, and India. As a Tibetan medicinal plant, it is used to treat pain, ulcers, etc.; its roots yield antispasmodic and anesthetic compounds, and other parts are used as a feed additive for yaks to enhance cold resistance in northwest Sichuan. In this study, samples were collected from Seda County (northwestern Sichuan, China) for sequencing. Using PacBio HiFi sequencing and Hi-C scaffolding, a high-quality chromosome-scale genome assembly was generated, with a genome size of 1599.64 Mb, a scaffold N50 of 62.01 Mb, and a contig N50 of 38.51 Mb. A total of 24 superscaffolds (93.65% of the genome) were anchored to 24 chromosomes. Compared with previously reported assemblies of A. tanguticus and A. acutangulus, this assembly shows improved scaffold length and completeness. Genome annotation identified 64.95% repetitive elements and 45,930 protein-coding genes, and comparative analysis of four Anisodus genomes revealed conserved patterns of gene density, GC content, LTR, and LINE elements. This study provides the first high-quality chromosome-scale genome resource of A. tanguticus from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, supporting studies on phylogeny, genetic diversity, and breeding, as well as further exploration of its genomic basis of high-altitude adaptation.

Background & Summary

Anisodus tanguticus (Maxim.) Pascher (A. tanguticus) is a cold-tolerant perennial herb of the Solanaceae family, distributed across the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau in China (including Tibet, Qinghai, and northwestern Sichuan) and extending to Nepal, Bhutan, Sikkim, and northeastern India. In China, it is a traditional medicinal plant known as ‘Tangchong Nabao’ in Tibetan medicine, historically used to treat pain, ulcers, colitis, gallstones, and trauma. Beyond its traditional medicinal use, the roots of A. tanguticus serve as a crucial raw material for extracting antispasmodic, anesthetic, and bronchodilator compounds, generating significant economic impact. In the northwest Sichuan Plateau region, other parts of the plant are collected and dried by local Tibetan communities for use as feed additives for yaks to protect against winter cold.

Here, we report the first high-quality chromosome-scale genome assembly of A. tanguticus from the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, with samples collected from Seda County (northwestern Sichuan, China) at elevations above 4,000 m by the Sichuan Academy of Grassland Sciences (SAGS) (Fig. 1). The genome assembly was generated using PacBio HiFi sequencing and chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) anchoring, resulting in a genome of approximately 1599.64 Mb, with scaffold and contig N50 values of 62.01 Mb and 38.51 Mb, respectively. Contigs were scaffolded into 24 superscaffolds, accounting for 93.65% of the total genome size (Table 1). As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the Hi-C heatmap reveals 24 individual superscaffolds, aligning perfectly with the 24 chromosomes of the SAGS assembly. This SAGS assembly shows improved scaffold length and completeness (99.20% BUSCO) compared with previously published A. tanguticus assemblies and the available A. acutangulus assembly. Genome annotation identified 64.95% repetitive elements and 45,930 protein-coding genes, consistent with other A. tanguticus genomes and supporting the quality of this assembly (Table 1). Moreover, a comparative Circos plot of four Anisodus genomes reveals conserved patterns in gene density, GC content, and LTR/LINE density, highlighting a shared genomic architecture (Fig. 2b). Methods

Plant materials

Fresh young roots, stems, and leaves of Anisodus tanguticus were collected from the medicinal plant cultivation base in Seda County, Sichuan Province, China, a region characterized by a continental highland monsoon climate with an annual mean temperature of −0.16 °C and an average elevation exceeding 4,000 m (Fig. 1), for whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing.

Library construction and sequencing

High-quality genomic DNA was isolated to construct a SMRTbell library with an average insert size of 15-20 kb. This library was sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA)1, yielding 91.76 Gb of raw HiFi data. Additionally, a Hi-C library was constructed and sequenced with paired-end 150 bp reads (PE150) reads on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating 173.54 Gb of raw data. Concurrently, whole-genome short-read sequencing (PE150) and transcriptome sequencing were performed using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI, Shenzhen, China), which supports high-throughput sequencing with reliable data quality. For whole-genome sequencing, 79.48 Gb of raw data were generated. Genome assembly

For genome preliminary assembly, raw polymerase reads were first subjected to control using the PacBio SMRT Analysis software package (https://www.pacb.com), which removes reads shorter than 50 bp, those with quality values below 0.8, and adapter-contaminated reads. Subsequently, Circular Consensus Sequencing (CCS) reads (HiFi reads) are generated from the filtered data using SMRT Link v9.0 (parameters:–min-passes = 3–min-rq = 0.99), ensuring ≥ 99% base-call accuracy (Phred score ≥ 30). Finally, these HiFi reads were assembled into primary contigs using Hifiasm (v0.16.1; https://github.com/chhylp123/hifiasm)2, which performs all-versus-all read overlap alignment, iterative error correction, and string graph construction to resolve haplotypes and repetitive regions while prioritizing contiguous primary assembly output.

Hi-C assisted scaffolding

Preliminary quality control of Hi-C data was performed using fastp (v0.20.1) with default parameters to remove low-quality reads3, generating high-quality clean reads for downstream analysis. Subsequently, HiCUP (v0.8) was employed to extract valid pairs reads4, representing valid chromatin interaction pairs, which were then used for scaffolding with ALLHiC (v.0.9.14)5. Following ALLHiC scaffolding, large-scale structural variations (e.g., inversions and translocations) were manually corrected through visual inspection of chromatin interaction heatmaps using Juicebox (v2.1543), yielding the final chromosome-level genome assembly6,7. Following the adjustment of chromosome coordinates, the Hi-C contact matrix was visualized using HiC-Pro8 and HiCExplorer (v3.7.2)9 (Fig. 2a).

Sequence consistency assessment

To assess the accuracy of genome assembly, the whole-genome short-read data were aligned to the assembled genome using BWA software10. The alignment rate, genome coverage, and sequencing depth were calculated to evaluate assembly completeness and sequencing uniformity.

Repetitive element annotation

Repeat annotation was performed using a combination of homology-based and de novo approaches. (1) Homology-based identification: Sequences were aligned against the curated RepBase database using RepeatMasker and RepeatProteinMask to detect interspersed repeats homologous to known repetitive elements11. (2) De novo prediction: A genome-specific repeat library was constructed de novo using LTR_FINDER (for LTR retrotransposons)12, Piler13, RepeatScout14, and RepeatModeler. This custom library was subsequently used by RepeatMasker to identify and classify repetitive elements. (3) Tandem repeat detection: Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) was applied independently to identify simple sequence repeats and tandem arrays15.

In total, 64.95% of the assembled genome was identified as repetitive DNA sequences. The most abundant repetitive elements were retrotransposons, among which long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons accounted for 50.44%, with Copia (28.56%) and Gypsy (16.49%) elements being dominant. The DNA transposons represented 3.67% of the genome (Table 1).

Protein-coding gene prediction

Protein-coding genes were comprehensively annotated through an integrated approach combining de novo prediction, homology-based searches, and transcriptome evidence. EvidenceModeler (v1.1.1)16 was employed to generate a consensus gene set by weighted integration of three annotation sources: (1) Homology-based prediction: utilizing TblastN (v2.2.26)17 for initial alignment and GeneWise (v2.4.1)18 for precise spliced alignment, leveraging cross-species protein homology. (2) De novo prediction: five ab initio tools were executed. Augustus (v3.2.3), Geneid (v1.4), Genescan (v1.0), GlimmerHMM (v3.0.4), and SNAP (v2013.11.29)19–23, each trained on taxon-specific parameters to minimize false positives. (3) Transcriptome-derived evidence: RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.4)24, followed by transcript assembly via Trinity (v2.1.1) and Cufflinks (v.2.2.1)25,26. The final consensus gene set was generated using EvidenceModeler (v1.1.1) by integrating gene predictions from the three evidence sources. The average transcript length and coding sequence length were 5,762.73 and 1,109.07 bp, respectively, with an average of 5.09 exons per gene. The average exon and intron lengths were 225.06 and 1,184.79 bp, respectively. The number of predicted genes in SAGS genome in this study was comparable to those in three previously published genomes, including two Anisodus tanguticus genomes from Lanzhou University (LZU; 46,606 genes) and the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (KIB; 44,252 genes), as well as the Anisodus acutangulus genome from Lanzhou University (LZU; 38,211 genes) (Table 1).

Gene function annotation

To assign biological functions, predicted protein-coding genes were aligned against several comprehensive databases, including NCBI non-redundant protein (NR), Swiss-Prot, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), InterPro, Gene Ontology (GO), and protein families (Pfam)27–31. InterProScan (v5.27.66) was used to search publicly available databases (ProDom, PRINTS, Pfam, SMART, PANTHER, and PROSITE) to annotate protein motifs and domains32–38. A total of 44515 genes (96.90% of the predicted protein-coding genes) were annotated using these databases. Non-coding RNA annotation

Transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were predicted using tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.7) with default parameters, leveraging its enhanced covariance models for high-specificity detection39. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences were identified via BLASTN searches (E-value threshold < 1e-5) against conserved plant rRNA references, utilizing sequence homology-based approaches. For the annotation of other non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs) and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), Infernal (v1.1.2) was employed to scan the Rfam database (release 14.1)40,41, applying covariance models for structural alignment42. Data Records

The raw sequence data and assembled genome sequences generated in this study have been deposited in public repositories. The raw sequence data are publicly accessible at the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) in the NGDC under accession number CRA02694643, as well as the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number SRP69653244. The assembled genome sequences have been deposited in the Genome Warehouse (GWH) under accession number GWHGGGA00000000.145 and in the NCBI GenBank database under accession number JBXYDY01000000046. Additionally, the chromosome length statistics, genome assemble statistics, base content statistics, BUSCO assessment, GEGMA assessment, Illumina reads coverage, single nucleotide polymorphisms, repetitive elements, gene function annotations, ncRNA prediction, gene families, GO term for genes and KEGG term for genes have been deposited at the Figshare database with https://figshare.com/s/12bee600d2489080a20c47.

Technical Validation

The completeness of the final assembled genome was assessed using BUSCO (v4.0.5) software by searching plant-specific databases that contain a total of 1,614 orthologous single-copy genes48,49. The results revealed 99.2% of the BUSCO genes were identified as complete, of which 35.3% were duplicated. Only 0.40% of BUSCO genes were fragmented, and 0.40% were missing from the genome. Following chromosome-scale scaffolding using Hi-C data, we anchored 93.65% of the genome assembly onto 24 chromosomes. These results indicated high genome assembly completeness. CEGMA (v2.0) software employs 248 most highly conserved core eukaryotic genes (CEGs) to assess the extent of comprehensive gene coverage50. The genome assembly showed a high completeness level, with 97.98% (243) of CEGs completely and partially covered, including 95.97% (238) of CEGs considered complete. The filtered short Illumina reads were aligned back to the genome assembly using BWA (v0.7.12-r1039). Approximately 99.66% of the short reads mapped to the genome, and the ratios of heterozygous and homozygous single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were 0.23% and 3.02e-05%, respectively, indicating that the assembly had high single-base-level accuracy.
