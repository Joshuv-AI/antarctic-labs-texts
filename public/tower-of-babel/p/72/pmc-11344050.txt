PathoTracker: an online analytical metagenomic platform for Klebsiella pneumoniae feature identification and outbreak alerting
Authors: Shuyi Wang, Shijun Sun, Qi Wang, Hongbin Chen, Yifan Guo, Meng Cai, Yuyao Yin, Shuai Ma, Hui Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Clinical metagenomics (CMg) Nanopore sequencing can facilitate infectious disease diagnosis. In China, sub-lineages ST11-KL64 and ST11-KL47 Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) are widely prevalent. We propose PathoTracker, a specially compiled database and arranged method for strain feature identification in CMg samples and CRKP traceability. A database targeting high-prevalence horizontal gene transfer in CRKP strains and a ST11-only database for distinguishing two sub-lineages in China were created. To make the database user-friendly, facilitate immediate downstream strain feature identification from raw Nanopore metagenomic data, and avoid the need for phylogenetic analysis from scratch, we developed data analysis methods. The methods included pre-performed phylogenetic analysis, gene-isolate-cluster index and multilevel pan-genome database and reduced storage space by 10-fold and random-access memory by 52-fold compared with normal methods. PathoTracker can provide accurate and fast strain-level analysis for CMg data after 1 h Nanopore sequencing, allowing early warning of outbreaks. A user-friendly page (http://PathoTracker.pku.edu.cn/) was developed to facilitate online analysis, including strain-level feature, species identifications and phylogenetic analyses. PathoTracker proposed in this study will aid in the downstream analysis of CMg.

Introduction

Clinical metagenomics (CMg)1 is a promising tool that facilitates the rapid identification of a broad spectrum of pathogens in clinical samples1–5. Timely treatment with targeting antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is essential for satisfactory outcomes5. Nanopore sequencing enables real-time analysis and species identification6 after 1 h sequencing in CMg5,7. Currently, assessing accurate AMR requires culture isolation of bacterial pathogens followed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), which requires several days. Detecting AMR while identifying a species will shorten the process duration. The time required for the entire CMg is only 6.5–7.0 h, including nucleic acid extraction (2 h, dual processes), library preparation (3.5–4.0 h, dual processes), and sequencing and analysis (∼1 h)8. Currently, AMR monitoring in CMg is conducted by detecting specific antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), such as blaKPC-29–11, or enriching low-abundant ARGs using wet experiments12–14. However, not all ARGs can be detected after 2 h9, 5 h10, or even 6 h11 of Nanopore sequencing. To date, no comprehensive study has focused solely on the analysis of dry experimental components.

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) is a major public health challenge in China associated with high mortality. The most prevalent sequence type (ST) in clinical CRKP in China is ST11, in which 47 and 64 are the two most prevalent serotypes (KL)15 with different pathogenicity16. Moreover, plasmids enable CRKP horizontal gene transfer and become variable17. Outbreaks often lead to an epidemic of the same type of strain over an extended period, especially for nosocomial CRKP infections that, owing to their inherent selective advantage, pose serious public health threats if not promptly detected18.

Currently, for a single newly sequenced K. pneumoniae isolate, only its ST, KL, AMR and virulence genes can be determined. Determining its origin and whether it is part of an outbreak requires empirical evolutionary distance assessment of multiple isolates9,19,20; however, collecting and comparing a certain number of strains is time-consuming21,22. The CMg Nanopore data are low-abundant and inaccurate; therefore, they cannot directly determine the genomic features of the strain and are unsuitable for evolutionary analysis. However, they enable real-time data analysis. We aimed to provide early warning of possible outbreaks at the early stages of the outbreak; at this time, no isolation is necessary, and the already sequenced CMg data can be used for downstream analysis.

To this end, we searched PubMed using the terms “metagenomics Klebsiella pneumoniae outbreak” for articles published up to 27 February 2024 with no language restrictions. We identified 42 articles, none of which provided early outbreak detection, whereas strain traceability and antibiotic sensitivity were obtained using short-time Nanopore sequencing. To target common strains in China, particularly those that are prevalent, high-risk ST11 CRKP strains, we developed a multilevel pan-genomic database. A novel, integrated, and convenient data analysis workflow was developed based on the database, enabling precise characterisation of targeted pathogens, accurate identification, and traceability of outbreaks with extremely low data volumes. Our proposed database will aid in driving precision medication and treatment for early-stage infections and prevent nosocomial infections.

Each sequence to be tested was first compared with every gene in the pan-genome. By summarizing the comparison results, the closest cluster to the sequence was first identified. Subsequently, analysis was performed within the cluster to select and obtain the isolates most closely related to the sequence being tested (Fig. 1b).

We included 100 bootstrap sampling tests (Supplementary Data 3a and 4) for performance evaluation. The correct rate for 100 bootstrap sampling tests to detect original strains was 93% (93/100, Supplementary Data 4). We also included Nanopore sequencing data from 29 CMg samples from a previous study8 for validation, to assess the ability of PathoTracker to obtain the genetic features of CMg data, such as ST and KL types (Figs. 1c and 3a). To verify the reliability of our results, we performed pure culture on the 29 CMg samples, and conducted next-generation sequencing (NGS) on the obtained K. pneumoniae strains to obtain the actual genomic information of strains for the purpose of validating the results of CMg data. These 29 newly sequenced strains were also used for validation. Except for the lack of PathoTracker database diversity (ST-KL) and samples with very few reads ( ≤ 20, lower quartile [Q1] = 20 reads), most CMg samples (13/15) correctly detected ST or ST-KL types. CMg Nanopore sequencing data cannot directly discriminate strain features

We extracted K. pneumoniae reads from the raw CMg data after 1 h Nanopore sequencing without any treatment due to long reads. One read was considered one contig for ST-KL typing. However, conventional methods, such as Kleborate, cannot infer basic genomic information directly from reads, despite the number of reads reaching 38,550 for BAL023 (Supplementary Data 8).

Twelve of 29 K. pneumoniae strains carried blaNDM-1 or blaKPC-2; however, only one strain was detected (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Data 5 and 9). Twelve strains carried the virulence plasmid, while seven strains were not detected (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Data 5 and 10). Therefore, the low volume of data did not allow for the simultaneous determination of strain features and species identification. Thus, we proposed the construction of a pan-genomic database containing all genes. In this context, each read of the low-volume data is crucial and exhibits a perfect match for the metagenomic characteristics.

The accuracy and running time of PathoTracker

We compared FastANI25 with PathoTracker (Fig. 3a). The FastANI result for BAL115 (11 reads) failed to detect ST11, while PathoTracker did. On the contrary, FastANI detected the KL and ST types for BAL437 (C8785) and SPU320 (four reads), respectively, which were not identified by PathoTracker. BAL270 (eight reads, of which PathoTracker detected one correct ST type) and SPU001 (two reads, of which PathoTracker provided no correct results) did not obtain FastANI results due to the low number of reads.

FastANI identified ST147 instead of ST273 for C8784 and C8786 (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Data 11), whereas PathoTracker detected ST273, the only ST273 isolate in the database (Supplementary Data 6). This discrepancy is possibly attributed to the MLST housekeeping genes of ST273 and ST147 differing by only one gene (Supplementary Data 12a); FastANI may highlight the importance of similar sequences in the calculation. The pan-genome included in this study distinguished strains with similar ST types because it retained all the genetic diversity of the isolates in the database.

PathoTracker’s required time was positively correlated with the “Total isolate number in select cluster” (R2 = 0.45) and the amount of sequencing data (R2 = 0.36) but not with other factors (Figs. S2 and S3). FastANI was also affected by the size of the data volume (Fig. S3). The slope of the BLAST combined with the time taken to select the cluster fitting curve was approximately half that of FastANI (Fig. S3a). However, within the cluster, the time required to select isolates was significantly influenced by the number of isolates contained in that cluster. The increase in the number of strains within a cluster extended the runtime (Fig. S3e), thus notably influencing C8783 and C8775, presented in Table 1, which were influenced by clusters 41 and 43 containing 112 and 103 isolates, respectively (Supplementary Data 3a and 13). Consequently, the average time required by PathoTracker in CMg validation (16.54 min) exceeded that required by FastANI (15.68 min) (Fig. 3b, p = 0.7782). PathoTracker minimises storage and Random-access memory (RAM)

FASTA files of 1187 all-type isolates and 425 China ST11 isolates included in this study occupied 6602.5 and 2405.9 M storage, respectively. The storage occupied by the pangenome of all clusters after processing was 562 and 229.8 M (Supplementary Data 3a, b), resulting in 11.75- and 10.47-times reduction in data volume, respectively. The number of isolates within a cluster showed a logarithmic relationship with the size of the pan-genome of that cluster (Fig. S4).

As the logarithmic trend line tends to level off, it is foreseeable that the storage size of the PathoTracker database would not significantly increase with the incorporation of more isolates into the database. The number and size of isolates compared with the sequences to be tested determined the RAM required for FastANI. Even for the CMg sample, BAL270, which had only eight reads (total size: 12 k bp), the RAM was 12.8 G. In contrast, the maximum RAM required to run the PathoTracker was < 0.25 G for both cultured isolates and CMg sample sequencing data (except for BAL023, 38,550 reads, 1.1 G), which was a 52 times reduction compared with FastANI (Table 1).

PathoTracker enables alerting of nosocomial infection outbreaks

C8784 and C8786, C8768 and C10301, as well as C8775, C10298 and C10300 were possibly derived from three distinct strains from the ST11 PathoTracker results (Supplementary Data 6 and 14). C8784 and C8786 (ANI > 99.99) were isolated from two different patients in the same hospital department within a 2-month interval. C8768 and C10301 were isolated from the same hospital department at 10-month intervals (ANI > 99.93). The C8775, C10298 and C10300, isolated from different hospitals and different departments (ANI > 99.97), were different from C8778 (ANI ≤ 99.89), the remaining ST11-KL64 strain in the validation set with virulence score > 4. This suggests the occurrence of homologous infections within a certain timeframe; the evolutionary relationships and early outbreak warnings for these strains can be better understood when only two strains are available, especially when ANIs were high in the same species.

A typical nosocomial outbreak with five strains previously sequenced by NGS and reported in Hangzhou, China, not included in this study’s database, verified that PathoTracker could accurately monitor nosocomial outbreaks26 (Supplementary Data 15a). The top five isolates were identical and isolated from Hangzhou in 2016. Other results were collected mostly from Hangzhou, indicating the transmission of the same clone in Hangzhou at that time. Another previous study was used to validate PathoTracker (Supplementary Data 15b)27, and seven strains previously sequenced by NGS from three hospitals were tested for outbreak surveillance. The aligned and different PathoTracker results reflect the variation in strain transmission and the difference between CRKP and hypervirulent CRKP.

PathoTracker can accurately identify AMR and virulence genes from CMg samples after 1-h Nanopore sequencing data

PathoTracker enabled ARG determination even without reads in the CMg samples. The AST results for meropenem (MEM) and imipenem (IMP) indicated the same resistant phenotypes (Supplementary Data 5). Except for database absence and a low read count, only three samples with virulence genes and two samples with ARG could not yield correct results using PathoTracker after 1 h Nanopore sequencing (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Data 16). AMR and virulence genes were not identified even after 72-h sequencing for some samples; therefore, extending the sequencing time beyond 1 h is unnecessary.

Extending the sequencing time does not aid in identity-addressing

PathoTracker accurately reported the ST for BAL270 and BAL437 with eight K. pneumoniae reads (Fig. 3a). However, BAL441 (four reads), SPU001 (two reads), and SPU320 (four reads) had very few reads to report the corresponding ST. While BAL270 and BAL354 had more ST-type hits with 72 h than data after 1 h Nanopore sequencing, other samples with lower read counts did not qualitatively improve after 72 h sequencing (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Data 18), while most had fewer than 50 reads, which were not sufficiently specific to detect the strain features.

Therefore, due to metagenomic uniqueness, specific DNA fragments are determined using wet experiments, and increasing the sequencing time does not necessarily improve the subsequent accuracy.

PathoTracker correctly identifies the closest relative of the missing types in the database

The PathoTracker results for BAL124/C8777 were ST983, ST380, and ST584 (Supplementary Data 6), and the housekeeping genes were similar to ST667 (Supplementary Data 12c). C8784 and C8786 were correctly grouped into the cluster where ST273 was located but were closer to ST147 and ST392 than ST273 (Supplementary Data 6) because ST273, ST147, and ST392 share similar housekeeping genes (four identical, Supplementary Data 7 and 12a). The C8772/SPU328 results were ST678 and ST2268, as they had three housekeeping genes identical to ST1073, which was not included in the database (Supplementary Data 12d). This shows that if the correct ST is absent from the database, PathoTracker will identify its closest relative.

PathoTracker reflects the evolutionary relationship of isolates

Clusters were automatically divided based on phylogenetic distances. Seven housekeeping genes in the same or neighbouring clusters were almost identical (Supplementary Data 19), indicating that isolates with similar housekeeping genes also had similar genomes. The isoforms of housekeeping genes changed in order as the clusters changed, and the evolution of isolates with the same ST varied based on the KL type (Supplementary Data 20). Cluster 1 contained isolates that were distant from others in the database (35 STs; Supplementary Data 20a).

Discussion

De novo evolution and outbreak adjudication for newly sequenced samples are time-consuming and computationally resource-intensive21,25,28. The varying scale of different databases divides the manual division of clusters in the phylogenetic tree. PathoTracker substitutes for starting from the beginning each time using an automatically pre-made phylogenetic analysis while reducing storage and RAM usage.

Given the complexity of genetic diversity, the large genome size of each K. pneumoniae (~5.5 M29), and less accurate Nanopore sequencing data, we developed a method that reduced the amount of data and conserved the genome information by constructing a multilevel de-duplicated pan-genome database. The pan-genome covers all genes and can be used to analyse CMg data, which are typically insufficient and incomplete. The amount of pan-genome data logarithmically, not exponentially, increase with the number of isolates30, demonstrating the storage savings advantage of PathoTracker. Furthermore, strains with long-read sequencing data in the database facilitated the successful detection of elements difficult to assemble using NGS data, such as IS.

Dividing clusters affects the PathoTracker results because the correct cluster was selected upon comparison based on the pan-genome of each cluster. As the selected BLAST comparison required less time, we increased the number of clusters and decreased the number of isolates per cluster in the building pipeline. A total of 93 and 41 clusters were divided in this study, higher than those in previous studies, where clusters were single- or double-digit31,32.

The tree constructed using Bacterial 120 (bac120) by default parameters33 (Fig.S5) and the hierarchical clustering (complete, mcquitty, average, median, central, and ward) for ANI distances by FastANI25 (Fig. S6) were worse than the strict phylogenetic analysis. Some KL47 and KL64 strains remained undifferentiated, potentially affecting downstream analyses. Therefore, although these two tools are faster than constructing a core genome phylogenetic tree, they were not selected. Sequences generated through Nanopore sequencing were considered already assembled due to their lengthy nature (average N50 > 3.3 k34, Supplementary Data 8), which reduced the time required for assembly; however, they cannot be directly used to discriminate strain features, such as ST typing determined via seven housekeeping genes35; nevertheless, PathoTracker enabled the determination of the exact identity. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 23 antimicrobials against all isolates provided effective guidance for treatment strategies. For carbapenemase-resistant strains, treatment options for infections include colistin, tigecycline (TGC), and ceftazidime/avibactam24,36. PathoTracker AST results are reliable and can be used to guide the clinical use of CRKP. All TGC AST results for CRKP were non-resistant and all MIC values of TGC reported by PathoTracker were sensitive, except for the neighbouring strains from the public database with no antimicrobial sensitivity information from SPU298 (Supplementary Data 5 and 6). Moreover, CRKP and hypervirulent CRKP could be effectively distinguished.

Due to the diverse genomic variations of K. pneumoniae, a universal definition of an outbreak based on Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) is lacking. SNPs of ≤ 426, ≤ 1027,37, and ≤ 1338 have been suggested as cut-offs for identifying outbreaks; ≤ 100 indicates possible strains from the same ancestor27. In this study, strains from the same outbreak event exhibited SNPs of ≤ 10, > 10, and some > 100 between each other (Supplementary Data 15c). Therefore, selecting a fully quantifiable indicator to alert and determine whether an outbreak has begun or whether strains originated from the same ancestor is challenging with only few CMg samples, and different thresholds and data sources may produce varying results39. PathoTracker reduces the bias of false negatives and misdirection by the top 5 or 10 selections in multiple steps, makes thresholds flexible, and overcomes the disadvantages of CMg sample data. In addition, PathoTracker separately constructed a new database specifically targeting the widespread prevalence of ST11 CRKP in China, successfully identifying potential past outbreaks comparable with the FastANI results (Supplementary Data 21).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first user-friendly and accurate online outbreak alerting analysis tool in China for insufficient data volume, with significant potential application to common clinical pathogens, such as Staphylococcus aureus and Acinetobacter baumannii. To accurately identify novel strains that gain or lose new virulence genes or ARGs through horizontal transmission under antibiotic pressure, PathoTracker incorporates the ResFinder and VFDB databases.

This study has some limitations. One limitation is the absence of diverse clinically complex isolates and the non-convergent curve of the pan-genome for the database, which must be expanded. Also, in this study, we did not create a universal standard for automatic classification of evolutionary tree clusters and manually selected classification thresholds based on the needs of specific data sets. This step must be optimised in the future. In addition, excessive variation among isolates within a cluster caused errors in cluster selection, as seen in the bootstrap sampling test, where Cluster 1’s isolate was not successfully detected. Furthermore, the low number of Nanopore reads complicated the assessment of CMg samples, which is an inherent error in metagenomics; hence, selecting the best criteria was difficult. Therefore, more analysis is warranted.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the ability of PathoTracker to provide an online service that enables strain identification via CMg using Nanopore sequencing. This study relied on the CRE-Network and incorporated ST11-CRKP isolates from various regions of China to facilitate the timely identification of outbreak emergence alerting, evolutionary processes, and traceability relationships. PathoTracker reduced the complexity and analysis time of AMR genes processed in wet experiments and minimised RAM and storage usage, showing promise for the clinical diagnosis of nosocomial hospital infections.

Materials and methods

Composition and construction of the database

We constructed PathoTracker, a K. pneumoniae database, for CMg samples to facilitate stain-level feature analysis. A total of 341 K. pneumoniae from the China CRE-Network research23,24 were sequenced using 150 bp paired-end Illumina NovaSeq and PacBio RS II or Nanopore and included in this database. The reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.3940 with “ILLUMINACLIP: TruSeq3-SE:2:30:10, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, and MINLEN:50” and inspected using FastQC v0.11.5 (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Hybrid assembly was conducted for all isolates using Unicycler v0.4.741 with the ‘normal’ mode. All genomes were annotated using Prokka v1.13.742, the K serotype, MLST, ARGs and virulence genes were analysed using Kleborate v0.3.035,43. For all newly-sequenced isolates, we measured the MIC of 23 antimicrobials via broth microdilution, namely MEM, IMP, ertapenem, cefoxitin, cefepime, ceftazidime, ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftazidime/clavulanic acid, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone/sulbactam, piperacillin/tazobactam, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, meromycin, colistin, TGC, chloramphenicol, fosfomycin, aztreonam, aztreonam/avibactam, and cefotaxime/clavulanic acid (Fig. 1a).

We downloaded all K. pneumoniae genomes from the RefSeq public database and selected 847 isolates with long-read sequencing data included in PathoTracker (as of June 2022, largest_contig > 5 M bp, whole genome, including plasmids). Among them, data from the public database that are traceable back to the location and time of submission were marked and sourced from the NCBI summary file (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/ASSEMBLY_REPORTS). If only the year of the strain collection was available, 1 July was used. The database is listed in Supplementary Data 1. Among them, 246 ST11-type isolates were selected and included in the Chinese ST11 PathoTracker database targeted ST11 isolates (Supplementary Data 2).

Public antibiotic resistance (ResFinder44) and virulence gene (VFDB45) databases were downloaded for comparison and output together in the results.

Pre-phylogenetic analysis and multilevel database architecture

To divide the database based on evolutionary relationships and facilitate rapid subsequent comparisons, the inferred maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree of the entire database was constructed using IQ-TREE v2.2.022 with “-b 100 -m GTR + G + I” on a core genome sequence alignment generated by Roary v1.7.821. Treecluster46 was employed based on the phylogenetic tree to divide isolates in the database into different “clusters”, each containing a certain number of similar isolates. The two largest evolutionary branches (capsule genotypes), KL47 and KL64, of the important sequence type ST11 in China were used to verify the cluster classification. The sum_branch method was used to divide the phylogenetic tree into an initial cluster boundary with a length threshold of 0.015. KL47 and KL64 in ST11 could not be fully distinguished at this threshold (Fig. 2a); therefore, to avoid excessive isolates in clusters influencing the accuracy, a threshold of 0.005 was used to split 0.015 clusters with more than 150 isolates (Supplementary Data 3c). After splitting, the ST-KL type was largely replaced (Fig. 2a) by 93 clusters (Supplementary Data 22, Fig. 4 and S1). The same procedure was applied to the Chinese ST11-type database, with the threshold set at 0.0001 (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Data 3b). The selected cluster was listed in the cluster ring in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2).

At the same time, the prevalence of the most common Carbapenemase genes carried by the isolates in China, namely blaNDM and blaKPC23, is shown in Fig. 2. The specific region in China where the Chinese ST11 strain originated was added to the annotation of the evolutionary tree, specifically, the middle ring in Fig. 2b.

For each cluster, Roary was used for pan-genome extraction, targeting the low data volumes of CMg samples ( ≤ 20 reads). Here, we created a specific “gene-isolate-cluster” index, which records the names of the genes contained in each isolate and the isolates in each cluster, storing the same gene only once and the relationships between these three levels to reduce the data while preserving the diverse information of all isolates (Fig. 1a). In the database, the pan-genome included 49,194 genes, while the core genome included 3157 genes. For the Chinese ST11-type database, the pan-genome included 22,004 genes, and the core genome included 4045 genes (Fig. S8).

As K. pneumoniae can easily acquire new ARGs and virulence genes, BLASTn was also used to compare the samples to be tested with the ResFinder and VFDB databases and output them together as part of the final results. The above steps were implemented using Python v3.6 and Shell.

Phylogenetic analysis and species detection in the website

We provided user-friendly phylogenetic analysis services for the assembled data in FASTA format. A phylogenetic tree was constructed via core genome analysis using Roary and IQ-TREE. We also provided a species detection service for Nanopore sequencing data. Taxonomy was assigned using Centrifuge48. Upon completion of the run, the above results and intermediate files are emailed to the users.

RAM monitoring

RAM was monitored once per second using the shell command “ps - O sz, rsz, vsz” in CentOS 7.9. In this study, BAL270, BAL259, and BAL023 with 8, 1095, and 38,550 reads, respectively, and their corresponding isolates were selected to examine RAM.

Website composition and architecture

PathoTracker was implemented on the UNIX operating system using Django v3.2.18 as the framework and Apache Tomcat as the server. A web user interface was developed using Atlantis Lite (https://github.com/themekita/Atlantis-Lite), JavaScript, HTML5, and CSS3.

AST and carbapenem resistance identification

The MIC of commonly used antimicrobials was determined using the agar dilution method as described in the CLSI instructions49. Escherichia coli ATCC® 25922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC® 27853 were used as quality control isolates.

Validation for PathoTracker and CMg sample source

The CMg samples and their data after 1 h of Nanopore sequencing were obtained from a large Nanopore CMg study in China8. Human reads were removed via mapping to hg38 using Minimap2 v2.2150 to increase the pathogen detection accuracy. Centrifuge v 1.0.3-beta48 with “--met 5 --time --ignore-quals -S result.tsv --report-file filtlong-report.tsv -x database -U rmh.fq.gz” parameters was used to detect K. pneumoniae. A total of 29 CMg samples were obtained and analysed using PathoTracker (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Data 5). To validate the PathoTracker results, 29 K. pneumoniae strains isolated from CMg samples were sequenced using the 150 bp paired-end Illumina NovaSeq platform (Supplementary Data 5 and 8).

Using the total number of clusters (93) as the limit, a random number was selected within each cluster as the test ID in cluster, following which seven random numbers were selected from the pool of 1187 as additional test isolate IDs. This yielded 100 isolates for the bootstrap-sampling test (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Data 3a and 4).

Criteria for detection of ST-KL type and AMR and virulence genes

Carbapenemase gene detection was based on ARG (blaKPC-2)23. The presence or absence of virulence genes (iucABCD, iroBCD, and rmpA2) on the classical virulence plasmid pLVPK was used to determine whether the strain contained the virulence plasmid26. The top 10 isolates from PathoTracker obtained from the comparison step closest to the sample were included in the adjudication. If < 5 isolates ( < 50%) presented AMR genes (carbapenemase genes), but at least one did, the strain might be carbapenem-resistant; if >5 isolates ( > 50%) had AMR genes, the sequence was confirmed to be carbapenem-resistant or resistant to MEM and IMP. The detection of virulence genes and ST-KL type followed the same criteria.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using stats R v4.1.0 and ggplot2 v3.3.651. Statistical analyses of two groups were performed using welch two sample t-test. The specific information about the isolates included in this study’s database can be found in Supplementary Data 1 and 2 as previously described.
