Panoramic radiograph–based dental age estimation in children and adolescents using multi-tooth methods: a scoping review of methods used and reported accuracy (2020–2025)
Authors: Małgorzata Łysiak-Majchrzak, Sylwia Jagła, Natalia Kazimierczak, Wojciech Kazimierczak, Teresa Matthews-Brzozowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate dental age estimation supports treatment planning in pediatric dentistry and orthodontics and forensic assessment when chronological age is uncertain. Because the accuracy of panoramic radiograph-based methods varies across populations, age groups, and method variants, a contemporary synthesis is needed to guide method selection.ObjectiveTo map the methods used for panoramic radiograph–based dental age estimation in children and adolescents and to summarize the reported diagnostic accuracy of the most frequently used method families in studies published between 2020 and 2025.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Studies published between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2025 were screened according to predefined eligibility criteria. Data were extracted using a standardized form, including study characteristics, dental age estimation methods, and reported accuracy outcomes (primarily mean absolute error [MAE] and mean difference/bias [DA–CA]). Thirty-four original studies were included.ResultsThe included studies comprised over 35,000 children and adolescents from multiple countries and were predominantly retrospective and clinic-based. Willems-family methods were the most frequently reported, followed by Demirjian-, Cameriere-, and London Atlas-based approaches. Across studies, accuracy reporting was heterogeneous with respect to MAE, bias metrics, sex stratification, and method variants. Cameriere family methods often showed lower MAE values in many cohorts; however, performance varied by population, age range, and method modification.ConclusionPanoramic radiograph–based dental age estimation methods show substantial heterogeneity in use and reported accuracy across populations. Willems-family methods are the most frequently applied, whereas Cameriere-family methods often demonstrate favorable accuracy in many cohorts, but no single method can be considered universally optimal. Introduction

Instrumental assessment of dental age in children is primarily based on two imaging modalities: panoramic radiography and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). Panoramic radiographs are widely used in pediatric dentistry as a routine diagnostic tool. Their popularity stems from the ability to visualize the entire dentition in a single image while maintaining a relatively low radiation dose, particularly with modern digital systems that significantly reduce exposure compared with earlier analog techniques. Panoramic radiographic methods that enable the assessment of tooth germ mineralization constitute a safe and effective approach to evaluating dental development in children and adolescents [1].

Accurate dental age estimation is clinically relevant across several disciplines. In paediatric dentistry, it supports the timing of preventive interventions, monitoring of eruption sequences, and identification of developmental anomalies such as delayed or accelerated dental maturation [2]. In orthodontics and dentofacial orthopaedics, knowledge of the developmental stage is integral to treatment planning decisions, including the optimal timing for functional appliance therapy, growth modification, and the initiation of fixed appliance treatment [3, 4]. In paediatric oral surgery, dental age informs decisions regarding the extraction of primary teeth and the management of impacted or supernumerary teeth [2]. Beyond direct clinical care, dental age estimation has established forensic applications in the identification of unaccompanied minors and age-disputed individuals in legal and humanitarian contexts, where no documentary evidence of birth date is available [5–7]. Given the breadth of these applications and the diversity of populations served by dental clinicians worldwide, the selection of an appropriate and validated dental age estimation method is a matter of practical clinical importance.

Over the years, numerous methods for dental age estimation have been developed and refined, including approaches proposed by Demirjian, Willems, Cameriere, Nolla, Haavikko, Moorrees, the London Atlas, and others [8–11]. The popularity of individual dental age estimation methods has varied over time and across different regions of the world, reflecting local preferences, population-specific validations, and the availability of reference data.

The aim of the present scoping review was to map contemporary panoramic radiograph–based dental age estimation methods used in children and adolescents and to summarize their reported diagnostic accuracy in studies published over the past five years.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and information sources

The review question was structured using the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework, which is commonly applied in scoping reviews [12]. The Population comprised children and adolescents. The Concept encompassed multi-tooth dental age estimation methods based on panoramic radiographs and the reported accuracy of these methods. The Context comprised clinical and forensic dental age-estimation research across diverse populations and geographic settings. The 2020-2025 publication period was an eligibility criterion, not a PCC component. The review question was: Which multi-tooth panoramic radiograph–based methods are used to estimate dental age in children and adolescents, in recent literature, and how is their accuracy reported?

This scoping review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [12]. The literature search was performed in three eletronic databases: PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. No formal review protocol was registered or published before conducting this scoping review. Searches targeted studies published between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2025; the final search was performed on 31 December 2025. No database filters were applied. Eligibility was restricted to full-text articles published in English to enable consistent extraction and interpretation of methodological and accuracy data. Backward and forward citation chasing was not performed.

The review was restricted to studies published between 2020 and 2025 for two reasons. First, this five-year window reflects contemporary practice and captures recent population-specific validations and method modifications that have emerged following earlier foundational work. Moreover, the performance of the established methods continues to be reported and updated in recent literature, making this period most relevant to current clinical decisions. Second, restricting the window to a defined recent interval reduces heterogeneity in reporting practices and contextual factors (e.g., digital radiography equipment and standards, software tools) compared with searches spanning several decades.

After removal of 166 duplicates, 963 records were identified. Manual screening of titles and abstracts resulted in 91 articles selected for full-text analysis. Screening and data charting

Data charting was performed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers (MŁ-M and WK) using a standardized charting form piloted on a random subset of five included studies before full data charting. The form was refined after pilot testing to ensure consistent capture of study characteristics, methodological details, dental age estimation methods, and accuracy outcomes. Disagreements in charted data were resolved through discussion; where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (TM-B) adjudicated.

Extracted variables included first author and year; country/region and population descriptors; study setting/design; sample size (total and sex-stratified where available); age range; dental age estimation method(s); and reported accuracy outcomes. Accuracy measures were recorded as provided by the authors, prioritizing mean absolute error (MAE, years) and mean difference/bias (dental age (DA) minus chronological age (CA), years), and whether results were reported overall or stratified by sex and/or age groups.

For descriptive synthesis of method usage, methods were grouped at the family level (e.g., Willems-family, Demirjian-family, Cameriere-family), while modified or population-specific versions (e.g., Willems I/II, Willems SA/BC, modified Cameriere equations) were retained as variants in the detailed accuracy tables. This approach was adopted to avoid double counting conceptually related methods in frequency analyses.

Data items

The following data items were charted: first author, publication year, country or region, population characteristics, study design and setting, sample size, sex distribution, chronological age range, sampling strategy, date-of-birth verification, dental age estimation method or method family, method variant where applicable, reported accuracy outcomes, sex-stratified outcomes where available, interrater reliability, intrarater reliability, and authors’ main conclusions. Accuracy outcomes were recorded as reported by the original authors, with priority given to mean absolute error (MAE) and directional bias expressed as dental age minus chronological age (DA–CA). Where original studies reported chronological age minus dental age (CA–DA), values were converted to DA–CA for consistency.

For descriptive synthesis of method usage, methods were grouped at the family level (e.g., Willems-family, Demirjian-family, Cameriere-family), while modified or population-specific versions (e.g., Willems I/II, Willems SA/BC, modified Cameriere equations) were retained as variants in the detailed accuracy tables. This approach was adopted to avoid double counting conceptually related methods in frequency analyses.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers independently evaluated methodological quality and risk of bias using an adapted Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cross-sectional studies [13]. The adapted scale comprised four evaluated domains: Clarity of stated aim (0–2), Sample selection (0–8), Comparability (0–2), and Outcome (0–4), with a total score 0–16.

Studies were classified according to the total score as follows: 13–16 points indicated high methodological quality and low risk of bias, 9–12 points indicated moderate quality and moderate risk of bias, while scores below 9 points reflected low quality and a high risk of bias. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (TM-B).

Results

Characteristics of included studies

This scoping review included 34 studies examining the reported accuracy of panoramic radiograph–based dental age estimation methods across diverse populations (Table 1). Most studies were retrospective and clinic-based, although population-based datasets were also represented. The included studies comprised more than 35,000 children and adolescents from 20 countries/regions, with sample sizes ranging from 400 to 5,017. Age ranges varied substantially across studies, and several cohorts extended into young adulthood while still including pediatric/adolescent participants.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment using the adapted Newcastle–Ottawa Scale is summarized in Table 2, with detailed data presented in Supplementary Table S2. Across the 34 included studies, NOS scores ranged from 9 to 13 out of 16, with a median score of 12 and a mean score of 11.6. No study was classified as low quality or high risk of bias. Seven studies were rated as high methodological quality and low risk of bias, whereas 27 studies were rated as moderate methodological quality and moderate risk of bias (Table 2). Risk-of-bias ratings were not used to exclude studies from the review; they were used to contextualize the descriptive synthesis.

Summary of risk of bias assessment

Overall, the included studies showed generally acceptable methodological quality for descriptive synthesis. The strongest domains were clarity of the stated aim, exposure assessment, handling of non-respondents, and assessment of the outcome, which were consistently scored highly across the included studies. The main recurrent methodological limitations concerned sample representativeness and comparability. Most studies were based on retrospective, clinic-based, or convenience samples, which limits the generalizability of their findings to broader pediatric and adolescent populations.

Because most included studies evaluated two or more method families simultaneously, risk-of-bias ratings were assigned at the study level rather than at the method level. Nonetheless, to facilitate comparison across the four most prevalent method families, the distribution of NOS quality categories was examined within the subset of studies that evaluated each method. Among studies reporting Willems-family methods (n = 15), five were rated high quality and ten moderate quality, corresponding to a mean NOS score of 11.9. Among studies reporting Demirjian-family methods (n = 13), two were rated high quality and eleven moderate quality (mean NOS 11.4). Among studies reporting Cameriere-family methods (n = 11), four were rated high quality and seven moderate quality (mean NOS 12.1). Among studies reporting London Atlas methods (n = 7), three were rated high quality and four moderate quality (mean NOS 12.0). The NOS score distributions were broadly similar across all four method families, indicating that risk-of-bias considerations do not systematically favour one method over another in the descriptive accuracy comparisons presented below. The main driver of lower scores across all groups was the use of clinic-based or convenience sampling rather than population-representative sampling frames.

Frequency of dental age assessment methods

When method usage was analyzed descriptively at the family level, Willems-family methods were the most frequently reported, followed by Demirjian-, Cameriere-, and London Atlas-based approaches (Table 3; Fig. 2). This pattern was consistent when considering both the number of studies and the cumulative number of patients, although the exact ranking varied slightly depending on whether studies, countries, or patient totals were used as the denominator.

Demirjian method

The Demirjian method was reported in 13 study–method comparisons (Table 5). Where MAE was reported, sex-stratified MAE ranged from 0.66–1.01 years in males and 0.75–1.18 years in females (Pan; Shan; Brkić; Zhou; Kwon). Bias (DA–CA) was predominantly positive (overestimation), with multiple studies reporting mean overestimation typically on the order of ~+0.55 to +1.46 years depending on population (e.g., Croatia, Romania, Argentina, India, Turkey, Saudi Arabia). Notable exceptions were observed in some Chinese cohorts, where Demirjian underestimated age (e.g. Kwon: −0.51 in boys with +0.48 in girls). Collectively, these findings indicate that Demirjian-derived bias is not stable across populations and can change in both direction and magnitude.

Cameriere method

The Cameriere method (including modified and/or population-specific equations) was reported in 14 study–method comparisons (Table 6). Where the MAE was available, the reported sex-stratified MAE ranged from 0.50–1.02 years in males and 0.51–1.03 years in females (e.g., Brkić; Gümüşboğa; Ishwarkumar). Across cohorts, bias (DA–CA) tended to be closer to zero than in Demirjian in several datasets but remained method- and population dependent, spanning underestimation (e.g., et al. Darıcı: ~−0.24/−0.23; Hato: ~−0.17/−0.10; Yavuz: ~−0.31/−0.39) and overestimation in some variants/populations (e.g., et al. Ishwarkumar population specific: +0.47/+0.58; Şahin: +0.17/+0.22). Importantly, some papers reported multiple Cameriere-based equations with materially different bias (e.g. et al. Egyptian formula vs. original; modified vs. original Cameriere), so interpretation should be tied to the specific equation used rather than treating “Cameriere” as a single fixed model.

Willems method

The Willems method was reported most frequently, with 17 study–method comparisons (Table 7). Sex-stratified MAEs were available in a subset and ranged from 0.70–1.37 years in males and 0.70–1.37 years in females, with higher errors typically reported in specific populations or variants (e.g., Saudi Willems variants; some older age ranges). Bias (DA–CA) demonstrated bidirectional behavior across studies: marked underestimation in some datasets (e.g., et al. Kwon: −0.80/−0.82; Lin: −0.82/−0.85; Pan: −0.07/−0.24) and clear overestimation in others (e.g., et al. Koç: +0.43/+0.34; Yavuz: +0.68/+0.68; Hato: +0.49/+0.76). Several papers also reported multiple Willems variants (e.g., Willems I vs. II; Willems SA vs. BC) with differing sex-specific performance, underscoring that the Willems approach is frequently reparameterized and not necessarily transportable without validation.

London atlas method

The London Atlas method was reported in 6 study–method comparisons (Table 8). Sex-stratified MAE (when available) ranged from 0.58–1.19 years in males and 0.58–1.22 years in females (e.g., Kuppusamy; Lin; Dalben; Koç; Zhou). Reported bias (DA–CA) was generally small to moderate, with both slight underestimation (e.g., Lin: −0.06/−0.05; Koç: −0.07 in boys with + 0.28 in girls) and overestimation in other populations (e.g., Dalben: +0.60/+0.56; Yavuz: +0.05/+0.26; Kuppusamy: +0.01/+0.07). Two studies reported MAE without paired MD values (or vice versa), limiting cross-study comparability even within this single method.

Other methods

Beyond the four most frequently reported approaches (Demirjian, Cameriere, Willems, London Atlas), the remaining dental age assessment (DAA) methods were represented by comparatively few study–method comparisons and were inconsistently reported (often lacking sex-stratified MAE and/or providing only mean differences [DA–CA]). This limited direct comparability across populations and precluded any meaningful synthesis beyond narrative comparison.

Across the included studies, Nolla most often underestimated chronological age, with mean differences ranging from near zero in some Turkish cohorts (e.g., ~ 0.0 to − 0.32 years) to substantial underestimation in Romanian and Saudi cohorts (approximately − 1.26 to − 1.71 years in Romania and − 1.07 to − 1.50 years in Saudi Arabia) [23, 32, 38]. When the MAE was reported, it was moderate (e.g., 0.84 years in boys and 0.94 years in girls in an Eastern Turkish cohort), but the direction and magnitude of bias appeared population- and age-structure dependent [32].

The evidence for the Haavikko method was sparse. In Croatian children, Haavikko showed relatively low MAE (~ 0.61 years) with a small overall underestimation (MD ≈ − 0.17 years) sex-stratified MD not always available. However, in another Croatian dataset, larger negative mean differences were reported (approximately − 0.6 years in boys and − 0.8 years in girls), again suggesting limited transportability without calibration [18].

Bedek-type models have been reported in limited settings [27]. In a South Indian cohort, MAE values were approximately 0.85 years (boys) and 0.88 years (girls) [43]. In an Eastern Turkish cohort, a Bedek 7-tooth model showed an MAE of ~ 0.70 years with a near-zero mean difference (≈ 0.0 in boys and − 0.1 in girls), although performance varied by the specific Bedek submodel (e.g., different tooth subsets suggested for boys vs. girls) [27].

Discussion

This scoping review provides a contemporary map of panoramic radiograph–based dental age estimation methods used in children and adolescents between 2020 and 2025. At the method-family level, Willems-family methods were the most frequently reported and accounted for the largest proportion of assessed patients, followed by Demirjian-, Cameriere, and London Atlas-based approaches. Importantly, this pattern should be interpreted as a reflection of method adoption and reporting frequency rather than direct evidence of superior diagnostic performance. In addition, the high prevalence of the Willems family is not surprising because it is historically derived from the Demirjian framework and is widely used as a practical reparameterized alternative in both clinical and forensic settings. The frequency findings therefore indicate which methods are most commonly implemented in recent literature but not which method is universally the most accurate.

These findings differ from the previously prevailing view that the Demirjian method is the most commonly applied dental age estimation technique [11]. The Willems method employs a modified version of the Demirjian approach to assess radiographs by classifying tooth development into one of eight developmental stages. Each developmental stage of a given tooth is assigned a score based on sex-specific tables, and the scores for seven teeth are summed, with the total score directly corresponding to dental age [32]. The Willems method simplifies the Demirjian procedure by eliminating the additional conversion step. In essence, it represents a reconstructed version of the Demirjian method, in which the parameters of the original dental development model are adjusted through reregression [42].

The prominence of the Willems family in the included studies is consistent with its practical advantages and broad international uptake. However, the present results also show that Willems-based performance is population- and variant dependent. Across cohorts, the direction of bias (DA–CA) was bidirectional, with clear underestimation in some Chinese datasets and overestimation in several Turkish and Croatian cohorts. This variability was also observed in studies that used different Willems variants (e.g., Willems I vs II; SA vs BC), indicating that the Willems approach should not be treated as a single transportable model without local validation [45]. In practical terms, the method remains a useful and often accurate option, but its performance should be interpreted within the context of the population, age structure, and variant applied.

Studies conducted in East Chinese populations demonstrated that the Willems method provided greater accuracy in dental age estimation compared with the classical Demirjian method [39]. Similarly, research in South Chinese populations indicated that the Willems method was more appropriate than the Demirjian approach [43]. Dental age estimates obtained using the Willems method in Croatian populations were also significantly more accurate than those derived from the Demirjian method. Recent evidence further supports the accuracy and reliability of the radiographic Willems method [48]. Nevertheless, many authors publishing between 2020 and 2025 continued to combine the Willems method with other approaches to enhance precision [18, 29, 31, 32, 35, 36, 39, 40, 46].

The Demirjian method remains widely used and ranks as the second most frequently applied approach; however, numerous studies indicate that it is characterized by lower accuracy compared with alternative methods. In an Eastern Turkish population, the Nolla method demonstrated higher accuracy than the Demirjian method [18]. Similarly, studies in the Riyadh region of Saudi Arabia reported that the London Atlas method achieved greater accuracy than Demirjian, although the differences were not statistically significant. Owing to the need to improve its accuracy and validity, various modification strategies have been proposed. These include statistical approaches such as the polynomial curve fitting method (PCFM) [40], as well as integration with machine learning algorithms. Among these, Gradient Boosting and Random Forest models combining the Demirjian method with different machine learning techniques demonstrated the highest performance [49]. Convolutional neural network (CNN) models have also shown superior effectiveness in dental age estimation [50].

The Cameriere method, which ranks third in this review, is the only method in this comparison based on quantitative analysis of permanent tooth root development, measured by assessing the width of open root apices. One of its principal advantages is high precision, resulting from direct morphometric measurements and the use of regression equations to calculate dental age [27]. The method demonstrates good agreement between dental and chronological age, particularly in younger age groups [24]. Furthermore, it shows good reproducibility and reduced dependence on subjective observer interpretation. As with other methods, the effectiveness of the Cameriere method may be limited by population differences, emphasizing the need for population-specific formulas. Its applicability is reduced in older adolescents, in whom root mineralization is largely complete; consequently, differences in apical width become minimal, diminishing the method’s ability to discriminate age accurately [24, 26].

It should be noted that Professor Roberto Cameriere has authored numerous foundational and validation studies on open-apex-based dental age estimation [51–53]. The present review did not include Cameriere's original methodological publications as primary study entries, because those foundational papers predate the 2020–2025 search window and/or did not meet the minimum sample-size criterion (≥400 individuals). The specific Cameriere-family equations applied in each included study (European formula, population-specific modifications, Bayesian adaptations) are detailed in Tables 4 and 6.

The London Atlas method, ranked fourth, is based on comparison of panoramic radiographs with a reference atlas illustrating successive stages of dental development. Its main advantage lies in the comprehensive assessment of both deciduous and permanent dentition, enabling age estimation across a wide developmental range. The intuitive atlas-based format and the availability of dedicated software facilitate rapid application in clinical practice and reduce subjectivity [21, 47]. The accuracy of the London Atlas is particularly high in younger age groups but decreases during transitional periods, especially between late childhood and early adolescence. During this stage, the development of third molars plays a critical role; their high interindividual variability may increase the margin of error [34].

A comparison of MAE values across the four most frequently used methods suggests that the Cameriere method demonstrates the highest accuracy. However, as this parameter was not consistently reported across all studies and the analyzed groups varied in sample size, this finding should be interpreted with caution and regarded as indicative. Available evidence indicates that AI-based models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), demonstrated superior diagnostic performance with MAEs ranging from 0.03 to 0.7 years [54, 55].

Methods outside the four most frequently reported families (e.g., Nolla, Haavikko, Moorrees/MFH, GH, Nicodemo, Bedek, Chaillet, Blenkin-Evans) were represented by relatively few study–method comparisons and were often incompletely reported, particularly with respect to sex-stratified MAE and bias. This limited direct comparability and precluded strong conclusions. Nevertheless, the available evidence suggests that several of these methods may perform well in specific populations, whereas others show substantial bias without local calibration. This pattern again supports the broader conclusion of the review: method performance is population-dependent [55, 56], and even historically established methods may require regional validation before clinical or forensic use.

Several limitations of the evidence base should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, most included studies were retrospective and clinic-based, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to the broader pediatric population. Second, verification of chronological age was not uniformly reported; although many studies used official records, others provided limited or no information on reference standard verification. Third, reporting of blinding and reproducibility procedures was inconsistent, and not all studies provided both interrater and intrarater reliability. Fourth, despite the large overall number of participants, age distributions within individual studies were often uneven, which may affect performance estimates in specific age strata. Finally, although risk of bias was assessed using an adapted Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, the appraisal was intended to support descriptive interpretation rather than to exclude studies or generate pooled estimates. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as a structured map of current evidence rather than as a quantitative synthesis of diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that panoramic radiograph-based dental age estimation methods are applied heterogeneously across populations, with the Willems family of methods being the most frequently reported in recent literature. Because no single method has proven universally transferable, clinicians should not assume that validation in one population justifies direct application to a different ethnic or geographic group. In settings where panoramic radiography is routinely performed, method selection should be guided by the best available local validation evidence; where no population-specific data exist, practitioners are advised to apply two or more methods in parallel and interpret results with an appropriate margin of uncertainty. The directional bias values (DA–CA) reported across studies reflect systematic tendencies of specific methods in specific populations rather than universal properties of those methods; these findings should not be over-generalized. The magnitude and direction of bias varied substantially with population composition, age structure, and the particular variant applied — reinforcing that such conclusions must remain population-specific. Taken together, these observations point to a priority for future research: population-specific validation studies paired with standardized reporting of MAE, bias (DA–CA), sex-stratified outcomes, and reliability measures.
