Improving trauma care in Pakistan: a need for intervention-based studies
Authors: Justine Broecker, Saad Liaqat Sahi
Published: 2026

This informative article written by Ullah et al1 references that 90% of trauma mortality occurs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), whereas 90% of trauma publications originate from high-income institutions.2 3 Data collection in LMICs, including Pakistan, remains constrained by limited resources, making studies like this one particularly valuable.3 4 Ullah et al provide an important retrospective analysis of demographics, injury patterns, and outcomes among patients presenting to a tertiary trauma center in Peshawar during a 5-year period.

Their findings reflect well-established themes in the LMIC trauma literature: injuries disproportionately affect young males, road traffic collisions predominate, and severe traumatic brain injury and hemorrhagic shock drive early mortality.5–9 These patterns translate into substantial disability-adjusted life-years lost and underscore the need for both a national trauma registry and systematic prehospital data collection, which are goals the authors explicitly endorse.

A recent systematic review by Atiq et al analyzed barriers, facilitators, and existing interventions to improve trauma care across Pakistan’s four phases of care.10 That review found that published literature has largely focused on barriers to healthcare service delivery, workforce, and essential resources, whereas studies examining financing, governance, and leadership challenges—and crucially analyses of interventions addressing those barriers—remain scarce. Filling these gaps is necessary to translate the epidemiological foundation laid by Ullah et al into actionable systems change. Defining clear metrics such as target 30-day mortality rates and injury-to-hospital transfer times would further guide quality improvement and policy efforts. Notably, Ullah et al report a modest decline in mortality over the study period, a meaningful achievement in a resource-constrained setting, yet the drivers of this improvement and attainable benchmarks for future progress remain undefined and warrant further study.9

Several injury patterns described also merit deeper investigation. What mechanisms predominate among burn patients, and how might they be addressed upstream? With severe traumatic brain injury present in 56% of non-survivors, what proportion is attributable to road traffic crashes versus falls or assault? Among penetrating injuries, are there sex-based differences in mechanism, such as intimate partner violence, that would require targeted responses beyond broader trauma system strengthening?

Prospective, intervention-focused, multicenter studies are the necessary next step to build upon the important descriptive work presented here.
