A Self-assembly Carrier-Free Nanovaccine based on Fluorinated CpG and Neoantigen Peptides for Hepatocellular Carcinoma Immunotherapy
Authors: Xiuyun Lin, Hui Li, Jie Liu, Guangfeng Wu, Liuqing Yao, Zhouwei Zhan, Xiufang Yang, Xiu Yang, Shuxian Cai, Na Yao, Fengfang Chen, Wenqiang Yan, Peng Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoantigen peptides evade central thymic tolerance due to their absence in normal tissues, making neoantigen vaccines a leading strategy for personalized cancer immunotherapy. Although nanoparticle-based delivery systems are widely used to enhance neoantigen immunogenicity, traditional nanovaccines often incorporate multiple adjuvants and inert carriers, which could hinder efficient antigen delivery and raise biosafety concerns. Herein, we report a carrier-free neoantigen nanovaccine (CpG-2′F/NeoWD), assembled via fluorination-induced self-assembly for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) immunotherapy. This nanovaccine is formed by the co-assembly of fluorinated CpG (CpG-2′F) with the HCC neoantigen peptide (NeoWD), yielding nanoparticles of approximately 69 nm with a high NeoWD loading efficiency of 67.18 wt%. In vitro studies demonstrate that the nanovaccine potently facilitates dendritic cell uptake and promotes endosomal/lysosomal escape, enhancing antigen cross-presentation efficiency by 2.05-fold compared to the antigen group. In vivo, by efficiently targeting lymph nodes (LNs), the nanovaccine orchestrated both innate and adaptive immune responses against cancer cells, leading to the pronounced expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and memory T cells. In both prophylactic and therapeutic Hepa1–6 HCC models, this nanovaccine effectively suppressed the progression of poorly immunogenic HCC. Therefore, this study presents a facile, safe and potently immunogenic carrier-free nanovaccine platform for HCC immunotherapy.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Neoantigen nanovaccine, Carrier-free nanovaccine；immunotherapy

Instructions

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a major global health challenge, with an estimated 1 million new cases projected by 2025 (Llovet et al., 2021). As the predominant form of liver cancer, HCC accounts for more than 90% of cases (Soares et al., 2019). Its pathogenesis is frequently associated with well-defined etiological factors, including chronic hepatitis virus infection and liver cirrhosis (Kanwal et al., 2011; Sankar et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025). This distinct epidemiological feature enables the identification of high-risk populations and provides a unique window for cancer preventive intervention. Compared with therapeutic strategies targeting established tumors, preventive approaches offer greater cost-effectiveness and clinical benefit in reducing disease burden (Yeo et al., 2025). Cancer immunoprevention aims to establish, through vaccination, pre-existing specific immunological memory against tumor-associated antigens in healthy or high-risk individuals (Martini and Wu, 2025), thereby enabling immune-mediated recognition and elimination of tumors at their earliest stage.

Neoantigen-based vaccines have emerged as a promising avenue in this regard. Derived from tumor-specific mutations, neoantigens are recognized as completely ‘non-self’ and can therefore bypass central thymic tolerance (Ott et al., 2017). This ‘non-self’ nature confers distinctive advantages: they bind major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules efficiently, thereby activating tumor-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and eliciting precise, potent antitumor immune responses (Peng et al., 2019). Leveraging this immunogenic advantage, a range of strategies have been employed to capture neoantigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) in situ (Huynh et al., 2026; Lien et al., 2026; Chiang et al., 2025; Huynh et al., 2025). The captured cargo is subsequently delivered to program dendritic cells (DCs), resulting in sustained immune stimulation. This in situ capture paradigm provides a robust foundation and candidate platforms for cancer immunotherapy.

Despite these advances, developing neoantigens into vaccines ex vivo still faces two major challenges. First, individual neoantigen peptides often exhibit low or negligible immunogenicity and are insufficient to prime robust initial immune responses capable of establishing protective immunological memory (Ni et al., 2020). This is attributable to poor accumulation of cancer vaccines in lymph nodes (LNs), inadequate uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and limited antigen cross-presentation (Yarchoan et al., 2017). Second, traditional nanovaccine technologies typically rely on exogenous carriers, such as liposomes (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2021), polymers (Xu et al., 2020) metal-organic frameworks (Zhang et al., 2016), and extracellular vesicle (EV) (Cao et al., 2026), for the co-delivery of antigens and adjuvants (Chen et al., 2022). These carrier materials not only pose risks of unpredictable biocompatibility and immunogenicity, but also result in low antigen encapsulation efficiency (Chen et al., 2022; Elsabahy and Wooley, 2015; Liu et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2026). Moreover, the dense carrier matrix may physically hinder efficient epitope exposure and recognition by APCs, ultimately compromising immune activation (Irvine et al., 2013). Therefore, the development of a minimalist vaccine platform that eliminates redundant carriers while enabling efficient co-delivery of antigens and adjuvants, coupled with LNs-targeting capability, is crucial for realizing the potential of neoantigens in cancer prevention.

The 2′-fluoro (2’-F) modification offers a promising strategy to address these challenges (Wang et al., 2023). By replacing the 2′-hydroxyl group with a fluorine atom, this modification enhances binding affinity and specificity, improves resistance to nuclease degradation, and reduces immunostimulatory effects (Jiang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). On this basis, a carrier-free neoantigen nanovaccine (CpG-2′F/NeoWD) was constructed for HCC immunotherapy through a fluorinated CpG-induced self-assembly strategy. This nanovaccine was formed by co-assembling 2′F-fluorinated mouse TLR9-specific B-type CpG oligonucleotides (CpG-2′F) with the HCC neoantigen peptide (NeoWD)(Zhao et al., 2022) (See Tables 1 and 2), yielding uniform nanoparticles of approximately 69 nm in diameter with a high NeoWD loading efficiency of 67.18 wt% (Scheme 1). In vitro studies demonstrated that the nanovaccine effectively promoted antigen uptake by DCs and facilitated endosomal/lysosomal escape, enhancing antigen cross-presentation efficiency by 2.05-fold compared to the antigen group. In vivo, the nanovaccine achieved 12.45-fold higher accumulation in LNs relative to the CpG-2′F-Cy5 group. It effectively stimulated DC maturation within LNs, promoting neoantigen presentation to T cells, which in turn drove T cell expansion and elicited robust antigen-specific cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses. In addition, the formation of memory T cells was facilitated. Furthermore, this nanovaccine upregulated the proportions of NK cells and M1 macrophages, reduced the levels of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and promoted the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, thereby remodelling the tumor immune microenvironment. In both prophylactic and therapeutic Hepa1–6 HCC models, this nanovaccine effectively suppressed the progression of poorly immunogenic HCC, demonstrating its potential as a promising new direction for HCC immunotherapy.

All experiments utilized ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm) generated by a Milli-Q water purification system (Merck Millipore, USA) to ensure experimental accuracy.

Apparatus

The particle size and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano particle analyzer (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Fluorescence spectra were obtained using a Cary Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). TEM images were captured with a Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope operated at 100.0 kV (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Cell viability based on CCK-8 assays was measured using an SH-1000 laboratory microplate reader (Corona Electric, Ibaraki, Japan). CLSM was performed using a Nikon A1 microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Flow cytometric analysis was conducted on a FACS Canto™ II flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). In vivo imaging was carried out using an IVIS Lumina Series III system (PerkinElmer, Shanghai, China).

DNA and peptide sequences

DNA sequences were synthesized and HPLC-purified by Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Peptide sequences were synthesized and purified by Shanghai Apeptide Co., Ltd.

Preparation of CpG-2′F/NeoWD and 2′F-CpG/OVA257–280 nanoparticles

Under aseptic conditions, CpG-2′F, OVA257–280 and NeoWD peptide were separately dissolved in sterile water to prepare stock solutions at concentrations of 100 μM 2′F-CpG solution, and 1 mM peptide solutions, respectively. The CpG-2′F solution was heated at 95 °C for 5 min to ensure denaturation into single strands. Subsequently, CpG-2′F and NeoWD or OVA257–280 were mixed in 1× PBS buffer at various molar ratios, 1:1, 1:3, 1:6.5, 1:12, and 1:15 (CpG-2′F: NeoWD) or 1:4 (CpG-2′F: OVA257–280), with a final CpG-2′F concentration of 50 μM. The mixture was gently shaken at 37 °C for 2 h and then incubated at 4 °C for 24 h to allow self-assembly into CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanoparticles. The products were purified by dialysis using a Slide-A-Lyzer (10 K MWCO, Thermo Scientific) to obtain nanovaccines. To quantify the loading efficiency of CpG-2′F and NeoWD in the nanoparticles, CpG-2′F was labeled with Cy5 and NeoWD was labeled with FITC. Standard curves for NeoWD-FITC and CpG-2′F-Cy5 were established by measuring fluorescence intensity at 520 nm (excitation 488 nm) and 665 nm (excitation 635 nm), respectively. CpG-2′F-Cy5/NeoWD-FITC nanoparticles were prepared following the same procedure as for unlabeled CpG-2′F/NeoWD. The nanoparticles were then disassembled by adding the complementary sequence of CpG (Com-CpG), and the fluorescence intensities of CpG-2′F-Cy5 and NeoWD-FITC were measured. The corresponding masses were calculated using the established standard curves. The loading efficiency (%, w/w) of NeoWD was determined as: (weight of loaded NeoWD-FITC/total weight of CpG-2′F-Cy5/NeoWD-FITC) × 100%, reflecting the mass fraction of the NeoWD-FITC in the final product. The encapsulation efficiency (%, w/w) of NeoWD was calculated as: (weight of loaded NeoWD-FITC/total weight of NeoWD-FITC added) × 100%, representing the utilization efficiency of the NeoWD-FITC.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Using GROMACS software, the system was solvated in a 9 × 9 × 9 nm3 TIP3P water box, employing the Amber ff99 force field under periodic boundary conditions with PME electrostatics and SHAKE constraints. After a 10 ns NPT equilibrium, the assembly process and interactions between the 2′F-CpG and polypeptide were analyzed over an additional 10 ns production run.

Cell Culture

Hepa1–6 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Mouse bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were differentiated from bone marrow cells isolated from the femurs and tibiae of 6-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing GM-CSF (20 ng mL−1) and IL-4 (10 ng mL−1) at 37 °C in 5% CO2, following an established protocol. On day 6, immature BMDCs were collected for subsequent experiments. Mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were differentiated from bone marrow cells isolated similarly from C57BL/6 mice. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and recombinant murine GM-CSF (20 ng mL−1) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. On day 7, mature BMDMs were harvested for subsequent experiments.

In vitro cytotoxicity and DC activation

The cytotoxicity of the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. BMDCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and incubated with increasing concentrations of the nanovaccine ([NeoWD] = 0, 1, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 μg mL−1) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using an SH-1000 microplate reader after adding CCK-8 reagent.

For DC activation assays, immature BMDCs were plated in 12-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well and stimulated for 24 h with the following treatments: PBS, NeoWD (10 μg mL−1), CpG-2′F, CpG-2′F + NeoWD, or CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine ([NeoWD] = 10 μg mL−1, [CpG-2′F] = 1 μM). After stimulation, cells were harvested, resuspended in FACS buffer (1 × PBS containing 1% FBS), and incubated with anti-CD16/32 antibody at 4 °C for 30 min to block Fc receptors. Cells were then stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD11c, CD80, CD86, CD40 and MHC-II on ice for 30 min. Stained cells were washed with FACS buffer and analyzed using a FACS Canto™ II flow cytometer. Culture supernatants were collected to measure the levels of TNF-α and IL-6 by ELISA, performed according to the manufacturers' instructions.

To evaluate CpG-2′F/NeoWD-induced M1 macrophage polarization, immature BMDMs were plated in 12-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well and stimulated for 24 h with the same set of treatments. Cells were then harvested and subjected to flow cytometry analysis following the same procedure as described above, using fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against F40/80, CD11b, CD86.

In vitro cellular uptake and intracellular antigen localization

BMDCs were seeded in confocal dishes at a density 3 × 104 cells per dish and incubated with the NeoWD-FITC, NeoWD-FITC + CpG-2′F or CpG-2′F/NeoWD-FITC nanovaccine ([NeoWD-FITC] = 10 μg ml−1, [CpG-2′F] =1 μM) for either 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h or 24 h. Lysosomes and nuclei were stained with LysoTracker Red and Hoechst 33342, respectively, following the manufacturer's protocols. After washing with 1 × PBS, cells were visualized using a CLSM. Hoechst 33342, FITC, and LysoTracker Red were excited at 405 nm, 488 nm, and 640 nm, respectively.

In vitro cross-presentation efficiency of DCs

Immature BMDCs (1 × 106 cells per well) were plated onto 12-well non-treated dishes and simulated with OVA257–280, CpG-2′F + OVA257–280, CpG-2′F/OVA257–280 nanovaccine ([OVA257–280] = 10 μg ml−1, [CpG-2′F] =1 μM) at 37 °C for 24 h. After various treatments, BMDCs were harvested and resuspended in FACS buffer. They were then incubated with anti-CD16/32 at 4 °C for 30 min and then stained with fluorophore-labeled antibodies (anti-CD11c, H-2Kb bound to SIINFEKL) for 30 min on the ice. The BMDCs were washed with FACS buffer and analyzed using a FACS CantoTMIIFlow Cytometer.

In vitro CD8+ T-cell activation assay

BMDCs were incubated with NeoWD (10 μg ml−1) or CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine ([NeoWD] = 10 μg ml−1) at 37 °C for 12 h, followed by washing with FACS buffer. In parallel, CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleen using a magnetic bead based negative selection method (MACS, Miltenyi Biotec) and fluorescently labeled with the CellTrace CFSE cell proliferation kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Treated BMDCs (1 × 105 cells ml−1) were then co-cultured with CFSE-labeled splenic CD8+ T cells at a ratio of 1:10 in round-bottom 48-well plates for 72 h. After culture, supernatants were collected and IFN-γ levels were quantified by ELISA following the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were washed with FACS buffer, incubated at room temperature with anti-CD16/32 antibody to block Fc receptors, and subsequently stained with anti-CD3 and anti-CD8a antibodies. CD8+ T-cell proliferation was finally analyzed by flow cytometry.

Animals

All animal procedures were performed in compliance with the protocol approved by the Fuzhou University Animal Care Committee (Approval No. Keshen 2026-SG-007). The Female C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from China Wushi, Inc. (Shanghai, China). All mice were housed in an SPF facility (12 h light/dark cycle, temperature 22 ± 2 °C, humidity 50 ± 10%) with free access to food and water, and were acclimatized for 7 days before the start of the experiment. The mean body weight of the mice at the start of the experiment was 21.5 ± 2.2 g (mean ± SD).

LN targeting, and biodistribution analysis

To evaluate LN accumulation, pharmacokinetics, and tissue distribution, CpG-2′F-Cy5/NeoWD nanovaccine (5 mg Kg−1 NeoWD), CpG-2′F-Cy5, or saline was injected subcutaneously at the base of the tail of C57BL/6 mice. For accumulation analysis, mice were euthanized 24 h post-injection, and draining LNs were collected (n = 4). For LN pharmacokinetic studies, LNs were harvested at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-injection (n = 3). Fluorescence imaging of LNs was performed using an in vivo optical imaging system (IVIS Lumina Series III, PerkinElmer). The fluorescence intensity was quantified using Living Image 4.1 software. In addition, major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys, were harvested from nanovaccine group at 24 h post-injectionfor ex vivo imaging to assess biodistribution (n = 3).

In vivo immune analysis of CpG-2′F/NeoWD

To assess immune responses induced by CpG-2′F/NeoWD, C57BL/6 mice were immunized subcutaneously at the tail base with CpG-2′F-Cy5/NeoWD nanovaccine (5 mg Kg−1 NeoWD), CpG-2′F-Cy5, or saline. On day 3 post-injection, spleens and LNs were harvested. Spleens were mechanically dissociated, passed through a 40 μm cell strainer, and red blood cells were lysed with ACK lysis buffer to obtain single-cell suspensions. LNs were mechanically minced and transferred into serum-free RPMI-1640 containing 1.5 mg mL−1 collagenase IV, 1.5 mg mL−1 hyaluronidase, and 0.2 mg mL−1 DNase I, followed by digestion at 37 °C for 45 min to liberate single cells. After digestion, the samples were filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to obtain purified single-cell suspensions. Cells were incubated with anti-CD16/32 antibody at 4 °C for 30 min to block Fc receptors, and subsequently stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (CD11c, CD80, and CD86) on ice for 30 min. Stained cells were washed with FACS buffer, resuspended, and analyzed using a FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer. TNF-α, IL-6 and IFN-γ from serum were determined by ELISA kits following the manufacturer's protocols.

For T cell exhaustion analysis, a pulsed dosing schedule was applied, in which mice received five injections at 2-day intervals. On day 7 after the final immunization, the expression of PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 on splenic CD3+ CD8+ T cells were assessed following the same procedures as described above.

In vivo antigen-specific cytotoxicity assay

C57BL/6 mice were immunized subcutaneously at the tail base with CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine (5 mg Kg−1 NeoWD), CpG-2′F + NeoWD, NeoWD, or saline. Seven days post-immunization, spleens were harvested and mechanically disrupted, followed by filtration through 40 μm cell strainers. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer to obtain single-cell suspensions in complete medium. Half of the splenocytes were treated with NeoWD peptide (10 μg ml−1) for 4 h at 37 °C. Untreated and NeoWD-incubated cells were then labeled with 0.5 and 5 μM CFSE dye, respectively, in serum-free medium for 15 min at 37 °C. CFSElow (NeoWD treated) and CFSEhigh (untreated) cells were mixed in equal numbers (2 × 106) and injected intravenously into the immunized mice. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected 4 h later and analyzed by flow cytometry. The numbers of CFSElow and CFSEhigh cells were measured. The percentage of NeoWD-specific killing was defined as [1 - number of CFSElow/number of CFSEhigh] × 100.

Long-term immune memory and hepatorenal safety of CpG-2′F/NeoWD

To concurrently evaluate the long-term immune memory induction, and potential hepatorenal toxicity of CpG-2′F/NeoWD, C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were randomly divided into groups and immunized by subcutaneous injection at the tail base. Mice were administered the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine (5 mg Kg−1 NeoWD), CpG-2′F + NeoWD, NeoWD, or saline, for a total of 5 immunizations at 2-day intervals. Twenty-one days after the final immunization, sera were collected for analysis of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (Scr). Spleens were also harvested for central memory T cell staining, which was performed as described in the section “In vivo immune analysis of CpG-2′F/NeoWD”.

In vivo evaluation of the prophylactic antitumor efficacy of the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine

To assess the preventive effect against tumor growth, female C57BL/6 mice (7 weeks old) were randomly assigned to assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group, total n = 20). The experimental unit was an individual mouse. Mice received either CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine (5 mg Kg−1 NeoWD), CpG-2′F + NeoWD, NeoWD, or saline via subcutaneous injection at the base of the tail. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence. No inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied; all mice were healthy and included in the analysis. The investigator performing tumor measurements was blinded to group allocation. Blinding was maintained throughout the experiment, including outcome assessment, and was only broken after data analysis was completed. Immunizations were administered 5 times at two-day intervals. Seven days after the final vaccination, mice were challenged subcutaneously in the right flank with 1 × 106 Hepa1–6 cells. Tumor growth was monitored using a vernier caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = length × width × width/2 (mm3). Mice were euthanized when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3 or at the end of the observation period (day 30 post-challenge). No animals were excluded from the study, and no adverse events were observed. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t-test, while one-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons among three or more groups. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and analyzed by the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

In vivo evaluation of the therapeutic antitumor efficacy of the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy against Hepa1–6 tumors, female C57BL/6 mice (7–8 weeks old at the start of the treatment phase) were used. The mean body weight at the start of the experiment was 21.5 ± 2.2 g (mean ± SD), with no significant differences among groups. Mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 106 Hepa1–6 cells in the right flank. Tumor growth was monitored regularly with a caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = length × width × width/2 (mm3). Once the average tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned into four groups (n = 5 per group, total n = 20). Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence. No inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied; all mice that developed tumors (∼50 mm3) were included. Mice with tumors failing to reach the threshold or with excessive ulceration/necrosis would have been excluded, but no such cases occurred. Each group was injected every two days with CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine (5 mg Kg−1 NeoWD), CpG-2′F + NeoWD, NeoWD, or saline. On day 27 after tumor inoculation, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were harvested for immunofluorescence staining of CD8 and GZMB. No animals were excluded from the study, and no adverse events were observed. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t-test, while one-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons among three or more groups. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Immunofluorescence staining results were not subjected to statistical analysis; only representative images are shown.

Histological analysis

For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, major organs were harvested from vaccinated C57BL/6 mice, fixed in 4% formalin at 4 °C for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned serially. For TUNEL assay, paraffin-embedded tumor sections were fixed and stained with fluorescently labeled dUTP according to the manufacturer's protocol. For immunofluorescence staining, tumor sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD8 or GZMB, followed by the appropriate fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibodies, according to the manufacturer's protocol. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a CLSM.

Results and discussion

Preparation, characterization and formation mechanism of CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine

The CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine was assembled by simply mixing CpG-2′F with the NeoWD (Scheme 1). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the formation of nanoparticles were obtained across a molar ratio range of 1:1 to 1:15 (CpG-2′F: NeoWD) (Fig. 1a and S1a). As the proportion of NeoWD increased, both particle size and surface potential declined gradually (Fig. S1b and S1c). Given that a size below 200 nm favors LNs accumulation and a low polydispersity index (PDI) was maintained (Fig. S1d), a molar ratio of 1:6.5 was selected for subsequent experiments. High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) elemental mapping confirmed the homogeneous co-localization of fluorine (F, derived from CpG-2′F), phosphorus (P, from the CpG-2′F backbone) and sulphur (S, from methionine residues in NeoWD) within the nanoparticles (Fig. 1b-c), providing direct evidence for the co-assembly of NeoWD with CpG-2′F. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed that the resulting nanoparticles had a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 69.04 nm (Fig. S2) and a zeta potential of −7.46 mV (Fig. S3). To quantify the antigen loading efficiency, we used fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled NeoWD. Notably, TEM and DLS results showed that FITC labeling did not noticeably alter the morphology or size distribution of the nanoparticles (Fig. S4). On this basis, the antigen loading efficiency was determined to be 67.18 wt%, corresponding to a NeoWD encapsulation efficiency of 69.86 wt% (Fig. S5). In addition, batch-to-batch reproducibility experiments further confirmed excellent consistency across independent preparations, validating the robustness of our synthesis protocol (Fig. S6). Stability assessments demonstrated that the nanoparticles remained stable in PBS for 7 days and maintained their size in medium containing 10% foetal bovine serum (10% FBS) for over 24 h (Fig. S7), satisfying the requirements for subsequent functional studies. Control experiments showed that only loose aggregates, rather than well-defined nanoparticles, were formed when unmodified CpG was mixed with NeoWD (Fig. S8). This indicates that fluorination is critical for the formation of stable nanostructures. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations further revealed that fluorination enhanced the interactions between CpG-2′F and the amino acid residues of NeoWD. This interaction facilitated the incorporation of more NeoWD peptides onto the CpG-2′F chains, promoting the formation of supramolecular chain-like structures. These structures spontaneously assembled into nanoparticles through and entanglement (Fig. 1d). Collectively, these results demonstrate the construction chain stacking of stable CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanoparticles via a fluorination-induced self-assembly strategy. Their high antigen loading capacity and excellent stability provide a robust foundation for eliciting efficient immune responses both in vitro and in vivo.

In vitro DCs uptake and activation by CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine

The immunological function of the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine was further evaluated. First, cytotoxicity assays revealed no significant toxicity against bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) (Fig. S9), establishing a safety profile for subsequent immunological studies. Efficient uptake by APCs is a critical initial step for initiating adaptive immune responses. To this end, the intracellular fate of the antigen was tracked using FITC-labeled CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) demonstrated that the nanovaccine was efficiently internalized by BMDCs after 3 h of co-incubation (Fig. 2). After 6 h, only weak fluorescence signals were observed in the free NeoWD-FITC group and its physical mixture with CpG, whereas the nanovaccine group exhibited stronger green fluorescence (Fig. S10 and Fig. 2). Notably, at this time point, the Pearson's correlation coefficient (PCC) between the NeoWD-FITC and lysosomes (red; Lysotracker) was 0.486 (Fig. S11), below the empirical threshold for lysosomal colocalization (>0.5), indicating successful neoantigen escape from lysosomes into the cytoplasm. As the incubation time extended to 12 h and 24 h, persistent green fluorescence signals were observed outside the lysosomes (Fig. 2), and this lack of colocalization was further supported by the PCC, which decreased to 0.345 at 24 h (Fig. S11). This phenomenon is attributed to the endosomal/lysosomal escape capacity conferred by fluorination, consistent with previous reports (Lv et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2020). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine achieves efficient cytoplasmic delivery of the neoantigen. The creates favorable conditions for antigen cross-presentation via the MHC pathway and thereby establishing a foundation for the activation of CD8+ T cell responses.

To further evaluate the immunostimulatory capacity of the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine, immature BMDCs were incubated with the formulation for 24 h, and the expression of co-stimulatory and MHC molecules was analyzed by flow cytometry. Compared with the PBS (as blank), NeoWD, CpG-2′F, or CpG-2′F + NeoWD, treatment with the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine most effectively upregulated the expression of CD80 and CD86 on BMDCs (Fig. 3a-b). In addition, CD40, a key co-stimulatory molecule associated with DC maturation (Fig. 3c), and MHC-II molecules, which are directly involved in antigen presentation, were also increased (Fig. 3d), indicating potent induction of BMDC maturation by the nanovaccine. On this basis, the cross-presentation efficiency of BMDCs after nanovaccine treatment was assessed. It should be noted that there is no commercially available antibody for NeoWD, therefore, the OVA257–280 was employed as a model antigen, and a CpG-2′F/OVA257–280 nanovaccine was prepared using the same approach (Fig. S12). Subsequent CLSM revealed that the uptake of CpG-2′F/OVA257–280 by BMDCs was comparable to that of CpG-2′F/NeoWD, and no significant difference was observed in the levels of MHC-II expression induced by the two nanovaccines, thereby confirming the consistency of the two nanovaccines in antigen delivery and MHC-II presentation (Fig. S13). On this basis, an antibody specific for the H-2Kᵇ/SIINFEKL complex was used to detect the presentation of the OVA peptide on MHC-I molecules, as an indirect measure of NeoWD cross-presentation efficiency. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that BMDCs treated with CpG-2′F/OVA257–280 exhibited the highest surface levels of SIINFEKL peptide, reaching 2.05-fold higher than those of the OVA257–280 group (Fig. 3e-f), confirming that the nanovaccine enhances antigen cross-presentation. Moreover, the CpG-2′F/OVA257–280 nanovaccine achieved higher antigen presentation efficiency than the liposome-loaded CpG-2′F + OVA257–280 mixture, demonstrating the superiority of the carrier-free assembly system in promoting antigen presentation (Fig. S14). Furthermore, cytokine levels in the culture supernatants were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The nanovaccine group induced higher secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which are closely associated with cellular immunity, compared to other groups (Fig. 3g-h). To further examine the direct interaction between the nanoparticles and macrophages, we co-incubated the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine with bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). The nanovaccine drove BMDMs polarization toward an M1 phenotype (Fig. S15), indicating that it promotes M1 polarization through direct cell contact rather than relying solely on secondary cytokine signals from activated DCs. Moreover, BMDCs pretreated with CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine promoted CD8+ T cell proliferation and upregulated their interferon-γ (IFN-γ) secretion (Fig. S16). This indicates that CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine could effectively activate BMDCs and trigger subsequent NeoWD-specific T cell responses.

Therefore, these results demonstrate that the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine not only enables efficient uptake by APCs and cytosolic delivery of neoantigens, but also promotes BMDCs maturation, enhances antigen cross-presentation and induces the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In addition, it drives macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, and elicits neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses. Collectively, these properties establish a foundation for eliciting potent and durable antitumor immune responses in vivo.

In Vivo LNs targeting, and induction of systemic cytotoxic activity

Given its in vitro performance, the LN targeting ability and metabolic distribution of the nanovaccine were further evaluated in vivo. For in vivo tracking, Cy5-labeled CpG-2′F/NeoWD (CpG-2′F-Cy5/NeoWD) was prepared. Its particle size distribution and stability were comparable to those of unlabeled CpG-2′F/NeoWD (Fig. S17), confirming that the Cy5 labeling did not alter key properties. Subsequently, following subcutaneous administration at the tail base of saline (as blank), CpG-2′F-Cy5, or CpG-2′F-Cy5/NeoWD nanovaccine in mice, major organs and inguinal draining LNs were collected for fluorescence imaging after 24 h (Fig. 4a). Imaging revealed that the fluorescence signal in LNs was higher in the CpG-2′F-Cy5/NeoWD nanovaccine group than that of CpG-2′F-Cy5 (Fig. 4b). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that the nanovaccine achieved 12.45-fold higher accumulation in LNs relative to the CpG-2′F-Cy5 group in LNs (Fig. 4c), indicating that the nanovaccine efficiently promotes neoantigen enrichment in LNs. In addition, imaging and quantification of major organs showed that the nanovaccine was mainly metabolized through the liver and kidneys (Fig. 4d-e). Collectively, these results indicated that the nanovaccine possesses LNs targeting delivery capacity, a critical advantage for eliciting antigen-specific adaptive immune responses and enhancing vaccine efficacy, thereby establishing a foundation for its immunostimulatory activity in vivo.

To evaluate the immune activation of the nanovaccine in vivo, LNs were collected from immunized mice on day 3 post-immunization and analyzed by flow cytometry. In the LNs, a higher frequency of CD11c+ DCs expressing the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 was observed in the nanovaccine group compared to other groups (Fig. 4f-g), indicating that the nanovaccine effectively promotes DCs maturation in vivo.

Consistently, analysis of splenic immune cells revealed increased proportions of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in mice immunized with the nanovaccine (Fig. 5a-b and S18), suggesting activation of adaptive immune responses and potential expansion of antigen-specific T cells. To further assess antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses, mice were immunized with the CpG-2′F/OVA257–280 nanovaccine, and their spleens were collected for analysis. Flow cytometry using SIINFEKL-MHC-I tetramers showed that the frequency of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells were 2.22-fold higher than that of CpG-2′F + OVA257–280 (Fig. 5c and S19), confirming effective expansion of antigen-specific T cells. Furthermore, mice treated with CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine displayed an increase in effector memory T cells (Tem; CD3+ CD8+ CD44+ CD62L−) (Fig. 5d), indicating that the nanovaccine not only activates immediate effector T cell responses but also promotes the establishment of immunological memory. Together, these results demonstrate that the nanovaccine efficiently promotes DC maturation and antigen presentation in LNs, thereby driving T cell expansion and activating antigen-specific T cell responses. Moreover, the concomitant induction of effector memory T cells further lays the foundation for sustained immune surveillance and durable antitumor protection.

Subsequently, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages (CD86+ in CD11b+ F4/80+), which are associated with DC maturation, were analyzed in the spleen. As shown in Fig. S20, the proportion of M1 macrophages was higher in the nanovaccine group than in other groups, indicating that the nanovaccine promotes macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Concurrently, a marked increase in the number of natural killer (NK) cells (CD3− NK1.1+) was observed in the nanovaccine group (Fig. 5e). These results demonstrate that the nanovaccine effectively enhances innate immune responses in vivo. Furthermore, the number of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs; CD11b+ Gr-1+) was reduced in the nanovaccine group, suggesting that the nanovaccine alleviates immunosuppression (Fig. S21). Cytokine analysis revealed that nanovaccine treatment significantly enhanced the secretion of TNF-α (Fig. 5f), a cytokine that promotes APC maturation and the establishment of an inflammatory microenvironment (Iberg et al., 2022). Elevated levels of IL-6 were also detected (Fig. 5g), a cytokine involved in T cell recruitment and differentiation, and known to suppress regulatory T cell (Treg) function (Yang et al., 2016). Notably, the highest serum concentration of IFN-γ, a key effector cytokine mediating cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses (Kowalczyk et al., 2003), was observed in the nanovaccine group (Fig. 5h), further corroborating the potent activation of adaptive immunity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the nanovaccine increases the proportions of M1 macrophages and NK cells, reduces MDSC levels, and promotes the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby enhancing innate immunity and reprogramming the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Upon secondary exposure to the same antigen, antigen-specific T cells typically mount more rapid and robust responses. To directly assess T cell functionality following vaccination, we performed carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-based cytotoxicity assays using splenocytes from immunized mice. Among the tested formulations, the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine induced the strongest NeoWD-specific cytotoxic activity, with approximately 77.8% target cell killing (Fig. 5i-j). These results demonstrate that the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine effectively activates NeoWD-specific T cell responses in vivo.

Beyond confirming its short-term antitumor immunity, we further evaluated its capacity to generate long-lasting immune memory and its potential liver and kidney toxicity. At 21 days after the last administration, serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (Scr) all remained within the normal physiological range (Fig. S22a-e), indicating that the five-dose regimen did not cause hepatic or renal injury. At the same time point, an increased proportion of central memory T cells (Tcm, CD3+ CD8+ CD62L+ CD44+) was observed in the spleens of the CpG-2′F/NeoWD group (Fig. S22f-g), suggesting that this nanovaccine can effectively establish long-term immune memory. Thus, the nanovaccine was able to build durable immune protection without delayed liver or kidney toxicity, demonstrating a favorable long-term safety profile.

In summary, these results demonstrate that the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine facilitates efficient targeting to LNs, where it promotes DC maturation and antigen presentation. This targeted delivery orchestrates a broad immune reprogramming, characterized by M1 macrophage polarization, NK cell activation, reduction of immunosuppressive MDSCs, and robust pro-inflammatory cytokine production, that ultimately drives the expansion of neoantigen-specific cytotoxic T cells and the establishment of immunological memory. Consequently, this coordinated cellular and humoral response translates into potent in vivo cytotoxicity, achieving ∼77.8% antigen-specific killing, while exhibiting a favorable long-term safety profile without delayed hepatic or renal toxicity, and underscoring the nanovaccine's capacity to elicit robust antitumor immunity.

CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine prevents Hepa1–6 tumor growth

Encouraged by the preceding in vitro and in vivo results, the prophylactic antitumor efficacy of the nanovaccine was further evaluated in a Hepa1–6 hepatocellular carcinoma xenograft model. To establish an appropriate dosing schedule, LNs pharmacokinetics was analyzed. After a single tail base injection, the nanovaccine reached peak accumulation in the LNs at 24 h, the signal decayed by approximately 50% by 72 h and was largely cleared by 96 h (Fig. S23). Based on this kinetic profile, a pulsed dosing schedule of five injections at 2-day intervals was designed. This schedule was intended to sustain immune activation while avoiding T cell exhaustion that could otherwise result from continuous antigen exposure. To assess the safety of this schedule, the expression of immune checkpoint molecules (PD-1+, TIM-3+ and LAG-3+) on splenic CD8+ T cells was analyzed 7 days after the final immunization. None of these inhibitory receptors were upregulated in the nanovaccine group (Fig. S24), confirming that the immunization schedule did not induce T-cell exhaustion. With its safety thus validated, this schedule was adopted for the prophylactic experiment. The experimental timeline is illustrated in Fig. 6a. C57BL/6 mice were immunized five times with either the nanovaccine or control formulations at 2-day intervals, followed by subcutaneous inoculation of Hepa1–6 tumor cells into the right flank. By day 7 post-tumor inoculation, all control mice had grown tumors (100%), whereas trace of tumors were found in the nanovaccine immunized group until day 11 (Fig. 6b-c). Compared with mice treated with NeoWD or CpG-2′F + NeoWD group, those receiving the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine exhibited suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 6b) and prolonged survival (Fig. 6d). To further characterize the observed tumor growth inhibition, apoptosis in tumor tissues was assessed by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining. A substantial increase in apoptotic cells was observed in the CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine group (Fig. 6e), consistent with its antitumor efficacy. In addition, histological analysis of major organs, including liver, spleen, kidney, heart and lung, by Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no obvious tissue damage or pathological alterations, indicating that the nanovaccine is safe (Fig. S25). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the nanovaccine effectively suppresses HCC growth with minimal adverse effects on healthy organs, its potential as a prophylactic cancer nanovaccine.

CpG-2′F/NeoWD Nanovaccine Hinders Hepa1–6 Tumor Growth

Given the excellent efficacy observed in the Hepa1–6 prevention model, the therapeutic antitumor activity was further evaluated in a Hepa1–6 tumor-bearing mice model. Mice were randomized into four groups when the average volume of subcutaneous tumors reached approximately 50 mm3. Saline (as blank), NeoWD, CpG-2′F + NeoWD, or CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine was administered via tail base injection (Fig. 7a). As shown in Fig. 7b, no tumor inhibition was observed with NeoWD. The CpG-2′F + NeoWD group exhibited only weak antitumor activity, yielding a tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rate of 30.07%. In contrast, CpG-2′F/NeoWD group displayed enhanced in vivo efficacy, with a TGI of 63.97%. No significant body weight loss or notable adverse effects were observed in any group during treatment (Fig. 7c), indicating that all treatments were well tolerated. To further explore the immune response within the tumor microenvironment, tumors were excised on day 27 for immune cell analysis. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that tumors from the CpG-2′F/NeoWD group exhibited increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and the highest granzyme B (GZMB) levels, suggesting the strongest T cell-mediated antitumor activity (Fig. 7d). These results demonstrate that this nanovaccine effectively promotes the infiltration of effector CD8+ T cells into the tumor and enhances their effector function, thereby driving the conversion of “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the nanovaccine elicited a CTL-mediated antitumor immune response, suppressed HCC growth, and was well tolerated, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic cancer nanovaccine.

Conclusions

In summary, to address the critical bottlenecks associated with traditional carrier-based nanovaccines in immunotherapy for HCC, particularly low antigen loading efficiency and carrier-mediated interference with immunogenicity, a “two-in-one” carrier-free neoantigen nanovaccine (CpG-2′F/NeoWD) was constructed based on a fluorination-driven self-assembly strategy. By leveraging fluorinated CpG to induce the direct co-assembly of the CpG adjuvant with the NeoWD neoantigen peptide, this system achieved a high antigen loading efficiency of 67.18 wt% and excellent stability, thereby eliminating the biological interference associated with exogenous carriers. Mechanistically, the nanovaccine efficiently targeted LNs and facilitated endosomal/lysosomal escape of the neoantigen, thereby enhancing antigen cross-presentation efficiency and promoting the release of inflammatory cytokines. This led to the synergistic activation of innate immunity (NK cells and M1 macrophages) and adaptive immunity (antigen-specific CD8+ T cells), as well as the induction of effector memory T cells, establishing durable immune protection. In a Hepa1–6 prophylactic tumor model, the nanovaccine suppressed tumor progression, prolonged mouse survival, and exhibited a favorable biosafety profile. In the therapeutic model, a CTL-mediated antitumor immune response was elicited by CpG-2′F/NeoWD nanovaccine, and HCC growth was suppressed. Overall, this study not only provides a novel material-based strategy for potentiating neoantigen immunogenicity but also offers a promising technological avenue for HCC immunotherapy in high-risk populations.
