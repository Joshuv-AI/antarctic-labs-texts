HIV self-testing uptake and associated factors among adolescent girls and young women in certificate and diploma colleges, Dodoma, Tanzania: a cross-sectional study
Authors: David J Osima, Ally Kassim Hussein, Mackfallen G Anasel, Billy Ngasala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo determine the prevalence and factors associated with the uptake of HIV self-testing (HIVST) among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) attending middle learning institutions in Dodoma City, Tanzania, in 2024.Design: A cross-sectional study design was employed.SettingThe study was conducted in five randomly selected colleges in Dodoma City, Tanzania.ParticipantsA total of 771 female students aged 15–24 years who provided informed consent were enrolled. Background

HIV self-testing (HIVST) is a process in which an individual collects their own specimen (typically oral fluid), performs an HIV test and interprets the result, often in a private setting and either alone or with someone they trust.1 In 2019, adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) aged 15–24 years accounted for 48% of new HIV infections globally and 59% in sub-Saharan Africa.2 3 Global efforts have successfully reduced the proportion of people living with HIV (PLHIV) who are unaware of their status from 46% in 2015 to 16% in 2020. However, progress towards achieving the first UNAIDS 95 target, ensuring that 95% of PLHIV know their status, has been slower than improvements in other areas of the HIV care continuum.4

Adolescents and young people represent an increasing proportion of individuals living with HIV worldwide.5 In 2020 alone, approximately 410 000 individuals aged 10–24 years acquired new HIV infections, including 150 000 adolescents aged 10–19 years.5 In Tanzania, new HIV infections remain a persistent concern. Data from the Tanzania HIV Impact Surveys (THIS) of 2017–2018 and 2022–2023 revealed that approximately 12 000 people continue to acquire new infections annually.6 7 In response, HIVST was introduced, initially targeting key populations and later extended to the general population, as part of national strategies to strengthen HIV testing services and accelerate progress towards HIV prevention and control by 2030.2

The uptake of HIVST in Tanzania has varied considerably across studies and regions. Among women of reproductive age, uptake was reported at 3.2%, while a similar study among men found an uptake rate of 3.9%.8 9 In contrast, a pilot study conducted in rural communities reported a significantly higher uptake of 97.6%. Similarly, a study in Dar es Salaam using peer-led and nurse-led distribution strategies among men achieved an uptake of 98.3%.10 11

HIVST represents an innovative and effective strategy for increasing HIV testing coverage by providing a convenient and confidential option that reaches underserved populations and enhances health system efficiency.12,17 Evidence shows that HIVST is more effective in improving testing uptake compared with provider-initiated testing and counselling and voluntary counselling and testing approaches.18,20

HIV infection rates remain disproportionately higher among AGYW compared with their male counterparts. In Sub-Saharan Africa, AGYW aged 15–24 years are three times more likely to contract HIV than males of the same age, with a new infection occurring approximately every 3 min.6 21 HIVST offers a promising approach to reducing these inequalities by addressing gender-based and age-related barriers to testing, thereby improving testing uptake and reducing transmission among this vulnerable group.3 4 21 22

Despite the national rollout of HIVST in 2019, the extent of its utilisation, as well as the determinants and barriers influencing its uptake among Tanzanian AGYW, remains inadequately explored. Understanding these factors is essential for informing the National AIDS, STIs and Hepatitis Control Programme (NASHCoP) in its efforts to scale up HIVST implementation and address barriers among key subgroups. Strengthening uptake in this population is crucial for reducing HIV transmission rates and achieving the first UNAIDS 95 target by 2030.

Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with the uptake of HIVST among AGYW enrolled in certificate and diploma colleges in Dodoma City, Tanzania, in 2024.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted in Dodoma City, Tanzania. Dodoma has experienced rapid population growth following the relocation of government offices from Dar es Salaam, which has accelerated migration and urbanisation. This demographic shift has increased the vulnerability of adolescents and young women to HIV infection, as economic pressures, expanding social networks and limited access to tailored HIV prevention services heighten the risk of unsafe sexual practices and hinder effective prevention efforts. Dodoma City has an estimated population of 765 179 people.23

The city hosts ten middle learning colleges: Mtumba Teachers’ College, Local Government Training Institute (LGTI) Dodoma, LGTI Hombolo, Dodoma Polytechnic of Energy and Earth Resources Management (Madini Institute), Dodoma Media College, Decca College of Health and Allied Sciences, College of Business Education, City College of Health and Allied Sciences, Capital Teachers’ College, and the Institute of Rural Development Planning.

Study population

The study population comprised female students aged 15–24 years enrolled in five colleges randomly selected from the ten listed institutions in Dodoma City. The selected colleges were Mtumba Teachers’ College, City College of Health and Allied Sciences, Institute of Rural Development Planning, LGTI Hombolo and Decca College of Health and Allied Sciences.

Sample size

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula by Kish and Leslie, assuming a prevalence of 50% based on a previous study among higher education students in Dodoma City.24 After adjusting for a design effect of 2, the minimum required sample size was 768 participants.24

Sample selection procedures

A two-stage random sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, a random number generator was used to select five colleges from the ten eligible institutions. In the second stage, a probability proportional to size approach determined the number of participants to be drawn from each selected college. Within each college, three classes were randomly selected. Female students in these classes were listed, assigned identification numbers and selected using a random number generator.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were AGYW aged 15–24 years who were enrolled in the selected colleges and provided informed consent. Students who declined to participate were excluded. Participants identified as medically unfit were also excluded, as confirmed by the college matron through documentation of a diagnosed illness preventing participation. In this study, ‘sick’ referred to students with a confirmed medical condition that rendered them unfit to take part.

Outcome variables

The outcome variable was HIVST uptake, categorised as ‘yes’ or ‘no’, measured as period prevalence.

Independent variables

Independent variables included socio-demographic factors (faculty category, marital status, residence and address) and behavioural factors (availability of HIVST kits, history of sexually transmitted infections, multiple sexual partners, alcohol use, drug injection and previous HIV testing). Potential barriers to HIV self-testing (HIVST) uptake were also examined, including test cost, limited awareness of where to obtain kits, low perceived risk of HIV infection, fear of judgement by healthcare providers (HCPs), fear of results or testing procedures, concerns about confidentiality, cultural resistance, gender norms and perceived reliability of HIVST kits.

Operational definition

Faculty category refers to classification based on the participant’s course of study, grouped into health-related and non-health-related programmes.

Data collection procedures and tools

Data collection was conducted by the principal investigator and two trained research assistants, each holding at least a bachelor’s degree. Training on research ethics and study procedures lasted 1 day, followed by a 1 day pre-test of the questionnaire. Data collection took place over 14 days in April 2024.

Class representatives assisted in organising sessions and scheduling data collection at convenient times. Selected participants met in available lecture halls, where the purpose of the study was explained, informed consent was obtained and structured questionnaires were administered. The questionnaire, originally paper-based, was digitised using Kobo Toolbox. Password-protected electronic forms were distributed to participants, who completed them independently using personal devices.

Data management and analysis

Data were submitted through the Kobo Collect platform to the researcher’s secure account. Mandatory response fields were built into the form to minimise missing data. Completed datasets were downloaded in Excel format, cleaned and imported into Stata version 15.1 for analysis.

Continuous variables were summarised as means with SD, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages in tables and charts. Barriers to HIVST uptake were visualised using bar graphs.

Associations between independent variables and HIVST uptake were examined using a modified Poisson regression model with robust standard errors. Bivariate analyses were first conducted using X2 tests; variables with p<0.20 were included in the multivariable model, accounting for model fit.25 26 Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) and 95% CIs were calculated, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences Research and Ethics Committee (Ref No. DA 282/298/01.C/2055). Additional permission was granted by the principals or rectors of participating colleges. The study’s purpose, potential risks and benefits were clearly explained before data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from adult participants and emancipated minors. To maintain confidentiality, unique identification numbers were used instead of names, and all electronic data were stored on a password-protected computer.

Patient and public involvement statement

Public involvement was incorporated during the planning phase. Class representatives from selected colleges were consulted to support recruitment, scheduling and logistics in ways that accommodated academic timetables. While the research question, focused on HIVST uptake among AGYW, was guided by existing evidence, it was informed by informal input from students and faculty who identified stigma, cost and accessibility as key barriers. These insights shaped questionnaire design and variable selection.

Students also contributed to logistical arrangements during fieldwork, including organising spaces and encouraging peer participation. Although participants were not involved in data analysis or interpretation, plans are in place to disseminate findings to participating colleges through presentations and simplified reports. Class representatives will assist in identifying the most effective formats for sharing results to ensure accessibility and community engagement.

The Central Zone, which includes Dodoma, Manyara and Singida regions, had the largest representation, 291 (37.74%). A majority of participants, 472 (61.22%), were enrolled in non-health-related programmes. Identified barriers to HIVST among AGYW in middle learning institutions in Dodoma City, Tanzania, 2024

Among the 411 participants (53.31%) who had never self-tested for HIV, several barriers were reported. Because participants could select multiple reasons, percentages exceed 100%. Barriers were analysed collectively for the 15–24 year age range rather than by age subgroup.

Individual barriers

Key personal barriers included fear of testing procedures (n=291; 70.8%), concerns regarding test reliability (n=286; 69.6%) and fear of receiving a positive result (n=283; 68.9%). Additionally, limited awareness of HIVST was reported by 193 participants (47.0%), while 73 (17.8%) did not perceive themselves to be at risk for HIV. These findings underscore the impact of limited knowledge, fear and low risk perception on testing behaviour.

Social barriers

Social influences strongly deterred HIVST uptake. Cultural resistance was cited by 392 participants (95.4%), fear of judgement by HCPs by 372 (90.5%) and fear of others discovering one’s testing behaviour by 273 (66.4%). These findings highlight the persistent role of stigma and social norms in shaping testing decisions.

Structural barriers

Systemic challenges also hindered access. Legal restrictions preventing HCPs from distributing test kits were cited by 360 participants (87.6%), while 280 (68.1%) mentioned poor accessibility to HIVST kits. Such constraints point to structural and policy barriers that limit the reach of HIV self-testing services (online supplemental figure 1).

Factors associated with HIVST uptake among AGYW in middle learning institutions in Dodoma city, Tanzania, 2024

After adjusting for potential confounders, several factors remained significantly associated with HIVST uptake.

Participants enrolled in health-related courses and those reporting multiple sexual partners were more likely to have self-tested for HIV (aPR 1.14; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.27 and aPR 1.23; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.45, respectively). The availability of HIVST kits (aPR 2.83; 95% CI 2.21 to 3.62) and prior testing experience (aPR 2.65; 95% CI 2.05 to 3.43) were the strongest predictors of uptake. Participants who perceived themselves to be at risk of HIV infection were also more likely to have self-tested (aPR 1.29; 95% CI 1.11 to 1.50).

For variables showing 100% row percentages, the denominators were limited to the 360 respondents who reported HIVST, indicating no bias rather than true universal uptake. Although some small subgroups (eg, injecting drug use) yielded crude prevalence ratios above 2.0, these associations did not remain statistically significant after multivariable adjustment.

The overall sample of 771 participants provided adequate statistical power, and the observed associations were robust across variables with larger subgroup sizes (such as faculty category, kit accessibility, multiple sexual partners, perceived risk and prior testing experience). These consistent findings strengthen the generalisability of the study results to the broader student population (table 3).

Discussion

This study found an HIVST uptake rate of 46.7% among AGYW in Dodoma City, Tanzania. Uptake remains below the UNAIDS target, which recommends that at least 95% of PLHIV should know their status. The relatively low utilisation observed may be attributed to limited awareness and restricted availability of HIVST kits within the study area. Barriers included cultural resistance, fear of judgement from HCPs, legal restrictions, concerns about testing procedures and results, and limited accessibility to HIVST kits. The least reported barrier was a low perception of HIV risk.

Prevalence of HIVST uptake

HIVST uptake among participants is low, reflecting limited use. The low uptake based on UNAIDS means that the community has to be aware of their HIV status by at least 95%.21 This low rate is likely due to the scarcity of self-testing kits and insufficient awareness. Improving awareness and accessibility is essential to boost utilisation. While other HIV testing options exist, increasing awareness of self-testing is key for better HIV knowledge and transmission reduction. The intervention is well received by AGYW, and enhancing access could further increase use, helping individuals learn their status and reduce transmission. This contrasts with studies in Uganda with 66% and Rwanda with 55,4% of HIVST uptake.27,29 Studies in South Africa and other African countries show high HIVST uptake of 80% among youth, driven by peer invitations and the ability to report results privately. Key factors include campus distribution, confidentiality, community outreach, free availability and access through local pharmacies.30,39

Socio-cultural and structural barriers

Cultural resistance due to male-dominant decisions and limited autonomy in health decisions among women in society. Different studies explained the effect of cultural norms on how they affect females in accessing HIV testing services, such as fear of being judged, judgement from parents and guardians that if they seek the HIV services, it seems like they have started a sexual relationship, which is not acceptable based on their age.40 41 Cultural taboos stated by another study that females cannot make their own decisions, which limits their engagement with HIV testing services.42 43

Judgemental attitudes among HCPs also deter AGYW from accessing HIV testing. Consistent with a study in Lusaka, Zambia, some HCPs reportedly used fear-based messaging, warning youth that HIV infection equates to certain death, further reinforcing stigma and fear.44 Such attitudes have been documented across Africa, including Tanzania, highlighting persistent gaps in youth-friendly and non-judgmental care.45,49

Legal and policy-related barriers were also identified. Despite the Tanzanian government’s 2019 policy revision permitting adolescents aged 15 years and older to access HIV testing without parental consent, findings suggest that awareness of this policy among healthcare workers remains inadequate. Evidence from other Sub-Saharan African countries shows that the age of consent for HIV testing can be as low as 12 years, indicating room for policy dissemination and operational strengthening.50,54

Psychological and individual-level barriers

Fear of the testing process, anxiety about results and concerns about test reliability were common among participants. These findings align with studies from Uganda, where fear of positive results and mistrust of self-test accuracy limited uptake55 in Kenya.56 The unreliability of the study was discussed in a study done in Africa, where people fear the result after using the HIVST kit.57 Conversely, in South Africa—where HIVST kits are freely distributed through pharmacies and universities—improved access and familiarity have increased confidence in testing outcomes.58 59

Determinants of HIVST uptake

Factors influencing HIVST uptake among AGYW include having multiple sexual partners, which act as risky sexual behaviours prompting the AGYW to seek the HIVST to ascertain their HIV status privately. Not only multiple sexual partners but also other sexual activities like heterosexual activity increase the risk, which led to the HIVST uptake study done in Colombia,60 supported by the study done in China.61

Participants studying health-related courses were associated with HIVST uptake, likely due to directly acquired knowledge in their colleges compared with their counterparts not taking the health-related courses. The health-related courses, like nurses, clinical officers and pharmacists, were among the participants in this study. Those who do not undertake the health-related courses, like teachers, human resources officers and community development officers, among these two groups, there are knowledge disparities, which may lead to high uptake among those with acquired HIVST awareness around their study area and low uptake among their fellows. Likely stated by the study done in North Carolina, the proper use and awareness of post-test support influence the uptake of HIV self-testing services62 as well as in South Africa.63 64

The availability of HIVST kits also influenced uptake, despite the HIVST kits being centralised in the facility and distributed by other stakeholders to the key vulnerable population still showing statistically significant increases in intervention uptake compared with other countries that have different modalities of HIVST kit distribution, which made the wide availability of the kits around the population. Aligning with the study done in Nigeria among youth, if the kits are available and easily accessible, they may facilitate the uptake of HIVST services,65 similar to the study done in the Philippines bringing the test closer.66 Compared with another study done in Sub-Saharan Africa, the cost of the kit was a barrier to HIVST among the youth.67

Other factors included participants’ testing experiences. A similar study done in Kasese district revealed that those with testing experience who test more than twice a year are more likely to test.68 Because those who have never been tested face other challenges, of being unaware of the testing sites.43 Participants’ experiences at testing sites that facilitated self-initiated HIV testing. The relevant findings were the privacy of testing sites and test results and being able to access healthcare services in addition to HIV testing.69

Feeling at risk of HIV infection, these two factors prompted the participants to test themselves. The participants may be exposed to different exposures during their lifetime, time as having a relationship with an HIV-positive partner, having a needle pricking history, sharing sharp objects with known HIV-positive individuals and being involved in car accidents; all these circumstances may lead to HIVST to know their HIV status. Supported by the study done in Zimbabwe, those clients who reported not being at risk were not willing to test.43 A similar study in Tanzania reported that most adolescents aged 15–19 years did not consider themselves at risk due to no sexual behaviours and therefore never had an HIV test.70 In the USA, a study revealed that those perceived at risk of STD/HIV were more likely to have HIVST.71 In contrast with other studies that have been shown, these factors are associated with HIVST uptake among AGYW.2935 36 72,78

Implications for public health and policy

The Ministry of Health, through the NASHCoP, needs to scale up HIVST among AGYW to achieve the recommended target of at least 95% coverage. Efforts should focus on increasing awareness of HIVST within sub-groups through targeted health promotion strategies, aiming to enhance trust in the reliability of the test and address fears related to the procedure and potential results. The Ministry of Health, through NASHCoP, should disseminate the legal policy to HCPs to raise their awareness about offering HIV testing services to individuals aged 15 years and above. This age group is legally permitted to provide consent for testing on their own, not only those aged 18 years and above.

To improve accessibility, the Ministry, with support from NASHCoP, should implement diverse distribution methods, such as through pharmacies, and educational institutions like campuses, and ensure that HIVST kits are provided free of charge.

Furthermore, in collaboration with the President’s Office—Regional Administration and Local Government, the Ministry should strengthen youth-friendly services in health facilities by promoting respectful and supportive care from HCPs and offering comprehensive counselling tailored to adolescents’ needs regarding HIV testing services.

Limitations of the study

The study faced several limitations, including response and social desirability biases. These were mitigated by ensuring participant privacy and providing clear information about the study’s purpose. Another limitation was the absence of comparative analysis by age group—for instance, whether certain barriers were more prevalent among participants aged 15–19 years compared with those aged 20–24 years. Additionally, the study did not account for potential confounding factors such as the level of education, place of residence (urban vs rural) and income level, all of which may influence or hinder the uptake of HIVST. The lack of data on participants’ years of study and the inability to establish temporal relationships between factors and outcomes further highlight the need for future research to better understand HIVST uptake among AGYW. Finally, the findings related to factors such as alcohol use, injection drug use and history of sexually transmitted infections may have limited generalisability due to their small representation among participants.

Conclusion

HIVST uptake among AGYW in Dodoma City was very low, below 95%, of the overall testing rates of PLWHIV. Factors influencing uptake include multiple sexual partners, health-related studies, kit availability and perceived risk. Barriers such as cultural resistance, provider judgement, legal issues and fears about testing hinder usage. Addressing these barriers and improving accessibility is crucial for better health outcomes, given AGYW’s high risk of HIV infection.
