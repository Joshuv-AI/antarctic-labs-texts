Exploration of the efficacy of eucalyptus oil (micro-capsules) and mangosteen extract against Eimeria tenella infection in chickens
Authors: Qianxian Xia, Shengjie Weng, Kaiyuan Li, Jieyu Shi, Jingui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Due to the increasing prevalence of drug-resistant Eimeria strains, there is an urgent need to develop novel, eco-friendly, and safe alternatives for controlling chicken coccidiosis. This study aimed to investigate the combined anticoccidial efficacy of eucalyptus oil (EO) and mangosteen extract (ME) and develop an advanced delivery system via microencapsulation to enhance their therapeutic potential. In a broiler chick model of Eimeria tenella (E. tenella) infection, the combination of EO (100 mg/kg body weight, oral gavage) and ME (250 mg/kg feed) exerted the optimal effect against E. tenella with an anticoccidial index (ACI) of 169, indicating moderate anticoccidial activity. This combined efficacy appears to be multifaceted. It may involve (i) preservation of intestinal barrier integrity, (ii) suppression of oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade, and (iii) restoration of cecal microbiota homeostasis. EO exhibits poor water solubility, posing challenges for its clinical administration. To address this, the sodium alginate-calcium chloride (CaCl₂) cross-linking method was used to prepare eucalyptus oil microcapsules (EOM). The results showed that the combined in-feed administration of EOM (50 mg/kg feed) and ME (250 mg/kg feed) yielded an ACI of 171. Furthermore, this treatment regimen alleviated cecal tissue damage and mitigated the inflammatory response. In conclusion, the combination of EO and ME may represent a potent, multi-target natural therapy for coccidiosis, and microencapsulation is a viable strategy to facilitate its practical application as a novel feed additive.

Introduction

Chicken coccidiosis, a significant intestinal parasitic disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Eimeria, causes global annual losses exceeding $13.75 billion in the poultry industry (Blake et al., 2020). Among the seven Eimeria spp. that infect chickens, Eimeria tenella (E.tenella) is well-known for its high virulence. E.tenella only infects the cecal epithelium, inducing severe hemorrhagic typhlitis that results in weight loss, bloody stools, cecal lesions, and death in severe cases (Gao et al., 2024). For over half a century, coccidiosis control has primarily relied on the prophylactic administration of in-feed chemical and ionophore anticoccidials (Chapman, 2014; Yu et al., 2023). However, sustained selection pressure from these anticoccidials has driven the emergence of drug-resistant Eimeria field isolates worldwide (Lan et al., 2017). This escalating drug resistance, including multi-drug resistance, severely compromises the clinical efficacy of these compounds (Lan et al., 2017; Ra et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2025). Due to the challenges of drug resistance and concerns regarding drug residues, it is necessary to develop novel, eco-friendly, and secure alternative treatment solutions to control chicken coccidiosis.

Fortuitously, phytogenic compounds sourced from medicinal plants offer a promising strategy in coccidiosis control. The potential of these compounds stems from their complex chemical compositions, which confer multi-target biological activities with lower risks of toxicity and residue accumulation (El-Shall et al., 2022). Several plant-derived compounds have demonstrated promising anticoccidial effects, primarily through their immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties (Zan et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Boyner et al., 2025). Eucalyptus oil (EO), an essential oil rich in the monoterpene 1,8-cineole (more than 70%), possesses anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and anticoccidial activities (Dhakad et al., 2018; Barboucha et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025b). Specifically, regarding intestinal health, dietary supplementation with EO has been proven to alleviate intestinal inflammation, modulate gut microbiota, and enhance the intestinal barrier function in weaned rabbits (Fu et al., 2025). Furthermore, recent studies have indicated that EO additives provide moderate protection against E. tenella infection. These benefits are mediated by the inhibition of parasite invasion, induction of parasite apoptosis, preservation of the intestinal epithelial barrier, and suppression of pathogenic bacterial proliferation (Huang et al., 2025b; Geng et al., 2025). In traditional medicine, mangosteen pericarp has been utilized to treat dysentery and intestinal infections due to its ability to clear "heat-toxin" (Yang et al., 2026). Modern pharmacological studies have further validated that mangosteen pericarp extract (ME), containing α-mangostin and other xanthones, demonstrates potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, in addition to a notable anticoccidial effect (Ghasemzadeh et al., 2018; Tocmo et al., 2021; Sriboonyong et al., 2022). Crucially, ME strengthens the intestinal barrier by upregulating tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and Occludin) and MUC-2 (Tocmo et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025a). Mechanistically, ME activates AhR and Nrf2 pathways to mitigate oxidative stress and LPS translocation, while simultaneously promoting anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization via NF-κB/MAPK signaling (Yang et al., 2026). The pathogenesis of coccidiosis involves a complex interplay of several factors: direct parasite-induced damage, host inflammatory response, and gut dysbiosis (Weng et al., 2024). This complex pathogenesis leads to poor therapeutic effects of single compounds or phytomedicine. Several studies have shown that combination therapies demonstrate superior anticoccidial efficacy compared to monotherapies (Geng et al., 2024; Eldeeb et al., 2025).

Plant essential oils have shown significant potential for improving the production performance and disease resistance of broilers (Khukhodziinai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). However, essential oils such as EO are volatile and unstable, have a short duration of action, are not resistant to storage, and have poor solubility and stability in aqueous media. Moreover, their hydrophobicity and intense aroma can reduce feed intake and impair feed homogeneity (Chen et al., 2022; Rodrigues et al., 2025). These drawbacks limit the practical application of EO in poultry production. Microencapsulation, a technique that entraps a liquid core within a protective solid shell, can improve the stability of plant essential oils, reduce chemical degradation, and stabilize and prolong their biological activity (Wani et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2025). Recent studies have showed that microencapsulation improves the utilization and therapeutic efficacy of plant essential oils in the treatment of chicken enteritis caused by pathogenic bacteria or Eimeria infections (Kumar et al., 2022; Rodrigues et al., 2025; Xing et al., 2025). Therefore, this technology can address the challenges faced by EO in its clinical application as an anticoccidial drug to a certain extent.

The objective of this study was to investigate the anticoccidial and cecal protective efficacy of EO and ME, either alone or in combination, and elucidate the underlying mechanisms by assessing their effects on oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and cecal microbiota. To improve the clinical administration of EO, we fabricated eucalyptus oil microcapsules (EOM) through ionic cross-linking of sodium alginate and calcium chloride (CaCl₂). Microencapsulation may represent a feasible delivery strategy to enable the in-feed application of EO and to support its combination with ME for controlling coccidiosis.

Material and methods

Experimental chicks and ethics statement

One-day-old Qingyuan Partridge chicks (a local yellow-feathered broiler breed in China) were procured from the Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences Institute of Poultry Science (Yangzhou, China) and housed in coccidian-free cages. Chicks had ad libitum access to a basal diet free of anticoccidial drugs and antibiotics, with housing facilities maintained at standard temperature and humidity. All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Yangzhou University (Yangzhou, China).

Experimental medicine, parasites and reagents

Eucalyptus oil (EO, batch no. E875574) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., with a 1,8-cineole content of 80%. Mangosteen extract (ME) was provided by the Department of Pharmacology, College of Veterinary Medicine, China Agricultural University, with an α-mangostin content of approximately 20%. Diclazuril (DIC) was obtained from the Yangzhou University Animal Hospital, manufactured by Sichuan Lansheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.

The sporulated oocysts of E. tenella (Houghton strain, provided by the Shanghai Veterinary Research Institute) were maintained by the Traditional Chinese Veterinary Medicine Laboratory of Yangzhou University. The strain was passaged every three months through inoculation in chicks to maintain viability and stored in 2.5% potassium dichromate solution.

Sodium alginate was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.; anhydrous calcium chloride, absolute ethanol, petroleum ether, and Tween-80 were all procured from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Preparation of EOM

EOM was prepared using a sodium alginate-CaCl₂ cross-linking method (extrusion method). Briefly, sodium alginate was dissolved in distilled water under magnetic stirring at 45 °C to obtain a 10 mg/mL (1.0% w/v) solution. This concentration was selected as the critical threshold to ensure sufficient wall impermeability and high encapsulation efficiency while maintaining optimal viscosity for droplet formation, as concentrations below this level may lead to oil leakage (Chang and Dobashi, 2003).

After the solution was prepared, Tween-80 (0.2% v/v) was added. Tween-80 was chosen as the emulsifier because its unsaturated fatty acid moieties have been shown to provide superior emulsion stability and higher encapsulation efficiency in alginate-based systems compared to other surfactants (Zheng et al., 2023). Following sonication for 20 min to remove air bubbles, an appropriate amount of eucalyptus oil was added, and the mixture was emulsified by magnetic stirring at 1000 r/min for 15 min.

The emulsion was then added dropwise into a 15 mg/mL CaCl₂ solution using a 1 mL syringe. The system was sealed and allowed to cross-link for 2 h. Finally, the microcapsules were washed three times with distilled water to remove residual cross-linking agent, yielding the final EOM.

Characterization of EOM

Determination of Encapsulation Efficiency of EOM: To quantify how effectively eucalyptus oil was entrapped in the alginate matrix (thereby indicating resistance to oil leakage and potential stability), the surface oil and total oil contents of EOM were determined by solvent extraction followed by gravimetric measurement, and encapsulation efficiency was calculated from these values. The surface oil and total oil contents of EOM were determined using solvent extraction and gravimetric methods, from which the encapsulation efficiency was calculated.

Surface Oil Content Measurement: To estimate non-encapsulated oil residing on the capsule surface, accurately weigh 2 g (to 0.0001 g) of microcapsules into a conical flask. Add 30 mL of petroleum ether (boiling range: 30–60 °C) and shake for 3 min. Filter the mixture into a pre-weighed round-bottom flask (recorded as m₀). Rinse the conical flask three times with 10 mL of petroleum ether each time and combine the washings into the same flask. Remove the petroleum ether by rotary evaporation at 50 °C. Dry the flask to constant weight in an oven, cool to room temperature, and weigh (recorded as m₁). Calculate the surface oil content using Eq. (1):(1)Surface oil content (g) = m₁ − m₀

Sequentially add anhydrous ethanol, petroleum ether, and anhydrous ethanol in a volume ratio of 2:1:1. Mix thoroughly, and then transfer the upper extraction layer to a pre-weighed round-bottom flask (m₀). Repeat the extraction twice and combine all extracts. Remove the solvents by rotary evaporation at 50 °C. Dry the flask to constant weight, cool, and weigh (recorded as m₁). Calculate the total oil content using Eq. The analysis of morphological features: To visualize the surface topology and structural integrity of microcapsules (indicative of coating completeness and potential release behavior), microcapsules were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Samples were mounted on stubs with conductive adhesive and sputter-coated with gold for 120 s under a vacuum below 10 Pa for 3–4 min before imaging. Observations were carried out at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV, beam current of 15 mA, working distance of 9.4 mm, and magnifications ranging from 500 to 20,000×.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis: To verify mineral incorporation associated with ionic cross-linking and to support the formation of an alginate–Ca network, elemental composition was assessed using EDS. EDS was performed on samples adhered to conductive tape under an accelerating voltage of 5–10 kV and beam current of 1–5 nA.

Malvern Laser Nano Particle Size analysis: To evaluate dispersion homogeneity and colloidal stability relevant to handling and feed mixing, particle size distribution, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano instrument after appropriate dilution of samples in PBS buffer.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy: To confirm the characteristic functional groups and potential interactions between eucalyptus oil and the alginate matrix after encapsulation, FTIR spectroscopy was performed. Sodium alginate, blank microcapsules, and eucalyptus oil-loaded microcapsules were ground with KBr and pressed into pellets. Spectra were recorded in the range of 500–4000 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.

Animal experiments

This study consisted of two independent animal experiments (Protocol 1 and Protocol 2) using the same E. tenella challenge model but different treatment forms: Protocol 1 evaluated EO alone or combined with ME, whereas Protocol 2 evaluated EOM as an in-feed delivery form alone or combined with ME. Unless otherwise specified, housing conditions, challenge dose, sampling time point, and anticoccidial efficacy evaluation (ACI) were identical between Protocol 1 and Protocol 2.

Animal protocol 1: Focused on evaluating the combined efficacy and pharmacological mechanism (anti-inflammatory, barrier protection, microbiota modulation) of the EO and ME. At 15 days of age, the Qingyuan Partridge chicks were weighed and randomly divided into six experimental groups, with three replicates cages per treatment and 10 birds per cage (30 birds/treatment). The groups were designated as the blank control group (Con), the E. tenella-infected model group (Mod), the E. tenella-infected treated with diclazuril group (DIC), the E. tenella-infected treated with eucalyptus oil group (EO), the E. tenella-infected treated with mangosteen extract group (ME), and the E. tenella-infected treated with combination group (EO + ME). On the day of infection, chicks in all groups except for the Con group were orally challenged with 5 × 10⁴ sporulated E. tenella oocysts. At 12 h post-challenge, treatments were administered daily until day 21 of age. Specific treatments were as follows: the Con and Mod groups received no treatment; the DIC group was treated with 1 mg/L diclazuril solution in their drinking water; the EO group received EO via oral gavage (100 mg/kg body weight); the ME group received ME mixed in the feed (250 mg/kg feed); and the EO + ME group received EO via oral gavage (100 mg/kg body weight) and ME mixed in the feed (250 mg/kg feed). Animal protocol 2: Focused on evaluating the practical application of EOM. At 15 days of age, the Qingyuan Partridge chicks were weighed and randomly divided into six experimental groups, with three replicates cages per treatment and 10 birds per cage (30 birds/treatment). The groups were designated as the blank control group (Con), the E. tenella-infected model group (Mod), the E. tenella-infected treated with diclazuril group (DIC), the E. tenella-infected treated with eucalyptus oil microcapsules group (EOM), the E. tenella-infected treated with mangosteen extract group (ME), and the E. tenella-infected treated with combination group (EOM + ME). The EOM group received EOM mixed in the feed (50 mg/kg feed); and the EOM + ME group received EOM mixed in the feed (50 mg/kg feed) and ME mixed in the feed (250 mg/kg feed) (Fig. 7A). The dosage of 50 mg/kg for EOM was chosen based on the premise that microencapsulation enhances the stability, targeted delivery, and bioavailability of the essential oil. This lower in-feed concentration, compared to the direct gavage dose of EO, was intended to evaluate the efficacy of the delivery system under conditions closer to practical application, highlighting the potential for dose reduction.

Following euthanasia, chicks were processed sequentially. The ceca were excised and immediately lesion-scored and photographed (typically within a few minutes per chick). Immediately after scoring and photography, the mid-cecum (middle third) was collected and subdivided into predefined portions: (i) one portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histology, (ii) one portion was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for SEM, and (iii) the remaining portion was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately and then stored at −80 °C for qPCR and biochemical assays.

Anticoccidial efficacy evaluation

During the entire trial, broiler chicks were checked daily for health status and mortality, and any chicks that died were immediately subjected to necropsy. Body weight was recorded at 7 dpi to evaluate growth performance. From 72 h post-infection onward, fecal samples were collected every 24 h into screw-capped tubes containing 2.5% potassium dichromate, and the number of oocysts per gram of feces was quantified using a McMaster counting chamber. At 7 dpi, the body weight at 0 DPI and 7 DPI (BW, the body weight at 7 DPI) was recorded respectively. Body weight gain (BWG) = (the body weight at 7 DPI) − (the body weight at 0 DPI), and surviving chicks were assessed for relative body weight gain (rBWG) compared with Con group. Cecal lesion scores were assessed by trained personnel blinded to treatment using a standardized 0–4 gross lesion scale described in the World Association for the Advancement of Veterinary Parasitology (WAAVP) guidelines: 0 = normal ceca with no visible lesions; 1 = mild petechial hemorrhages; 2 = moderate hemorrhage with noticeable wall thickening and/or limited bloody contents; 3 = severe hemorrhage with abundant blood/clots and marked thickening/distension; 4 = extremely severe hemorrhagic/necrotic lesions with pronounced distension and caseous cores. Lesion value (points) = mean cecal lesion score × 10; Oocyst value (points) was assigned based on the oocyst reduction ratio (treated oocysts per gram of feces (OPG) / Mod OPG × 100): <1% = 0, 1–25% = 5, 25–50% = 10, 51–75% = 20, >75% = 40. Then, the ACI value was calculated using the formula (Holdsworth et al., 2004).

ACI = (rBWG + survival rate) – (lesion value + oocyst value)

ACI values were interpreted as follows: ACI < 120, inactive. 120 ≤ ACI < 160, mild efficacy. 160 ≤ ACI < 180, moderate efficacy. ACI ≥ 180, excellent efficacy.

Histopathological observation

To assess microscopic tissue injury, inflammatory cell infiltration, goblet cell status, and mucosal architecture following infection and treatment, cecal tissues were examined by routine histopathology and mucin staining. The cecal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning into 4–5 μm slices. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), respectively, and finally observed under an optical microscope for histological morphology. For the quantitative analysis of goblet cells, ImageJ software was used. Five distinct visual fields were randomly selected from each section (n = 3 chicks/group), and the number of PAS-positive goblet cells per unit area of the villus was counted and statistically analyzed.

SEM of the cecum

To visualize ultrastructural changes on the cecal mucosal surface, cecal tissues were examined by SEM. Trimmed tissue samples were fixed in standard EM fixative, subjected to graded dehydration and critical-point drying, sputter-coated with gold, and examined by scanning electron microscopy.

Assessment of antioxidant indicators

To evaluate oxidative stress status in the cecal tissue during E. tenella infection and determine whether treatments mitigated oxidative injury, myeloperoxidase (MPO) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activities were quantified. The activities of MPO and GSH-Px in cecal tissues were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT–qPCR)

To determine whether treatments modulated host inflammatory responses at the transcriptional level, mRNA expression of target genes in cecal tissue was quantified by RT–qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from chicken cecal tissues using Trizol reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Only RNA samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were selected for subsequent analysis. Reverse transcription of mRNA into cDNA was performed immediately. Quantitative PCR was performed using the 2 × ChamQ SYBR Green PCR Master Mix and gene-specific primers. The cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, followed by a melting curve analysis from 65 °C to 95 °C. Each biological sample was run in technical triplicate (three wells per gene). For each group, n = 3 individual birds were used as biological replicates. Primers were synthesized by TSINGKE, and their detailed sequences listed in the table (Table 1). 16S rRNA sequencing

To investigate whether EO/ME (or EOM/ME) altered E. tenella infection–associated gut dysbiosis and to characterize microbial community shifts linked to treatment effects, cecal microbiota profiles were analyzed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Cecal contents were aseptically collected immediately after necropsy by gently expressing luminal contents into sterile tubes (avoiding mucosal scraping), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Total DNA was then extracted using a commercial fecal DNA extraction kit and assessed for purity and concentration. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were then amplified with barcode-indexed primers. Amplicons were purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit, and sequencing libraries were constructed. Paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated to produce feature tables and representative sequences, which were subsequently used for taxonomic classification, community diversity analysis, differential abundance analysis, correlation analysis, and phylogenetic inference.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (MEAN ± SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with SPSS 19.0 software and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 6. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 were considered highly statistically significant.

Effects of EO and ME combination on cecal tissue morphology and function

Infection with E. tenella resulted in marked pathological alterations in the ceca of chicks characterized by severe atrophy (shortening of cecal length), wall thickening, and the formation of necrotic cores, compared to the control group. Administration of a combination of EO and ME significantly mitigated these macroscopic lesions and prevented cecal shortening, outperforming individual treatments with either EO or ME alone, and yielding efficacy comparable to DIC (Fig. 1B). As demonstrated in the results, E. tenella infection induced severe structural disruption of the cecal glands and disorganization of the intestinal villus, accompanied by massive inflammatory cell infiltration, severe hemorrhagic lesions, and substantial oocyst accumulation. All treatment groups ameliorated pathological damage to varying degrees in both intestinal villi and glands. Individual administration of either EO or ME significantly reduced the number of E. tenella oocysts in cecal tissues. Notably, the combination of EO and ME alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration and hemorrhage in the intestinal epithelium and further enhanced the reduction in oocyst load, achieving a clearance rate comparable to that of the DIC group (Fig. 1C).

This study comprehensively evaluated the quality of EOM through systematic characterization of their encapsulation efficiency, physicochemical properties, structural morphology, and chemical composition. The encapsulation efficiency, determined by petroleum ether extraction, was 74.3%, demonstrating effective core material retention. Particle size analysis (n = 3) indicated an average microcapsule diameter of 332.9 ± 15.3 nm. The PDI was 0.579 ± 0.05, indicating a relatively uniform particle size distribution that met the basic requirements for microcapsule preparation. Furthermore, the zeta potential was measured at −15.2 ± 1.1 mV, suggesting that the microcapsules possessed a sufficient surface charge to maintain dispersion stability through electrostatic repulsion (Fig. 6A).

FTIR spectroscopy provided chemical evidence of successful encapsulation. Compared with blank microcapsules and raw sodium alginate, the EOM exhibited a strengthened hydroxyl characteristic peak at 3427 cm⁻¹, indicating successful wall material cross-linking. The appearance of a new characteristic absorption peak at 975 cm⁻¹, which was absent in the spectra of the blank microcapsules and sodium alginate, confirmed the successful incorporation of eucalyptus oil within the microcapsule structure. This peak was indicative of the presence of EO components, distinguishing the loaded microcapsules from the empty carriers. (Fig. 6B).

SEM images showed that the EOM exhibited a regular spherical morphology with distinctive surface protrusions and wrinkles, likely resulting from shrinkage during the drying process. The microcapsules maintained structural integrity without visible cracks or pores, indicating that the sodium alginate-CaCl₂ cross-linked wall material provided effective mechanical protection and encapsulation performance (Fig. 6C). Elemental distribution analysis confirmed homogeneous dispersion of Ca, Cl, and Na elements across the microcapsule surface, corroborating the structural uniformity and stability of the encapsulation system (Fig. 6D).

In conclusion, EOM with high encapsulation efficiency, uniform size distribution, intact microstructure, and excellent chemical stability were successfully fabricated. The sodium alginate-CaCl₂ encapsulation system proved effective for efficient EO encapsulation, providing a solid foundation for improving stability and bioavailability in subsequent applications.

Discussion

The parasite Eimeria spp. is characterized by a complex life cycle that progresses through sequential stages of asexual (schizogony) and sexual (gametogony) reproduction, culminating in the formation of oocysts, which are subsequently shed into the environment via feces (Burrell et al., 2020). This cycle involves multiple invasions of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). Notably, the release of numerous second-generation schizonts induces extensive disruption of IECs, leading to a loss of intestinal barrier integrity (Dubey and Jenkins, 2018). Several studies have showed that the extent of intestinal structural damage is a critical indicator of chicken coccidiosis severity (Pham et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023). Consequently, preserving the structural and functional integrity of the intestine is considered a critical strategy for controlling chicken coccidiosis and providing a direction for the development of novel anticoccidial drugs and feed additives.

Recently, various natural remedies have demonstrated anticoccidial efficacy, highlighting their potential as alternative control agents (Yuan et al., 2023; Mo et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023). Our previous research has showed that EO exerted a moderate anticoccidial effect (Huang et al., 2025b). Additionally, adding ME to the feed can alleviate cecal lesions to some extent (Sriboonyong et al., 2022). In this study, we investigated the combined anticoccidial efficacy of EO and ME. The results showed that the combined EO and ME formulation resulted in a higher rBWG, lower cecal lesion score, and lower oocyst output than the single treatments. This superior efficacy was confirmed by a markedly elevated ACI of 169, which surpassed those achieved with either EO or ME monotherapy. Notably, the anticoccidial efficacy of the combined EO + ME formulation was comparable to that of the synthetic drug diclazuril, positioning it as a promising alternative for coccidiosis control.

The intestinal mucosa is the primary site of Eimeria replication and the main battleground between the parasite and host. The histological and SEM results demonstrated damage to cecal crypts, disorganization of intestinal villi, and the presence of numerous oocysts in cecal tissues following E. tenella infection, consistent with previous findings (Yang et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025b). Compared to individual treatments with EO or ME, the combined EO + ME formulation further alleviated cecal tissue damage and inhibited oocyst formation and shedding caused by E. tenella infection. Glycoproteins secreted by goblet cells flush foreign pathogenic factors and prevent harmful substances from contacting IECs (McCauley and Guasch, 2015). Mucins, a specialized subclass of glycoproteins, inhibit E. tenella invasion into IECs (Tierney et al., 2007). However, some studies have indicated that E. tenella degrades cecal mucin rapidly through mucin-degrading enzymes (Zhou et al., 2021). Therefore, promoting the secretion of cecal glycogen and mucin is considered an effective strategy for mitigating coccidiosis (Zhou et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024). In this study, the combined EO + ME formulation significantly increased mucus/mucin production or goblet cell density in the cecal mucosa of broiler chicks, suggesting that this combination protects the intestinal mucosal barrier against E. tenella invasion.

The observed combination of EO and ME was also deeply rooted in the complementary antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of the constituent extracts. Coccidiosis is characterized by an intense inflammatory response and severe oxidative stress, which are major contributors to tissue pathology and poor growth performance (El-Ghareeb et al., 2023). Research indicated that extracts of natural herbs possess excellent antioxidative and anti-inflammatory abilities, which could treat coccidiosis (El-Shall et al., 2022; Mo et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023). Natural active components, such as myricetin, chlorogenic acid, and curcumin, can improve growth performance and ACI by inhibiting the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) and oxidative stress indicators (NO and MDA) in E. tenella-infected broilers (Liu et al., 2022a; El-Ghareeb et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). CConsistent with the above studies, EO + ME combination therapy effectively increased the GSH-Px content, reduced MPO activity, suppressed the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17, and increased the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β. These findings suggest that EO + ME combination therapy significantly mitigated the oxidative stress and inflammatory responses induced by E. tenella infection. Oxidative stress and inflammation are closely intertwined, engaging in a reciprocal causal relationship and collectively contributing to the pathogenesis and progression of numerous diseases. Recent studies have demonstrated that activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant signaling pathway effectively suppresses the E. tenella-induced activation of the ChTLR15/ChNLRP3 inflammatory signaling pathway, thereby mitigating intestinal inflammatory damage (Yang et al., 2022; Mo et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2024). Thus, these studies may partly elucidate the potential mechanisms by which the EO + ME combination therapy maintains intestinal barrier function.

The diverse gut microbiota plays a vital role in digestion and intestinal protection. The interaction between the gut microbiota and the intestinal mucosa in chickens forms the intestinal microbial barrier. The intestinal mucus layer serves as the primary site for microbial colonization and is also key to maintaining microenvironmental homeostasis. Concurrently, the dynamic equilibrium of gut microbial abundance and diversity is essential for maintaining and regulating the intestinal mucosal barrier function (Makki et al., 2018; Broadwater et al., 2025). However, studies have shown that E. tenella infection damages the cecal mucus layer, leading to a reduction in microbial diversity and abundance. This is primarily manifested as a decreased abundance of beneficial genera, such as Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium, and an increased abundance of potentially pathogenic genera, like Escherichia and Clostridium (Zhou et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2024; Broadwater et al., 2025). In this study, the EO + ME combination therapy restored the diversity of the microbiota to a greater extent than DIC. This is potentially because DIC possesses only anticoccidial activity and lacks regulatory effects on the gut microbiota. At the phylum and family levels, the EO + ME combination therapy increased the relative abundance of Firmicutes, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminococcaceae, while decreasing the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, Enterococcaceae, and Enterobacteriaceae. Previous studies have indicated that E. tenella infection increases the colonization of Salmonella and Escherichia coli in the cecum, leading to severe diarrhea and enteritis (Arakawa et al., 1981; Campos et al., 2022). These results suggest a potential reduction in opportunistic pathogens within the Enterobacteriaceae family (which includes genera such as Salmonella and Escherichia), thereby contributing to the restoration of microbial homeostasis. To further investigate the potential mechanism by which this combination therapy regulated the gut microbiota, LEfSe analysis identified Rikenellaceae as the characteristic bacterial group at the family level. Interestingly, Rikenellaceae and Ruminococcaceae play a combined role in regulating the host's intestinal metabolism. The higher relative abundance of the family Ruminococcaceae andRikenellaceae, an important group for producing short chain fatty acids (e.g., important energy sources for hosts), can be beneficial for improving growth performance (Qiu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022b; Song et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2024). These results indicated that the EO + ME combination therapy can ameliorate E. tenella-induced coccidiosis in broiler chicks by modulating the cecal gut microbiota.

Microcapsules are miniature containers with a polymer wall shell, which can protect the encapsulated core material. The sodium alginate-CaCl₂ system is a commonly used method for preparing microcapsules (Martins et al., 2015). Sodium alginate, a natural polymer, has good film-forming properties and biocompatibility (Wani et al., 2023). In this study, sodium alginate was cross-linked with CaCl₂ to form a stable microcapsule structure, which exhibited an encapsulation efficiency of 74.3% for EO, with an average particle size of 332.9 ± 15.3 nm, PDI of 0.579 ± 0.05, and zeta potential of −15.2 mV. Analyses via SEM, EDS, and FTIR spectroscopy revealed that this microcapsule structure effectively encapsulated EO, reduced its volatilization and oxidation in the external environment, and protected its active components (Chen et al., 2022; Mazza et al., 2023; Rodrigues et al., 2025). Furthermore, the co-administration of EOM with ME demonstrated combined anticoccidial efficacy. The results showed that the EOM + ME combination therapy resulted in a higher rBWG, a lower cecal lesion score, and a lower oocyst output compared to EOM, ME, and DIC. Notably, the resulting ACI value of 171 not only surpassed that of the individual agents (EOM/ME, ACI range: 120–160) but also exceeded that of diclazuril (ACI = 165). Additionally, the formulation significantly suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17, while increasing the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β, thereby mitigating the intestinal inflammatory damage caused by E. tenella infection. Taken together, these results showed that EO microencapsulation enhances its anticoccidial efficacy, as evidenced by the improved ACI value, histopathological analysis of cecal tissues, and modulation of inflammatory factors. Consequently, this microencapsulation-based delivery strategy for combination therapy provides a promising outlook for developing novel, plant-derived anticoccidial drugs.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the EO + ME combination formulation exerted significant combined anticoccidial effects against E. tenella infection in broilers. The combination therapy improved the weight gain and reduced intestinal lesions more effectively than either EO or ME alone, achieving an efficacy comparable to DIC. The anticoccidial mechanism of this combination is multifaceted, involving enhancement of the intestinal mucosal barrier, mitigation of oxidative stress and inflammation, and restoration of gut microbiota homeostasis. Furthermore, microencapsulation improved the practicality of the EO-based formulation for in-feed use and further strengthened the combined efficacy of EO and ME, as supported by improved anticoccidial index and alleviated cecal damage and inflammation. These findings highlighted the EO + ME combination as a promising, multi-target natural alternative to conventional anticoccidial drugs for controlling chicken coccidiosis, and support microencapsulation as an effective delivery strategy to enhance the applicability and performance of this EO-based therapy.

Limitations

A limitation is that PAS staining and SEM were not conducted for the EOM group; therefore, mucin/goblet-cell changes and mucosal ultrastructure could not be directly evaluated. Mechanistic interpretation of EOM is thus based on the available endpoints, and adding PAS/SEM would strengthen the conclusions. Additionally, the rationale for the reduced dosage of EOM (50 mg/kg feed) compared to raw EO (100 mg/kg BW) relies on the theoretical enhancement of bioavailability by microencapsulation. As specific pharmacokinetic data were not collected in this study, this remains a hypothesis that warrants verification in future research.
