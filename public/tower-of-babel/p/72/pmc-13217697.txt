Climate change worry, awareness and general anxiety in medical students: a multicentre cross-sectional study with implications for medical curricula
Authors: Neslişah Gürel Köksal, Hülya Güç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change is an escalating public health threat with growing mental health impacts, including climate change worry. Medical students are both future health professionals and a learner group with high baseline stress and anxiety. Evidence from middle-income settings and disaster-affected regions remains limited. We examined general anxiety and its associations with climate change worry and awareness among Turkish medical students to inform undergraduate curricula.MethodsWe conducted a multicentre cross-sectional study (October 2024–April 2025) in two Turkish medical schools in the north (Giresun) and south (Hatay). Students completed an online survey with sociodemographic items and validated measures: Climate Change Awareness Scale (CCAwS), Climate Change Anxiety Scale (CCAS; Anxiety and Helplessness), and Anxiety Assessment Scale (AAS). Group comparisons used Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests with Bonferroni–Dunn post hoc procedures.ResultsOverall, 443 students participated (mean age 21.81 ± 2.23 years; 50.8% female). Mean scores were 72.91 ± 13.73 (CCAwS total), 20.51 ± 6.37 (CCAS-Anxiety), 11.71 ± 2.34 (CCAS-Helplessness), and 29.64 ± 9.00 (AAS total); internal consistency was high (α = 0.907–0.934). Compared with clinical students, preclinical students reported higher climate change worry (CCAS-Anxiety; median 21 vs. 20; p = 0.017) and higher general anxiety (AAS total; median 30 vs. 29; p = 0.008), including higher AAS Physiological Tension (p = 0.004), Worry (p = 0.018), and Feeling Unsafe (p = 0.032). Regional differences were limited: Giresun students had higher CCAwS energy consumption awareness (median 13 vs. 12; p = 0.002), while Hatay students had higher AAS Feeling Unsafe (p = 0.040). By year, CCAwS Awareness of Causes was higher in Year 1 than Year 4 (p = 0.032), and CCAS-Anxiety was higher in Year 1 than Year 5 (p = 0.043).ConclusionsClimate change awareness was moderate to high, while climate change worry and general anxiety were more pronounced in preclinical years. Findings support early, balanced integration of climate–health and disaster-related mental health content into medical curricula, alongside coping and action-oriented supports to protect student wellbeing.

Background

Climate change, driven by anthropogenic emissions and systemic transformations, is an increasingly urgent threat to population health and health systems [1, 2]. Beyond physical health impacts such as heat-related morbidity, air pollution, and vector-borne diseases, accumulating evidence highlights salient mental health consequences, including anxiety, depressive symptoms, and post-traumatic stress in the context of both acute events and gradual environmental change [3–5]. In this article, we use the term “climate change worry” to refer to eco-anxiety a climate-related form of psychological distress that has been defined as a chronic fear of environmental doom and described as a multifaceted experience that may include cognitive, emotional, and somatic components. Importantly, scholars caution against pathologising adaptive concern while recognising clinically relevant distress in vulnerable groups [3–6]. Alongside climate change worry, youth also report a broader range of “ecological emotions” (e.g., sadness, anger, helplessness, and hope), which may shape how learners interpret climate-related threats and engage with solutions [7].

Within undergraduate medical education, global and national frameworks increasingly call for embedding climate and planetary health competencies into curricula, while simultaneously prioritising the wellbeing of learners [2, 8–12]. Medical students are future health professionals who will counsel patients and communities about climate-related risks, yet they already face substantial academic pressure, uncertainty about the future, and high rates of anxiety and stress. Evidence syntheses indicate that depression, depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation are prevalent among medical students globally [13], and national data from Türkiye similarly highlight substantial levels of depression, stress, and burnout and their associated factors in medical student populations [14]. Recent BMC Medical Education studies show that medical, nursing and physician assistant students recognise climate change as a health issue, express concern about its impact on healthcare delivery, and report gaps in climate-health teaching, underscoring the need for curricula that address both knowledge and emotional responses [15, 16]. Understanding how general anxiety, climate change worry, and climate change awareness intersect in medical students is therefore essential to designing educational content and support strategies that are psychologically safe and action-oriented [4, 5, 17–23].

In Türkiye, evidence on climate change awareness and related psychological outcomes in medical students remains limited, particularly in multicentre designs spanning regions with differing environmental and disaster experiences. Türkiye is located in the Mediterranean region, which is widely characterised as a climate change hotspot where hazards such as heat extremes, drought/water stress, and other climate-related risks are expected to intensify [24]. Within the country, regional differences in climate hazard profiles and recent disaster experiences may influence how students perceive and emotionally respond to climate-related threats. Including medical schools from two geographically and socio-contextually distinct regions may therefore help capture heterogeneity in climate change worry and awareness across different environmental and disaster contexts [24]. Building on validated Turkish instruments for climate change awareness, climate-related anxiety, and general anxiety [25–27], as well as the availability of a validated Turkish adaptation of an eco-anxiety measure [28], we conducted a multicentre cross-sectional study in two geographically and socio-contextually distinct medical schools. Our primary aim was to determine medical students’ general anxiety levels and examine their association with climate change worry and awareness across training phases and regions. A secondary aim was to derive implications for the integration of climate and disaster-related mental health content into undergraduate medical curricula.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a multicentre cross‑sectional analytic study of undergraduate medical students at two Turkish medical schools (Hatay in the south, Giresun in the north), reported in accordance with the STROBE guideline for cross‑sectional studies. A total of 443 students participated (Giresun n = 247; Hatay n = 196). Of approximately 1520 eligible students across both schools, 443 completed the survey (estimated response rate 29,1%).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Giresun Training and Research Hospital Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision No. 16.10.2024/08). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Participation was voluntary; electronic informed consent was obtained on the first page of the online survey before students could proceed. Participants could discontinue at any time by closing the survey prior to submission. Data were collected anonymously (no personal identifiers) and confidentiality was maintained throughout.

Use of generative AI tools

A large language model (ChatGPT) was used solely for language editing (grammar and style) in early manuscript drafts; all text was reviewed and verified by the authors, who take full responsibility for the content.

Sample size and power

For a comparison of two independent groups, a Mann–Whitney U–based power assessment indicated that, with 95% power and α = 0.05, an effect size d = 0.37 corresponds to a noncentrality parameter (δ) = 3.61. On this basis, the final sample was deemed sufficient to yield reliable estimates for the planned between-group comparisons.

Inclusion criteria: enrollment as an undergraduate medical student at one of the participating medical schools during the study period; ability to access and complete the online questionnaire; complete submission of the survey.

Exclusion criteria: declining electronic informed consent; survey submissions that were incomplete; duplicate submissions ; responses from individuals not enrolled as undergraduate medical students at the participating institutions.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected online (Google Forms) between October 2024 and April 2025 without restriction by academic year or phase. The survey link was distributed by the investigators to all cohorts via student representatives, and six reminders were issued at 15-day intervals. Participation was voluntary; electronic informed consent was obtained on the first survey page, and respondents who declined consent could not proceed. The average completion time was ~ 20 min. We used convenience sampling; to minimize duplicate entries, required fields were implemented and respondents were instructed to complete the survey in a single session.

Variables

Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, marital status, year of study, perceived household income, and smoking status. The primary outcome variables were the scale scores obtained from the three instruments administered in the study.The survey instrument consisted of three previously published and validated scales. Students’ awareness of global climate change was assessed using items from Deniz et al. [25]. Climate change–related anxiety was measured with the Turkish adaptation of the Climate Change Anxiety Scale by Gezer and İlhan [26], and general anxiety was assessed with the Anxiety Assessment Scale developed by Malakcıoğlu [27]. We used the validated Turkish versions of these instruments, and no new items were developed for this study.

Climate Change Awareness Scale (CCAwS): This is a 21-item, five-point Likert instrument developed to assess university students’ awareness of global climate change. The subscales are: Awareness of Effects on the Natural and Human Environment (items 1–9), Awareness of Global Organizations and Agreements (items 10–15), Awareness of Causes (items 16–18), and Awareness of the Relationship with Energy Consumption (items 19–21). The Turkish validity–reliability study was reported by Deniz et al. [25] (8). In the present study, internal consistency was Cronbach’s α = 0.908 (total), with α = 0.815–0.909 across subscales.

Climate Change Anxiety Scale (CCAS): The 10-item Turkish form, adapted by Gezer and İlhan [26], confirms a two-factor structure: Anxiety and Helplessness (9). The five-point Likert scoring yields a total of 10–50, with higher scores indicating greater climate-change-related anxiety. In this study, internal consistency was α = 0.934 (total); by subscale, Anxiety α = 0.905 and Helplessness α = 0.801.

Anxiety Assessment Scale (AAS): The AAS Developed by Malakcıoğlu [27] and validated in medical students, this 10‑item scale comprises three subscales: Physiological Tension (PT), Worry (W), and Feeling Unsafe (FU) (10). The three‑factor structure was supported by EFA/CFA; in the original study, Cronbach’s α values were approximately 0.77–0.84 for the subscales and 0.85 for the total score. In the current sample, internal consistency was α = 0.907 (total); PT α = 0.754, W α = 0.837, FU α = 0.731.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Distributional assumptions for continuous variables were evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with Lilliefors correction when n > 50 and the Shapiro–Wilk test when n < 50. Non-normally distributed two-group comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U test; comparisons across three or more independent groups used the Kruskal–Wallis H test with Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn post-hoc procedures for significant omnibus results. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD and median (min–max); categorical data as n (%).To explore independent associations with general anxiety, we fitted a multiple linear regression model with the Anxiety Assessment Scale (AAS) total score as the dependent variable. Age (years), sex (female vs. male), training phase (preclinical [Years 1–3] vs. clinical [Years 4–6]), region of study (Hatay vs. Giresun), the Climate Change Awareness Scale (CCAwS) total score, and the Climate Change Anxiety Scale (CCAS) total score were entered simultaneously as predictors. Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals) were examined using residual plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test, and were considered adequately met. Unstandardised regression coefficients (B) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), p values, and the coefficient of determination (R²) are reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

To examine independent correlates of general anxiety, we fitted a multiple linear regression model with AAS total score as the outcome and age, sex, training phase, region, CCAwS total score, and CCAS total score as predictors (Table 5). The model was statistically significant and explained 23.2% of the variance in AAS total scores (adjusted R² = 0.221). Climate-related worry (CCAS total) was the strongest independent correlate of general anxiety (B = 0.44, p < 0.001), corresponding to an approximate 4.4-point increase in AAS total for each 10-point increase in CCAS total. In contrast, climate-change awareness (CCAwS total) showed a small inverse association with general anxiety (B = − 0.07, p = 0.019). Female sex remained independently associated with higher AAS total scores (B = 2.20, p = 0.005). Age, training phase (preclinical vs. clinical), and region (Hatay vs. Giresun) were not independently associated with AAS total after adjustment (all p > 0.05; Table 5).

Discussion

In this multicentre study of medical students from two geographically and socio-contextually distinct Turkish regions, climate change awareness was generally moderate to high, whereas climate change worry and general anxiety were more pronounced in the preclinical phase. Preclinical students scored higher on climate change worry and across multiple dimensions of general anxiety than their clinical counterparts, while regional differences were limited to specific subdomains (energy consumption awareness and perceived safety). By simultaneously examining climate change awareness, climate change worry, and general anxiety in a medical education context—and by including an earthquake-affected province—these findings extend the international literature on eco-anxiety and student wellbeing [17–23].

Our modelling results suggest that training phase alone may not fully explain differences in general anxiety. Although preclinical students reported higher climate change worry and higher general anxiety in bivariate comparisons, the independent association between training phase and general anxiety was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after adjustment for climate change worry, climate change awareness, sex, age, and region. In the same model, climate change worry emerged as the strongest independent correlate of general anxiety, whereas climate change awareness showed a small but statistically significant protective association, and female sex remained associated with higher general anxiety scores. This pattern implies that the higher anxiety observed among preclinical students may be largely accounted for by elevated climate change worry and the sociodemographic composition of the preclinical group, rather than by training phase per se.

These findings also reinforce that climate change awareness and climate change worry should not be treated as interchangeable constructs. Awareness as captured by the CCAwS primarily reflects a cognitive dimension (recognition of climate-change drivers and impacts), whereas climate change worry reflects an affective threat response that may be sustained by rumination, uncertainty, and anticipated future harm. From a stress–appraisal perspective, anxiety is more likely when perceived threat is high and perceived coping capacity or control is low; in this framework, climate change worry may index the “threat appraisal” component, while greater awareness—particularly when paired with clearer understanding of solutions, roles, and pathways for engagement—may support perceived control and coping, thereby showing a small protective association with general anxiety. This conceptual distinction helps interpret why preclinical and clinical students could have comparable awareness but differ in worry and general anxiety: earlier-stage students may experience greater future-oriented uncertainty and lower perceived agency to act within the health system, whereas clinical exposure may foster efficacy and more action-oriented coping that buffers worry and general anxiety. Given the cross-sectional design, bidirectional pathways remain plausible (e.g., higher baseline anxiety increasing climate change worry, or climate change worry driving information seeking), underscoring the need for longitudinal and mixed-method studies that explicitly measure efficacy, coping styles, and action opportunities [4, 5, 17–23].

When positioned against the broader literature, our findings are consistent with evidence indicating salient climate-related psychological outcomes in medical and health students [17]. Studies from Germany reporting climate-related depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms in medical students highlight the vulnerability of this population [18]. In Türkiye, climate worry and hopelessness in youth have been linked to climate awareness and to broader wellbeing [19]. Other work suggests that climate awareness can predict eco-anxiety, supporting the emotional correlates of climate knowledge [20], while longitudinal evidence among adolescents indicates that the awareness–worry relationship may be dynamic and bidirectional over time [21]. In our data, awareness was similar across phases, yet climate change worry and general anxiety were higher in preclinical students, further supporting the interpretation that worry is not merely a derivative of awareness and that developmental and contextual factors—such as future-oriented uncertainty—may shape this relationship [21, 22].

Although we observed some differences by year of study, these did not form a consistent or monotonic pattern across years and were confined to selected comparisons (e.g., Year 1 vs. Years 4–5 on specific subdomains). Accordingly, we interpret year-level findings as exploratory rather than a central interpretive axis. Future studies designed to test cohort and developmental explanations should examine mechanisms that may vary with age and stage, such as future anxiety, perceived agency, and exposure to action opportunities within training [21, 22].

The educational implications are therefore twofold: curricula should build knowledge while also supporting emotionally safe engagement and coping. Evidence linking future anxiety with climate awareness in nursing students may help explain elevated climate change worry earlier in training, when career uncertainty is greater [22]. Conversely, evidence that hope and coping strategies can attenuate adverse mental health sequelae related to eco-anxiety suggests that educational programmes should incorporate not only information but also structured coping and action pathways [23]. Educational interventions that improve climate/health-related knowledge and attitudes [29, 30] could—if introduced early and paired with skill-building—bolster perceptions of competence and autonomy, potentially stabilising climate change worry and general anxiety. In practical terms, this argues for preclinical curricula that combine climate–health content with psychologically informed components such as emotion regulation, communication skills for climate-related health risks, and guided opportunities for constructive action, while remaining attentive to psychological safety [12, 23].

Regional findings warrant careful and context-sensitive interpretation. Higher energy consumption awareness among students in Giresun may reflect differences in the visibility of sustainability practices or teaching about healthcare’s environmental footprint, which is substantial globally [31]. However, our study lacks qualitative data to clarify underlying drivers of this difference. Higher Feeling Unsafe scores in Hatay suggest lower perceived safety and resonate with constructs of risk appraisal and fragile contexts [32, 33]. A preprint among Yemeni medical students in a fragile setting—reporting emotions such as eco-anxiety and anger—supports the idea that contextual stressors can shape emotional responses to climate and environmental threats [32]. Because we did not measure individual hazard exposure or disaster-related trauma directly, these regional findings should be regarded as associative and hypothesis-generating; nevertheless, it is plausible that the 2023 earthquake context in Hatay may contribute to heightened safety concerns among learners in that province.

More broadly, the health impacts of climate change and the imperative for urgent climate action to protect health are well established [10]. National and institutional policies—such as environmental health action plans informed by a “One environment, one health” approach—aim to embed climate–health integration within medical education [11]. Our findings suggest that elevated climate change worry and general anxiety in early training should be explicitly considered in curricular design: content should be balanced, hope-oriented, action-based, and mindful of psychological safety, with supports that help students translate concern into constructive engagement [12, 23]. In addition, evidence linking risk appraisal with pro-environmental behaviour suggests that students should be supported not only with knowledge but also with actionable pathways and visible institutional practices that make engagement feasible and meaningful [33].

This study has several strengths. The multicentre design across two distinct regions improves contextual relevance and supports comparison across different environmental and disaster contexts. Including students across all training phases and using validated Turkish instruments to capture cognitive (awareness) and affective (worry) climate-related constructs alongside general anxiety strengthened interpretability, and internal consistency was high in this sample. Analytically, the multivariable approach allowed us to distinguish the independent contribution of climate change worry and climate change awareness to general anxiety, providing a nuanced interpretation aligned with medical education planning.

Overall, these findings indicate that medical students can exhibit moderate to high climate change awareness while simultaneously experiencing elevated climate change worry and general anxiety early in training. Integrating climate–health and disaster-related mental health content in undergraduate medical education may be most effective when paired with psychologically safe, skill-based, and action-oriented supports that strengthen coping, efficacy, and constructive engagement—particularly for learners in disaster-affected or otherwise fragile contexts [12, 23].

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Convenience sampling via an online survey may have introduced selection bias and limits representativeness. Analytically, we did not test interaction effects, multivariable models were exploratory and not fitted for all outcomes, and effect sizes were not calculated for all comparisons, limiting interpretation of magnitude and mechanisms. Finally, the study included only two medical schools and did not measure individual disaster exposure, so generalisability—particularly for region-based findings—should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

Medical students demonstrated moderate-to-high climate-change awareness, but climate-related worry and general anxiety were more prominent in the preclinical years. Climate-related worry was the strongest correlate of general anxiety, while awareness showed a small protective association, suggesting that cognitive awareness and affective worry may operate through different pathways. These findings support early climate–health curriculum integration that combines knowledge with coping and action-oriented skill building, alongside wellbeing supports especially in disaster-affected contexts. Longitudinal and intervention research is needed to test mechanisms and evaluate educational approaches that reduce distress while strengthening climate-health competence.
