Dysregulated glia and periglomerular cells in the olfactory bulb and altered lipocalins in the olfactory epithelium underlie olfactory dysfunction in a murine model of eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis
Authors: Ryoji Kagoya, Hironobu Nishijima, Kyohei Horikiri, Kei Ogawa, Megumi Kishimoto-Urata, Shinji Urata, Kenji Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis (ECRS) is a chronic inflammatory disease. It is characterized by type 2 inflammation and olfactory dysfunction. Although olfactory dysfunction in patients with ECRS may have sensorineural and central components, the underlying mechanism remains to be elucidated. We aimed to investigate changes in the olfactory bulb (OB) and olfactory epithelium (OE) associated with sinonasal type 2 inflammation, using a murine model of ECRS.MethodsWe developed a murine model of ECRS based on the topical application of calcipotriol and ovalbumin for 14 days and subsequent daily intranasal challenge with ovalbumin for 5 days. Histopathological analyses were performed to assess glial cells and periglomerular cells (PGCs) in the OB. Bulk RNA sequencing was performed to determine the impact of sinonasal type 2 inflammation on the OB and OE. Differentially expressed genes were identified using a false discovery rate < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment). For molecules exhibiting marked fluctuations at the gene expression level, the protein expression was evaluated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).ResultsIn the ECRS group, a significant increase was noted in the number of microglia/macrophages and astrocytes in the OB. In addition, the number of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive PGCs was significantly reduced in the OB of mice with ECRS. Bulk RNA sequencing analysis revealed a significant decrease in the gene expression levels of the lipocalin family in both the OB and OE. Subsequent ELISA confirmed a significant reduction in the protein levels of lipocalin 4 and major urinary protein 5 in the OE; conversely, lipocalin 3 levels in the OE were significantly increased. No significant differences were observed in the protein expression levels of these molecules within the OB.ConclusionSinonasal type 2 inflammation causes various changes in the olfactory system. These changes may be involved in the pathogenesis of sensorineural and central olfactory dysfunction in ECRS.

Background

The paranasal sinuses possess chemosensory receptors for odor stimulation and various defense mechanisms against pathogens [1]. Sinus inflammation frequently leads to olfactory dysfunction [2], which is conventionally categorized based on the site of injury as conductive, sensorineural, or central [3]. Conductive olfactory dysfunction is caused by physical blockage of the route from the anterior nostril to the olfactory epithelium (OE). Sensorineural olfactory dysfunction is caused by damage to the olfactory epithelial cells due to various external stimuli. Central olfactory dysfunction originates from abnormalities in the intracranial olfactory pathway following the olfactory bulb (OB) [3].

Eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis (ECRS) is a type 2 inflammatory disease of the paranasal sinuses characterized by nasal polyps with eosinophil infiltration. Olfactory dysfunction is a hallmark symptom of the disease [4]. The main mechanism of olfactory dysfunction associated with ECRS is thought to be a conductive disturbance caused by the nasal polyps. Recently, it has been suggested that olfactory dysfunction in patients with ECRS may have sensorineural and central components because inflammatory changes in the OE of patients with ECRS are histologically confirmed, and the degree of nasal airflow and olfactory dysfunction do not always match [4]. The volume of the OB in patients with CRS with nasal polyp (CRSwNP) was reported to be reduced compared with that in healthy participants [5]. Due to the technical and ethical difficulties associated with collecting human olfactory nerve tissue, research on olfactory dysfunction using human samples has not progressed very well.

In recent years, basic research using animal models of various types of rhinosinusitis has provided insights into the changes that occur in the OE and OB due to sinonasal inflammation [6–10]. Hasegawa-Ishii et al. [7] demonstrated an increase in microglia/macrophages in the OB of a murine model of bacterial rhinosinusitis using lipopolysaccharides (LPS). They also revealed a decrease in tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive periglomerular cells (PGCs) in OB [7]. PGCs are interneurons in the glomerular layer of the OB that coordinate input signals from olfactory neurons. TH-positive periglomerular cells are dopaminergic neurons that receive synaptic inputs from olfactory nerves; these cells are known to decrease in number following the deprivation of olfactory input [11]. Our group revealed an increase in microglia/macrophages and proinflammatory cytokines in the OB of a murine model of toll-like receptor 3-mediated upper respiratory tract inflammation [9]. In addition, we established a murine model of short-term type 2 sinonasal inflammation model using calcipotriol and ovalbumin (OVA) and showed histological injury of the OE [12]. However, only a few previous basic researches have discussed the impact of sinonasal type 2 inflammation on the OB [13–15].

In the present study, we investigated the changes in the OB associated with sinonasal type 2 inflammation using histological analysis of glial cells and PGCs in the OB. We also conducted a bulk RNA sequence analysis (RNA-Seq) of the OB and OE samples collected from our ECRS model. Furthermore, we performed an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for several molecules that exhibited fluctuations in RNA-Seq.

Methods

Mice

Female BALB/c mice (7-weeks-old) were purchased from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan). They were housed in a temperature-controlled facility maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle and given with free access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the University of Tokyo Animal Care Committee and conducted in accordance with the University's guidelines for the handling and care of laboratory animals.

Tape stripping was first performed 10 times on the ventral side of the right ear using cellophane tape. Subsequently, mice in the ECRS group were topically treated on the right ear once daily for 14 days with 2 nmol of calcipotriol (Bio-Techne, Tokyo, Japan) in 20 μL of absolute ethanol along with 100 μg of ovalbumin (OVA) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in 20 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following the topical treatments, daily intranasal administration of 2400 μg of OVA in 20 μL of PBS was performed for 5 days. Mice in the control group received topical treatment with the vehicles only (20 μL of absolute ethanol and 20 μL of PBS) on the right ear for 14 days, followed by intranasal administration of 20 μL of PBS only for 5 days. All mice were sacrificed on day 19.

Fixation and tissue preparation

The mice were deeply anesthetized by intramuscular injection of ketamine and xylazine. They were fixed via transcardial perfusion with 20 mL of 10% neutral-buffered formalin, followed by decapitation. Following the removal of the mandibles, the remaining heads were post-fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 1 week at room temperature (RT). The specimens were subsequently decalcified in Kalkitox (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) for 4 h at RT, washed, dehydrated using a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 4-μm-thick coronal sections were obtained at following two distinct levels: (1) the anterior border of the eyes (Fig. 1B) and (2) the center of the eyes (Fig. 1C). These sections were used to evaluate eosinophils and basophils in the OE and glial cells and PGCs in the OB, corresponding to levels (1) and (2), respectively. The prepared sections were mounted on MAS-coated slides (Matsunami Glass, Osaka, Japan) and subsequently subjected to immunostaining as described below.

Sirius red staining

To evaluate eosinophil infiltration in OE, Sirius Red staining was performed [16]. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were incubated with 0.5% Sirius Red solution (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) dissolved in absolute ethanol for 1 h at RT, washed in acidified water (0.5% hydrogen chloride), counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted.

Histological analyses

To evaluate the degree of type 2 inflammation in the olfactory nasal mucosa, the numbers of infiltrated eosinophils and basophils were counted manually in sections cut at the level of the anterior boarder of the eyes. The number of each type of granulocyte per 500 μm length of the OE was counted in the following two areas: nasal septum and lateral region (Fig. 1B).

To determine the glial cells of the OB, the numbers of microglia/macrophages (Iba-1 + cells) and activated astrocytes (GFAP + cells) were counted in sections cut at the level of the center of the eyes (Fig. 1C). Each type of glial cell was counted in the area of rectangular combination and the total value was calculated as the cell count per 0.2 mm2. The number of glial cells per 0.2 mm2 of the OB was counted in the following three areas: the granule layer (GRL), external plexiform layer (EPL), and glomerular layer (GML) (Fig. 1C). The numbers of TH + and calretinin (CR) + PGCs per 500 μm length of the GML were also counted manually on the sections cut at the level of the center of the eyes (Fig. 1C). The following two areas were evaluated: medial and lateral (Fig. 1C).

Buried food test

Olfactory function was assessed using the Buried Food Test (BFT) performed according to the method described by Yang et al. [17], with minor modifications. A clean test cage (325 mm L × 235 mm W × 170 mm H) containing a 1.5 cm depth of clean bedding was prepared. The subject mouse was first introduced to the test cage for a 5-min acclimatization period and then returned to its home cage. Subsequently, one food pellet (Cyanide-free Mouse Feed, Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan) was buried 1.5 cm beneath the bedding surface in a random corner of the test cage. The mouse was then placed in the center of the test cage, and the latency to locate the buried food was measured. If the mouse failed to locate the food within a maximum cutoff time of 240 s, the latency was recorded as 240 s.

RNA extraction

For RNA extraction, anesthetized mice were transcardially perfused with 10 mL saline and then decapitated. The OE and OB were micro-dissected, minced, and homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Total RNA was extracted according to manufacturer's instructions.

Transcriptomic library preparation and bulk RNA-Seq

Transcriptomic library construction and bulk RNA-Seq were performed using three independent biological replicates for each group (n = 3 per group) by Rhelixa Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). RNA quality was determined using a Bioanalyzer. The RNA integrity number (RIN) of all samples was confirmed to be > 8.0. Isolation of mRNA and cDNA library preparation were carried out using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, E7490) and NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (E7530), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was performed as follows.

The quality of the raw paired-end reads was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.7). Low-quality bases (Phred score < 20) and adapter sequences were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.38). The trimmed reads were aligned to the reference genome using the RNA-seq aligner, HISAT2 (v2.1.0). The resulting SAM files were converted into BAM files using Samtools (v1.9). The BAM files were then used to estimate the abundance of uniquely mapped reads using featureCounts (v1.6.3). Raw read counts were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM). The samples were hierarchically clustered based on the Euclidean distances of the normalized counts using the stats (v3.6.1) and gplots (v3.0.1.1) R packages. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the normalized counts, and each sample was projected onto the 2D plane defined by the first and second principal components (PCs) using the same R packages. Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the correlation between samples. Histograms and a pair plot of the normalized counts, as well as heatmaps generated from the Z-scores of the normalized counts, were created using the stats (v3.6.1) and gplots (v3.0.1.1) R packages.

Differential expression analysis was conducted using raw read counts and DESeq2 package (v1.24.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the following criteria: absolute log2 fold change (|log2 FC|> 1) and adjusted p-value < 0.05, calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method for false discovery rate (FDR) control.

Protein extraction and ELISA

The OE and OB tissues were resected and homogenized in 500 μL of CelLyticTM MT Cell Lysis Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) with 5 μL of protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) and 120 U/mL of benzonase endonuclease (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). The homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g at RT for 5 min, following which the supernatants were obtained and stored frozen at − 80 °C before analysis. Blood samples (60 μl/mouse) were collected from the tail vein at day 19, and allowed to clot at RT for 30 min. Serum supernatant was obtained after centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 5 min at RT. The protein levels of IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the samples were measured using the Mouse ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein levels of major urinary protein (MUP)5, lipocalin (LCN)3, and LCN4 in the samples were measured using the Mouse ELISA Kit (Abbexa, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using Microplate Reader ChroMate (Awareness Technology, FL, USA).

Total RNA extracted from the OE and OB was reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Biotechnology, Shiga, Japan). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was carried out on an Applied Biosystems™ QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (SPSS Inc, IL, USA). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess differences between the Control and ECRS groups as well as for comparison between the right and left sides, followed by BH correction. Data were presented as the means ± standard error with individual data points superimposed. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted P-value (Padj) of < 0.05.

Results

Calcipotriol + OVA-treatment induces infiltration of eosinophils and basophils in the OE

First, we histologically confirmed the establishment of type 2 inflammation in the OE of calcipotriol- and OVA-treated mice. The eosinophil numbers in the nasal septum and lateral region (right side) were calculated and compared between the two groups. Representative images are shown in Fig. 1D. The eosinophil counts were significantly higher in the ECRS group than in the Control group in both areas (Fig. 1E). Basophil numbers were counted and compared. The basophil counts in each area were also significantly higher in the ECRS group compared with the Control group (Fig. 1F, G). We also quantified the protein levels of the proinflammatory cytokines in the OE. The levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 in the OE were significantly elevated in the ECRS group compared with the control group (Fig. 1H). TNF-α in the OE was not detected by ELISA. To evaluate olfactory perceptual function, we performed the BFT. The latency to find the buried food was significantly longer in the ECRS group than in the Control group (Fig. 1I).

Microglia/macrophages increase in the OB of mice with ECRS

Next, we evaluated glial cells in the OB of mice with ECRS. Iba-1+ cells (microglia/macrophages) were counted in the GRL, EPL, and GML of the right and left OB sides and compared between the groups. Representative images are shown in Fig. 2A. The Iba-1+ cell count in each right-sided OB area was significantly higher in the ECRS group than in the Control group. Discussion

In the present study, we revealed that microglia/macrophages and astrocytes in the OB increased in a murine model of ECRS. We also observed a concurrent decrease in the number of TH+ PGCs in the GML of the OB of the ECRS model. Furthermore, using bulk RNA-Seq and ELISA, we confirmed significant fluctuations in the gene and protein expression levels of the lipocalin family molecules LCN3, LCN4, and MUP5 in both the OE and OB of ECRS mice. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating changes in PGCs and activated astrocytes in the OB and lipocalin family molecules in the OE of mice with ECRS.

Glial cells are crucial components of the OB and are involved in the maintenance of neuronal homeostasis [18]. Microglial cells, the immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), are responsible for recognizing and scavenging dead cells and pathogens [19]. Under normal conditions, microglial cells remain in a resting phenotype and play roles in neuronal activities such as synaptogenesis, neurogenesis, and release of neurotrophic factors [20]. However, when the neuronal system is injured and the microenvironmental homeostasis is disturbed, microglial cells shift into an active phenotype and secrete proinflammatory cytokines [21]. Proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)−1β and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, are known to exert harmful effects on neuronal cell survival and inhibit neurite outgrowth [22]. These cytokines also lead to microglial activation and reduce neurogenesis [22].

In the present study, mice with ECRS exhibited a higher density of microglia/macrophages and increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines in the OB compared to controls. We previously hypothesized that elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines in the nasal cavity could be transmitted to the OB via the olfactory nerve, inducing microglial activation and proliferation [9]. Our results revealed that protein expression level of IL-1β is elevated in the OE, which is consistent with the hypothesis [9], albeit the degree of elevation is mild. Although several reports have described the impact of bacterial or viral sinonasal inflammation on OB [6–10], discussion of OB changes associated with type 2 inflammation remains limited [13–15]. Huang et al. [13] showed that the expression levels of Iba1, Il1b, and Tnfa were elevated in a murine ECRS model using OVA and Aspergillus proteases. Zhang et al. [14] reported that Iba1+ cells, IL-1β and TNF-α were increased in the OB of mice with ECRS induced by papain. Zhang et al. [15] reported that microglia and proinflammatory cytokines were increased in the OB of rat with ECRS induced by OVA and Aspergillus proteases. They also revealed reduced OB volume accompanied by thinning of the olfactory neuron layer and the glomerular layer. Our results align with these previous reports. Furthermore, this is the first study to demonstrate a reduction in TH+ PGCs and an increase in activated astrocytes in the OB, as well as a decrease in lipocalin family molecules in the OE, in mice with ECRS.

A few reports have discussed the role of type 2 cytokines in the CNS [23, 24], although there have been no reports on the role of the cytokines in the OB. IL-4 and IL-13 exert both neuroprotective and neurotoxic effects [23]. These cytokines can reduce inflammation by inducing M2 polarization of microglia, while they had cytotoxic effect on dopaminergic neurons expressing IL-13Rα when reactive oxygen species are produced in the model of Parkinson disease [23]. The role of IL-5 in the CNS is characterized in the spinal cord injury model [24, 25]. After spinal cord injury, IL-5 upregulates the expression of calcitonin gene-related peptides and contributes to urinary dysfunction [24, 25]. In our study, an increase in the gene expression of Il5 and the protein level of IL-4 was observed in the OB of the ECRS model; however, no significant changes in the expression of IL-13 were observed. Further investigations are needed to determine the role of type 2 cytokines in OB.

Astrocytes were also increased in the GML of the OB in our murine ECRS model. In the OB, astrocytes coordinate neuronal metabolism and blood flow in response to the neural activity [26]. They also control the survival of adult-born neurons [27]. In contrast, astrocytes are activated by microglia-derived factors and induce neuroinflammation [18, 28]. They may also play a role in OB inflammation in ECRS mice.

It was revealed that TH+ PGCs were decreased in the GML of the OB of mice with ECRS. PGCs are interneurons located in the GML of the OB that regulate the transmission of olfactory information. PGCs can be divided into two subtypes based on their synaptic connectivity to olfactory nerve axons. TH+ PGCs have synaptic connections with the olfactory nerves and are classified as type 1, whereas CR+ PGCs are classified as type 2 and have no synaptic connections to the olfactory nerves [29]. TH+ PGCs are dopaminergic neurons that play a role in the inhibitory regulation of olfactory input [30] and decrease when olfactory input is blocked [11]. Given our previous finding of olfactory epithelial damage in this ECRS model [12], the TH+ PGC reduction likely reflects this decreased olfactory input. Conversely, CR+ PGCs, which lack synaptic connections to the olfactory nerves [29], showed no significant changes. CR+ PGCs are anaxonic neurons [31] and have been proposed to improve signal-to-noise ratios by filtering random excitatory stimuli [32]. Since CR+ PGCs are less well understood than TH+ PGCs, further investigation is warranted [31].

We observed that the basal expression levels of lipocalin family genes are relatively high in both OB and OE, with most genes showing a decrease in the ECRS model compared to the controls. Lipocalin is characterized by a barrel-shaped tertiary structure containing a ligand-binding site that enables the transport of water-insoluble molecules [33]. In the olfactory system, LCNs assist olfactory signal transduction by binding to water-insoluble odor molecules [33]. Nakamura et al. [34] found that applying recombinant LCN1 to the olfactory epithelium of Cynops pyrrhogaster amplified olfactory receptor responses to odorants. Ijichi et al. [35] reported that LCN15 localizes to the Bowman’s glands in the human olfactory cleft mucosa and is concentrated in the olfactory mucus; its concentration is lower in patients with olfactory dysfunction. Furthermore, Chapman et al. [36] demonstrated LCN15 potentiates the human odorant receptor OR51E1 in response to isovaleric acid. Although the role of MUPs in olfactory signal transduction has not yet been studied, their combination in mouse urine contributes to individual recognition [37]. While the impact of Type 2 inflammation on LCN production remains unexplored, IL-4 and IL-13 have been reported to modulate exocrine secretions from the olfactory mucosa [38, 39]. Given that LCNs are secreted by Bowman’s glands [35], their levels may be altered under Type 2 inflammatory conditions.

Although RNA-Seq predicted decreased protein levels of most lipocalin family members in mice with ECRS, subsequent ELISA revealed an increase in the level of some molecules. Such discrepancies between mRNA and protein expression are often observed and attributable to various factors [40]. Our results can be attributed to the following reasons. Because LCNs can bind a broad spectrum of ligands and are involved in the regulation of the immune system [33], they may increase when the tissue is injured. Subsequently, because of the buffering mechanisms regulating the production and degradation of proteins and their stability compared with RNA, only the RNA expression level may decrease [40]. Increased LCNs can be stored within Bowman's glands for a certain period. However, if chronic inflammation causes prolonged damage to Bowman's gland tissue, LCNs may eventually decrease. No previous studies have investigated the role of LCNs in the OB. Although we observed a reduction in RNA expression levels of LCNs in the OB of our ECRS model, we detected no significant changes at the protein level. Consequently, the functional relevance of these reduced LCN transcripts in the OB to olfactory dysfunction remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

The current study has several limitations. First, the observed changes in the OB may not be specific to sinonasal type 2 inflammation, as increases in Iba-1+ cells and decreases in TH+ PGCs in the OB have been confirmed in LPS-induced rhinosinusitis, a model of type 1 inflammation [7]. Furthermore, the precise mechanism by which sinonasal inflammation is transmitted to the OB remains to be fully elucidated. Given that systemic type 2 cytokine levels did not increase in the present model, it is suggested that the inflammatory signal may be transmitted locally. However, this study could not rigorously demonstrate the specific pathway—as suggested in previous reports [41, 42]—wherein cytokines ascend to the central nervous system via the nerves. Secondly, basic information regarding the function of LCNs in the olfactory system is extremely limited. Therefore, the interpretation of the significance of our results requires a great deal of additional information. The LCNs showing variation in our experiment were not LCN15, which is decreased in patients with olfactory dysfunction [35]. This suggests that the LCNs which are crucial for the transduction of olfactory signals differ across species. Thirdly, the long-term effects of chronic type 2 inflammation on the OB have not been fully elucidated. While the ECRS model used in this study was established over a relatively short period of 19 days, understanding the impact of persistent, long-term sinonasal type 2 inflammation on the central nervous system is a critical issue in clinical practice. Yet, it remains important to note that ECRS not only causes physical obstruction of the olfactory cleft but also induces various biochemical changes that affect olfactory signal transduction in the OB. This is because predicting the treatment efficacy for olfactory dysfunction and planning surgical or medical treatment accordingly are difficult for ECRS [43].

In conclusion, we demonstrated various changes in the OB and OE of ECRS mice. These changes are likely to represent mechanisms contributing to the molecular pathology of sensorineural and central olfactory dysfunction associated with ECRS, and thus constitute crucial information for the future development of optimal management strategies for olfactory dysfunction in ECRS.
