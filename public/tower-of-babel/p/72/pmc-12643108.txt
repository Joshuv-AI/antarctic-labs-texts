A Scoping Review of Approaches to Extragenital Chlamydia and Gonorrhea Screening for Adolescents and Young Adults in the United States
Authors: Loren M Dobkin, Barbara L Brush, Erin M Kahle, Patricia Drenth, Yasamin Kusunoki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To identify effective strategies for extragenital sexually transmitted infection screening among adolescents and young adults (AYAs).Data Sources:Following the Joanna Briggs Institute method, searches were conducted in PubMed and CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature) for relevant, peer-reviewed articles published from January 2000 through December 2024.Study Selection:The search strategy identified studies pertaining to chlamydia, gonorrhea, extragenital sites, screening, and AYAs. Studies were included if most participants were 12 to 24 years old or if their outcomes were disaggregated. Selected studies described at least one approach to extragenital screening, reporting corresponding measures of uptake, preferability, or acceptability. Nine studies met the inclusion criteria.Data Extraction:Data on study characteristics, sampled populations, approaches, relative successes, infection prevalence, and correlates were extracted, critically appraised, and descriptively synthesized.Data Synthesis:Included studies demonstrated higher extragenital screening uptake in research studies (89%–100%) compared to routine care (0%–74%) and when standardized protocols and health care staff trainings were incorporated. Extragenital infections were prevalent among female AYAs with genital infection, as well as sexual and gender minority individuals assigned male at birth.Conclusion:Increasing clinical standardization as well as enhancing health care and nursing staff training may improve detection of common extragenital infections and help prevent associated sexual and reproductive health complications.Keywords: adolescent, chlamydia, extragenital, gonorrhea, oropharyngeal, rectal, screening, sexually transmitted infection, STI, uptake, young adult

In the United States, adolescents and young adults (AYAs) ages 15 to 24 years account for approximately half of all cases of Chlamydia trachomatis (chlamydia) and Neisseria gonorrhoeae (gonorrhea) infection, yet they often encounter barriers to testing and treatment (Leichliter et al., 2017; Orionzi, 2023; Shannon & Klausner, 2018). Untreated chlamydia and gonorrhea can increase the risk of reproductive health disparities, infertility, subfertility (Li & Chen, 2023; Workowski et al., 2021), and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth, stillbirth, and low birth weight (Ahmadi et al., 2018; den Heijer et al., 2019; He et al., 2020). Untreated chlamydia and gonorrhea can also increase the risk of HIV infection, contributing to 20% of new U.S. HIV cases among U.S. AYAs ages 13 to 24 years (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2023b; Cohen et al., 2019; Williams et al., 2023).

Marginalized groups, including youth of color, face higher sexually transmitted infection (STI) risks and inadequate treatment due to social and health care inequalities (Boutrin & Williams, 2021). Untreated rectal STIs further increase HIV risk among young men who have sex with men (MSM) and transgender women of color who were assigned male at birth (Baiers et al., 2024; Hess et al., 2017). Although doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis reduces specific STIs in these groups (Luetkemeyer et al., 2023; Sankaran et al., 2024; Szondy et al., 2024), it does not replace the need for routine STI screening (CDC, 2023a; National Coalition of STD Directors, 2024).

Bacterial STIs can locate to one or more genital or extragenital sites (Workowski et al., 2021). Although chlamydia and gonorrhea are treatable with antibiotics, diagnosis is challenging because they are often asymptomatic. Detection requires site-specific testing, as the infections can be identified only through specimens collected from affected anatomic sites (Dalby & Stoner, 2022). Oropharyngeal and rectal infections may persist in individuals through autoinoculation between extragenital and genital sites, as well as through transmission between individuals (Khosropour et al., 2024; Rank & Yeruva, 2015; Workowski et al., 2021). While urine tests are commonly used to screen AYAs for cervical and urethral infections, they cannot be used to detect extragenital infections (Uprety & Cárdenas, 2019).

In 2021, the CDC updated the STI Treatment Guidelines to recommend routine screening for chlamydia and gonorrhea at the rectum and oropharynx in individuals assigned female at birth who report sexual activity at these sites as well as in all individuals assigned male at birth who report sexual activity with male partners (Dalby & Stoner, 2022; Workowski et al., 2021). Compared to adults, AYAs face barriers such as limited health care access, stigma around sexual activity, and concerns about confidentiality, making it harder to disclose sexual activity and receive timely and appropriate STI screenings (Jenks et al., 2022; Leichliter et al., 2017; Mukherjee et al., 2023; Orionzi, 2023; Shannon & Klausner, 2018; Workowski et al., 2021).

Given current challenges to adequately diagnosing, treating, and preventing transmission of chlamydia and gonorrhea among AYAs, there is an urgent need to develop effective approaches to extragenital screening in young, high-risk groups. Novel STI screening modalities, such as specimen self-collection and home-based testing, are becoming more widely available but may differentially impact AYAs compared to older adults (Brown et al., 2019).

This scoping review addresses the following research question: How do different approaches to extragenital screening for chlamydia and gonorrhea affect the likelihood of uptake among at-risk AYAs ages 12 to 24 years in the United States? The primary aim was to catalog and compare strategies for oropharyngeal and rectal screening, focusing on implementation measures of uptake (reach), acceptability, and preferability (Brownson et al., 2018). The review also explores the prevalence and behavioral correlates of extragenital infections in different subpopulations, aiming to identify effective clinical strategies for diagnosing extragenital STIs in disproportionately affected AYAs and to highlight gaps in the literature.

Methods

This review follows the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) method for conducting scoping reviews and is reported according to the guidelines set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR; McGowan et al., 2020; Peters et al., 2021; Tricco et al., 2018).

Search Strategy

To address the research question, searches were conducted in PubMed and CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature) using key terms and synonyms related to chlamydia, gonorrhea, extragenital sites, screening, and AYAs, with guidance from a health sciences library informationist.

Eligibility Criteria

This scoping review encompasses peer-reviewed research involving AYAs ages 12 to 24 years in the United States who were screened for chlamydia and gonorrhea at extragenital sites. The lower age limit of 12 years was chosen to include younger individuals who may face risks associated with early sexual activity, although chlamydia and gonorrhea diagnoses typically peak after age 15 years. Early sexual debut has been linked to reproductive tract complications from inadequate treatment (Menon et al., 2015), suggesting the importance and challenges of screening at-risk adolescents younger than 15 years. Age 12 years was selected as the minimum age for study inclusion because in most U.S. states specifying a lower age limit, minors ages 12 years and older can consent to their own sexual health care (CDC, 2022a). Studies were included if more than 50% of participants were ages 12 to 24 years, or if a smaller proportion was in this range and their outcomes were reported separately from other age groups. Studies were retained if they described at least one approach to asymptomatic screening (either exclusively or alongside symptomatic testing) for chlamydia and gonorrhea at rectal, oropharyngeal, or both anatomic sites and reported corresponding measures of uptake, preferability, and/or acceptability. Because this review focused on the adoption of contemporary laboratory assays for diagnosing chlamydia and gonorrhea, which have higher accuracy than their predecessors, it was limited to studies using the current nucleic acid amplification tests, which were first introduced in the early 2000s (Geisler et al., 2022; Workowski et al., 2021). Quantitative, qualitative, and quality improvement studies published from 2000 through December 2024 were evaluated for inclusion based on these criteria.

Studies were excluded if they did not align with the aims or methodological standards of this scoping review. This review focuses on high-quality evidence regarding strategies for screening AYAs for extragenital STIs per the recommendations of the 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines, which set the standard for STI-related clinical practice nationwide (Dalby & Stoner, 2022). Because it typically is not peer reviewed, grey literature was excluded (Brindley, 2022). Because this review specifically focuses on the detection of extragenital infections—requiring a more invasive specimen swab collection process compared to the urine-based testing commonly used for cervical and urethral infections—studies were excluded that did not distinguish extragenital from genital screening uptake (Watson et al., 2002; Workowski et al., 2021). In addition, because most chlamydia and gonorrhea cases present asymptomatically (Workowski et al., 2021), studies limited to symptomatic patients were excluded. Studies that focused exclusively on persons living with HIV were also omitted due to their distinct pathways to health care access in the United States (Asghari et al., 2018). Further exclusions consisted of studies conducted outside of the United States, non-English publications, editorials, opinion pieces, reviews, and animal studies.

Data Extraction and Data Analysis

All results from PubMed and CINAHL were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2023), which facilitated search management and duplicate removal. Data were extracted using a tool developed to capture key study characteristics (authors, publication year, purpose, design, and location), population details (AYA age range, sample size, proportion of total, and key demographics), screening (protocol, context), and outcome measures (uptake, positivity rates at extragenital sites, percentage of infections that would be missed by urine screening, and correlates of extragenital infection).

Quality Assessment

The level of evidence of all selected articles was assessed using one of three JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists (JBI, 2020) according to study design, including analytical cross-sectional (Moola et al., 2020), prevalence (Munn et al., 2020), and quasi-experimental (Barker et al., 2024). Two researchers (P.D., L.M.D.) independently evaluated and scored each publication to determine the risk of bias and whether the methodological quality was at least moderate to high. Any discrepancies or uncertainty was resolved via consultation with additional researchers (E.M.K., Y.K.), who reviewed articles in question and facilitated final consensus on inclusion.

Results

A total of 550 studies were initially retrieved, with 135 duplicates removed. An additional 335 articles were excluded as ineligible during title and abstract screening. Of the remaining 80 studies that underwent full-text review, 71 were excluded. As illustrated in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (see Figure 1), the most common reasons for exclusion were participant ages 12 to 24 years were not the majority or their outcomes were not disaggregated (n = 41); no measures of screening uptake, preferability, or acceptability were included (n = 15); studies were conducted outside the United States (n = 10); extragenital and genital screening outcomes were not separated (n = 4); and participants were not tested using nucleic acid amplification tests (n = 1). The final 9 studies were determined to meet methodological standards for inclusion, according to JBI checklists for analytical cross-sectional (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019; Woods et al., 2023), prevalence (de Voux et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023), and quasi-experimental (Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020) studies (see Supplementary Table S1).

The nine included studies were published from 2017 through 2023, but only two followed the most recent 2021 update to the CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023). Broadly, the primary purposes of the included studies were to assess the prevalence and predictors of extragenital infection (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019), to evaluate interventions and preferences for increasing screening and/or treatment (Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Woods et al., 2023), and to describe the frequency of extragenital screening and testing in routine care (de Voux et al., 2019; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023). All the studies used quantitative analyses, using cross-sectional (Chamberlain et al., 2017; de Voux et al., 2019; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023) or pretest and posttest (Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020) designs. Six studies were conducted in urban areas (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019).

Participant Characteristics

AYAs between ages 12 and 24 years composed most participants for all but two studies, the latter of which disaggregated their screening uptake results from older adults (de Voux et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018). Five studies had sample sizes in the hundreds (Chamberlain et al., 2017; de Voux et al., 2019; Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023), one study had 60 participants (Deiss et al., 2019), and three studies had more than 1,000 (Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Seven studies selected participants according to sex, gender. and/or sexual identity, featuring analyses of young MSM (Chamberlain et al., 2017; de Voux et al., 2019; Mustanski et al., 2017), patients assigned female sex at birth (Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Woods et al., 2023), as well as self-identified lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer (LGBTQ) and homeless (Shannon et al., 2019) youth. The remaining two study populations were defined by their clinical settings, including all patients attending a juvenile detention clinic (Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023) or presenting to an AYA sexual health clinic (Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020) during the respective study periods. Except for the smallest study, which evaluated patients in the Navy (Deiss et al., 2019), all other studies limited analysis to sexually active AYAs self-reporting recent sexual activity, presenting to a sexual health care clinic, or both. In seven studies, most participants self-identified with a race/ethnicity other than non-Hispanic white (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023).

Extragenital Screening Protocols and Contexts

Extragenital STI screening outcomes were analyzed in two ways: either focusing on participants who self-reported sexual activity at extragenital sites (Llata et al., 2018; Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023) or analyzing extragenital outcomes among all participants regardless of whether they reported extragenital sexual activity (Chamberlain et al., 2017; de Voux et al., 2019; Deiss et al., 2019; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019; Woods et al., 2023). Outcomes were evaluated in two mutually exclusive contexts: routine health care (de Voux et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023) and research study conditions (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Routine care outcomes were assessed retrospectively via medical record reviews (Llata et al., 2018; Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023) and self-report (de Voux et al., 2019), and research outcomes were measured prospectively (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019).

Extragenital Screening Outcomes

Uptake and measures.

All the studies screened asymptomatic and tested symptomatic participants alike, except the one by Deiss and colleagues (2019), who screened only asymptomatic patients. These processes are hereafter broadly referred to as “screening” to convey the asymptomatic context in which diagnosis most frequently occurs (Workowski et al., 2021). Eight studies reported absolute measures of extragenital screening uptake (Chamberlain et al., 2017; de Voux et al., 2019; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023). Among these, there was a near total spread of screening uptake by study population, screening protocol, and context. Studies in research settings showed higher screening uptake (89.4%–99.5%) compared to routine care (0%–74.2%). In research settings, uptake was measured by the percentage of eligible individuals consenting to extragenital screening (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Shannon et al., 2019) or the return rate of screening kits (Mustanski et al., 2017). In routine care, uptake was measured by the percentage screened during health care visits (Llata et al., 2018; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023) or in the past year via self-report (de Voux et al., 2019).

Mode and location of specimen collection.

Five studies specified the mode of specimen collection as either by a health care provider or self-collection (Deiss et al., 2019; Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023). Two studies evaluated screening via provider collection in different settings: during U.S. Navy annual examination visits, with more than 92% uptake (Deiss et al., 2019), and during juvenile detention facility clinic visits, with less than 58% uptake (Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023). Three studies featured self-collection (Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019), but only two of these reported absolute measures of uptake (Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Both studies occurred in the context of a research study rather than routine care, with uptake ranging from 89.4% to 99.5% (Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Only one study included home-based self-collection, although it also included clinic-based self-collection and did not distinguish between these two venues when reporting screening uptake (Mustanski et al., 2017).

Relative measures and preferences.

In two studies, results were disaggregated from AYAs and the majority older sample, both revealing lower uptake of extragenital screening among AYAs compared to older age groups (de Voux et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018). Llata et al. (2018) also stratified the percentage of patients with positive rectal infection by age group, finding that chlamydia and gonorrhea rectal positivity was highest among those younger than 25 years.

In only one study were participants asked about preferences for and acceptability of extragenital specimen collection (Woods et al., 2023). This sample of patients assigned female at birth significantly preferred self-collection over provider collection and clinic-based screening over home-based screening (Woods et al., 2023).

Middlebrook & Ruud (2020) evaluated an intervention to increase extragenital screening uptake, comparing preintervention and postintervention screening rates. The authors evaluated an AYA clinic quality improvement initiative, which aimed to standardize the extragenital screening process by implementing a site-specific sexual history–taking intake form, written protocols for obtaining extragenital specimens, staff nurse education and training, and full insurance coverage for extragenital screening. Medical record reviews showed a more than 700% increase in rectal and oropharyngeal tests during chlamydia and gonorrhea screening visits postintervention. However, the study authors did not compare patient characteristics between the preintervention and postintervention periods or account for other potential factors influencing the increase.

Extragenital Versus Genital Positivity and Correlates

Among the three studies with the lowest extragenital screening uptake (de Voux et al., 2019; Woods et al., 2023; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023), only one reported chlamydia and gonorrhea positivity. Ybarra and Benjamins found only two (0.8%) oropharyngeal infections (of 128 screened) and no (0%) rectal infections (of four screened). In contrast, studies with higher screening uptake reported greater extragenital positivity, with 3.3% to 7.5% of participants testing positive for oropharyngeal and 8.3% to 27.4% testing positive for rectal chlamydia and/or gonorrhea infections (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019).

Five studies described the percentage of positive chlamydia and gonorrhea results at extragenital versus genital (cervicovaginal or urethral) anatomic sites (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). All reported rectal positivity, and three additionally reported oropharyngeal positivity (described in Supplementary Table S1; Deiss et al., 2019; Shannon et al., 2019; Ybarra & Benjamins, 2023).

Among studies examining rectal and genital results, trends in the relative positivity at rectal versus genital sites and their correlates diverged according to study population characteristics. Young MSM or people assigned female sex at birth were sampled across multiple studies with relatively high screening uptake (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Among young MSM, rectal positivity was substantially higher than genital positivity (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Whereas Mustanski and colleagues (2017) reported that 35.3% of urethral infections occurred concurrently with rectal infections in their sample of MSM, the other studies did not explicitly state the degree of rectal and genital concurrency. Nevertheless, among the remaining studies sampling young MSM, even if rectal infection occurred with every single genital infection, urine screening would still fail to detect most chlamydia and gonorrhea infections because rectal infections vastly outnumbered genital infections (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Studies also supported that sexual risk (reporting anal sex behavior) was correlated, although imperfectly, with a higher likelihood of rectal positivity among their young MSM participants (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Mustanski et al., 2017).

Among samples of individuals assigned female at birth, rectal and cervicovaginal positivity rates were similar (see Supplementary Table S1; Deiss et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018; Shannon et al., 2019). Deiss et al. and Shannon et al. found high rates of concurrent genital and rectal infections (83% and 74%, respectively), suggesting that urine screening alone would miss only a minority of rectal infections. Genital infection strongly correlated with rectal infection, and anal sexual behavior did not (Deiss et al., 2019; Shannon et al., 2019). Llata et al. (2018), who sampled only females reporting anal sex, also found that most infections occurred concurrently at genital and rectal sites but did not separate data for those younger than 25 years from the majority older sample.

Discussion

Before 2021, approximately half of AYAs in the United States were receiving the recommended annual genital STI screening (Kulkarni et al., 2024). In 2021, the updated CDC STI Treatment Guidelines began emphasizing more invasive extragenital screening and multiday (compared to single-dose) treatment approaches for this population, who already face barriers to care (Leichliter et al., 2017; Orionzi, 2023; Shannon & Klausner, 2018). Among the studies reviewed, those that disaggregated AYA data demonstrated lower absolute uptake of extragenital screening in this group compared to older adults (de Voux et al., 2019; Llata et al., 2018). One study also found a higher likelihood of infection among AYAs versus older adults (Llata et al., 2018), consistent with research linking younger age to increased risk of extragenital STI positivity (Allan-Blitz et al., 2021), and trends showing peak risk of genital infection and reinfection between ages 15 and 24 years (Currenti et al., 2024; Shannon & Klausner, 2018; Workowski et al., 2021). Adolescents’ extragenital STI risk may be partly due to strategic sexual behaviors aimed at reducing perceived risks of pregnancy and infection (Habel et al., 2018; Halpern-Felsher et al., 2005). However, this scoping review highlights a significant gap in the literature regarding the implementation of extragenital screening guidelines for AYAs.

The percentages of AYAs undergoing extragenital screening in these study samples varied widely, ranging from 0% to nearly 100% of participants. The results of this scoping review underscore the importance of reporting screening uptake in this hard-to-reach population, as very low uptake may hinder the detection of extragenital infections. Given that approximately one quarter of female patients sampled by Woods et al. (2023) endorsed receptive anal intercourse and/or had genital infections, it is possible that rectal infections went undiagnosed in this sample due to the absence of rectal screening.

The proportion of AYAs undergoing extragenital screening varied depending on whether screening took place within a research study or during routine care. Research studies, as outlined in a protocol paper (Crosby et al., 2018) for the study by Chamberlain et al. (2017), implemented targeted outreach efforts to actively recruit participants. These efforts led to near-universal participation in extragenital screening among recruited AYAs under study conditions. In contrast, fewer than three quarters of AYAs underwent extragenital screening in routine care settings, where such structured recruitment efforts were absent.

The limited number of studies that specified the mode of specimen collection or included home-based screening restricted the ability to evaluate the relative effectiveness of these approaches. Woods et al. (2023) found that AYAs preferred self-collection over provider collection for rectal specimens, aligning with findings from Scotland, where younger AYAs also showed a strong preference for self-collection (Brown et al., 2019). Although the 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines generally encourage the self-collection of extragenital specimens as a “reasonable alternative” to provider collection when “self-collection is preferred,” they acknowledge that this is based on evidence pertaining only to patient populations of older adult “men” (den Heijer et al., 2019; Sexton et al., 2013; Workowski et al., 2021, p. 66). Yet despite this CDC recommendation, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has not yet approved any assays for self-sampling rectal or oropharyngeal specimens, effectively prohibiting self-collection unless individual laboratories conduct resource-intensive validation studies (Chongsuwat & Cody, 2023; Hockman et al., 2024; Navas et al., 2025; Tao & Gift, 2022). Further research is needed to explore how this restriction on extragenital self-collection in resource-limited clinics may pose an additional barrier to extragenital screening, specifically for underserved AYAs.

Among the studies of routine care included in this scoping review, the highest rate of extragenital screening uptake occurred in patients attending sexual health clinics in the CDC’s STI Surveillance Network (Llata et al., 2018). These clinics operate under competitive funding, which is contingent on strict adherence to standardized protocols, use of detailed patient questionnaires, and regular staff training (CDC, 2022b). Supporting this, Middlebrook and Rudd’s (2020) findings suggest that increasing the standardization of protocols, questionnaires, nurse training, and resources for extragenital screening may increase completion rates of routine extragenital screening (Middlebrook & Ruud, 2020). All registered nurses who received Middlebrook and Ruud’s intervention demonstrated an increase in their knowledge of how to elicit a sexual history of extragenital risk from AYAs and extragenital collection processes. Recently, 130 surveyed pediatric providers in a large urban hospital system cited identical knowledge gaps as barriers to implementing extragenital screening for their patients (Babayev et al., 2025). More than 40% of these providers reported discomfort taking a detailed sexual history from AYAs, and more than half acknowledged inadequate understanding of the extragenital collection process. Further evaluation research is needed to support that providing corresponding staff training and standardizing extragenital screening protocols reliably results in increased extragenital screening uptake among AYAs.

Several studies with high screening uptake indicated that most STI-infected individuals would be missed by urine screening alone, particularly among young MSM and transgender women (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Mustanski et al., 2017; Shannon et al., 2019). Although STI risk increases with decreasing age among transgender women (Brown et al., 2024), only one study in this scoping review included transgender women AYAs (Shannon et al., 2019). Van Gerwen et al. (2023) recently highlighted the importance of offering home-based gender-affirming STI testing to reduce gender dysphoria and enhance sexual health care engagement for transgender women adults. However, more research is necessary to better understand the potentially unique preferences of young transgender AYAs.

In several studies of young MSM, denying recent extragenital sexual risk behaviors decreased but did not eliminate the chances of a corresponding positive extragenital result (Chamberlain et al., 2017; Mustanski et al., 2017). This finding is consistent with studies involving older MSM (Burns et al., 2020) and may be attributable to inadequate extragenital treatment following more remote past extragenital risk behaviors (de Voux et al., 2019), nondisclosure due to perceived stigma (McKenney et al., 2018), or an oral sexual exposure etiology for rectal infection, hypothesized to occur via the gastrointestinal tract (Batteiger et al., 2019; Khosropour et al., 2024). These results support the 2021 STI Treatment Guide-lines, which recommend routine screening at extragenital sites for all MSM, regardless of reported site-specific sexual risk (Workowski et al., 2021).

Similarly, rectal coinfection was highly prevalent among female participants with genital infections, yet it did not correlate with reported anal sexual activity (Deiss et al., 2019; Shannon et al., 2019). High rates of rectal infection among genitally infected older females have also been observed, with no correlation to anal sexual activity (Llata et al., 2018; Peuchant et al., 2022). This may be due to autoinoculation between cervicovaginal and rectal sites of infection, as recently documented to occur in a prospective study of female adults (Peuchant et al., 2022), and/or oral sexual exposure (Batteiger et al., 2019; Khosropour et al., 2024). It is important to consider rectal coinfection in females with genital infections because this affects antibiotic selection, favoring doxycycline to better ensure rectal clearance (Peuchant et al., 2022; Rank & Yeruva, 2015; Workowski et al., 2021). Note that in addition to more invasive extragenital screening, the 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines also recommend more extended treatment follow-up for AYAs who test positive for extragenital infections. For rectal chlamydia, researchers recommend weeklong multidose doxycycline over single-dose azithromycin, along with tests of cure following azithromycin and all oropharyngeal gonorrhea treatment (Dalby & Stoner, 2022; Workowski et al., 2021). Completing a week of antibiotics when they are otherwise feeling well may be particularly difficult for AYAs, given their heightened privacy concerns and barriers to sexual health care. In a follow-up study of Shannon et al.’s (2019) sample of LGBTQ and homeless AYAs, Keizur et al. (2020) attempted to increase treatment access, finding that offering rapid same-day test results with immediate onsite medication dispensing significantly reduced AYA average time to treatment from several weeks to several days. Although dispensing treatment onsite at clinics has been shown to improve AYA adherence to other sexual health medications (Bersamin et al., 2018), it is a more common practice for single-dose azithromycin than extended-dose doxycycline (Ridelman et al., 2024). Higher costs and transportation difficulties associated with pharmacy pickup may impede extended doxycycline treatment among AYAs (Lieberman et al., 2019; Ridelman et al., 2024). Further research is needed to evaluate when the risks of limited access and adherence to doxycycline outweigh its benefit of higher efficacy for treating rectal chlamydia compared to single-dose azithromycin in AYA populations. Although follow-up tests of cure are now recommended to help shore up gaps in antibiotic efficacy, there is little evidence supporting their clinical effectiveness, including among AYAs (Allan-Blitz & Klausner, 2024; Jenks et al., 2022).

Limitations

This scoping review has several notable limitations, primarily due to the limited existing literature on extragenital screening and treatment among U.S. AYAs. The studies reviewed included subpopulations representing various segments of the 12- to 24-year-old age range. These mostly urban, relatively small samples were not inclusive of all high-risk AYA groups and were not generally representative of all AYAs in the United States. The results from the sampled youth in their specific settings may not be generalizable, and their relative methodological rigor was not compared. Outcome measures were also heterogenous and mostly derived via cross-sectional study designs, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Despite these limitations, this review contributes new insights and fills preliminary gaps in the literature regarding extragenital infection screening among AYAs.

Implications for Practice

The results of this scoping review suggest that actively reaching out to at-risk AYAs and implementing standardized clinical protocols for extragenital screening can help increase screening uptake by AYAs. Outreach initiatives should involve strategic promotion of extragenital screening, for example via social media (Teadt et al., 2020). Furthermore, it is essential that in all AYA care settings, patient questionnaires routinely ask all patients about sexual activity at extragenital sites and health care staff are trained to offer corresponding site-specific extragenital screening. Incorporating patient preferences, such as allowing specimen self-collection during screening, may be critical for increasing subsequent screening uptake. Health care staff should be mindful that certain groups of AYAs, including sexual and gender minorities assigned male at birth, as well as those assigned female at birth with cervicovaginal infections, may be at higher risk for extragenital infections even without reporting sexual risk at extragenital sites. These individuals may still benefit from extragenital screening and treatment recommendations.

As Middlebrook and Ruud (2020) demonstrate, nurses can play a critical role in moving such changes forward, serving as clinical innovation implementors as well as stewards of clinical protocols, all the while upholding AYA preferences and privacy protections. Beyond providing direct care, registered and advanced practice nurses often receive dedicated training in the scientific, ethical, and practical challenges of providing health care and corresponding program development for AYAs (American Nurses Association, 2018). This nursing expertise may be applied to help surmount common barriers to extragenital screening for AYAs and facilitate evaluation research, helping to fill extant gaps in practice and in the literature.

Conclusion

Screening AYAs ages 12 to 24 years for extragenital infection is challenging but essential for detecting and treating STIs, as well as preventing future sexual and reproductive health complications. This scoping review emphasizes that successful treatment relies on innovative screening strategies, including intentional care engagement, improved clinical standardization, and staff training. In addition, this review points to the need for future research to assess adherence to current CDC treatment and follow-up guidelines for extragenital infections among highly impacted AYAs.
