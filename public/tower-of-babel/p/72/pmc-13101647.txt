Harnessing Tumor Organoids to Revolutionize Immunotherapy Research
Authors: Zhen Wang, Peipei Yin, Shizhan Cui, Hui Liu, Dan Li, Yang Yu, Baoshan Cao, Xiaojuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmunotherapy has emerged as a revolutionary approach to cancer treatment, yet the lack of robust preclinical testing platforms impedes progress in this field. Tumor organoids, three‐dimensional structures derived from patient‐specific samples, differ from conventional models by faithfully recapitulating the histological, genetic, and phenotypic characteristics of native tumors. Moreover, complex organoid models can partially recapitulate aspects of the tumor microenvironment through the integration of immune cells, vascular‐associated components, and cancer‐associated fibroblasts. This review systematically examines current methodologies for constructing TME‐simulating organoids, along with their applications and future challenges in immunotherapy research, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, cell immunotherapy, bispecific antibodies, oncolytic viruses, and cancer vaccines. By elucidating the advantages and limitations of organoid technology in immunotherapy studies, this review explores its potential to establish a novel platform for predictive evaluation of immunotherapy efficacy. Ultimately, it aims to provide innovative perspectives for future research directions in this field and contribute to enhancing therapeutic outcomes for cancer patients.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health burden with rising incidence. According to data from the World Health Organization, approximately 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths were reported in 2022, and incidence is projected to exceed 35 million annually by 2050 [1]. Current cancer treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and molecular targeted therapy, have improved outcomes in selected patients but remain limited by invasiveness, systemic toxicity, resistance, and poor efficacy in advanced or metastatic disease. These limitations have driven the rapid development of cancer immunotherapy, which stimulates or modulates the patient's immune system to recognize and attack cancer cells more effectively. Owing to its transformative clinical impact, immunotherapy was named the Breakthrough of the Year in 2013 by Science, and the discovery of immune checkpoint inhibitors such as CTLA‐4 and PD‐1 has been widely recognized with the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2018 [2, 3]. However, there are challenges in translating immunotherapy from preclinical studies to clinical applications, with one of the foremost challenges being the development of preclinical models that can accurately simulate human immunity [4]. The tumor microenvironment (TME), shaped by genomic instability and intra‐ and inter‐tumor heterogeneity, drives drug resistance, treatment failure, and tumor progression [5]. Traditional preclinical models often fail to reflect the immunobiological characteristics of human cancer accurately. For example, while mouse models are useful for studying the efficacy of classic drugs, the immune system of mice differs significantly from humans and may not be relevant for all forms of immunotherapy [6]. Cancer cell lines, which are traditional models cultivated in 2D, lack the structural complexity, diversity, and cell‐to‐cell or cell‐to‐matrix interactions present in tumors, making it difficult to replicate the tumor microenvironment's functions [7]. Therefore, immunotherapy currently lacks appropriate models that accurately recapitulate the tumor microenvironment to improve evaluation of efficacy and safety and accelerate clinical translation [8].

In recent years, organoids have emerged as a revolutionary tool in biomedical research, offering unprecedented insights into human biology and disease. They are 3D cell aggregates grown from pluripotent stem cells or adult stem cells that spontaneously form organ‐like or tissue‐like structures, enriched with cell types typically found in the originating tissue [9]. Organoids have emerged as a new model for studying organ development and predicting clinical treatments [10]. Organoids have the advantages of long‐term expansion, cryopreservation, genetic modification, and maintaining genetic and phenotypic stability [11]. They are widely used in cancer research, including the creation of organoid biobanks, drug screening, co‐culture for tumor microenvironment recognition, and applications in immunotherapy [12, 13]. Tumor organoids, derived from patient‐specific tissue samples, simulate the characteristics of the original tumor, accurately capturing the key biological factors such as genomic, phenotypic, and cellular heterogeneity observed in vivo while offering the flexibility and accessibility of in vitro systems [14, 15]. Moreover, increasingly complex tumor organoid models incorporating selected components of the TME have been developed, supporting their use as preclinical, hypothesis‐generating platforms for immunotherapy research. For instance, a gel–liquid interface co‐culture system combining lung cancer organoids with paired peripheral blood mononuclear cells demonstrated in vitro immune response patterns concordant with patient outcomes following anti–PD‐1 treatment in an exploratory cohort, highlighting the feasibility of organoid–immune co‐culture models while underscoring the need for further validation [16]. This review first discusses the development of tumor organoids, particularly focusing on methods for constructing complex tumor organoid models that simulate the TME. It then summarizes their research applications in different immunotherapy approaches and concludes with a discussion of challenges in organoid culture.

Development of Tumor Organoids

The development of tumor organoid technology is closely linked to continuous optimization of culture conditions, media formulations, and three‐dimensional (3D) culture systems to sustain long‐term growth and preserve tumor‐specific functions.

Advances in Tumor Organoid Culture Systems

The organoid field was pioneered in 2009 when Hans Clevers's team showed that single Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells can self‐organize into crypt–villus structures in 3D Matrigel [17]. Identification of Lgr5 and its ligand R‐spondin led to defined culture media containing R‐spondin, EGF, and Noggin, forming the foundation of epithelial organoid systems [18, 19]. These advances enabled the successful derivation of tumor organoids from a broad spectrum of malignancies, including liver [20], lung [21], breast [22], ovarian [23], kidney [24], pancreatic [25], gastric cancers [26], as well as non‐epithelial tumors such as rhabdomyosarcoma [27] and neuroendocrine tumors [28], thereby substantially expanding their applications in cancer research. As the field progressed, model performance was recognized to depend on cell source. Tumor organoids are primarily derived from adult stem cells (ASCs) or pluripotent stem cells (PSCs), with patient‐derived ASC organoids preserving tumor‐specific genetic complexity and intratumoral heterogeneity, making them particularly suitable for biobanking and drug screening. In contrast, PSC or normal ASC–derived organoids engineered through genome‐editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9 provide genetically defined models for investigating early tumorigenesis.

The advent of tumor organoids has advanced precision oncology through scalable living biobanks and standardized platforms that preserve genetic fidelity and support high‐throughput screening. Coupled with multi‐omics profiling and AI‐based imaging, these systems enable quantitative response assessment and clinical translation [29]. In metastatic colorectal and gastroesophageal cancers, such a biobank achieved 100% sensitivity, 93% specificity, 88% positive predictive value, and 100% negative predictive value in predicting treatment responses in phase I/II trials [30].

Despite these advances, important limitations remain. Growth factor–rich organoid media commonly contain Wnt activators, TGF‐β pathway inhibitors, and nicotinamide, which sustain epithelial proliferation but may alter T cell and NK cell activation. Conversely, immune‐cell media lack epithelial‐supportive factors, resulting in intrinsic media incompatibility that complicates tumor–immune coculture systems. ECM composition further influences immune modeling capacity. Although Matrigel remains widely used in 3D organoid culture, its complex and undefined composition, murine origin, and batch variability pose scientific and translational challenges [31]. Its dense basement membrane–like structure may further interfere with human immune signaling and restrict immune cell migration, prompting the development of tissue‐derived ECM hydrogels and defined synthetic matrices that better recapitulate physiological biochemical and mechanical cues [32, 33]. These constraints underscore the need for immune‐compatible matrices and optimized culture strategies in next‐generation tumor organoid platforms.

In conclusion, advances in cell sourcing, 3D culture systems, genome engineering, and scalable biobanking have transformed tumor organoids into powerful models. Further refinement of media compatibility and matrix design will be essential for establishing immune‐integrated platforms that enhance predictive accuracy and accelerate clinical translation (Figure 1).

Cancer cell lines are derived from original patient tumor tissues and typically grow as monolayers. This growth pattern offers several advantages, including rapid proliferation, cost‐effectiveness, and ease of culture, observation, and measurement [6, 34]. These benefits have contributed to their widespread use in research. However, cancer cell lines also exhibit certain limitations. They possess transformed characteristics and exhibit genetic instability, growing in nonphysiological structures. Additionally, the efficiency of their generation is often low. After multiple passages, these cell lines may undergo significant genetic changes, which can compromise their ability to accurately reproduce tumor heterogeneity [11, 34].

PDTX models are established by transplanting fresh tumor tissues from patients into immunodeficient mice [35]. These models can preserve key histological and genetic features of the parental tumors, including aspects of tumor cell composition and intratumoral heterogeneity [36]. However, successful engraftment is not universal, and prolonged passaging may lead to genetic and phenotypic divergence from the original patient tumors [36]. Moreover, PDTX models are technically complex, costly, time‐consuming, and resource‐intensive to establish and maintain [11]. Importantly, PDTX models rely on a murine host environment, in which immune components are absent or functionally compromised, limiting their ability to faithfully recapitulate human tumor–immune interactions [14, 37]. In addition, cytokine signaling and stromal–immune crosstalk in mouse hosts are subject to interspecies incompatibilities, which further constrain the translational relevance of immunotherapy‐related findings derived from PDTX systems.

Tumor organoids, also known as “tumor surrogates,” are patient‐derived tumor organoids (PDTOs) that are directly obtained from patient tumor tissues through mechanical disruption or enzymatic dissociation and cultured in 3D in specific conditions to mimic the biological characteristics of the tumor [38].

PDTOs allow for functional and dynamic studies of tumor biology and modeling, rather than static, single‐timepoint characterization [39]. Compared to cancer cell lines, tumor organoids are cultured in 3D scaffolds or matrices, allowing cells to naturally organize and interact, thus better promoting cell‐to‐cell interactions. This results in a more accurate representation of the in vivo environment than 2D cultures, and the heterogeneity of the 3D structure allows cells to experience different microenvironments, fostering a range of differentiation. Overall, 3D cell cultures exhibit greater structural complexity and balance, more accurately reflecting patient‐specific tumor characteristics and maintaining key genetic and phenotypic features of the original tumor. Importantly, when combined with genomic and transcriptomic sequencing, PDTOs faithfully capture the highly personalized neoantigen landscapes of individual patients, representing a well‐documented and significant advantage for immuno‐oncology research [40]. Leveraging this feature, recent studies have demonstrated that autologous organoids can be directly used as functional platforms to screen tumor‐reactive and neoantigen‐specific T cell receptors (TCRs) [40]. By integrating single‐cell TCR/RNA sequencing with organoid‐based coculture systems, candidate TCRs identified from tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes or antigen‐enriched peripheral T cells can be functionally validated through direct interaction with patient‐matched tumor organoids. In contrast to animal models, Patient‐Derived Tumor Organoids (PDTOs) are easier to establish, provide faster and more reliable results, and accurately represent human tissue, with lower maintenance costs [41]. Unlike patient‐derived xenograft models established in immunodeficient mice, PDTOs are not confounded by interspecies incompatibilities in cytokine signaling or stromal–immune interactions, which can substantially limit the translational relevance of immunotherapy studies. In addition, because PDTOs do not require experimental animals, their use avoids ethical constraints associated with animal experimentation and aligns with the principles of animal welfare [42].

Complex Tumor Organoid Model Construction

Traditional tumor organoids, cultured from tumor cells alone, are limited in modeling the interactions between tumor cells and their microenvironment, restricting their utility for cellular immunotherapy platforms [43]. To overcome this, complex organoid models co‐culture tumor cells with key TME components, such as immune cells, vascular elements, and cancer‐associated fibroblasts, better recapitulating the in vivo tumor environment and enabling more accurate studies of tumor biology and immunotherapy responses.

Matrigel Immersion Culture

In traditional submerged culture, dissociated tumor cells are embedded in ECM scaffolds such as Matrigel, collagen, or synthetic hydrogels, followed by the addition of exogenous immune cells [39]. This approach offers flexibility in immune cell source selection and enables controlled evaluation of cytotoxicity. However, several limitations arise in immune‐focused applications. First, exogenous immune cells are introduced into an artificial ECM environment that may not accurately reflect physiological infiltration patterns. Second, as noted in Section 1.1, Matrigel's dense basement membrane–like architecture can physically restrict T cell and NK cell migration. Third, murine‐derived matrix proteins and undefined growth factors may bind or sequester cytokines, altering immune signaling gradients and potentially confounding functional readouts.

Collagen‐based matrices partially alleviate migration constraints by better mimicking the fibrillar interstitial stroma of solid tumors, thereby facilitating immune cell motility. Forsythe et al. [44] used collagen‐based ECM to create patient‐derived tumor organoids (PDTOs) and co‐cultured them with immune cells from peripheral blood monocytes, spleen, and lymph nodes to assess immune therapy efficacy. Their results showed significant therapeutic effects on appendiceal cancer organoids. Fully defined synthetic hydrogels further improve reproducibility and allow tunable stiffness and ligand density, enabling more precise interrogation of tumor–immune interactions. However, this method requires a labor‐intensive process of adding exogenous immune cells, which may not accurately reflect natural immune cell infiltration, potentially affecting immune therapy assessment.

Air‐Liquid Interface (ALI) Culture

Tumor organoids can also be cultured using ALI, where tumor tissue fragments are embedded in collagen gel or other ECM and exposed to air on the top surface while the bottom interacts with a liquid culture medium [6, 39]. This approach allows tumors to grow as a cohesive unit with their native matrix, preserving endogenous immune cells [39]. Esser et al. [45] cultured tissue samples from 42 patients who underwent nephrectomy for renal cell carcinoma in a collagen‐based ALI culture system. They characterized the resulting PDTOs through immunohistochemical staining and RNA sequencing. The results confirmed that the organoids cultured in the ALI system retained immune and stromal cells from the TME.

A significant advantage of the ALI method is that it does not require manual reconstruction, includes stromal cells, and can preserve the TME for an extended period [46]. These ALI‐cultured organoids essentially maintain fibroblasts and a variety of immune populations, including T cells, B cells, NK cells, and macrophages [37]. They even preserve the diversity of T cell receptors, suggesting that ALI‐cultured organoids hold great potential in immunotherapy. However, some studies indicate that the immune components may decline over time, possibly being unable to maintain their quantity and function in the long term [47].

Microfluidic Organ‐On‐a‐Chip Culture

Microfluidic organoid‐on‐a‐chip platforms integrate tumor organoids with microfluidic devices to establish dynamic, controllable, and physiologically relevant 3D culture systems [48]. In these platforms, dissociated tumor tissues are embedded in biomimetic extracellular matrices and introduced into microfluidic chambers, where fluid flow, nutrient gradients, and shear stress can be precisely regulated to better recapitulate the in vivo TME [49]. Ding et al. [49] developed a droplet‐based microfluidic strategy in which primary tumor cells suspended in Matrigel are encapsulated into microfluidic organoid microspheres (MOS), enabling high‐throughput generation of uniform patient‐derived organoids for drug screening and personalized therapy. Similarly, Choi et al. [50] successfully cultured pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma organoids from patient needle biopsies using microfluidic devices and demonstrated that these organoids closely resembled infiltrative stromal collagen organoids at both the genetic and phenotypic levels.

Beyond organoid formation, microfluidic systems support real‐time imaging and monitoring of tumor growth and treatment responses while maintaining consistent organoid size and microenvironmental conditions. Importantly, these platforms enable controlled coculture of tumor organoids with immune or stromal cells under continuous perfusion, facilitating the investigation of tumor–immune interactions within a complex TME [41]. For example, co‐culture of tumor organoids with NK cells in microfluidic chips revealed that tri‐specific killer engager antibodies (TriKEs) significantly enhanced NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity, highlighting the potential of microfluidic organoid models for immunotherapy prediction. Moreover, by simulating biomechanical cues such as shear flow, mechanical stress, and matrix stiffness, microfluidic technologies better capture key physical properties of the TME that influence tumor behavior [6]. Despite these advantages, challenges remain, including long‐term maintenance of organoid viability, reproducibility across platforms, and scalability for clinical translation, underscoring the need for further technological optimization.

Adding Vascular Components

Blood vessels are primarily responsible for transporting oxygen, nutrients, and removing waste. When organoids cultured in vitro reach a certain size and complexity, central necrosis becomes inevitable unless vascularization occurs. The lack of vasculature limits the size, complexity, maturity, and functionality of organoids, making the cultivation of vascularized organoids crucial [51]. After mesodermal induction, endothelial networks form through angiogenesis and mature into stable blood vessels, which are covered by pericytes and surrounded by a basement membrane. One study achieved vascularized organoids by coculturing mesodermal progenitor cells with tumor organoids to form blood vessels in vitro [51]. Lim et al. (56) established endothelial cell–cocultured hepatocellular carcinoma organoids by co‐culturing PDX‐derived organoids with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). With the advent of microfluidic technology, Quintard et al. [52] developed a microfluidic chip in which organoids were surrounded by a mixture of HUVECs and fibroblasts and perfused via hydrogel propulsion and air injection, improving organoid growth, maturation, and functionality. Despite these advances, these approaches remain technically challenging, difficult to scale, and still fall short of fully recapitulating the complexity, stability, and dynamic circulation of native vasculature. Consequently, vascularization remains a major bottleneck for long‐term immunotherapy studies, as effective immune cell infiltration and functional modeling require physiologically relevant and robust blood vessel networks.

Adding Cancer‐Associated Fibroblasts (CAFs)

In the TME, cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) have been shown to play multiple roles in tumor initiation and progression. They secrete growth factors, inflammatory ligands, and extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, promoting tumor cell proliferation, drug resistance, and immune evasion, among other effects [53]. Currently, co‐culture models of CAFs with tumor organoids have been established. For instance, Wallisch et al. [54] described in detail the co‐culture steps of colorectal cancer organoids with fibroblasts and used this model for drug testing. Strating et al. [55] proposed a long‐term co‐culture model of colon cancer organoids and CAFs in highly standardized serum‐free media and optimized ECM conditions. The results showed that under these conditions, cancer cells and CAFs spontaneously organized into upper‐layer structures, while the ECM became stiffer and contracted, closely resembling the histology of patient‐derived colon tumors. Takeuchi et al. [56] co‐cultured patient‐derived pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells with mesenchymal cells (which can generate CAFs) and endothelial cells derived from human‐induced pluripotent stem cells. Using air‐liquid interface co‐culture technology, they generated fused pancreatic cancer organoids, summarizing the tumor immune microenvironment, including the heterogeneity of CAFs.

Application of Tumor Organoids in Immunotherapy

Tumor immunotherapy refers to the use of immunological principles and methods to enhance the immunogenicity of tumor cells and their sensitivity to effector cell–mediated killing. It aims to stimulate and boost the body's anti‐tumor immune response, as well as introduce immune cells or effector molecules into the patient's body to collaborate with the immune system in attacking tumor cells and inhibiting tumor growth. Current immunotherapies include immune checkpoint inhibitors, cellular immunotherapy, bispecific antibody therapy, oncolytic viruses, tumor vaccines, etc. (Figure 3).

Immunotherapy.

The history of the development of tumor immunotherapy is detailed elsewhere [2]. The first scientific attempt to regulate the immune system to cure cancer was made by two German doctors, Fehleisen and Busch, who observed significant tumor regression following erysipelas infection. The next major advancement came from William Bradley Coley, often referred to as the father of immunotherapy who in 1891 first attempted to use the immune system to treat bone cancer, although his subsequent research was largely overlooked. Later, with numerous advancements in immunology and cancer research, discoveries were made regarding tumor antigens, and theories of immune surveillance and immune editing were proposed. This led to the application of tumor biotherapies, such as cytokines, antibodies, and cell therapies. In 2013, cancer immunotherapy reached a new turning point with milestone results, and by 2018, two scientists were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their discoveries of immune checkpoint inhibitors.

In recent years, the development of tumor organoid technology has enabled researchers to move beyond initial attempts at co‐culturing tumor organoids with immune cells, instead creating more sophisticated models that simulate the TME to study tumor immunity. Today, tumor organoids are being used to predict and evaluate the efficacy of immune therapies, playing a crucial role in the development of personalized cancer treatment strategies. The rapid advancement of immunotherapy has led to the development of an increasing number of immunotherapeutic drugs, offering new hope for cancer patients. Researchers are also exploring combination therapies and conducting in‐depth studies of the tumor microenvironment, providing a foundation for developing new therapeutic strategies. The integration of tumor organoid technology with immunotherapy has the potential to revolutionize cancer treatment, enabling the development of more effective and targeted therapies.

Application in Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors Research

In the treatment of solid tumors, T cells often become exhausted, characterized by reduced cytokine expression and effector capacity. This exhaustion renders T cells resistant to reactivation, making it challenging to mount an effective immune response against cancer. Exhausted T cells exhibit high levels of inhibitory surface molecules, which prevent T cell activation [57]. These inhibitory surface molecules, known as immune checkpoints, are expressed on the surface of T cells and play a crucial role in T‐cell exhaustion. Two prominent immune checkpoints are programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA‐4). These checkpoints inhibit T cell activation, preventing the immune system from effectively targeting cancer cells. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance antitumor immune responses. By targeting regulatory pathways in T cells, ICIs can overcome T‐cell exhaustion and restore the immune system's ability to recognize and eliminate cancer cells. This approach has shown significant promise in the treatment of various types of cancer, offering new hope for patients with solid tumors.

Blocking the interaction between CTLA‐4 and CD 80/86, as well as the interaction between PD‐1 and PD‐L1/2, is the most prominent and effective strategy in ICIs [58]. ICIs targeting PD‐1/PD‐L1 and CTLA‐4 have shown clinical responses in various tumors, including melanoma, skin squamous cell carcinoma, non‐small cell lung cancer, and head and neck cancers [59]. Monoclonal antibodies have been developed to inhibit these interactions, ensuring sustained T cell activation and effective antitumor responses. These drugs include ipilimumab, which targets CTLA‐4; nivolumab and pembrolizumab, which target PD‐1; and atezolizumab, avelumab, and durvalumab, which target PD‐L1 on tumor cells [58]. Currently, an increasing number of immune checkpoints have been identified, including T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), T‐cell immunoglobulin and mucin‐domain containing‐3 (TIM‐3), lymphocyte‐activation gene 3 (LAG‐3), V‐domain Ig suppressor of T cell activation (VISTA), B and T lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA), and signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα). Functional monoclonal antibodies targeting these molecules are under development [60].

Tumor organoids are frequently used to evaluate the efficacy of ICIs. Scognamiglio et al. [61] showed that patient‐derived chordoma organoids exhibit heterogeneous, dose‐dependent in vitro responses to nivolumab regardless of PD‐L1 status, supporting their use as exploratory platforms for studying immune checkpoint inhibitor effects rather than predictors of clinical outcome. Zhou et al. [62] explored using immune checkpoint inhibitors to improve cholangiocarcinoma treatment by co‐culturing cholangiocarcinoma patient‐derived organoids with peripheral blood mononuclear cells or T cells, discussing the application of this novel co‐culture system in screening new immune therapeutic antibodies and precision medicine. Esser et al. [45] cultivated clear cell renal cell carcinoma patient‐derived organoids in an air‐liquid interface culture, testing the treatment effect of nivolumab, and found that tumor organoids retained key features of the original tumor and could detect treatment responses, thus serving as a tool for personalized therapy research. In addition, while immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown success in cancer therapy, there remains a lack of effective treatment strategies to overcome cancer immunotherapy resistance. The use of tumor organoid models is helpful in developing new strategies to combat resistance. Sun et al. [63] tested the antitumor efficacy of targeting innate immune kinase TANK‐binding kinase 1 (TBK1) inhibition combined with PD‐1 blockade using patient‐derived tumor models. The results indicated that targeting TBK1 is a novel and effective strategy for overcoming resistance to cancer immunotherapy.

Currently, available ICIs (e.g., PD‐1/PD‐L1 monoclonal antibodies) continue to face challenges such as resistance and off‐target toxicity [64]. Therefore, combination therapies have become a key focus of future research. Combining ICIs with other immunotherapy strategies (such as ACT therapy and tumor vaccines) or conventional therapies (such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy) is expected to enhance therapeutic effects synergistically while minimizing adverse reactions [60]. Additionally, as tumor immunology research progresses, other immune checkpoint targets may be identified, offering new opportunities for the development of ICIs. This further highlights the broad prospects of tumor organoids for studying combination therapies and screening for immune checkpoint targets [65].

Application in Cell Immunotherapy

Cellular immunotherapy, commonly referred to as adoptive cellular therapy (ACT), can be enhanced by the use of tumor organoids in ACT research. This includes various approaches such as tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, T‐cell receptor‐engineered T cells (TCR‐T) therapy, chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell (CAR‐T) therapy, and chimeric antigen receptor natural killer cell (CAR‐NK) therapy.

TIL Therapy

TIL therapy involves isolating lymphocytes from obtained tumor tissue, expanding them in a culture medium enriched with interleukin‐2 (IL‐2), and reinfusing the expanded TILs into the patient to enhance the immune cells' antitumor activity [66]. The most commonly used method for TIL production is isolating tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes from tumor tissue and expanding these cells in vitro [67].

The expert consensus guidelines from 2024 provide an overview of the management and best practices for TIL therapy. The guidelines mention that Lifileucel is the first FDA‐approved autologous cryopreserved TIL cell therapy product, showing clinical significance in treating melanoma, with an objective response rate (ORR) of 31.4%, as evaluated by an independent review committee. The guidelines also note that early data indicate promising results in treating advanced or metastatic non–small cell lung cancer, recurrent, metastatic or persistent cervical cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and breast cancer [68].

Tumor organoids, particularly patient‐derived tumor organoids (PDTOs), have emerged as physiologically relevant platforms for evaluating and optimizing TIL therapy, with melanoma being the most extensively studied model. By preserving tumor architecture and cellular heterogeneity, organoids enable the assessment of TIL infiltration, activation, and cytotoxicity in a personalized context. Ou et al. [69] demonstrated that melanoma organoids co‐cultured with TILs expanded using IL‐2 and αPD‐1 showed enhanced CD8+ T‐cell enrichment, improved chemotaxis, and superior tumor‐killing capacity, effects that were further augmented by the small‐molecule inhibitor Navitoclax.

Owing to their polyclonal T‐cell receptor (TCR) repertoire, TILs may better address intratumoral heterogeneity than other adoptive cell therapies, such as CAR‐T or TCR‐T cells, particularly in solid tumors [67]. Despite challenges related to labor‐intensive manufacturing, high costs, and limited accessibility, tumor organoid‐based platforms are expected to facilitate personalized TIL selection, mechanistic studies of immune resistance, and the rational design of combination therapies. In this context, insights from emerging immunomodulatory strategies–including nano‐enabled delivery systems and immune‐sensitizing approaches explored in chemoimmunotherapy and related settings—may provide complementary rationale for optimizing TIL‐based regimens without altering their cellular therapeutic core [70, 71, 72]. Collectively, these advances are anticipated to accelerate the clinical translation of TIL‐based immunotherapies.

TCR‐T Therapy

TCR‐T therapy involves genetically modifying T cells to express specific TCRs targeting tumor‐associated antigens. These modified T cells are then expanded ex vivo and infused back into the patient, enabling them to specifically recognize and kill tumor cells, thereby achieving tumor treatment goal [73]. TCR‐T therapy relies on its ability to recognize tumor‐specific antigens located either on the cell surface or derived from inside the cell [74].

On August 1, 2024, the first engineered T‐cell therapy for the treatment of solid tumors, Tecelra (afamitresgene autoleucel), was successfully approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of patients with unresectable or metastatic synovial sarcoma who had previously received chemotherapy [75]. The objective response rate (ORR) for TECELRA treatment in adult patients was 43% [76]. This suggests that TCR‐T therapy holds great promise.

PDTO models have been used to test the antitumor efficacy of TCR‐T therapy. Li et al. [77] proposed a tumor‐specific T cell co‐culture system, which effectively expands tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) by co‐culturing colorectal cancer organoids with TILs ex vivo. The generated TCRs were then isolated and transferred to peripheral T cells to generate tumor‐specific TCR‐T cells. They observed and evaluated the tumor‐specific reactivity of the TCR‐T cells and their cytotoxic ability against autologous tumor cells in PDTOs. Wang et al. [78] combined ultradeep single‐cell sequencing with autologous tumor organoids to identify and functionally validate tumor‐responsive TCRs in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, highlighting the feasibility of organoid‐based platforms for in vitro characterization of tumor‐specific T‐cell reactivity in a proof‐of‐concept setting.

A significant advantage of TCR‐T therapy is that TCR‐T cells are naturally expressed and fully humanized, thereby reducing the risk of immune rejection. Moreover, TCR‐T cells possess immune memory, allowing them to persist in the body for extended periods and enhancing their therapeutic effects [10]. Although TCR‐T therapy has developed into a promising method to target various tumor antigens in solid tumors, many obstacles still remain, such as the limited number of identified targets with sufficient safety and efficacy, tumor antigen heterogeneity, immune evasion by tumors, off‐target effects, and T‐cell unresponsiveness and exhaustion [73]. These challenges suggest that organoids could serve as a powerful tool for future research.

CAR‐T Therapy

CAR‐T therapy involves using autologous T cells, which are extracted and genetically engineered to expand extensively in vitro [10]. These T cells are then re‐infused into the patient's body for immune treatment. CAR‐T therapy enables T cells to recognize target cells through implanted antibodies instead of their own TCR recognition, thereby activating T cells to trigger cytotoxic effects. In 2012, Carl June reported on CAR‐T therapy, and in 2017, CAR‐T‐CD19 received FDA approval as a new drug for the treatment of pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Currently, six CAR‐T cell products have been approved by regulators: tisagenlecleucel (tisa‐cel) 1, 2, 3; axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel) 4, 5; lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso‐cel) 6, 7; and brexucabtagene autoleucel (brexu‐cel) 8, 9, all targeting CD19 and applicable for the treatment of certain B‐cell malignancies. Additionally, idecabtagene vicleucel (ide‐cel) 10 and ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta‐cel) 11 target B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA) and are used for the treatment of multiple myeloma [79]. Nowadays, CAR‐T therapy is also being explored for various solid tumors.

Tumor organoids have been developed as an in vitro model for testing CAR‐T cell therapy in solid tumors. Jacob et al. [80] co‐cultured patient‐derived glioblastoma organoids with 2173BBz CAR‐T cells to test the efficacy of CAR‐T cells targeting EGFRvIII. The results showed significant expansion of CAR‐T cells in the co‐culture system, and EGFRvIII‐positive cells were targeted and killed by the CAR‐T cells. However, glioblastoma organoids still maintained EGFRvIII‐positive tumor cells three days later. This indicates that glioblastoma organoids can be used for rapid testing of CAR‐T cell therapeutic responses to endogenous tumor targets. Tang et al. [81] isolated PBMCs from healthy volunteers, cultured them under specific conditions, and transduced activated T cells using lentiviral vectors to generate CAR‐T cells. They established patient‐derived craniopharyngioma organoids and evaluated the cytotoxic effects of B7‐H3‐targeted CAR‐T cells. The results showed that B7‐H3‐targeted CAR‐T cells had limited anti‐tumor effects in the PDTO model compared to traditional 2D cell culture models, suggesting that CAR‐T cells' poor infiltration into craniopharyngioma organoids might impair anti‐tumor efficacy. This further underscores the limitations of CAR‐T therapy for solid tumors. To better understand the mechanisms of cell therapies and optimize treatment design, Dekkers et al. [82] proposed applying the BEHAV3D imaging system for live tracking of engineered T cells cultured with patient‐derived solid tumor organoids. They characterized a group of highly cytotoxic T cells with behavior‐specific gene features, delving into the cellular immunotherapy mechanisms for solid tumors. Önder et al. [83] designed bridge CAR‐T cells (AdCAR‐T) by introducing broad‐spectrum tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs) and CAR “linkers,” and evaluated their cytotoxic effects in tumor organoids derived from metastatic breast cancer patients. In the PDTO model, they demonstrated the feasibility of using AdCAR‐T‐based precision immunotherapy to target various tumor‐associated antigens, offering a new strategy for targeted immunotherapy in solid tumors.

Progress has also been made in studying the toxicity of CAR‐T therapy using PDTO models. Luque et al. [84] simulated CAR‐T cell therapy responses in heterogeneous tumor‐derived organoids. Their study showed that a small number of CAR‐T cells had limited efficacy, whereas larger numbers of CAR‐T cells had significantly better efficacy but increased side effects. They suggested that appropriate CAR‐T cell therapy strategies could enhance efficacy while reducing side effects, indicating that CAR‐T therapy holds great promise as a cancer immunotherapy. Liang et al. [85] co‐expressed SMAD7 (a TGF‐β signaling inhibitor) and HER2‐targeted CAR in engineered T cells. The results showed that SMAD7 significantly reduced inflammatory cytokine production triggered by antigen and enhanced CAR‐T cell infiltration and sustained activation of patient‐derived tumor organoids. This research used the PDTO model to demonstrate the feasibility of SMAD7 co‐expression as a method for improving the efficacy and safety of CAR‐T therapy in solid tumor treatment.

CAR‐T therapy has shown efficacy in treating chemotherapy‐resistant hematologic malignancies such as leukemia, myeloma, and non‐Hodgkin's B‐cell lymphoma [86]. However, its application in solid tumors faces challenges, including limited tumor penetration and infiltration, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and adverse events associated with the therapy [86, 87]. Current research is focused on expanding CAR‐T cell therapy to solid tumors and further improving its safety and efficacy.

CAR‐NK Therapy

CAR‐NK is a cellular engineering strategy that modifies NK cells to specifically recognize and kill tumor cells. CAR‐NK cells are activated by targeting tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs) and release cytotoxic cytokines, such as granzyme, to kill tumor cells [88]. CAR‐NK therapy is currently in preclinical or clinical research stages, mainly targeting CD19, NKG2D, CD7, or CD3 [89].

Several studies have demonstrated the effective anti‐tumor effects of CAR‐NK therapy using tumor organoid platforms. Lin et al. [90] created CD276‐targeted CAR‐NK cells from pluripotent stem cells and established patient‐derived esophageal squamous cell carcinoma organoids to assess the cytotoxicity of CD276‐targeted CAR‐NK cells. The results showed that the CD276‐targeted CAR‐NK cells exhibited specific and significant cytotoxicity in CD276‐positive organoid models. Zhu et al. [91] constructed ROBO1‐targeted CAR‐NK cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of ovarian cancer patients and established organoids from the same ovarian cancer patients. The study demonstrated that ovarian cancer organoids could be lysed or killed by ROBO1‐NK cells. Schnalzger et al. [92] established metastatic colorectal cancer organoid models and monitored tumor antigen‐specific cytotoxicity of EGFRvIII‐ or FRIZZLED receptor‐targeted CAR‐NK92 cells. The study showed that CAR‐NK92 cells effectively killed tumor organoids.

Compared to CAR‐T cells, CAR‐NK cells recognize their targets in a human leukocyte antigen‐unrestricted manner and do not exhibit the toxicity associated with CAR‐T therapy, such as cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity, nor do they carry the risk of graft‐versus‐host disease [93]. CAR‐NK cells can be engineered to target multiple antigens, enhancing in vivo proliferation and persistence, increasing infiltration into solid tumors, overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and ultimately achieving effective anti‐tumor responses [94]. Three key challenges in the CAR‐NK field that need to be addressed in the near future include clinical safety, cryopreservation of CAR‐NK cells, and challenges posed by the tumor microenvironment [95].

Application in Bispecific Antibodies Therapy

Bispecific antibodies can simultaneously bind two different antigens, thereby enhancing recognition or interacting with cytotoxic cells and targeting tumors to bring them into close proximity, resulting in tumor cell cytotoxicity [96]. Blinatumomab is the first bispecific T cell engager (TCE) targeting CD19 and CD3, which was approved by the FDA (2014) and EMA (2015) for the treatment of relapsed and/or refractory pre‐B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (RR B‐ALL). Since then, 10 other innovative bispecific antibodies have been approved between 2021 and 2024 [97]. Currently, over 200 clinical trials of bispecific antibodies are underway, with the most popular targets being CD3, PD‐L1, EGFR, PD‐1, HER2, and others [97].

Tumoroids can be used for screening bispecific antibodies as well as evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of bispecific antibody treatments. Herpers et al. [98] established a colorectal cancer organoid biobank and subsequently conducted large‐scale functional screening of bispecific antibodies targeting two antigens, identifying MCLA‐158, a therapeutic EGFR × LGR5 bispecific antibody, which was effective against epithelial tumors. This further demonstrates that tumoroids have predictive value in supporting personalized medicine. Yuhong Liu and colleagues [99] introduced a new immunotherapy method based on hydroxyapatite (HA) gene delivery systems to produce bispecific antibodies for HER2‐positive cancer treatment. The results showed that the generated BsAbHER2 exhibited strong anticancer activity in patient‐derived HER2‐expressing tumor organoid models. This also proved that organoids can closely mimic the complex structures and cellular heterogeneity of tumors, allowing for personalized testing of bispecific antibody efficacy. Kastenschmidt et al. [100] cultured patient‐derived follicular lymphoma organoids from surgical biopsy samples. Treatment of the tumor organoids with therapeutic bispecific antibodies resulted in B cell killing and T cell activation, providing insights into the mechanistic basis of novel immunotherapies. This study confirmed that tumor organoids offer a powerful platform for evaluating treatment responses in follicular lymphoma and advancing precision medicine.

Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) enhance antitumor immunity by simultaneously engaging T cells and tumor cells, thereby promoting immune synapse formation and T‐cell–mediated cytotoxicity, while potentially reducing off‐target toxicity compared with conventional therapies. However, BsAb development remains challenging due to the need to identify suitable antigen pairs and to optimize molecular architecture, binding affinity, and stability to meet safety, developability, and manufacturability requirements [101]. Tumor organoid–based models provide a personalized and physiologically relevant platform to address these challenges, as patient‐derived organoids preserve tumor‐specific antigen expression and intratumoral heterogeneity, enabling antigen characterization and BsAb candidate screening. Moreover, organoid–immune cell co‐culture systems allow direct assessment of BsAb‐mediated T‐cell redirection, antitumor efficacy, and potential on‐target toxicities, while also facilitating the evaluation of combination strategies and resistance mechanisms. Together, these advantages support the rational optimization of BsAb design, dosing strategies, and clinical translation.

Application in Oncolytic Viruses Therapy

Oncolytic viruses are genetically modified virus vectors that exhibit oncolytic and immune‐stimulating properties [102]. Their anti‐tumor effects occur through replication within tumor cells, directly lysing the tumor cells, and triggering the release of cytokines and activation of the immune system to induce systemic anti‐tumor effects.

Currently, several oncolytic viruses are used in the treatment of malignant tumors. Rigvir is used to treat melanoma, oncolytic adenovirus H101 for head and neck cancers, and a modified version of herpes simplex virus type 1, T‐VEC, was approved by the FDA for melanoma treatment. In 2021, a modified HSV called Delytact was approved in Japan for the treatment of malignant gliomas. Many different viruses, including adenoviruses and herpes simplex viruses, have been utilized as oncolytic viruses [103].

Patient‐derived organoids are now used to assess the oncolytic and anti‐tumor activity of oncolytic viruses, as well as the anti‐tumor effects when combined with other immunotherapies. Hamdan et al. [104] cultured kidney cancer organoids from tumor tissue obtained from patients who underwent radical nephrectomy. After adding oncolytic adenovirus to the organoid culture, most cancer cells were killed by day 4, proving that organoids can be used as a platform for adenovirus testing. After optimizing kidney cancer organoids as a functional test platform for adenoviruses, the researchers used this platform for ADCC experiments with PBMCs and polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs), showing that when PBMCs and PMNs were combined, all PD‐L1‐expressing cells were killed. This suggested that the organoid platform further enhanced the efficacy of oncolytic adenovirus and the synergistic effects of the immune system in tumor eradication. Thai et al. [105] used colorectal cancer organoids as a test platform and demonstrated that oncolytic virus V937 induced oncolysis in colorectal cancer organoids. They also described how V937 treatment might increase the expression of ICAM‐1 on tumor cells, leading to increased V937 infection and oncolysis. The researchers further used a co‐culture system of colorectal cancer organoids and immune cells to describe the mechanism of action of V937 monotherapy and its combination with anti‐PD‐1 pembrolizumab, with the results indicating that oncolytic virus therapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors is more effective. Rangsitratkul et al. [106] used human bladder cancer patient‐derived organoids as a test tool to measure the immunostimulatory effects of a novel oncolytic vesicular stomatitis virus (VSVd51‐hGM‐CSF) in autologous patient immune cells. The study showed that the virus induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) in bladder cancer organoids and activated autologous immune cells in vitro, suggesting that VSVd51‐hGM‐CSF is a promising viral immunotherapy that could benefit bladder cancer patients.

Oncolytic virus therapy is a promising cancer treatment with the advantages of selectively killing tumor cells and enhancing immune responses. Although these studies suggest that patient‐derived tumor organoids are promising platforms for studying the infectivity and cytotoxicity of oncolytic viruses, the immune response triggered by oncolytic viruses has not yet been studied in complex immune organoids, and safety remains a consideration [107].

Application in Cancer Vaccines

The mechanism of cancer vaccines involves triggering immune responses against tumor‐associated antigens, aiming to activate the immune system to recognize and fight tumor cells [108]. Therapeutic cancer vaccines have been developed for decades, but their clinical efficacy has remained elusive. So far, only one therapeutic cancer vaccine, sipuleucel‐T, has been approved by the U.S. FDA for prostate cancer, extending patient survival by only four months [109]. Most cancer vaccines are still in preclinical and clinical research phases [110].

Some researchers have pointed out that immune organoids are of significant importance for vaccine design and decision‐making [111]. Tumor organoids provide an effective and reliable platform for studying the anti‐tumor activity of vaccines in vitro, and the developed cancer vaccines may offer new hope for cancer treatment. Huang et al. [112] developed an in situ DC vaccine (HELA‐Exos) formed by engineering exosomes and co‐cultured PBMCs from breast cancer patients with patient‐derived tumor organoids for two weeks. The results showed that HELA‐Exos promoted in situ DC activation and subsequent tumor‐reactive CD8 T cell activation, exhibiting strong anti‐tumor activity in breast cancer organoids. Some researchers have also established cervical cancer tumor organoids, suggesting that they can serve as a reliable and powerful tool for screening therapeutic HPV vaccines [113]. Tumor organoids can also be used to address chemotherapy resistance in cancer vaccines. Qin et al. [114] used patient‐derived pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma organoids to test the function of melanoma‐associated antigen A isoforms (MAGEA) in chemotherapy resistance, and the results suggested that DNA vaccines targeting multiple MAGEA antigens could induce a strong immune response against gemcitabine‐resistant tumor growth, proposing a new treatment strategy for pancreatic cancer.

Cancer vaccines are a promising tumor treatment method with advantages such as specificity and durability, but they also face challenges, including high production costs, antigen recognition issues, individual variability, and immune suppression [115]. Several issues must be addressed, including the selection of suitable tumor antigens and adjuvants, appropriate delivery methods, and effective strategies to overcome immune attacks. The reasons for cancer vaccine failures include obstacles related to the vaccine, such as target antigen selection, administration routes, successful delivery to target sites, platform design, effective production, and adjuvant implementation. Additionally, the major obstacles to overcome are tumor‐induced immune suppression and immune evasion mechanisms, which result in insufficient immune effector cell homing to tumors, reduced lifespan and efficacy, and functional impairment and exhaustion [116].

Summary and Outlook

Research in immunotherapy has catalyzed the development of innovative cancer treatments. The advent of precision medicine marks a significant shift in cancer therapy, moving from a tumor‐type‐based approach to one focused on the molecular characteristics of tumors and their microenvironments. Preclinical models that effectively replicate the tumor microenvironment are essential for advancing cancer immunotherapy. The emergence of organoid technology has revitalized tumor research in this context.

Understanding the factors that contribute to varying patient responses to immunotherapy is crucial. Tumor organoids are three‐dimensional structures derived directly from cancer patient tissues, eliminating the need for prior cultivation. These organoids reflect the genetic and molecular traits of the patient's specific cancer, preserving the inherent heterogeneity of the original tumor. They effectively simulate the complex architecture of tumors, including diverse cell types and spatial organization, which is vital for studying tumor heterogeneity and understanding the differential responses observed across tumor types and individuals.

Despite their considerable promise, tumor organoid models face several critical challenges that limit their current application in immunotherapy research. These challenges can be broadly categorized into five key aspects: tumor heterogeneity, model limitations, standardization and reproducibility, immune microenvironment representation, and constraints associated with patient‐derived samples.

First, one major issue is the intrinsic heterogeneity of tumors and the difficulty of fully capturing this diversity in organoid cultures. Tumors consist of genetically, phenotypically, and functionally diverse cell populations, and organoids derived from limited tissue samples may fail to represent the full spectrum of intratumoral heterogeneity.

Second, tumor organoid models exhibit inherent limitations in fully recapitulating the in vivo tumor microenvironment. Although organoids represent a substantial improvement over two‐dimensional cultures, commonly used matrix‐based systems lack essential components such as functional vasculature, immune infiltrates, and complex stromal interactions. These missing elements are critical for accurately modeling tumor progression and immunotherapy mechanisms in vivo, thereby restricting the biological completeness of current organoid systems.

Third, the absence of standardized culture protocols poses a major barrier to reproducibility and cross‐study comparison. Variations in extracellular matrix composition, growth factor supplementation, culture media formulations, and passaging strategies result in substantial differences in organoid growth efficiency, cellular composition, and phenotypic stability. For example, the success rate for forming pure non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) organoids is only 17% [117], compared to 41% [27] for rhabdomyosarcoma organoids and 74.4% [118] for cholangiocarcinoma organoids. Establishing standardized and optimized protocols is therefore essential to ensure reproducible generation of organoids with consistent three‐dimensional architecture, gene expression profiles, and treatment responses [38].

Fourth, the limited representation of the immune microenvironment significantly constrains the utility of tumor organoids for immunotherapy research. Most tumor organoid models lack endogenous immune cell populations, impairing the study of immune evasion mechanisms and limiting evaluation of immunotherapies that depend on dynamic immune–tumor interactions, such as immune checkpoint blockade or adoptive cell therapies. Advanced approaches, including air–liquid interface cultures and microfluidic organ‐on‐chip platforms, have been developed to incorporate immune components; however, these systems are still challenged by gradual immune cell depletion and restricted long‐term stability.

Fifth, patient‐derived organoid models face practical and biological constraints. Obtaining sufficient high‐quality tumor tissue is often challenging, particularly from small biopsies or advanced‐stage patients. Moreover, maintaining tumor purity during organoid establishment is difficult, as normal epithelial or stromal cells may be co‐isolated and overgrow malignant cells; notably, up to 75% of short‐term tumor organoid cultures have been reported to contain normal cell contamination [119]. In addition, prolonged in vitro culture may result in genetic or phenotypic drift, potentially reducing fidelity to the original patient tumor. These factors limit the long‐term stability and translational relevance of patient‐derived organoid models.

Several approaches may help address the current limitations of tumor organoid models. Tumor heterogeneity can be better captured through multi‐region sampling and the establishment of parallel organoid cultures from the same patient, supported by single‐cell and spatial profiling technologies. Limitations in recapitulating the tumor microenvironment may be partially overcome by advanced co‐culture systems incorporating immune and stromal components, including air–liquid interface cultures and microfluidic organ‐on‐a‐chip platforms. To improve reproducibility, standardized protocols for tissue processing, extracellular matrices, and culture media, together with automated culture and quality control frameworks, are increasingly being developed. The lack of a complete immune microenvironment can be addressed using short‐term co‐culture with autologous immune cells, enabling functional interrogation of tumor–immune interactions despite limited immune cell persistence. Finally, challenges associated with patient‐derived models may be mitigated through optimized tissue handling, early‐passage cryopreservation, and periodic molecular characterization to maintain fidelity to the original tumors.

Despite these challenges, tumor organoid models have become valuable platforms in immunotherapy research. They are widely used as in vitro systems to investigate and compare diverse immunotherapeutic strategies, including cell‐based therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, bispecific antibodies, oncolytic viruses, and cancer vaccines. As organoid technologies continue to evolve, increasingly sophisticated models may support patient‐specific functional testing, evaluation of treatment responses and toxicity, and early‐stage therapeutic development. The promising evolution and adaptation of these models will facilitate clinical translation efforts to create new combination therapies, ultimately advancing the field of immunotherapy and realizing the potential for personalized treatment approaches.
