Gender and culture shape prosociality more than heat stress in a five-country experiment
Authors: Alessandra Cassar, Andrew Hobbs, Jesse Anttila-Hughes, Francesco Bogliacino, Travis J Lybbert, Irvin Rojas, Kelvin Mashisia Shikuku, John Chetwynd, Jake Cosgrove, Alexander Courtman, Andrew Hall, Stephanie Hermoso, Scott Klaus, Antonia Sottile, Nikita Tkachenko, Bruce Wydick, Valerio Capraro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA consistent finding regarding human societies is that higher temperatures lead to antisocial behavior, including more crime, violence, aggression, and civil conflict. A large body of research documents this relationship at the societal level, but an understanding of the psychological and physiological mechanisms underpinning the effect of heat on antisocial behavior remains less understood. We present an experiment designed to isolate the impact of elevated temperatures on social preferences, namely egalitarianism, welfare maximization, selfishness, spite, and competitiveness. We exogenously varied temperatures across lab sessions in five countries in which randomly matched participants make incentivized distributional choices to others and decide whether to compete in a tournament. As an additional manipulation, we include a psychological stressor to potentially trigger an antisocial response when combined with higher temperatures. Our sessions were carried out with university student participants in Colombia, India, Kenya, Mexico, and the United States (N=1,636). We find that despite a strong negative effect on mood, elevated temperatures had little or no effect on social and economic preferences across countries, especially in comparison to the substantial and significant correlations we find with gender and culture. Future research should more closely examine the alternative channels through which elevated temperatures negatively impact social behavior.Keywords: climate change, heat stress, gender, culture, prosociality

Our multicountry experiment investigates the direct impact of heat stress on prosocial preferences linked to economic behavior, complementing previous research on the associations between heat and antisocial actions such as aggression and violence. Despite previous findings suggesting temperature-induced behavioral changes, our results show that elevated temperatures do not significantly influence economic decisions or prosocial behavior. Instead, gender and cultural differences markedly shape economic preferences. This study advances our understanding of the factors that actually govern social and economic preferences across diverse populations, indicating that cultural and, especially, gender influences are more potent than environmental stressors such as heat.

Introduction

The association between high temperature and human conflict has been a long-running object of social commentary. Observations on heat’s negative effects on behavior can be found in sources ranging from Aristotle’s (350 BCE) reflection on the prevalence of conflict in hotter climates (1) to novels such as To Kill a Mockingbird (Harper Lee) and The Sun Also Rises (Ernest Hemingway), in which heat waves spur human violence. Modern advances in both data availability and econometric methods have allowed social scientists to test and quantify this relationship, with evidence showing high temperatures to be associated with higher crime (e.g. (2, 3)), inter-group violence (e.g. (4, 5)), and institutional breakdown (e.g. (6–8)). Advances in research design indicate many of these associations to be causal (9, 10), but operate via a variety of complex socioeconomic and biological mechanisms across temporal and spatial scales. For instance, it is now well-documented that high temperatures hinder economic productivity, especially in agriculture (6, 11, 12), leading to group-level disputes over the distribution of scarce resources (13), and conflict at the scale of the region or nation state (9, 14–17).

Ample observational studies also document a direct link between heat and individual-level human aggression and violence across a variety of contexts, including sports, prisons, sexual assault, murder, and suicide (18–22). Nevertheless, a host of confounding factors has made causal channels difficult to identify. For example, Cohen and Gonzalez (3) find crime in Mexico increases with temperature but show 28% of this increase to be due to higher alcohol consumption on hot days. Strong serial correlation in temperature means that correlations with temperature may be driven by more temporally complex cognitive and physiological mechanisms, such as poor sleep (23) or elevated cortisol (24). Recent systematic surveys furthermore find ambiguous and conflicting evidence on the causal effects of high temperatures on specific types of behavior (25–27). Exactly why high temperatures have such a strong contemporaneous association with individual-level aggression and violence thus remains unclear.

We present laboratory experiment results from five countries designed to test the extent to which the association between high temperatures and individual level violence may be driven by a direct effect of heating on social preferences themselves. To do so, we exogenously manipulated the ambient temperature that randomly matched individuals experienced while engaging in standard experimental economic games, thereby allowing us to isolate and estimate the direct causal effects of temperature on contemporaneously measured social preferences. We focus on preferences linked to pro- and antisociality (egalitarianism, maximization of total payoffs, selfishness, spite) as well as competitiveness in order to focus on key psychological mechanisms governing conflict, and compare our results to characteristics known to predict variation in social preferences such as gender and cultural context. We further vary our main heating treatment via a psychological “trigger” event, losing an experimental game, to test whether heat alters prosociality only when combined with a stressor, as is widely in suggested in the literature (19, 20, 25).

Hypotheses

We expand the original heat-aggression hypothesis (18) to prosocial preferences and account for gender and culture as potential moderators of this relationship. Specifically, we test three hypotheses, each with two parts.

(H1)

Heat stress (a) reduces individual prosociality and (b) when combined with a psychological trigger.

Heat stress could deteriorate prosocial preferences due to a fight-or-flight response to acute stress (28), which encompasses both behavioral and physiological reactions activated by perceived threats or stressors (29).a

While field studies appear to support the heat-aggression hypothesis, results from laboratory studies provide little evidence of direct effects (2, 25). This raises the possibility that heat stress alone may be insufficient to erode prosociality. H1(b) states that it is the interaction of heat stress and an additional irritant or trigger that erodes prosociality. Almås et al. (25), for example, find that heat induced destructive behavior only among an ethnic subgroup in Kenya that was recently marginalized politically.

Another explanation for the elusiveness of direct laboratory evidence linking heat to reduced prosocial behavior is based on differential stress responses by gender, where effects on men may be counteracted by opposite effects on women. While the fight-or-flight response has long been assumed implicitly to operate similarly in men and women (28), this notion has more recently faced increasing scrutiny. In light of both physiological evidence of a distinctive female endocrine systemb and evolutionary considerations of the specific selection pressures primarily faced by women,c the female acute stress response may contrast with a pure fight-or-flight response and a gendered alternative has emerged: the tend-and-befriend response (31, 34). This theory posits that women likely developed adaptive reactions to stress and threats, aimed at optimizing survival for themselves and their offspring, based on a dual tendency: increasing caregiving for children (tend) and seeking social connections (befriend). These affiliations are expected to bolster help and support from group members, facilitating navigation of stressful situations through the establishment and utilization of prosocial interactions with individuals offering protection, resources, and information. Based on this literature, we derive:

(H2)

Economic preferences differ by gender: women (a) exhibit greater prosocial preferences than men and (b) react to heat stress by increasing their prosociality.

This hypothesis has been extensively tested. While the evidence of overall higher female prosociality and cooperation is mixed (35–40), there is substantial evidence that women prefer egalitarian distributions more than men (41, 42), are less motivated by overall resource maximization than men (43), and are less competitive than men (44, 45). Focusing on gender differences in fundamental economic preferences is especially valuable since such gaps have been found reflected in gender differences in economic outcomes, occupational choices, and educational decisions (46).

Group-level characteristics may have more predictive power than gender. Collective threats and risks change social norms and promote cooperation via social learning by making individuals adhere to more similar norms of behavior (56). According to the tightness–looseness theory of culture, societies facing significant threats such as environmental disasters, frequent diseases, or inter-group conflicts tend to develop “tight” cultures to promote cooperation (57). By enhancing coordination and cooperation, these groups could more effectively address the risks they encounter. Conversely, in environments with lower levels of threat, “loose” cultures could allow for greater individual flexibility, advantageous for innovation and creativity but at the cost of higher challenges in the collective mitigation of risks. Supporting these expectations, both field studies where individuals have experienced actual threats and experiments manipulating stress and threats have shown that individuals and groups do react to stressors by adopting stronger cooperative norms. Specifically related to gender gaps, in the absence of hardship, gender preferences often differ, but there is evidence that under greater duress male and female behavior and preferences become more similar. Evidence for behavioral convergence across gender under conditions of hardship has been found via experiments across countries differing in terms of economic growth and development (42), across generations experiencing less duress (58), and across different level of victimization within the same sample (59).

Our final guiding hypothesis builds on the research that shows considerable cross-cultural variation in social norms and economic preferences (57, 60), linking these measures to both individual-level behaviors (e.g. savings, education, occupation, healthcare, volunteering) and aggregate outcomes (42, 46). Prosociality, trust, impartial norms of fairness, have long been associated to market development and growth (61), where these outcomes vary widely between countries and country sub-cultures. So, lastly, we posit:

(H3)

Economic preferences (a) vary across cultural contexts and (b) are affected by heat stress differently across cultural contexts.

Previous research has found important and persistent differences in economic behavior and prosociality across cultures (42, 62–64). Rather than attributing behavioral differences by country to specific cultural features, we test for implicit and overall culture effects using cross-country comparisons as a summary of the myriad factors that shape culture. Since we conducted these experiments in one specific university location within each country, differences between countries in this study may be more reflective of local contexts and not necessarily representative of a prevailing national culture.f

Methods

Motivated by these hypotheses and as depicted in Fig. A1, our experimental design consists of a series of interactive economic games played by randomly matched individuals in rooms where we manipulated temperatures (i.e. provided a “heat treatment”) and additionally manipulated the exposure to a trigger by randomly varying the ordering of a tournament task and thereby adding an element of randomness in the likelihood to lose the tournament (“heat-trigger treatment”). This design expands on Almås et al. (25), who use laboratory experiments in Kenya and United States to test the impact of higher temperatures on an array of social preferences and find little evidence for a direct impact of temperatures up to 30 °C on behavior. In their study, a notable exception was found among a sub-group of Kenyan participants from an ethnic group that had recently been politically marginalized in a national election. For this group, exposure to higher temperatures led to an increased willingness to destroy others’ assets in an experimental game. This finding motivated us to introduce a psychological trigger, as an additional treatment to intersect with the heat treatment, to formally test the hypothesis that heat alone is not sufficient to affect behavior but requires additional conditions.

Experimental sites and logistics

The experiment followed a standardized protocol replicated at the following research facilities: the National University of Colombia (Bogota, Colombia), the Delhi School of Economics (Delhi, India), the International Livestock Research Institute (Nairobi, Kenya), Chapingo Autonomous University (Texcoco, Mexico), and the University of California at Davis (California, USA). In the analysis that follows, we order these locations by income per capita and refer to them by their respective countries (i.e. United States, Mexico, Colombia, India, Kenya). Each session had on average 11.3 participants, comprised both male and female subjects, and lasted about 60 min. The sessions were scheduled at varying times throughout the day with the assignment to heat and heat-trigger treatments uncorrelated with time of day.g

Participant recruitment used social media, university radio, email, flyers, and classroom announcements. This material provided no specific information about the experiment beyond the general description stated in the informed consent. Experimental protocol

The experiment consists of several incentivized games played by randomly matched participants in the same room with exogenously varied ambient temperature (heat treatment), followed by a survey. Games were randomly presented in one of two prespecified orders, Compete After (CA)/Compete Before (CB) treatment, to induce the heat-trigger treatment in the subset of subjects who lost to another player in the competitive tournament. Figure A1 summarizes this design and the Appendix reports the experimental instructions. At the end of each session, one of the eight incentivized tasks was selected at random for payment. Each participant in the session received compensation based on their individual earnings from that specific task.

Dictator games. To construct the preference types, we employed a modified version of the protocol described by Fehr et al. (67), using their three dictator games (Share, Costless Share, Costless Envy) and adding a fourth (Envy). These games measure whether a participant prefers an egalitarian split to a division of resources in which the opponent would have less (Share, Costless Share) or more (Envy, Costless Envy) than the participant. The left panel in Fig. A1 shows the specific allocation of points corresponding to each game. These dictator games were always presented in the same order (i.e. tasks A, B, C, and D) and, depending on the participant’s choices in all four games, we were able to classify their preferences into the types shown in Fig. A2.

Specifically, in task A (Costless Share) the choice was between the egalitarian split [10 for self, 10 for other person] or [10 for self, 5 for the other person], where choosing the egalitarian split indicates a desire to give when it costs nothing to the decider. In task B (Costless Envy), the choice was between the egalitarian split [10, 10] or [10 for self, 15 for the other person], where the egalitarian split indicates a desire to not let the other have more, even when it would cost nothing to the decider. In task C (Costly Share), the choice was between the egalitarian split [10, 10] or [15 for self, 5 for the other person], where sharing equally now is costly (ahead aversion). Task D (Costly Envy), [10, 10] and [13 for self, 16 for the other person], where choosing the egalitarian outcome is now costly (behind aversion). If tasks A, B, C, or D were chosen for payment at the end of a session, participants would receive the points they allocated to themselves for that task plus the points allocated to them as “the other player” by the randomly matched participant.

Competitiveness game. The competitiveness protocol, depicted as tasks 1, 2, and 3 in Fig. A1, was adapted from Niederle and Vesterlund (68) and involves solving puzzles under different payment schemes. In the piece-rate task, participants have 2 min to solve 12 Raven puzzles and are paid 1 point per correct matrix. In the competition task, participants have 2 min to try to solve as many puzzles as possible from a new set of 12 matrices, earning 2 points per correct matrix if they win the tournament, 0 if they lose. A player wins the tournament if their total score for round 2 is greater than the total score of a randomly matched opponent, where the total score is calculated by adding to the number of correct answers a number obtained by rolling a six-sided die. i In the final task, participants are asked to choose how they would like to be paid for the final round between a modified piece-rate j or the competition payment. Subjects that choose to compete are classified as competitive.

Heat treatment

To set the experimental room to varying temperatures ranging from slightly cold (18 °C) to hot (34 °C), we used electric heaters placed in locations hidden from the participants. For statistical power considerations and to give us the ability to identify whether there are temperature points at which behaviors change, approximately half of the ambient temperatures in each country were set (uniformly) between 18 °C and 29 °C and half were set (uniformly) between 30 °C and 34 °C. k In each country, for the first day of sessions, the temperatures in the experimental room started at the warmest settings and decreased throughout the day, while for the following day the temperatures started at the coolest settings and increased throughout the day. Experimental sessions took place roughly uniformly from 9:00 to 18:00. This pattern was repeated in each country site so the assignment of participants to temperatures was as-if random with respect to observable and unobservable characteristics. Outside air temperature and humidity, and experimental room air temperature and humidity were recorded at the beginning and at the end of each session.

Participants could not be blinded to the perceptible ambient temperature; however, we did not disclose any manipulation, made no mention of temperature in recruitment or instructions, used identical scripts and procedures across conditions (participation was limited to one session), and conducted an open-ended debrief to assess hypotheses. Only a small minority referenced temperature, and even fewer articulated any link between temperature and prosocial behavior. Importantly, our main results are null with respect to temperature and prosociality, reducing concerns that demand characteristics might have driven behavior in the hot condition.

Figure 1, left panel reports the cumulative distribution functions, by country, of the ambient temperatures measured in each session, ranging from 18 °C to 34 °C. The highest room temperatures (i.e. the average dry bulb temperature registered with a standard thermometer in the room during the session, a combination of both the preexisting ambient temperature plus the heat manipulation) were realized in India and the lowest were registered in Colombia, respectively our hottest and coldest sites (see Table 1). An alternative measure of heat stress is wet bulb temperature, which incorporates humidity levels. (25) we use dry bulb temperature as our main treatment variable, using an indicator equal to 1 if this room temperature was greater or equal to 30 °C (hereafter “heat treatment”), 0 if below.l In the Appendix, we replicate the analysis using wet bulb temperatures at different thresholds, finding similar results. Heat-trigger treatment

We cross-cut the heat treatment with a trigger treatment that consisted of two parts: random ordering of the tasks such that the competitiveness games came before the dictator games and losing the tournament due to having a lower point total (sum of number of puzzles solved and the die roll) than a randomly matched peer. In this experimental setup, losing the tournament due to either subpar performance on the Ravens matrices or an unfavorable die roll could serve as a psychological trigger. This trigger, through inducing frustration and a subtle stress, has the potential to influence subsequent decision-making in the dictator games. Specifically, we created two game sequences (see Fig. A1). In CA sessions (occurring 1/3 of the times), the potential trigger in round 2 came after the dictator games A–D, whereas it came before in the CB sessions (occurring 2/3 of the times). In the CB order, participants began with a task that could potentially provoke mild frustration if they lost the Raven’s matrices competition before proceeding to the dictator games. Subjects who were exposed to the heat treatment and the CB order and who lost the tournament were exposed, for our analysis, to the heat-trigger treatment.

Data

We use participant choices from the dictator games to identify four types of preferences: egalitarian, maximizer, selfish, and spiteful. The mapping we use to type subjects based on their choices is similar to Fehr et al. (67) but augmented for the fourth dictator game (Costly Envy), and it shown in Fig. A2 (see also Section 3.2). Egalitarian (fraction = 0.20), maximizer (0.45), selfish (0.03), and spiteful (0.10) are each binary variables equal to one if either the weak or strong form of the behavior represents a participant’s choices. Types are not exhaustive (20.2% of participants’ behavior fell into none of these categories), but between these four types they are mutually exclusive. In addition, we classify as competitive those who choose the tournament payment for third round of the competitiveness game. This fifth preference type is determined independently from the previous four, thus it is not mutually exclusive.

In addition to these core outcomes, we collected other secondary data to use in the analysis that follows. We use a simple lottery choice task to elicit risk aversion, which is a standard control in the analysis of competitiveness. We also collected data on beliefs about opponent performance in the tournament to control for confidence, also to be used as control in the analysis of competitiveness. Importantly, after the announcement of the tournament result, we elicited self-reported levels of happiness, frustration, and energy on a 0–10 scale to test whether the trigger treatment was effective in altering mood. A survey administered after the experiment included questions on basic demographics (age and gender), socioeconomic status (SES), life satisfaction, social ties, stress reactions, social trust, and perceived stress (PSS-10). Results

We first assess the effectiveness of the trigger by checking whether temperature altered participants’ emotional states. Panel A of Table 2 shows that temperature above 30 °C had a substantial effect on self-reported dispositions, with participants reporting a significant increase in frustration, tiredness, and unhappiness (with effects in the 0.13-15σ range), leading to a 0.17σ increase in a negative-state index comprised of these three feelings. These null results of our heat treatment persist under a host of alternative specifications and robustness checks. Guided by our preanalysis plan, we report in the Appendix a series of estimations that allowed for alternative specifications of heat that include a continuous regular temperature (Table A11) and a continuous wet bulb temperature (Table A12), which show similar null results on preferences. We use alternative wet bulb temperatures as different discrete indicators of high heat at 21 °C (Table A13), 23 °C (Table A14), and 25 °C (Table A15), finding some decrease in spite and increase in egalitarianism at 23 °C but which does not hold at the other measures. We carry out estimations within narrower bands around 30 °C (Table A16), finding modest evidence for a reduction in spite above 30 °C. We also estimate spline regressions (see Appendix Fig. A7) that seek to capture nonlinear changes in the different economic behaviors. These estimations indicate slight increases (decreases) in egalitarian (maximizing) behaviors that are statistically insignificant and do not survive controls for multiple hypothesis testing. Finally, results for the heat-trigger treatment specification expanded with additional controls are shown in Table A3 and tell a similar story: even when combined with the trigger, heat stress does not statistically affect economic preferences.

In sum, although we find participants’ mood deteriorates under heat stress, we see no effect on economic behavior, and these null results are robust to alternative measures of heat, as well as different linear and nonlinear specifications. The heat-trigger treatment similarly has no effect on behavior in the experiment. The only statistically significant heat treatment effect is to possibly slightly reduce, rather than increase, spiteful behavior, a result which holds only in some specifications and holds the opposite sign of both expected outcomes and prior work (25). These experimental results provide no empirical support for H1.

H2: women are more prosocial than men

Our results reveal remarkably similar gender differences in economic behavior across countries, as can be seen in Fig. 3 and in the radar graphs of Fig. A4. Pooling data across all temperatures, Appendix Table A2 reports means and standard deviations by gender in each country for each outcome, and their respective t-tests for gender gaps. These initial tests, without controls, show that women tend to be more egalitarian than men everywhere (significantly so in Colombia, India, and Mexico). Men tend to maximize joint payoffs more than do women (with the exception of India and Kenya where maximization preferences are similar). As for selfishness, the least frequent among our preferences, men appear more selfish in each of the five countries, although this result is borderline significant only for India. We do not see significant gender differences for spitefulness, with the exception of India where it is more frequent in men. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the largest gender gaps are found for competitiveness, which is significantly higher for men than for women everywhere except in Kenya, consistent with a large literature on gendered competition (see Ref. (44)).

Overall, we find strong evidence that women are more prosocial than men on average in this sample (H2(a)), but statistically insignificant evidence that these differences fade or intensify under heat stress (H2(b)). While statistically insignificant, a suggestive pattern of convergence is consistent with the literature that finds gender norms tend to be weaker in harsher environments (44, 59). This represents an interesting area for future research.

H3: preferences vary by country and context; heat effects remain insignificant

Last, we examine variations in prosocial behavior across different countries and assess whether the effects of heat treatment vary by country. As shown in Figs. 2 and 3, and the radar graphs in Fig. A4, preferences for the joint maximization of resources are the most frequently observed across countries, but are far more prevalent in the United States, Mexico, and Colombia.q An ANOVA test to determine whether there were significant differences in maximization preferences across countries reveal a statistically significant difference across them (F(4, 1,631) = 27.91, P=0.000), while the country coefficients in Table 4 suggest that these maximizing preferences are the highest in the United States (the baseline category).

Preferences for egalitarian splits are less frequent in all countries, although with significant variation across them (F(4, 1,631) = 6.39, P=0.000), and far more common among non-US participants (Table 4).

Selfish choices, those that prioritize a participant’s own payoff independently on the payoff for the other person, are the least displayed in every one of the five countries, with rates lower than 1%, although relatively higher in United States, Kenya, and India. The results from the ANOVA test of differences across countries reject the null hypothesis that the means are equal (F(4, 1,631) = 5.81, P=0.000).

Slightly more frequent are instances of spiteful behavior, but with strong heterogeneity between countries (F(4, 1,631) = 12.08, P=0.000). Spiteful behavior in the laboratory is highest in India and Kenya, and lowest in Colombia and Mexico, although little significance is observed in the regressions with controls reported in Table 4.

Countries appear to show differences in competitiveness (F(4, 1,631) = 9.51, P=0.000) although the coefficients in Table 4 are all insignificant except for India.

In summary, the visual results are statistically confirmed by Table 4, which shows stark differences across cultures, especially in egalitarian and maximizing behavior. Furthermore, the interactions Country × Heat in Table 4 show little evidence for differential effects of elevated temperature across cultures. With respect to the baseline category (United States below the heat threshold), heat appears to produce no statistical effect on behavior.r

Estimating the effects of heat separately by country, rather than pooling the data with country fixed effects, yields results that are broadly consistent with our main finding of negligible heat effects. While a few country-specific coefficients reach conventional significance levels—showing lower egalitarianism and competitiveness in Colombia (Table A5.1), modestly higher egalitarianism and lower competitiveness in India (Table A5.2), lower selfishness and spitefulness in Kenya (Table A5.3), slightly higher competitiveness in Mexico (Table A5.4), and no effects in the United States (Table A5.5)—these effects are small, uneven across outcomes, and do not exhibit a coherent or systematic pattern across countries or preference domains. Overall, these results do not alter our main conclusion that heat has limited and inconsistent effects on economic preferences.

In conclusion, we find strong evidence for H3(a), that is that prosocial behavior systematically varies across countries by cultural context (as represented by the locations of our five laboratories). As with gender, we find no significant evidence that the heat treatment had effects that varied by country (H3(b)).

These results complement our three main hypotheses and follow from the fact that we found no evidence of heat treatment effects in our analysis so far, even though heat treatment did affect subjects’ emotional state (Table 2). Discussion

As the effects of climate change grow increasingly evident and severe, research examining its consequences has expanded across a broader range of disciplines. Throughout the social sciences, observational studies have assessed the effects of temperature increases on crime, conflict, political unrest, and other detrimental facets of human social behavior (e.g. (9, 27)). We take an experimental approach and manipulate temperatures in a series of laboratory settings to estimate the impact of heat on social and economic preferences, allowing us to compare potential magnitudes to the relative within-sample variation in preferences with gender, cultural context, and individual characteristics. Our five-country experiment suggests that while climate may drive the evolution of social preferences at longer time scales (69, 70), high temperatures have negligible immediate and direct effect on social preferences per se, especially relative to the influence of the other factors. Gender and cultural contexts are much stronger predictors of social preferences than heat stress, even when that stress is combined with a negative psychological trigger.

Our findings align with a large body of existing literature documenting substantial variation in social preferences with gender and culture. Across our five-country sample, men tended to be more competitive, and chose to maximize aggregate payoffs, while women displayed greater preference for egalitarianism, and less selfishness, findings congruent with previous work (39, 44, 71). Likewise, we find culture—the shared beliefs, values, and norms captured by the different laboratory locations—to be a strong predictor of prosocial behavior, also in line with prior work (42, 57, 61). Compared to the other four countries, participants in the United States showed significantly greater tolerance for inequality, and a marked preference for aggregate resource maximization.

The results of our experiment align with recent work by Almås et al. (25) and indicate a null effect of heat stress on individual prosocial behavior that appears at odds with both long standing intuition and observational evidence that heat stress negatively impacts cooperation and prosocial interactions (9). This suggests that the mechanisms behind the well-documented correlation between high temperatures and contemporaneous violence or aggression likely operate through channels other than shifts in individual prosociality. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that our design has limitations that could have attenuated the effects. Although manipulation checks indicate that the heat treatment affected mood, the induced temperatures may have been insufficient in intensity or duration to erode prosocial behavior. In terms of intensity, this seems unlikely: room temperatures greater than 30 °C (with maximum 34 °C, substantially hotter than in Almås et al. (25)) made subjects uncomfortable and was effective in inducing higher levels of tiredness, frustration, and unhappiness. This heat treatment was especially intense in Delhi given the ambient conditions—and yet even in these sessions higher temperatures failed to create less-social behavior in the laboratory. Our laboratory experiments may also have fallen short in terms of duration, however, as the average total session length was just over 1 h. By comparison, the experimental sessions of Almås et al. (25) were twice as long on average, but also found no statistical effect on prosociality. It is certainly conceivable that multiple hours of exposure to heat—perhaps combined with uncertainty about when the heat stress would be relieved—may erode prosociality where a 1 h exposure did not. Such an extended and uncertain exposure to heat stretches the ethical limits of research designs using controlled experimental methods, which raises a broader methodological issue.

A second potential limitation of our design stems from the necessarily complex, interactive origins of real world aggression and violence and the controlled setting of the lab. The primary virtue of laboratory experiments is the exceptional degree of control they offer researchers to isolate causal effects from confounding factors. In our context, this degree of control may have unintentionally neutralized the potency of heat stress as an antisocial force. Controlled conditions may reduce mental fatigue (72) and enable subjects to more effectively reign in destructive impulses. Where interactions are not face-to-face and instead carefully mediated by technology and design, there is also limited scope for natural social interactions, including the escalating dynamics that often define social confrontations under duress, or the possibility of group identity interacting with temperature to drive discriminatory or competitive behavior (13, 69, 73). Moreover, outside the laboratory, heat stress often coincides with resource scarcity, heightened competition, or sociopolitical tensions and with other behaviors (e.g. spending more time outside during summer and increased alcohol consumption on hot days (3)). Perhaps these naturally occurring confounding factors are necessary to overwhelm the normal restraint on aggression that prevails in social interaction with strangers. With these reflections in mind, this study rules out a direct causal link from heat stress to an erosion of prosocial preferences in a controlled indoor setting that neutralizes the kind of confounding factors that may otherwise amplify such an effect. We consider this an important contribution, but recognize that in the course of normal social interaction, heat may influence behavior through mediators that are not present in a laboratory setting.

Finally, as we highlighted in the motivations for our hypotheses, there is the possibility that multiple psychological or physiological mechanisms are activated under heat stress. These mechanisms may exert opposing influences on prosocial behavior, effectively counterbalancing each other. For example, heat stress might simultaneously increase stress and discomfort, which could reduce prosocial tendencies, while also triggering adaptive social cohesion or mutual support mechanisms in response to shared adversity. This seems particularly relevant with weather shocks such as heat waves since these shared hardships constitute a common threat to residents in a given area and can thereby be remarkably unifying rather than divisive. These opposing effects could lead to the null outcome observed in our controlled experimental setting, despite field data indicating a significant relationship. Future research should aim to disentangle these competing effects and identify the contextual factors that mediate their relative strengths, potentially exploring these tensions by designing hybrid studies that incorporate real-world complexity into experimental setups, or by analyzing observational longitudinal data to isolate heat stress effects from confounding variables. Bridging these findings is critical for a more comprehensive understanding of the interaction between heat stress and social behavior.
