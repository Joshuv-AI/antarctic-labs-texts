Weight stigma amid pediatric healthcare providers: a cross-sectional survey study assessing the roles of weight controllability beliefs and blame
Authors: Taylor Dennison, Sarah Nutter, Nita Chauhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWeight stigma affects healthcare providers’ practice and contributes to poor patient outcomes. While it has been well-documented among providers of adult patients, research among providers of pediatric patients is limited and descriptive. The aim of this study was to answer the question: to what extent do pediatric healthcare professionals hold weight stigma towards patients and their parents, and what factors influence these attitudes?MethodsAn anonymous cross-sectional online survey was distributed from June-August 2023 to all healthcare workers, including providers-in-training, working with pediatric patients at a single centre in a province in central Canada. Participants who did not complete the survey, or who submitted it with excessive missing data, were removed. Primary outcome measures included weight stigma towards pediatric patients and their parents.Results113 respondents were included. There was both a significant and positive direct effect of weight controllability beliefs on weight stigma towards parents (b = 0.58, 99% CI: 0.27, 0.89), as well as a significant and positive indirect effect, via blame (b = 0.33, 99% CI: 0.10, 0.61). Towards patients, there was a significant and positive indirect effect of weight controllability, via blame (b = 0.28, 99% CI: 0.08, 0.49).ConclusionsOur findings highlight the importance of weight controllability beliefs in the stigma towards pediatric patients with higher body weights and their parents, and the ensuing blame that can occur when providers believe weight is within individual control. This work adds to the limited and descriptive weight stigma research on healthcare providers in pediatric healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05995-w.

Background

Weight stigma includes the stereotypes, negative attitudes and beliefs towards individuals with higher body weights. Researchers have well-documented weight stigma among healthcare professionals (HCPs) and professionals-in-training, including physicians, nurses, and medical students [1]. These attitudes can negatively impact healthcare encounters via negative judgment of patients, poorer patient-centered communication, less time spent with patients, and fewer offered treatment options [2–4], which can increase patient avoidance of healthcare and doctor shopping [5]. In a sample of 400 family physicians in Canada, 33% of participants reported often feeling frustrated by patients with higher weights, 28% of participants perceived patients with higher weights as non-adherent with treatment recommendations, 18% reported feelings of disgust when treating higher-weight patients, and about 50% agreed that higher-weight people increase demand on the public healthcare system [6]. The negative impact of weight stigma on healthcare encounters and healthcare utilization is especially concerning when considering the health consequences associated with day-to-day experiences of weight stigma. Physical and psychological health consequences include increased symptoms of depression and anxiety [7], disordered eating and clinical eating disorders [7],– [8] physiological dysregulation (measured via blood pressure, heart rate, c-reactive protein, cholesterol, triglycerides, and glucose) [9], increased cortisol [10], decreased motivation to engage in physical activity [11], increased risk of suicide [12],– [13] and increased risk of mortality [14]. Thus, it is difficult to differentiate between the potential health consequences associated with higher body weights alone from the health consequences associated with weight stigma [14–16].

While weight stigma has been well-documented among HCPs working with adult patients, research reporting weight stigma among HCPs working with pediatric patients is limited [17–19]. In a sample of 308 nurses, medical assistants, and nursing assistants working in pediatric settings in the United States, participants reported elevated scores on items related to stereotyped characteristics of pediatric patients with higher body weights as well as elevated scores on items examining weight controllability beliefs [17]. Similarly, among 555 pediatric HCPs in the Netherlands, elevated negative attitudes and frustrations towards higher-weight pediatric patients were reported by general physicians and pediatricians compared to other pediatric health professionals [19]. Most recently, implicit weight stigma was reported among a sample of 177 pediatric nurses, physicians, and physician assistants in the United States, with greater implicit preference for thin faces reported amongst professionals aged 36–65 compared to those aged 25 and younger [18]. In addition, in virtual reality research conducted with healthcare pre-professionals, when higher-weight pediatric patients had pain-related presenting concerns, these concerns were rated as more likely related to psychological or behavioural health issues, as well as the desire to avoid non-preferred activities than if the patient had a lower body weight [20]. In another study with medical students, pediatric patients with higher weights were more likely to receive physical therapy or psychology referrals, were indicated as requiring follow-up at a significantly higher rate, and received significantly lower ratings for perceived adherence to treatment recommendations [21]. The limited extant research on weight stigma in pediatric healthcare settings highlights the potential impact of weight stigma for pediatric patients but is both limited and descriptive in nature. Such paucity of research is of critical importance to address, given the health consequences associated with everyday experiences of weight stigma among adults.

Thus, the aim of this research was to examine weight stigma among HCPs working in pediatrics, with a focus on better understanding potential factors that may influence the extent to which pediatric HCPs hold weight stigmatizing attitudes towards pediatric patients and their parents. Based on the results of previous research, we identified contact quality and quantity [22], blame [23], and internalized weight stigma [24] as potential mediating factors to examine alongside weight controllability beliefs [25] and weight stigma (see Fig. 1 for proposed model). Specifically, we hypothesized that weight controllability beliefs may be directly and/or indirectly associated with weight stigma via contact quality and quantity, blame, and internalized weight stigma.

Methods

This study was approved by the University of Saskatchewan Behavioural Research Ethics Board (Beh-REB 3892). This single-centre study followed the reporting guidelines for Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) (see supplementary document).

All HCPs, including HCPs in training, working in pediatrics within a province in central Canada were invited to participate in this study using an anonymous survey link and in-hospital posters at multiple facilities within the same university network. The survey was distributed amongst healthcare professionals working with pediatric patients, with informed consent being obtained before the commencement of the survey. Participation occurred between June and August 2023. The survey was conducted online using SurveyMonkey. Following informed consent, participants completed the survey measures and demographics questionnaire.

Measures

Contact quantity and quality

Participants completed five items measuring contact quantity (α = 0.82; 1[none] to 7[a great deal]) and four items measuring contact quality (α = 0.88; 1[none] to 7[a great deal]) with people with obesity in their day-to-day life [26]. One contact quality item was removed due to poor item-total correlation (i.e., “Our interaction was intimate”).

Weight stigma towards pediatric patients

12 items from the Attitudes about Patients with Obesity questionnaire [27] were modified to assess weight stigma towards pediatric patients (α = 0.80; 1[strongly disagree] to 6[strongly agree]). Item wording was modified from “patients with obesity” to “children with obesity.”

Weight stigma towards parents

6 items from the Attitudes about Patients with Obesity questionnaire [27] were modified to examine weight stigma towards parents of pediatric patients (α = 0.82; 1[strongly disagree] to 6[strongly agree]). Item wording was modified to focus on parents (i.e., “Parents of children with obesity are less adherent to therapy recommendations”).

Weight controllability beliefs

The 8-item Beliefs about Obese Persons scale [28] (BAOP) was used to measure the extent to which participants believed weight is individually controllable (α = 0.79; 1[strongly disagree] to 6[strongly agree]). One item from the scale was removed due to poor item-total correlation (i.e., “Obesity is the result of a biological disorder”).

Blame

A 12-item unpublished measure used in previous research [23] was used to measure blame towards individuals with higher body weights (α = 0.88; 1[strongly disagree] to 6[strongly agree]). One item from the scale was removed due to poor item-total correlation (i.e., “I feel sympathy towards people with obesity”).

Internalized weight stigma

The 11-item Modified Weight Bias Internalization Scale [27] was used to measure internalized weight stigma (α = 0.91; 1[strongly disagree] to 6[strongly agree]). Two items were removed due to poor item-total correlation (i.e., “Because of my weight, I feel that I am just as competent as anyone;” and, “I am OK being the weight that I am”).

Demographics

Participants provided their job title, years in practice, practice setting (i.e., inpatient, outpatient, both), percentage of pediatric practice, perception of the percentage of patients with obesity that they see as part of their practice, age, gender, race/ethnicity, and weight perception (i.e., slightly thin, slightly fat, etc.).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted in SPSS version 27 with the PROCESS macro for SPSS (model 4). Upon removing participants who did not complete the survey, a small number of missing data points were identified and were mean imputed (i.e., 2 cases on contact quality, 1 on weight stigma towards parents, 4 cases on weight stigma towards patients, 4 cases on blame, 2 cases on weight controllability beliefs, and 3 cases on internalized weight stigma). Skewness and kurtosis values for each variable were within the acceptable range of −2 to + 2. Regression-based analyses were conducted to examine the direct and indirect associations (via blame and contact) between weight controllability beliefs and weight stigma towards parents and pediatric patients. Separate analyses were conducted for blame, contact quality, and contact quantity as potentially influential factors on the relationship between weight controllability beliefs and weight stigma towards patients and parents. Given the number of analyses conducted, we used a 99% confident interval as opposed to 95%.

Results

Participants

Data was collected from 143 participants. Data from 30 participants was removed either due to non-completion or excessive missing data, for a final sample of 113 participants (see Table 1. for demographics). The majority of the sample was comprised of white women between the ages of 35 and 54.

Using PROCESS macro for SPSS, we examined the direct and indirect effects of weight controllability beliefs on weight stigma via blame, contact quantity, and contact quality. First, we examined the direct and indirect effects of weight controllability beliefs on weight stigma towards parents of pediatric patients. The model with blame was significant, indicating both a significant and positive direct effect of weight controllability beliefs on weight stigma towards parents (b = 0.58, 99% CI: 0.27, 0.89), as well as a significant and positive indirect effect, via blame (b = 0.33, 99% CI: 0.10, 0.61). The models with contact quantity (direct effect b = 0.82, 99% CI: 0.59, 1.05; indirect effect b = 0.09, 99% CI: − 0.004, 0.21) and quality were not significant (direct effect b = 0.86, 99% CI: 0.62, 1.09; indirect effect b = 0.05, 99% CI: − 0.01, 0.14). Then, we examined the direct and indirect effects of weight controllability beliefs on weight stigma towards pediatric patients. The model with blame was significant; however, only the indirect effect of weight controllability, via blame, was significantly and positively associated with weight stigma towards pediatric patients (b = 0.28, 99% CI: 0.08, 0.49). Once blame was entered into the model, the direct effect of weight controllability beliefs and weight stigma towards pediatric patients was non-significant (b = 0.02, 99% CI: − 0.24, 0.28). The models with contact quantity (direct effect b = 0.26, 99% CI: 0.05, 0.47; indirect effect b = 0.04, 99% CI: − 0.02, 0.10) and quality were not significant (direct effect b = 0.24, 99% CI: 0.04, 0.43; indirect effect b = 0.06, 99% CI: − 0.02, 0.16). Discussion

We examined weight stigma towards pediatric patients and their parents in a sample of 113 pediatric HCPs in central Canada. Our results suggested that weight controllability beliefs and blame towards people with higher weights are important influences on weight stigma in the pediatric healthcare context. Specifically, our results indicated that weight controllability beliefs were significantly associated with weight stigma towards parents of pediatric patients, both directly and indirectly, via blame. Further, our results indicated that weight controllability beliefs were significantly and indirectly associated with weight stigma towards pediatric patients via blame.

These results extend the descriptive findings of the limited extant research on weight stigma in pediatric healthcare settings [17–21] to indicate that both weight controllability beliefs and blame towards people with obesity may be key contributors to weight stigma in pediatric HCPs. These results are particularly concerning given the identified harms of weight stigma in pediatric healthcare settings, including loss in patient trust, and changes in healthcare decisions by parents such as seeking new providers or avoiding future appointments [29, 30].

The potential impact on patient care that may result from weight stigma towards pediatric patients and their parents is consistent with the identified harms of a weight-centric health paradigm [31]. Mauldin, May, and Clifford [31] describe the weight-centric health paradigm as based on several flawed assumptions about weight and health, including the following: (1) higher weights as causing poor health and mortality; (2) weight is under an individual’s control; (3) advising patients to lose weight is an effective and harmless treatment intervention; (4) when weight loss and health improvements are not maintained, it is the patient’s fault; and (5) health improvements seen after weight loss interventions are due to the weight loss, and not due to the aspects of the intervention itself (i.e., behavioural modifications). Such assumptions about weight create the conditions under which pediatric HCPs may come to stigmatize pediatric patients and their parents based on weight. Although it is beyond the scope of this article to fully examine each of these flawed assumptions, researchers have increasingly demonstrated complex relationships between weight and health that suggest weight is not a reliable proxy for individual health or mortality risk [32–34], that weight is not within individual control [35], and that behavioural weight loss efforts are not effective long term [36]. Further, researchers have well-documented the history of the body mass index (BMI) and its flaws in accurately predicting individual health [34, 37]. Thus, Mauldin, May, and Clifford [31] suggest that it is imperative to shift from healthcare paradigms that are weight-centric to those that are weight-inclusive and emphasize treating health conditions without a focus on weight, utilizing appropriate cardiometabolic screening for all patients regardless of weight, and engaging in conversations and healthcare practices to support all patients in optimizing their health to the extent that they would like to.

A weight-inclusive paradigm shift is consistent with calls for more attention on reducing weight stigma in pediatric medicine, including educating current and future HCPs on weight stigma [29, 38, 39] Our results suggest that targeting weight controllability beliefs and blame may be important to examine in future research, which is consistent with previous findings that education about the genetic and environmental complexities of weight may lead to reductions in weight stigma and blame towards patients with higher body weights [40]. As a part of professional development and sensitivity training opportunities, it may also be important to emphasize the importance of language and the need for neutral weight-based language with patients and colleagues. In a systematic review of research on language preferences, Puhl [41] recommended the use of neutral language in research and practice, including terms or phrases such as “weight,” “unhealthy weight,” or “your weight may be damaging to your health,” as opposed to terms that are perceived as more stigmatizing such as “obesity,” “fat,” or “morbid obesity.” It is also important to consider potential differences in preferred terminology, or terminology that is deemed acceptable between patients and HCPs, as a recent study indicated such differences between the general public, HCPs, and obesity researchers [42].

Limitations

A notable strength of this research is our effort to move beyond describing weight stigma among pediatric HCPs, to examining factors that may influence the extent to which pediatric HCPs hold weight stigmatizing attitudes towards patients and their parents. However, this study is not without its limitations. Our sample size was small, lacking in diverse representation, and is geographically limited to HCPs working with pediatric patients in a singular province and university centre, albeit with multiple locations, in central Canada. Thus, our findings may not be generalizable across Canada, and so larger and more diverse samples are needed. Our survey was also focused on self-reported attitudes but did not examine how these attitudes may lead to differential treatment of pediatric patients with obesity and their families, which is important to examine in future research. For example, qualitative research with pediatric HCP’s may be one way to better understand the language and approach used when working with pediatric patients in their families that we were unable to capture in our survey-based study.

Conclusions

This research adds to the limited and descriptive previous research examining weight stigma towards pediatric patients. Our findings highlight the importance of weight controllability beliefs in the stigma towards higher-weight pediatric patients and their parents, and the ensuing blame that can occur when healthcare providers believe that weight is within individual control.

Further research is needed to replicate these findings in larger and more diverse samples as well as to elucidate the consequences of this bias on provider behaviour and pediatric patients’ experiences with higher body weights in healthcare.
