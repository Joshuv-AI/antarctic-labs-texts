Aortic Calcification in a Long-Term Warfarin Patient
Authors: Thamara Rodrigues da Costa, Paula Caroline Costa Gonçalves, Guilherme Sobreira Spina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Arterial calcification is a complication of multiple pathologies in medicine. Long-term vitamin K antagonist (VKA) exposure has been associated with vascular and valvular calcification in humans; however, the clinical evidence is predominantly observational.1,2 Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist used for prevention and treatment of thromboembolic disorders. Warfarin inhibits vitamin K recycling, reducing gamma-carboxylation of vitamin K–dependent proteins beyond the coagulation cascade.2,3 Reduced vitamin K–dependent activation of matrix Gla protein (MGP), a key endogenous inhibitor of vascular mineralization, provides biological plausibility for VKA-associated calcification.2, 3, 4 We present a young patient with extensive thoracic aortic calcification after mechanical mitral valve replacement in 2012 and prolonged warfarin therapy.

History of Presentation

The patient is a 45-year-old Black woman diagnosed with rheumatic fever at 23 years of age, initially presenting with heart failure and arthritis. She experienced progressive dyspnea over several years, prompting multiple interventions for mitral valve disease.

Past Medical History

The patient underwent mitral commissurotomy and papillotomy in 2006, balloon mitral valvuloplasty in 2009, progressing with a new residual area of 1.8 cm2, and mechanical mitral valve replacement in 2012. Secondary prophylaxis with benzathine penicillin G was prescribed. Postoperatively, she continued outpatient follow-up using warfarin, atenolol, and benzathine penicillin G. From the early postoperative period, she continued warfarin therapy to maintain the target international normalized ratio (documented weekly dose: 14 mg/wk).

Differential Diagnosis

In a relatively young patient, extensive aortic calcification raises consideration of atherosclerosis and other causes of vascular/valvular calcification (chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, and inflammatory disease) in addition to potential medication-associated contributions such as long-term VKA exposure.

Investigations

The medical record documented that transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated mixed rheumatic mitral valve disease with predominant mitral stenosis and concomitant moderate mitral regurgitation, along with moderate aortic regurgitation. The original initial transthoracic echocardiography report/images and quantitative measurements are no longer available for review; therefore, the quantitative criteria used at that time (eg, mitral valve area, mean transmitral gradient, regurgitation quantification) cannot be provided. Posttreatment chest computed tomography (CT) revealed extensive coarse diffuse calcifications along the thoracoabdominal aorta, consistent with a “porcelain aorta” phenotype,5,6 along with a fusiform aortic arch aneurysm (maximum diameter: 4.8 cm) (Figures 1 and 2).

A structured review of medical records found no records of chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, smoking, or systemic inflammatory disorder, and laboratory tests (renal function, lipid profile, calcium, and parathyroid hormone) were within normal limits. Prolonged exposure to vitamin K antagonists remained the main relevant nontraditional factor. Management

The patient continues warfarin due to the mechanical mitral valve because direct oral anticoagulants still have no established role in patients with mechanical heart valves.8

The retrospective review of medical records found no record of invasive coronary angiography or hemodynamic cardiac catheterization. This likely reflected a low pretest probability of obstructive coronary artery disease at the time of valve intervention (a young, premenopausal woman, initially without documented ischemic symptoms or left ventricular systolic dysfunction), consistent with guideline-based medical practice.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After 7 years from the initial CT, CT angiography of the thoracic and abdominal aorta demonstrated persistence of the fusiform aneurysm with enlargement of the ascending aorta to 4.8 × 5.4 cm (Figure 4). Outpatient follow-up remains ongoing.

Discussion

Vascular calcification is increasingly recognized as an actively regulated process and is frequently encountered in association with atherosclerosis. Long-term VKA exposure has also been associated with vascular and valvular calcification in humans; however, the evidence is predominantly observational and does not establish causality.1,2

Warfarin inhibits the vitamin K cycle, reducing gamma-carboxylation of vitamin K–dependent proteins beyond the coagulation cascade.2,3 Among extrahepatic vitamin K–dependent proteins, MGP is a key endogenous inhibitor of vascular mineralization, and its anticalcifying function depends on vitamin K–dependent carboxylation.2, 3, 4 Reduced functional MGP diminishes restraint of bone morphogenetic protein signaling (including bone morphogenetic protein-2 activity), promoting osteogenic transdifferentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells and favoring medial calcification.2,9

In parallel, vitamin K–dependent pathways involving Gas6/Axl signaling have been implicated in vascular cell survival; experimental data suggest that suppression of Gas6/Axl during warfarin exposure may facilitate calcification, and vitamin K2 attenuated warfarin-induced aortic calcification in an animal model.10 Thus, by antagonizing vitamin K, warfarin may inhibit the Gas6/Axl pathway.10 Therefore, reduced activity of vitamin K–dependent protective mechanisms may contribute to progressive arterial wall calcification during long-term VKA exposure.

In our patient, prolonged warfarin therapy was a prominent long-term exposure, and extensive thoracoabdominal aortic calcification with a “porcelain aorta” phenotype was identified on CT imaging. Porcelain aorta has important implications for future surgical or transcatheter procedures because heavy aortic calcification can impede safe cross clamping or cannulation and may increase embolic risk with aortic manipulation.5,6 CT is a noninvasive method for mapping calcification distribution and procedural planning and can be complemented by 3-dimensional reconstructions to depict extent and morphology.7

Given that human evidence linking VKA exposure to calcification is predominantly observational, warfarin should be presented as a potential contributor rather than a proven cause, and alternative explanations and confounders should be explicitly addressed (chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, inflammatory disease, and prior radiation exposure).1,2

Conclusions

Extraosseous calcification is related to the stiffening of important arteries such as the aorta and is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Although typically associated with age, diabetes, or kidney failure, chronic warfarin use is emerging as a potential contributing factor.1,2 In this patient, long-term warfarin exposure in the absence of traditional risk factors coincided with development of extensive aortic calcification, suggesting a possible association. However, further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between VKA therapy and aortic calcification and to explore preventive or therapeutic strategies.1,2
