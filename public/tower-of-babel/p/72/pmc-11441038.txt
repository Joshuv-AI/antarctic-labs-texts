African swine fever virus modulates the endoplasmic reticulum stress-ATF6-calcium axis to facilitate viral replication
Authors: Yanjin Wang, Jiaqi Li, Hongwei Cao, Lian-Feng Li, Jingwen Dai, Mengxiang Cao, Hao Deng, Dailang Zhong, Yuzi Luo, Yongfeng Li, Meilin Li, Dingkun Peng, Zitao Sun, Xiaowei Gao, Assad Moon, Lijie Tang, Yuan Sun, Su Li, Hua-Ji Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAfrican swine fever (ASF), caused by African swine fever virus (ASFV), is a devastating infectious disease of domestic pigs and wild boar, which threatens the global pig industry. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a multifunctional signaling organelle in eukaryotic cells that is involved in protein synthesis, processing, posttranslational modification and quality control. As intracellular parasitic organisms, viruses have evolved several strategies to modulate ER functions to favor their life cycles. We have previously demonstrated that the differentially expressed genes associated with unfolded protein response (UPR), which represents a response to ER stress, are significantly enriched upon ASFV infection. However, the correlation between the ER stress or UPR and ASFV replication has not been illuminated yet. Here, we demonstrated that ASFV infection induces ER stress both in target cells and in vivo, and subsequently activates the activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) branch of the UPR to facilitate viral replication. Mechanistically, ASFV infection disrupts intracellular calcium (Ca2+) homeostasis, while the ATF6 pathway facilitates ASFV replication by increasing the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. More specifically, we demonstrated that ASFV infection triggers ER-dependent Ca2+ release via the inositol triphosphate receptor (IP3R) channel. Notably, we showed that the ASFV B117L protein plays crucial roles in ER stress and the downstream activation of the ATF6 branch, as well as the disruption of Ca2+ homeostasis. Taken together, our findings reveal for the first time that ASFV modulates the ER stress-ATF6-Ca2+ axis to facilitate viral replication, which provides novel insights into the development of antiviral strategies for ASFV.

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF), caused by African swine fever virus (ASFV), is a deadly hemorrhagic disease, which affects both domestic pigs and wild boar [1–3]. The disease was initially endemic in Sub-Saharan Africa, and subsequently spread to Eastern Europe, the Russian Federation, and Asia [4,5], posing a major threat to the pig industry worldwide due to its high mortality as well as the unavailability of licensed vaccines outside Vietnam. ASFV is a double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the family Asfarviridae [2]. The ASFV virion displays a multilayered architecture with 260–300 nm in diameter and is composed of an outer membrane, capsid, inner membrane, core shell, and nucleoid [6,7]. The genome of ASFV is approximately 170–194 kilobase pairs (kb) in length, encoding more than 150 viral proteins, some of which are involved in the evasion of innate immune responses and approximately half of which remain to be identified [3]. More importantly, ASFV has evolved multiple strategies to evade the immune responses.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a complex network of tubular membranes that exists exclusively in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells and performs multifunctional signaling roles, including the synthesis, processing, posttranslational modification and quality control of proteins [8–10]. Most secretory proteins and the noncytosolic domains of transmembrane proteins are cotranslationally transported into the ER lumen to ensure proper folding into their native conformations [11]. Notably, the capacity of protein folding in the ER is limited and prone to errors [12]. When folded proteins exceed the ER processing capacity, the accumulation of unfolded and misfolded proteins occurs, leading to ER stress. To maintain ER homeostasis, the unfolded protein response (UPR) in eukaryotic cells can be activated upon ER stress to reduce global protein synthesis and increase the ER folding capacity, thereby decreasing the incoming polypeptide load [13,14]. The cell machinery regulating protein load and quality in the ER lumen is monitored by three transmembrane proteins: protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) [15,16]. Binding immunoglobulin protein (BiP), an ER-resident chaperone and the principal regulator of UPR, transiently binds to the luminal domain of each sensor during homeostasis [17]. However, in the presence of accumulated unfolded and misfolded proteins in the ER lumen, BiP can detach from the sensors, bind to the newly synthesized polypeptides, and activate the three sensors, thereby initiating downstream signaling [17]. Accumulating evidence has shown that the multifunctional signaling in the ER is closely related to the viral replication and pathogenicity [18,19]. A comprehensive analysis of the interplay between viruses and ER stress is expected to unveil viral pathogenic mechanisms.

Viruses usually hijack the cellular machineries in favor of viral replication [18,20]. Thus, a defining feature of viral pathogenicity is the complex interactions between viruses and host cells, followed by the control of host cell signaling pathways. One of the most common disturbances of the host cell signaling pathways during viral infections is cellular calcium (Ca2+) oscillations. Ca2+ is a versatile second messenger that is responsible for regulating a plethora of cellular processes, including cell survival, proliferation and apoptosis [21]. Multiple viruses encode regulatory proteins that disturb normal Ca2+ homeostasis and hijack the cellular Ca2+ signaling to regulate viral infections and pathogenesis [22–24]. A better understanding of the mechanism underlying the Ca2+ signaling modulated by the virus can provide new targets for the development of antivirals.

As a large DNA virus, ASFV hijacks ER for the synthesis, processing and modification of its viral proteins, as well as the formation of the inner membrane of the virion [25]. Previous studies have shown that ER stress can be induced by the infection with the Vero cells-adapted ASFV or the ectopically expressed viral proteins [26–31]. However, the pathogenesis of ER stress and the subsequent signaling activation induced by the wild-type ASFV (ASFV-WT) infection in target cells remain largely unknown. Here, we demonstrated that ASFV infection significantly triggers ER stress and activates the ATF6 branch of the UPR both in target cells and in vivo. Moreover, we revealed that the activation of the ATF6 pathway results in the Ca2+ release from ER via the inositol triphosphate receptor (IP3R) channel. This study demonstrates for the first time that ASFV modulates the ER stress-ATF6-Ca2+ to facilitate its replication, which provides new insights into the understanding of ASFV infection and pathogenesis and the development of novel antivirals.

Materials and methods

Cells, virus strain, and antibodies

HEK293T (human embryonic kidney) cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (catalog no. D6221; Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (catalog no. 10099-141C; Gibco) and 1% antibiotics-antimycotics (10,000 IU/ml penicillin and 10,000 µg/ml) (catalog no. 15240-062; Gibco). Primary porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) and wild boar lung (WSL) cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (catalog no. C11875500BT; Gibco) with 10% FBS and 3% antibiotics-antimycotics. All cells were grown at 37°C with 5% CO2. The ASFV HLJ/2018 strain (GenBank accession no. MK333180.1) was propagated in PAMs. Following infection, the virus-containing supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes to remove cell debris and then aliquots were stored at –80°C for further studies.

Rabbit anti-BiP (catalog no. A0241) and anti-eIF2α (catalog no. A0764) polyclonal antibodies (PAbs) were purchased from ABclonal. Rabbit anti–calreticulin (calr) (catalog no. #12238) and anti-p-eIF2α (catalog no. #3398) PAbs were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Rabbit anti-ATF4 (catalog no. 10835-1-AP) and anti-TRAF2 (catalog no. 26846-1-AP) PAbs were purchased from Proteintech. Rabbit anti-calnexin (canx) (catalog no. ADI-SPA-865F) PAb was purchased from ENZO. Rabbit anti-ATF6α (catalog no. ab227830), anti-IRE1 (catalog no. ab37073), and anti-IRE1α (catalog no. ab124945) PAbs were purchased from Abcam. Rabbit anti-Flag (catalog no. F7425) and anti-Myc (catalog no. M4430) PAbs were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (catalog no. 926-32210) and goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (catalog no. 926-32211) PAbs were purchased from Li-Cor Biosciences. Alexa Fluor 633-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (catalog no. A21071) and 488-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (catalog no. A21206) antibodies were purchased from Invitrogen. The swine anti-p30 PAbs were prepared in our lab [32].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging

For the examination of ER morphology, PAMs were infected with the ASFV HLJ/2018 strain at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5. The mock-infected PAMs were included as a negative control. At 24 hours postinfection (hpi), the cells were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde over night at 4°C and embedded in epoxy according to the manufacturer's protocols. The samples processed above were analyzed on H-7650 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 80 kV.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from PAMs with specific inhibitor treatment or viral infection by a Simply P total RNA extraction kit (catalog no. BSC52M1; BioFlux) and converted to cDNA using a FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (catalog no. KR118-02; Tiangen), according to the manufacturer's protocols. The cDNAs were used to analyze the mRNA transcription levels of BiP, PERK, activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), IRE1, spliced X-box-binding protein 1 (XBP1s), unspliced XBP1 (XBP1u), calr, and canx by RT-qPCR using a QuantStudio system (Applied Biosystems, USA). The relative mRNA levels of the target genes were normalized to the internal reference GAPDH, and all primers are listed in Table S1. The relative fold changes in gene expression were determined by the 2–ΔΔCT threshold cycle (CT) method.

ASFV genomic DNA was extracted from the cells and cell supernatants using a MagaBio plus virus DNA purification kit (catalog no. 9109; BioFlux) according to the manufacturer's protocols. ASFV genomic DNA copies were quantified by qPCR targeting the B646L gene using the primers listed in Table S1.

Western blotting analysis

Cells were lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer (catalog no. P0013F; Beyotime) containing 1 mM phenyl methyl sulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (catalog no. BL507A; Biosharp), for 30 minutes at 4°C and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 5 minutes to remove cell fragments. A BCA protein assay kit (catalog no. PC0020; Solarbio) was used to quantify the protein according to the manufacturer's protocols. Equivalent amounts of protein were boiled in SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) loading buffer (catalog no. P0015L; Beyotime) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (catalog no. PC0020, Millipore). The nitrocellulose membranes were blocked with 5% fat-free milk in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated for 6 hours at 4°C with primary antibodies at a 1:1000 dilution. The membranes were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween (PBST) for three times and then incubated for 1 hour at 37°C with secondary antibodies diluted at 1:10,000. The membranes were washed with PBST and then scanned using an Odyssey infrared imaging system (Li-Cor BioSciences). The relative expression levels of each protein were quantified by the ImageJ software.

Laser confocal microscopy

Approximately 2 × 105 HEK293T cells were cotransfected with pEGFP-ATF6α [33] and pCA-B117L-Flag (0.5 μg each). At 24 hours posttransfection (hpt), the nuclear translocation of the ATF6α was analyzed by laser confocal microscopy. HEK293T cells were cotransfected with pCA-ATF6-Myc and pCA-B117L-Flag (0.5 μg each). At 24 hpt, the colocalization of ATF6 and pB117L was checked as above. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (catalog no. BL539A; Biosharp) for 30 minutes, washed with PBS for three times, and then permeabilized with 0.15% triton X-100 (catalog no. 1139100; BioFroxx) for 15 minutes at room temperature. The cells were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 880 laser-scanning confocal microscope after incubation with the relevant primary and secondary antibodies.

RNA interference assay

The siRNAs against PERK, ATF4, IRE1, XBP1, and ATF6 were transfected into PAMs using the X-tremeGENE siRNA transfection reagent (catalog no. 04476115001; Roche). At 8 hpt, the cells were infected with ASFV-WT at an MOI of 1 or 0.1. At 2 hpi, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with fresh medium for another 24 hours. The siRNAs used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Inhibitor treatment assay

For the inhibitor treatment assay, 5 × 105 PAMs cultured in 24-well plates were infected with ASFV-WT at an MOI of 1 or 0.1. At 2 hpi, fresh medium and specific inhibitors [1 μM GSK2606414, 20 μM ISRIB, 20 μM 4μ8c, 2.5 μM toyocamycin, 75 μM AEBSF, 10 μM ceapin-A7, 30 μM BAPTA-AM, 20 μM 2-aminoethyl diphenylborinate (2-APB), 75 μM dantrolene sodium (Dan) (catalog no. S5478; Selleck), or 1 μM thapsigargin (Tg) (catalog no. T9033; Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (catalog no. D2650; Sigma-Aldrich)] were added for 24 hours. GSK2606414 (catalog no. HY-18072; MCE), ISRIB (catalog no. HY-12495; MCE), 4μ8c (catalog no. HY-19707; MCE), toyocamycin (catalog no. HY-103248; MCE), AEBSF hydrochloride (catalog no. HY-12821; MCE), ceapin-A7 (catalog no. HY-108434; MCE), BAPTA-AM (catalog no. HY-100545; MCE), 2-APB (catalog no. HY-W009724; MCE). Finally, the supernatant and cell debris were harvested for the examination of ASFV genome copies and viral titers, and the cell lysates dissolved in NP-40 buffer were examined for the expression of the viral protein p30. The mock-treated PAMs infected with ASFV-WT served as a negative control.

Virus titration

Hemadsorption (HAD) assay is a classical method to titrate ASFV-WT. For the HAD assay, PAMs were seeded in 96-well plates and infected with ASFV-WT for 72 hours, and 1% porcine red blood cells diluted in PBS were added. The cells were cultured for another 48 hours, followed by viral titration by HAD assay.

Calcium measurement

Cytosolic calcium (Ca2+) in HEK293T and WSL cells were examined using a Fluo-4 Flux analysis kit (catalog no. S1061S; Beyotime). Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded into 96-well plates and transfected with pCA-ATF6-Myc or pCA-B117L-Flag. At 24 hpt, the culture medium was removed, the cells were washed with PBS, and then Fluo-4 AM staining solution were added for 30 minutes at 37°C, according to the manufacturer's instructions. WSL cells were seeded in 96-well plates and transfected with the ATF6 mRNA, followed by infection with ASFV-WT (MOI = 1). At 24 hpi, the cells were washed with PBS, and Fluo-4 AM staining solution was added according to the manufacturer's instructions. The fluorescence signals were recorded and analyzed using an EnVision multimode plate reader (catalog no. 6005182; Perkin-Elmer) at 535 nm (excitation at 485 nm). The green fluorescence was detected under a microscope (TE2000-U, Nikon, Japan).

Cytosolic Ca2+ in PAMs was examined using Mag-Fluo-4 AM (catalog no. AAT-20401; AAT Bioquest) as described previously [24]. Briefly, PAMs were seeded into 96-well plates and infected with ASFV-WT, and the mock-infected PAMs were used as a control. At 6, 12, and 24 hpi, the PAMs were incubated with Mag-Fluo-4 AM for 45 minutes at 37°C. The fluorescence signals were analyzed at 535 nm (excitation at 485 nm). The same method was used to determine the Ca2+ level in the PAMs infected with ASFV-WT of different MOIs. The green fluorescence was detected under a microscope (TE2000-U, Nikon, Japan).

Ca2+ level in tissues were measured using a Ca2+ colorimetric assay kit (catalog no. S1063S; Beyotime). Briefly, 20 mg of tissues were added with 200 μL of lysis buffer and homogenized with an appropriate homogenizer. Subsequently, the lysates were centrifuged with 12,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected for the Ca2+ measurement according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Representative sections of spleen and lung tissues were stored in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature in our previous study [34]. Paraffin embedding and sectioning were performed by the histology service of Harbin Veterinary Research Institute. Four-micrometer sections were examined by IHC for BiP and ASFV p30 expression. Briefly, the samples were dewaxed at 60°C and rehydrated by successive incubations in 100% xylene, 100% ethanol, and 96% ethanol. The endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 1% H2O2 diluted in methanol in the keep from light at 37°C. The slides were incubated with anti-BiP (1:50) and anti-p30 (1:50) MAbs, at 4°C overnight and subsequently hybridized with HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse lgG (catalog no. AP308P; Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 hour. The immunocomplex was analyzed using the 3,3’-diaminobenzidine liquid substrate system.

Plasmid construction and transfection

The full-length sequences with a Myc tag at the C-terminus of the ATF6 and PERK genes were amplified from PK-15 cells by RT-PCR and inserted into the pCAGGS vector. The plasmid pCA-B117L-Flag harboring the ASFV B117L gene was constructed previously in our laboratory. All the constructed plasmids were confirmed by sequencing. Transfection with these expression plasmids was performed in HEK293T cells using the X-tremeGENE HP DNA transfection reagent (catalog no. 06366236001; Roche), according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells in 24-well plates were cotransfected with an ATF6 reporter system harboring the ATF6 promoter and the plasmid expressing the firefly luciferase gene (ATF6-Fluc) (100 ng), pTK-Rluc (10 ng), and pCA-B117L-Flag (500 ng). The cells cotransfected with the reporter plasmid and the empty vector (pCAGGS) were served as a negative control. At 24 hpt, the cells were washed with PBS and then lysed in passive lysis buffer for 30 minutes at 4°C. The samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 5 minutes to remove cell debris, and then the luciferase activities were analyzed by a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (catalog no. E1910; Promega) according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay

HEK293T cells were seeded on 6-well plates and then cotransfected with pCA-B117L-Flag and pCA-ATF6-Myc or pCA-PERK-Myc (2 μg each). At 24 hpt, the cells were harvested and lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer for 30 minutes at 4°C with gentle shaking. WSL cells were seeded on 6-well plates and transfected with pOK12-B117L-Flag (4 μg), followed by infection with ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) at 8 hpt. At 24 hpi, the cells were harvested and lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer. The samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 minutes to remove cell debris and then the supernatants were incubated with anti-Flag M2 magnetic beads (catalog no. M8823; Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C for 8 hours. Subsequently, the precipitated proteins were washed five times with PBS, followed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting analysis.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using a CellTiter-Glo luminescent kit (catalog no. G7571; Promega) according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA). Statistical differences between groups were assessed by Student's t test. All experiments were performed independently at least three times. Error bars represented the standard deviations (SD) or standard errors of the means (SEM) in each group, as indicated in the figure legends (ns, not significant, P > 0.05; *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

ASFV infection induces ER stress and the downstream ATF6 branch of the UPR in PAMs

To investigate whether ASFV infection induces ER stress in PAMs, we initially analyzed the transcription and expression of BiP, a marker of ER stress, in the ASFV-infected PAMs, and the ER stress inducer tunicamycin (Tu) was used as a positive control. Tu is a well-recognized ER stress activator that can aggravate ER stress specifically by inhibiting N-glycosylation in the ER [35]. Interestingly, we observed a biphasic change in both mRNA and protein levels of BiP, with an initial decrease followed by an increase (Figure 1(A,B)). Notably, the expression of BiP significantly decreased at 6 hpi and then gradually increased at 24 hpi in the ASFV-infected PAMs. In addition, the BiP expression kinetics induced by ASFV infection at an MOI of 0.1 was similar to that of the viral infection at an MOI of 1 (Figure S1A and B). A previous study has revealed that ER swelling is associated with ER stress [36]. Furthermore, we observed the significant swelling of ER near the nucleus in the ASFV-infected PAMs, whereas extremely dense cytoplasm containing morphologically normal organelles in the mock-infected PAMs (Figure 1(C), S1C and D). In response to ER stress, UPR is activated to maintain ER homeostasis in eukaryotic cells. UPR includes three signaling pathways, i.e. the ATF6, PERK-eIF2α, and IRE1α-XBP1 pathways. ATF6 is a type II transmembrane protein located in the ER, expressed as a 90-kDa protein (p90ATF6) [37]. Upon ER stress, p90ATF6 is cleaved in the Golgi by the site-1 and site-2 proteases, resulting in the release of N-terminal 50-kDa protein (ATF6α). Subsequently, the ATF6α transports into the nucleus and activates the transcription of ER chaperone genes, including the glucose-regulated proteins 94 (GRP94), protein disulfide isomerase (PDI), calreticulin (calr), and calnexin (canx). Calr and canx also known as the “canx-calr” cycle, which is involved in protein glycosylation [38]. PERK is a type I transmembrane protein and is activated by dimerization and phosphorylation, followed by the phosphorylation of eIF2α [39]. The resulting p-eIF2α selectively activates the translation of the downstream ATF4 [40]. Moreover, the IRE1α-XBP1 pathway is the most conserved branch of the UPR. Under ER stress, IRE1 is activated by dimerization and auto-phosphorylation, thereby mediating the splicing of the downstream XBP1 mRNA [41].

To clarify the activation of the three UPR pathways in the context of ASFV infection in PAMs, we examined the expression of various UPR components. We initially analyzed the transcription kinetics and protein expression of key molecules in the three branches of the UPR pathways. We found that the expression and transcription of the ATF6 pathway key molecules were increased in the ASFV-infected PAMs (Figure 1(D) and S2A–C). In addition to the transcription kinetics of XBP1s and XBP1u, the XBP1 mRNA splicing assay showed that the XBP1s did not increase in the context of ASFV infection (Figure 1(E)), indicating that the IRE1α-XBP1 pathway is not activated during ASFV infection. Similar results were obtained when the PAMs were infected with ASFV at an MOI of 0.1 (Figure S2D–H). Taken together, these data demonstrated that ASFV infection activates the ATF6 branch of the UPR in PAMs.

The ATF6 or IRE1α-XBP1 pathway is beneficial for ASFV replication in PAMs

It has been established that the host's intrinsic defensive system includes the cellular UPR [22]. To clarify if the activation of UPR pathways inhibits ASFV replication, PAMs were transfected with specific small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs), targeting key molecules in the three branches, followed by ASFV infection. Unexpected, the results showed that the knockdown of ATF6 or XBP1 resulted in a reduction in ASFV replication (Figure 2(A–C)). Next, to further explore the engagement of the UPR pathways in ASFV replication, the specific inhibitors targeting key molecules in the three branches were added to the ASFV-infected PAMs. The results showed that the ASFV replication was reduced upon treatment with ceapin-A7 and AEBSF (ATF6-specific inhibitors) or toyocamycin (an XBP1-specific inhibitor), but not with GSK2606414 and ISRIB (PERK-specific inhibitors, which can effectively reverse eIF2α phosphorylation and restore cellular translation) or 4μ8c (an IRE1-speicific inhibitor) (Figure 2(D–F)). These findings consistent with the siRNA knockdown results. Moreover, ceapin-A7 or AEBSF reduced ASFV replication in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2(G–L)). Similar results were obtained when the cells were infected with ASFV at an MOI of 1 (Figure S3). Collectively, these data demonstrated that inhibition of the ATF6 and IRE1α-XBP1 branches of the UPR impairs the ASFV replication. The robust effects of the ATF6 pathway on ASFV replication encourage us to uncover the underlying mechanism. It is well-accepted that the functional roles of calr and canx are governed by the cellular Ca2+ level [42]. Thus, we speculated that the ATF6 pathway is involved in regulating the intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis. To test this hypothesis, we initially determined whether ASFV infection disrupts the Ca2+ homeostasis in PAMs. The results showed that the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level exhibited a progressive increase from 0 to 24 hpi, consistent with the viral growth kinetics (Figure 3(A,B)). Moreover, the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level was increased at 24 hpi upon ASFV infection at higher viral MOIs (Figure 3(C,D)). The bright images of the PAMs for Ca2+ detection were shown in Figure S6A and B. The ASFV-activated ATF6 pathway results in increased cytoplasmic Ca2+ level

Ca2+ functions as a multifunctional second messenger closely related to the intracellular signal transduction. To confirm the regulatory effect of the ATF6 pathway on intracellular Ca2+, we overexpressed or knocked down the ATF6 in HEK293T cells to examine its impact on the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. As expected, the overexpression of ATF6 resulted in increased cytoplasmic Ca2+ level in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 4(A,B)), whereas ATF6 knockdown reduced the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level (Figure 4(C)). We also validated the results using the ceapin-A7 and AEBSF. The results showed that the inhibition of ATF6 pathway reduces the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level (Figure 4(D,E)). In addition, siATF6-3 exhibited an efficient knockdown of ATF6 expression, with no cytotoxicity to HEK293T cells (Figure S7A and B). The concentrations of ceapin-A7 and AEBSF used in this study showed no cytotoxicity to HEK293T cells (Figure S7C and D). These findings substantiate the correlation between the ATF6 pathway and cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. Since the plasmid transfection of primary macrophages is difficult, the ATF6-specific siRNAs or specific inhibitors were used to investigate the effects of the ATF6 pathway on the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level in PAMs. The results showed that the knockdown or inhibition of the ATF6 pathway significantly decreased the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level in PAMs (Figure 4(C–E)). Taken together, these data demonstrated that the ATF6 pathway is crucial for regulating the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. Afterward, the ATF6-targeting siRNAs or inhibitors were used to evaluate the functional role of the ATF6 pathway in Ca2+ homeostasis in the context of ASFV infection. Since the plasmid transfection of primary macrophages is difficult, we also overexpressed ATF6 in WSL cells to determine the relationship between the ATF6 pathway and Ca2+ homeostasis in the context of ASFV infection. The IP3R channel is responsible for ER-dependent Ca2+ release during ASFV infection

Next, we attempted to evaluate how the ATF6 pathway regulates the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. We noticed that PAMs were cultured in calcium-free RPMI-1640, ensuring that the increased cytoplasmic Ca2+ level in the ASFV-infected PAMs mainly originated from intracellular Ca2+ storage rather than extracellular sources. ER serves as an important organelle for the storage of Ca2+, making it highly sensitive to viral infections. Therefore, the correlation between Ca2+ oscillations in the ER and ASFV replication was assessed using Tg, an inhibitor of the sarco-endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ ATPase that depletes Ca2+ in the ER. As shown in Figure 5(A–C), the depletion of Ca2+ in the ER significantly inhibited ASFV replication. The results suggested that Ca2+ oscillations in the ER are important for ASFV replication. The ER Ca2+-releasing channel, IP3R, and ryanodine receptor (RyR), have been functionally characterized [44]. IP3R-mediated intracellular Ca2+ increase is the major Ca2+ signaling pathway in astrocytes in the central nervous system. RyR is a Ca2+-selective channel of the ER, and one of its functions is to release large amounts of Ca2+ from the ER into the cytoplasm during muscle contraction. Hence, we examined the effects of the IP3R-specific inhibitor 2-APB or the RyR-specific inhibitor Dan on the ASFV-induced Ca2+ oscillations in the ER. The results revealed a reduction in cytoplasmic Ca2+ level when inhibiting the IP3R channel, but not the RyR channel, in the ASFV-infected PAMs (Figure 5(D,E)), indicating that the IP3R channel is responsible for the ASFV-induced Ca2+ oscillations in the ER.

Subsequently, 2-APB or Dan was used to investigate the effects of the IP3R or RyR channel on ASFV replication. The results showed that 2-APB inhibited ASFV replication (Figure 5(F–H)), whereas Dan displayed no effect on ASFV replication (Figure 5(I–K)). Moreover, the concentrations of Tg, 2-APB, and Dan used showed no cytotoxicity to PAMs (Figure S8A–C).

The ASFV B117L protein (pB117L) is involved in the regulation of the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level

Ca2+ signaling regulates multiple cellular signaling pathways, and many viruses utilize specific viral proteins to regulate the intracellular Ca2+ level to facilitate viral replication. Viroporins function as the channels of ion exchange. Recently, Gladue et al. have shown that the ASFV pB117L is located in the ER and is a potential viroporin [45], thus, we speculate that pB117L induces ER stress and regulates cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. To verify this hypothesis, HEK293T cells were transfected with pCA-B117L-Flag to investigate the effects of pB117L on BiP expression. The results showed that the ectopically expressed pB117L promoted BiP expression in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6(A)), indicating that pB117L can induce ER stress. Next, we confirmed that pB117L significantly induced the activation of the ATF6 promoter (Figure 6(B)). Co-IP assay further revealed that pB117L-Flag interacted with ATF6-Myc (Figure 6(C)). To clarify the ATF6-B117L interaction occurred in the context of ASFV infection, the C-terminally Flag-tagged B117L gene under the control of the B117L promoter was cloned into the pOK12 vector to generate pOK12-B117L-Flag. WSL cells were transfected with pOK12-B117L-Flag and subsequently infected with ASFV-WT to activate the B117L promoter, allowing the expression of pB117L-Flag in the context of ASFV infection. The interaction between pB117L and endogenous ATF6 was confirmed in the context of ASFV infection by using Co-IP assay (Figure 6(D)). Afterward, we confirmed that the colocalization of ATF6 and pB117L by laser confocal microscopy, with a colocalization coefficient of 0.79 (Figure 6(E)). We also showed that pB117L promoted the translocation of ATF6α to the nucleus (Figure 6(E)), which is an important indicator of the activation of the ATF6 pathway. Collectively, these results indicate that pB117L induces ER stress and triggers the activation of the ATF6 pathway. Furthermore, we investigated the effects of pB117L on the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. As shown in Figure 6(F,G), the ectopically expressed pB117L increased the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level, indicating that pB117L is involved in the regulation of the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level. The overexpression of pB117L had no obvious cytotoxicity to HEK293T cells at the indicated time points (Figure S9A). Next, to determine the involvement of pB117L in the increase of cytoplasmic Ca2+ level induced by ASFV infection, we attempted to generate the B117L gene-deleted mutant ASFV. Unfortunately, the mutant virus with the B117L gene deleted could not be generated, as reported recently [45]. Therefore, we designed siRNAs to knock down the expression of the B117L gene. The results showed that the knockdown of B117L resulted in a decrease in cytoplasmic Ca2+ level in the context of ASFV infection (Figure 6(H)). Consistently, the knockdown of B117L resulted in reduced ASFV replication (Figure 6(I)). In addition, siB117L-3 exhibited efficient effects on ASFV infection, with no cytotoxicity to PAMs (Figure S9B and C). Moreover, the overexpression of pB117L increased the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level in the context of ASFV infection in WSL cells (Figure S9D). Altogether, the results suggested that pB117L is involved in the ASFV-induced ER stress and the regulation of cytoplasmic Ca2+ level.

ASFV infection induces ER stress and activates the three branches of the UPR in pigs

To explore whether ASFV infection induces ER stress in vivo, we examined the expression of BiP in the lungs and spleens of the ASFV-infected pigs in our previous study [36]. Briefly, two groups pigs (n = 3) were inoculated intramuscularly with 103.0 TCID50 of the ASFV HLJ/2018 strain or RPMI 1640. Following infection, all the three ASFV-infected pigs developed fever at 3 days postinfection (dpi) and died at 9 dpi, and the mock-infected pigs were euthanized at the end of the experiment. The lungs and spleens were collected from all the ASFV-infected or mock-infected animals and subjected to RT-qPCR and Western blotting analyses. The findings revealed that ASFV infection resulted in a more than 15-fold increase in BiP mRNA transcription in the lungs and a more than 40-fold increase in the spleens compared with that of the mock-infected pigs (Figure 7(A,B)). The BiP expression in the lungs and spleens of the ASFV-infected pigs was increased compared with that in the mock-infected pigs (Figure 7(C,D)). Consistent results were obtained for protein expression level by IHC (Figure 7(E,F)). ASFV infection was confirmed by IHC with an anti-p30 monoclonal antibody (MAb). To investigate the activation of the three UPR pathways in the ASFV-infected pigs, the transcription kinetics of key molecules in the three branches of the UPR pathways were analyzed by RT-qPCR. The results revealed the increased transcription of PERK, ATF4, IRE1, XBP1u/s, calr, and canx, in the lungs (Figure 8(A–C)) and spleens (Figure 8(E–G)) of the ASFV-infected pigs. To demonstrate the specificity of the transcripts of cellular proteins in our study, we verified the mRNA transcription of HELZ in the tissues of the ASFV-infected pigs. The data revealed that there was no discernible change in HELZ transcription in the lungs (Figure 8(D)) and spleens (Figure 8(H)) of the ASFV-infected pigs. Discussion

As intracellular parasitic organisms, viruses usually hijack the protein synthesis system of host cells to produce nascent proteins, thereby facilitating viral replication. The protein folding demand of cells and viruses exceeds the ER capacity, resulting in the accumulation of unfolded and misfolded proteins in the ER lumen, leading to ER stress and the subsequent activation of UPR [19]. The cellular UPR has been recognized as an innate defense mechanism of the host that can degrade microbial components, inhibit global translation, or induce cell apoptosis as a means to combat viral invasion. Simultaneously, viruses have evolved various strategies to modulate UPR for their replication. Increasing evidence has also revealed that the UPR signaling pathways usually regulate other pathways, such as type I interferons (IFNs), inflammation, autophagy, and lipid metabolism [47–49]. In addition, it is generally acknowledged that ER stress induced by viral infection is associated with the pathogenicity of the virus.

In this study, we demonstrated that ASFV infection triggers ER stress both in vitro and in vivo. Interestingly, ASFV hijacks UPR to facilitate viral replication. We have also demonstrated that ASFV infection results in intracellular Ca2+ oscillations, and the ATF6 pathway favors ASFV replication by increasing the cytoplasmic Ca2+ level.

The regulation of protein quality control and homeostasis of the ER has been studied extensively in flaviviral infections. Flaviviruses, including dengue virus (DENV), yellow fever virus (YFV), West Nile virus (WNV), Zika virus (ZIKV), Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV), rely on the extensive membrane network of the ER for replication, translation, and assembly [50–52]. ZIKV and DENV can reorganize the ER membrane to form a viral replication complex (also referred to as the viral replication organelle) in the perinuclear area, which facilitates the viral life cycle. Therefore, the members of the Flaviviridae family are easily to trigger ER stress, and the initiation of UPR is also crucial for their replication. Among DNA viruses, the mechanisms of herpesvirus-induced ER stress have been elucidated [53]. During the viral replication, the synthesis and maturation of a large number of glycosylated envelope proteins depend on the ER, thereby inducing ER stress. In this study, we demonstrated that ASFV infection induces ER stress both in target cells and in vivo. In comparison with other viruses, ASFV infection induces a biphasic change of BiP in both mRNA and protein levels, with a drop at first and an increase afterwards.

UPR is an ancient but critical cellular adaptation mechanism that decreases ER loading and increases capacity and folding to maintain intracellular homeostasis [14]. UPR mainly includes three branches, namely PERK-eIF2α, IRE1α-XBP1, and ATF6 pathways [16]. The activation of the PERK-eIF2α pathway mainly alleviates the ER stress response by inhibiting protein synthesis. The IRE1α-XBP1 pathway is the most evolutionarily conserved in the process of the UPR, facilitating the entry of misfolded and unfolded proteins into degradation channels, thereby favoring the restoration of ER homeostasis. The ATF6 pathway serves two different functions: one promotes the expression of chaperones to help unfolded proteins fold into their native conformations, and the other initiates the ER-related degradation pathway to eliminate misfolded proteins. Therefore, the roles of the ATF6 pathway are versatile. It is noteworthy that most viruses can trigger all three branches of the UPR. However, recent studies have shown that the three branches of the UPR are selectively activated upon viral infection. Reportedly, WNV and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections preferentially activate the IRE1α-XBP1 and ATF6 pathways, rather than the PERK-eIF2α pathway [54]. Classical swine fever virus (CSFV) infection selectively activates the PERK-eIF2α and IRE1α-XBP1 pathways [55]. Our findings show that ASFV infection only induces the activation of the ATF6 pathway in target cells. However, alongside the ATF6 pathway, XBP1 knockdown slightly inhibited ASFV replication. Consequently, we presumed that the UPR branches collaborating could occur upon ASFV infection. Therefore, further research is warranted to define the functions of the IRE1α-XBP1 branch in the context of ASFV infection.

Ca2+ serves as ubiquitous intracellular signaling molecule that is widely distributed and regulates a variety of cellular functions. Throughout the virus life cycle, numerous processes are Ca2+-dependent, such as viral entry, activation of transcription, viral particle morphogenesis, and viral particle release [24–26]. Several studies have demonstrated that the Ca2+ regulation proteins encoded by viruses represent attractive therapeutic targets for controlling a variety of viral infections [56,57]. In this study, we provided the novel data that cytoplasmic Ca2+ is required for ASFV replication. Mechanically, we revealed that the activation of the ATF6 branch results in the increased cytoplasmic Ca2+ level and the Ca2+ release from ER via the IP3R channel. This is the first time to report the crosstalk between the ER stress, ATF6 pathway, Ca2+, and ASFV replication. The underlying mechanisms by which Ca2+ regulates ASFV replication are highly intricate, encompassing multiple stages of ASFV infection, which requires further investigation. Furthermore, we showed that the ASFV pB117L plays crucial roles in the ER stress and the downstream activation of the ATF6 branch, as well as the disruption of Ca2+ homeostasis. Nevertheless, further investigation is required to determine whether additional viral protein(s) besides pB117L is able to induce ER stress.

In this study, we have showed that ASFV infection triggers ER stress both in target cells and in vivo. More specially, the different MOIs (0.1 or 1) of ASFV infection induced the activation of the ATF6 branch in PAMs, but not the other two branches. Notably, the ATF6 branch is predominately activated in the lungs and spleens of the ASFV-infected pigs, while the other two branches are slightly activated, attributed to the complex microenvironment in pigs. Furthermore, in comparison with cell cultures, the time course of the infected pigs was notably longer. In this process, ASFV infection induces various immune responses in pigs and the consequent generation of several cytokines. The cytokines are likely to trigger the activation of the other two UPR pathways, thereby resulting in the indirect activation of the other two UPR branches, which requires further investigation in the future. During the preparation of our article, the induction of ER stress and inhibition of stress granules by ASFV infection were reported [58]. However, we revealed for the first time that ASFV manipulates the ER stress-ATF6-Ca2+ axis for efficient replication in PAMs, and further identified the ER stress and intracellular Ca2+ oscillations in the ASFV-infected pigs.

In conclusion, we demonstrate for the first time that ASFV infection triggers the activation of the ER stress-ATF6-Ca2+ axis to favor viral replication (Figure S11). These findings not only expand our understanding of the replication and pathogenesis of ASFV but also provide novel insights into the development of antiviral strategies for ASFV.
