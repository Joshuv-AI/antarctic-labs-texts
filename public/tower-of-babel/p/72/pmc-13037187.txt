The association between asthma and heart failure – a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Laura Vindeløv Bjerkén, Jonas Aggerholm Baekdal, Charlotte Suppli Ulrik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and aimAsthma and heart failure are highly prevalent diseases with substantial impact on quality of life and life expectancy. Studies have reported on the association between cardiovascular diseases and asthma. The aim of this review is to provide an update on the current evidence for an association between asthma and heart failure (HF) with regard to prevalence and outcome.MethodsA systematic literature search, last updated in ultimo May 2025, was performed on PubMed in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. The identified articles were systematically screened for eligibility. A random-effects meta-analysis was performed to pool hazard ratios and odds ratios for HF in asthma patients.ResultsA total of 11 studies were included. Four studies found that asthma was statistically significantly associated with an increased risk of HF. Two studies found a non-significantly increased risk of HF in asthma patients. The pooled HR for HF incidence in asthma patients was 2.03 (95% CI: 1.55; 2.68). Similarly, the pooled OR was 1.61 (95% CI: 1.09; 2.38). Three studies found increased morbidity and mortality in asthma patients with HF compared to controls. One study showed a non-significant increase in mortality in patients with concomitant asthma and HF, whereas another reported a decreased risk compared to HF alone.ConclusionsThe available evidence suggests a significant association between asthma and incident HF, as well as an increased risk of hospitalization in patients with both conditions, even after adjusting for sex, age, and other known cardiovascular risk factors.

Introduction

Asthma is a highly prevalent heterogeneous chronic inflammatory airway disease with multiple phenotypes and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [1]. Heart failure (HF) is another highly prevalent disease affecting millions of people worldwide [2], and probably just as many people have an unrecognized cardiac dysfunction [3]. Because of their heterogenous nature, including their clinical manifestations, both diseases may be challenging to diagnose. Asthma and HF constitute a worldwide health challenge due to the substantial impact on quality of life and, especially for heart failure, lifetime expectancy [4].

Emerging evidence suggests that asthma and HF may share pathophysiological pathways and risk factors, pointing to a potential bidirectional relationship. Chronic systemic inflammation, a hallmark of both conditions, has been implicated in the progression of asthma and the development of HF [5,6]. Additionally, shared comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes may contribute to the increased prevalence of HF among individuals with asthma [5,6]. However, these mechanisms are predominantly characteristic of patients with severe or poorly controlled asthma, who represent a minority of the overall asthma population. Severe asthma is associated with a higher systemic inflammatory burden, more frequent exposure to systemic corticosteroids, and a markedly increased prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities compared with mild disease [7,8].

Some medications used in the management of asthma, such as beta2-agonists have been hypothesized to influence cardiovascular outcomes and may exacerbate HF symptoms in susceptible individuals [9].

Despite these concerns, the extent to which asthma influences HF incidence and outcomes, and vice versa, remains poorly understood. Given the rising prevalence of chronic diseases and the increasing complexity of managing multimorbidity, understanding the interplay between asthma and HF is critical. Insights into this relationship could inform clinical practice, guiding strategies to improve diagnostic accuracy, optimize treatment, and ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

The aim of this systematic review is to provide an update on the current evidence of an association between asthma and heart failure. Additionally, it examines the impact of concomitant asthma and HF on critical clinical outcomes including hospitalization and mortality. By synthesizing available evidence, this review seeks to provide a foundation for future research and informing clinical decision-making.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the ‘Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses’ (PRISMA) guidelines [10].

Systematic search

We performed a systematic search in the PubMed database last updated on 27 May 2025. The search contained MeSH terms (‘Asthma’ AND ‘Heart Failure’) combined with free text words: asthma OR bronchial asthma OR asthmatic OR asthma attack OR asthma-COPD overlap (ACOS) OR respiratory disease, AND heart failure, OR left ventricular systolic dysfunction, OR reduced ejection fraction, OR cardiac failure, OR heart dysfunction OR heart disease OR cardiovascular disease OR CVD (suppl. material, search protocol).

Study selection

Studies were reviewed by two separate reviewers (LB and CU) using Covidence, first on the title/abstract level, then on the full-text level. In cases of disagreement the third author (JB) was consulted, and the article was discussed until consensus was reached. Studies were included if they fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: (1) Original research that addressed concomitant asthma and heart failure, (2) reported on incidence, prevalence, or outcome, (3) published in 2000 or later, and none of the following exclusion criteria: (1) non-English, (2) not reporting original data, (3) non-human studies (4) No Non-Asthma control group. A total of 4443 hits were found, 14 duplicates were identified automatically by Covidence and removed. The remaining 4429 articles were screened by title and abstract, and 57 potentially eligible articles were screened by full text review. Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa-scale (NOS) [11] (Suppl. material, Risk of bias assessment). The assessment was divided into three domains: Cohort/Sample selection, Comparability and Outcome, with a maximum total score of 9 stars. Studies scoring 4 or lower were considered to be of low quality and high risk of bias, 5–6 stars were of moderate quality and risk of bias, and 7–9 stars were considered high quality and low risk of bias.

Data extraction and statistical analysis

Data was extracted from both text, tables, and figures in the included studies.

Data was extracted by LB and JB using a standardized extraction tool designed in Excel. Disagreements were solved through consensus. Extracted information included: 1) Hazard ratios (HRs), including 95% confidence interval (CI), for developing HF in patients with asthma; 2) ORs including 95% CI for the association between asthma and HF.

Statistical analysis was performed in R version 4.3.2 utilizing the ‘Metafor’ package.

HRs and odds ratios (ORs) were log-transformed, and standard errors were calculated from the confidence intervals. Log-transformed HRs and ORs were pooled using a random-effects model before back transforming to the original scale for interpretation. Heterogeneity was assessed with I2 statistics. A meta-analysis of the HR for mortality in patients with concomitant asthma and HF was not performed due to significant variability in the definitions of both exposure and outcome between studies.

Results

A total of 11 studies fulfilled the prespecified criteria and were included in the present systematic review. The included studies comprised a population of a total of 1.133.91 participants, ranging from 191 [12] to 407.190 [13].

Seven of the included studies were cohort studies with three prospective [14–16], and four retrospective [12,13,17,18], and the remaining four were cross-sectional studies [19–22]. Six studies [13–15,19–21] investigated the risk of HF among asthma patients. Six studies reported on the association between concomitant asthma and HF on different outcomes, including mortality [12,14,16–18,22]. Three studies were considered to have low risk of bias [13–15], five studies were found to have moderate risk of bias, and three studies had a high risk of bias [12,20,21].

Asthma and risk of heart failure

Iribarren et al. [13] investigated the association between asthma and cardiovascular disease, including HF (ICD9 diagnosis), in a cohort of 203,595 adult members of the Kaiser Permanente Northern California health-care plan (Table 1). Eligible participants were age ≥ 18 years and had a history of health care service use due to asthma (ICD9 diagnosis code). The asthma-group was compared to an asthma-free, age-, sex-, and ethnicity matched group. The adjusted HR for HF was 2.33 (95% CI: 2.25, 2.41) for patients with asthma compared to the non-asthma group. After further adjustments for diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, BMI and smoking status, the HR for HF was 2.08 (95% CI: 2.01, 2.15). Furthermore, this association was statistically significant in both sexes (men: HR 2.04 (95% CI: 1.93–2.16); women: HR 2.20 (95% CI: 2.10–2.31)).

Steppuhn et al. [19] assessed the association between asthma and eight selected chronic conditions, including HF. The study included 2242 randomly selected participants age ≥ 18 years with a self-reported asthma diagnosis from two consecutive surveys of the ‘German Health Update’ (Table 1), a German recurrent national telephone health survey. Participants with asthma were compared to 41,070 controls without asthma. The results were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), education level and smoking status. A statistically significant association was found between asthma and HF compared to the control-group with an OR 2.74 (95% CI: 2.13–3.52).

Ingebrigtsen et. al [15]. evaluated the association between asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma-COPD-overlap-syndrome, and the risk of HF (ICD8/10 diagnosis). Furthermore, they analyzed the potential influence of age at asthma-onset on the association with HF. The study included 2119 participants age ≥ 40 years with a self-reported asthma diagnosis from the ‘Copenhagen general population study’ compared to a control-group of 42,058 participants without respiratory disease (Table 1). The analysis was adjusted for family history of cardiovascular disease, smoking status, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, total cholesterol levels and physical activity in leisure time. Both early-onset and late-onset asthma – defined as asthma diagnosis before or after 40 years of age – were significantly associated with an increased risk of HF compared to non-asthmatics. Early-onset asthma was associated with an increased risk of incident HF (HR 2.2, 95% CI: 1.4–3.4) compared to ‘no respiratory disease’, and this association remained statistically significant, though attenuated, in individuals with late-onset asthma (HR 1.5 95% CI: 1.1–2.2).

Carter et al. [14] assessed the association between respiratory disease, including asthma, and cardiovascular disease, including HF. The cohort comprised 60,424 patients age ≥ 18 years who had been admitted at least once to one of seven NHS hospitals across the Northwest of England, with an ICD10 diagnosis of asthma, along with 302.120 age- and sex-matched controls (Table 1). The analysis was adjusted for tobacco use, ethnicity, pre-existing cardiovascular diseases, cardiac procedures, and common causes of mortality [14]. No statistically significant association was found between asthma and risk of HF (OR 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12). However, a higher unadjusted baseline prevalence of HF was found among asthma patients compared to non-asthma patients (3.1% vs. 2.5%, p < 0.001) [14].

Lee et al. [21] investigated the association between childhood- and adult-onset asthma and the risk of specific cardiovascular conditions, including HF. The cohort consisted of US adults 18–90 years of age enrolled in the National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHANES) with a self-reported asthma diagnosis and an asthma-free control group. The asthma group was stratified into two groups: ‘adult-onset asthma’ (diagnosed after the age 18, n = 534, mean age 53.1 ± 18.4) and ‘child-onset asthma’ (diagnosed before age 18, n = 702, mean age 37.7 ± 16). They adjusted for age, systolic blood pressure, HDL cholesterol, BMI, Hs-CRP, smoking, and diabetes mellitus. No significant association was found between asthma and congestive HF in either asthma subgroup (adult-onset: OR 1.46, 95% CI 0.58–3.66; child-onset: OR 0.88 95% CI 0.27–2.88). A sub-analysis explored the influence of sex on the association between asthma and cardiovascular disease. Overall, women with adult-onset asthma exhibited a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular disease (OR 2.4, 95% CI 1.2–4.7). However, this association was not statistically significant when specifically examining HF (men: OR 1.69 95% CI 0.42–6.89; women: OR 1.29 95% CI 0.36–4.63).

Lin et al. [20], investigated the association between asthma control (poorly controlled vs. well-controlled) and hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and mortality in obese individuals. They included US adults age ≥ 18 years with a BMI ≥ 30 enrolled in NHANES with a self-reported asthma diagnosis compared to an asthma-free control-group. The asthma-group was divided into a ‘well-controlled’ group (n = 873) and a ‘poorly controlled’ group (n = 937). Asthma was categorized as well-controlled if the individual had not experienced an asthma attack or emergency care visit for asthma in the past year. The results were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, race, education, and smoking status. The results demonstrated that both poorly controlled asthma and well-controlled asthma were statistically significantly associated with an increased risk of HF compared to the control-group among obese individuals (well-controlled asthma: OR 1.35 95% CI: 1.01–1.82; poorly controlled asthma: OR 2.60 95% CI: 1.46–4.66). Furthermore, well-controlled asthma was statistically significantly associated with a lower risk of HF compared to poorly controlled asthma (OR 0.62 95% CI: 0.39–0.99).

The pooled HR for incidence of HF in asthma patients was 2.03 (95% CI: 1.55; 2.68, I2 = 66.4%, p < 0.0001). Similarly, the pooled OR was 1.61 (95% CI: 1.09; 2.38, I2 = 88.6%, p = 0.017) (Figure 2). Impact on morbidity and mortality

Morbidity

Sumino et al. [16] investigated the influence of certain comorbidities (including HF) in patients with asthma on clinical outcomes. Outcomes assessed included all-cause mortality, all-cause hospitalizations, asthma hospitalizations and asthma exacerbations (not further defined in study). The cohort consisted of 779 adults aged ≥ 18 years with a concomitant asthma and HF diagnosis (ICD-9) from the National Veterans Affairs and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services encounter database compared to a reference group of patients with asthma only (n = 7,884). The analysis was adjusted for sex, and prescription of glucocorticoids, digoxin, lipid-lowering agents, diuretics, and oral hypoglycemics. A statistically significant increased risk of all-cause hospitalization was observed only in the subgroup of patients aged 46–64 years in patients with concomitant asthma and HF, compared to the asthma-only group. Additionally, the authors reported a numerical, but not statistically significant, trend towards an increased OR for asthma-related hospitalizations in the asthma and HF group, compared to the asthma only group. A similar non-significant numerical trend was observed for asthma exacerbations across all age groups [16].

Gulea et al. [17] evaluated the impact of a concomitant diagnosis of asthma or COPD diagnosis on post-discharge management – including referrals to HF services and discharge medication – as well as in-hospital mortality in patients admitted with acute HF. The cohort comprised of 14,400 adults aged ≥ 18 years enrolled in the national”Heart Failure Audit” between 2012–2018 with a new diagnosis of HF and coexisting asthma diagnosis, compared with 170,297 HF-only patients (Table 1). Asthma was defined as a history of childhood asthma and atopy, or a diagnosis confirmed by a respiratory physician. Analyses were adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities, place of care and ‘New York Heart Association’ (NYHA) status. They reported on likelihood of being referred to a cardiology outpatient clinic at discharge and found that patients with concomitant asthma and HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) had an increased OR of being referred compared HFrEF alone (OR: 1.08 95% CI: 1.03–1.14). In contrast, among patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) had a lower OR for being referred at discharge in those with asthma compared to those without (OR: 0.93 95% CI: 0.88–0.98).

Mortality

Carter et al. [14], as described above, reported that HF was significantly and independently associated with increased all-cause mortality in patients with asthma compared controls (HR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.75–1.87).

Nakayama et al. [12] investigated the prognostic implications of a concomitant diagnosis of asthma and HF. The assessed outcomes included: all-cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal ischemic stroke, and hospitalization due to HF. The cohort consisted of 191 outpatients from Sagamihara National Hospital in Japan, who were ≥ 20 years of age and had a concomitant HF and asthma diagnosis compared with a HF-only control-group (Table 1). HF was defined according to the criteria by the ‘American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Stage C classification’ and asthma was defined according to the Global Initiative for Asthma guidelines (‘with clinical symptoms, spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility test, airway hyper-responsiveness test, fractional exhaled nitric oxide measurement, or sputum eosinophilia’). In the multivariable analysis, adjustments were made for age, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease and plasma BNP levels. The results showed that asthma was statistically significantly associated with an increased risk of the composite primary outcome (HR: 3.08, 95% CI: 1.42–6.71).

Sumino et al. [16], as described above, found an increased risk of mortality in the concomitant asthma/HF group compared to the asthma only group. This association was only statistically significant among patients aged > 65 years (OR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.09–1.85). In contrast, Gulea et al. [17] reported a decreased risk of mortality in patients with both asthma and HF compared to those with HF alone (OR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.79–0.88).

Soto-Campos et al. [22] evaluated causes of death in hospitalized patients without respiratory disease compared to patients with COPD or asthma. They included all deaths among patients admitted for any reason at 13 different Spanish hospitals during four months in 2008 (January, April, July and October), encompassing seasonal variation. The cohort consisted of three groups: patients with an asthma diagnosis (n = 82), patients with a COPD diagnosis (n = 283), and a control group without asthma or COPD (2,461). All age groups were included, and the mean ±SD age was 75 ± 14. The asthma group was stratified into two categories: a ‘high degree of certainty’ of asthma when the medical records contained diagnostic test and a ‘moderate degree of certainty’ when asthma-compatible symptoms or prescription for asthma medication were documented without confirmatory testing. Analyses were adjusted for age, sex, smoking, previous history of diabetes mellitus, obesity, hyperlipidemia and arterial hypertension. The most frequent cause of mortality in the asthma-group was cardiovascular disease. However, asthma was not statistically significantly associated with HF-related mortality compared to the non-asthma group (OR 1.57 (95% CI 0.83–2.99) p = 0.16).

Barge-Caballero et al. [18], investigated the impact of concomitant asthma or COPD on therapeutic management and prognosis in patients with HF. The cohort comprised of 2577 patients with HF referred to a specialized HF hospital unit at a tertiary Spanish hospital between January 2010 and June 2012; of these, 96 patients had a concomitant diagnosis of asthma. The mean age of the cohort was 63.9 ± 12.1 years. In the multivariable analysis, adjustments were made for year of first consultation, time since HF diagnosis, age, sex, diabetes, previous history of neoplasm, previous admission due to HF, NYHA class III or IV, Left ventricular ejection fraction > 40%, automatic defibrillator, systolic blood pressure, bilirubin, hemoglobin, NT-proBNP, beta-blocker use, loop diuretic use and ACE-Inhibitor/Angiotensin receptor blockers/sacubitril-valsartan use. The authors did not find a significant association between concomitant asthma and the composite outcome of death or hospitalization due to HF (HR: 1 95% CI: 0.72–1.39), nor with the combined outcome of cardiovascular death or heart transplantation (HR:1.36 95% CI: 0.88–2.11).

Impact on treatment

Barge-Caballero et al. [18], as described above, reported a statistically significantly lower rate of beta-blocker prescription in patients with concomitant asthma or COPD and HF compared to those with HF alone. During follow-up 94,1% of patients with HF alone were prescribed beta-blocker compared to 89.6% of patients with both COPD and HF, and 87.5% of patients with asthma and HF (p = 0.002).

Similarly, Nakayama et al. [12], reported that the use of beta blockers was significantly less frequent in the asthma and HF group compared to the HF-only group (55% vs. 83%, p = 0.01).

Gulea et al. [17], also found that beta-blockers were less often prescribed to patients with both asthma and HF compared to those with HF alone (OR: 0.66 95% CI: 0.59- 0.67).

Iribarren et al. [13], investigating the association between the use of asthma medication – including long acting β2-agonist, anticholinergic agents, leukotriene modifiers, and xanthine derivatives – and the risk of developing HF. Patients with asthma who were treated with any asthma medication had a significantly increased risk of HF compared to the individuals without asthma (HR: 2.24 (95% CI: 2.16–2.32)). In contrast, asthma patients who were not treated with asthma medication, had a significantly lower risk of HF compared to the non-asthma control group (HR: 0.71 95% CI: 0.62–0.81).

Discussion

Despite considerable heterogenicity among the studies – including variations in the definitions of asthma and HF as well as differences in study population characteristics- the findings suggest that concomitant asthma may influence HF-related morbidity and potentially mortality, although impact on mortality is less consistent. The observed associations remain even after adjustment for potential confounders, but may be modified by comorbidity burden, and asthma severity.

Risk factors and comorbidities

Most of the studies included in this review found that the association between asthma and HF remained significant after adjusting for smoking in their multivariable analyses [13,15,17,19–22]. These results indicate an association between HF and asthma independent of smoking as a risk factor.

In contrast, Pollevick et al., reported that asthma was only statistically significantly associated with an increased risk of CVD among smokers [23]. A possible explanation for the conflicting results could be that most of the studies do not take former smoking or lifetime tobacco exposure into account but use current smoking status at baseline in the adjusted models. Thereby some participants with a significant history of smoking may be included in the studies as non-smokers. Ingebrigtsen et al. addressed this bias in a sensitivity analysis by including only asthma patients with <10 pack-years, rather than categorizing smoking status. After this adjusting for pack-years there remained a significant association between asthma and HF [15].

Two of the included studies investigated how age at the time of asthma diagnosis affected the association between asthma and HF. Ingebrigtsen et al. [15], reported a stronger association between ‘early onset’ asthma and HF compared to ‘late onset’ asthma (HR 2.2 vs. 1.5). In contrast Lee et al. [21], reported that only ‘adult onset’ asthma was associated with an increased risk of HF and not child-onset, however the association was not significant (OR 1.46 (95% CI: 0.58–3.66) vs. OR 0.88 (95% CI: 0.27–2.88)). These conflicting findings may suggest that age is an effect modifier in the association between asthma and HF. Most of the studies included in this review had age-matched cohorts and did not investigate the direct effect of age in general on the association between heart failure and asthma. However, in their multivariate analysis adjusting for age among other covariate factors, several of the included studies reported that there remained a significant association between asthma and HF [12,13,15,19].

Impact of sex

Previous research suggests women with asthma face a higher risk of CVD than men [7,8]. Some of these studies attribute the higher CVD risk in women to female sex hormones, which may increase lung inflammation and trigger systemic inflammation more strongly in women [7]. However, this theory remains unvalidated in humans, necessitating further research to clarify the gender differences in asthma-related HF risk. Only two studies included in this review addressed sex-specific significance with conflicting results. Iribarren et al. found a stronger asthma-HF association in women, while Lee et al. reported the opposite [13,21].

Impact on outcome

The findings of the studies suggests that concomitant asthma and HF have implications for prognosis. However, the impact on morbidity and mortality is inconsistent and may be influenced by age [16]. While several studies did not reach statistical significance, many reported trends or point estimates suggesting a potential association between concomitant asthma and HF outcomes. For example, Nakayama et al. [12] reported a significant association between concomitant asthma and HF in regard to their primary outcome (mortality and hospitalization), an association primarily driven by a higher rate of hospitalization due to HF in the asthma and HF group compared the HF only group. Soto-campus et al. concluded that cardiovascular disease was the most common cause of death in their asthma cohort, with non-significant trends toward higher HF-related mortality [22].

Gulea et al. reported that concomitant asthma and HF was associated with a reduced OR for mortality compared to the HF-only group. They speculate that some of the explanation may be a cardioprotective effect of anti-inflammatory treatment used in asthma [17]. In a study by Yu et al. [24] they report that patients with asthma who experienced exacerbation within one-year preceding HF diagnosis, had an increased mortality rate compared to the group of patients with concomitant asthma and HF who did not experience exacerbation. They report that some of the increased mortality in concomitant asthma and HF may be explained by an increased inflammatory load following acute exacerbations that eventually impact the CVD [24]. In contrast Barge-Caballero et al. [18] found that the presence of asthma did not significantly impact the analyzed outcomes for HF patients. This may reflectselection bias in their cohort with the patients referred to the HF department generally being younger and with fewer comorbidities.

Only one of the included studies reported on the risk of asthma exacerbations and hospitalization due to asthma and found a numerical trend towards increased risk of exacerbations and asthma hospitalization for the asthma and HF group compared to the asthma-only group, though this did not reach statistical significance [16]. These findings indicate that while statistically significant associations for mortality are limited,it could be part of reason why a worse prognosis is seen in concomitant asthma HF: increased risk of exacerbation leading to an increased inflammatory load resulting in increased mortality. Associations may be modified by age, comorbidity burden, and asthma severity, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. As only few studies investigated the impact on prognosis of concomitant asthma and HF, and varied in what outcome was reported on, further investigation is warranted.

Impact of asthma treatment on the association between asthma and HF

Only a few studies explored the bidirectional impact of asthma and HF on medical treatment. Iribarren et al. [13] found that only asthma patients using asthma medication had an increased risk of HF (HR 2.4). Hirono et al. [25] reported more frequent HF hospitalizations among asthma patients using β2 agonists compared to those not using them. Similarly, Nakayama et al. [12] found β-stimulant use to be associated with worse outcomes, although statistical significance was not demonstrated. These findings imply that asthma treatment in itself may contribute to increased HF risk in asthma patients or at the very least raise the possibility that asthma treatment may act as a mediator or effect modifier in susceptible patients, particularly in the context of poorly controlled disease. However, this finding most likely also reflects confounding by indication, whereby treatment use serves as a marker of more severe asthma, poorly controlled asthma and a higher burden of comorbidities, which are themselves associated with increased HF risk. This interpretation is supported by Lin et al. [20], who found increased HF risk only in patients with poorly controlled asthma. Poorly controlled asthma likely reflects a greater disease severity, higher systemic burden, more frequent exposure to systemic corticosteroids and a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors all of which are associated with an HF risk, and cannot only be interpreted as a protective effect of asthma control per se.

Gulea et al. [17] suggest that the worse prognosis in HF patients with asthma compared to HF patients without asthma, might in part be due to undertreatment with beta-blockers which have a known positive effect on HF prognosis. They reported a decreased likelihood of beta-blocker prescription at discharge (OR 0.66, 95% CI: 0.59–0.67) for the HFrEF-asthma group compared to HF only. A similar result was found in the study by Barge-caballero et al. [18], who reported a significantly lower frequency of beta-blocker prescription in both the HF-COPD and HF-asthma group compared to the HF-only group. The finding is supported by Nakayama et al. [12]. This is despite evidence that suggest beta-blockers are safe to use in asthma patients if carefully up titrated [25].

Implications

As there seems to be an association between asthma and HF in terms of incidence and impact on prognosis, there may be a need for better integration of cardio- and pulmonary treatment offers to identify asthmatics who may be at increased risk of HF complications and to cooperate on outpatient treatment and follow-up.

Limitations

Important limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this review. The studies included demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in both outcome measures and definitions of asthma and HF, limiting the ability to make direct comparisons across the studies. Most of the studies used either self-reported asthma diagnosis rather than physician diagnosed asthma [15,17,19,21], or asthma diagnosis codes [13,14,16], which may have resulted in misclassification and thereby inclusion of some individuals without asthma or patients with COPD or COPD-asthma overlap. This limitation is especially relevant in older populations, where overlap between asthma and COPD is more common. However, prior research has demonstrated that self-reported asthma is a valid method to define asthma in population-based settings [26,27]. Nevertheless, misclassification remains a potential source of bias and may have influenced the observed associations. None of the studies reported data on asthma duration or severity.

Due to the limited number of studies, formal subgroup analyses stratified by diagnostic criteria were not feasible. Moreover, the potential for publication bias was not formally assessed, as the limited number of studies renders funnel plots and Egger’s test unreliable. Therefore, publication bias cannot be ruled out, and these limitations collectively warrant a cautious interpretation of the pooled estimates.

Furthermore, all included studies were observational introducing the inherent limitations of non-randomized research. Although multivariate adjustments were applied in analysis in all studies, the possibility of unaccounted residual confounding cannot be excluded, and comorbidities may have been incompletely or inconsistently accounted for across studies. Additionally, some of the studies – particularly the cross-sectional ones – did not adequately address potential risks of bias, such as sample size justification or the handling of non-responders (Suppl. material, risk of bias assessment).

The symptomatic overlap between asthma and HF, particularly dyspnea and wheezing, introduces a potential risk of bias. Symptoms of HF may initially be misidentified as asthma, leading to a false asthma diagnosis, and misclassification as exposed. When these patients are subsequently correctly diagnosed with HF, it creates a false temporal association where HF appears to follow asthma in cohort studies. We cannot exclude that this misclassification may have inflated the observed HR estimates. Additionally, patients with asthma undergo more frequent medical surveillance compared to healthy controls. This difference in surveillance have inflated the HR estimates as well.

Conclusion

The results suggest a significantly increased risk of HF among individuals with asthma compared to those without, as the risk/odds of heart failure where twice as highin individuals with asthma compared to the general population. Moreover, the coexistence of asthma and HF was associated with an increased risk of hospitalization, even after adjustments for potential confounders. These results highlight the need for an improved integration of asthma and HF management including coordinated outpatient follow-up. Further research is needed to determine whether the observed associations are causal and to elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.
