Preparing for the long road: Insights from the first few steps!
Authors: Naima Maqsood
Published: 2026

During my experience in the past several months in the electrophysiology (EP) laboratory, I have strived to better understand the occupational risks associated with a career in EP and have implemented changes to alleviate those risks. Occupational risks are not unique to EP, and all jobs have challenges—but most of all, at every step, I am reminded that a career in EP is exciting and rewarding.

Before delving into the occupational risks, let us remember why we chose this career in the first place. For me, EP is a unique field of medicine with the perfect blend of clinical prowess and procedural satisfaction. We all relate to the joy of ablating a supraventricular tachycardia that plagued a patient’s daily life, or “curing” patients from heart block with a pacemaker. I am confident that this is a shared feeling within the EP community, as is the goal to provide the best care for our patients. We, therefore, owe it to our patients to be able to continue doing this well and owe it to ourselves to be able to do so for a long time. With this goal in mind, knowledge about the risks is empowering. It allows us to be better prepared and enhance awareness among our colleagues. I recently educated a staff member regarding the higher incidence of certain cancers among interventional cardiologists and electrophysiologists, after he had casually mentioned that there were no data to suggest an increased risk.1, 2, 3, 4

With this background, here are my insights:1.The principles of time, distance, and shielding are the universal truths for radiation safety. As the primary operator, distance is the variable least in our control. However, this makes shielding and time all the more important.2.The term “as low as reasonably achievable” applies to patients and providers alike. Good radiation safety habits, such as bringing the image intensifier closer to the patient, raising the table, using less steep projections when able, and using the minimum number of frames necessary, when practiced repeatedly will reduce radiation exposure significantly over one’s career.13.There is a fine balance between the amount of coverage a provider may opt for in reducing radiation exposure and the toll on the body, particularly our backs. Radiation protection garments are no longer made from lead but from lightweight composite materials or lead-free alternatives. They are certainly lighter than in the past, but still, the weight is real. My lightweight lead garment weighs around 13 lb. There are always options for additional radiation protection in the form of arm shields and radiation hats, which add weight, but also radiation protection drapes, which do not add weight. Levitating radiation protection systems are available, and while they are currently quite cumbersome, they may pave the way for more streamlined systems in the near future. And even though cumbersome, if available to you, they are worth considering.4.Taking the use of lead as a given well-fitted lead is crucial. Not all institutions will have policies that provide personalized lead for trainees; however, we must advocate for this. Go to your program director, go to your chief. Be the “squeaky wheel.” Ill-fitting lead, even of 13 lb, places a much greater burden on the spine than the equivalent weight of well-fitted lead. Furthermore, the way a garment sits on the body during the procedure plays into the amount of strain experienced at the end of the procedure. To that point, getting brief but specific training on how to put on your lead matters (ie, tight, resting on the hips, not draped over the shoulders).5.I cannot stress enough the importance of building a strong core. I am quite new to this journey as well, but pride myself on having been consistent for 1 year. In fact, one of my attendings and I even competed against one another in a core-strengthening competition—I won, if only by a hair. And I have noticed a difference. The goal here should be to start slow and be persistent (or shall I say, incessant!). If I am able to continue this long-term, I will undoubtedly be stronger than I ever was or ever would have been if I chose a career where I did not have the motivation to do so. I have been performing the following exercises at least twice a week:a.Glute bridges: Add a resistance band with lateral leg extensions for a more advanced version.b.Wall squats with a resistance band: Increase the amount of time in the squat position for a more advanced version.c.The bird dog: Train to the weaker side. Start with single leg lifts if you have to and then progress to simultaneous arm/leg raises.d.Side plank: Start with the kneeling version and then advance to a full side plank.e.Plank: Essential to engage your core and keep the neck and shoulders straight to avoid strain on the neck.f.Bent over row: Start with very light weights and progress slowly.g.Seated machine row (if available at your gym).h.Chest press and shoulder press.

I received initial guidance from a physical therapist and since then have relied on content from reputable YouTube channels of certified physical therapists. Starting slow, starting with basic versions of exercises, and ensuring proper form are the key to progressing without getting injured.

We are fortunate to be in an era where EP is ripe with innovation. The use of electroanatomic mapping systems and intracardiac echocardiography has eased our reliance on fluoroscopy; however, certain procedures, such as device implantations and extractions, cannot be performed without the use of fluoroscopy. Minimum fluoroscopy and fluoroless workflows are becoming more common. However, fluoroscopy is a major tool in our armamentarium, and although its use carries some risk, we must leverage it well, particularly early on in our careers. Minimal fluoroscopy is for our long-term benefit but never to the patient’s benefit—so remember that. The best way to practice is to empower ourselves with the knowledge to use it well and not hesitate to use it.
