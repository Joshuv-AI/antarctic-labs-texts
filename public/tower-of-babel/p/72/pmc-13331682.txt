Rational design and synthesis of dicyanovinylene–umbelliferone as an effective water indicator for organic solvents
Authors: Seda Cinar, Ebru Aktan, Zeynel Seferoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An umbelliferone structure has been synthesized as an efficient water probe based on the ICT effect through a D–π–A system. Rationally designed probe 5 has a hydroxyl group as an electron donor (D) and a dicyanovinylene group as an electron acceptor (A) unit representing the smaller molecular structure compared to other water sensors larger in molecular size. Absorption and emission responses of 5 in various solvents and in the presence of water have been measured. The fluorescence intensity of the probe increased with the increase in water content in organic solvents. Absorption spectra revealed the bathochromically shifted deprotonation maximum which shows a linear increment in intensity with the addition of water. Rapid color change of solutions of 5 in ACN, EtOH, and DMSO from yellowish to pinky color indicated the water presence. In addition, ICT characteristics have been confirmed through HOMO/LUMO gap calculations by the DFT method. Water content detection results (LOD = 0.00095 (wt%), 0.0020 (wt%), and 0.020 (wt%) for ACN, EtOH, and DMSO, respectively) are competitive with and even better than recent literature values. Therefore, 5 could be used as a successful water probe for detecting low levels of water content in water miscible organic solvents.

Introduction

Water, a well-known contaminant in moisture-sensitive organic solvents, is of great interest for its determination by simple and easy-to-handle analytical methods. Water content in organic solvents is critical as it can act as a proton source that can interfere in the reaction medium, resulting in the deactivation of catalysts, reduced yields, or a change in solvent polarity. Quantification of water content in organic media can be performed by gas chromatography,1 near-infrared spectroscopy,2 nuclear magnetic resonance,3 or the most frequently used Karl Fischer titration,4,5 but these methods require experienced personnel and costly chemicals and instruments. Besides these difficulties, relatively low-cost and easily synthesized colorimetric or fluorometric chemosensors have been developed for the determination of trace amounts of water in organic solvents.6 Derivatives of chalcone,7 naphthoxazole,8 thioxanthone,9 azo dye,10 merocyanine dye,11 pyridinium dye,12 tetraphenylethylene,13 thiophene,14 naphthalimide,15 or styryl pyrene16 have been developed and used as probes for water detection. These structures often reflect solvatochromic properties yet have high limits of water detection (LOD). Therefore, it is very important and challenging to design and construct simple fluorescent probes for the selective detection of water in organic solvents.

In addition to these structures, coumarins have special emphasis for their tunable absorption and fluorescence properties through easy modifications and water probes based on coumarin structures have also been developed.17–20 Coumarins, a well-known class of biologically active molecules, have been frequently studied for their sensing ability of a specific analyte via the rational design of substituents on the skeleton. In the presence of electron-donating groups (EDG) and electron-withdrawing groups (EWG) at the same time on the core, the molecule displays intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) and fluorescence intensity increases. As 3- and 7-positions of coumarins are the para-positions, elongation of π-conjugation with EWGs through 3-positions creates push–pull effect with the improvement of ICT process against specific analytes.21,22 Introduction of strong electron-withdrawing groups into the coumarin skeleton can notably provide large Stokes shifts. These unique properties of coumarins highlight 7-hydroxycoumarins (umbelliferones) as the hydroxy group at the 7-position enables nucleophilic substitution reactions, strong electron donation, and deprotonation in basic media. Substitution at the 7-position allows the tracking of biological analytes in living cells via fluorescence turn-on/off mechanisms,23–29 and protonation/deprotonation states provide their use as pH probes.30–33 There are many manuscripts explaining the effective usage areas and potentials of coumarin derivatives in anion/cation recognition, and these studies are also explained by DFT methods.34 Yet, very few studies have been reported on umbelliferones such as water sensor,19 and the literature indicates the need for small, readily available chemosensors for the detection of specific analytes, such as water, in any environment.

In particular, cyano and formyl groups are well-known EWGs and exert significant electronic effects on para-positions of aromatics35 which are also used as π-extenders in synthesizing more conjugated systems.20,36,37 Subsequent reactions of cyano and formyl groups allow creation of even stronger EWGs such as dicyanovinylene functionality35 which have been preferred previously in developing push–pull systems.13,14 The present study describes the rational design and synthesis of a novel umbelliferone 5 which readily includes electron donating hydroxy group at 7-position as well as investigation of its sensing ability towards trace amounts of water in organic solvents. The molecular design strategy was based on the introduction of dicyanovinylene at 3-position as strong electron-withdrawing group into the umbelliferone skeleton in order to enhance ICT process as well as the acidity of the hydroxyl group, as this characteristic undoubtedly fits 5 into typical D–π–A structures. Enhanced acidity of phenolic hydrogens makes coumarins more susceptible to deprotonate in basic medium, in water or in the presence of a nucleophile, as this approximation has clearly been assigned in the Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) map of 5 given below, where the acidic hydrogen can be deprotonated by water molecules, which points out the use of 5 as a water indicator. Additionally, aforementioned cyano and formyl groups bearing umbelliferones as the precursors of probe 5 and unsubstituted umbelliferone have been synthesized and screened for their water sensing ability for the purpose of comparison with the ability of newly developed probe 5 by means of their electron-withdrawing characteristics.

Experimental section

Materials and instruments

Chemicals and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Carlo Erba and were used without further purification. Reactions were monitored by TLC using precoated silica plates (Kieselgel 60, F254, E.Merck), visualized with UV light (365 nm and 254 nm). NMR spectra were measured on Bruker Avance (1H NMR: 300 MHz, 13C NMR: 75 MHz), Bruker Avance (1H NMR: 400 MHz, 13C NMR: 100 MHz), and Bruker Avance NEO 500 (1H NMR: 500 MHz, 13C NMR: 126 MHz). Chemical shifts (δ) were given as ppm and coupling constants (J) as Hz. In the 1H NMR data, the multiplicities of the signals were presented as follows: s = singlet, d = doublet, dd = doublet of doublets, bs = broad singlet. Mass spectra were recorded on an Agilent 1200/6210 High Resolution Mass Time-of-Flight (TOF) LC/MS spectrometer. Photophysical characterizations were performed at room temperature. All solvents for spectral analysis were purchased as spectrophotometric grade and dried over molecular sieve 4 Å. Typical drying has been done three times using the fixed volume of solvent and the amount of sieve. All solvents were stored over 4 Å molecular sieves before use in this study. Absorption and emission spectra were recorded on a Horiba Duetta spectrophotometer.

Synthesis of compounds

Preparation of 3-cyanoumbelliferone (1)

3-Cyanoumbelliferone has been prepared according to the given literature.38 2,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde (1.38 g, 10 mmol), malononitrile (0.825 g, 12.5 mmol) and 0.05 M aq NaHCO3 (50 mL) were placed in a flask and stirred vigorously at r.t. After 5 h, 2 mL conc. HCl was added and the resulting mixture was heated at 100 °C for 2–2.5 h. After cooling, resulting precipitates were filtered and crude solids were recrystallized with AcOH : MeOH (9 : 1, v/v). Yield 80% (1.50 g), mp: 268–270 °C. 1H NMR (500 MHz, DMSO-d6): δ = 6.69 (s, 1H), 6.80 (d, J = 8.6 Hz, 1H), 7.55 (d, J = 6.2, 1H), 8.68 (s, 1H), 11.25 (bs, 1H); HRMS (APCI); m/z calcd for C10H4NO3 [M–H]−: 186.0191, found 186.0152.

Preparation of 3-cyano-7-acetylcoumarin (2)

3-Cyanoumbelliferone (3.0 g, 16.0 mmol) was dissolved in pyridine (25 mL), and acetic anhydride (25 mL) was added to this solution and mixed for 30 min at room temperature. Then, the mixture was poured onto ice, and the solids formed immediately. Solids were filtered, washed with water, and recrystallized from ethanol. Yield: 63% (2.30 g), mp: 174 – 175 °C. 1H NMR (500 MHz, acetone-d6): δ = 2.34 (s, 3H), 7.28 (dd, J = 8.5, 2.1 Hz, 1H), 7.31 (d, J = 2.0 Hz, 1H), 7.91 (d, J = 8.5 Hz, 1H), 8.78 (s, 1H) ppm. Preparation of 3-formyl-7-acetylcoumarin (3)

3-Cyano-7-acetylcoumarin (2.30 g, 9.91 mmol) was dissolved in formic acid (40 mL) at 90 °C. To the clear solution, Raney Ni (3 g) (washed with ethanol) in formic acid (5 mL) was added in one portion and continued refluxing. After 2 hours, the mixture was left to cool to room temperature. First, solution was filtered over Celite and washed with hot EtOAc (100 mL). The crude product mixture was evaporated to dryness. Solids were washed with DCM (20 mL) and filtered over a silica gel column. Combined filtrates were evaporated, and white solids were purified by washing with diethyl ether. Yield: 43% (1.0 g), mp: 168 –170 °C. 1H NMR (300 MHz, DMSO-d6): δ = 2.32 (s, 3H), 7.26 (dd, J = 8.5, 2.1 Hz, 1H), 7.38 (s, 1H), 8.04 (d, J = 8.5 Hz, 1H), 8.69 (s, 1H), 10.02 (s, 1H) ppm. Preparation of umbelliferone (6)

Umbelliferone has been prepared according to the given literature39 with a slight modification. pH effect

pH as a principal physiological parameter in chemical and biological processes is an important variable that determines the stability of a molecule bearing exchangeable protons in acidic and basic media. Spectroscopic responses of a molecule over a wide pH range are used to determine its pKa value for the development of pH-sensitive fluorescent materials. After synthesis of 5, the pH dependence of the phenolic proton at the 7-position was examined in Britton–Robinson buffer between pH 4.0 and 10.0 to better understand the deprotonation state. The possible deprotonation form of 5 and its resonance contributors are presented in Scheme 2. The absorption and emission spectra recorded in different pH values show that 5 has an absorption maximum at 416 nm in acidic pH 4.0–5.5, and a red-shifted maximum at 496 nm which indicates the deprotonated form between pH 6.0–10.0 (Fig. 1A). At higher pHs, maximum number of deprotonated 5 is reached and both the absorption and emission intensities reached a plateau (Fig. 1 insets). Given Fig. 1B, compound 5 exhibits an emission maximum at 535 nm at all pH values. Similar to the absorption spectrum, the emission band intensity increased up to pH 10.0, indicating the intramolecular charge transfer (ICT).

In order to signify the proposed synthetic state-of-the-art, pKa values of 5 and its precursors 3-cyanoumbelliferone (1), 3-formylumbelliferone (4), and umbelliferone (6) have been calculated from Henderson–Hasselbalch equation and summarized in Table 1 (Fig. S15–S20). As the strength of the electron-accepting moiety at 3-position of the coumarin ring increases, pKa of the –OH group at 7-position decreases, indicating the enhanced acidity and the success of the synthetic state-of-the-art. In the literature, the pKa value of umbelliferone has been given as 7.60,39 , 7.78 (ref. 40) and 8.01 (ref. 41) by means of its absorption intensity, fluorescence intensity, and capillary electrophoresis data, respectively. Unfortunately, pKa values for 3-cyanoumbelliferone (1) and 3-formylumbelliferone (4) have not been found in the literature. In this study, both the absorption and emission data of aforementioned umbelliferones were used to calculate the pKa values. As can be seen in Table 1, pKa of umbelliferone (6) has been calculated as 7.80 from absorption and 8.06 from emission maximum intensity changes, which are quite similar to the values given in the literature. Umbelliferones 4 and 1 have electron-withdrawing formyl- and cyano-groups on 3-position respectively, apart from bare umbelliferone 6. Calculated pKa values for 4 and 1 are around 6, indicating the –CHO or –CN groups have reflected their electron-withdrawing effect on pKa as the –OH group donating electrons. Introducing the more electron-withdrawing group being dicyanovinylene clearly lowered the pKa value below 6. It has been calculated as 5.89 from absorption and 5.68 from emission data (Fig. S21–S22).

Photophysical properties of 5

As umbelliferones show unique photophysical changes in different solvents,42 the UV-vis spectral behavior of compound 5 was examined in common polar and apolar solvents (Fig. 2A). In apolar solvents, the absorption maximum remained relatively constant at around 425 nm and no net solvatochromic effect was observed.

Absorption and normalized absorption spectra (A and C) and emission and normalized emission spectra (B and D) of 5 in various solvents (1 × 10−5 M) (inset: photographs of 5 in various solvents under ambient (left) and UV-light (λex: 365 nm) (right)).

In contrast to apolar solvents (ET(30)43 for DCM (40.7), THF (37.4), toluene (33.9), and cyclohexane (30.9)), 5 exhibited ICT-based absorption peaks in different intensities of the deprotonated form around 510 – 520 nm in polar solvents (ET(30) for MeOH (55.4), EtOH (51.9), i-PrOH (48.8), ACN (45.6), DMSO (45.1), and DMF (43.2)). Emission spectra revealed a slight red shift on emission maxima from 540 nm to 552 nm as the solvent polarity increased (Fig. 2B). 5 has the most intense emission in alcohols, and the least intense emission in ACN and THF. What is more, DMF and DMSO have clearly red-shifted the emission maximum by ca. 10 nm but 5 did not fluoresce in DCM, toluene, and cyclohexane (Fig. 2B).

Detection of water content in organic solvents

From the above discussion, it is important to note that both the absorption behavior of 5 in polar solvents and the pH-dependent deprotonation cause the red-shifted absorption maximum, as these highlight the sensitivity of 5 to the change of solvent polarity that utilizes the molecule as a sensor for the detection of trace amounts of water in water-miscible organic solvents. Thus, we further investigated the absorption and emission behavior of 5 in water-miscible solvents (DMSO, ACN, and EtOH). 5 has a primary absorption band at 416 nm and a band at 518 nm due to its deprotonated form in spectroscopic grade acetonitrile kept in molecular sieve 4 Å (Fig. 3A). Addition of a minimum amount of water led to a rapid decrease of the 416 nm band and resulted in the ICT enhancement and significant intensity elevation at the 518 nm band. As the water concentration increased, further gradual decrease in the 416 nm band intensity and increase in the longer wavelength absorption intensity has occurred. The limit of detection (LOD) values has been calculated according to the LOD = 3σ/m, where σ is the standard deviation of 5 in solvents and m is the slope of the absorption calibration curve for water addition. LOD of 5 has been calculated based on the intensity increase at 518 nm and found 0.00095 wt% in ACN (Fig. 3B), as this value is an outstanding one among reported water sensors based on coumarin or other fluorophores studied in ACN solvent (Table S1).44 In DMSO, 5 has its primary absorption band at 420 nm and an ICT-based band at 528 nm. The addition of water has slightly decreased the intensity of 420 nm band and increased the intensity of 528 nm band. The limit of detection (LOD) has been calculated based on the intensity increase at 528 nm and found to be 0.020 wt% in DMSO (Fig. S23). Similarly, the water-sensing ability of 5 in EtOH has been studied, and the LOD was calculated as 0.0020 wt% (Fig. S24).

(A) Effect of water addition on 5 (1 × 10−5 M) absorption in ACN (B) limit of water detection in ACN (inset: photographs of 5 with increasing amount of water under ambient (left) and UV-light (λex: 365 nm) (right)).

In order to emphasize the advantages of novel compound 5 as a water sensor, we also studied the water sensing responses of known 3-cyanoumbelliferone 1, 3-formylumbelliferone 4, and umbelliferone 6, where the substituent effect will be realized. Spectral studies have been conducted in EtOH, DMSO, and ACN for all the compounds. As summarized in Table 2, in ethanol, umbelliferones 5, 4 and 1 have shown good linearity with the water percent increment and have similar LOD values (Fig. S24, S25 and S27). In DMSO, 5, 4, and 1 have competitive LOD values, but bigger than in EtOH (Fig. S23, S26 and S28). Interestingly in ACN, umbelliferones 4 and 6 did not give a linear spectroscopic response to water addition, but 1 has given a response to water increment with LOD value of 0.0011 (Fig. S29 and S31). Yet, newly synthesized 5 has shown even better linear change in absorption intensity with increasing water content and LOD has been calculated as 0.00095 wt%. In addition, umbelliferone 6 has not shown applicability for water sensing in any of the studied solvents as it has no clear absorption maxima increment or decrement (Fig. S30). The calculated LOD values for 5 are as low when compared to the literature values (Table S1) and these results indicate that probe 5 can successfully be utilized to determine the water content in organic solvents.

Fluorimetric water titrations of 1, 4, 5, and 6 have revealed fluorescence increase in emission maxima intensities (Fig. S32 and S33). Further, to verify and realize the practical application possibility of sensor 5, the test paper strips experiment has been performed. First, in a solution of 5 in EtOH dried over molecular sieve 4 Å (10 µM, 1 mL), Whatman #1 filter paper was immersed and kept for 5 minutes and air dried. Daylight and UV-light photographs of the filter papers are given in Fig. 4A and C, respectively.

Illustration of color changes in Whatman #1 filter paper functionalized with sensor 5 in EtOH (A and C) and in EtOH : H2O (1000 : 5, v/v) (B and D) under day light and UV-light (365 nm), respectively.

Then, EtOH : H2O solution (1000 : 5, v/v, 1 mL) has been prepared and a filter paper just functionalized with 5 has been immersed in this solution. After a very short time, transformation of original yellowish color to pinky color has been observed, yet 5 minutes of incubation time has been performed and then the paper has air dried. Photographs of test papers in the presence of water in EtOH (Fig. 4B and D) indicates the naked eye detection of water is notably possible. Rapid color change with fluorescence enhancement is in good agreement with spectroscopic titration results, as well, binary solution of 0.5% H2O content which is considerably important reflects the applicability of sensor 5 as a highly sensitive water probe. In addition, water content visualization in DMSO and ACN has similar color transformations. In order to support the practical use of sensor 5, stability under storage conditions have been investigated. At the laboratory conditions, sensor 5 has been stored in a transparent Eppendorf tube at ambient temperature. During one-year of multiple spectroscopic analyses different stock solutions of same solid has been prepared and used. Same solid's different solutions show nearly the same absorption band profiles at ∼420 and 520 nm, reflecting the original and deprotonation states, respectively (Fig. S38). These observations verify the stability of 5 for long term sensor studies. Stability of sensor 5 has also been demonstrated by the reversibility test by subsequent addition of TFA to the DMSO solution of 5 containing 9.090 wt% water (Fig. S39). Initially, bare 5 shows absorption bands of protonation–deprotonation states in DMSO. Addition of water increases the deprotonation band intensity and TFA addition on this mixture provides enough protons for 5 to gain the lost hydrogens which in turn generates original absorption band of 5 at 420 nm.

Theoretical calculations

The ground state geometries of 1, 4, and 5 were obtained in the gas phase with the DFT/B3LYP method and the 6-31G+(d,p) basis set. Front and side view of the optimized geometries of the compounds are shown in Fig. 5. The geometry optimization results provide information about the structure of the molecules. Some selected structural parameters of the compounds are given in Table S2. The C1–O16 bond length of 1 and 4 is 1.358 Å, of 5 is 1.356 Å. The C11–O13 bond lengths of 1, 4, and 5 were found to be 1.363 Å, 1.361 Å and 1.362 Å, respectively. The C21–N22 bond length of 5 is 1.164 Å. The angle C1–O16–H17 was determined to be 110.7° for 1 and 4, and 110.8° for 5. The dihedral angles indicate that the molecules have a planar structure. For example, for 5, the dihedral angles of C11–C15–C18–C20 and C15–C18–C20–C21 are 180.0° (Table S2).

Theoretical maximum absorption wavelengths of the compounds were calculated in solvents using the TD-DFT/IEFPCM method (Table 3). As an example, the maximum absorption wavelength of 5 was determined experimentally as 428 nm in ethanol and as 415 nm by the TD-DFT method (oscillator strength f = 0.8315). Density Functional Theory (DFT) allows us to study the global reactivity descriptors of molecules, and thus theoretically their chemical reactivities. It was observed that the energy band gap ΔE values decrease in the order of 1 (4.062 eV) > 4 (3.927 eV) > 5 (3.312 eV) (Fig. 7). Compound 5 possesses the lowest band gap, identifying it as the molecule with the lowest chemical hardness. Chemical hardness serves as an indicator of the resistance of nuclei and electrons toward changes in electron distribution. Hardness values decrease in the order 1 (2.031 eV) > 4 (1.964 eV) > 5 (1.656 eV); this indicates that the chemical reactivity of the compounds increases in the same order.

The global electrophilic index (ω) quantitatively expresses the capacity of a system to accept electrons from the outside. High ω values indicate that the structure is electron-poor and exhibits a strong electrophilic character, low values prove that the structure behaves as an effective nucleophile with a tendency to donate electrons.45 The ω values increase in the order of 4 (12.199) < 1 (12.390) < 5 (14.162); consequently, the electrophilic character follows the same increasing trend (Table S3).

Conclusions

To conclude, we have demonstrated the synthesis, spectroscopic evaluation and water sensing ability of 7-dicyanovinyleneumbelliferon 5. Notably, 5 has pronounced absorption and emission intensity changes in organic solvents upon water addition with LODs of 0.00095 (wt%) in ACN, 0.0020 (wt%) in EtOH, and 0.020 (wt%) in DMSO. Very sensitive water detection ability of 5 has been verified by comparative spectroscopic studies done with 3-cyanoumbelliferone 1, 3-formylumbelliferone 4, and umbelliferone 6 in accordance with the rational molecular design. In these analyses, 5 showed the most sensitive water response in ACN when compared with 1, 4, and 6. We also showed the enhanced electron withdrawing ability of dicyanovinylene group through deprotonated phenolic oxygen in a coumarin skeleton with the calculated pKa values. Susceptibility of hydroxyl group of 5 to nucleophilic attacks and the EWG effect of dicyanovinylene group have also been stated with the DFT/TD-DFT calculation. Although there are no distinctive color changes with solvents, probe 5 has a significant color change even in the presence of a minimum amount of water from pale yellow in its ground form to pink in its deprotonated form. This rapid color change could be used in the visualization of the water content of commercially purchased organic solvents with the test paper strip application.
