Total cholesterol and the associated factors among adults aged 18–69 years in Uganda: analysis of the 2023 national population-based non-communicable disease survey data
Authors: Saidi Appeli, Saadick Mugerwa Ssentongo, Jonathan Izudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a leading cause of mortality in people with non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Elevated total blood cholesterol is a modifiable risk factor for several CVDs. In Uganda, there is limited information about factors associated with total cholesterol at the national level. This study examined the factors associated with total cholesterol among adults aged 18–69 years in Uganda using a large, nationally representative dataset.MethodsThe study analyzed the 2023 Nationwide Non-communicable Disease (NCDs) risk factor survey data that involved Ugandans aged 18–69 years. The national survey used a cross-sectional design and adhered to the World Health Organization’s STEP-wise approach to surveillance (STEPS). The dependent variable was total cholesterol, measured as a continuous variable. To determine the factors associated with total cholesterol, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed, and Beta coefficients (β) with respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsWe analyzed data for 3,694 participants, with a median age of 36 years (interquartile range: 27- 49). We found that every 1-year age increase (range, 18–69 years), female sex, completion of secondary or tertiary/university levels of education, being married or in a cohabiting relationship, overweight or obesity, being diabetic, and being hypertensive were positively associated with total cholesterol. Conversely, being underweight and residing in rural, northern, or western Uganda showed a negative association with total cholesterol.ConclusionThe study found that total cholesterol is associated with several socio-demographic and metabolic factors that are either modifiable or non-modifiable. Efforts to mitigate the burden of cardiovascular diseases among individuals aged 18–69 years in Uganda should focus on lowering total cholesterol and managing the modifiable underlying risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28475-x.

Introduction

Elevated total cholesterol or hypercholesterolemia is an abnormality of lipids in the bloodstream [1], and a modifiable risk factor for several cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) such as coronary heart and cerebrovascular diseases, among others [2–4]. With an estimated 17 million deaths annually, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death globally [5]. Most of the non-communicable disease (NCD)-related mortality is caused by cardiovascular diseases [6]. Several factors contribute to total cholesterol, including both genetic and secondary factors such as unhealthy/sedentary lifestyles and physical inactivity [1, 7].

Existing data show that the overall prevalence of hypercholesterolemia in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) was 25.5% [8], with national-level data showing prevalence rates of 38% in Nigeria [9] and 6.7% in Uganda, varying between men (4.4%) and women (8.9%) [10]. In Uganda, the prevalence of most NCD risk factors has increased between 2014 and 2023, from 1.5% to 3.3% for high blood glucose; from 19.3% to 24.1% for overweight and obesity; from 28.5% to 31.1% for current alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity from 26.6% to 31.9% [11]. The increasing levels of NCDs and their risk factors are exacerbated by poor health-seeking behavior among Ugandans, with data showing that the proportion of adults who reported never having their blood cholesterol levels examined stagnated around 98.8% in 2014 [10] and 97.6% in 2023 [11]. This poor health-seeking behavior, coupled with the increasing burden of NCDs, including hypercholesterolemia, is more likely to reverse all past gains. Conversely, maintaining low cholesterol levels (< 50 mg/dl) is associated with a lowered risk of major cardiovascular events [12].

Few studies have examined cholesterol levels at the national level in Uganda despite the increasing magnitude of NCDs. In the past, two studies conducted in Southwestern and Central Uganda [13–15] have examined total cholesterol, but both studies analyzed relatively smaller sample sizes, with one of them dating nearly 10 years ago. Existing evidence, therefore, cannot provide an accurate picture of risk factors for total cholesterol at the national level as data are at the regional and district levels. Moreover, existing NCD prevention and control efforts in Uganda, coupled with implementation challenges [16], have largely focused on managing blood pressure, blood glucose, and lifestyle-related behaviors such as physical inactivity and harmful use of tobacco and alcohol [17]. Cholesterol management has received comparatively less attention in both programmatic and policy frameworks. Moreover, understanding the factors associated with total cholesterol at the national level provides valuable information to inform NCD control and prevention efforts at the national level and similar settings in SSA. To fill this gap, this study examines the factors associated with total cholesterol among Ugandan adults aged 18–69 years using a large and nationally representative sample, the 2023 Uganda national Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) risk factor STEPS survey.

Materials and methods

Data source and ethical considerations

This study leveraged secondary data (Additional file 1) from the 2023 Uganda national Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) risk factor STEPS survey, which is the most recent NCD survey in Uganda. The survey was conducted to determine the magnitude and the associated risk factors of NCDs at the national level. The survey design and methodology have been extensively described in the past [11, 18]. However, in brief, the survey employed the WHO STEPwise approach, which is a sequential process of data collection using a three-step process. Step 1 involved the collection of demographic and behavioral information. Step 2 focused on physical measurements, including height, weight, girth (waist, hip), blood pressure, and pulse rate. Step 3 involved biochemical measurements using point-of-care devices to measure blood glucose, blood lipids, including total cholesterol. Besides, the survey utilized a three-stage stratified sample selected from an updated master sampling frame that was constructed from the 2014 Uganda National Population and Housing Census. The first stage was a random sample of 310 enumeration areas from 8 strata using population proportion to size sampling. In the second stage, 14 households within each enumeration area were randomly selected, and in the third stage, one eligible household member was randomly selected at the household level. Eligible individuals were Ugandan adults aged 18–69 years, had been a member of the household for at least six months preceding the survey, and gave written informed consent. Excluded household members included those: (1) unable to stand without support (individuals with limb amputation without a prosthesis); (2) under the influence of alcohol or other drug(s) abuse; (3) very close to dying or in a state of failing health; and, (4) not fully conscious, dull, or less responsive than normal. The survey sample size was 4,322, estimated using the WHO STEPS sample size calculator, with an assumed 50% prevalence of risk factors, a 5% level of significance (α=0.05), a margin of error of 0.05 (δ=0.05), an expected response rate of 80%, and a 1.5 design effect to account for clustering.

However, of the 4,322 anticipated participants, 3,694 provided informed consent and gave data, resulting in an 85.1% response rate. Data were collected between February and March 2023 by trained and supervised research assistants. Meetings were held regularly to examine the data that were collected and promptly address any issues that arose. The data were entered in real time using hand-held Android tablets, along with Global Positioning System (GPS) data.

Variables and measurements

The outcome variable was total blood cholesterol measured in millimoles per litre (mmol/L) as a continuous variable during the third step of the STEPS survey using point-of-care and verified tools from the WHO. A blood sample from a finger prick was taken to measure total cholesterol using the CardioChek PA meter (Miller Medical Supplies). Total cholesterol was retained and modelled as a continuous outcome variable to preserve the full gradient of variation in the data and to maximize statistical efficiency. This approach is consistent with population-level NCD research [19, 20]. The NCD risk factors measured included age measured on a continuous scale, sex (male or female), residence (urban or rural), and region (Central, Western, Eastern, and Northern). Education level was recoded into four categories: no formal education, primary education, secondary education, and tertiary or university education (including vocational and postgraduate training). Marital status was categorized into three groups: never married, married or cohabiting, and separated, divorced, or widowed, and employment status as employed or unemployed.

Additional risk factors measured included tobacco smoking (yes or no), alcohol consumption measured on a binary scale (yes or no), eating at least 5 daily servings of fruits per day (yes or no), and eating at least 5 daily servings of vegetables per day (yes or no). Participants who reported having consumed an average of more than five servings of fruits or vegetables per day over the past seven days preceding the survey were considered to have adequate consumption of fruits and vegetable, otherwise inadequate consumption. Physical activity was measured on a binary scale (yes or no) based on whether or not an individual’s work involved moderate-intensity activity. Anthropometric and clinical measurements were based on the WHO guidelines, with the body mass index (BMI) calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters.

BMI was categorized into underweight if < 18.5 kg/m2, normal weight if it was between 18.5 kg/m2 and 24.9 kg/m2, overweight if between 25.0 kg/m2 and 29.9 kg/m2, and obese if ≥ 30 kg/m2. Fasting blood glucose was measured as a continuous variable and then categorized as ≤ 7.0 mmol per liter (mmol/L) and > 7.0 mmol per liter (mmol/L) to depict normal fasting blood glucose and diabetic status, respectively. Hypertension (high blood pressure) was measured on a binary scale by dichotomizing the average of two systolic and diastolic blood pressures. Individuals with systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg were considered hypertensive, otherwise normotensive (normal blood pressure).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed in Stata/SE version 17. Missing observations were imputed via multiple imputations using chained equations (MICEs), with predictive mean matching for continuous variables, logistic regression for binary variables, multinomial logistic regression for nominal categorical variables, and ordinal logistic regression for ordered categorical variables, assuming the observations were missing at random. We descriptively summarized categorical data using frequencies and percentages, and numerical data using median and interquartile ranges when skewed. We fitted a simple linear regression model as a function of the predictors to test for the association between total cholesterol and each independent variable. Independent variables with a probability value (p-value) ≤ 0.05 were considered in a multiple linear regression model, along with variables known in the literature as being associated with total cholesterol levels. To minimize multicollinearity, independent variables with a variance inflation factor (VIF) ≥ 5 were excluded from the final multiple linear regression model. Goodness of model fit was assessed using the F-statistic, and a statistically significant F-value suggested a good model fit to the data. We reported beta coefficients (β) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), both unadjusted and adjusted. Since the STEPS NCD survey utilized a complex sample design, our analysis accounted for the complex survey design using the primary sampling unit, strata, and sampling weight to ensure unbiased parameter estimates and adjusted for variance estimates using Taylor linearization. Therefore, data aspects such as heteroscedasticity or normality of residuals were well-tolerated.

Results

Discussion

This study examined the predictors of total cholesterol levels among adults in Uganda using a nationally representative sample. We found that each 1-year increase in age, female sex, completion of secondary or tertiary/university education, being married or cohabiting, overweight or obesity, diabetes, and hypertension were positively associated with total cholesterol, whereas being underweight and residing in rural, Northern, or Western Uganda were negatively associated with total cholesterol. These findings suggest age and sex as the non-modifiable determinants of total cholesterol, with modifiable factors as BMI status, diabetes, and hypertension (behavioral), and educational level, marital status, residence, and region as additional modifiable structural or contextual factors.

The finding that every additional 1-year increase in age is positively associated with total cholesterol is consistent with the finding of a previous study in China [3]. This finding could be explained by the body’s inability to remove cholesterol from the bloodstream with increasing age [21]. Our finding that being a female is positively associated with higher total cholesterol level compared to being a male is inconsistent with a study conducted in the United States of America (USA) that showed females have lower variation in cholesterol than males [22]. However, Anish and colleagues found no significant gender difference in Cholesterol in young adults in India in a study that examined gender difference in blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol in young adults with comparable routine physical exertion [23]. Our findings may also be explained by a combination of physiological and social factors. First, women tend to have a higher prevalence of excess abdominal fat compared to men, which is linked to unfavorable cholesterol profiles [13]. Second, women's caregiving and household responsibilities may leave them with little or no time for physical activity hence accumulating total cholesterol. In contrast, one study reported lower cholesterol levels in females than males [24].

Compared to individuals who live in urban areas, those in rural areas have lower levels of total cholesterol, consistent with previous studies conducted in Myanmar and systematic review and meta-analysis findings [25, 26]. This could be due to the consumption of less refined and processed foods in rural than urban areas, including rural residents consuming a higher fibre diet than urban residents [27].

This study found that individuals with secondary or higher levels of education have higher total cholesterol compared with those without formal education whereas, which is inconsistent with a cross-sectional study using data from the National Survey of Non-Communicable Disease STEPs 2018 (NCD STEPS) in Afghanistan, where total cholesterol had no significant association with level of education [28]. This could be explained by a relatively higher socioeconomic status associated with higher levels of education. People with higher levels of education may have greater access to attractive, unhealthy diets such as a fast foods and experienced marked physical inactivity. These factors contribute to decreased energy expenditure resulting in excessive fat accumulation in adipose tissue.

Married/cohabiting individuals showed a positive association with total cholesterol, consistent with the findings of a community-based survey in western Uganda [13]. Similarly, among the adult Nigerian subpopulation, married individuals have been found to have higher mean serum total cholesterol than their non-married counterparts [29]. The reasons for this finding are unclear and additional studies would be needed.

Individuals from the northern or western part of the country showed lower total cholesterol compared to those from the central region, whereas those in the eastern region showed no association with total cholesterol. A community-based survey in south-western Uganda showed a lower prevalence of hypercholesterolemia [13], consistent with our findings. Regional disparities in total cholesterol levels in Uganda may account for the variations in total cholesterol levels in this study. However, qualitative studies that explore dietary diversity may be needed to explain the findings further.

We found that obese and overweight individuals have higher total cholesterol, whereas underweight individuals have lower total cholesterol. These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported a significant association between body mass index and total cholesterol [30, 31]. In overweight and obese individuals, excess fat releases more fatty acids into the bloodstream, causing the liver to produce more cholesterol. Additionally, in obese/overweight individuals, insulin resistance and inflammation may worsen cholesterol levels [32]. Lifestyle factors such as unhealthy diets, limited physical activity, and high alcohol consumption can add to this effect. In contrast, underweight individuals have less body fat and therefore produce less fat and cholesterol.

Our study found that people with diabetes and those with hypertension have elevated total cholesterol. A study in Ghana showed a higher prevalence of hypercholesterolemia among persons with type 2 diabetes [33], similar to our findings.

Also, a study conducted in Poland to assess the control of hypertension and hypercholesterolemia reported a high prevalence of hypercholesterolemia in participants with hypertension [34], which is consistent with our findings. Existing evidence indicates that hypertension and diabetes are often comorbid and jointly elevate the risk of serious cardiovascular events [35]. The finding may be explained by the mechanical damage to artery walls by hypertension, which encourages lipid infiltration and retention hence exacerbating dyslipidemia, including total cholesterol [36]. Also, people with diabetes mellitus largely have insulin resistance, leading to fat accumulation and higher total cholesterol.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has strengths and some limitations. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use a nationally representative sample of Ugandan adults (18–69 years) to examine total cholesterol and the associated factors, making the findings generalizable to Uganda. Limitations include the analysis of cross-sectional data that cannot demonstrate causation. There are also unmeasured confounders and residual confounding that cannot be underestimated. Certain findings would have been best explained by qualitative data, necessitating further research. Additionally, the physical activity variable was operationalized narrowly as a binary indicator of whether a participant's work involved moderate-intensity activity, as captured by the STEPS survey instrument. This measure likely misclassifies the operationalization of physical activity. As such, the resulting exposure misclassification may have attenuated the estimated association between physical activity and total cholesterol and the null finding for physical activity should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Lastly, there is a possibility of information bias that might have been introduced during data collection, including social desirability bias.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study showed that higher total cholesterol is associated with several socio-demographic and metabolic factors, both modifiable and non-modifiable. In particular, older ages, female sex, secondary or higher levels of education, being married or in a cohabiting relationship, overweight or obesity, and being diabetic or hypertensive are associated with higher total cholesterol levels. Conversely, individuals in rural areas and in northern or western Uganda, as well as those underweight, have lower total cholesterol levels. Therefore, efforts to mitigate the burden of CVDs in Uganda should focus on lowering cholesterol and managing its underlying risk factors. Lastly, this study measured only total cholesterol and did not assess the individual lipids, namely low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides (TAGs). These lipids (LDL, HDL, and TAGs) potentially have opposing relationships regarding cardiovascular disease risk. Therefore, future studies should examine the factors associated with the specific lipid components separately to provide a better understanding.
