Proteomic insights into nano-chitosan-induced salinity tolerance in solid matrix-primed mung bean seedlings
Authors: Sujoy Kumar Sen, Dipayan Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMung bean (Vigna radiata (L.) R. Wilczek) is a nutritionally important short-duration legume widely cultivated across the globe. Its productivity is becoming limited in many biogeographic zones due to soil salinity. In the present study, a novel solid matrix priming (SMP) strategy using chitosan and nano-chitosan was evaluated to enhance salinity tolerance in mung beans. SMP-mediated nano-chitosan priming significantly improved germination kinetics, seedling vigour, and growth performance under moderate and high salinity by enabling controlled hydration and minimising imbibitional injury compared to hydro-priming. Biochemical profiling using Native PAGE revealed enhanced activities of α-amylase and other antioxidant isozymes, while SDS-PAGE coupled with Orbitrap high-resolution LC–MS identified differentially expressed proteins uniquely associated with nano-chitosan treatment. Targeted HR-LCMS analysis demonstrated salinity-responsive modulation of major secondary metabolites, including phenols and isoflavones, known for their antioxidant and stress-protective roles. Integrative STRING and Cytoscape analyses further indicated coordinated interactions between these metabolites and salt stress-responsive genes, reflecting comprehensive metabolic reprogramming for adaptation under salinity stress and thereby maintaining productivity. The present findings indicate nano-chitosan-based SMP as a cost-effective, biodegradable, and environmentally sustainable strategy for improving early-stage salt tolerance and metabolic resilience in mung bean.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55605-7.Keywords: Mung bean, Salinity stress, Solid-matrix priming, Nano-chitosan, PAGE, OHR-LCMSSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Leguminous pulses are globally important food crops because of their high-quality protein content and their ability to enhance nutritional adequacy in diets dominated by carbohydrates1. By complementing cereal-based staples, pulses fulfil protein deficiencies and also contribute to soil health, environmental sustainability, and long-term food security worldwide2. Pulses had a significant in global agricultural output and experienced a logarithmic increase in demand during recent global crises, including the COVID-19 pandemic3. With a protein content of ~ 20–25%, pulses provide nearly three times more protein than rice and about twice that of wheat, indicating their critical role in sustainable nutrition and food security strategies worldwide4. To signify the importance of pulses in nutritional security, food security, sustainable agriculture, climate resilience, and livelihood support, the United Nations designated 2016 as the International Year of Pulses (IYP)5. The United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) declared 10 February as World Pulses Day in 2018, first observed in 2019, to sustain momentum from the International Year of Pulses (2016) and promote pulses for nutrition, food security, sustainable agriculture, climate resilience, and the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals6. To emphasise the significance of the role of pulses in enhancing crop diversification, improving nutrition, strengthening soil health, and promoting resilient and sustainable food systems worldwide, the 2025 World Pulses Day was celebrated under the theme “Pulses: Bringing diversity to agrifood systems.”

Vigna radiata (L.) R. Wilczek, a member of the family Fabaceae sensu stricto, is an important non-endospermic leguminous pulse crop. It is known across the globe by different common names, including green gram, mung bean, moong, golden gram, and Oregon pea7. Mung bean has market potential for its nutritional, medicinal, economic, and agronomic values8. Its productivity often gets affected by susceptibility to a wide spectrum of biotic and abiotic stresses, a challenge that is further exacerbated by climate change9. Among the various abiotic stresses, viz., salinity, heat, cold, drought, and heavy metal toxicity, salinity stress mostly affects agricultural productivity10. Salinity stress, inducing ionic toxicity and osmotic imbalance, retards plant growth, thereby reducing productivity11. It was reported that due to natural salinisation and anthropogenic activities, arable lands are progressively becoming saline, a trend likely to have severe global consequences, potentially leading to the loss of ~ 50% of cultivable land by 205012.

Salinity tolerance involves coordinated mechanisms, including osmotic adjustment through vacuolar sequestration of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and cytoplasmic accumulation of compatible solutes, restriction of toxic ion transport to leaves, and tissue-level ion compartmentalisation to protect cellular metabolism. In mung bean, growth reductions are driven mainly by Cl⁻ toxicity rather than Na⁺, as Cl⁻ mostly accumulates in leaves, exceeding vacuolar buffering capacity and damaging sensitive organelles such as chloroplasts13. It was reported that under glasshouse conditions, salinity stress decreases mung bean growth and productivity, causing yield reductions from 41% to 91% when cultivated in 50 mM NaCl–treated pot soil12. Salinity impacts mung bean at all developmental stages, impacting seed germination, restricting vegetative growth, reproductive development and yield14. Salinity stress results in chlorosis and necrosis and suppresses plant growth and productivity by reducing root and shoot elongation, leaf number and area, flowering and pod formation, seeds per pod, 100-seed weight, and overall seed yield15,16.

Overcoming the adverse effect of salinity stress can be achieved by the sustainable approach of seed priming, a proven pre-sowing strategy, that can enhance stress tolerance and improve seedling establishment17. Several priming techniques are available, including solid matrix priming (SMP), hydro-priming, osmo-priming, halo-priming, thermo-priming, and bio-priming18. Among these, SMP remains comparatively underexplored despite being a promising alternative to conventional liquid priming methods19. In the present study, SMP was selected as a pre-sowing treatment to investigate its morphological and biochemical modulation in terms of protein profiling of mung bean seedlings exposed to varying salinity levels.

Advances in nanotechnology-based seed priming (nano-priming) have opened new avenues for crop stress management, as it has been reported to improve seed germination, seed growth, and yield20. Nano-chitosan matrix as a priming agent for mung bean has been reported in our previous study as a novel seed invigoration strategy that mitigated salinity-induced oxidative stress in mung bean by enhancing protein content, antioxidant activity, and phenolic compounds21. The present study is based on the hypothesis that the priming elicitors, viz., chitosan, nano-chitosan, and water (hydropriming), will alleviate the detrimental effects of salt stress in mung bean seedlings (Fig. 1). The effects will be investigated with respect to oxidative stress on-gel markers and differentially expressed proteins through OHR-LCMS analysis obtained from SDS electrophoresis to understand the cellular changes in mung bean cultivars for sustainable growth under salinity stress.

Materials and methods

Experimental set-up and germination parameters

Mung bean seeds (Cultivar SAMRAT) of consistent size were utilised to investigate the impact of hydro-priming, chitosan priming (chitosan, low molecular weight, CAS Number: 9012-76-4, MDL number: MFCD00161512, UNSPSC Code: 12352201) and nano-chitosan (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Fig. 1) priming under salt stress levels of 0, 4, and 8 dS/m. A conductivity meter was used to measure the electrical conductivity of the saline solution (d/Sm = deci siemens per meter). Mung bean seeds were treated to surface sterilisation in a 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for five minutes. Subsequently, they were thoroughly rinsed twice with double-distilled water. The dried seeds were subsequently primed with water (hydro-priming), chitosan, and nano-chitosan through solid matrix priming utilising celite as the matrix (1:1), kept in air-tight plastic zip packets at a 10% moisture level. Celite was used only as an inert solid matrix carrier to facilitate uniform seed hydration and delivery of priming agents during SMP. Under the experimental conditions used in this study, celite itself did not show any independent physiological or molecular contribution to salinity tolerance. All seeds underwent priming for 24 h at room temperature, followed by thorough washing with double-distilled water. To avoid overcrowding and facilitate root and shoot measurements, 25 seeds per 90 mm Petri plate (Borosil Make) were allocated to each treatment, with three replicates, permitting germination under control (0 dS/m), moderate (4 dS/m), and severe (8 dS/m) salinity stress conditions. Seeds were germinated inside a seed germinator (REMI Make) maintained at 25 ± 2 °C temperature, 85–95% relative humidity, a 12 h photoperiod, and moderate aeration conditions. The number of germinated seeds was recorded daily. Seven-day-old mungbean seedlings were used for morphophysiological analyses, and the root length (RL) and shoot length (SL) were measured in centimetres using a ruler and thread method.

The following formulae were employed to examine various germination parameters, seedling vigour index, and stress tolerance indices:

Total Germination Percentage (TG%) = [(Total number of germinated Seeds/Total Seeds) x 100]22.

Germination Index (GI) = [(7 x n1) + (6 x n2) + . + (1 x n7)]23,

where ‘n1, n2, … n10’ are the number of germinated seeds on the first, second, ……… the seventh day and ‘7, 6, … 1’ are the weights given to the number of germinated seeds on the first, second, ……. the seventh day, respectively.

Mean Germination Time (MGT) = ∑ (n × d) / N21,

where ‘n’ represents the number of seeds germinated each day, ‘d’ denotes the number of days since the investigation commenced, and ‘N’ signifies the total number of seeds germinated at the end of the experiment.

Coefficient of Germination Velocity(CGV) = [{(∑NiTi)/(∑Ni)} × 100]24,

where ‘Ni’ indicates the number of seeds germinated on the ith day and ‘Ti’ denotes the duration from the commencement of the experiment to the ith interval.

Mean Germination Rate (MGR) = [1 / MGT]25.

Germination Stress Tolerance Index (GSI) = [(P.I. of stressed seeds / P.I. control seeds) × 100], where P.I. stands for Promptness Index26.

Root Length Stress Tolerance Index (RLSI) = [(RL of stressed plant / RL of control plants) × 100], where RL stands for Root Length26.

Plant Height Stress Tolerance Index (PHSI) = [(Height of stressed plant / Height of control plants) × 100]26.

Shoot Height Stress Tolerance Index (SHSI) = [(SL of stressed plant / SL of control plant) × 100], where SL stand for Shoot Length26.

Vigour Index (VI) = [(RL + SL) × germination percentage of seed]27.

SDS gel electrophoresis of salinity-induced mung bean seedlings after SMP

SDS PAGE mediated separation of protein banding pattern was carried out using Bio Rad Mini PROTEAN Tetra Cell, following the standard protocol as prescribed by Berkelman et al.28.

Protein extraction from mung bean seedling for SDS PAGE electrophoresis

Mung bean proteins were extracted from salinity-induced seedlings using the cryogenic grinding method. Seedlings were first ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. The resulting powder was suspended in lysis buffer containing trichloroacetic acid (TCA), dithiothreitol (DTT), and acetone, and mixed thoroughly to ensure complete homogenization.

The homogenate was transferred to fresh Eppendorf tubes, supplemented with additional lysis buffer for complete cell disruption, and vortexed for uniform mixing. The samples were then incubated at − 20 °C for 40 min to facilitate protein precipitation. Following incubation, the tubes were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully discarded, and the resulting pellet was washed with 1 mL of wash buffer containing DTT and acetone, followed by a second incubation at − 20 °C for 30 min.

After another centrifugation step at 14,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was air-dried. Finally, the dried pellet was resuspended in rehydration buffer containing CHAPS and urea to solubilise and denature the proteins for subsequent SDS-PAGE analysis29.

Experimental set-up for SDS PAGE electrophoresis

For SDS–PAGE analysis, a stock solution of acrylamide was prepared by dissolving 29.2% acrylamide (w/v) (Himedia, GRM1110-500G, Batch# 0000279528) and 0.8% N’N’-bis-methylene-acrylamide (w/v) (Himedia, MB005-25G, Batch# 0000198755) in 100 ml of double-distilled water (DDW). The solution was filtered and stored in a dark glass bottle at 4 °C until use. SDS solutions of 0.4% (w/v) and 0.8% (w/v) were separately prepared for the resolving and stacking gels, respectively, and kept at room temperature. For the preparation of Tris–HCl buffers, 1.5 M Tris–HCl (Himedia, MB029-100G, Batch# 0000420487) (pH 8.8) was obtained by dissolving Tris free base in 15 ml of DDW, adjusting the pH with 1 N HCl, and making up the final volume to 25 ml. Similarly, 1 M Tris–HCl (Himedia, MB030-100G, Batch# 0000089451) (pH 6.8) for the stacking gel was prepared by dissolving Tris-trihydrochloride in 15 ml of DDW, adjusting the pH to 6.8 with 1 N HCl, and bringing the final volume to 25 ml. Both buffers were stored at 4 °C.

The sample loading buffer was prepared by dissolving 24 mg Tris free base, 5 mg EDTA, 200 mg SDS, and 1 g sucrose in 19 ml DDW and adjusting the pH to 8.0. After that, 20 mg bromophenol blue (Himedia, RM117-5G) was added, and the solution was filtered through filter paper. Finally, 1 ml β-mercaptoethanol was added to the filtered solution, which was stored in a dark bottle at 4 °C. The electrophoresis running buffer (pH 8.3) was prepared by dissolving 3.03 g Tris base, 14.4 g glycine, and 1 g SDS in 800 ml DDW, adjusting the pH to 8.3, and making the final volume up to 1 L before storage at 4 °C. A 10% ammonium persulfate (APS) (Himedia, GRM1095-500G, Batch# 0000142945) solution was freshly prepared just before gel casting to ensure efficient polymerisation.

For sample preparation, the protein extract and loading buffer were mixed in a 1:2 ratio, heated at 95 °C for one minute, and then gently loaded into the wells of the stacking gel. The stacking (5%) and resolving (10%) gels were prepared using appropriate proportions of distilled water, 30% acrylamide–bisacrylamide solution, gel buffer, SDS, and freshly prepared APS. Once polymerised, the gel assembly was mounted in the electrophoresis tank (Bio Rad, Mini PROTEAN Tetra Cell, Sl. No. 552BR028247). The running buffer was poured into the tank, and protein samples were carefully loaded into their respective wells. Electrophoresis was carried out at a constant voltage of 50–60 V using a GeNei power supply (GeNei, Model No. SLM-LVP-300, Sl. No. 20091011185) until the desired separation of protein bands was achieved.

After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with a solution containing methanol, acetic acid, and DDW in a 5:1:4 ratio, to which 0.25% Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (Himedia, RM-250, Batch# 0000157217) was added, dissolved, and filtered. The gel was immersed in the staining solution and incubated for six hours at room temperature. Subsequently, the gel was transferred to a destaining solution composed of methanol, acetic acid, and DDW until distinct protein bands appeared. Finally, the gel was scanned using a GS-800 calibrated densitometer (Model Powerlock 2100XL-USB), and the resulting protein banding patterns were analysed with Image Lab Software (version 5.1)30,31.

On-gel isozyme patterns of Native PAGE after SMP of mung bean seedlings

The on-gel antioxidant activity of four different isozymes, namely superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) peroxidase (POD), NADPH Oxidase (NOX), and α-Amylase, was determined by Native-PAGE29 to assess the effects of treatment on antioxidant defence and metabolism under salinity stress.

Preparation of mung bean seedling extracts for on-gel assay

To analyse the isozyme patterns, salinity-induced mung beans were initially cryocrushed and homogenised in phosphate buffers specific to each isozyme. The homogenate was then incubated at − 20 °C for 30 min, followed by cold centrifugation to obtain the supernatant. The resulting supernatant was stored at − 20 °C and subsequently used for the detection of specific isozymes through gel electrophoresis29.

Electrophoresis setup for on-gel assay

Isozyme profiling was carried out under refrigerated conditions to prevent protein denaturation in the collected supernatant, following the standard protocol of Williams & Reisfeld32. The Native PAGE procedure was similar to the SDS-PAGE method described earlier, with some differences: SDS was not used, the samples were not heat-denatured before loading, β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) was not used as protein is required in its native quaternary structure, three-dimensional conformation having biological functionality, and the entire electrophoretic setup was maintained at 4 °C.

Staining of the isozymes and band detection

After electrophoresis, the gels were specifically stained according to the detection requirements of each isozyme. The developed gels showing the isozyme banding patterns were scanned using a GS-800 calibrated densitometer (Model No. Powerlock 2100XL-USB). The resulting images were analysed for band intensity and pattern using Image Lab software (version 5.1).

NADPH Oxidase (NOX) (EC 1.6.3.1) activity

After electrophoresis, the gel was immersed in a staining solution containing 10 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.4), 0.05 g of NBT (Himedia, RM578-1G, Batch# 0000246382), and 134 µM NADPH (Himedia, RM576-100MG, Batch# 00003277249). The gel was then incubated in this staining mixture under ice-cold conditions (inside a freezer) until distinct bands became visible33.

Catalase (CAT) (EC 1.11.1.6) activity

Catalase isozyme staining was performed according to the method described by Woodbury et al.34. The gel was first immersed in 5 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and incubated for 10 min. Following incubation, it was rinsed with double-distilled water and subsequently transferred to a staining solution prepared by mixing equal volumes of 2% (w/v) potassium ferricyanide and 2% (w/v) ferric chloride. The gel was kept in the dark at room temperature until distinct bands appeared.

Peroxidase (POD) (EC 1.11.1.7) activity

After completion of electrophoresis, the gel was carefully removed from the gel plate and immersed in a solution containing 5 mM benzidine prepared in 0.05 M borate buffer. Subsequently, 5 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) (Merck, CC2C620059) was added to the mixture, and the gel was incubated in the dark under ice-cold conditions until distinct bands became visible35.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) (EC 1.15.1.1) activity

Upon completion of electrophoresis, the gel was immersed in a staining solution containing 0.05 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 1 mM EDTA (Himedia, RM1370-100G), 0.05 mM riboflavin (Himedia, PCT0214-25G, Batch# 0000113139), 0.1 mM NBT, and 300 µl TEMED (N, N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine) (Himedia, GRM1572-1ML, Batch# 0000214495). The gel was then incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min. After incubation, it was rinsed thoroughly with double-distilled water and subsequently exposed to light until the isozyme bands became clearly visible36.

α-Amylase (EC 3.2.1.1) activity

After electrophoresis, the gel was immersed in 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 5.0) containing 0.5% starch and incubated at 30 °C for about 30 min. Subsequently, it was rinsed with distilled water and treated with 3% iodine solution for approximately 1 min to visualise the enzyme bands. The gel was then washed again with distilled water, and the distinct bands were recorded using a calibrated GS-800 densitometer37.

Orbitrap high resolution liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (OHR-LCMS)

The most prominent and differentially expressed SDS gel band of mung bean seedlings primed with nano-chitosan (viz., band 4) was analysed for liquid chromatography mass spectroscopy (Q-Exactive Plus Biopharma-High Resolution Orbitrap, Thermo Fischer Scientific Pte. Ltd.).

In-gel digestion of differentially expressed gel band

The differentially expressed protein gel fragments were excised and rinsed with 0.1 ml of 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC). The pieces were then dehydrated by immersing them in a mixed solution of 0.1 ml acetonitrile (ACN) and 50 mM ABC in a 2:1 ratio (designated as Solution A) for 5 min. After removing the supernatant, 0.1 ml of 25 mM ABC was added again, and the samples were incubated for 5 min, followed by another dehydration step with Solution A. This alternating process of rehydration and dehydration was repeated twice before proceeding to the reduction step to cleave disulfide bonds.

For reduction, the gel pieces were rehydrated with 50 µl of 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and incubated at 56 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the DTT solution was discarded, and the gel fragments were rinsed with 100 µl of 25 mM ABC. Next, alkylation was carried out by incubating the samples in the dark with 100 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for 30 min. After incubation, the IAA was removed, and the gel pieces were rinsed with 1 ml of ABC. Following alkylation, the gels were again dehydrated using the same procedure as previously described.

For trypsin digestion, the gel fragments were rehydrated with sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega) in 25 mM ABC at 0 °C and incubated for 30 min to allow the enzyme to be absorbed. The pieces were then transferred to tubes containing 25 mM ABC and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. After digestion, the peptides were extracted by removing the supernatant and vortexing the gel fragments with 0.1 ml of extraction buffer containing 50% ACN and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) for 10 min. The extracted supernatant was collected into a single tube.

Subsequently, 100 µl of a second extraction solution (60% ACN and 0.1% TFA) was added, followed by sonication for 1 min (2-second pulse, 1-second gap, 25% amplitude) and vortexing for 10 min. The supernatant was combined with the previously collected extract. A third extraction was performed using 80% ACN and 0.1% TFA, vortexed for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was also pooled. Finally, the combined peptide extracts were vacuum concentrated to a final volume of around 20 µl29.

Zip tip C18 for MS analysis

ZipTip C18 purification for MS analysis was performed using the supernatant obtained from in-gel digestion. At first, 10 µl of 100% HPLC-grade acetonitrile was passed through the ZipTip to remove any residual impurities. The tip was then washed with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in Milli-Q water to ensure complete removal of contaminants. Peptides were subsequently bound to the ZipTip by aspirating and dispensing the sample for ten cycles. Following peptide binding, the tip was again washed with 0.1% TFA in Milli-Q water to eliminate unbound materials. This washing step was repeated five times to ensure maximum purity. Finally, bound peptides were eluted using 3–5 µl of elution buffer consisting of 50% acetonitrile in 0.1% TFA. The elution was performed by aspirating and dispensing the elution buffer eight times under airtight conditions, and the collected eluate was transferred into a clean sterile microcentrifuge tube for subsequent mass spectrometric analysis29.

Column specification

The analytical separation was carried out using a PepMap RSLC C18 column (2 μm, 100 Å, 50 cm) designed for ultrahigh-resolution analysis. A pre-column, Acclaim PepMap 100 (100 μm × 2 cm, nanoviper), was employed for sample loading and initial purification29.

The mobile phase system consisted of two solvents: Solvent A, comprising 0.1% formic acid (FA) in Milli-Q water, and Solvent B, consisting of 80% acetonitrile and 20% Milli-Q water containing 0.1% FA.

Sample runtime, software program, and database

The electrophoretic run for the samples was carried out for a duration of 60 min. At the end of the run, data analysis in relation to peptide sequences was carried out using Thermo Proteome Discoverer software version 2.2. The identification of peptide sequences derived from the OHR-LCMS analysis was performed using the Vigna radiata protein database.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Protein–protein enrichment analysis of the OHR-LCMS identified proteins was studied using the STRING database (version 12.0; https://string-db.org/). STRING generated biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) were aligned with respect to the percentile ranking of the obtained GO (Gene ontology) score for obtaining top-ranked processes38. The proteins that have obtained an average score of 0.5 were selected for the second round of STRING analysis39. STRING-generated interaction data were passed through Cytoscape (version 3.10.4; https://cytoscape.org/) to obtain the topological gene score40. Topologically, the proteins that have crossed the average percentile ranking of 0.6 were selected for identifying the most significant KEGG-enriched pathway and sub-cellular compartment of protein localisation41,42. Sub-networking analysis of highly interconnected nodes was performed using MCODE (version 2.0.0) statistical package, considering node density cut from 0.1 and node score cut off to 0.2, considering the p-value cut off ≤ 0.0543.

Statistical analysis

All the graphs were made using OriginLab software (https://www.originlab.com › origin). Data were analysed using Tukey’s post hoc test at p < 0.05. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) were performed using XLSTAT (v_2019.2.2).

Results

Morphological and germination responses of mung bean seeds

Chitosan and nano-chitosan both influenced the germination and early seedling growth responses of mung bean (Vigna radiata) under non-saline conditions as well as low (4 dS/m) and moderate salinity levels (8 dS/m). Regarding treatments, nano-chitosan and chitosan priming resulted in higher total germination percentage compared to the untreated control (Fig. 2). Hydroprimed seeds also showed improvements in TG% relative to the unprimed control under moderate salinity (4 dS/m), but at higher (8 dS/m) salinity, the effect of hydropriming was milder. The observation revealed that germination of chitosan-based primed seeds may be attributed to the modulation of membrane integrity and activation of antioxidant systems, leading to enhanced stress tolerance in legumes44,45.

A similar trend was observed for the germination index, with nano-chitosan-primed seeds consistently showing the highest values across all salinity conditions, followed by chitosan- and hydroprimed treatments. Kumari et al.46 state that the improved GI under nano-chitosan priming reflects faster and more synchronised germination, consistent with its positive effect on water uptake and reserve mobilisation during early imbibition. The unprimed control displayed a gradual decline in GI with increasing salinity, reaffirming the inhibitory effect of NaCl on enzymatic and metabolic activities during germination, as previously documented in our previous report for the mung bean47.

Mean germination time displayed an inverse pattern, with the lowest values recorded for nano-chitosan-primed seeds across all treatments, signifying accelerated germination kinetics and higher physiological efficiency. As reported earlier, the faster germination rate under nano-chitosan priming may be due to improved osmotic adjustment and enhanced hydrolytic enzyme activation, enabling rapid radicle emergence even under saline conditions48. Hydroprimed seeds showed intermediate, inconsistent responses, possibly reflecting transient hydration effects without substantial biochemical modulation. The coefficient of germination velocity was higher in the control and hydroprimed seeds due to delayed germination, while the lower CGV in nano-chitosan-based priming resulted from rapid and uniform germination within the early observation period49.

The germination stress index followed a similar trend, with nano-chitosan, chitosan, and hydropriming enhancing seed germination at 4 dS/m. This salinity tolerance was recorded even at 8 dS/m for nano-chitosan-treated seeds. The enhanced GSI under chitosan-based treatments indicates improved vigour and a reduced lag phase between imbibition and radicle protrusion. While untreated and hydroprimed seeds displayed proportionate delay in seed germination with increasing salinity, suggesting compromised metabolic reactivation due to ionic and osmotic stress50.

Mean germination rate and vigour index (Fig. 3) further indicate the positive influence of nano-chitosan priming. At 0 dS/m, nano-chitosan-primed seeds recorded the highest MGR, followed by chitosan and hydroprimed treatments. Under both 4 and 8 dS/m, nano-chitosan and chitosan priming maintained a significant advantage over untreated seeds, indicating their role in sustaining growth rate and seedling vigour under stress. This effect can be attributed to the ability of chitosan derivatives to enhance antioxidative enzyme activity and osmoprotectant accumulation as revealed in our previous study, thereby mitigating oxidative damage during salt-induced osmotic imbalance21. Similar observation was reported by Bigham Soostani51 and Hassan52 while studying the salinity response of chitosan nanoparticles in Brassica napus and Catharanthus roseus, respectively. The relative indices of shoot, root, and plant height were recorded as highest in nano-chitosan-primed seedlings under all salinity regimes. These results indicate that nano-chitosan priming effectively retains cell turgor, root elongation, and shoot expansion under saline conditions, supporting enhanced tolerance and biomass allocation efficiency. The decline in these indices with increasing salinity in untreated controls aligns with previous findings claiming that high ionic concentrations impair root–shoot growth through reduced water uptake and nutrient assimilation53.

The enhancement in germination performance and morphological traits in nano-chitosan and chitosan-primed seeds indicates that chitosan-based priming facilitates stress mitigation through biochemical and structural modulation. The improved performance likely arises from chitosan’s ability to induce protective signalling pathways, improve ROS scavenging, and stabilise macromolecular structures, as previously reported in our earlier study21. It was also noted that nano-chitosan showed slightly superior effects to bulk chitosan, which could be attributed to its higher surface reactivity and improved cellular interaction efficiency, leading to more effective priming-induced metabolic activation54,55.

The PCA biplot revealed a clear separation between treatments, reflecting the differential germination responses of mung bean seedlings under varying priming and salinity conditions. The first two principal components (F1 and F2) account for 80.82% of the total variance, with F1 and F2 explaining 66.33% and 14.48% respectively, indicating that most of the variation in the dataset is cumulated by these two components (Fig. 4a). The remaining components (F3 to F9) shares 19.18%. Treatments with chitosan and nano-chitosan priming, specifically under higher salinity levels (8 dS/m), were positioned on the positive side of F1 and F2 axes, in close association with growth and stress indices such as PHSI, SHSI, GI, and MGR (Supplementary Dataset File 1). This clustering indicates a statistical association between nano-chitosan-based priming and improved germination-related indices under saline conditions.

The factor loading matrix indicates that for most treatments, F1 was dominant, particularly for 0-Cont (0.919), 8-Cont (0.925), 0-Chitosan (0.746), and 4-NanoChito (0.747), confirming that F1 largely represents the variance related to overall germination efficiency and vigour (Supplementary Dataset File 1). While F2 contributed more significantly for treatments such as 4-Hydro (0.565), 8-Chitosan (0.468), and 8-NanoChito (0.517), reflecting the influence of salinity-induced stress response parameters.

The control and hydro-primed treatments across different salinity levels were mainly distributed on the negative side of F1 in the PCA biplot, whereas most germination and seedling performance parameters, including GI, VI, TG%, RLSI, SHSI, PHSI, and MGR, were positioned on the positive side of F1. This distribution indicates that these parameters were positively associated with chitosan- and nano-chitosan-primed treatments rather than with the control and hydro-primed groups. In contrast, CV and MGT were located on the negative side of F1, indicating an opposite association pattern. Notably, 8-Cont and 8-Hydro were positioned farthest from the positively associated germination parameters, reflecting a stronger influence of salinity stress under these treatments. Their relative separation from MGR further suggests comparatively slower and less synchronized germination responses under high salinity conditions.

HCA was conducted to identify the similarity within the seed priming treatments. The dendrogram (Fig. 4b) showed two distinct clusters at a dissimilarity threshold of ∼4000. While the first cluster contained control and hydro-primed treatments at all salinity levels, suggesting similar physiological responses under experimental conditions. Distinct sub-clusters were formed for chitosan- and nano-chitosan-primed treatments in the second cluster. Based on this separation, nano-chitosan-based priming treatments resulted in statistically detectable variation in seedling growth parameters as compared to untreated control and hydro-primed groups.

SDS PAGE mediated protein banding pattern of solid matrix primed mung bean seedlings under study

Morphological observations indicated that nano-chitosan and chitosan priming were associated with comparatively improved seedling performance under salinity stress. Therefore, SDS-PAGE profiling was performed to examine treatment-associated variations in soluble protein banding patterns. Hydroprimed seedlings served as a comparative treatment, while untreated seedlings were used as controls. SDS-PAGE is widely employed as an analytical technique for comparative protein profiling and assessment of relative banding variation in plant systems56,57.

SDS–PAGE analysis revealed the presence of 8 major protein bands in both primed and non-primed seedlings under salinity stress conditions (Fig. 5a and b). Variations among treatments were mainly observed in the relative intensity of specific bands rather than in the appearance or disappearance of bands. The densitometric analysis presented in Fig. 5c-i and c-ii was used to compare relative band density among treatments. Jain and Sharma58 reported the occurrence of 6–11 protein bands in mung bean genotypes during seed storage protein profiling, supporting the present findings. It was also reported that mung bean seed storage proteins consist of albumins, globulins, and prolamins, collectively representing nearly 85% of the total protein content59. Among these, salt-soluble globulins constitute approximately 60–70% of the total seed proteins and are categorised into three major classes, viz., legumin-like (11–12 S), vicilin-like (8 S), and basic (7 S) globulins57,60.

(a) SDS–PAGE gel profile and (b) densitometrically scanned image of proteins isolated from Vigna radiata seedlings subjected to different priming treatments under salinity stress conditions. Lane 1: unprimed control; Lane 2: hydro-primed seedlings; Lane 3: nano-chitosan primed seedlings; Lane 4: chitosan primed seedlings. (c-i) and (c-ii) relative density profile of protein bands obtained from densitometric analysis of SDS–PAGE gel. Numerical labels (1–8) indicate corresponding protein band numbers identified in the scanned gel profile.

Among the detected bands, bands 1, 5, and 8 showed relatively stable expression across treatments, whereas noticeable variations in band intensity were observed, particularly in bands 2, 3, 4, and 6. Since the lower-intensity bands were faint in the original gel image, they were more clearly represented in the scanned densitometric profile and corresponding graphical analysis. The analysis was therefore primarily based on the densitometric scanning results rather than visual observation alone from the gel photograph.

On comparing the obtained result with hydro-primed seedlings, it was observed that there was a significant decrease in band intensity, specifically band 2, 3, and 4, indicating greater tolerance and adaptability of nano-chitosan primed seedlings under salinity stress. Soni et al.61 reported similar observation by stating that nano-chitosan primed rice seedlings showed superior morphological and biochemical performance compared to hydro-primed seedlings, indicating the strong potential of chitosan nanoparticles and chitosan as eco-friendly priming agents for salinity stress tolerance.

In the case of chitosan primed seedlings, it was observed that the intensity of bands remains almost constant under salinity stress, except for bands 3 and 4, which show a significant difference. The obtained result demonstrates that under salinity stress, chitosan-primed seedlings probably upregulate the expression of some defensive proteins, thereby inducing potential tolerance. However, the degree of protein expression is significantly higher in nano-chitosan-primed seedlings. The above observation was supported by the expression intensity of bands 2, 3, and 6 in nano-chitosan primed seedlings, which showed significant differences with respect to other treatments. The obtained result correlates with the observation of Lyalina et al.62, stating that chitosan hydrolysate seed priming enhances lettuce germination, root branching, biomass, and pigment content, particularly at 0.1 mg/mL. Riseh et al.63 also stated that chitosan seed coatings enhance germination, growth, and stress tolerance while offering an eco-friendly solution to agricultural technology. Thus, chitosan is a sustainable alternative to synthetic agrochemicals, improving plant growth, nutrient mobilisation, stress resilience, and disease protection64.

NATIVE PAGE mediated isozyme activity detection of solid matrix primed mung bean seedlings under study

Previous studies reported that treatment with chitosan is closely correlated to antioxidant-related stress responses, osmotic adjustment, and enhanced seedling performance under salinity65–69. Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis was applied as an analytical method to analyse relative isozyme banding patterns of selected antioxidant enzymes under saline environments in the present investigation. As Native PAGE provides comparative visualisation of isozyme activity patterns rather than quantitative enzyme activity, results must be interpreted as indicative trends.

The enzyme complex NADPH oxidase is responsible for generating superoxide within plant tissues70. It was reported that NOX complex transfers electrons from NADPH to molecular oxygen across the membrane, resulting in the generation of ROS71. Among these, superoxide is the most common ROS generated as NADPH oxidase donates electrons to oxygen72. The present study also detected one isoform of NADPH oxidase (Fig. 6a and b) in all the studied treatments, indicating the generation of intercellular ROS under salinity stress. The increase in intercellular stress content was reported in our previous study in which salt stress significantly increased proline, MDA, and H₂O₂ contents in mung bean seedlings, indicating enhanced oxidative stress21. The degree of NOX expression indicates that greater ROS generation occurs in nano-chitosan-primed mung bean seedlings (Fig. 6c). Based on the existing reports, this enhanced ROS level under stress triggers protective and acclimation responses, including cell wall reinforcement and regulation of stress-responsive gene expression73,74. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulate gene expression during abiotic stress through both ABA-dependent and ABA-independent signalling pathways. They also play critical roles in plant development by influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, and organ morphogenesis75–78. In stress responses, ROS enhance plant tolerance by reinforcing cell walls through papilla formation and lignification, activating signalling cascades such as MAPKs, and modulating metabolic fluxes. ROS also contribute to cellular homeostasis and strengthen defence mechanisms against both biotic and abiotic stresses79–81. At basal levels, ROS act as important signalling molecules, while excessive ROS accumulation can overwhelm antioxidant defences, resulting in oxidative damage and programmed cell death82. This balance is maintained by a well-coordinated antioxidant defence system that scavenges excess ROS while preserving signalling-competent ROS levels83,84, as observed in the present study.

(c) Graphical representation depicting the relative density on-gel enzyme.

Under salinity stress, mung bean seedlings display increased production of superoxide radicals (O₂⁻), mainly due to enhanced NADPH oxidase activity and electron leakage from the mitochondrial and chloroplastic electron transport chains85,86. Excessive accumulation of these ROS causes oxidative damage and disturbs cellular homeostasis85. To counteract the harmful effects of superoxide radicals, plants synthesise superoxide dismutase (SOD), an antioxidant enzyme that converts O₂⁻ into the less toxic molecules H₂O₂ and O₂87. The present study reports three isoforms of SOD (Fig. 7a and b), which is in accordance with the observation of Reddy et al.88. It was reported that SOD isozymes, specifically SOD-I and SOD-II are localised in the chloroplasts and catalyse the transfer of to H2O289,90. The greater band intensity of the middle isozyme in the present study indicates that under salinity stress, SOD functions to maintain the functionality of the photosynthetic apparatus and thereby maintains cellular homeostasis (Fig. 7c). The observation was in accordance with Manaa et al.91 which states that in comparison to control, the intensity level of SOD isozymes increased under salinity stress. The observed isozyme pattern was consistent with our previously reported quantitative SOD data21.

(c) Graphical representation depicting the relative density on-gel enzyme. Numerical (1, 2, 3…) at the top of graphical bars denotes the band numbers.

Hydrogen peroxide, generated as an intermediate during SOD activity, is a reactive oxygen species and must be detoxified because of its harmful effects on biological systems92. Peroxidase (POD) is an important antioxidant isozyme that protects plant tissues, including the photosynthetic apparatus, from ROS produced during SOD activity93. POD exerts its protective role by catalysing the conversion of H₂O₂ and organic hydroperoxides (ROOH) into water (H₂O) and their corresponding alcohols (R–OH), thereby reducing oxidative damage29. Peroxidases participate in a wide range of physiological processes, including lignin and cell wall synthesis, providing defence against pathogens and herbivores, and contributing to abiotic stress tolerance94. Our findings report one POD isoform in mung bean seedlings (Fig. 8a and b), with relatively greater band intensity in nano-chitosan primed treatments (Fig. 8c). Previous studies have reported associations between enhanced peroxidase activity and improved stress tolerance under saline and drought conditions95. The present observations suggest that nano-chitosan priming may be associated with the modulation of antioxidant-related responses under salinity stress.

Another important antioxidant protein-complex, which protects against cellular damage from the end product of SOD activity, is the catalase (CAT) enzyme96. Catalase is a peroxisomal antioxidant enzyme that uniquely decomposes hydrogen peroxide directly97, through a two-step reaction, yielding two molecules of water and one molecule of oxygen98. One catalase isoform was detected in the present study (Fig. 9a and b). Relative CAT band intensity appeared lower in hydroprimed seedlings than in chitosan and nano-chitosan primed treatments (Fig. 9c). These observations suggest that chitosan-based priming may influence antioxidant-associated responses under saline conditions. Reported quantitative CAT activity assays in our previous study support the present observation21.

α-Amylase is an essential enzyme that breaks down starch granules into smaller organic molecules, providing energy and nutrients required for successful seed germination. Reports suggest that salt stress reduces α-amylase activity99. Xiong et al.100 reported that barley seeds exhibit decreased α-amylase activity when exposed to high salt conditions. The present study also reports decreased α-amylase activity in all the studied priming conditions, except for seeds primed with nano-chitosan (Fig. 10a and b). The obtained result clearly indicates the potential of nano-chitosan to upregulate the expression of essential enzymes to withstand salinity stress (Fig. 10c). Thus, nanopriming enhances seed germination and seedling growth under drought and salt stress by promoting water uptake, boosting α-amylase and soluble sugars, and regulating genes linked to hormone balance, antioxidants, and ion homeostasis101.

OHR‑LCMS analysis of the differentially expressed SDS band

The OHR-LCMS analysis (Fig. 11), processed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer 2.2, leads to the identification of peptides corresponding to the target proteins (band 4 of land 3 in Fig. 4, i.e., protein band was analysed from seedling primed with nano-chitosan). The analysis in total detected 260 types of peptides, of which 231 are distinct peptides (Supplementary Dataset File 2). Peptide mapping revealed distinct protein groups supported by both shared and unique peptide fragments, enabling confident protein identification through bottom-up proteomics102.

Representative OHR-LCMS total ion chromatogram (TIC) of proteins extracted from differentially expressed SDS–PAGE bands of nano-chitosan-primed mung bean seedlings subjected to salinity stress. The chromatogram displays peptide ion abundance across the retention time range of 0–60 min, with major peaks indicating the presence of highly abundant peptide fragments used for protein identification and comparative proteomic analysis.

The detected proteins included enzymes associated with glycolysis, carbon metabolism, amino acid metabolism, translational machinery, heat shock proteins, detoxification enzymes, and transport-related proteins. These proteins were identified based on peptide matching and database annotation and therefore represent putative functional associations of biological pathways. Similar protein classes have previously been reported by Yu et al.103 in mung bean seed and sprout proteomic studies in terms of catalytic activity, binding, and structural molecule activity.

Proteomic profiling identified several enzymes involved in primary carbon and energy metabolism, including sucrose synthase, ADP-glucose phosphorylase, phosphoglucomutase, GAPDH, pyruvate kinase, phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) carboxylase, malate dehydrogenase, malic enzyme, transketolase, and RuBisCO (rbcL/rbcS). These proteins are associated with glycolysis, the Calvin cycle, and starch/sucrose metabolism, indicating efficient carbon fixation, energy production, and metabolic flux required for active growth, biosynthesis, and physiological maintenance in metabolically active tissues104,105. The detection of multiple lipoxygenase isoforms, phospholipases, and 12-oxophytodienoate/2-alkenal reductases suggests activation of lipid signalling and oxylipin-mediated pathways, including jasmonate biosynthesis, which are associated with stress responses, defence mechanisms, and developmental processes such as germination106–112. These enzymes regulate membrane lipid turnover, maintain redox homeostasis, and mediate adaptive signalling pathways in plants.

A broad range of translational and proteostasis-related proteins, including ribosomal proteins, elongation factors (EF-Tu, EF2, EF1-α), tRNA ligases, Hsp70/Hsp90 chaperones, luminal-binding protein, calnexin, endoplasmin, ubiquitin, and 26 S proteasome subunits, indicates active protein synthesis, folding, and turnover109,110. The elevated abundance of heat-shock proteins and proteasomal components suggests enhanced chaperone activity and regulated protein degradation, essential for maintaining cellular protein homeostasis during growth and stress adaptation111–113.

The presence of glutathione S-transferases, detoxification enzymes, disease-resistance proteins, and polyamine/peroxisomal enzymes indicates active oxidative stress management and immune responses114,115. Enzymes involved in one-carbon and redox metabolism, such as adenosyl homocysteinase, S-methyltransferase, serine hydroxymethyltransferase, radical SAM proteins, and short-chain dehydrogenase/reductases (SDRs), show the importance of methylation reactions and redox regulation in cellular metabolism116,117.

Proteins associated with intracellular trafficking and organelle maintenance, including coatomer, SEC and exocyst components, V-ATPase, organellar import factors, and CLPX3, reveal active vesicle-mediated transport and efficient organelle function118–120. The identification of regulatory proteins such as TOR and receptor kinases, transcription factors, mediator/RNA polymerase subunits, DEAD-box helicases, and dicer indicates multilayered regulation of growth, transcriptional activity, and post-transcriptional processes121–123.

From the STRING interaction network, Cytoscape identified 96 nodes with clustering coefficients of 0.156 and network density of 0.030, respectively (Fig. 14). Parameters such as betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, degree, radiality, stress, and eigenvalue metrics were used to calculate the average topological score (Supplementary Dataset File 8).

Central regulatory proteins included A0A3Q0EIA4 (TOR kinase) and A0A1S3U4Q9 (ATM kinase), which function in nutrient and energy signalling, genome stability, and stress adaptation under adverse conditions141. A0A3Q0F2H5 (ankyrin repeat-containing protein BDA1-like, 451 amino acids) functions as a membrane-associated signalling scaffold that regulates stress-induced defence and programmed cell death. During salinity stress, it modulates ROS and hormone-dependent signalling pathways, helping balance cellular protection with growth and preventing excessive stress-triggered damage142,143. Enhanced NOX-mediated ROS generation in primed seedlings, as revealed through on-gel analysis, functions as a signalling molecule rather than a damaging agent, activating antioxidant machinery (SOD, POD, CAT) that maintains redox homeostasis. This balanced ROS signalling aligns with the topological identification of stress-responsive genes involved in antioxidant defence, ion transport, and energy regulation.

A0A1S3TQ34 (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunit A, eIF3A) plays an important role in maintaining the synthesis of proteins required for osmotic and ionic adjustment, ROS detoxification, and stress recovery under salinity conditions. Modulation of eIF3 function allows plants to selectively regulate the production of plastid-targeted and other stress-responsive proteins during salt stress144. A0A1S3VF54 (elongation factor Tu, EF-Tu), a translational GTPase involved in aminoacyl-tRNA delivery during protein synthesis, is recognised as a stress-responsive protein whose abundance increases under saline conditions. Enhanced expression of chloroplast EF-Tu has been reported in Arabidopsis under salt stress, while soybean EF-Tu family genes improve tolerance to drought and salinity when overexpressed145.

Identified A0A1S3V0X8 (elongation factor 1-alpha, EF1α) and A0A1S3U5C1 (elongation factor 2, EF2) also support translational regulation during stress. EF1α contributes to protein folding and stability through its chaperone activity, whereas EF2 regulates selective translation of proteins involved in ion homeostasis, stress signalling, and cellular protection under salinity stress146,147. Ribosome-associated proteins, including A0A1S3U1H3 (60 S ribosomal protein L10), A0A1S3VR49 (60 S ribosomal protein L8-3), A0A1S3VYW3 (threonine-tRNA ligase), and A0A1S3VNQ1 (ubiquitin–40 S ribosomal protein S27a), indicate active translational reprogramming and proteostasis during stress adaptation. In particular, RPL10A has been linked with ABA-mediated stress responses, while overexpression of ubiquitin–RPS27a genes enhances antioxidant defence and osmoprotection under salinity stress148.

Proteostasis-related genes viz., A0A1S3UWN7 (HSP70), A0A1S3UF73 (HSP80), and A0A1S3VRB7 (HSP90-5), function as molecular chaperones that stabilise proteins, maintain chloroplast integrity, and reduce ROS accumulation and photoinhibition during salinity stress111,149. A0A1S3UFW8 (endoplasmin homolog), A0A1S3TQK5 (OST subunit 2), and A0A1S3UFK6 (CLPX3) contribute to ER proteostasis, protein glycosylation, mitochondrial quality control, ATP maintenance, and redox balance, thereby supporting cellular adaptation under osmotic stress150.

Several metabolic enzymes associated with energy production and redox regulation were enriched, including A0A1S3VRS8 (PPi-dependent phosphofructokinase subunit alpha), A0A1S3V6B6 (transketolase), A0A1S3U7A0 (GAPDH), A0A3Q0ET31 (acetate-CoA ligase), A0A1S3TQ50 (acyl-activating enzyme 17), and A0A1S3VKH8 (serine hydroxymethyltransferase). These proteins are involved in glycolysis, the Calvin cycle, pentose phosphate pathway, lipid metabolism, and one-carbon metabolism, thereby sustaining energy supply, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant defence during salinity stress151.

Protein turnover and stress signalling were represented by A0A1S3VSK8 (Xaa-Pro aminopeptidase), A0A1S3U4J7 (aminopeptidase), A0A1S3VRB2 (oligopeptidase A), and A0A1S3U6I8 (cullin-1), which regulate peptide metabolism, ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, osmoprotectant accumulation, and ABA-dependent stress responses152,153.

Genes associated with vesicle trafficking, ion homeostasis, and chloroplast maintenance included A0A1S3W0M1 (Exo70 family protein), A0A1S3VTL4 (Sect. 23), A0A1S3TJQ2 (V-type proton ATPase subunit a), A0A1S3U6H5 (RuBisCO large subunit-binding protein beta), A0A1S3VKE9 (TIC40), and A0A1S3V1E0 (RNA helicase). These proteins contribute to membrane trafficking, vacuolar Na⁺ sequestration, chloroplast protein import, photosynthetic stability, ion balance, and antioxidant activation under salinity stress154.

The improved germination, vigour, and seedling growth observed in nano-chitosan-primed mung bean under salinity stress are strongly supported by Cytoscape topological network analysis. The coordinated enrichment of genes involved in ion homeostasis, osmotic regulation, antioxidant defence, translational control, and proteostasis suggests that nano-chitosan priming enhances stress adaptation and sustained biomass accumulation under saline conditions.

MCODE-mediated analysis of functional sub-networking

MCODE_v2.0.0 (Molecular Complex Detection; node score cutoff 0.2, K-core 2) analysis was performed in Cytoscape to identify highly interconnected functional modules within the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. The analysis revealed several densely connected clusters (MCODE modules), representing functional hubs that coordinate related biochemical and signalling processes (Fig. 15). The occurrence of multiple smaller clusters suggests functional specialisation within the network. Under salinity stress, plants commonly experience ATP depletion, ionic imbalance, oxidative damage, and disruption of metabolic homeostasis155. The identified subnetworks indicate that nano-chitosan priming promotes coordinated metabolic, translational, and stress-responsive mechanisms that collectively enhance cellular stability and seedling performance under saline conditions.

Functional sub-networking of highly interconnected nodes using MCODE (version 2.0.0). Networks were built considering node density and node score cut-off value of 0.1 and 0.2, respectively, with kappa-core value of 2.

Sub-network-1 represents a coordinated central carbon metabolism module associated with energy conservation and redox regulation under salinity stress. The strong interconnections among the proteins indicate close coordination between glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Within this module, A0A1S3VRS8 (pyrophosphate–fructose-6-phosphate 1-phosphotransferase subunit alpha) catalyses the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate using inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) instead of ATP, thereby supporting an energy-efficient glycolytic pathway during ATP-limited stress conditions156. A0A1S3V778 (pyruvate kinase) catalyses the final rate-limiting step of glycolysis, producing pyruvate and ATP157. The close association between these enzymes suggests coordinated regulation of glycolytic flux to maintain energy production under stress. Glycolysis-derived pyruvate subsequently enters the TCA cycle, where A0A1S3UVZ0 (malate dehydrogenase) regulates the reversible conversion of malate and oxaloacetate while maintaining the NADH/NAD⁺ balance, thereby supporting cellular redox homeostasis and mitochondrial respiration158. The proteins in this cluster form an integrated metabolic network that sustains energy production and redox buffering, contributing to improved germination, growth rate, and seedling vigour under saline conditions159.

Sub-network-2 is associated with maintenance of photosynthetic efficiency, chloroplast stability, and metabolic flexibility under salinity stress. Salt stress frequently affects RuBisCO activity, chloroplast translation, and carbon fixation because of ionic imbalance and oxidative damage160,161. In this module, A0A1S3U6H5 functions as a RuBisCO chaperonin involved in the proper folding and assembly of the RuBisCO large subunit, thereby supporting efficient carbon fixation in the Calvin–Benson cycle162. A0A1S3TN79 (enolase) links glycolysis with downstream carbon metabolism and contributes to the redistribution of carbon flux between the chloroplast and cytosolic pathways163. Its association with RuBisCO assembly proteins suggests coordinated regulation between carbon fixation and metabolic utilisation. Besides this, A0A1S3UXU1, a chloroplast RNA stem-loop binding protein, is involved in chloroplast RNA processing and transcript stabilisation, thereby maintaining chloroplast gene expression and photosynthetic activity164. Collectively, these network proteins support coordinated photosynthetic and metabolic regulation, thereby enhancing the physiological and morphological performance of nano-chitosan-primed mung bean seedlings under salinity stress.

Sub-network-3 represents exocyst-associated vesicle trafficking proteins. Exo70 proteins are components of the exocyst complex that direct secretory vesicles to specific plasma membrane sites during exocytosis165. This process is essential for cell polarity establishment, membrane remodelling, cell wall deposition, and stress adaptation166,167. The coordinated interaction among Exo70-related proteins indicates enhanced vesicle trafficking and membrane dynamics in primed seedlings. Such regulation likely supports rapid radicle emergence, cell expansion, and stress-responsive cellular remodelling, thereby contributing to improved salt tolerance and seedling growth.

Sub-network-4 represents a highly integrated module linking amino acid activation, protein synthesis, carbon metabolism, and chloroplast-associated regulation. The central components include several aminoacyl-tRNA ligases, such as histidine- (A0A3Q0FBD8), glutamate- (A0A1S3V813), glutamine- (A0A1S3V788), threonine- (A0A1S3VYW3), methionine- (A0A1S3VXA2), and isoleucine-tRNA ligases (A0A1S3TJH6), which are responsible for accurate tRNA charging during translation168. The presence of translation-related proteins, including 60 S ribosomal protein L5-2 (A0A1S3V164), elongation factor 2 (A0A1S3U5C1), and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunits A (A0A1S3TQ34) and B (A0A1S3U4Z4), indicates coordinated regulation of translation initiation, elongation, and ribosome assembly169. This translational machinery is functionally linked with carbon metabolism proteins, including pyrophosphate–fructose-6-phosphate 1-phosphotransferase (A0A1S3VRS8), enolase (A0A1S3TN79), pyruvate kinase (A0A1S3V778), and malate dehydrogenase (A0A1S3UVZ0), which collectively maintain ATP production and redox balance required for sustained protein synthesis158.

The module also includes chloroplast-associated proteins such as RuBisCO large subunit-binding protein β (A0A1S3U6H5) and chloroplast stem-loop binding protein (A0A1S3UXU1), indicating coordination between photosynthetic carbon fixation, chloroplast gene expression, and cytosolic translation170. ABC transporter E family member 2 (A0A1S3VRY0) may support amino acid and metabolite transport necessary for sustained biosynthetic activity171,172. This network reveals that nano-chitosan priming maintains metabolic and translational homeostasis under salinity stress. It supports reserve mobilisation, stress-responsive protein synthesis, photosynthetic stability, and improved biomass accumulation.

Sub-network-5 is associated with intracellular membrane trafficking and secretory pathway organisation. Coatomer (COPI) subunits α and β′ are involved in retrograde vesicle transport between the Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum, as well as intra-Golgi trafficking173. Their interaction with AP-1 complex subunit γ (A0A1S3VYC4) indicates coordination with clathrin-mediated transport between the trans-Golgi network and endosomes174. This network likely contributes to salinity tolerance by maintaining membrane trafficking efficiency, ensuring proper localisation of transporters and stress-responsive proteins, and preventing protein mislocalisation under ionic stress conditions.

Sub-network-6 represents a molecular chaperone and proteostasis module involved in maintaining protein stability during salinity stress. HSP90-5 (A0A1S3VRB7) and stromal HSP70 (A0A1S3VJ48) are chloroplast-localised chaperones that facilitate proper protein folding, stabilisation, and maturation under stress-induced protein denaturation conditions175,176. Their association with endoplasmin homolog (A0A1S3UFW8), an ER-localised HSP90 family protein, suggests coordinated proteostasis regulation across multiple cellular compartments177. Efficient protein folding and stabilisation are essential for maintaining photosynthetic machinery, metabolic reactivation, and cellular homeostasis under saline conditions. The coordinated chaperone network identified in this sub-network probably contributes to the enhanced germination, seedling vigour, and growth observed in nano-chitosan primed mung bean seedlings exposed to salinity stress.

Sub-cellular protein localisation in response to salinity stress

Salt stress resistance in nano-chitosan primed mung bean seedlings is also revealed by the enriched GO cellular components (Fig. 16a), viz., cytoplasmic, chloroplastic, and mitochondrial compartments. Salt stress induces osmotic and ionic imbalance, leading to protein misfolding and translational arrest in the cytoplasm/cytosol (GO:0005737; GO:0005829). Enrichment of the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 (eIF3) complex and 43S/48S preinitiation complexes supports selective translation of stress-responsive mRNAs, an adaptive mechanism under salinity stress178.

(a) Sub-cellular localisation analysis of differentially expressed proteins identified from nano-chitosan-primed mung bean seedlings under salinity stress. The bar graph represents the distribution of proteins across different cellular compartments based on GO Cellular Component annotations, with protein count corresponding to each sub-cellular localisation category. (b) KEGG pathway annotation of the identified proteins showing the number of proteins associated with individual metabolic and regulatory pathways. The analysis highlights the involvement of proteins associated with stress adaptation, cellular metabolism, transport, and energy-related pathways contributing to salinity tolerance in nano-chitosan-primed mung bean seedlings.

Activation of proteasome complexes and regulatory particles helps the degradation of damaged or misfolded proteins, maintaining proteostasis during salt-induced oxidative stress179. The chloroplast stroma (GO:0009570) participates in stress acclimation by regulating redox balance and carbon metabolism180. The mitochondrial matrix (GO:0005759) generates ATP and participates in metabolic flexibility required for ion homeostasis181. Thus, these compartment-specific processes support cellular resilience to salinity stress.

Upregulation of SOD, POD, and CAT isozymes, as revealed through on-gel analysis, effectively regulates salinity stress by maintaining redox homeostasis within the enriched GO cellular compartments. In the cytoplasm, SOD rapidly dismutates superoxide radicals generated by NADPH oxidase into H₂O₂, preventing oxidative inhibition of translational machinery and supporting the functioning of eIF3-associated 43S/48S preinitiation complexes182. In chloroplasts, chloroplastic SOD and POD limit ROS accumulation generated from disrupted photosynthetic electron transport, thereby preserving stromal redox balance, carbon metabolism, and photosystem integrity183,184. Identified CAT and POD activity, then detoxify excess H₂O₂, preventing oxidative damage to chloroplast proteins and membranes185. In mitochondria, enhanced SOD and CAT activities mitigate ROS leakage from the respiratory electron transport chain, protecting ATP production required for ion transport and osmotic balance186.

KEGG-based functional categorisation in response to salinity stress

The significant enrichment of carbon metabolism (vra01200), glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (vra00010), TCA cycle (vra00020), pyruvate metabolism (vra00620), and the pentose phosphate pathway (vra00030) indicates a major metabolic energy production under salinity stress (Fig. 16b). Enhanced glycolytic and TCA flux ensures sufficient ATP supply, while activation of the pentose phosphate pathway increases NADPH availability, which is critical for ROS detoxification and maintenance of redox balance during salt-induced oxidative stress181,187. At the molecular level, the clear separation of nano-chitosan-primed seedlings from the control and hydro-primed treatments in the PCA biplot, particularly at 8 dS m⁻¹ and in strong association with vigour and stress indices (PHSI, SHSI, GI, MGR, and TMW), is maintained by extensive metabolic processes. The significant enrichment of central carbon metabolism, including glycolysis and the TCA cycle, indicates enhanced ATP generation required for germination, growth, and ion homeostasis under saline conditions (Fig. 16b).

The strong enrichment of amino acid biosynthesis (vra01230) and related pathways, viz., alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (vra00250), arginine biosynthesis (vra00220), glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (vra00260), and cysteine and methionine metabolism (vra00270), indicates an adaptive accumulation of osmoprotective and stress-responsive amino acids. Glutamate and arginine act as precursor molecules for proline and polyamines, which stabilise proteins and membranes while contributing to osmotic adjustment under high salinity188,189. Methionine and cysteine metabolism also support the production of sulfur-containing antioxidants and stress signalling molecules190.

The enrichment of carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (vra00710) and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (vra00630) indicates metabolic flexibility that allows plants to maintain carbon assimilation and bypass salt-induced inhibition of photosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 3). Such adjustments are essential for sustaining growth and metabolic homeostasis under saline conditions188. Glutathione metabolism (vra00480) and sulfur metabolism (vra00920) are significantly enriched, participating in antioxidant defence systems under salt tolerance. Glutathione acts as a redox buffer, scavenging reactive oxygen species induced by salt stress and protecting cellular components from oxidative damage191.

The activation of aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (vra00970) and RNA transport (vra03013) indicates sustained translational capacity, while enrichment of the proteasome pathway (vra03050) suggests active protein quality control. Together, these processes ensure rapid synthesis of stress-responsive proteins and removal of damaged or misfolded proteins generated under saline stress191. Enrichment of secondary metabolite and alkaloid biosynthesis pathways like isoquinoline alkaloid (vra00950) and tropane, piperidine and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis (vra00960) enhanced production of protective metabolites that function as antioxidants, osmolytes, or signalling compounds, strengthening salt stress resilience192.

Conclusion

The present study has shown that solid matrix priming (SMP) with nano-chitosan effectively enhances salinity stress tolerance in mung bean seedlings through integrated physiological, biochemical, metabolic, and molecular regulation. Compared with hydro-priming and chitosan priming, nano-chitosan-based SMP showed superior improvement in germination percentage, germination index, mean germination rate, vigour index, and overall seedling growth under salinity stress conditions (0, 4, and 8 dS/m).

Proteomic profiling through SDS-PAGE and Orbitrap HR-LCMS identified several differentially expressed proteins. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analyses revealed that nano-chitosan priming activates interconnected functional networks associated with central carbon metabolism, photosynthesis, translation, vesicle trafficking, membrane transport, and proteostasis. This helps to maintain efficient energy production and redox balance under salinity stress. Cytoscape-based topological network analyses demonstrated that identified proteins formed highly interconnected functional modules, indicating coordinated metabolic reprogramming in response to salinity stress. MCODE states that nano-chitosan priming does not act through a single pathway but instead activates a complex regulatory network involving metabolic reprogramming, translational control, photosynthetic maintenance, membrane trafficking, and stress-protective chaperone systems. These coordinated responses together contribute to improved germination, biomass accumulation, and seedling establishment under salinity stress. Therefore, based on our previous and current studies, it can be stated that nano-chitosan-mediated SMP may serve as a promising approach for enhancing crop establishment and productivity in salt-affected agricultural systems.
