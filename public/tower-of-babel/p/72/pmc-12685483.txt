Effect of Spinal Versus General Anesthetic on 30‐Day Outcomes in Patients With Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Undergoing Hip Arthroplasty for Femoral Neck Fracture
Authors: David R Christian, Mark A Plantz, Manasa Pagadala, Isaac SontagMilobsky, Michael Peabody, Erik B Gerlach, David W Manning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPatients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and femoral neck fractures are at high risk for postoperative complications and mortality. One consideration to reduce risk is the type of anesthesia, although this has not been investigated. The purpose of this study was to compare 30‐day complications between use of general and spinal anesthetic in patients with COPD and femoral neck fractures who underwent hip arthroplasty.MethodsPatients with COPD treated with hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fractures were identified on the American College of Surgeons' National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS‐NSQIP) database between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2020. Demographics, patient variables, and surgical variables were recorded. Patients were divided into cohorts based on general or spinal anesthetic. Propensity score matching was used to match the two groups. Thirty‐day outcome measures were compared between groups using chi‐squared test. Logistic regression was used to assess for risk factors for 30‐day complications.ResultsFive thousand and forty patients with COPD were identified who underwent arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture—3800 with general anesthesia and 1240 patients with spinal anesthesia. The general anesthesia cohort had higher rates of diabetes (18.4% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.007), congestive heart failure (10.7% vs. 6.7%, p < 0.001), and chronic kidney disease requiring dialysis (2.6% vs. 1.5%. p = 0.019). After matching, the general anesthesia cohort had higher rates of mortality (8.4% vs. 5.8%, p = 0.042), nonhome discharge (85.5% vs. 79.2%, p < 0.001), and unplanned intubation (1.9% vs. 0.7%, p = 0.048). Logistic regression identified general anesthesia to be an independent risk factor for 30‐day mortality (RR 1.514 [1.022–2.245]), nonhome discharge (1.626 [1.237–2.138]), and unplanned intubation (RR 1.488 [1.012–2.187]).ConclusionsGeneral anesthesia is an independent risk factor for 30‐day mortality, nonhome discharge, and unplanned intubation in patients with COPD undergoing arthroplasty procedures for femoral neck fractures. If possible, spinal anesthetic should be considered as it may reduce the risk of complications in this patient population.Keywords: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, femoral neck fracture, general anesthesia, hip fracture, spinal anesthesia

Introduction

The majority of orthopedic procedures have a low risk of postoperative complications and mortality rates [1]. However, patients sustaining hip fractures have been identified as a population with significantly higher rates of postoperative complications, especially mortality [1, 2]. Hip fracture patients are typically elderly and have a high rate of medical comorbidities that contribute to their perioperative risk. In all patients with hip fractures undergoing surgical intervention, multiple risk factors for adverse events and mortality have been identified, including age > 70, congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), atrial fibrillation, and malignancy metastatic to bone [1].

Anesthetic type has been one area of interest to improve the safety of orthopedic procedures, especially total joint arthroplasty. In the setting of elective total joint arthroplasty, patients receiving regional anesthetic have been shown to have lower rates of surgical site infection, shorter hospital stays, and lower cardiac and pulmonary adverse events [3, 4, 5, 6]. These findings have led the majority of hip and knee arthroplasty surgeons to adopt using neuraxial anesthetic in total joint arthroplasty [7].

There has also been interest in the use of spinal anesthesia for patients undergoing surgery for hip fractures. When patients are able to tolerate necessary positioning, spinal anesthetic can be considered in these patients. Spinal anesthetic has the theoretical benefits of avoiding intubation, a lower risk of intraoperative hypotension, and avoiding neuroactive medications. However, while the total joint arthroplasty literature supports its use, there is inconclusive evidence for the effectiveness of spinal anesthesia to reduce postoperative complications and mortality in hip fracture patients [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Two prior randomized‐control trials have found no major differences in adverse events or early functional outcomes between spinal and general anesthesia for patients undergoing surgery for femoral neck or peritrochanteric femur fractures. However, these studies have not investigated arthroplasty procedures, nor have they investigated patients with underlying respiratory disease specifically [10, 12]. Prior research has shown that pulmonary vascular resistance increases significantly both during and after arthroplasty prosthesis insertion, particularly with the use of cement. This can lead to increased right heart strain, which can be particularly troublesome in patients with underlying cardiac or respiratory issues. Furthermore, bone cement implantation syndrome (BCIS) remains a challenging respiratory complication in orthopedic surgery that is a unique complication from cemented arthroplasty.

Furthermore, patients with COPD who sustain hip fractures requiring surgical intervention have been shown to be at significantly higher rates of postoperative complications and mortality than controls, with these findings extending up to one year from the time of surgery [16, 17]. These studies report mortality rates up to 26% at the one‐year postoperative timepoint in patients with severe COPD who sustain hip fractures [17]. Due to the known increased risk of complications in this population, methods to reduce modifiable risk should be investigated. The effect of the type of anesthesia utilized in patients with COPD undergoing arthroplasty for displaced femoral neck fractures has not been investigated. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to compare (i) the mortality rate, (ii) the rate of pulmonary complications, and (iii) the incidence of 30‐day medical complications with the use of general versus spinal anesthesia for patients with COPD undergoing arthroplasty procedures for femoral neck fractures. We hypothesized that the use of general anesthetic would have higher rates of mortality, pulmonary complications, and other medical complications and would be identified as an independent risk factor for adverse events.

Methods

A retrospective cohort of patients undergoing hip arthroplasty (hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty) for femoral neck fractures was identified using the American College of Surgeons' National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS‐NSQIP) database between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2020 [18]. The data reported in the ACS‐NSQIP database has previously been described and validated [19, 20, 21]. Current procedural terminology (CPT) codes 27125, 27130, and 27236 were used to identify hip arthroplasty procedures along with International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision (ICD‐9) codes 820.00, 820.01, 820.02, 820.09, 820.8, and ICD‐10 codes S72: .001A, .002A, .009A, .011A, .012A, .019A, .021A, .022A, .023A, .024A, .025A, .026A, .031A, .032A, .033A, .034A, .035A, .036A, .041A, .042A, .043A, .044A, .045A, .046A for femoral neck fracture. From this cohort, patients with a diagnosis of COPD were subsequently identified. Cases with incomplete or missing data were not included in the final cohort. Cases with concurrent surgical procedures for other injuries were excluded from this analysis.

Patient data and outcome measures are reported by all ACS‐NSQIP‐participating institutions using a standardized protocol to facilitate reliable comparisons across different hospitals [18]. There are dedicated surgical clinical reviewers at all participating institutions who are trained to abstract data from medical charts [18]. ACS‐NSQIP also conducts interrater reliability audits at participating sites to assess data quality and reliability, with an acceptable disagreement rate of 5% or less [18]. Prior literature has demonstrated excellent interrater reliability scores, with overall disagreement rates less than 1.6% [21].

Patients were divided into two cohorts based on the use of general or spinal anesthetic, as reported in the ACS‐NSQIP database. General anesthesia was defined as drug‐induced loss of consciousness, loss of response to pain, and muscle relaxation requiring breathing assistance (i.e., ventilation). Spinal anesthesia was defined as any case where local anesthetic was injected into the subarachnoid space preoperatively. A summary of the different anesthesia modalities—and the proportion of cases with those types of anesthesia—is provided in the supplemental material (Table S1). Of note, only general and spinal anesthesia were compared for the purposes of this study.

Demographic data and patient variables including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), medical comorbidities, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification were recorded. Medical comorbidities of interest included COPD, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, ascites, CHF, renal failure, dialysis use, chronic steroid use, and bleeding disorders. Surgical variables including anesthesia type and operating room duration were collected. Operating room duration was subsequently reported into three categories: < 60 min, 60–120 min, > 120 min.

Propensity score matching was used to match the two groups—general or spinal anesthesia—based on age, sex, BMI, the aforementioned medical comorbidities, ASA classification, and operating room duration. All variables were matched exactly between the two cohorts in a 1:1 manner. After matching, the same 30‐day outcome measures of interest were again compared between groups. The 30‐day outcome measures of interest included hospital readmission, reoperation, nonhome discharge, mortality, medical complications, and surgical complications. These various outcome measures were compared between groups. Surgical complications of interest included superficial surgical site infection, wound infection, deep surgical site infection, dehiscence, and bleeding. Medical complications of interest included pneumonia, unplanned intubation, pulmonary embolism, ventilator use > 48 h, renal insufficiency, renal failure, urinary tract infection, cerebrovascular accident, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, deep venous thromboembolism, systemic sepsis, and septic shock.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). Categorical variables were reported as numerical values and percentages of the total population or subgroup. Differences between groups for categorical variables were compared using chi‐squared tests, or Fisher's exact tests, when appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression was subsequently used to identify variables that were independently associated with the 30‐day outcome measures of interest after matching. All independent variables of interest were included in the multivariate logistic regression, including sex, age, BMI, the aforementioned medical comorbidities, ASA class, and operative time. Risk ratios and the associated 95% confidence intervals were reported for each variable that achieved statistical significance. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

The study involves the use of data already collected and reported in a deidentified manner via the ACS‐NSQIP database. No patient health information (PHI) was recorded for purposes of this study.

Results

A total of 6095 cases were screened and extracted from the ACS‐NSQIP database. A total of 5040 patients with COPD undergoing hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture were included in the final cohort. Figure 1 provides a flowchart that summarizes the total records screened, the excluded records with reasons for exclusion, and the records included in the final analyses both before and after propensity score matching. General anesthesia was utilized for 3800 patients and spinal anesthesia was used for 1240 patients. There was no difference in sex between groups (p = 0.616). Patients receiving spinal anesthetic tended to be older with a higher proportion of the cohort aged 80–89 (40.5% vs. 36.9%, p = 0.026) and aged > 90 (15.8% vs. 11.9%, p < 0.001). The general anesthesia cohort had a higher proportion of patients with Class I and Class II obesity. The general anesthesia group had higher rates of medical comorbidities including diabetes (18.4% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.007), CHF (10.7% vs. 6.7%, p < 0.001), hypertension (75.0% vs. 67.0%, p < 0.001), CKD requiring dialysis (2.6% vs. 1.5%, p = 0.019), and bleeding disorders (24.6% vs. 10.3%, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Discussion

The primary observation in this study was that the use of general anesthesia was an independent risk factor for adverse events in patients with COPD undergoing hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fractures. While the use of spinal anesthetic has previously been demonstrated to be advantageous in elective orthopedic procedures such as total hip arthroplasty with a reduction in deep surgical site infection rates, length of hospital stay, postoperative morbidity, 30‐day mortality, length of stay, and in‐hospital complications, the results in patients with hip fractures have been inconclusive [3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15].

COPD has been shown to be a significant risk factor for complications and mortality after orthopedic injuries and procedures. Dodd et al. evaluated risk factors for 30‐day mortality after operatively treated hip and pelvis fractures utilizing the ACS‐NSQIP database and found COPD to be a significant risk factor with an odds ratio of 1.34 (1.14–1.58) [2]. Similarly, de Luise and colleagues evaluated COPD as a risk factor for mortality after hip fracture in the Danish population‐based healthcare databases and found patients with COPD had a higher risk of mortality up to one year from the injury with a relative risk ratio of 1.71 [16]. Cha et al. also studied the mortality risk associated with COPD in hip fracture patients and found that COPD increased the mortality risk by 1.6 times at 30 days postoperative and 1.7 times at one‐year [17].

30‐Day Mortality

In the present study, we isolated patients with COPD and femoral neck fractures undergoing hip arthroplasty for population homogeneity. After multivariate logistic regression analysis to control for confounding variables, use of general anesthesia was found to be an independent risk factor for 30‐day mortality, nonhome discharge, unplanned intubation, surgical complications, and bleeding requiring transfusion. These findings are in agreement with the prior database and retrospective studies evaluating all hip fracture patients and anesthetic type; however, they differ from the randomized controlled trials which showed no difference in outcomes. However, this study is unique in that it specifically evaluated patients with COPD and femoral neck fractures, while prior studies evaluated all hip fracture patients. It is possible that anesthesia type is more impactful in this patient population and was therefore not observed in previous studies. In this patient population, these findings support the use of spinal anesthetic to reduce the risk of perioperative complications.

In our cohort, general anesthesia was associated with a higher mortality rate than spinal anesthesia—even after propensity score matching between groups. Several studies have investigated the rate of mortality and other major complications after the use of spinal or general anesthesia in hip fracture patients with varied results. Several retrospective studies, database, and registry studies have demonstrated reductions in postoperative complications and mortality rates with the use of spinal anesthetic. Pass et al. utilized a European database, the Registry for Geriatric Trauma (ATR‐DGU), and found higher in‐hospital and 120‐day mortality rates with the use of general anesthetic [15]. In their study, general anesthetic also had a negative impact on walking ability 7 days after surgery with a longer hospital stay [15]. Weinstein et al. utilized the ACS‐NSQIP database and found general anesthesia to be associated with a higher incidence of 30‐day mortality, myocardial infarction, and stroke in hip fracture patients [13]. Similarly, Neuman et al. performed a retrospective review and found general anesthesia to be associated with higher mortality and pulmonary complications [11]. In contrast, Morgan et al. specifically investigated patients with COPD undergoing hip fracture surgery with the use of general versus spinal anesthetic. In their subgroup analysis, they did not find any reduction in 30‐ or 90‐day mortality rates with the use of spinal anesthetic [8]. However, it should be noted that this study included all hip fracture surgeries—including closed reduction and open reduction internal fixation—not specifically arthroplasty procedures, which is the focus of our study. Additionally, multiple randomized controlled trials have been performed which did not demonstrate a difference in 60‐day or one‐year mortality or reduction in postoperative delirium between anesthesia types [9, 12, 14].

Pulmonary Complications

In our study, spinal anesthesia was associated with an increased rate of unplanned intubation after controlling for potential confounding variables. Other studies on the topic have reported conflicting results. Morgan et al. found that patients undergoing spinal anesthesia were at increased risk of developing a postoperative pneumonia or venous thromboembolism [8]. Additionally, Neuman et al. performed a retrospective review and found general anesthesia to be associated with higher rates of pulmonary complications [11]. In contrast to these studies, Li et al. demonstrated that regional and general anesthesia had comparable rates of cardiovascular, respiratory, and other complications in a large, randomized trial of patients undergoing surgery for femoral neck or peritrochanteric hip fractures [12]. However, it should be noted that these surgeries included closed and open reduction with internal fixation and did not investigate arthroplasty, which is the focus of our study.

Other Medical Complications

Our study also investigated the rate of various other 30‐day medical complications between the general and spinal anesthesia groups. In our cohort, spinal anesthesia was associated with a higher rate of nonhome discharge but otherwise similar rates of other medical complications. Morgan et al. queried the Nottingham hip fracture database and did not observe a difference in mortality but found hip fracture patients receiving a spinal anesthetic were less likely to require transfusion or develop a urinary tract infection [8]. Additionally, there was a relatively low rate of underlying respiratory disease in this cohort. One randomized trial, however, demonstrated decreased pain scores at 24‐h postoperative and reduced prescription pain medication use at 60 postoperative [10]. These varied results have led to continued investigation into the utility of neuraxial anesthesia in this high‐risk patient population.

The strength of this study is that it provides a large sample of validated data with significant statistical power to compare relatively less common complications, such as 30‐day mortality between the two groups of interest. Additionally, the study investigates a specific patient population (i.e., patients with COPD) who may be at risk for specific medical complications after surgery. This specific research question has not been investigated to this extent in prior literature to our knowledge. Lastly, the use of propensity score matching helps control for potential confounding variables between the two patient cohort groups.

There are several limitations to this study. COPD was utilized as reported in the ACS‐NSQIP database and, therefore, the severity of COPD was not able to be explored as a variable when evaluating perioperative outcomes. It is possible that the severity of COPD could influence decision making regarding decision making for the type of anesthetic to administer. Additionally, there were differences in demographics and medical comorbidities between groups, which could act as confounding variables when evaluating rates of medical and surgical complications. However, we performed multivariate logistic regression analysis to control for possible confounders, and general anesthetic was found to be an independent risk factor for 30‐day mortality, nonhome discharge, unplanned intubation, and bleeding requiring transfusion. Finally, the present study is a retrospective observational study; therefore, it is limited by what is included in the medical record and subsequent reporting to the ACS‐NSQIP database. Furthermore, the results of this study are applicable to COPD patients undergoing hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fracture and cannot be extrapolated to other types of procedures or those where avoiding general anesthesia is not feasible.

Conclusion

In patients with COPD undergoing hip arthroplasty for femoral neck fractures, general anesthesia is associated with a higher incidence of 30‐day complications relative to spinal anesthetic. Specifically, general anesthesia was identified as an independent risk factor for 30‐day mortality, nonhome discharge, and unplanned intubation. If medically safe and patients can tolerate necessary positioning, spinal anesthetic should be considered to potentially reduce the risk of complications for patients with COPD undergoing arthroplasty for femoral neck fractures.
