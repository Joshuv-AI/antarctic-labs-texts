Metabolomics data of root and stem tissues in five-year-old Sophora flavescens
Authors: Ning Zhang, Juan-Fang Li, Si-Chen Zhao, Xiao-Yu Wang, Min Li, Fei-Li Xu, Xiao-Qing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset provides full metabolomic data for root and stem samples of Sophora flavescens, a commonly used Chinese medicine. Metabolites were extracted separately from root and stem samples and subjected to high-resolution LC-MS in positive and negative modes. Raw spectral data were used for peak detection, alignment, normalization, and metabolite annotation using established metabolomic pipelines and public databases. Principal component analysis and partial least-squares discriminant analysis were used to obtain tissue-specific metabolic profiles. Differential metabolite analysis was conducted to determine the metabolites that were more enriched and depleted in roots and stems, and pathway enrichment analysis was performed to determine the metabolic pathways related to these metabolites. The dataset contains raw LC–MS files, processed feature tables, annotated metabolite lists, and statistical analysis results in a standard format. In these data, there are important materials for studying the tissue-specific metabolism different of S. flavescens, as well as for conducting research on the different distribution of medicine materials and studying biosynthesis routes and quality identification of medicinal plants. The same will be helpful for comparative metabolomics, integrative multi-omics, and natural products.Keywords: Metabolomics, Sophora flavescens, Medicinal plants, Tissue-specific metabolism, LC-MS

Background

Sophora flavescens is a perennial medicinal plant that is commonly used in traditional Chinese medicine[1], and its roots are the main parts used in clinical practice, which contain a variety of secondary metabolites such as bioactive alkaloids and flavonoids, and have multiple pharmacological effects such as anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and immune regulation [2]. The effects and quality of S. flavescens originating from medical materials are closely related to every kind of form and position of internal plant metabolites [3].

Previous phytochemical and metabolomic studies have primarily focused on the roots of S. flavescens; in contrast, systematic comparisons between root and stem tissues, particularly at defined developmental stages, have been limited [3]. Moreover, the accumulation and tissue specificity of secondary metabolites may vary with plant age, thereby influencing both pharmacological activity and quality. Five-year-old plants are commonly harvested in traditional medicinal practice; however, comprehensive metabolomic datasets characterizing tissue-specific metabolic profiles are scarce.

Previous studies on phytochemicals and metabolomics have mainly considered the roots of S. flavescens, and there has been no systematic comparison of the roots and stems until the progressing stage [3]. In addition, the accumulation and tissue specificity of secondary metabolites may change at different stages of plant growth, which will have a joint effect on both pharmacological activity and quality. Although five-year-old plants are often used in medicine, there are no complete metabolomic datasets for tissue-specific metabolic profiles.

To address these deficiencies, we collected an LC-MS-based metabolomics dataset from the roots and stems of five-year-old S. flavescens. This dataset was used to capture tissue-dependent metabolic differentiation and to refer to metabolic and biosynthetic pathway resources with bioactives. Openly available data support the reuse of comparative metabolomics, biosynthetic pathway exploration, and integrative multi-omics studies, thus promoting research on natural products, the biology of medicinal plants, and the quality assessment of traditional Chinese medicinal materials.

Data Description

Metabolomics data were obtained from the roots and stems of 5-year-old Sophora flavescens. To ensure comparability of the biological samples, strict position repetitiveness of the tissue sample was achieved. Samples were taken from aboveground stem Sections 3 cm above the root-shoot junction for the stem samples, and root samples were taken from the main taproot at a depth of approximately 2 cm below the soil surface. Three replicates of the tissue were collected independently; thus, six samples (three root and three stem samples) were obtained. All samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen after harvesting and stored at −80 °C until further metabolite extraction.

Untargeted metabolomics was performed using high-resolution LC-MS. The raw data contained full-scan MS in both the positive and negative ionization modes. Metabolite features were extracted, aligned, and normalized to obtain a quantitative abundance matrix of metabolites for each sample.

PCA and Venn diagram analyses were conducted to determine major metabolic differences between the stems and roots of 5-year-old Sophora flavescens. In the principal component analysis (PCA) score chart, the samples from the root (high group) and stem (control group) tissues were well separated along PC1, which contributed 67.10% to the total variance, indicating clear tissue-specific metabolic differences. PC2 explained 12.10% of the total variance.

The Venn diagram further indicates the number of metabolites shared between the two tissues. A total of 3482 metabolites were shared by the stem and root tissues, and 422 metabolites were uniquely identified in the control group, whereas 920 metabolites were uniquely identified in the high group.

To understand the metabolic differences between the roots and stems of 5-year-old Sophora flavescens, the root tissues were selected as the secondary metabolite high group, and the stem tissues were chosen as the control group. Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were based on the criteria of P < 0.05 and OPLS-DA VIP>1, and combined with the fold change and statistical significance. The results showed that, compared with stem tissues, 503 metabolites were significantly upregulated and 1253 metabolites were significantly downregulated in the root tissues, while 2531 metabolites showed no significant difference. These results indicate that there were some differences in metabolic differentiation between the root and stem tissues of Sophora flavescens, and a large number of metabolic products showed obvious tissue-specific accumulation characteristics.

To further study the biological functions of DAMs between root and stem tissues of 5-year-old Sophora flavescens, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted, and the main metabolic pathways were visualized. As shown in supplementary table 1, 78 KEGG pathways were enriched, of which 23 were significantly enriched (P < 0.05).

The most significantly enriched pathways were most closely related to secondary metabolism and physiological regulation of plants. Specifically, the enrichment significance of flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (map00944) was the highest (P = 5.847E-9), with 17 DAMs involved, followed by isoflavonoid biosynthesis (map00943, P = 1.683E-7, 15 DAMs) and flavonoid biosynthesis (map00941, P = 2.148E-7, 16 DAMs). These three pathways are the three main branches of the phenylpropanoid metabolic network, suggesting that flavonoid-related secondary metabolic products are the main differences in metabolic functions between roots and stems. Tyrosine metabolism (map00350, P = 5.951E-5, 13 DAMs) and linoleic acid metabolism (map00591, P = 4.394E-6, 9 DAMs) were also significantly enriched, suggesting their involvement in tissue-specific metabolic adaptations.

In terms of secondary metabolite biosynthesis, pathways such as stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, and gingerol biosynthesis (map00945, P = 0.02806, four DAMs), cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis (map00073, P = 0.03619, four DAMs), and biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites (map00999, P = 0.0102, 14 DAMs) were significantly enriched, which is consistent with the experimental design that roots serve as the secondary metabolite high-expression group. For primary metabolism and signal transduction, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (map00010, P = 0.01398, four DAMs), plant hormone signal transduction (map04075, P = 0.005629, four DAMs), zeatin biosynthesis (map00908, P = 0.03495, five DAMs), and ABC transporters (map02010, P = 0.02898, 12 DAMs) were significantly enriched, implying that metabolic differentiation between roots and stems is accompanied by coordinated regulation of energy metabolism, hormone signaling, and metabolite transport.

Other significantly enriched pathways included phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (map00400, P = 0.005332, 6 DAMs), lysine degradation (map00310, P = 0.01541, 7 DAMs), alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (map00592, P = 0.01629, 6 DAMs), and galactose metabolism (map00052, P = 0.02, 6 DAMs), which collectively constitute the metabolic network underlying the tissue-specific accumulation of metabolites in Sophora flavescens. In contrast, pathways such as cyanoamino acid metabolism (map00460), carotenoid biosynthesis (map00906), and biotin metabolism (map00780) showed no significant enrichment (P > 0.05) with only one or two DAMs involved, indicating that these pathways have a limited contribution to the metabolic differences between roots and stems.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Plant materials and sampling

To study the differences in flavonoids and alkaloids in the roots and stems of the plants, the root and stem parts of S. flavescens grown under uniform growth conditions were sampled in July 2025. Three biological replicates were collected for each tissue. In Qin Chakou Village, Luonan County, Shaanxi Province, China, S. flavescens plants grow immediately after planting. The cells were rinsed with PBS, quickly immersed in liquefied nitrogen for fixation, and stored at − 80 °C for metabolomic analysis [3].

UHPLC-Q exactive analysis

A 100 mg tissue sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifugation tube containing grinding beads. Then, 800 µL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1 (v: v) containing four internal standards 0.02 mg/ml l-2- chlorophenyl alanine, etc.). These internal standards were used to correct for metabolite extraction efficiency and instrumental variation. The sample solution was homogenized in a cryogenic tissue grinder for 6 min (parameters: −10 °C, 50 Hz) and then sonicated in a cryogenic environment for 30 min (parameters: 5 °C, 40 kHz). The sample was then allowed to stand at −20 °C for 30 min and centrifuged for 15 min (4 °C, 13,000 g). The supernatant was transferred to an injection vial with an insert for further analysis.

The identification of metabolites is accomplished through a rigorous and multi-step strategy, which is based on high-resolution LC-MS data and carried out in both positive and negative ionization modes.LC-MS analysis was performed using a UHPLC-Q Exactive system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a BEH C18 column with a sample injection volume of 3 µL [4]. The mobile phase system comprised mobile phases A and B: mobile phase A was an aqueous solution containing 2% acetonitrile (ACN) with 0.1% formic acid (FA) added to optimize separation efficiency, and mobile phase B was an acetonitrile solution containing 0.1% formic acid. Throughout the chromatographic analysis, the flow rate was maintained at 0.40 mL/min. The column temperature was precisely controlled at 40 °C using a column oven to ensure separation efficiency and result stability.

After LC-MS detection, the raw data files were imported into the Progenesis QI analysis system. Preprocessing steps, including filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, were performed to generate a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity. Metabolites were identified by matching MS/MS spectra with the Plant Metabolite Database (MJDBPM). After merging and deduplicating the chromatographic datasets, they were uploaded to the Majorbio cloud platform for subsequent analysis [5]. Preprocessing workflow: First, variables with > 20% missing values across samples were excluded. Next, the peak intensities were normalized. Then, data points with an RSD > 30% from QC samples were removed. Finally, a log10 transformation was applied to obtain the data matrix.

The data matrix was processed using R and MetaboAnalyst[6], and KEGG enrichment analysis of metabolites was performed [7]. Apply PCA and OPLS-DA to the preprocessed data matrix using the ropls package [8]. Screening for significantly different metabolites based on VIP values and p-values (VIP > 1 and p < 0.05). Fisher's exact test was conducted using the scipy.stats module to determine associations with experimental treatments[9].
