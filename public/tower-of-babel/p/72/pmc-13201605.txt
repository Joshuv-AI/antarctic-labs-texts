Perspectives from machine learning and multi-omics to decoding the effects of VDAC2 malignant subsets on tumor evolution
Authors: Jianing Yan, Jingzhi Wang, Haotian Dong, Guoliang Ye, Yongfu Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVDAC2’s known role in cancer and immune regulation via enhancing the CD8+ T cell-mediated killing, and it is worth systematically digging out the role of VDAC2 in pan-cancer based on this research. Bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomic analyses were utilized to explore the role of VDAC2 from multiple perspectives in pan-cancers. RT-PCR, cell co-culture, CCK-8 assay, Transwell invasion assays, and ELISA were performed to validate the expression level and biological function. VDAC2 was upregulated in the majority of pan-cancers, and functional enrichment analyses displayed that VDAC2 may take part in the biological progress of energy metabolism, mitochondrial damage and cell proliferation. The landscape of VDAC2 expression and immune infiltration was constructed, and the VDAC2-BAK1-IFNγ pathway was identified in digestive cancer. VDAC2 had the potential to serve as a novel prognostic, screening cancer indicator and immune therapeutic target sensitive to various drugs. Overexpression of VDAC2 significantly promoted gastric cancer cell proliferation, invasion and immune invasion, as validated in vitro experiments. In short, our pan-cancer analysis constructed a comprehensive landscape of VDAC2’s oncogenic role, establishing VDAC2 + -BAK1-IFNγ as an important pathway in tumor progression and immune evasion. VDAC2 emerges not only as a valuable prognostic biomarker but also as a promising novel therapeutic target.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Voltage-Dependent Anion Channel 2 (VDAC2) is one of the relevant components of the outer mitochondrial membrane, playing a crucial role in recruiting BAK to mitochondria and restraining its activity at mitochondria1. As an important central regulator governing both cellular metabolism and apoptosis, VDAC2 occupies a critical position at the intersection of cancer and tumor microenvironment. In cancer pathogenesis, VDAC2 is frequently implicated in facilitating the characteristic metabolic shift towards heightened glycolytic flux (the Warburg effect). Meanwhile, it confers resistance to apoptotic cell death, thereby promoting tumor cell survival and proliferation. Conversely, dysregulation or dysfunction of VDAC is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to mitochondrial impairment, which manifests as disrupted energy metabolism and perturbed calcium homeostasis. Ultimately, it can activate the pathogenic cell death pathways2.

Recently, a novel study pointed out that VDAC2 deficiency conspicuously elicited uncontrolled IFNγ-induced BAK activation and mitochondrial damage for improving cancer therapy via enhancing the CD8+ T cell-mediated killing in vitro and in vivo, which certainly increased our interest in exploring the novel roles of VDAC2 in cancers3. According to these evidence, bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomic analyses were utilized to explore the role of VDAC2 from multiple perspectives in pan-cancers (Fig. 1). Collectively, our study systematically explored and delineated the common oncogenic characteristics of VDAC2 across diverse tumor types and further identified associated drugs for tumor therapy and successfully validated its biofunction role in gastric cancer. Compared with previous studies, our research is more comprehensive and provides an overall analysis of the role of VDAC2 in solid tumors from a broad cancer perspective.

Results

Systematic analysis of VDAC2 expression and localization

Expression level of VDAC2 was detected using TCGA and GTEx database, and VDAC2 was overexpressed in the majority of cancers in Fig. 2A, B (Fig. 2A, B). GSVA analysis showed VDAC2 was closely linked to several important oncogenic activities (Fig. 2C). High-expression VDAC2 was focused on C1, C2, and C3 immune subtype especially in C2 (IFN-γ dominant type), suggesting its association with IFN-γ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2D). The mutation data of VDAC2 from cBioPortal were displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1 A (Fig S1A). Then, we compared the expression level of VDCA2 and MSI groups, and the results found that higher VDAC2 mainly existed in MSI-H of COAD, STAD, and MSI-L of UCEC (Fig. 2E). Finally, the expression and localization of VDAC2 were decoded at spatial transcriptomics resolution (Fig. 2F and Fig S1B). All of these results proved that upregulated VDAC2 (VDAC2+) was focused in the tumor zone and correlated with several infiltrated immune cells.

Biofunction enrichment analysis of VDAC2

Enrichment analysis was employed by both GO and KEGG methods, revealing significant enrichment in various pathways (Fig. 3A). On the other hand, the GSEA analysis showed enrichment in pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, Myc targets V1, Myc targets V2 (Fig. 3B), indicating VDAC2 may take part in the progress of energy metabolism, mitochondrial damage and cell proliferation. The relationship between VDAC2 and immune genes, such as chemokine, chemokine receptor, immunoinhibitor, immunostimulatory, and MHC, was detected as Fig. 3C. This evidence pointed out that VDAC2 played a critical role in tumor development progress.

Interactions between VDAC2+ malignant cells and immune cells

As the associations between VDAC2+ and biological functions mentioned before, the immune cell infiltration landscape was further revealed using TIMER (Fig. 4A). Obviously, VDAC2+ was associated with lower CD8+ T cell and higher Th1, Th2 cell infiltration, suggesting its roles in tumor immune invasion. Moreover, VDAC2+ malignant cells exhibited more intense incoming, outgoing signals in cell communications than VDAC2− counterparts (Fig. 4B, C). Analysis of the immune cycle implied that VDAC2+ decreased cancer cell killing and increased immune invasion in colon cancer, esophageal cancer, rectal cancer, and gastric cancer (Fig. 4D, F). The lower expression level of IFN-γ also correlated to higher level of VDAC2 in gastric cancer (Fig. 4E, G). The above results strongly illustrated that VDAC2+ interacted with lower CD8+ T cells and triggered immune invasion.

VDAC2 + -BAK1-IFNγ pathways in tumor-immune microenvironment

As mentioned before, novel research revealed that IFNγ stimulation increased BIM, BID and BAK expression, and VDAC2 can lead to IFNγ-induced BAK activation and mitochondrial damage. Hence, we further estimated the expression of BAK1 in pan-cancer (Fig. 5A), in which we found that BAK1 was elevated in most cancer types. The expression landscape of BAK1 in immune cells was displayed in Fig S2. Then, co-expression analyses of VDAC2/BAK1, BAK1/IFNγ in digestive tumors were performed, as shown in Fig. 5B–E. Obviously, BAK1 was positively related to VDAC2 in colon cancer, esophageal cancer, rectal cancer, and gastric cancer. Higher level of IFNγ was prominently associated with BAK1 in colon cancer, esophageal cancer, rectal cancer, except gastric cancer (Fig. 5B–E). This slight difference caught our attention, which needed us for further validation.

Analysis of upstream/downstream regulatory factors of VDAC2

As elucidated in the pan-cancer expression profile of VDAC2 through integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, we employed a multi-tiered approach to identify its upstream as well as downstream modulators. KnockTF 2.0 database was utilized to screen the most upregulated/downregulated 25 TFs and our results showed that PTTG1 was recognized as the most significant activator TFs and NFATC2 was identified as the most repressive TFs (Fig. 6A). Then, promoter methylation level was detected in digestive tumors, which illustrated that VDAC2 correlated to the mean value of promoter methylation level in colon cancer and rectal cancer (Fig. 6B). The methylation level in different sites of each tumor were shown in Fig. 6C. StarBase 2.0 database was used to identify the potential RBPs, miRNAs interacted to VDAC2, which found that hsa-miR-541-3p, hsa-miR-103a-3p, hsa-miR-212-3p, hsa-miR-107, hsa-miR-132-3p, hsa-miR-654-5p, were high-confidence miRNAs targeted to VDAC2 and HNRNPC, HNRNPM, RBMX, DDX54, DHX9, PCBP2, YBX1, RNPS1, ALYREF, G3BP1 were top 10 high-confidence integrated RBPs to VDAC2. Moreover, TCGA SpliceSeq was applied for the investigation of cross-tumor and tumor-normal alterations in the splicing patterns of VDAC2. The result suggested that the alternate promoter in Exon2 was the most likely splicing event (Fig. 6D).

Prognostic prediction values of VDAC2 in pan-cancer

As the differential expression level of VDAC2 in pan-cancer, VDAC2’s prognostic values were so interesting to us. The Kaplan–Meier Plotter tool was used to analyze the overall survival time of patients with different expression levels of VDAC2 and CD8+T cells. Notably, the tumors associated with high VDAC2 expression were linked to poor prognosis and could be partially reversed with high CD8+T cells (Fig. 7). External validations using other datasets were shown in Fig S2. All of this evidence implied VDAC2 had the potential to be a novel prognostic biomarker.

Analysis of therapeutic target and drug sensitivity of VDAC2

EXtreme Sum (XSum) algorithm was performed to filter potential drugs and molecules as effective therapeutic drugs by the Connectivity Map (Cmap) database. The results indicated that STOCKN.35874, MS.275, W.13 were the top 3 applicable drugs via differential expression level of VDAC2 (Fig. 8A). Then, the TIDE database was used to estimate the anticancer immune response of VDAC2. 25 immune checkpoint blockade sub-cohorts and 7 genes with previously reported association with tumor immune evasion were input to compare the anticancer immune response with VDAC2. The results displayed that VDAC2 had a strongly anticancer immune response in several tumors, especially in Glioblastoma (AUC = 1.00, Fig. 8B). GDSC and CTRP databases showed the relationship between overexpression of VDAC2 and resistance to various therapeutic drugs and agents. (Fig. 8C, D).

Validation of differential expression, biological function, and clinical significance of VDAC2 in GC

Finally, the expression level and biofunction of VDAC2 were validated in GC. The results of RT-PCR displayed that VDAC2 was significantly upregulated in the GC cell line and tissue (Fig. 9A, B). The ROC curve of VDAC2 was shown in Fig. 8C with a satisfied AUC (0.808), with a satisfied diagnostic sensitivity and specificity were 86.67% and 66.67%, cut-off line = 10.14, respectively (Fig. 9C). As the prominent expression level of VDAC2, AGS was chosen for further functional experiments. VDAC2 expression in AGS expressing shRNAs against VDAC2 or nontargeting shRNA (shNT), overexpressing VDAC2 or control vector was detected by RT-PCR (Fig. 9D). The expression of BAK1 was also detected by RT-PCR using the above-mentioned cell lines, and it demonstrated that BAK1 was regulated by VDAC2 (Fig. 9E).

A, B RT-PCR showed that VDAC2 was upregulated in GC cells (A) and tissues (B). C ROC curve showed that VDAC2 can be a promising GC screening biomarker. D: RT-Overexpression, a downregulated AGS cell line was constructed, as proven via RT-PCR. E The expression level of BAK1 was regulated by VDAC2.

The results of CCK-8 indicated that upregulated VDAC2 prominently promoted GC cell proliferation (Fig. 10A, B). Likewise, overexpression of VDAC2 also stimulated cell invasion, as revealed by the transwell assay (Fig. 10C, D). Then, co-culture was performed using AGS-shRNA, AGS-OE and CD8T+ cells. IFN-γ from culture supernatants was measured by ELISA (Fig. 10E, F), which directly displayed that decreased IFN-γ was associated with upregulated VDAC2-BAK1 axis. All of these experiments revealed the important roles of VDAC2 in GC, which can be a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

Discussion

VDAC2 is a pivotal protein in mitochondrial outer membrane permeability, which has been implicated in momentous cellular processes such as calcium signaling, metabolism and apoptosis. While its roles in specific cancer types have been sporadically reported, it still lacks a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis. Overall, the present research conducted the first extensive multi-omics investigation of VDAC2 across pan-cancer types, utilizing data from TCGA, GTEx, and several public databases. Our findings robustly establish upregulated VDAC2 as a significantly dysregulated gene with strong prognostic value, uncovering its multifaceted role in tumorigenesis, immune modulation, and therapeutic response.

Our initial analysis revealed a consistent pattern of VDAC2 mRNA and protein overexpression in the majority of solid tumors compared to normal adjacent tissues. A particularly intriguing finding from our study is that VDAC2+ associates with several immune subtypes, especially in C2 (IFN-γ dominant type), which suggests its association with IFN-γ and CD8+ T cell and is in accordance with the results of recent research3. Further immune infiltration analysis also indicates that VDAC2+ is associated with lower CD8+ T cell and higher Th1, Th2 cell infiltration, especially inhibited the function of immune cells to kill the cancer cells. This implied that tumors with elevated VDAC2 perhaps create an immunosuppressive niche, potentially through the secretion of metabolites or cytokines that hinder effective immune surveillance. It is crucial to interpret these results with caution because of the retrospective nature of the TCGA data and the high heterogeneity inherent in pan-cancer. Thus, according to the literature guidance, we constructed VDAC2 + -BAK1-IFNγ pathways in the tumor-immune microenvironment. Public data displayed that IFNγ exhibits strong correlations to colon cancer, esophageal cancer, rectal cancer, except gastric cancer (Fig. 4B–E). Hence, we performed further validation of the VDAC2 + -BAK1-IFNγ pathway in GC, and our results firstly proved the VDAC2 + -BAK1-IFNγ pathway in GC tumorigenesis, strongly supporting the perspective about VDAC2 as a dual-action target to overcome tumor immune evasion3. Meanwhile, Swapneel demonstrated that VDAC2 deletion in tumors enhanced their sensitivity to IFNγ through the release of mitochondrial DNA and activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes pathway, implying the critical roles of VDAC2-BAK14. Li identified that a novel molecule, WEHI-3773, can inhibit apoptosis mediated by BAX by blocking VDAC2-mediated BAX recruitment to mitochondria, which highlighted the coordination of BAX and BAK apoptotic activity through interaction with VDAC25. All of this evidence positions VDAC2 not only as a cell-intrinsic oncogene but also as a potential modulator of the tumor immune landscape, offering a novel rationale for combining VDAC2-targeting strategies with immunotherapy.

Our study also demonstrated a strong prospect for VDAC2 clinical application. VDAC2+ correlated with poor prognosis and could be partially reversed with high CD8+T cells, which indicated that VDAC2+ is an important prognostic risk factor. Wang pointed out that VDAC2 is associated with TNM stage and overall survival time in lung adenocarcinoma, which suggests VDAC2 is a promising prognostic indicator in cancers6. The ROC curve of VDAC2 in GC showed that VDAC2 has the potential to be a novel screening biomarker. The functional role of VDAC2 was further substantiated by drug sensitivity analysis, which revealed that high VDAC2 expression was associated with resistance to several conventional chemotherapeutics such as STOCKN.35874, MS.275, W.13. Similarly, it has been revealed that sulindac sulfone can directly bind to VDAC2 and suppress the mTORC1 pathway and induce G1 arrest in colon cancer7. These results present a promising strategy for personalized therapy, where VDAC2 expression can serve as a biomarker for selecting patients who might benefit from these targeted agents.

Despite these insights, our study still has some limitations. Given the size of the dataset, we are unable to present all the baseline data in the article. Fortunately, all the baseline data can be retrieved from the public dataset. In this study, we conducted a verification using a small sample in a single center and demonstrated a significant mean difference with a highly significant statistical significance. Nevertheless, large-scale studies are still worthy of being conducted in the future. Though we have validated the expression level and biofunction of VDAC2 in GC in vitro, further in vivo experimental validation is required to establish causal relationships between VDAC2 expression and the observed phenotypes in each cancer type. The precise mechanism by which VDAC2 + -BAK1-IFNγ pathways influence immune cell infiltration remains to be elucidated and is a compelling direction for future research.

To bridge the gap between bench and bedside, the clinical translation of this target necessitates further investigation. From a deployment standpoint, integrating VDAC2 and its model into the existing clinical workflow (e.g., health checkup package, follow-up monitoring system) would be essential to provide accurate decision support for clinicians without disrupting their routine. From a business standpoint, if the results of the multi-center study are outstanding, the test kits and large-scale hematological testing systems are worth developing and launching by molecular biology companies. Regional leading hospitals can also consider introducing this site-based testing to establish a comprehensive system for screening, diagnosis, treatment and prognosis monitoring for cancer patients. In conclusion, the innovative features of the present study offer a comprehensive landscape of VDAC2’s oncogenic role, establishing VDAC2 + -BAK1-IFNγ as an important pathway in tumor progression and immune evasion.

Methods

Public data sources and clinical samples

Total genomic expression profiles and corresponding clinical information of pan-cancer types were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://genome-cancer.ucsc.edu/), comprising normalized RNA-seq data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) data portal (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/index.html). The Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) online database was used to acquire protein immunohistochemistry data. The relationship between VDAC2 expression and tumor mutation burden (TMB) or microsatellite instability (MSI) was analyzed using the UCSCXenaTools (v1.4.7) online tool (https://shiny.hiplot.com.cn/ucsc-xena-shiny)8. A t-test analysis was used to calculate the relevance. Clinical gastric cancer (GC) tissues with relevant clinical data and paired adjacent non-tumorous tissues (5 cm away from the edge of GC tissue) were gained from 30 newly diagnosed adult patients with advanced GC who underwent gastrectomy at The First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University, China, between 2021 and 2023. All patients took part in the study and underwent curative resection voluntarily. All procedures were performed based on the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study is approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University (No. KY2022001).

Machine learning and spatial transcriptomics processing

Our study employed the Sparkle database (https://www.grswsci.top) to conduct spatial transcriptome analysis on pan-cancer tissue sections using Cottrazm (Construction of Tumor Transition Zone Microenvironment) algorithm (v0.1.1)9,10. This method predicts the cell composition at each spatial point using an inverse convolution algorithm by integrating single-cell transcriptomic data, spatial transcriptomic data, and the positional information of spatial points. By integrating spatial transcriptomic data and HE staining images, we can precisely identify the tumor boundary that connects the malignant area and the non-malignant area. Combining single-cell transcriptomic data, spatial transcriptomic data, and the positional information of spatial points, we predicted the cell composition of each spatial point using the deconvolution algorithm. Using spatial transcriptomic data and single-cell transcriptomic data, we rebuilt the cell type-specific genomic expression profiles at the sub-spot resolution (sub-spot level).

Ridge regression is a regularization technique used in machine learning to address overfitting and multicollinearity issues. It falls under the category of L2 regularization. It achieves this goal by adding a penalty term proportional to the sum of the squares of the regression coefficients to the loss function of the standard linear regression.

Biological function annotation

The “ClusterProfiler” R package (v4.0) was utilized to annotate the functions of differentially expressed genes to systematically assess their functional associations. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment pathways with P values and q-values less than 0.05 were considered as significant statistical difference categories. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) is performed using Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp)11. During this analysis, we calculated the normalized enrichment score (NES) for each gene set and conducted significance tests and multiple hypothesis tests on these scores. These statistical tests help to evaluate the enrichment degree of the gene sets between the high-expression group and the low-expression group, thereby identifying the biological processes or metabolic pathways that show significant differences between the two sets of samples. Different functional statuses of 14 tumor cell types were distinguished according to the CancerSEA database12. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed via the GSVA algorithm (v1.46.0) to thoroughly score each gene set in order to estimate the potential changes in biological functions across various samples13. Genomic mutation data were obtained from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/), an online tool for decoding pan-cancer genomics. Cellchat software (v1.4.0) was applied to analyze the cell-cell communication status14. Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) was used to correct P values.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

The TIMER 2.0 database (http://timer.cistrome.org/) was utilized to evaluate the relationship between the expression level of VDAC2 and immune cell infiltration15. Six immune subtypes were applied to divide the TCGA samples for further analysis, including C1 (Wound Healing), C2 (IFN-γ Dominant), C3 (Inflammatory), C4 (Lymphocyte Depleted), C5 (Immunologically Quiet), C6 (TGF-β Dominant)16. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to calculate the relevance.

Upstream/downstream regulatory network

KnockTF 2.0 (http://www.licpathway.net/KnockTF/index.html) was a database that contained expression profiles following the knockdown of transcription factors (TF)17. Methylation level analysis includes 1st Exon (the first exon), 5’UTR (5’ untranslated region [UTR]), TSS1500 (from −200 to −1500 bp upstream of TSS), and TSS200 (from −200 bp upstream of TSS), using boxplot, visualized the methylation level at each site. StarBase 2.0 database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/), an extensive atlas integrating precise RNA interactions, was used to explore the RNA-binding protein (RBP), microRNA interacting with VDAC218. The “Single Gene” button of the TCGASpliceSeq database (https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/TCGASpliceSeq) was used to identify all possible splicing events of a single gene. Hplot1 function of the fromto package was performed to draw a heatmap to visualize the pan-cancer splicing events19.

Clinical potential value evaluation

Kaplan Meier plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) was applied to assess the prognostic significance of VDAC2 expression20. The pRRophetic software package was mainly used to predict clinical chemotherapy responses according to gene expression levels21,22. The CTRP2.0 (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp.v2.1/) and PRISM Repurposing dataset (https://depmap.org/portal/prism/) were employed to estimate drug sensitivity by the R software package “oncoPredict”(v0.2)23. Drug sensitivity analyses were conducted to identify compounds potentially effective against VDAC2-high tumors. Its algorithm was mainly based on the relationship between baseline gene expression levels and the sensitivity of in vitro cell lines to drugs to establish a ridge regression model. It was worth noting that we used the scale function to perform a z-score on the expression levels and drug sensitivity data, and identified the samples with z-score values greater than 3 and less than −3 as outliers and removed them. To prevent the deviation caused by extreme data, we used the scale function to perform a z-score on the expression levels and drug sensitivity data. Then, cor.test function was utilized to perform a Spearman correlation analysis to calculate the correlation between AUC and gene expression levels. The top 500 remarkably overexpressed or downregulated genes were considered as VDAC2-related signatures. Next, 1288 compound-related signatures extracted from the database website (https://www.pmgenomics.ca/bhklab/sites/default/files/downloads), were used to compute the matching score and visualized by pHeatmap package (v1.0.12) in R software24,25. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to calculate the relevance.

Cell culture

Human GC cell line (HGC-27, AGS) and GSE-1 cell line were obtained from Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The human shNT vector, VDAC2-specific shRNA vectors, VDAC2-expressing vector, as well as the control vector were purchased as Kai’s study from HANBIO, China26. Cells stably expressing shVDAC2 were gathered by puromycin treatment for selecting positive clones.

RNA extraction and PCR detection

Simplify, all RNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two micrograms of RNA were used as a template and reverse transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using a GoScript Reverse Transcription (RT) System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, PCR was carried out using GoTaq qPCR master mix (Promega). The cycling parameters were as follows: predenaturation at 95°C for 5 min, denaturation at 94°C for 15 s, annealing at 50°C for 30 s, extension at 72°C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the external reference control. Primer sequences were designed as following : VDAC2-Forward CGGAATTCATGGCGACCCACGGACAG, VDAC2-Reverse CTAGCTAGCAGCCTCCAACTCCAGGGCG; BAK1-Forward CATCAACCGACGCTATGACTC, BAK1-Reverse GTCAGGCCATGCTGGTAGAC; GAPDH-Forward, 5’-ACCCACTCCTCCACCTTTGAC-3,’ GAPDH-Reverse, 5’-TGTTGCTGTAGCCAAATTCGTT-3’. The ΔCt method was applied for quantifying, and a higher ΔCt value implied a decreased expression level.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates. After incubation of 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, 10 μl CCK-8 reagent (GLPBIO, CA, USA) was added to each well and cultivated at 37 °C for 2 h. The optional density (OD) of each well at 450 nm was investigated by a multifunctional microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Every sample had three replicates.

Transwell invasion assays

Transwell invasion assays were performed via transwell chambers (Corning, NY, USA) with 8.0 μm-pore polycarbonate membranes paved with matrigel mix (100 µg/ml) (BD, NJ, USA). Cells were diluted to 1 × 105/ml with serum-free RPMI 1640 medium. The bottom chamber was filled with 500 μl of RPMI 1640 medium added with 20% FBS. In turn, the upper chamber was supplemented to 200 μl of cell suspension. After incubation at 37°C for 24 h, the cells on the membrane of the lower surface were washed with PBS three times and then fixed with paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min. Each assay was repeated three times.

Co-culture of CD8+ T cells with GC cells

CD8+ T cells were sorted by density gradient centrifugation using the Rosette SepTM Human CD8+ T Cell Enrichment cocktail kit. The steps were as follows: 2 ml of peripheral blood was added with 100 μL of cocktail solution and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Then, an equal volume of PBS solution containing 2% fetal bovine serum was added and mixed. The diluted sample was added on top of the density gradient medium and centrifuged at 1200 g for 20 min. The purified CD8+ T cells were enriched between the plasma and the density gradient centrifugation solution. The enriched CD8+ T cells were washed using PBS solution containing 2% fetal bovine serum. Subsequently, the cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin. CD8+ T cells were seeded in the upper chamber of the Transwell insert via the Transwell system, and the insert was placed in a six-well plate containing AGS cells of each group for co-culture. Supernatant was collected for testing after 24 h of co-culture. IFN-γ from culture supernatants was analyzed with an IFN-γ Human Uncoated ELISA Kit (Invitrogen)27.

Statistical analysis

All of the statistical analyses in this study were implemented via R software (version 4.4.0) (https://www.r-project.org/) or Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) 22.0 software. GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) was utilized for figure drawing. All statistical tests with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).
