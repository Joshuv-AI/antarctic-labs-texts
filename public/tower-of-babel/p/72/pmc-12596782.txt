Encephaloduroarteriomyosynangiosis with the middle meningeal artery for proliferative cerebral angiopathy treatment: A case report
Authors: Karen Eloisa Xochipa-Ruiz, Fernando Lara-Torres, Martin Roberto Casas-Martínez, Diego Julián Alvis-Peña, Jorge Antonio Morales-Báez, Gustavo Melo-Guzmán
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Cerebral proliferative angiopathy (CPA) is a rare vascular disorder characterized by diffuse vascular networks intermingled with functional brain tissue, lacking a well-defined nidus. Unlike classical arteriovenous malformations, CPA often involves bilateral arterial supply, fragile neovessels, and hemodynamic instability, posing significant therapeutic challenges.Case Description: We report an 18-year-old female with progressive neurological decline, focal seizures, and imaging findings consistent with CPA. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed diffuse vascular anomalies in the left hemisphere, while digital subtraction angiography demonstrated bilateral internal carotid supply with no dominant feeder or nidus. Given her recurrent ischemic symptoms, indirect revascularization was indicated. Intraoperatively, superficial temporal artery thrombosis was identified, prompting an alternative arachnoid synangiosis using the middle meningeal artery (MMA). A tailored encephaloduroarteriomyosynangiosis was performed. At 12-month follow-up, the patient showed significant clinical and radiological improvement, including motor function recovery, seizure control, and reduced cortical congestion on imaging.Conclusion: This case highlights the feasibility of synangiosis using the MMA as an alternative donor artery for indirect cerebral revascularization in CPA when standard extracranial vessels are compromised. Surgical revascularization may offer functional recovery in selected cases with progressive symptoms. Given the uncertainty of long-term outcomes, prospective cohort studies may be required to validate this approach.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral proliferative angiopathy (CPA) is a rare cerebrovascular malformation that was frequently misclassified as a diffuse arteriovenous malformation (AVM) before its seminal description by Lasjaunias et al.[9] CPA represents approximately 3.4% of all cerebral AVMs,[9] yet its pathophysiology, natural history, and optimal treatment remain poorly understood.[18] CPA is characterized by a diffuse vascular network that is interspersed with normal brain parenchyma, the absence of dominant arterial feeders, transdural supply, and a disproportionately small venous drainage relative to lesion size.[1,9,16]

The term “proliferative” reflects the prevailing theory of ongoing neovascularization involving malformed vessels, often with progressive recruitment of meningeal arterial supply and, in some cases, transdural components. These newly formed vessels are structurally fragile and associated with an increased risk of hemorrhage.[19] In addition, angioectasia and arterial stenosis may coexist likely due to aberrant vessel wall proliferation, contributing further to hemodynamic instability.[9]

Epidemiologically, CPA affects a wide age range from infancy to the seventh decade of life, with a mean age at presentation onset of 25.35 ± 19.92 years.[9,17,18] Early reports described a female predilection (ratio 1.3–1.5:1),[12] although more recent Asian cohorts have noted a slight male preponderance (3:2).[12,13] The largest systematic review and meta-analysis to date, which included 115 patients from 50 studies, identified a slight female majority (ratio 1.38–1), with right-hemispheric involvement in 42% and supratentorial localization in 77% of cases.[8,18]

Clinically, CPA most commonly presents with seizures (34– 70%) and severe, often refractory headaches (40–46%).[5,9,12,18] Progressive neurological deficits such as hemiparesis, aphasia, and cognitive decline occur in 16–37% of patients, typically due to chronic hypoperfusion and arterial steal phenomena.[5,9,18] Intracranial hemorrhage is less frequent than in classical AVMs (12–18%), but when it occurs, the risk of rebleeding is high, up to 67%, suggesting intrinsic fragility of the neovascular network.[11,15,18]

Diagnosis is primarily established through digital subtraction angiography (DSA) which typically reveals a diffuse, poorly circumscribed nidus fed by multiple small-caliber arteries showing angioectatic patterns, avascular zones, possibly due to ischemic damage or vascular steal phenomenon, absence of early venous drainage, and persistent contrast puddling extending from the late arterial into early venous phases.[3,7,9,16]

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) often show a diffuse mesh of flow voids within preserved parenchyma. Advanced imaging, such as catheter cone-beam CT angiography, enhances visualization of vessel entanglement and confirms the lack of a compact nidus.[6,12,14] These imaging findings are essential to differentiate CPA from diffuse AVMs, capillary telangiectasias, and dural arteriovenous fistulas.

Treatment strategies remain controversial and must be individualized. The alternatives available consist of expectant management, surgical revascularization, endovascular therapy, or radiosurgery. Expectant management is often preferred for pediatric cases with high risks.[10] Surgical interventions are rare due to the typical involvement of eloquent cortex and preserved parenchyma, with only 17 reported cases to date, with inconsistent outcomes.[2,18] Surgical intervention is limited to indirect revascularization techniques, such as encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis (EDAS), encephalomyosynangiosis, or calvarial burr holes, intended to enhance perfusion and reduce ischemic symptoms.[6,7,16] These surgical interventions are generally reserved for intractable epilepsy, disabling headaches, or recurrent ischemic or hemorrhagic events.[1,7]

CASE PRESENTATION

History

We present the case of an 18-year-old female patient with no relevant perinatal history. At age 8, she experienced a right-sided facial paralysis for which she did not receive medical attention; the episode resolved spontaneously without specific treatment after 1 month. Two years later, she had a recurrence of right facial paralysis, where she was kept under observation without a specific intervention. No underlying cause was identified during that episode. Following these two events, she was left with residual right facial paresis.

At age 13, she developed progressive weakness in the right upper and lower limbs. Three years later, she presented an episode of generalized tonic-clonic seizure lasting approximately 10 min with loss of consciousness and urinary incontinence, with a 15-min postictal period. She was evaluated at a community hospital where levetiracetam was initiated and a brain MRI was ordered, where a hemispheric vascular malformation was observed so she was referred to our institution.

Presentation and imaging

The patient reported intermittent, pulsatile holocranial headache rated 8/10 on the Visual Analog Scale with no clear onset, no circadian predominance, no associated symptoms or irradiation, but it intensified with physical activity. She also reported right brachiocrural motor weakness, severely limiting her ability to ambulate independently. She experienced tonic-clonic seizures approximately every 6 months, which were well-controlled at the time with levetiracetam.

Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15, oriented in time, person, and space. She had occasional paraphasias but preserved fluent speech. Visuospatial impairments were detected through the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, with a score of 18 points. Cranial nerve examination showed asymmetry of facial folds, consistent with a central right facial paresis, evident at rest and during facial expression. Hemiparesis was noted, with strength in the right lower limb rated 3/5 proximally and distally, and foot dorsiflexion/plantarflexion at 2/5. Right upper limb strength was 4−/5 proximally, 2/5 distally, and 0/5 in the hand. Hyperreflexia, spasticity, and increased muscle tone were present on the right side, along with hemihypoesthesia. Independent ambulation was not possible.

The patient presented with a brain MRI, which revealed diffuse vascular distribution, with huge vascular lacunae surrounded by apparently normal parenchyma in the time of flight angiography (TOF) sequence, showing T2 hyperintensity involving the left basal ganglia, with corresponding diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging and low signal on ADC [Figure 1].

Surgical technique

Surgery was performed electively under optimal conditions. A preoperative Doppler ultrasound was used to map the course of the superficial temporal artery (STA). A Dandy skin incision was made, and dissection of the STA was attempted but intraoperative thrombosis was observed and did not reverse with local heparinization.

Therefore, it was decided to perform revascularization through the middle meningeal artery (MMA). Subperiosteal dissection of the temporal muscle was carried out, preserving its vascularization. The muscle was retracted and a left frontotemporoparietal craniotomy was performed; the MMA was intact. Dural opening in two semilunar flaps preserved arterial patency. Beneath the dura, a tangle of dilated arterialized vessels covered more than 90% of the cortical surface. The malformation was deemed unsuitable for resection or direct revascularization.

The MMA was dissected from the dura and transposed onto the cortical surface. A subarachnoid dissection was performed along the arterial path, and the arterial adventitia was brought into contact with the subarachnoid space. Duromyosynangiosis was then performed by extending the dural opening laterally in a star-shaped cut. The temporal muscle was descended and its edges were sutured to the inverted dural margins in close contact with the cortex. The bone flap was cut with a window to accommodate the muscle. Adequate vascularity of the graft was confirmed, and routine skin closure followed [Figure 3].

Postoperative details and follow-up

Extubation was performed in the operating room and transcranial Doppler ultrasound confirmed preserved pulsatility at the site of intracranial muscle transposition, with a mean MMA velocity of 23 cm/s. The postoperative course was successful, without new neurological symptomatology.

The patient was followed weekly during the 1st postoperative month, and monthly thereafter for 3 months. At each visit, a neurological examination was performed and transcranial Doppler was realized, demonstrating improved cortical flow patterns, with identifiable systolic and diastolic peaks.

She exhibited progressive motor improvement, regaining independent ambulation with crural strength rated 4+/5 Medical Research Council (MRC) and brachial strength 4/5 (MRC). Her language improved, seizures remained controlled, and her headaches resolved completely without analgesic treatment.

At 6 months, a follow-up angiography demonstrated improvement of the arterial flow with less arteriovenous shunts, with the presence of early venous flow in late arterial phase, with the MMA bypass with adequate flow with neovessels in the precentral cortex [Figure 2].

The first postoperative MRI showed decreased cortical congestion in the left hemisphere, without new diffusion-restricted ischemic lesions [Figure 1].

At the time of writing, the patient has been followed for 1 year postoperatively, with sustained clinical and radiological improvement, and no new ischemic events related to the vascular malformation. At a time, patients show notable enhancement in quality of life [Figure 4].

DISCUSSION

Proliferative angiopathy is a rare vascular entity that may present with either ischemic or hemorrhagic events, the latter being potentially fatal in terms of both function and overall morbidity and mortality. The most common clinical manifestation is progressive neurological deficit induced by recurrent ischemic insults, often presenting with focal symptoms corresponding to the topography of the malformation.[7]

In our case, the patient exhibited all the previously described epidemiological features: a female adolescent presenting with focal motor and sensitive deficits as well as seizures in the context of recurrent symptoms suggestive of an ischemic pathophysiology.

The underlying pathophysiology of CPA remains incompletely understood. However, mounting evidence supports a model of chronic cortical ischemia that triggers compensatory angiogenesis. Perfusion-weighted MRI and positron emission tomography/single-photon emission computed tomography studies consistently demonstrate reduced cerebral blood flow and impaired cerebrovascular reserve in the perilesional regions.[4,5,16]

Upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor and other proangiogenic mediators has been implicated in this process. The presence of transdural collateral supply observed in 37– 62% of the cases is thought to represent an adaptive response to sustained oligemia.[2,9,18] This ischemia-driven pathophysiology more closely resembles that of Moyamoya disease than classical AVMs, carrying important therapeutic implications.

Chen et al.[1] proposed a management algorithm based on a systematic review of 23 studies, categorizing patients into three groups: expectant/medical, elective, and urgent intervention. Elective treatments include radiotherapy, endovascular intervention, and indirect revascularization. In expectantly managed cases, clinical and radiological progression was common, and some presented with hemorrhage. Among the elective interventions, radiotherapy resulted in headache improvement. Endovascular treatment was performed in two cases involving no eloquent areas, including partial embolization of the ECA and pial supply to the occipital lobe to reduce the shunt.[1]

Indirect revascularization was performed in four cases, demonstrating clinical and radiological improvement of hypoperfusion. Techniques included calvaria burr holes and EDAS. Among the seven reported EDAS cases, most showed motor deficit improvement, headache relief, and cessation of ischemic events in two cases. However, a follow-up study reported clinical deterioration after 2 years of indirect revascularization. Radiologically, three studies described diffuse neovascularization post-EDAS, one as early as 7 months after the procedure.[1]

In the most extensive review of indirect revascularization outcomes, Somji et al.[16] showed six studies with seven patients treated with indirect revascularization, 4 with EDAS; five studies showed clinical improvement, resolution of motor deficits, relief of headaches, and cessation of transient ischemic attacks, and three demonstrated neovascularization on follow-up DSA. Nonetheless, complications have been reported. Kimiwada et al. described delayed neurological deterioration 2 years posEDAS,[6] and Sakata et al. reported two cases of intraventricular hemorrhage within 3 months after surgery.[14] These findings highlight the importance of long-term surveillance following revascularization.

A limited number of reports have described the use of EDAS, an indirect revascularization technique involving the placement of the STA over the cortical surface in direct contact with the fenestrated arachnoid membrane. Clinical improvement has been observed in these cases, although the small sample size limits definitive conclusions regarding long-term efficacy.[7,16]

In this case, the use of the MMA is proposed as a viable alternative for indirect revascularization. The MMA offers several advantages: it provides sufficient flow, is easily preserved and accessible intraoperatively, and is topographically closer to the cortical surface, which facilitates adherence between its adventitia and the fenestrated arachnoid membrane. This technique may be especially useful in situations where the STA is not viable due to thrombosis or unfavorable anatomical conditions.

Postoperative recovery typically involves extensive rehabilitation. Reports indicate significant clinical improvement following EDAS, as it presents in this case report, although due to the limited number of cases, long-term outcomes remain uncertain. However, Kimiwada et al. reported deterioration 2 years post-revascularization, and Sakata et al. described complications at 2 weeks and 3 months postoperatively, including intraventricular hemorrhage.[6,14]

Long-term prognosis is heterogeneous. The meta-analysis by Yamaki et al.[18] reported that 50.7% of patients improved, 40.2% remained neurologically stable, and 9% deteriorated, with a mean follow-up of 17 months. Recurrent hemorrhage and progressive ischemia remain key concerns, underscoring the necessity for lifelong monitoring.[18]

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates, in a schematic manner, the development and management of a rare vascular malformation. Despite the limited literature available to guide its treatment, it is evident that management must be individualized, as no consensus currently exists regarding the optimal therapeutic approach. However, in the presence of progressive symptoms, the trend leans toward surgical intervention.

Depending on the extent and location of the lesion, surgical, endovascular, and radio neurosurgical strategies should be considered viable and non-negligible options.

In this case, indirect revascularization through encephaloduroarteriomiosynangiosis represented a feasible therapeutic option, particularly in the absence of more effective alternatives. Clinical improvement was evident, and after 1 year of follow-up, no postoperative complications have been observed. Both clinical and radiological parameters showed improvement; nonetheless, systematic long-term follow-up remains essential.

The use of the MMA as an alternative conduit for indirect revascularization emerges as a technically sound option. However, its recommendation cannot yet be generalized until additional cases employing this technique are reported. Continued monitoring of this case is of critical importance to further evaluate the long-term viability and effectiveness of this surgical approach.
