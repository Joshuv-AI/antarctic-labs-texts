LRRK2 deficiency mitigates amyloid β deposition-mediated pathology in a murine Alzheimer’s disease model by reprogramming microglia
Authors: Qiuyang Zhang, Hsuan Lo, Yue Song, Linjuan Feng, Hanlin You, Xiaochun Chen, Yanping Wang, Xiaodong Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), primarily expressed in microglia, is responsible for the modulation of innate immune responses and associated with various immunological disorders. Available evidence documents that though as the predominant etiological factor for familial Parkinson’s disease, LRRK2 mutations rarely occur in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and that LRRK2 polymorphism is potentially associated with late-onset AD. However, the role of LRRK2 in AD immunopathogenesis remains unknown. In this study, we investigated the impact of LRRK2 deficiency on cognitive function, Aβ plaque accumulation, and plaque-associated neuropathology in AD mice. The results revealed that compared with the 5xFAD mice, the 8-month-old 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice reported improved learning and memory, reduced cerebral and hippocampal Aβ plaque burden, and decreased microglia and astrocytes within the central region of hippocampal Aβ plaques. The 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice also showed a decrease in several complement and proinflammatory cytokines in the brain, indicating an altered microglial phenotype. Furthermore, the absence of LRRK2 prevented synaptic loss and restored the disrupted equilibrium between excitatory and inhibitory synapses in the 5xFAD mice. These findings suggest that LRRK2 may play an essential role in Aβ plaque pathology, glial responses to plaques, and neuronal dysfunction in the brain of the 5xFAD mice and that a genomic transgene-blockade of LRRK2 may reprogram the microglial responsivity, thus mitigating the neuropathological and behavioral deficits in AD mice. The 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice reduced cognitive impairment in the Morris water maze test compared with the 5xFAD mice. The protective effect of LRRK2 inhibition is not dependent on the APP production process or Aβ degradation. Conversely, 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice enhanced microglial phagocytosis, reducing Aβ aggregation and glial activation. Additionally, compared with the 5xFAD, the 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice exhibited preserved synaptic structure, characterized by higher PSD95 expression, lower C1qa/C3 expression in both excitatory and inhibitory synapses, upregulated VGLUT1 expression, and downregulated VGAT expression. Subject terms: Hippocampus, Diseases

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders, is characterized by a gradual and irreversible decline in cognitive functions [1]. As a complex ailment, most AD cases are sporadic (sporadic AD, SAD) and only a small proportion of them are early-onset familial AD [2], accounting for approximately 5% of the total [3]. Studies have evidenced that AD is the primary cause of dementia among the elderly population, with its pathology attributed to the production of amyloid β (Aβ) through the enzymatic cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) and subsequent aggregation of Aβ, resulting in neuronal loss and impaired neuronal function [4].

Recent research has investigated the genetic effects of AD, showing that AD shares the specific notable etiological relations with Parkinson’s disease [5], including causative disease genes [6], of which mutations of the leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) gene have been identified as the predominant etiological factor underlying familial PD [7]. Other studies show that the genomic locus of LRRK2 resides within a linkage peak associated with SAD [8] and that the genetic mutation of LRRK2 (G2019S, R1441C) variant increases LRRK2 enzyme activity, which induces abnormal functions and in turn produces neurotoxicity [9, 10]. As the main pathological substrate of AD, amyloid has been found in LRRK2 carriers with PD [11]. A remarkable investigation into patient-derived cells, brain tissue, and a mouse PD model has demonstrated that LRRK2 promotes APP phosphorylation by interacting with the intracellular domain (AICD) to induce neurotoxicity [12]. LRRK2, by virtue of its kinase activity, not only facilitates APP processing but also engages in the inflammatory response, mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic dysfunction [13–15], and aberrant autophagosome function [16, 17], all of which collectively contribute to neurodegeneration. However, despite the existing literature of the prominent LRRK2 involvement in and the potential association of its gene mutations with the development of AD, much remains unsettled regarding the role of LRRK2 in the neuropathogenesis of in AD.

In the central nervous system (CNS), microglia fulfill diverse phagocytic roles by eliminating surplus synapses during neural circuit development [18], and effectively clearing the brain of potentially hazardous protein aggregates [19]. Microglial activation and aberrant synaptic pruning have been documented in various neurodegenerative disorders, such as AD and PD [20, 21]. Multiple studies have indicated that activated microglia can release diverse inflammatory mediators, such as complement, cytokines, and chemokines, contributing to the impairment of neurons and synapses in AD [22, 23]. Moreover, the integral constituents of complement proteins C1q/C3 in neurons, which interact with the complement receptor 3 (CR3) in microglia, assume a significant role in microglial phagocytosis and facilitate synaptic pruning [24]. A recent study provides compelling evidence through the use of constitutive C1q knockout in two mouse models of AD, that the absence of C1q may reduce glial activation and preserve neuronal integrity [25].

Meanwhile, in microglia, LRRK2 is abundantly expressed. Recent evidence indicates that the LRRK2 gene is involved in the regulation of innate immune responses [26]. The deficiency of microglial LRRK2 has been found to significantly enhance migration, phagocytosis, and α-synuclein elimination [27], suggesting the protective effects of LRRK2 knockdown in PD models. However, much remains desired to illuminate the potential protection of LRRK2 inhibition against cognitive impairment and neurodegeneration in mice with amyloid plaque accumulation in the brain at the advanced stages of AD.

In this study, we utilized 5xFAD;LRRK2 knockout (KO) mice to investigate the potential involvement of LRRK2 in the development of plaque-associated neuropathological alterations and cognitive deterioration. We found that the absence of LRRK2 in the 5xFAD mice produced a protective effect against plaque-associated synapse loss, reduced glial reactivity, improved amyloid pathology, and preserved cognitive function. These findings suggest that targeting LRRK2 can serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for AD treatment.

Materials and methods

Mice

All animal experimental protocols were approved by Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Medical University (FJMU IACUC 2021-0354) and observed ICMJE ARRIVE guidelines. Male 5xFAD mice (Stock #: 034840-Jax) and female homozygous LRRK2–deficient mice (Stock #: 012453-Jax), both with a C57BL/6 J background, were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. The mice were crossbred to obtain 5xFAD;LRRK2 KO (heterozygotes) mice (referred to as 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- mice) and 5xFAD;LRRK2 KO (homozygotes) mice (referred to as 5xFAD;LRRK2-/-mice). The offspring mice were genotyped by polymerase chain reaction using the following primers: APP, 5′-AGAGTACCAACTTGCATGACTACG- 3′ and 5′-ATGCTGGATAACTGCCTTCTTATC-3′; Lrrk2 -/-, 5′-GGT CGC CGT CAC CGT CTG G-3′ and 5′-TGA AGC AAC GTC TCG ATC TGC-3′. These mice were raised to 8 months of age and only males were used in this study to minimize gender-specific variability in behavioral tests. Mice were randomly assigned to an experimental group as follows: WT, 5xFAD, 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- mice, 5xFAD;LRRK2-/-mice (n = 14, n = 16, n = 17 and n = 17 for the groups shown left to right, respectively). All animals were maintained in a pathogen-free environment on a 12-h light/dark cycle in rooms with controlled temperature (21 ± 1.5 °C) and humidity (50 ± 10%).

Morris water maze test

As described previously [28], a modified Morris water maze test was used to evaluate spatial learning and memory (SANS SA201 by Saiangsi, Inc., China). Swimming patterns of the mice were tracked with a video camera linked to a computer-based image analyzer. During acquisition training, each mouse underwent four trials per day for five consecutive days to find the hidden platform. For each trial, the mouse was allowed to swim a maximum of 60 s to find the hidden platform. Latency time was recorded for each trial. At day 6, 24 h after the last training trial, a probe trial was performed to assess spatial memory. The submerged platform was removed from the water maze. Mice were placed and released from the northeast position, opposite to the previous location of the platform. Each mouse was allowed to swim freely for 60 s, and the number of crossings over the original position of the hidden platform was recorded.

Grip strength test

The grip strength test was performed to assess the neuromuscular function of the mice with a grip strength tester YLS-13A (Yiyan Technology, Shangdong, China), as previously described [29]. In brief, the tails of experimental mice were held so that their forelimbs grasped the grip plate and then the mice were gently pulled back until they released the grip plate. The maximum muscle strength was recorded automatically with the instrument. The test was repeated five times in sequence with appropriate rest intervals in between. All the mice underwent adaptive training for three days. The results of the grip strength test was presented as grams.

Rotarod test

As described previously [21], mice were placed onto a rotating rod that automatically accelerated from 0 to 40 rpm over 5 min (SANS SA102 by Saiangsi, Inc., China). The duration each mouse stayed on the rotating rod was recorded. The test was performed in triplicate.

RT–qPCR analysis

RNA extraction, cDNA production, and SYBR Green-based qPCR were performed as described previously [21]. The mRNA expression was normalized to GAPDH as indicated. The primer sequences used are summarized in Table S1.

Western blot

Samples were collected from the brain tissues and were analyzed by quantitative immunoblotting with primary antibodies (Table S2). The immunoblots were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence development and quantified with the Fiji software.

Brain tissue treatment

Mice were anesthetized with a ketamine (100 mg kg−1)/xylazine (20 mg kg−1) cocktail and perfused with 0.9% saline. One hemisphere was immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 6 h for immunohistochemistry analysis. The remaining hemisphere was dissected for the isolation of the hippocampus and cortex and snap-frozen for biochemical analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

The fixed tissue was then embedded with OCT and sliced into coronal sections (40 μm thick) in a matrix (CM1950; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Brain slices were incubated with glycine median and treated with primary and secondary antibodies (Table S2) as previously reported [27]. The paired images in all the figures were collected at the same gain and offset settings and subjected to uniform post collection processing. The images were either presented as a single optic layer after the acquisition of z-series stack scans from individual fields or displayed as maximum intensity projections to represent confocal stacks. For quantitative assessment of the accumulation of protein and the distributions of various markers, images were obtained with identical settings and exported to ImageJ. The glia morphology and associations with Aβ plaques (6E10), microglia (IBA1), and astrocytes (GFAP) were detected by immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy (LSM 710, Carl Zeiss) as described previously [30]. Images were collected at the same exposure settings. Confocal Z-stack images (optical slices of 0.2 mm) of plaques and surrounding glia were collected using a 63× objective. Three images per mouse were acquired from three equidistant planes, 500 mm apart. Immunofluorescence intensity analysis and 3D reconstruction of Z-stack images were performed with the confocal image analysis software (ZEN Black, Carl Zeiss). Glia counts were performed using stereological methods after image collection.

Transmission electron microscopy

Hippocampal samples were dissected and the CA1 regions were cut into sections (1 mm×1 mm×1 mm). Tissue blocks were prefixed in a mixture of 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 2 h. Samples were then prepared as previously described [27]. The image was detected by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai G2 20 S-TWIN), operated at 200 kV by 60,000× magnification.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, USA) and represented as means ± SEM in graphs. The means of different groups were compared by two-tailed Student’s t-test and Two-way ANOVA analysis. A P-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific statistical analysis, sample size, and P value were specified in each figure.

Results

LRRK2 overexpression in the hippocampus and cortex of the AD mouse model

Previous studies have indicated that the mRNA level of LRRK2 is notably elevated in the temporal cortex of AD individuals with diffusely-distributed Lewy bodies, commonly referred to as Lewy body variant (LBV), when in comparison with AD control subjects [14]. To ascertain the potential elevation of LRRK2 expression in AD mice, the LRRK2 expression was measured in the 5xFAD mice at 8 months. As the 5xFAD mouse model is recognized for its aggressive amyloid pathology, characterized by developing plaques as early as two months of age [31], the present study employed immunohistochemistry to investigate the expression of LRRK2 in various brain regions in both 5xFAD and WT mice, namely, the olfactory bulb, neocortex, striatum, hippocampus, cerebellum, and brain stem. The results revealed a significant elevation of LRRK2 expression in the olfactory bulb (P < 0.0001), neocortex (P = 0.0066), and hippocampus (P = 0.0028) of the 5xFAD mice when compared with the WT mice (Fig. 1A, B). Intriguingly, the 5xFAD mice reported a decrease in LRRK2 expression in the brain stem (P = 0.014) and cerebellum (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the mRNA expression of LRRK2 was upregulated in the olfactory bulb (P < 0.0001), striatum (P < 0.0001), and hippocampus (P = 0.033) of the 8-month-old 5xFAD mice (Fig. 1C). To ascertain the expression of LRRK2 in specific cell types, LRRK2 (green) was respectively double-stained with βⅢ-tubulin, GFAP, or IBA1 (Red), which revealed the presence of LRRK2 in neurons and microglia, not in astrocytes (Fig. 1D–H). Furthermore, the co-localization analysis of LRRK2 (green) and 6E10 (red, indicating Aβ) demonstrated a significant increase in the presence of LRRK2 on the microglia that surrounded plaques (Fig. 1F, G). Collectively, these findings indicate that LRRK2 is highly expressed within neurons and microglia in the critical brain areas of AD, potentially regulating the AD-associated microglial functions.

LRRK2 deficiency ameliorates cognitive decline and motor dysfunction in AD mice

Given the potential of inhibiting LRRK2 as a therapeutic intervention for tauopathies [27, 32] and the findings of LRRK2 upregulation in AD mice presented in Fig. 1, we hypothesized that the inhibition of LRRK2 activity may ameliorate cognitive decline in the AD mouse model. To investigate the impact of LRRK2 deficiency on cognitive alterations in mice, we crossbred two types of transgenic mice, namely the 5xFAD and LRRK2-deficient mice. Subsequently, we evaluated the cognitive and motor functions of the resulting offspring (WT, 5xFAD, 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice).

The Morris water maze test showed that compared with the WT mice, the AD mice exhibited a significant impairment in spatial learning and memory, reporting prolonged escape latency time during the training trials and a reduced number of crossings over the platform during the probe trial (Fig. 2A–C). However, these impairments in the AD mice were significantly reversed by the deficiency of LRRK2, in which 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice displayed a significant improvement in the acquisition performance from day 2 to day 5, suggesting that these mice learned the location of the hidden platform earlier than the 5xFAD mice (P = 0.007) (Fig. 2B). During the probe trial, wherein the platform was removed, the performance of 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice bore a similarity to that of WT mice, but significantly surpassed that of 5xFAD mice (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.026; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that even a partial knockdown of the LRRK2 gene in mice, thus a reduced LRRK2 function, may enhance spatial learning and cognitive flexibility of AD mice.

Additionally, no discernible disparities in grip tests were observed between 5xFAD and WT mice, while the absence of the LRRK2 gene greatly amplified the grip strength of 5xFAD transgenic mice (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the results of the accelerating rotarod task, which assesses alterations in motor learning, coordination, and repetitive behavior in mice, showed that the duration of rod retention was shorter in the 5xFAD transgenic mice than in both WT and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice (WT vs. 5xFAD, P = 0.021; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E). Together, these results suggest that LRRK2 deficiency ameliorates memory deficits and promotes motor coordination in AD mice.

The alleviation of Aβ burden by genetic inhibition of LRRK2 does not involve APP processing alteration and enzymic degradation in AD mice

The formation of Aβ plaque, a critical contributor to AD pathologies, is caused by the aggregation of Aβ [33]. Thus, we speculated whether genetic inhibition of LRRK2 may yield beneficial effects on alleviating Aβ plaque formation. Immunofluorescent staining with Aβ antibody revealed a notable decrease in the quantity of Aβ plaques in the hippocampal CA1 and cortex (CTX) regions of the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- (CA1: P < 0.0001; CTX: P = 0.0002) and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice (CA1: P < 0.0001; CTX: P = 0.0006), when in comparison with the 5xFAD mice (Fig. 3A and D, E). Furthermore, the 5xFAD mice exhibited a notable increase in the size of Aβ plaques, whereas the deletion of the LRRK2 gene resulted in a reduction in the size of Aβ plaques in the AD mouse model (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.043; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.042) (Fig. 3B, C). These findings provide compelling evidence that the absence of LRRK2 significantly attenuates the aggregation of Aβ in the mouse model of AD.

Aβ is produced through a series of enzymatic cleavages facilitated by β- and γ-secretase during the processing of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) [34]. The activity of β-secretase is facilitated by the beta-site-APP cleaving enzyme (BACE), which subsequently cleaves APP to produce soluble APPβ (sAPPβ) [35]. However, α- and β-secretases engage in a competitive relationship for APP as a substrate, exerting conflicting impacts on Aβ production. Precisely, the overexpression of α-secretase elevates sAPPα levels while decreasing the generation of Aβ peptides and plaques [36]. Moreover, the activity of α-secretase is found within a group of proteins referred to as a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM), which encompasses ADAM9, ADAM10, ADAM17/TACE, and ADAM19 [37]. Thus, we assessed the impact of LRRK2 deficiency on Aβ production, aiming to determine whether LRRK2 deficiency reduces Aβ aggregation by acting on the processing of APP.

Interestingly, in comparison with the 5xFAD mice, the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice displayed no significant alteration in the protein expression levels of full-length APP (APPfl), sAPPβ, BACE1, ADAM10, and ADAM17 during the APP processing (Fig. 3F–K). Moreover, our findings indicated that BACE1 level was elevated in the 5xFAD mice when compared with the WT mice (P = 0.0004) but was not significantly altered in the LRRK2-/-, 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice when compared with the 5xFAD mice (Fig. 3H). In light of these findings, we proceeded to investigate the potential impact of LRRK2 activity loss on the expression of amyloid β-degrading enzymes, namely, insulin degrading enzyme (IDE) and neprilysin (NEP), in an AD mouse model. Consistently, the expression of IDE and NEP was not significantly different across the WT, 5xFAD, 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, and LRRK2-/- mice (Fig. 3L, M). Together, these findings demonstrate that LRRK2 deficiency alleviates Aβ burden in the AD mouse model neither by altering APP expression and processing nor by affecting the enzymic degradation of Aβ.

LRRK2 deletion reduces plaque-associated microgliosis and inflammatory cytokines in the 5xFAD mice

In the context of AD, the presence of microglia and astrocytes is commonly associated with plaque clearance. Research has demonstrated that astrocytic and microglial cells play a crucial role in modulating neuroinflammation in AD [38], while facilitating effective communication between astrocytes and microglia, improving the degradation of Aβ aggregates [39]. Therefore, we first examined the colocalization of these cells with plaques in mouse brains by immunofluorescence staining with IBA1 (Red) and GFAP. The confocal microscopy in the hippocampal CA1 and CTX regions revealed that compared with those of the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice, microglia in the 8-month-old 5xFAD transgenic mice featured enlarged cell bodies and thicker, shorter processes with reduced branching (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A and Fig. S1A), which conformed with the morphological alterations associated with reactive microgliosis. Furthermore, GFAP-positive astrocytes exhibited a distinct clustering pattern within the CA1 and CTX regions of the 5xFAD mice. However, in the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice, the astrocytes were more widely dispersed, displaying a reduced clustering around plaques (Fig. S1B) and a small averaged size in both regions of interest (ROIs) (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.042, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.044) (Fig. S1D). Additionally, when compared with the 5xFAD mice, quantitative image analysis demonstrated a significant reduction of IBA1 immunoreactivity of microglia (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001) and GFAP immunoreactivity of astrocytes (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.010, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.036, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.015) in the CA1 and CTX region of the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice (Fig. 4B, C and Fig. S1C).

As demonstrated in the data presented above, the presence of Aβ plaques may induce the activation of astrocytes and microglia, resulting in morphological changes that are associated with reactive microgliosis. It follows that LRRK2 deletion may inhibit these alterations. Thus, we assessed the quantity of microglia and astrocytes surrounding Aβ plaques in the hippocampal CA1 region of the 8-month-old 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– and 5xFAD mice. Cell numbers in the proximal area (located within the central region of large plaques) and distal area (encompassing the surrounding region of large plaques) were analyzed with the three-dimensional (3D) reconstructed images. Intriguingly, the 5xFAD mice reported a higher number of IBA1- and GFAP-positive cells within the central region of plaques, whereas the 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice exhibited a significant reduction in glia in close proximity to plaques (IBA1: P = 0.002; GFAP: P = 0.0096) (Fig. 4D, E and Fig. S2A, 2B). Furthermore, distal to the plaques, a decrease was observed in astrocytes (P = 0.0056), not in IBA1-positive cells (Fig. 4F and Fig. S2C). The present findings evidence a reprogrammed microglial response in the 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice, which results in a reduction in the number of microglia around the plaques and in their activation level.

The microglial phenotypic switch towards beneficial functions involves multiple molecular mechanisms, including the activation of receptors and channels that promote anti-inflammatory microglial function, and the inhibition of synthesis of nitric oxide (NO), chemokines, and inflammatory cytokines [40]. The expression of LRRK2 in immune cells is significantly elevated and subject to strict regulation in response to immune stimulation. Moreover, studies have established its functional association with crucial pathways related to immune cell function, including cytokine release, autophagy, and regulation of immune-pathways [41]. Accordingly, we investigated the potential influence of LRRK2 deficiency on the polarization of microglia into M1/M2 phenotypes in AD mice by qRT-PCR to identify markers associated with microglial phenotypes. The results revealed a reduction in the expression of M1 phenotypic markers, including inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-1α (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001), and inducible NO synthase (iNOS) (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0009), and a decrease in M2-like polarization signals such as IL-4 (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001), transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001), and arginase 1 (Arg1) (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001), in the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/– and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice when compared with the 5xFAD mice (Fig. 4G). The above findings suggest that the absence of LRRK2 facilitates the microglia reprogramming by regulating microglial polarization signal and alleviating the Aβ-induced microglial immune response.

Thus, we next assessed whether the LRRK2 deficiency-induced microglial reprogramming can effectively reduce the formation and expansion of Aβ plaques by enhancing the microglial phagocytosis. Numerous studies have documented the presence of cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68) in activated phagocytic microglia, which has been linked to neuroinflammation in AD [42]. The co-localization of IBA1 with lysosome-associated membrane protein CD68 demonstrated a significantly-elevated CD68 expression in the microglia of the CA1 and CTX regions of the 5xFAD mice (Fig. 4H). Quantitative analysis indicated a reduction in the number and total volume of CD68 structures within the cells in the CA1 and CTX of the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/– and 5xFAD;LRRK2–/– mice [CA1(number): 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0042, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0007; CA1(volume): 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; CTX(number): 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0261; CTX(volume): 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001] (Fig. 4I–L). These findings evidence that the absence of LRRK2 markedly ameliorates chronic neuroinflammation, reduces microglial CD68 expression, and enhances the clearance of Aβ aggregates, indicating that the inhibition of LRRK2 can effectively mitigate specific detrimental AD-associated microglial responses.

LRRK2 deficiency alleviates complement hyperactivation and promotes the stability of synaptic structures in the 5xFAD mice

Previous studies indicate that Aβ is one of the main pathogenic factors for synaptic dysfunction by directly impacting neurons or via the microglia-induced excessive synaptic pruning [43, 44]. Therefore, we further investigated whether the lack of LRRK2 also compromises the stability of synaptic structures and the complement pathway in the AD mice. The co-staining of synaptic markers synaptophysin (SPH, a presynaptic marker protein) and complement C1qa revealed a notable decrease in SPH density and significant increase in C1qa expression in the hippocampal CA1 and CTX of the 5xFAD mice (Fig. 5A). However, the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice reported a largely intact SPH density (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0495, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0045; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0071, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0069) and a reduced C1qa expression (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5B–E). Meanwhile, consistent with the findings of the western blot analysis, the levels of synaptophysin and postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95, a postsynaptic marker protein) were markedly reduced in the 5xFAD mice when compared with the WT mice (PSD95: P = 0.002; synaptophysin: P = 0.004), but highly elevated in LRRK2-deficient 5xFAD mice (PSD95: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.005, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.003; synaptophysin: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.003, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.016) (Fig. 5G–I). In addition, the western blot analysis and qRT-PCR demonstrated a notable decline in the expression of C1qa (at the protein level) (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.003) and of C1qa, C3, and CR3 (at the mRNA level) within the hippocampus of the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice when in comparison with the 5xFAD mice (C1qa: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; C3: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.017, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.007; CR3: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.003) (Fig. 5F, J). Given that PSD95 may serve as a critical scaffolding molecule in excitatory synapses [45], these findings suggest that the deficiency of LRRK2 potentially mitigates the AD-associated disruption in synaptic structure and may reduce the activation of the complement system in the AD pathology.

The impact of LRRK2 deficiency on synaptic ultrastructure in the hippocampal CA1 was vividly visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 5K). Our findings showed that when compared with the WT mice, the AD mice displayed a reduction in the thickness of the postsynaptic density, which was restored in the LRRK2-deficient 5xFAD mice (5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0031; 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5L). Notably, the width of the synaptic cleft remained unaltered across all genetic mouse models (Fig. 5M). Taken together, our findings elucidate that LRRK2 deficiency contributes significantly to the maintenance of neuronal structure in the pathogenesis of AD, in which the absence of LRRK2 attenuates complement activation within neurons and promotes the stability of synaptic structures.

LRRK2 deficiency improves the balance in excitatory/inhibitory synapses in the 5xFAD mice

Based on our discovery of increased C1qa expression within the complement system of the AD mice and the role of C1q in promoting microglial clearance of apoptotic neurons and synaptic pruning [46], we proceeded to investigate the impact of LRRK2 deficiency on microglial engulfment of synaptic structures in the AD mice. The immunofluorescence and reconstruction techniques revealed that the quantity of PSD95 within microglial phagolysosomes of the hippocampal CA1 region was significantly elevated in the 8-month-old 5xFAD mice when compared with the WT, 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, the 5xFAD mice reported a higher microglial phagocytosis of PSD95 when compared with the other three groups (volume: WT vs. 5xFAD, P = 0.043, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.028, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0064; number: WT vs. 5xFAD, P = 0.0484, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0288, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0113; average size: WT vs. 5xFAD, P = 0.0097, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0256, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0048) (Fig. 6B–D).

Many reports indicate that the early manifestation of AD involves the disruption of neuronal activity, particularly an imbalance in the underlying excitation/inhibition (E/I) process. This phenomenon is acknowledged as a crucial connection between structural brain pathology and cognitive impairment [47]. Additionally, complement C1q has been reported to contribute to the microglial elimination of excitatory and inhibitory synapses in AD mouse models [48]. Another complement activation fragment of C3 is strongly correlated with the pathology of AD, which can be ameliorated via the inhibition of complement C3 [49], suggesting that C3 may potentially mitigate neuropathology in mouse models with mutant APP. Therefore, we examined whether the deficiency of LRRK2 may impact the expression of excitatory and inhibitory synapses, and C1qa/C3 in AD mice. The immunostaining of vesicular glutamate (VGLUT1) and GABA (VGAT) transporters revealed, in the hippocampal CA1 and CTX of the AD mice, an elevation in inhibitory synapses (VGAT immunoreactive) (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0013, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0001; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0011, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0001), a decrease in excitatory synapses (VGLUT1 immunoreactive) (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0423; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P = 0.0043, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P = 0.0477) (Fig. 6E–H and Fig. S3A–S3D), and a wide distribution of C1qa/C3 protein around the excitatory and inhibitory synapses (Fig. 6A and Fig. S3A). Additionally, other reports suggest that the presence of deposited C3 can directly activate C3 receptors on the microglia, thereby initiating synapse elimination via phagocytosis [50]. As anticipated, the expression of C3 was upregulated in the excitatory and inhibitory synapses within the hippocampal CA1 and cortex of the AD mice. Conversely, the absence of LRRK2 diminished the overexpression of C3 in AD mice (CA1: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001; CTX: 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2+/-, P < 0.0001, 5xFAD vs. 5xFAD;LRRK2–/–, P < 0.0001) (Fig. S4). Collectively, our observations suggest that the absence of LRRK2 restores the balance of excitatory and inhibitory synapses in AD pathology, potentially preserving the synaptic function in AD mice.

Discussion

The role of LRRK2 in tauopathies has attracted growing attention from the neurological researchers. Its single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are closely associated with the incidence of late-onset AD [51]. The present study provides insight into the functional role of LRRK2 in the progression of AD-like pathology in the 5xFAD mouse model. We found a strong attenuation of Aβ plaque formation in brain tissues from LRRK2-deficient 5xFAD mice, indicating a critical role of LRRK2 in the generation of AD-like pathology. The diminished Aβ pathology observed in the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice was associated with the ameliorated cognitive and motor behavioral deficits, thereby illustrating the potential protective effects of LRRK2 inhibition against AD, a finding that aligns with previous observations in PD [27].

In AD, the function and activation of microglia and astrocytes can potentially modulate Aβ deposition [38]. However, the precise impact of glial LRRK2 expression on AD progression remains uncertain. In our study, the deficiency of LRRK2 significantly diminished the excessive activation of microglia and astrocytes, as evidenced in both morphological and molecular assessments. Additionally, the absence of LRRK2 was highly correlated with reduced microglial over-activation, a decrease in the expression of inflammatory factors, and limited microglial recruitment, which is not related to the accumulation of microglia around local plaques but to the LRRK2-modulated the microglial functional in AD pathology. Furthermore, we observed similar results between unilateral and bilateral LRRK2 gene knockout, indicating that the gene expression threshold required for proper function may already be compromised in the heterozygous state. Several studies have highlighted the role of LRRK2 in vesicle trafficking, inflammatory responses, autophagy, and neurite outgrowth [52, 53]. Unlike studies focusing solely on LRRK2-/- cells or mice [53–55], our findings suggest that losing just one allele can effectively reduce microglia-mediated inflammation and mitigate the manifestations of AD pathology. The bilateral knockout further intensified these effects, particularly in the behavioral tests, confirming the critical role of LRRK2 dosage in maintaining microglial cellular homeostasis and neuroprotection.

Given the observed decrease in Aβ deposition in LRRK2-deficient 5xFAD mice, we attempted to investigate the underlying factors contributing to the LRRK2-reduced Aβ plaques, explicitly examining the APP production process and the Aβ degradation. A critical report demonstrates that as a substrate of LRRK2, APP can be phosphorylated by LRRK2 at Thr668 within the intracellular domain (AICD), which enhances the translocation of APP to the nucleus, thereby facilitating cytoskeletal dynamics and apoptosis [56]. Similarly, in the current study, LRRK2 inhibition did not impact the APP processes in the pathological mechanisms of AD (Fig. 3), which suggests that LRRK2 primarily impacts the activity of APP rather than its expression.

Meanwhile, the lack of LRRK2 did not impact the expressions of BACE1, the beta-site-APP cleaving enzyme, and Aβ-degrading enzymes, thus indicating the reduction of Aβ does not originate from the inhibited sAPPβ production nor from the promoted Aβ degradation. A recent report has demonstrated that the inhibition of LRRK2 kinase attenuates the astrocytic activation in response to amyloid β1-42 fibrils [57]. The current study showed that the deficiency of LRRK2 resulted in a notable decrease in the population of activated microglia in the vicinity of Aβ deposition, concomitant with a diminished proximity of microglia to Aβ plaques (Fig. 4). Likewise, the decrease in the excessive activation of microglia was observed along with the diminished expression of CD68, a protein found in phagocytic lysosomes within the activated microglia [58]. This indicates that the deficiency of LRRK2 in AD mice can reduce the Aβ deposition, alleviating the excessive microglial activation. However, further investigations are still required to explore the mechanism underlying the reduced Aβ deposition by LRRK2 inhibition in AD mice.

It is well-established that Aβ stimulation or AD pathology can activate microglia and that TNF-α and IL-1α are upregulated in senile plaques and dystrophic neurites in AD patients [59] and in an AD mouse model [60]. In a similar line, our results revealed a strong modulation of the complement and cytokine milieu by LRRK2. The upregulation of proinflammatory transcripts (TNFα, IL-1α, and iNOS) and anti-inflammatory transcripts (IL-4, TGFβ, and Arg1) in the 5xFAD mice was significantly suppressed by LRRK2-deficiency, which evidences that LRRK2 has a dual impact on microglial activation. Furthermore, other studies have reported that the suppression of LRRK2 alleviates the inflammatory reactions of microglia [61] and that the elimination of LRRK2 may change the phenotype of LPS-primed microglia cells, resulting in a reduction in their “activated” morphology and in oxidative stress indicators, and an upregulation of WAVE2 (Wiskott-Aldrich protein verprolin homology protein 2), a crucial regulator involved in actin remodeling and inflammation [62].

Interestingly, TGFβ is a prominent anti-inflammatory cytokine that has been extensively investigated for its ability to inhibit classical activation of microglia or macrophages. A recent study has provided evidence suggesting that TGFβ significantly enhances IL4-induced alternative activation of microglia by upregulating the expression of Arg1 and Ym1 [63]. Moreover, TGF-β1 can decrease inflammatory mediators, including TNFα, IL-1β, NO, and LPS-induced ROS, thereby attenuating neurotoxicity. The neuroprotective function of TGF-β1 against Aβ toxicity has also been documented in both in vitro and in vivo models of AD. Consequently, various pharmaceutical agents capable of elevating TGF-β1 levels in the CNS have been undergoing trials as novel neuroprotective candidates against Aβ-induced neurodegeneration [64]. Regrettably, our findings revealed that the absence of LRRK2 reduced not only inflammatory molecules but also anti-inflammatory molecules in the progression of AD pathology. Therefore, further investigations are waited for elucidation.

Regarding the involvement of complement and their receptors in AD pathology, our findings reported a substantial increase in C1qa and C3, thereby providing additional support for the pathophysiological significance of the complement system in the 5xFAD model. In contrast, a significant decrease in the expression of C1qa and C3 was observed in the 5xFAD;LRRK2+/- and 5xFAD;LRRK2-/- mice. These findings suggest that a persistent activation of microglia may play a role in the deterioration of synaptic function and cognitive decline in the context of brain injury or neurodegenerative diseases. In addition, previous research has demonstrated that complement system and microglia mediate synaptic loss in AD mouse models at early AD stages before plaque deposition [22]. The present study also showed that the complement system also played an additional role in the glial response to amyloid deposition and synaptic health at late AD stages. Moreover, an additional murine model of retinal ischemia and reperfusion has demonstrated that the C1q-mediated microglial activation and the subsequent neurodegenerative alterations in the early stages of injury can be prevented through the genetic elimination of C1qa [65]. The deficiency of C3 exerts a protective effect on the APP/PS1 mice, mitigating the age-related decline in synapses and neurons [30]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the inhibition of LRRK2 can potentially function as a crucial mechanism to suppress the activation of the complement system. Additionally, the findings of our study suggest that the increased C1qa/C3 expression in synapse potentially contributes to the disruption of inhibitory and excitatory transmission in AD mice. Similarly, the existing body of literature has also posited that imbalances in synaptic transmission in AD are linked to the activation of the complement system [66, 67]. Furthermore, the absence of LRRK2 may serve as an important “brake” to suppress the complement system, which seems to rectify the imbalanced expression of inhibitory and excitatory synapse, thereby safeguarding the connectivity and functionality of synapses.

Conclusion

The current study reveals that the absence of LRRK2 in the 5xFAD mice provides neuroprotection against AD-associated neurodegeneration, maintains the balance of inhibitory and excitatory synapses, and mitigates cognitive decline by reducing plaque accumulation. These findings suggest that LRRK2 may have a detrimental impact on synaptic and neuronal function in the aged brain with high plaque burden and that inhibiting LRRK2 signaling can be a potential therapeutic target for AD treatment. Our future investigations employing inducible conditional LRRK2 knockout mice will ascertain whether depleting LRRK2 after the brain development or after the onset of AD pathology can safeguard synapses and preserve cognitive function.
