The Role of Copper in Struvite Formation in the Context of Infection Urinary Stones: A Journey at the Interface of Physics, Chemistry and Microbiology
Authors: Jolanta Prywer, Agnieszka Torzewska, Dominika Szczerbiec, Ewa Mielniczek-Brzóska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infection urinary stones account for approximately 10–15% of all urinary stones worldwide, with a rising incidence observed in recent decades, particularly in countries with a high Socio-Demographic Index (SDI). This trend has been partially attributed to dietary changes, including increased consumption of processed foods. Heavy metals belong to a group of substances, the source of which can be both food and the human environment. Among many heavy metals, in this study, we focus on copper and investigate its influence on the nucleation and growth of struvite crystals, the primary component of infection urinary stones. Experiments were conducted in artificial urine, both in the presence and absence of Proteus mirabilis, a urease-producing bacterium commonly associated with infection urinary stones. In a bacteria-free system, bacterial urease activity was mimicked by the addition of aqueous ammonia solution. Our results demonstrate that the presence of copper in artificial urine induces a slight shift in the struvite crystallization toward lower pH values, indicating that crystal formation initiates earlier compared to a control test. Additionally, the amount of precipitated struvite increases modestly in the presence of copper. Struvite crystals formed in copper-containing artificial urine are larger and exhibit altered habit and morphology. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses confirm that copper does not incorporate into either the bulk or surface structure of the struvite crystals. X-ray diffraction (XRD) data show that struvite remains the sole crystalline phase, consistent with the control samples. Microbiological assays reveal that copper, at the concentrations tested, does not affect the viability of P. mirabilis, indicating an absence of bacteriostatic or bactericidal effects. To elucidate the physicochemical mechanisms underlying copper’s influence on nucleation and growth of struvite, speciation analysis of chemical complexes was performed. This revealed the formation of various copper complexes in artificial urine, including Cu(OH)+, CuCit−, CuC2O4, Cu(OH)2, CuHPO4, Cu(NH3)2+, Cu(NH3)22+, and Cu(NH3)32+. These chemical complexes modulate the equilibrium and formation of complexes with Mg2+ and PO43− (e.g., MgHCit, MgCit−, MgOH+, MgC2O4, MgSO4, MgHPO4), contributing to the observed shift in struvite crystallization to lower pH values.

1. Introduction

Infection urinary stones are a type of urinary stones whose formation is associated with urinary tract infections (UTIs) caused by bacteria producing the enzyme urease. Such bacteria include, for example, Proteus or Klebsiella, which, using their enzyme—urease—break down one of the components of urine, specifically urea, causing the formation of ammonia in the urine. This initiates a whole series of chemical reactions leading to an increase in urine pH and the formation of solid phases giving rise to infection urinary stones. The main component of this type of stone is highly crystalline magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate (MgNH4PO4·6H2O; MAPH), also known as struvite. Struvite crystallizes in the orthorhombic system, in space group Pmn21, and has the following unit cell parameters: a = 6.9650(2) A, b = 6.1165(2) A, c = 11.2056(3) [1]. A detailed description of the formation of struvite and poorly crystalline and amorphous solid phases that are also a component of this type of stones is available in the literature [2,3,4,5,6]. Stones of this type, depending on the region of the world, constitute about 10–15% of all urinary stones [7], and in countries with a high Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), a continuous increase in their occurrence has been observed in recent decades [7,8]. It should also be noted that in the case of infection urinary stones, a high recurrence rate is observed [7,9,10]. Therefore, one may wonder what factors in countries with a high SDI influence the increase in the occurrence of UTIs, which can lead to infection urolithiasis. This increase is usually attributed to lifestyle and diet, including increased consumption of processed foods and reduced physical activity. Environmental factors also seem to be very important, including air and water pollution, exposure to harmful chemicals, as well as increasingly frequent heat waves, which can lead to dehydration, which causes reduced urine output and thus difficult flushing of bacteria from the urinary tract. Understanding the extent to which disease and ill health can be attributed to modifiable environmental risks can help identify opportunities for preventive action.

Heavy metals belong to a group of substances whose source can be both food and the human environment. Among the many heavy metals, for research in this article, we have chosen copper. A review of the literature indicates that the effect of copper on the formation of urinary stones has been studied, but these studies only concern stones made of calcium oxalate (CaOx), which are not associated with UTIs but usually constitute so-called metabolic stones. Some of these studies indicate that copper consumption is slightly associated with a higher risk of calcium oxalate stone formation [11,12]. Researchers indicate that copper slightly affects the formation of calcium oxalate in urine by creating insoluble salts with oxalate ions, which may accelerate the growth of subsequent layers of urinary stones [13]. Other studies [14,15,16] indicate that copper may play an indirect role in the formation of urinary stones, in that it can adsorb on different surfaces of CaOx crystals, causing the formation of layers of this stone. It is also possible that copper can replace other elements during the formation of urinary stones, influencing the composition and the process of their formation. All this means that copper may have a minor role in the pathogenesis of CaOx stones.

Despite our efforts, we found no studies on the effect of copper in the context of infection stone formation. Therefore, the aim of the research presented in this paper is to assess the effect of copper on the nucleation and growth of struvite, the main component of infection urinary stones. Theoretically, copper may at least affect the chemical environment of urine, which may or may not favor struvite formation.

Copper is an essential trace element that plays key roles in the human body. For example, copper is essential for the formation of red blood cells. It helps maintain a healthy immune system, increasing the body’s ability to fight infections. Copper is involved in the maintenance of nerve cells and supporting brain development; it helps in the formation of collagen, which is important for bones, skin, and connective tissue. Copper is a cofactor for enzymes involved in energy production at the cellular level; it plays a role in protecting cells from oxidative damage. Copper is an element classified as a trace element and is found in the human body in trace amounts. Nevertheless, it is extremely important for its proper functioning, and both deficiency and excess can have serious consequences for health.

Excess copper can be dangerous to health and life. It most often occurs when working with materials containing this element. Copper poisoning causes a number of symptoms and requires rapid implementation of treatment. Excess copper in the body can also occur after drinking water contaminated with copper or as a result of cooking acidic dishes in copper vessels. A source of toxic copper levels can also be the consumption of pesticides [17]. In addition, excess copper can appear in patients with Wilson’s disease—a congenital disorder of copper metabolism leading to the accumulation of this element in the body. Copper deficiency most often develops as a result of improper absorption of this element in the body. In addition, people who take large amounts of supplements containing zinc on a daily basis may develop copper deficiency. This is because zinc makes it difficult to absorb this element. There is also a genetic syndrome, the symptom of which is copper deficiency—this is the so-called Menkes disease.

The source of copper can be food and the human environment. Food products rich in copper include, for example, liver. Calf liver contains as much as 15 mg of copper per 100 g [18]. Oysters contain, depending on the species, place of catch and method of farming, from 1 to 8 mg of copper in 100 g of oysters [18]. Baker’s yeast, sesame and tahini seeds, cocoa and chocolate, squid and lobster, sunflower seeds, dried tomatoes, and pumpkin seeds [18] are also rich in copper. Other food products with a high copper content are soybeans, flax seeds, shiitake mushrooms, wheat bran, watermelon seeds, dried plums and peppers [18].

Copper in various valence states and in the form of various complexes also occurs in the environment. Human activities, such as mining operations, contribute to the release of copper into the environment, especially into the ground. Copper is released into water due to weathering of soil, industrial discharges and from sewage treatment plants. The concentration of copper in drinking water can increase significantly during its distribution if pipes and plumbing fixtures contain copper. Copper can then leach into drinking water. This process can be enhanced by low water pH, higher temperature and reduced hardness. In drinking water, copper is usually found in free form. In rivers, copper is usually adsorbed to insoluble particles or complexed with inorganic ligands [19].

Sources in the literature indicate that in healthy adults, the normal level of copper in urine is between 10 µg and 30 µg per 24 h [20,21]. Levels above 100 µg per 24 h are generally considered dangerous levels of copper in urine. A healthy adult usually excretes between 800 and 2000 mL of urine per day, assuming a normal fluid intake of about 2 L [22]. This range is quite wide, which is related to factors such as the amount of fluids consumed, physical activity and general health. Based on these data, it can be assumed that the lower limit of the norm for copper concentration in urine, depending on the assumed daily urine volume, is from 5 µg/L for a daily urine volume of 2000 mL to 12.5 µg/L for a daily urine volume of 800 mL. In turn, the upper limit of the norm is 15 µg/L and 37.5 µg/L for a daily urine volume of 2000 mL and 800 mL, respectively.

In these studies, we assume 1 L of urine excreted per day as the average value. Taking into account the above reference values of copper in urine and the assumed average volume of urine excreted per day, we selected the following concentrations for testing: 10 µg/L (concentration 1), 50 µg/L (concentration 2) and 150 µg/L (concentration 3). Concentration 1 is within the lower limits of the norm or is slightly lower than this norm depending on the assumed daily volume of urine excreted, i.e., it belongs to low concentrations. Concentration 2 can be considered slightly elevated and concentration 3 can be considered very high.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Assessment of Struvite Nucleation and Crystallization Progress in Artificial Urine Without and in the Presence of Bacteria

The assessment of struvite nucleation and crystallization progress was performed in artificial urine, the composition of which is presented in Table 1. This composition is accepted in the literature [23,24].

The mineral concentration in artificial urine of this composition corresponds to the average 24 h urine concentration of a healthy adult. Due to the fact that we focus on struvite nucleation and growth, we did not add the first component from Table 1, CaCl2⋅2H2O, to the artificial urine. Adding this component would result in the formation of poorly crystalline and amorphous phases (PCaAP) that are also a component of this type of stone and would interfere with the possibility of objective assessment of the progress of struvite crystallization. A detailed description of the formation of struvite and PCaAP in infected urine is presented, for example, in Refs. [23,25,26,27].

Artificial urine was prepared by dissolving reagent-grade chemicals (Sigma Aldrich; Merck Group, Darmstadt, Germany) in deionized water. The resulting solution was filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe membrane filter and stored for a maximum of 48 h at 4 °C. The initial pH of the artificial urine was always controlled and was 5.80 ± 0.05.

The experiment was carried out without bacteria and in their presence. In the first case, the presence of bacteria was simulated by successive addition of an aqueous ammonia solution (NH3⋅H2O) at a concentration of 1.2 M. Such successive addition of such a solution increases the pH and provides ammonium ions, which closely mimics the process of urea decomposition in urine by urease-positive bacteria. This procedure was also used earlier—for example in Refs. [28,29]. In the second case, the experiment took place in the presence of urease-positive bacteria Proteus mirabilis (P. mirabilis). The strain used was isolated from a human urinary stone. The urine component TSB (Table 1) is used to stimulate bacterial growth. Before the experiment, bacteria were grown on a TSB overnight at 37 °C. From the obtained culture, a suspension of P. mirabilis was prepared in artificial urine at a concentration of 5 ⋅ 105 CFU/mL and incubated at 37 °C.

Both types of experiments, with and without bacteria, were performed up to a pH value of 9–9.5. This is because this is the highest pH that can be obtained in the presence of bacteria. Such a value in the presence of bacteria is obtained after 24 h of the experiment and does not increase further [30,31]. The pH of artificial urine was monitored using a digital pH meter (Elmetron CPC-401, Zabrze, Poland), which is accurate to 0.01. In this paper, we report average results with an accuracy of 0.1. The experiments were performed under thermostatic conditions at a temperature of 37.0 ± 0.5 °C, which was maintained at a constant level by circulating water in a water bath. In order to assess the repeatability of the results obtained, the experiments were performed at least three times.

The amount of crystallized struvite in the case of the experiment without bacteria was determined by weighing the dried samples after centrifugation (5702 Eppendorf) of urine at 4400 rpm for 10 min. The samples were weighed five times; then, the results were averaged and the measurement uncertainty was calculated. In the case of the experiment in the presence of bacteria, it is not possible to separate the crystallized struvite from the bacterial suspension. Therefore, the amount of crystallized struvite was determined indirectly by determining the magnesium content in the whole sediment containing both the bacterial suspension and the crystalline sediment. Magnesium is the main component of struvite; as such, its amount indicates the amount of crystallized struvite. For these analyses, a 1 mL sample containing crystals with bacterial suspension was centrifuged (Sigma 1–15) at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The obtained precipitate was dissolved in an aqueous solution of nitric acid (1:1, 65% HNO3 in double-distilled water). After mineralization, magnesium concentrations were determined by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS, SpectrAA-300 Varian, Palo Alto, CA, USA).

The source of copper in the presented studies was the standard solution of 1000 mg Cu2+ (CuCl2 in H2O) Titrisol® (Darmstadt, Germany). Starting from this standard solution, by diluting with water, a solution with a lower concentration of Cu2+ was obtained, which was then added to the samples in appropriate amounts to obtain samples with the Cu2+ concentrations selected for the study. The addition of aqueous CuCl2 to the artificial urine samples means that chloride anions have been added along with Cu2+. It should be noted, however, that four of the urine components (Table 1) contain chlorine anions and in amounts expressed in grams per liter that are orders of magnitude more than the chlorine anion added with Cu2+, meaning that the amount of chlorine introduced into the urine solution along with Cu2+ is of no significant importance in the context of the experiment being conducted.

To assess whether the Cu2+ ions introduced into the urine solution incorporated into the structure of struvite crystals, the samples were subjected to X-ray diffraction (XRD) testing. Two samples were tested, the first without the presence of copper (baseline) and the second sample with the highest concentration of Cu2+ ions (150 μg/L). Both samples were free of bacteria. The precipitates appearing during the experiments were taken at pH equal 9.5. In order to separate the precipitates formed from artificial urine, the samples were centrifuged (a centrifuge 5702 Eppendorf, 4400 rpm for 5 min (Hamburg Germany)). Then, the resultant precipitates were rinsed in distilled water three times and each time centrifuged. The prepared precipitates were dried at room temperature for 24 h and examined directly after drying. The prepared dry samples were examined by XRD using an X’pert PRO MPD diffractometer (PANalytical) (Almelo, The Netherlands). A Cu Kα radiation monochromatized by nickel filter was applied. The XRD patterns were measured in reflection mode in conventional Bragg–Brentano configuration for the range of 2θ from 10° to 90°. A continuous scan was used (step 0.0167°); the measurement time of one step was 30 s and total measurement time was approximately 20 min.

Samples prepared in the same manner as for XRD analysis were also subjected to Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. The aim of EDS studies was to assess whether copper is incorporated into the struvite structure (into its volume) and the aim of XPS was to assess whether copper is subject to adhesion/adsorption on the struvite faces as XPS provides surface-sensitive information, typically probing the top few nanometers of the sample. Additionally, the surface of the sample was imaged using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Apreo2S LoVac microscope (Waltham, MA, USA) using high vacuum mode. The sample was applied to a conductive carbon tape placed on the microscope stage. The samples were not sputtered with a conductive layer before imaging. EDS analysis was performed for one sample by analyzing images from four locations for a given sample, using magnifications in the range from 1000× to 10,000×, at an energy of 15 keV. XPS analysis was performed using an AXIS Ultra spectrometer (Kratos Analytical Ltd. (Manchester, UK)). The studies were conducted in ultra-high vacuum (UHV) conditions. Spectral analysis was performed, and additionally a table with quantitative analysis was prepared based on high-resolution elemental spectra. 2.2. Determination of the Viability of Bacteria and Spectrophotometric Measurements

To assess the effect of copper on struvite nucleation and growth, the turbidity of artificial urine was measured for all tested copper concentrations and for the control (no copper) as the absorbance of light at a specific wavelength. The optimized wavelength was estimated to be 600 nm (for experiments without bacteria and in the presence of bacteria). The absorbance was measured with a Schimadzu 2600 (Kyoto, Japan) (in the absence of bacteria) and Ultrospec 2000 (Pharmacia Biotech, Peapack, NJ, USA) spectrophotometer (in the presence of bacteria) using cuvettes with an optical path length of 10 mm. During all experiments, samples were taken at regular intervals and observed under an Opta Tech MN 800 (Warsaw, Poland) (in the absence of bacteria) and Nikon Eclipse TE2000-S (Tokyo, Japan) (in the presence of bacteria) optical microscope to assess the progression of struvite crystal growth, habit, and size. Specifically, these samples were prepared by taking a drop of artificial urine solution with the precipitated solid phase, i.e., struvite, placing it on a glass slide, and covering it with a cover slip. The samples were observed under transmitted light.

During the experiment, the influence of copper on the viability of bacteria was also determined at 0, 4, 8, and 24 h of incubation. A sample consisting of 1 mL of bacterial suspension with copper at the tested concentrations and without copper (control test) was taken and serially diluted in 0.9% NaCl. Then, the obtained suspension was inoculated on TSA (tryptic soy agar) medium. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the grown colonies were counted. The number of live bacteria was expressed as CFU/mL. The control was a suspension of bacteria in urine without added copper.

2.3. Assessment of the Influence of Copper on the Formation of Chemical Complexes in Artificial Urine—Theoretical Analysis

The explanation of the influence of copper on nucleation and growth of struvite in artificial urine is based on the theoretical analysis of chemical complexes formed in urine, both in the presence of copper and in its absence (control test). Such analysis was performed using the HySS version 4.0.31 (Hyperquad Simulation and Speciation) software [32]. By introducing the equilibrium constants (stability constants) of complex formation and introducing the initial molar concentrations of ions forming complexes in artificial urine, this software allows the calculation of molar concentrations of different chemical complexes in a given pH range. In this way, it becomes possible to analyze the influence of copper on the formation of chemical equilibria and on the formation of struvite crystals in artificial urine. The stability constants used in the HySS program were calculated using the EQUIL computer code [33] for a temperature of 37 °C.

3. Results

3.1. Effect of Cu2+ on Nucleation and Growth of Struvite in the Absence of Urease-Positive Bacteria

In the presented studies, three concentrations of Cu2+ were tested: concentration 1 = 10 μg/L corresponding to the lower limit of the norm of copper in urine, concentration 2 = 50 μg/L being slightly above the upper limit of the norm and concentration 3 = 150 μg/L significantly exceeding the permissible norms and defined in the literature as toxic to humans [20,21]. The obtained results are compared to the control sample (symbol 0) without Cu2+ ions. The process of nucleation and growth of struvite was assessed based on absorbance (Abs) measurements of artificial urine for the control sample (symbol 0) and samples containing Cu2+ (symbols 1, 2 and 3). A sudden increase in absorbance indicates the appearance of solid phases in the solution; in this case the expected solid phase is struvite. Absorbance measurements were performed at wavelength λ = 600 nm, which was selected based on the absorption spectrum of struvite in artificial urine compared to the absorption spectrum of Cu2+ in water with Cu2+ concentration of 1 mg/mL. The absorbance value for this wavelength is high for struvite, while for the Cu2+–water solution it is practically zero; therefore, for this wavelength, the presence of Cu2+ will not affect the absorbance measurement for struvite.

Figure 1 shows the dependence of Abs on pH, in the case where the presence of bacteria, or rather the activity of bacterial urease, was simulated by successive addition of 1.2 M aqueous ammonia solution in portions from 2 to 5 μL. From Figure 1 it can be concluded that the sudden increase in Abs occurs first for sample 3 with the highest Cu2+ concentration, then for sample 2, then for sample 1 and finally for the control sample 0 containing no Cu2+. Specifically, Abs ≅ 0.02 is reached for pH 7.97, 8.06, 8.12 and 8.24 for samples 3, 2, 1 and 0, respectively, with Cu2+ concentrations of 150, 50 10 and 0 μg/L, respectively. This means that copper causes a shift in struvite nucleation towards lower pH, which is not the desired effect. It should be noted, however, that this shift is not proportional to the Cu2+ concentration. While Cu2+ concentration 1 shifts nucleation towards lower pH the most, Cu2+ concentration 3 shifts this nucleation towards lower pH very slightly compared to Cu2+ concentration 2. From Figure 1 it also follows that the absorbance increase rate for all tested samples, including the control sample, is very similar.

The maximum absorbance value usually indicates the amount of solid phase formed. From Figure 1, based on the maximum absorbances for pH above 8.5, it can be concluded that the least solid phase can be expected in the case of the Cu2+ sample with concentration 1, slightly more for the Cu2+ sample with concentration 3 and the most for the Cu2+ sample with concentration 2. Although the difference in mass for samples 2 and 3 should not be significant, the maximum Abs values for these samples are not significantly different. However, based on Abs, determining the amount of precipitate formed for the control sample is somewhat problematic, because it seems that in this case the absorbance has not yet reached saturation for pH 9. Additionally, such inference about the mass of the formed solid precipitate based on the maximum absorbance may be unreliable because the amount of added aqueous ammonia solution for each tested Cu2+ concentration is slightly different (Table 2). This means that the formed precipitate is dispersed in a slightly different volume, which may affect the Abs value. However, it should be noted that in this case the amounts of added aqueous ammonia solution do not differ significantly (Table 2). In any case, weighing the dried crystalline precipitate seems more objective. Based on the obtained sample masses (Table 3), it can be stated that the masses are comparable for Cu2+ concentrations 2 and 3, and in the case of concentration 1, the mass of the solid precipitate is slightly lower compared to Cu2+ concentrations 2 and 3. These results seem to be consistent with the conclusions drawn from the maximum absorbance. For the control test (symbol 0), the weighed precipitate mass cannot be compared with the mass estimated from maximum absorbance at pH 9 because, as explained above, the absorbance has not reached saturation state at this pH.

Figure 2 is a set of microscopic images illustrating struvite crystals formed in artificial urine for the control test (Cu2+ concentration 0) and for all tested Cu2+ concentrations (1, 2 and 3) depending on pH. In the case of the control test (without Cu2+) the habit and morphology of struvite crystals is standard; i.e., the crystals show a characteristic coffin-like habit (Figure 2, column 0 and Figure 3a—top row) with the (001) face usually elongated along the crystallographic b axis. However, there are also crystals without the (001) face as shown in the bottom row of Figure 3a. Such crystals commonly occur during crystallization in urine and are nothing new. The morphology of struvite growing without any admixtures usually consists of such faces as (001) and (001¯), which are pedions, i.e., single faces that occur at the ends of the c axis and are not related by symmetry relations. The morphology of struvite characteristic of growth in urine also consists of domes of the type {h0l} and {0kl}, which are two non-parallel faces related to each other by a plane of symmetry (mirror plane). The {h0l}-type domes found in struvite are usually {101¯} and {101}. The dome {101¯} consists of the two (101¯) and (1¯01¯) faces, while the dome {101} consists of the two (101) and (1¯01) faces. In struvite there is also a {012} dome, which is formed by two (01¯2) and (012) faces. Sometimes the {012} dome is replaced by the {011} dome, and in some cases both of these domes are present in one crystal at the same time. The dome {011} consists of the two (01¯1) and (011) faces. In the morphology of struvite characteristic of crystallization processes in urine, the {010} pinacoid also sometimes occurs. In general, the pinacoid is an open form consisting of a pair of faces, each of which intersects a different end of the same crystallographic axis. In the case of {010}, this is a pair of faces (010) and (01¯0), which intersects the ends of the crystallographic b axis. In the absence of bacteria, slightly truncated corners are often observed [34].

In samples containing copper, in addition to the standard crystals characteristic of the control experiment, crystals with significantly truncated corners appear. The angle of these corner truncations suggests that these crystals are bounded by {011} rather than {012} form. These crystals are usually much larger than those of the standard habit (Figure 3b). In the presence of Cu2+, crystals lacking the {010} form also appear, resulting in the formation of sharp corners at the ends of the c-axis, as shown in Figure 3c. For this habit, a new form also appears, probably {102}. The occurrence of the {011} form, or the replacement of the {012} form by the {011} form, may be related to the interaction of Cu2+ ions and/or chemical complexes formed with Cu2+ with these faces.

It is well known that additives play a crucial role in crystal growth by influencing nucleation, growth rate, morphology, and crystal habit. Additives can promote or inhibit nucleation by modifying surface energy or solubility. They can also bind to specific crystal faces, altering their growth rates and leading to modified habits. So-called tailor-made additives are specially designed complexes or chemical compounds intended to produce crystals with specific habits, morphologies, or properties [35,36,37,38]. It is therefore possible that, in this case as well, appropriate chemical complexes formed in urine in the presence of Cu2+ may interact electrostatically with specific struvite faces and lead to an enhanced expression of these faces, since their relative growth rates may be reduced by binding of the respective complexes. Cu2+ ions and/or their complexes present in urine may also adsorb onto various struvite surfaces, which may result in an increased area of these surfaces relative to other faces.

One may also question whether electrostatic interaction of copper and/or its complexes with the corresponding struvite faces is even possible, considering the relatively low concentrations of copper in the studied system compared to other chemical complexes present at much higher concentrations (the analysis of chemical complexes is presented in Section 3.2). It should be noted that in urine a large proportion of ions are monovalent, in contrast to copper and magnesium ions, which are divalent. As is known, the valence of the cation has a fundamental influence on electrostatic interactions and ionic strength. A divalent cation at the same concentration contributes four times more to the ionic strength than a monovalent cation. Therefore, copper, as a divalent cation in the studied system, may interact electrostatically more effectively than monovalent cations present at much higher concentrations.

It should also be noted that struvite crystals exhibit spontaneous polarization, which means the existence of a net electric dipole along the c-axis. Specifically, the (001) face carries a negative and the (001¯) face a positive electric charge. On negatively charged faces, Cu2+ can adsorb preferentially, neutralizing polarization-induced charge more effectively and potentially altering growth kinetics, which can lead to modifications in habit and morphology. At this stage, however, these observations primarily provide a basis for more in-depth studies of ion competition in the investigated system.

The comparison of microscopic images in Figure 2 and Figure 3 indicates large differences in the absolute sizes of the crystals. Detailed analysis of struvite crystal dimensions grown in the absence of bacteria is presented in Figure 4a, which depicts absolute crystal lengths along the crystallographic a-axis (red symbols) and b-axis (blue symbols) as a function of Cu2+ concentration. Solid lines represent mean values, while hatched areas indicate the range between minimum and maximum measurements for each axis. These results were obtained by measuring approximately 50 struvite crystals for each Cu2+ concentration and the control in optical microscope images. Only crystals permitting reliable measurement of both axial dimensions were selected; clustered crystals or those not oriented perpendicular to the microscope optical axis were excluded to prevent measurement artifacts.

The analysis reveals that the smallest crystals form in the absence of Cu2+. At the lowest examined concentration 1 (10 μg/L of Cu2+), lengths along the b-axis increase substantially, whereas lengths along the a-axis show only minor enhancement. Furthermore, the spread between minimum and maximum b-axis lengths is greatest at this lowest Cu2+ concentration. Experiments conducted at the intermediate concentration 2 (50 μg/L of Cu2+) result in reduced crystal lengths along both axes. At the highest Cu2+ concentration 3 (150 μg/L), a slight decrease in crystal lengths along both axes is observed, with mean values comparable to control measurements. These dimensional changes are also illustrated in Figure 3. Notably, virtually no twinning—either penetration or contact—is observed throughout the experimental series.

In addition to absolute dimensions, the aspect ratio (AR)—defined as the ratio of crystal length along the b-axis (lb) to that along the a-axis (la)—exhibits concentration-dependent variation. These crystal dimensions are defined in Figure 2, panel a5. Figure 4b depicts the change in AR of struvite crystals as a function of Cu2+ concentration. The mean AR value increases slightly at concentration 1 compared with the control test. At concentration 2, the mean AR decreases marginally, whereas at concentration 3 it increases again slightly, attaining values comparable to the control test. The greatest spread between minimum and maximum AR values is observed at concentration 3.

Another interesting phenomenon occurring in samples containing Cu2+ is the existence of habits suggesting a decrease in struvite symmetry. When we look at a small crystal lying on a large crystal (Figure 3b), we see truncated corners in the small crystal, but only two. The Miller indices of the faces in the small crystal are identical to those in the large one. The truncated corners result from the appropriate growth rate of the {101} form in relation to the rate of the {011} form. The {101} form consists of two symmetrically equivalent faces (101) and (1¯01), which means that when the growth rate of this form in the crystal changes, all four corners should be equally truncated. However, this does not happen, which means that the symmetrically equivalent faces (101) and (1¯01) do not grow equally fast. It is similar in the case of the habit shown in Figure 3d. Here, a face appears, which, based on the angle at which the crystal is cut, can be interpreted as a (23¯0) face. However, this face is one of four faces belonging to the form (23¯0), which is a rhombic prism, to which three other faces belong: (230), (2¯3¯0), (2¯30). However, we do not observe these faces in the struvite habit.

Due to such differences in size, morphology and habit of growing crystals, it may be interesting to answer the question of whether the crystalline precipitate that is formed is definitely struvite; whether the solid phase that is formed does not have a lower symmetry than struvite, as may be suggested by the microscopic images of struvite in urine (Figure 3); and whether it is possible that Cu2+ is substituted for Mg2+ in the struvite structure in some percentage. On the one hand, copper (Cu2+) and magnesium (Mg2+) have the same electric charges and similar ionic radii (0.73 Å and 0.72 Å, respectively), which may favor such an exchange. On the other hand, despite our efforts, we have not found in the available literature any reports of Cu2+ replacing Mg2+ in the struvite structure. The literature sources only indicate that if there is an exchange of Mg2+ for Cu2+, it is in a dittmarite-type structure, which is monohydrate and crystallizes in a completely different space group. Therefore, it seems unlikely that Cu2+ replaces Mg2+ in any percentage in the struvite structure. However, we performed XRD, EDS and XPS studies to check this.

The XRD results illustrated in Figure 5 indicate that the obtained diffraction peaks differ in intensity; however, both spectra show the same symmetry, characteristic of struvite, its mm2 point group and crystallographic system, which is consistent with the reference spectrum for struvite in the International Center for Diffraction Data (ICDD PDF-2 version 2024) card no. 01-077-2303. This means that we are dealing with struvite and there is no reduction in the symmetry of the obtained solid phases, as could be suggested by the habits and morphologies illustrated in Figure 3.

To check whether Cu2+ or chemical complexes formed by Cu2+ are able to bind permanently to the struvite surface (adsorption leading to adhesion), XPS tests were performed at three points of the sample (Figure 6). The SEM photo of the tested crystals is presented in Figure 7a. It should be noted that, as described in the Materials and Methods, the resulting crystalline sediment was separated from the artificial urine by centrifugation. Then, the centrifuged crystalline sediment was rinsed three times in distilled water and centrifuged each time. The dried sample was subjected to SEM tests. This procedure aimed to separate the chemical elements present in the artificial urine from the tested crystals. However, repeated rinsing and especially centrifugation could have caused the crystals to be slightly crushed (Figure 7a), especially in the corners, and therefore differ in appearance from those presented in Figure 2 and Figure 3. However, Figure 7a shows significant differences in the crystal sizes, which is discussed above, and also shown in Figure 3 and Figure 4. A typical XPS survey spectrum of the obtained crystalline precipitate in the binding energy scale for the three examined points is shown in Figure 6. This spectrum shows characteristic positions of photoelectron peaks and places where peaks characteristic of Cu 2p should appear if copper was present in the sample. However, these peaks are not observed. A fragment of the spectrum in the binding energy range characteristic for Cu 2p is shown in the inset in Figure 6; this fragment of the spectrum proves the absence of copper in the surface layers of the sample. Table 4 presents a quantitative analysis prepared on the basis of XPS spectra (Figure 6).

The quantitative analysis presented in Table 4 indicates that the P/Mg ratio corresponds to the stoichiometric ratio of these elements in the structure of struvite. This result means that Cu2+ does not permanently bind to the struvite surfaces, i.e., there is no permanent adhesion. However, it should be noted that the sample with crystals was rinsed and centrifuged several times, which could have removed copper from the struvite surface. However, in the artificial urine solution, adsorption could have occurred with the participation of van der Waals forces or electrostatic interactions. It should be noted that struvite shows spontaneous polarization along the z axis, which justifies the occurrence of such interactions. Such a process would then justify the changes in the growth rate of individual faces and, consequently, the changes in the struvite habit (Figure 3). However, further analysis would be required to explain this phenomenon, which is beyond the scope of the research presented here.

To check whether Cu2+ is incorporated into the struvite structure, but into its deeper layers, we performed EDS analysis, the result of which is presented in Figure 7b. The presented EDS spectrum of the tested crystalline material concerns one measurement point out of the four measured. The inset shows the average results of the quantitative analysis (atomic percentage) from the four tested points ± standard deviation. Based on this spectrum, it can be concluded that no copper is detected in the tested sample. The EDS spectrum (Figure 7b) also indicates the presence of peaks for carbon and potassium. The peak for C is probably not caused by the actual presence of carbon in the sample, but rather by an artifact. A typical reason for such an artifact may be carbon contamination from the environment (air). Sometimes the EDS detector may introduce artifacts that appear as carbon peaks. On the other hand, the presence of K, in a very small atomic percentage, is probably a residue of KCl and/or KH2PO4, which are components of urine. Finally, it can be stated that the EDS spectrum confirms that the tested material is struvite; copper is not incorporated into the struvite structure. Moreover, the average P/Mg ratio from the four measurement points is 1.01 (inset in Figure 7b), which corresponds to the stoichiometric ratio of these elements in the struvite structure. This result is consistent with the XRD results (Figure 5).

In summary, the obtained XRD, XPS and EDS results indicate that copper is not incorporated into the structure of struvite, nor does it permanently bind to the faces of struvite. The observed changes in the habit and morphology of struvite could therefore, at this stage of the research, be explained in such a way that appropriate chemical complexes formed with the participation of copper can, in the artificial urine, interact electrostatically with atoms or ions on the appropriate faces of struvite, thus influencing the growth rate of these faces and consequently their size, but these interactions do not lead to permanent adhesion. After repeated rinsing of the crystalline material and centrifugation, no copper is found on the surfaces of struvite, which indicates a lack of strong adhesion.

The next paragraph presents a detailed analysis of chemical complexes that can potentially occur in urine in the presence of copper.

3.2. Analysis of Experimental Observations in the Context of the Formation of Chemical Complexes in Artificial Urine

In order to explain the experimental results obtained, in particular the shift in struvite nucleation towards lower pH in the presence of Cu2+ at different concentrations, chemical complexes that can be formed in artificial urine in the presence of Cu2+ ions were analyzed, depending on the increasing urine pH compared to the control test (without Cu2+ ions). In artificial urine, both in the case of the control test and in the presence of Cu2+ ions, numerous chemical reactions can occur, such as dissociation or hydrolysis. As a result, various chemical complexes are formed, which create different chemical equilibria. Using the HySS software (ver. 4.0.31) [32], we conducted an analysis of these chemical complexes, the aim of which was to determine the dominant chemical form of these complexes in the presence of Cu2+ ions at the tested concentrations (concentrations 1, 2, 3) compared to the control test (Cu2+ concentration 0 = 0 μg/L). In the speciation analysis, we focus in particular on the complexes that can be formed by Cu2+ ions and Mg2+, PO43− and NH4+ ions, as they are the constituent ions of struvite.

Understanding the structure and stability of chemical complexes in the growth solution, i.e., in artificial urine containing Cu2+ ions, is essential to comprehending the kinetics of struvite crystal growth in the presence of these ions and their influence on the crystal growth processes. The motivation to perform this speciation analysis is to learn the mechanism of formation of solid phases in artificial urine for increasingly lower pH with increasing Cu2+ ion concentration (Figure 1).

Table 5, column 0, presents the initial concentrations, cini, of individual ions resulting from the composition of artificial urine (Table 1) without the presence of Cu2+. In the absence of bacteria, their presence is simulated by successive addition of aqueous ammonia solution, as described in Section 2.1. Table 2 gives the amount of 1.2 M aqueous ammonia solution added. As shown in Table 2, the amount of aqueous ammonia solution added to each measurement series (control sample and in the presence of Cu2+ ions) is comparable but may affect the concentration of ions presented in Table 5, column 0, due to the slight increase in the sample volume (initial volume 25 mL). Therefore, the amount of ammonia solution added was considered when calculating the concentrations of individual ions presented in Table 5, column 0. Columns 0′, 1, 2 and 3 of Table 5 present the concentrations of all these ions for, respectively, the control test (without Cu2+) and Cu2+ concentrations 1, 2 and 3 after adding the aqueous ammonia solution, i.e., their final concentrations, cend. From Table 5, columns 0′, 1, 2 and 3, it can be seen that the molar concentration of individual ions is slightly reduced (compared with column 0), resulting from the addition of the aqueous ammonia solution, i.e., from the slight dilution of the samples.

After entering the final ion concentrations, cend, (Table 5) and stability constants of the formed complexes (Table 6) into the HySS computer program, the corresponding molar and percentage concentrations of chemical complexes in a given pH range were calculated. The results of this analysis, i.e., the concentrations of dominant complexes in artificial urine for the control sample and for samples with the presence of Cu2+ ions, are presented in Figure 8 and Figure 9.

It can be seen from Figure 8 that in the pH range where struvite is formed, copper occurs in the form of free Cu2+ ions (Figure 8a) and in the form of various complexes, e.g., Cu(OH)+, CuCit−, CuC2O4, Cu(OH)2, CuHPO4, Cu(NH3)22+, Cu(NH3)32+. Some of these complexes are electrically charged, which means that electrostatic interactions can potentially occur between these complexes and the struvite faces, which are electrically charged. This can lead to the observed changes in the struvite habit, but does not lead to permanent adhesion of these complexes to the struvite faces, as described in paragraph 3.1.

In the case of Figure 8g,h the molar concentrations were calculated not for the final concentrations presented in Table 5, but after the actual addition of 250 µL of aqueous ammonia solution, which corresponds to the pH = 7.5 obtained in the experiment. A pH of 7.5 was selected for analysis because it is characteristic of the initial stage of struvite formation. For this reason, the vertical lines indicate this pH and the arrows focus attention on the concentrations obtained for this pH. A detailed analysis of the chemical complexes formed with the participation of copper shows that their concentration strictly depends on pH. For example, for pH = 7.5, regardless of the concentration of copper ions, the largest part of copper ions introduced into urine is bound in the following complexes: CuCit− (42%), Cu(OH)+ (35%), Cu(OH)2 (13.2%), Cu(NH3)2+ (4.5%), CuC2O4 (4.5%), CuHPO4 (2.6%), Cu(NH3)22+ (1%) and Cu(C2O4)22− and Cu2+ (0.8%). With the increase in pH, the percentage of copper complexes with NH3 increases significantly. For example, for pH = 8.1, regardless of the concentration of copper ions, the largest part of copper ions introduced into urine is associated with Cu(OH)+ (32%), CuCit− (21%), Cu(OH)2 (18.9%), Cu(NH3)22+ (12.8%) Cu(NH3)32+ (10.3%), Cu(NH3)2+ (3.8%), and Cu(NH3)42+ (1.3%) and about 1% are complexes such as CuHPO4, Cu(C2O4)22−, CuC2O4, Cu2+.

In the same pH range, magnesium occurs in the form of free Mg2+ ions and various complexes can be formed with Mg2+ ions, i.e., MgHCit, MgCit−, MgOH+, MgC2O4, MgSO4, MgHPO4. The molar concentrations of some of these complexes are shown in Figure 9. It should be noted that in artificial urine, the molar concentration of Mg2+ ions is much higher than the molar concentration of Cu2+ ions introduced into it. Therefore, the concentrations of the forming magnesium complexes are also much higher compared to the concentrations of copper complexes. Furthermore, a first look at these graphs presented in Figure 9 may suggest that there are no differences in the molar concentrations of the presented ions and/or complexes depending on the Cu2+ concentration. However, these differences do exist and to make them visible, each graph shows an enlargement of its fragment for pH from approximately 7.5 to 8, characteristic of the first phase of struvite formation. These enlarged fragments show how Cu2+ ions change the chemical equilibrium between different magnesium complexes. Based on these enlarged fragments, it is observed that with the increase in the concentration of copper ions, the concentration of free magnesium ions and complexes such as MgC2O4, MgSO4, MgOH+ and MgNH4PO4, increases (see Figure 9a,c,e,g,h). Moreover, the formation of these complexes shifts towards lower pH with the increase in the concentration of copper ions.

However, the concentrations of such complexes as MgCit−, HPO42−, and MgHPO4 (Figure 9b,d,f) decrease with the increase in Cu2+ concentration in the entire pH range characteristic of struvite crystallization. It is usually assumed that such a decrease in the concentration of chemical complexes results from the fact that they are used to create solid phases. In the analyzed case, the indicated complexes can form struvite according to the following reactions:MgCit− + HPO42− + NH4+ → MgNH4PO4 + HCit2−(1)MgHPO4 + NH4+ + OH− → MgNH4PO4 + H2O(2)

Additionally, Figure 9h (inset for pH 7.5 to 8) shows that the formation of the MgNH4PO4 complex, which ultimately gives struvite, shifts slightly towards lower pH values as the copper ion concentration increases. This trend is consistent with the presented experimental studies (Figure 1). Furthermore, the inset for pH 8.76 to 8.78 in Figure 9h shows that the percentage concentration of the MgNH4PO4 complex increases with increasing copper concentration, which means that the final amount of struvite formation also increases. This is consistent with the described experimental observations, in particular with the results presented in Table 3, which shows the percentage increase in the mass of the crystalline phase with increasing Cu2+ concentration compared to the control test. This is also consistent with the data presented in Figure 1, from which it can be concluded that the amount of the solid phase increases slightly depending on the Cu2+ concentration (a detailed analysis is provided in Section 3.1).

3.3. Effect of Cu2+ on Nucleation and Growth of Struvite in the Presence of Urease-Positive Bacteria

The graph of the dependence of absorbance on pH in the presence of bacteria is presented in Figure 10. Based on this figure, it can be seen that absorbance increases equally quickly for all tested Cu2+ concentrations, as in the case of the control test (without Cu2+) up to pH of about 7.8. Starting from this pH, Abs for concentration 1 of Cu2+ increases slightly faster. From pH = 8, Abs for concentration 2 of Cu2+ also increases slightly faster in comparison with the control test. The slowest, even slower than for the control test, Abs increases for the highest Cu2+ concentration = 150 µg/L. These observations may suggest that the formation of the solid phase occurs more efficiently in the presence of Cu2+ concentrations 1 and 2 and least efficiently for concentration 3 in comparison with the control test. For Cu2+ concentrations 1 and 2, the formation of the solid phase is shifted towards lower pH, and for Cu2+ concentration 3 towards higher pH in comparison with the control test. This is a rather subtle effect, although it is consistent with the trends observed in the absence of bacteria (Figure 1).

From Figure 10, it can also be seen that the maximum absorbance values (for pH around 9) for Cu2+ concentrations 0, 1 and 2 are comparable. In the case of Cu2+ concentration 3, the absorbance is the highest for this pH, which could suggest that the amount of solid sediment formed is the highest in this case. However, the measurement of Mg2+ content in the samples, which indicates the amount of struvite formed, does not confirm this. Figure 11 shows the Mg2+ content for all the tested samples depending on the incubation time of bacteria in artificial urine. The incubation time is closely related to the increasing pH. For the first few hours, the pH remains constant, reaching 6 after about 4 h, and then begins to rise, reaching values between 7.2 and 7.7 by 6 h. By 8 h, it is around 8.7 to 9.0, and this high pH (9.5–9.8) persists for up to 24 h. The results presented in this figure could suggest that the Mg2+ content varies slightly for individual samples; however, considering the standard deviation presented by the vertical lines, it should rather be assumed that the amount of Mg2+ is comparable for all samples, and therefore the amount of struvite formed is also comparable for all samples. Due to such a standard deviation, it is also rather impossible to compare these results with the amount of solid sediment formed in the absence of bacteria. On the other hand, it should be emphasized that in the case of working with organisms such as bacteria, standard deviations of such an order are nothing surprising.

Figure 12 illustrates the crystallization progress for the control test and samples with different Cu2+ concentrations after 8 and 24 h of the experiment. From this set of microscopic images, it can be seen that in experiments with bacterial presence, crystal dimensions similarly increase with Cu2+ addition, most notably at the lowest concentration (10 μg/L), as Figure 12, panel b2 illustrates. Such large crystals coexist with markedly smaller crystallites (Figure 12, panel b3). Struvite crystals forming in the presence of bacteria do not exhibit corner truncation or other habit modifications characteristic of bacteria-free experiments (Figure 3), consistent with reports in the literature. The results presented, for example, in Ref. [34] demonstrate that struvite crystals growing in the presence of Proteus mirabilis develop more regular habits, with reduced variability in relative growth rates compared to bacteria-free conditions. Consequently, the crystal habits observed in this experiment represent a resultant influence of both Cu2+ ions and bacterial presence. This underscores that analysis of struvite crystal dimensions in bacteria-free systems provides more reliable results by revealing the specific effects of Cu2+ ions on crystal habit while eliminating bacterial contributions.

The literature review offers insights into why Cu2+ and bacterial presence do not induce changes in the habit and morphology of struvite crystals that are characteristic of non-bacterial growth. Proteus mirabilis bacteria have negatively charged polymers in the outer layer of their cell wall, e.g., lipopolysaccharide (LPS). It is known from the literature that bacteria can release lipopolysaccharides into the urine solution during growth and interact with chemical complexes, which inhibits the effect of these complexes on the habit and/or morphology of struvite crystals. This hypothesis is supported by data in the literature. For example, from Refs. [42,43,44] it is known that in struvite stones formed as a result of urinary tract infections involving Proteus mirabilis, LPS can participate in the formation of stones, mainly by binding ions. Negatively charged LPS can, through electrostatic interactions, bind free cations in urine (such as calcium and magnesium ions), causing the accumulation of these ions around bacteria, which may affect the process of struvite formation. It can therefore be assumed that LPS can also bind copper ions. Positively charged ions interact more often with Gram-negative bacteria, such as Proteus mirabilis, via LPS. It is known from the literature [45,46] that copper interacts with Proteus mirabilis, influencing its virulence factors and pathogenicity. Although it should be noted that in Refs. [45,46] much higher concentrations of Cu2+ were studied compared to those presented in these studies.

As for the effect of Cu2+ on the number of bacteria, the results in this matter are presented in Figure 13. This figure shows that the number of bacteria for samples containing Cu2+ is comparable to the number of bacteria for the control test for 0, 4 and 24 h of bacterial incubation. This indicates that Cu2+ does not have a bactericidal effect on these bacteria, but the bacteria also do not benefit from the presence of Cu2+. A certain deviation here is the number of bacteria for the 8th hour of incubation. The results presented in Figure 13 could suggest that in this case the number of bacteria is lower for the concentration 2 of Cu2+ = 50 μg/L. However, it should be noted that the number of bacteria is expressed in values of the order of 107 CFU/mL, where CFU denotes colony-forming units per mL. For the control test and for Cu2+ concentrations 1 and 3, the number of bacteria is of the order of 4 × 107 CFU/mL, and for the concentration 2 of Cu2+ it is of the order of 2 × 107 CFU/mL. It follows that the order of magnitude does not change, so such changes in the number of bacteria are considered small, although it seems that this concentration requires a deeper analysis, which, however, goes beyond the research presented in this paper. The conclusion is therefore that Cu2+ at the tested concentrations does not have a bactericidal or beneficial effect on Proteus mirabilis. Sources in the literature indicate that the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for Proteus is 625 μg/mL [47]. MIC is a parameter characterizing antibacterial ability, and the lower its value, the stronger the bacteriostatic ability. We can therefore assume that the Cu2+ concentrations we tested are too low, and this is the reason why we do not observe Cu2+ showing bacteriostatic or bactericidal ability in relation to Proteus mirabilis.

4. Conclusions

The XRD results indicate that the crystalline precipitate formed in the presence of copper is a classic struvite, the same as that formed in the control test (without Cu2+). Both in the presence of bacteria and in the absence of them, there is a slight shift in the formation of struvite in artificial urine towards lower pH values; however, this shift is not proportional to the copper concentration. This is not a beneficial effect. The greatest shift towards lower pH is observed for the lowest Cu2+ concentration (10 µg/L). At the same time, it is observed that in the presence of Cu2+, the struvite crystals formed are larger by a certain percentage than the standard ones (without the presence of copper) and have a changed morphology and/or habit. These changes in the habit and/or morphology of struvite cannot be explained by permanent adhesion of copper or its chemical complexes to struvite surfaces because EDS and XPS studies show that there is no permanent incorporation of copper into either the bulk or surface structure of struvite. At this point, it can be assumed that copper and/or chemical complexes formed in the presence of copper interact with the struvite surfaces in urine, which leads to a modification of the growth rate of these surfaces, which ultimately leads to the indicated differences in the habit and morphology of struvite. However, further studies are necessary to clarify this issue in detail.

It should also be emphasized that in the presence of bacteria, only an increased crystal size is observed in the presence of copper, without changing the habit and morphology. This observation can be explained by the fact that negatively charged LPS released by bacteria into the urine can interact with chemical complexes, which consequently do not interact with the struvite faces and, consequently, do not lead to a change in the habit and/or morphology of struvite crystals.

The amount of the formed solid phase increases slightly in the presence of copper compared to the control test. However, in the presence of bacteria this effect is not observed.

In relation to the Proteus mirabilis, copper, in the tested concentrations, does not affect the viability of the bacteria, which means that it does not show either bacteriostatic or bactericidal effects.

The effect of copper on struvite nucleation and growth was explained based on the speciation analysis of chemical complexes. This analysis indicates that in the artificial urine solution, copper forms various chemical complexes such as Cu(OH)+, CuCit−, CuC2O4, Cu(OH)2, CuHPO4, Cu(NH3)2+, Cu(NH3)22+, and Cu(NH3)32+, which affect the formation of complexes with Mg2+, PO43−, such as MgHCit, MgCit−, MgOH+, MgC2O4, MgSO4, and MgHPO4, which are crucial for the formation of struvite. In particular, the formation of some of these complexes shifts towards lower pH with the increase in copper concentration, which explains the shift in struvite appearance at lower pH.
