Prognostic factors affecting the ruptured intracranial aneurysms: A 9-year multicenter study in Fujian, China
Authors: Qiu-Yu Huang, Qing Huang, Shao-Wei Lin, Fan Wang, Yi Sun, Yi-Le Zeng, Bang Liu, Ying-Ying Cai, Ze-Long Chen, Si-Ying Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:A multicenter retrospective study was conducted to explore the factors affecting short-term prognosis and long-term outcomes of intracranial aneurysms (IA) rupture. Further, the prognosis prediction model was constructed based on survival analysis, contributing to the development of prevention strategies for aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage.Methods:Data of 1280 patients with IA rupture were gathered between 2014 and 2022 in Fujian, China. Logistic regression was implemented to study the short-term prognostic factors of IA rupture. Survival analysis of 911 patients among them was performed to explore the long-term outcome status by Cox risk assessment. Nomogram prognosis models were constructed using R software.Results:The findings displayed that blood type O (OR = 1.79; P = 0.019), high systolic pressure (OR = 1.01; P < 0.001), Glasgow Coma score (GCS) 9–12 (OR = 2.73; P = 0.022), GCS < 9 (OR = 3.222; P = 0.006), diabetes (OR = 2.044; P = 0.040), and high white blood cell count (OR = 1.059, P = 0.040) were core influencing factors for poor short-term prognosis. Survival analysis revealed that age > 60 years (HR = 2.87; P = 0.001), hypertension (HR = 1.95; P = 0.001), conservative (HR = 6.89; P < 0.001) and endovascular treatment (HR = 2.20; P = 0.001), multiple ruptured IAs (HR = 2.37; P = 0.01), Fisher 3 (HR = 1.68; P = 0.09), Fisher 4 (HR = 2.75; P = 0.001), and Hunt-Hess 3 (HR = 0.55; P = 0.05) were the major risk factors for terrible long-term outcomes.Conclusions:People over 60 years with characteristics of type O blood, high systolic pressure, diabetes, high white blood cell count, and onset GCS < 12 will have more complications and a worse short-term prognosis. Those aged > 60 years with hypertension, conservative and endovascular treatment, multiple ruptured IAs, Fisher ≥ 3 and Hunt-Hess 3 have a greater risk of poor long-term prognosis.

1. Introduction

Globally, stroke is increasing year by year as the population ages.[1,2] Intracranial aneurysm (IA) is a local pathological expansion caused by vascular median injury.[3] About 0.7% to 1.9% of IAs may rupture under inducements, leading to aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). The research on the natural history of aSAH (Pakarinen Research) has shown that IA rupture seriously threatens human life with a 5-year mortality of 65% to 70%.[4–6] The prognosis of IA rupture is a complicated process involving various factors.[7,8] Serious complications that cause death and disability after aSAH were approximately 30%, with cerebral vasospasm (CVS) and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) as the highest.[9–11] Besides, individual behaviors, aneurysm characteristics and intervention manners can also affect the outcomes.[12,13] Considering such hazards, it is of positive significance to explore early screening methods for high-risk groups with a poor prognosis.

Nomogram is a function curve showing independent variables in coordinates, providing quick estimation and prediction of survival state. Nomogram has been applied to the current outcome research of various diseases based on its novelty and reliability.[14] Prognostic risk models of IA rupture are still rarely explored so far, and we aimed to carry out survival analysis in multiple observation centers in Fujian, China. Nomogram models were constructed to show the survival status intuitively for providing innovative treatment strategies.

2. Material and Methods

2.1. Study design and setting

Case collection was conducted according to the 2010 World Health Organization International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (I60.0–I60.7) through a special database of stroke centers in each institution. The complete data of patients diagnosed with IA rupture were collected from several provincial and municipal authoritative medical institutions in Fujian, China from 2014 to 2022.

Patients were followed up regularly by telephone, Internet or face-to-face interviews. Information was collected every 3 to 6 months, including out-of-hospital examination and survival status. The initial follow-up observation point was the moment of diagnosis in hospital, and lifetime was measured in days. Those who were lost to follow-up, died for other reasons, or survived at the cutoff time were considered to be censored. The deadline for follow-up was December 1, 2022.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (2021-434). Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

2.2. Study participants

The inclusion criteria were as follows: aged > 18 years and first onset; patients with IA confirmed by computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography or digital subtraction angiography; patients qualified to receive stroke (including SAH) treatment in accordance with “China Stroke Center Process Certification and Quality Control Assessment Standards”; no major change in the size and number of beds during the study period; and complete case data. Those with a family history of SAH, serious chronic diseases and a history of stroke were excluded.

2.3. Variables

The overall survival (OS) was applied as the survival evaluation index. The short-term prognosis status was assessed according to Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS), and World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) was introduced into quantifying life quality after aSAH.[15] Patients with IAs rupture were divided into good (GOS: 1–3) and poor (GOS: 4–5) outcome groups.[14,16] Hunt-Hess Rating Scale, Glasgow Coma Score (GCS), and Fisher imaging classification were used to evaluate acute brain injury, brainstem function and DCI, respectively.[16,17]

2.4. Data measurement

The data including disease history, individual factors, physiological and biochemical indexes, clinical manifestations (onset time, GCS, Hunt-Hess, Fisher, location and size of IAs), intervention status (therapy time, surgical method and complications) and discharge evaluation (GOS and WFNS) were collected. Survival analysis was performed for 911 patients among them, and Nomogram models were further constructed.

2.5. Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Log-Rank was used for univariate analysis. Cox proportional hazards models were implemented to screen the independent prognostic factors. Hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated. Kaplan–Meier was introduced to analyze the survival rates and construct survival curves. Prognosis nomogram, as well as calibration and decision curves were constructed utilizing R 3.5.3 and Empower Stats.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic characteristics

A total of 1280 patients confirmed with IAs rupture were collected and divided into a Good group (977 patients) and a Poor group (303 patients). There were no significant differences in the demographic characteristics (sex, ABO blood types, drinking, marriage, education), specific physiological and biochemical status (pulse pressure, natremia, apolipoprotein-A1/B, high density lipoprotein, low density lipoprotein, triglyceride, total cholesterol, platelets), chronic or family disease history (stroke, CHD, hyperlipidemia, hypertension family history, stroke family history) between patients with good and poor prognoses (Table 1 and Supplemental Tables S1–S5, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MD/J593, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/MD/J594, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/MD/J595, Supplemental Digital Content 4, http://links.lww.com/MD/J596, Supplemental Digital Content 5, http://links.lww.com/MD/J597). However, the 2 groups showed statistical differences in individual factors (age, smoking history), specific physiological and biochemical status (heart rate, systolic blood pressure/diastolic blood pressure [SBP/DBP], white blood cell [WBC], hemoglobin, kalemia, calcium, temperature, total protein, lactate dehydrogenase, glucose, creatine kinase), chronic or family disease history (hypertension, diabetes, diabetes family history), and brain injury assessment (GCS, Hunt-Hess, Fisher) (Table 1 and Supplemental Tables S1–S5, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MD/J593, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/MD/J594, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/MD/J595, Supplemental Digital Content 4, http://links.lww.com/MD/J596, Supplemental Digital Content 5, http://links.lww.com/MD/J597).

3.3. Multivariate analysis of risk factors affecting short-term outcome

Further multivariate logistic analysis showed that GCS 9–12 (OR = 2.73, 95% CI [1.15–6.47], P = .022), GCS < 9 (OR = 3.222, 95% CI [1.392–7.458], P = .006), age > 60 years (OR = 1.925, 95% CI [1.154–3.212), P = .012], blood type O [OR = 1.793, 95% CI [1.102–2.918], P = .019), diabetes (OR = 2.044, 95% CI [1.033–4.045], P = .040], high SBP [OR = 1.017, 95% CI [1.007–1.026], P < .001] and WBC [OR = 1.059, 95% CI [1.003–1.119], P = .040] were the independent risk factors for poor prognosis of IAs rupture (Table 2).

4. Discussion

IA is a cerebrovascular disease with an extremely poor prognosis, and its rupture can lead to aSAH and a variety of complications, such as cerebral infarction, vascular spasm, and hydrocephalus.[3] These complications increase the cost of treatment and the probability of poor prognosis, indicating the need for new strategies to reduce the incidence of IAs complications. However, before developing specific interventions, it is of great importance to understand the factors affecting the prognosis of IAs. In this study, we analyzed the risk factors associated with short-term prognosis and long-term survival of IAs and constructed a nomogram prognostic model for predicting OS. The results showed that people over 60 years with characteristics of type O blood, diabetes, SBP, WBC and onset GCS < 12 will have more complications and a worse short-term prognosis. Additionally, age > 60 years, hypertension, conservative and endovascular treatment, multiple IA ruptures, and Fisher classification ≥ 3 increased the risk of poor long-term prognosis.

Age is one of the most critical factors affecting aSAH outcomes. The mortality of patients under 50 years old was just 0.6% to 0.9%, while that of people over 80 years old surged to 21.4%.[18] Large-scale prognostic surveys also confirmed that age ≥ 60 years was an independent core risk factor for poor prognosis of IA rupture. The effect of age on poor prognosis was particularly pronounced in females, and it is speculated that it would be related to vascular weakness caused by a decline in estrogen after menopause.[19] Consistently, we also found age was associated with both short-term prognosis and mortality risk of IAs rupture. The risk of poor short-term prognosis and long-term death in patients aged ≥ 60 years was 1.92 times (95% CI: 1.15–3.21) and 2.87 times (95% CI: 1.54–5.33) higher than that of those under 60 years old, respectively. Previous studies have demonstrated that injury factors such as hypertension and atherosclerosis may cause vascular compliance to decrease, resulting in a significantly increased chance of rebleeding. In addition, acute intracranial hypertension and arterial stenosis are the main causes of hydrocephalus after aSAH in older patients.[20–22] Based on the results, more emphasis should be placed on screening of elderly patients to prevent IA rupture or treat potential unruptured IAs timely, thus improving patients’ survival and outcomes.

Combined chronic systemic diseases, especially hypertension and diabetes, are prominent foundations for poor outcomes of stroke.[23,24] Hypertension results in decline of cerebral ischemic tolerance, and vascular dysfunction after SAH further promotes the activation of Ca2+ channel together with light chain myosin phosphorylation, which aggravates the contraction of smooth muscle cells and leads to serious complications such as CVS and DCI.[25] Meanwhile, hypertension is one of the most dangerous inducements for postoperative ischemic cerebral infarction in IA patients. Hypertension may cause extensive intimal hyperplasia, causing poor surgical vascular conditions to increase the difficulty of the operation and raising cerebral edema risk and secondary bleeding greatly.[26] Nastasovic et al[27] demonstrated that 46% of patients developing CVS after aSAH suffered from hypertension, which indicated that the hypertensive population was susceptible to the complications of IAs rupture, and adverse prognostic events. Furthermore, Kurtz revealed that blood glucose was positively correlated with stroke mortality.[28] A large-scale follow-up study found that elevated blood glucose was significantly associated with mortality within 14 days after aSAH, and further confirmed the significant impact of glucose on stroke prognosis.[27] There is extensive systemic inflammatory basis for diabetics, and SAH induces intracranial hypertension and cerebral edema, affecting neuroendocrine function and insulin resistance.[29] Moreover, hyperglycemia induced the expression of p-selectin and von Willebrand factor (vWF), enhancing platelet activation and the release of intracellular oxidative stress to further aggravate the systemic inflammatory injury.[29] The risk of DCI in diabetic patients after SAH was higher compared with non-diabetic ones, which may be related to the decrease in nitric oxide synthase activity induced by impaired glucose tolerance.[30] In our study, patients with diabetes and high SBP were 2.04 times (95% CI [1.03–4.04]) and 1.02 times (95% CI [1.00–1.02]) more likely to have a poor prognosis, respectively. Moreover, the long-term mortality risk of aSAH patients with hypertension was 1.96 times (95% CI: 1.23–3.09) higher than that of the population with normal pressure. In summary, actively stabilizing blood pressure and glucose is still the key to improving stroke outcome, and maintaining the homeostasis to the greatest extent is an effective strategy to reduce complications and prolong patients’ lifespan.

Recent research has reported that ABO blood type may be involved in various cerebrovascular diseases. Williams indicated that rs505922 and rs651007 mutations in ABO blood type genes were strongly correlated with stroke.[31] Mechanistic exploration revealed that the ABO blood type system was inextricably linked to the expression of critical coagulation factors such as FVIII and vWF in the body. The susceptibility to cerebral hemorrhage in people with O blood type has been proved. In type O groups, the plasma vWF level can be reduced by about 25% to 30%. As a key for mediating platelet adhesion, vWF plays a role in promoting platelet activation and physiological aggregation processes, and its deletion will increase the bleeding risk significantly.[32] An epidemiological survey also confirmed that cerebral hemorrhage incidence in people with O blood type was 1.33 times (95% CI [1.25–1.42]) than that with non-O blood type, and postoperative rebleeding was also increased.[33,34] For hemorrhagic diseases, blood type O groups have more complications and deterioration risk, which may be core factors for poor outcomes. However, some scholars hold different opinions that there were no significant correlations between blood types and aSAH prognosis.[35] The relationship between the 2 remains controversial, and in-depth exploration in this field is still of positive significance. To avoid fatal complications and adverse prognosis, patients with O blood type need to be paid more attention clinically and receive early therapy actively.

GCS combined with neurological assessment is an important reference for the long-term survival of stroke.[36] The lower GCS means the more serious acute brain injury as well as the higher fatal complication risks such as intracranial hypertension, CVS, and DCI.[37,38] Hunt-Hess grade is another momentous evaluation system for stroke outcome, and it is a meaningful basis for rapid identification and treatment of critically ill patients. Studies have verified that aSAH not only promotes the release of MMP-9 but also destroys the basement membrane and pivotal junction proteins such as Claudin-5, ZO-1 and ROCK, leading to blood-brain barrier damage and thus inducing brain edema.[39] Existing data demonstrated that the case mortality rate of Hunt-Hess grade I–III was only 13%, while that of grade IV–V reached 72%. High Hunt-Hess grade reflects obvious cerebral ischemia and abnormal cell metabolism, which led to diffusing intracranial hypertension under synergistic action of the inflammatory cascade and aquaporin, and consequently affected the prognosis seriously.[40,41] Fisher is a reliable indicator to evaluate the probability and severity of CVS after SAH. CVS is the main complication of IA rupture and the main cause of death and disability.[42,43] Severe cerebral hypoxia occurs in CVS stage, and harmful metabolites trigger oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, promoting TXA2 for neuronal inflammation.[44] In summary, standardizing the use of various clinical neurological scoring systems is an important part of improving the prognosis of SAH.

In our study, patients without surgery had the highest mortality risk (HR = 6.89, 95% CI: 3.85–12.32). They failed to have surgery on time or miss the best therapeutic opportunity because of the older age, prominent chronic diseases, economic situation, or patient’s will. These patients were often complicated with acute intracranial hypertension or rebleeding, and therefore the mortality increased. In terms of therapeutic plans, endovascular interventions showed a higher risk of long-term death than clipping. The International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial found that the 5-year mortality rate of patients with intravascular therapy was slightly lower than that of clipping, but there was no significant difference in the long-term living ability.[13] Intravascular therapy has the characteristics of less trauma and high efficiency, while clipping takes the advantages of stability and low recurrence. Therefore, reasonable choices should be made according to the individualized conditions.[45]

A nomogram prediction model was further constructed to conveniently show the IAs rupture outcomes based on survival analysis. The model has a positive reference for the development of prevention strategies. However, our research also has limitations. Firstly, although this is a multicenter study with a long time span, the quality of the institutions is uneven and the sample size was still limited, the predictability is therefore insufficient. We will follow up with precision in organizing databases and setting strict criteria to eliminate cases or institutions with poor quality. At the same time, we will continue to join with high-quality research centers to make the study more representative. Secondly, since this is a retrospective study, the results were inevitably biased, which would lead to an over-or under-estimation of the association. In addition, psychological impact had not been considered in the survey. Despite such limitations, these findings may still have a high clinical impact, but the relevance should still be validated in a larger cohort and multicenter and randomized clinical settings. These deficiencies will be improved in follow-up study to make conclusions more representative.

5. Conclusions

People over 60 years with characteristics of type O blood, high systolic pressure, diabetes, high WBC, and onset GCS < 12 will have more complications and a worse short-term prognosis. Those aged > 60 years with hypertension, conservative and endovascular treatment, multiple ruptured IAs, Fisher ≥ 3 and Hunt-Hess 3 have a greater risk of poor long-term prognosis. The nomogram prognosis model based on these characteristic achievements shows excellent accuracy and reliability, providing a reference for prevention strategies. In the prevention of SAH, the O blood population as a bleeding prone group, which needs to attract more attention and receive earlier treatment. Meanwhile, they should control high-risk factors for stroke actively and avoid the occurrence of adverse prognosis.
