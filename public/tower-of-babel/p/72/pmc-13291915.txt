Mechanism of action of soybean oil in arthropods: mortality results from tracheal infiltration and obstruction of gas exchange structures
Authors: Ricardo Castro-Torres, Jorge Valdez-Carrasco, Esteban Rodríguez-Leyva, Lauro Soto-Rojas, J Refugio Lomeli-Flores
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOils are widely used in arthropod pest control, yet their modes of action remain poorly understood and often differ across studies. To investigate the mechanism of action of a commercial soybean oil formulation, we evaluated its physical and toxicological effects on three arthropod species across several developmental stages: adults and nymphs of Uroleucon sonchi (L.) (Hemiptera: Aphididae), adults and eggs of Tetranychus urticae Koch (Trombidiformes: Tetranychidae), and larvae and eggs of Plutella xylostella (L.) (Lepidoptera: Plutellidae). Adults, immature stages, and eggs were either topically treated or immersed in 2% soybean oil, and mortality was assessed alongside morphological and ultrastructural analyses using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Bright-field microscopy, Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM), Structured Illumination Microscopy (SIM), and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). Immersion caused immediate mortality in U. sonchi and T. urticae, linked to oil penetration through spiracles and tracheal flooding, confirmed by fluorescent oil markers and bright-field, SIM, and CLSM imaging. In contrast, P. xylostella larvae exhibited partial and delayed mortality, likely due to spiracular morphology. Egg susceptibility varied by species and age. Egg hatch was completely inhibited in T. urticae, whereas only recently laid P. xylostella eggs were fully susceptible. SEM revealed extensive oil coverage and obstruction of respiratory structures, although TEM showed no damage to the cuticle. These findings indicate that soybean oil induces mortality by physically obstructing gas exchange structures rather than through chemical toxicity or cuticular disruption, and that spiracle architecture may modulate susceptibility. The implications for pest management programs are briefly discussed.

Introduction

Lipid-based molecules exhibit lethal effects on various developmental stages of numerous arthropod species. Two primary mechanisms have been proposed to explain the mode of action of these molecules: spiracular obstruction, which leads to respiratory failure, and molecular damage or disruption of the cuticle (Taverner et al. 2001, Stadler and Buteler 2009). Of these, the most widely supported mode of action is spiracular obstruction.

Studies have demonstrated that when larvae of Epiphyas postvittana (Walker) (Lepidoptera: Tortricidae) and Anticarsia gemmatalis Hübner (Lepidoptera: Erebidae) were submerged in mineral oil, the oil infiltrated the spiracles, tracheae, and tracheoles, ultimately causing asphyxiation (Taverner et al. 2001, Stadler and Buteler 2009). Similarly, in Pediculus humanus capitis De Geer (Psocodea: Pediculidae), a dimethicone treatment obstructs spiracles and inhibits water excretion, increasing osmotic stress and resulting in gut rupture and mortality (Burgess 2009). In cockroaches, mineral oil-induced mortality was observed only after full immersion, whereas topical applications had no effect, further reinforcing the idea that mortality in these insects results primarily from spiracular and tracheal obstruction (Buteler and Stadler 2011). Collectively, such studies, often involving high oil volumes, have led to the conclusion that suffocation is the primary mode of action. However, experiments using lower oil volumes have revealed effects even in the absence of tracheal penetration, which suggests other modes of action, such as toxicity or cuticular permeability (Najar-Rodríguez et al. 2008), yet results have been inconsistent across oil types and arthropod species.

The observed variation in oil effects may be a result of arthropod interspecific differences in morphology, physiology, and size. Key traits influencing susceptibility include the accessibility and organization of respiratory openings, the structure and chemical composition of the cuticle or eggshell, and the relative capacity of different life stages to tolerate temporary disruption of gas exchange. In insects, spiracle size, position, and protective structures differ widely among orders, potentially affecting oil entry into the tracheal system. For example, certain insects such as lepidopterans have spiracles with opener and closer muscles, and their morphology is quite elaborate, including spines that protect the spiracle from the entrance of foreign substances (Snodgrass 1935, Schmitz and Wasserthal 1999). Other insects have simpler spiracles, some of which may not present a closer muscle (Snodgrass 1931, Noble-Nesbitt 1989). Mites reportedly may be able to close their respiratory structures (McEnroe 1961).

Regarding the cuticle, it has been proposed that lipid molecules can penetrate the integument and disrupt its structure or impair the specialized cells responsible for its production (Najar-Rodríguez et al. 2008). Structurally, the cuticle consists of two main layers: the epicuticle, its outermost layer, composed primarily of nonpolar molecules such as quinones, lipids, proteins, and possibly waxes and cement substances, which protect against desiccation and uncontrolled water absorption; and the procuticle, responsible for mechanical strength and elasticity, composed mainly of chitin sheets cross-linked by various proteins and other compounds like melanin (Nation 2022). Studies have demonstrated that certain oils can alter the structure and physical properties of the cuticle. For example, in Anthonomus grandis grandis Boheman (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), both mineral and soybean oils softened the body wall, likely through structural interactions, whereas cottonseed oil had no effect (Stadler et al. 2002). Additional studies indicate that mineral oils can increase cuticle permeability, leading to dehydration and mortality in different arthropods, possibly by interacting with the epicuticular wax layer and facilitating oil diffusion into the body (Taverner 2002). In Spodoptera litura (F.) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) and Aphis gossypii Glover (Hemiptera: Aphididae), even small amounts of mineral oil applied to localized body regions caused symptoms of intoxication, dehydration, and necrosis, suggesting cuticular penetration and accumulation in fat tissue and the nervous system (Najar-Rodríguez et al. 2008). Essential oils such as lemongrass [Cymbopogon citratus (DC.) Stapf; Poaceae] oil have also been shown to alter the ultrastructure of the epicuticle, procuticle, and gut, inducing cytotoxic effects in ticks (Ixodida: Ixodidae) (Agwunobi et al. 2020a, 2020b). These effects likely stem from interactions between the hydrophobic regions of fatty acids or hydrocarbons in the oils and the molecules composing the cuticle and cell membranes, leading to structural modifications.

Despite the widespread use of oils in arthropod pest management, their precise modes of action remain incompletely understood. Most previous studies inferred modes of action primarily from mortality, with limited direct visualization of respiratory or cuticular structures. Moreover, few studies have systematically compared different arthropod taxa and developmental stages to link interspecific differences in morphology with susceptibility to oil treatments. The existing literature reports diverse modes of action, including spiracular obstruction and suffocation, cuticular damage, cuticular penetration, and toxicity, which may depend on the oil type and the species examined; this highlights the need for studies that directly examine how oils interact with respiratory and integumentary structures.

Preliminary observations in our laboratory using aphids revealed that soybean oil-treated individuals showed changes in body wall turgidity and appearance, prompting the hypothesis that soybean oil may act through physical mechanisms, such as obstruction of respiratory structures, and potential chemical effects on cuticular components. To determine the effects of soybean oil, we designed a study focusing on two key aspects: (1) oil effects on the respiratory system, including spiracles, tracheae, and associated structures, and (2) oil effects on the ultrastructure and morphology of the cuticular layers. The study was conducted on three arthropod species: adults and last instar nymphs of the aphid Uroleucon sonchi (L.) (Hemiptera: Aphididae), adults and eggs of the twospotted spider mite, Tetranychus urticae Koch (Trombidiformes: Tetranychidae), and larvae and eggs of the diamondback moth, Plutella xylostella (L.) (Lepidoptera: Plutellidae). These species and developmental stages were selected due to their diverse morphology and biology, which offer valuable insights into the mechanisms of action of soybean oil.

Materials and Methods

Biological Material and Soybean Oil

The three arthropod species were collected from the surrounding area of the Colegio de Postgraduados, located in Texcoco, Mexico. Uroleucon sonchi individuals were maintained in a plant nursery on Sonchus oleraceus L. (Asteraceae), from which they were collected as needed. Two-spotted spider mites were obtained from Brugmansia versicolor Lagerh (Solanaceae) plants, while diamondback moths were reared on cauliflower heads (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis; Brassicaceae), sourced from individuals collected in nearby cabbage fields. A commercial soybean oil formulation containing 93% oil and 7% emulsifier (Golden Pest Spray Oil, Stoller de México, León, Guanajuato, Mexico) was used. Prior to dilution, the product was thoroughly shaken to ensure proper homogenization. Dilutions were prepared with distilled water either on the same day or the day before use and shaken immediately before application.

Determination of Toxicity

Ten adults and last-instar nymphs of U. sonchi, as well as ten first-instar and ten fourth-instar larvae of P. xylostella, were topically treated with 2% soybean oil. Insects were placed on a piece of aluminum foil over ice until they became immobile. Once immobilized, a small amount of oil was carefully applied to the ventral region of the abdomen and thorax using a sharpened wooden toothpick, avoiding the pleural area where the spiracles are located. Immediately afterwards, insects were placed on blotting paper for 1–2 min to remove excess oil and then transferred to a Petri dish containing a leaf segment of their respective host plant. Individuals were maintained at room temperature, and mortality was evaluated after 48 h. For the control, an equal number of insects of each species and stage were treated in the same manner using distilled water. This process was repeated four times to obtain four replicates, each consisting of ten randomly selected individuals from a single insect cohort, for the oil treatment and control.

To mimic a field application, another group of ten insects was immersed in 2% soybean oil. Insects were individually immersed in a drop of oil for 1–2 s and immediately transferred to blotting paper. In this case, a small wick of absorbent paper was used to gently remove excess liquid from their bodies. Knockdown was assessed immediately, and mortality was evaluated after 48 h. As a control, insects were treated in the same manner using distilled water. This process was repeated four times, each consisting of ten randomly selected individuals from a single insect cohort, to obtain four replicates for the oil treatment and control.

For T. urticae adults, individuals were handled using a wooden toothpick. The toothpick tip was briefly immersed in 2% soybean oil and then used to gently touch individual mites, which adhered to the coated surface. Each adult was immediately transferred into a small droplet of 2% soybean oil in a Petri dish, where it was briefly immersed with the aid of a toothpick. Excess oil was removed after 1 s using a small wick of absorbent paper, and the adult was then transferred to a 3-cm Petri dish containing a disk of filter paper and a small fragment of host plant. As in the previous experiments, knockdown was assessed immediately, and mortality was evaluated after 48 h. Control individuals were treated in the same manner using distilled water. The experiment consisted of four replicates for the oil treatment and the control, each including ten randomly selected individuals from a single cohort. An experiment determining the effect of topical oil application on T. urticae adults was not conducted because of the small size of the individuals, which might have resulted in inadvertent contact with the respiratory openings and potentially confounded the results.

Knockdown and mortality were considered to occur when all body movements ceased, and the individuals were unresponsive to a gentle touch with an entomological pin.

Determination of Egg Hatching After Immersion

Ten T. urticae eggs aged 24–48 h were placed on a small piece of transparent adhesive tape (3M, Maplewood, MN, USA) and gently pressed down with a fine eyelash mounted on a wooden handle. A small droplet of 2% soybean oil was applied with a capillary tube for 1 s, immediately blotted with absorbent paper, and kept at room temperature for 48 h. Because T. urticae eggs are transparent, embryo development was observed directly under a compound microscope. As a control, eggs were treated in the same manner but immersed in distilled water. Four replicates were conducted for the oil treatment and control.

For P. xylostella, ten eggs, either <24 h or 48 h old, were placed on adhesive tape following the same procedure as for T. urticae. A small droplet of 2% soybean oil was gently applied for 1 s and immediately blotted for the treatment, whereas distilled water was used for the control. Eggs were maintained at room temperature for 96 h. Because P. xylostella eggs are opaque, direct observation of embryo development was not possible; instead, the number of larvae that hatched after 96 h was recorded. Four replicates were conducted for the oil treatment and control.

Obstruction of Gas Exchange Structures

Scanning Electron Microscopy

To assess whether oil obstructs or interferes with gas exchange structures, arthropods were immersed in 2% soybean oil and observed under Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). Fifteen adults and nymphs of U. sonchi, 20 eggs and ten fourth-instar larvae of P. xylostella, and 20 eggs and 15 adults of T. urticae were fully immersed in oil for 1 s and then blotted with absorbent paper. Individuals that survived within 5 min of treatment were euthanized in a lethal chamber with chloroform. Specimens were subsequently fixed in 1% OsO4 for 2 h, rinsed three times (15 min each), and dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series (30%–100%; 15 min per step). Osmium tetroxide was used to fix fatty acids from the treatments to make them insoluble in the dehydration solvents and prevent their removal during processing. They were then dried using a critical-point dryer and sputter-coated with gold. Imaging was performed using a JSM-6390 SEM (Jeol, Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 5–15 keV. The control was immersed in distilled water for 1 s and processed identically for SEM observation.

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy

A stock solution was prepared by saturating pure soybean oil with the fluorescent dye Oil Red O (Helena Laboratories, USA). A 10% dilution was subsequently prepared with distilled water to ensure a sufficient concentration of Oil Red O for detectable fluorescence. Five U. sonchi adults were immersed for 1 s, blotted with a small wick of absorbent paper, and mounted between two 100 µm-thick coverslips in 30% glycerol. To minimize body compression, five paper hole reinforcement rings were stacked as spacers. Specimens were scanned using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a 20x dry objective. Oil Red O was excited with a He-Ne laser at 592 nm, while the cuticle was excited at 488 nm. Emission filters for Texas Red and Alexa Fluor 488 were used for fluorescence detection. Only U. sonchi individuals were used in these experiments, as they died during immersion, were small and translucent enough to allow the internal structure observation, and sufficiently large for treatment and manipulation. The control group consisted of five U. sonchi adults that were immersed in distilled water for 1 s and dissected in the same manner as the adults treated with oil.

Structured Illumination Microscopy

To confirm the Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) results, a separate group of ten U. sonchi adults was treated with 10% soybean oil labelled with Oil Red O as in the previous experiment, dissected ventral-side up in 25% glycerol, and mounted between two coverslips (without spacers) in 30% glycerol after removing all internal organs. Samples were visualized under a Zeiss Axio Zoom.V16 microscope equipped with an ApoTome.2 module (Zeiss, Germany). The same excitation wavelengths were used as in the previous experiment.

Bright-Field Microscopy

Bright-field microscopy was performed using the same sample preparation protocol as for structured illumination microscopy. Briefly, a group of ten U. sonchi adults treated with soybean oil labelled with Oil Red O was dissected ventral-side up in 25% glycerol, and all internal organs were removed. Specimens were then mounted between two coverslips without spacers in 30% glycerol and examined under a Zeiss Axio Zoom.V16 microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Damage to the Integument

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Five adults and fifth-instar nymphs of U. sonchi were placed on aluminum foil over ice until immobilized. A small amount of 5% soybean oil was carefully applied to the ventral surface of the body using a wooden spatula fashioned from a sharpened toothpick, ensuring no oil was applied to the spiracles. Specimens were then placed in a Petri dish with a host plant leaf and maintained at room temperature for 48 h. Afterwards, individuals were euthanized in a chloroform chamber, and the ventral side of the abdomen was immediately dissected in cold 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (in mM: 684 NaCl, 13 KCl, 50.7 Na2HPO4, 5 KH2PO4; pH 7.4). Samples were fixed in 2.5% paraformaldehyde with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 24 h at 4°C. Subsequently, tissues were post-fixed in 1% OsO4 for 2 h, rinsed three times (15 min each) in PBS, dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series (60% –100%; 15 min per step), and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were examined under a JEM-1200 Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) (Jeol, Japan).

Statistical Methods

For the experiments determining toxicity and egg hatching, ten individuals or eggs per treatment, including the control, were considered a replicate (experimental unit). There were four replicates per treatment. Data were analyzed using Fisher’s Exact Test in the statistical software R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team 2023).

Results

Determination of Toxicity

All topically treated U. sonchi survived 48 h after treatment. No alterations typically associated with toxic effects, such as ataxia, immobility, or anorexia, were observed. Similarly, no mortality or adverse effects occurred in the control group. In contrast, U. sonchi immersed in soybean oil exhibited immediate knockdown in 100% of the individuals, followed by death 48 h post-treatment. Tarsal shaking was commonly observed immediately after treatment and lasted for 1–2 s. No mortality was recorded in the corresponding control (Table 1). In T. urticae, all individuals immersed in oil exhibited immediate knockdown followed by death without the leg tremors observed in U. sonchi. All control individuals survived without adverse effects (Table 1).

First- and fourth-instar P. xylostella larvae survived the topical oil application, with no differences between treated and control groups (P = 1) (Table 1). However, immersion resulted in 35% mortality in first-instar larvae 48 h post-treatment (P < 0.001). Immediately after immersion, all larvae remained motionless for 5–10 s, but they recovered and began seeking shelter. Some larvae resumed feeding but later died, as evidenced by chewing marks and feces near the dead individuals. Fourth-instar larvae presented 30% mortality 48 h post-immersion (P < 0.001). Similar to the first instars, they became motionless immediately after treatment but recovered after 5 s and began seeking shelter. After 24 h, some larvae resumed feeding and behaved normally, while others remained immobile. In these individuals, some uncontrolled muscle contractions became evident approximately 1 h after immersion (Supplementary Video S1) and later died.

Egg Hatching After Immersion

Tetranychus urticae eggs exhibited 0% hatching after 48 h (P = 1) (Table 2). Embryos remained active up to 24 h post-treatment; however, by 48 h, no movement could be discerned. Embryos appeared as clumped tissue masses, and the shape of the chorion changed from perfectly spherical to irregular (Fig. 1A and B). All treated eggs died regardless of their age. Control eggs showed no irregularities, and embryos hatched normally (Fig. 1C and D).

Obstruction of Gas Exchange Structures

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The body of treated U. sonchi was covered by a more or less evenly spread layer of oil, which completely obstructed the spiracles (Fig. 3A and C). However, in control individuals, the spiracles were clearly defined along the pleural line (Fig. 3B and D). Results were similar in T. urticae: the entire body was coated with oil, including the setae and peritremes (Fig. 4A and B), which remained unobstructed and well-defined in water-treated individuals (Fig. 4C and D). Fourth instar larvae of P. xylostella, in contrast, showed a fully coated body surface, though the spiracular openings remained uncovered (Fig. 5A), and the inside of the spiracles appeared free of oil (Fig. 5B). Control larvae showed clean spiracles, entirely free of oil with the atrial spines clearly defined (Fig. 5C and D).

Structured Illumination Microscopy

In treated U. sonchi individuals, tracheae and some tracheoles were found to be filled with oil, with the pigment distributed throughout the body. In the green channel (λ = 488 nm), strong fluorescence from the body wall was detected due to the presence of chitin (Fig. 8C), accompanied by faint fluorescence in the tracheae. In the red channel (λ = 592 nm), distinct fluorescence corresponding to Oil Red O was observed, without any autofluorescence from the cuticle (Fig. 8D). The merged channels revealed the colocalization of tracheae with the labeled oil (Fig. 8E and F). Additionally, vesicles were observed outside the tracheae (Fig. 8D and F), originating from oil that had detached from the external body wall. Since the oil is not soluble in glycerin, the droplets acquired a spherical shape. The oil was not uniformly and continuously distributed within the tracheae; instead, certain regions displayed discontinuous oil zones interrupted by air bubbles within both the tracheae and tracheoles (Fig. 8B and F[mdash]arrows).

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy

Confocal analysis further demonstrated that the oil entered the bodies of U. sonchi individuals naturally and was not a consequence of dissection. Autofluorescence of the cuticle was detected in the green channel (488 nm), while the red channel displayed fluorescence associated with Oil Red O (Fig. 9A–C). In the Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) microscopy channel, tracheae appeared as dark, tubular structures emerging from the lateral regions of the body (Fig. 9C). The overlay of red and DIC channels showed clear colocalization of the fluorescent signal with these structures (Fig. 9D), supporting the findings observed in the dissections. As expected, no red fluorescence was detected in the tracheae of the control group. Observation depth was limited to approximately 30 μm from the dorsal surface, due to laser diffraction and refraction within the tissues. These effects reduced laser power and hindered pigment excitation. Nevertheless, dissections revealed that the oil had traveled deeper into the body, reaching the tracheoles.

Damage to the Integument

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Three layers were clearly distinguished in both treated and control U. sonchi integuments using TEM: the outermost layer was thin and strongly electron-opaque; beneath it was a second, somewhat electron-opaque layer, followed by a wider, slightly electron-transparent layer exhibiting a lamellar arrangement (Fig. 10A and B). Based on their characteristics, the first layer was identified as the envelop, the second as the epicuticle, and the third as the exocuticle. No structural differences were observed between treated and control insects because the three layers exhibited similar thickness and arrangement in both groups.

A superficial electron-opaque layer is visible, followed by a clear layer and a multilayered laminar-structured layer. No differences are apparent between the two treatments.

Discussion

Determination of Toxicity

In insects treated topically on the abdomen with 2% soybean oil, no toxic effects, such as mortality or behavioral changes, were observed in any of the species. In contrast, there was mortality only in individuals following immersion for 1 s. These findings suggest that soybean oil does not exert toxic effects nor penetrate through the cuticle. This outcome aligns with previous studies, which reported mortality in cockroaches and lepidopteran larvae only after full submersion in oil, but not following localized application (Taverner 2002, Stadler and Buteler 2009, Buteler and Stadler 2011). However, according to some authors (Taverner 2002, Najar-Rodríguez et al. 2008), petroleum oil, particularly its unsulfonated residues, can directly penetrate the cuticle, accumulate in adipose tissue, and cause neurotoxicity in aphids and lepidopteran larvae when applied topically at concentrations between 0.01% and 2%. This discrepancy may stem from differences in hydrocarbon composition: petroleum oil used in prior studies contained over 98% saturated hydrocarbons (C24), whereas soybean oil is composed primarily of unsaturated C18 fatty acids (Dijkstra 2016). In Callosobruchus maculatus (F.) (Coleoptera: Chrysomelidae), the application of olive oil and its main components (oleic, linoleic, palmitic, and stearic fatty acids) caused mortality in eggs, first instar larvae, and reduced adult longevity in a dose-dependent manner (Aider et al. 2016). This effect was attributed to spiracle blockage, micropylar obstruction, and an interaction between the carboxylic acid functional group and the cuticle. Indeed, SEM analysis showed spiracular obstruction in adults and residues on the integument of the larvae. However, no evidence was presented supporting the hypothesis of chemical interactions between the fatty acids tested and the cuticle (Aider et al. 2016). Similarly, Lienard et al. (1993) found that the application of the major fatty acids found in the seed oil of Cassia occidentalis had larvicidal and ovicidal effects on C. maculatus. The authors attributed the effect to the physical and toxic effects of the oil, although no evidence was presented for the latter. In the present study, the application of oil directly to the cuticle did not show evidence of penetration, neither directly through the epicuticle, nor through the pore canals, since the procuticle remained intact and no symptoms were evident after treatment. On the other hand, compelling evidence of respiratory blockage and tracheal flooding was found, as seen directly, through microscopy techniques, or through the immediate knockdown effect found in aphids and mites.

Mortality of P. xylostella larvae following immersion was not immediate, occurring only after 48 h and at a lower rate than in U. sonchi or T. urticae. Toxic effects can be excluded as no adverse effects were observed in any of those species treated via direct topical application to the cuticle. In U. sonchi, further analysis using Structured Illumination Microscopy (SIM) and confocal microscopy confirmed oil infiltration into the tracheal system. Given the small diameter of these tracheae, capillary action likely facilitated oil entry, explaining the near instantaneous mortality in both adult and immature aphids, except for eggs. This mechanism resembles that described for inorganic oils, where tracheal penetration is identified as the primary mode of action (Taverner et al. 2001, Stadler and Buteler 2009, Buteler and Stadler 2011). In contrast, the lower mortality observed in P. xylostella larvae may be attributed to their ability to rapidly close their spiracles or to the shape of their spiracles. Lepidopteran spiracles contain both opener and closer spiracles, as well as structures such as spines (Snodgrass 1931, 1935, Schmitz and Wasserthal 1999) that may have prevented the penetration of oil through the spiracular openings into the tracheal system. In contrast, other species have simpler spiracles that rely mostly on cuticle elasticity to close the spiracles (Snodgrass 1931, Noble-Nesbitt 1989). Further research is warranted to determine whether and how this mechanism may affect survival to oils in other species of insects.

Obstruction of Gas Exchange Structures

SEM images of adults and immature stages clearly showed that the oil completely coated the body surface, including gas exchange structures. Knockdown occurred within seconds in T. urticae adults and U. sonchi adults and nymphs. These observations suggest that spiracle obstruction alone may not account for the effect. Indeed, CLSM and dissections for U. sonchi confirmed that oil penetrated the spiracles and entirely filled the tracheal space, displacing air. This would be expected to rapidly interrupt oxygen delivery to tissues, leading to knockdown and near-instantaneous mortality. Although mite tracheae were not examined, SEM images revealed that their peritremes were fully covered by oil and exhibited the same symptoms as aphids, which suggests that the oil likely penetrated through their respiratory structures.

Plutella xylostella larvae exhibited low mortality compared with the other species in this study. SEM images showed that, while the spiracles and peritremes of aphids and mites were completely covered by oil, P. xylostella spiracles were relatively unobstructed, and the atrial space appeared free from oil. One notable difference between U. sonchi, T. urticae, and P. xylostella was the shape of their respiratory openings. While in the first two species, they were relatively flat, P. xylostella spiracles were raised and conical. This, together with the ability to close them, as mentioned above, likely conferred the larvae some protection from oil penetration. Nevertheless, this protection was not 100% effective, and a small amount of oil may have penetrated the spiracles and reached the tracheae and tracheoles, which would explain the mortality and the uncontrolled movements observed several hours after immersion.

In the case of eggs, SEM images clearly showed that soybean oil forms a continuous film over the egg surface, supporting the hypothesis that mortality resulted from asphyxiation rather than toxicity. This is consistent with our previous observations showing that soybean oil was not toxic to adults or larvae when applied topically. Similar physical effects have been reported in the eggs of mites (Takeda et al. 2020) and in several insect species, including Ostrinia nubilalis (Hübner) (Lepidoptera: Crambidae) and Grapholita molesta (Busck) (Lepidoptera: Torticidae) (Seni 2023). Although reduced hatching has occasionally been attributed to potential toxicity, as in Choristoneura rosaceana (Harris) (Lepidoptera: Tortricidae) (Wins-Purdy et al. 2009), the present results indicate a mainly physical mode of action. In P. xylostella, the egg surface is covered with a porous chorion from which aeropyles protrude. These structures facilitate gas exchange and are particularly important during early and mid-embryogenesis (Nation 2022). SEM images from our study clearly showed soybean oil accumulating over the chorion and directly obstructing the aeropyles, likely limiting gaseous exchange, which is essential for normal embryonic metabolism and development (Hinton 1981).

The timing of application relative to embryonic development strongly influenced mortality only in P. xylostella. Eggs aged less than 24 h before oil treatment failed to hatch, suggesting that early-stage embryos are especially vulnerable to prolonged hypoxia. In contrast, most eggs laid at least 48 h prior to treatment successfully hatched, probably because they had already completed much of their development and were close to eclosion at the time of oil application. The results were markedly different in T. urticae, where all treated eggs died within 24 h, regardless of age or developmental stage. Mite eggs can perform gas exchange through specialized stigmata and openings in the shell (Dittrich and Streibert 1969, Dittrich 1971). Oil application likely blocked these structures and prevented oxygen uptake, which caused mortality.

The observed differences in egg mortality between T. urticaeand P. xylostella may be explained by structural and physiological features of the egg. In many insect and mite species, a thin film of air lies beneath the chorion, functioning as a temporary reservoir that permits the embryo to withstand brief interruptions in gas exchange, such as during short-term flooding (Hinton 1981, Nation 2022). In P. xylostella, this air film may have provided fully developed larvae with sufficient oxygen to complete eclosion; however, it was probably not enough to sustain developing embryos. In T. urticae, the air volume beneath the chorion may be even smaller and insufficient to allow successful emergence even in late-stage larvae. Further research is needed to determine which species and developmental stages are most affected, and under what environmental conditions. In addition, although the morphological evidence strongly supports a physical mode of action, direct physiological measurements, such as determination of respiratory rate, may help further confirm our results.

Damage to the Integument

The structure of the three integument layers—the outer epicuticle, inner epicuticle, and exocuticle—was remarkably similar between treated and control groups for U. sonchi. These findings indicate that soybean oil did not penetrate the cuticle or induce structural damage. In previous studies, lemongrass essential oil apparently caused cuticular damage in ticks after 5 min of immersion followed by 24 h of incubation (Agwunobi et al. 2020a). This damage was attributed to the toxic effects of terpenes present in the oil. By contrast, knockdown and mortality in the present study occurred within seconds in aphids and mites, with no evidence of toxicity after topical application, and no visible damage to the integument.

Implications for Integrated Pest Management Programs

Our results suggest that soybean oil may be effective against small arthropods such as aphids and mites, and may have negative effects on selected life stages of other pests such as P. xylostella. In addition, the lack of topical toxicity observed in our study suggests that soybean oil may be compatible with natural enemies. One of the major limitations of synthetic and some botanical pesticides is their detrimental impact on beneficial organisms through topical toxicity, contamination of food sources, or sublethal physiological effects (Croft and Brown 1975, Ndakidemi et al. 2016). Some compounds may even stimulate pest reproduction via hormetic responses (Cutler 2013). In contrast, soybean oil does not appear to induce such adverse effects, making it advantageous over many insecticides. Furthermore, its physical mode of action (blocking gas exchange structures and infiltrating the tracheal system) reduces the likelihood of resistance development.

The absence of topical toxicity, however, may have limitations: as shown in our results, soybean oil is only effective against individuals that are covered by the oil, particularly their respiratory structures. Therefore, its efficacy is likely to be minimal against pests such as leaf miners, eriophyid mites, or other gall-forming or burrowing arthropods. Some species or life stages may also evade control through physical adaptations, such as spiracle morphology or behavior. Moreover, the potential repellent effects of the oil against pests and natural enemies deserve attention. It is known that certain oils may repel some pests (Stansly et al. 2002, Ouyang et al. 2013), but there is little information about their effects on the preference and behavior of beneficial organisms, particularly in terms of predator–prey dynamics and host-seeking behavior. Additional research is therefore warranted to evaluate soybean oil performance across a broad diversity of pest taxa and ecological contexts.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that soybean oil causes mortality primarily through obstruction of gas exchange structures and penetration into the respiratory system, which leads to death by asphyxia rather than cuticular damage, integument penetration, or systemic toxicity. Soybean oil efficacy varied across species and developmental stages: U. sonchi nymphs and adults, as well as T. urticae eggs and adults, were highly susceptible, whereas P. xylostella larvae and older eggs were comparatively less affected. The physical mode of action of soybean oil makes it a safer alternative to broad-spectrum conventional insecticides; however, its effectiveness is strongly influenced by the target species and life stage.
