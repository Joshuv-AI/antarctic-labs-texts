Physical contact under surveillance: cultural norms, visibility and teacher self-regulation ın Türkiye
Authors: Damla Güler, Semiyha Tuncel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical contact in physical education represents a complex pedagogical practice shaped not only by instructional needs but also by broader social, cultural, and professional expectations. In contexts where bodily interaction may be subject to social interpretation and potential misunderstanding, teachers continuously negotiate appropriate forms of pedagogical touch. Although previous research has primarily examined physical contact through risk and no-touch discourses, less attention has been paid to how teachers negotiate pedagogical touch within broader conditions of visibility and normative expectation. This study aims to explore how physical education teachers in Türkiye negotiate pedagogical touch within overlapping normative climates shaped by cultural expectations, social interpretation, and perceived visibility.MethodsThis qualitative study was designed using a phenomenological approach. The study was conducted with eight physical education teachers working in different regions of Türkiye. Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsThe analysis generated three themes: (1) surveillance and normative regulation, (2) subjectification, and (3) rthe maintaining and reorganizing of pedagogical contact. While some teachers became increasingly cautious and reorganized instruction through indirect strategies such as objects, peer support, and technological mediation, others continued to view physical contact as pedagogically necessary in situations involving learning, safety, and care. The findings further suggest that teachers’ self-regulation was shaped not only through formal institutional expectations but also through professional ethics, experiential learning, and broader normative climates.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that pedagogical touch in physical education has not disappeared but has become increasingly negotiated within broader relations of visibility, ethics, and social interpretation. The study contributes to existing literature by distinguishing between institutional monitoring, social interpretation, media discourse, digital visibility, and broader normative expectations rather than treating them as identical forms of surveillance. The findings also highlight the importance of context-sensitive professional guidance that supports pedagogical judgment and child protection without reinforcing defensive no-touch practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04866-y.

Introduction

Physical contact in educational settings is a complex issue with pedagogical, ethical, cultural, and social dimensions. Especially in physical education classes, physical contact is frequently used for pedagogical reasons [1]. However, perceptions of physical contact are shaped by fears related to physical contact, cultural differences, and social norms [2, 3]. Due to this complex structure, teachers in physical education classes often find themselves torn between whether or not to engage in physical contact [4].

While teachers strive to create an effective learning environment, they must also be mindful of the risk of physical contact being misinterpreted [5]. Physical contact between teachers and students is associated not only with the risk of sexual abuse but also with emotional support, religious factors, and violence in various cultural and social contexts [6]. However, in Western societies, physical contact in physical education classes is increasingly being addressed within the context of moral panic and risk discourse [7]. Similar trends are also evident in countries like Turkey, which are culturally distinct but increasingly engaged with global discourses. At this point, Turkey’s cultural fabric holds particular significance for understanding how decisions regarding physical contact in the context of physical education are shaped.

Turkey’s cultural, religious, and social norms—particularly regarding gender roles and privacy—limit and shape students’ experiences in physical education [8]. Despite coeducation in Turkey, full equality of opportunity is not fully achieved in physical education classes. This stems from the influence of gender stereotypes on both teachers and students [9]. However, the existing literature has primarily addressed physical contact within the framework of risk perception, moral panic, and no-touch discourse. The only study conducted in the Turkish physical education context revealed how teachers attempt to balance pedagogical necessities with concerns about being misunderstood [4]. Nevertheless, less attention has been given to how physical contact is negotiated within broader normative climates shaped by cultural expectations, social interpretation, and concerns about appropriate bodily interaction in physical education settings. In the Turkish context in particular, limited research has examined how teachers interpret and regulate pedagogical touch under conditions of visibility and potential misunderstanding.

Therefore, the present study aims to explore how physical education teachers in Türkiye negotiate pedagogical touch within overlapping normative climates shaped by cultural expectations, social interpretation, and perceived visibility. Drawing on Foucauldian perspectives on visibility and self-regulation, while also engaging with social norms literature, the study conceptualizes pedagogical touch not simply as a matter of surveillance or individual pedagogical choice, but as a socially negotiated practice shaped by perceived expectations surrounding appropriate bodily interaction. In this sense, the study contributes to existing no-touch and risk literature by offering a more socially and culturally situated understanding of how pedagogical touch is negotiated within the Turkish physical education context.

Theoretical framework

The theoretical foundation of this study draws primarily on Michel Foucault’s conceptualizations of panopticism, subjectification, and technologies of the self. In physical education, physical contact is not only a pedagogical act but also a socially interpreted practice shaped by care, risk and the possibility of misunderstanding. Previous research has shown that bodily touch in physical education may function as a caring and pedagogical practice while also becoming problematic within broader no-touch discourses, moral panic, and concerns regarding professional vulnerability [1, 5–7, 10]. Accordingly, the present study approaches physical contact not as a neutral instructional technique, but as a socially negotiated practice shaped through relations of visibility, interpretation, and normative expectations.

According to Foucault, power is “discipline.” Discipline is not the direct imposition of force from the top down, but rather power used in a way that enables the individual to regulate themselves. By analyzing the relationship between the body, the individual, and “power-knowledge,” Foucault demonstrated that knowledge becomes an instrument of power through the classification and regulation of individuals. In this context, power and knowledge operate together to form a mechanism that directs and controls individuals’ behavior [11]. Educational institutions are among the primary settings where disciplinary order is concentrated. Within these institutions, the behaviors of teachers and students are shaped through the monitoring and evaluation of performance and the establishment of acceptable boundaries [12]. In this sense, physical contact in physical education may be understood as a practice negotiated within broader relations of visibility and social interpretation.

Foucault’s concept of the Panopticon is useful for understanding how the possibility of observation may encourage anticipatory self-monitoring. Rather than relying on direct coercion, panoptic forms of power operate through the awareness that one may potentially be observed, evaluated, or judged [11]. In educational settings, such visibility may shape how teachers regulate their conduct and negotiate acceptable professional boundaries. In the present study, panopticism is not used to suggest constant literal surveillance. Rather, it serves as a conceptual lens for understanding how the possibility of observation, misunderstanding, and social judgment may shape teachers’ decisions regarding physical contact.

At the same time, the study distinguishes between different forms of regulation rather than treating all of them as identical forms of surveillance. Institutional surveillance refers to formal or semi-formal mechanisms of monitoring within schools, including administrative expectations and professional accountability. Social surveillance refers to informal monitoring and judgment by parents, colleagues, students, and wider community members. Media discourse refers to public narratives that may frame teacher–student contact as suspicious or risky, while digital visibility refers to the possibility that bodily interactions may be photographed, recorded, or circulated through digital platforms. Cultural and religious expectations, by contrast, are approached not simply as surveillance mechanisms, but as normative frameworks through which appropriate bodily interaction is socially defined.

For this reason, the regulation of physical contact cannot be explained solely through surveillance. The findings also point to the importance of perceived normative climates: what teachers believe others consider acceptable or inappropriate within educational settings. Social norms literature is useful here because it suggests that individuals regulate their behavior not only because they are observed, but also because they anticipate how others may interpret and evaluate their actions. Borinca, Andrighetto, Valsecchi, and Berent [13] show that tolerant ingroup norms reduce suspicion toward outgroup prosocial behavior and increase willingness to accept help, suggesting that the meaning of a helpful act depends strongly on the normative context in which it is embedded. This insight is relevant to physical education because supportive physical contact may similarly be interpreted with trust or suspicion depending on the perceived norms surrounding bodily contact, morality, professionalism, gender, and child protection.

More broadly, Borinca, Sainz, and Gkinopoulos [14] emphasize that social norms shape intergroup contact, prejudice reduction, inclusive environments, and social relations across different educational and sociopolitical contexts. Borinca, Griffin, McMahon, Maher, and Muldoon [15] further demonstrate that descriptive ingroup norms influence trust, solidarity, and behavioral intentions during public health crises, particularly among individuals initially skeptical of expert guidance.In the present study, this literature helps move the analysis beyond a primarily Foucauldian account of surveillance by explaining how descriptive and injunctive norms may shape teachers’ perceptions of professional risk and acceptable pedagogical contact. Thus, teachers’ self-regulation is understood not only as a product of panoptic visibility, but also as a response to perceived group-based norms about care, morality, professionalism, gender, and child protection.

Based on Foucault’s approach, subjectivation refers to the process by which an individual becomes a subject through the relationship they establish with themselves within practices and relationships [16]. This process of transformation operates through self-regulation, a key dimension of subjectivation. Self-regulation refers to an individual’s ability to regulate their own behavior in accordance with internalized norms, without relying on an external regulatory mechanism [17]. In this process, which Foucault defines as the “conduct of conduct,” the individual becomes both the object and the agent of power [18]. In this study, subjectification is not understood as either complete submission or complete autonomy. Rather, it is approached as an ambivalent process through which teachers negotiate professional responsibilities, ethical concerns, pedagogical care, and fears of misunderstanding. Teachers’ self-regulation may therefore reflect not only disciplinary pressure, but also practical judgments related to student well-being, professional ethics, and child protection.

The concept of “Technologies of the Self,” developed by Foucault in his later works, further deepens this process. This concept refers to individuals working on themselves to become a specific ethical subject [19]. In the context of physical education, teachers may reorganize bodily interaction, develop alternative pedagogical strategies, or establish personal ethical boundaries in order to navigate uncertainty surrounding physical contact. Rather than completely avoiding pedagogical touch, teachers may adapt and negotiate their practices according to context, perceived risk, and student needs. In this sense, physical contact is approached in the present study not as a fixed pedagogical behavior, but as a continuously negotiated practice situated within relations of visibility, norms, ethics, and professional judgment.

Methodology

Research design

This study was conducted using a phenomenological qualitative research design with the aim of gaining an in-depth understanding of physical education teachers’ experiences regarding decisions involving physical contact. Creswell and Poth [20] note that the phenomenological design is an appropriate approach when researchers wish to focus on a specific phenomenon. As Van Manen [21] notes, the fundamental aim of phenomenological inquiry is to focus on an ordinary experience and understand how it is lived. In this study, moments of physical contact—which physical education teachers frequently encounter in their daily practice—are examined as experiences negotiated within broader relations of visibility, social interpretation, and normative expectations.

Participants

In this study, criterion sampling was chosen as the purposive sampling strategy. Purposive sampling focuses on situations that provide in-depth information on topics critical to the research objective [22]. Criteria sampling, one of the purposeful sampling methods, is a method based on the principle that participants to be included in the study must meet predefined criteria [23]. The criteria established in this study are that participants must have at least five years of professional experience and be employed at schools under the Ministry of National Education.

Participants were recruited through professional networks and voluntary participation. Teachers who met the inclusion criteria were contacted directly by the researchers and informed about the purpose and scope of the study. Efforts were made to include participants from different regions, school types, genders, and professional backgrounds in order to obtain a broader range of experiences regarding physical contact.

The study sample consists of eight physical education teachers (3 female, 5 male) working in various provinces of Turkey. The participants’ ages range from 31 to 51, and their years of professional experience range from 7 to 28. Throughout their professional careers, the teachers have worked in various provinces of Turkey (primarily Balıkesir, Istanbul, Düzce, Tokat, Izmir, Kars, and Muş). They have also gained work experience in various types of schools at the middle school (including middle schools and imam hatip middle schools) and high school levels (vocational high schools, sports high schools, and other types of high schools). An examination of their graduate education reveals that two participants are currently pursuing doctoral degrees, one participant left their master’s program at the thesis stage, and the others have not pursued graduate education. Participant characteristics

Although the sample size was relatively small, the aim of the study was not statistical representation but the development of an in-depth understanding of how physical education teachers experience and interpret physical contact within specific sociocultural and professional contexts. Consistent with phenomenological inquiry, emphasis was placed on experiential depth, contextual richness, and variation rather than numerical representativeness. Therefore, variation in gender, region, school type, and professional experience was intentionally considered during participant selection in order to capture differing experiences related to physical contact.

The adequacy of the sample was further evaluated in relation to the concept of information power, which suggests that fewer participants may be sufficient when the study aim is focused, the sample is specific to the research question, established theory informs the analysis, and rich data are generated through strong interview dialogue [24]. In the present study, participants possessed direct professional experience relevant to the phenomenon under investigation, the study was theoretically informed by Foucauldian perspectives, and the semi-structured interviews produced detailed experiential accounts. In addition, the analysis focused on in-depth interpretive exploration rather than broad comparative generalization. For these reasons, the sample size was considered sufficient to generate analytically rich insights relevant to the aims of the study.

Data collection

The data for this study were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews. This approach allowed for the acquisition of more in-depth information [25]. The semi-structured interview guide was specifically developed for this study based on previous literature regarding physical contact [1, 5–7] and Foucault’s [11, 18, 19] concepts of surveillance, subjectification, and power relations. Although the interview guide was theoretically informed, the interview questions were intentionally phrased in an open-ended and experience-oriented manner in order to avoid directing participants toward predefined Foucauldian interpretations. Participants were not directly asked about concepts such as surveillance, discipline, or subjectification; instead, they were encouraged to describe their experiences, decision-making processes, and interpretations regarding physical contact in their own terms. The full English version of the interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Sample interview questions included: “What experiences have influenced your decisions regarding physical contact?”, “Can you describe the decision-making process you follow when faced with a situation that requires physical contact?” and “Can you describe how your thoughts and practices regarding physical contact have evolved over time?” The interviews were conducted after obtaining the participants’ voluntary consent, and each interview lasted an average of 40 min. All interviews were recorded using a voice recorder with the participants’ permission and subsequently transcribed verbatim. The interviews were conducted online (via Teams) to ensure ease of access for the participants and to facilitate the research process. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the relevant university prior to the start of the study (Date: 29/09/2025, Decision No.: 08/47). All participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedure, and confidentiality principles and their written and verbal informed consent was obtained. The confidentiality of participants’ personal information was protected, and pseudonyms were used during the reporting process.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using the reflexive thematic analysis approach developed by Braun and Clarke [26]. This approach adopts an analytical process in which meaning is produced through the researcher’s theoretical positioning and interpretive process rather than being derived from a fixed structure discovered within the data. In this direction, the analysis was conducted as a flexible and interpretive process that took into account both the researcher’s subjective interpretation and the contextual nature of the data. The analysis process was carried out by following the six-stage cyclical framework proposed by Braun and Clarke [27]. In the first stage, the interview transcripts were transcribed verbatim and the data set was read repeatedly to gain familiarity with the data.

In the first stage, interview transcripts were transcribed verbatim and repeatedly read to achieve familiarity with the dataset. The first researcher conducted the primary coding process through close engagement with the transcripts, while codes and developing themes were continuously discussed between the researchers to critically examine interpretations and strengthen conceptual coherence. Reflexive memoing was used throughout the analytical process to document analytic decisions, emerging interpretations, and reflections regarding the researchers’ theoretical positioning.

In the second stage, meaningful units related to participants’ experiences of physical contact were coded systematically. The analysis combined inductive and deductive approaches. The inductive phase focused on generating codes grounded in participants’ experiences and meanings, whereas the deductive phase interpreted these meanings in relation to broader concepts informing the study, including visibility, social interpretation, and normative expectations surrounding pedagogical touch. Foucauldian concepts informed the analytical sensitivity of the study; however, themes were not predetermined prior to coding, and theory was used as an interpretive lens rather than a rigid analytical framework.

In the third and fourth stages, related codes were grouped into candidate themes and reviewed in relation to the dataset in order to strengthen conceptual coherence and distinction between themes. Some themes were merged, reorganized, or removed during this process based on their explanatory relevance and consistency across participants’ accounts. In the fifth stage, themes were refined and clearly defined. In the final stage, the themes were presented and interpreted in relation to the broader conceptual framework of the study. Throughout the analysis, attention was paid to interpretive depth, reflexivity, and transparency in analytic decision-making in line with the epistemological assumptions of reflexive thematic analysis [26]. MAXQDA software was used to support data organization and coding.

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The researchers are academics working in the field of physical education and sport pedagogy within the Turkish higher education system and have prior research experience related to physical contact, child protection, teacher identity, and pedagogical practices in physical education. Their professional backgrounds provided familiarity with the cultural, institutional, and pedagogical contexts in which the participants worked and facilitated communication during the interview process. At the same time, the researchers recognized that their disciplinary backgrounds and prior engagement with issues related to physical contact, child protection, and pedagogy could shape both data collection and interpretation.

Given the sensitive nature of the topic, including issues related to gender, bodily contact, professional vulnerability, religious-cultural expectations, and fears of misinterpretation, attention was paid to creating an open and non-judgmental interview environment. The researchers were aware that their own positions as academics in physical education, as well as their assumptions regarding pedagogical touch, professionalism, child protection, and appropriate teacher-student boundaries within the Turkish sociocultural context, could influence both how participants narrated their experiences and how meanings were interpreted during analysis. Particular attention was therefore paid to how issues related to gender, cultural expectations, regional differences, and professional vulnerability might shape both participants’ accounts and the researchers’ interpretations of those accounts.

During the interviews, participants were encouraged to describe their experiences in their own terms, and care was taken not to impose Foucauldian concepts such as surveillance, discipline, or subjectification directly within the questioning process. Throughout the analytical process, reflexive memoing and ongoing discussions between the researchers were used to critically examine assumptions, interpretations, and theoretical positioning. Rather than treating the findings as objective reflections of reality, the researchers approached the analysis as an interpretive and contextually situated process shaped through continuous engagement with the data.

Results and discussion

The analysis generated three main themes: surveillance and normative regulation, subjectification, and the maintaining and reorganizing pedagogical contact.

Surveillance and normative regulation

This theme demonstrates how participants described physical contact as negotiated within broader normative expectations related to visibility, social interpretation, and local sociocultural contexts. Participants emphasized that the boundaries of appropriate bodily interaction were not fixed but shaped differently across regional settings, school environments, parental expectations, media discourse, and digital visibility. Rather than responding only to formal institutional expectations, participants described regulating pedagogical touch in relation to anticipated social judgment and the possibility of misunderstanding.

Several participants emphasized that perceptions of physical contact varied depending on the sociocultural characteristics of the region in which they worked. Participants working in eastern provinces such as Kars and Muş more frequently referred to concerns related to bodily boundaries, gender interaction, and community visibility compared to participants working in western urban contexts such as İzmir and İstanbul. One participant described this situation as follows:

In extremely conservative environments, if you’re teaching physical education, you absolutely cannot wear leggings, you absolutely cannot touch anyone, and there might not even be such a thing as physical education. Under the current conditions in Türkiye, physical contact is difficult (Işıl).

In extremely conservative environments, if you’re teaching physical education, you absolutely cannot wear leggings, you absolutely cannot touch anyone, and there might not even be such a thing as physical education. Under the current conditions in Türkiye, physical contact is difficult (Işıl).

This statement reflects the participant’s personal perception of conservative community norms and local sociocultural pressures rather than a description of formal educational policy or legal regulations in Türkiye. The participant used the expression to emphasize how strongly certain local moral expectations were perceived to shape bodily interaction and teacher behavior within specific social environments. Similarly, Taner stated: “We could say that in the eastern region, these norms are interpreted more narrowly, whereas in the western region, they are viewed as more acceptable.” Samet also emphasized how local context shaped professional behavior: “We work in Eastern Anatolia. The parent profile was different at the first school I worked at, and it’s different at my current school. Still, I haven’t had any issues so far, but the environment you’re in does influence you culturally.”

Rather than reflecting a homogeneous cultural structure, these accounts suggest that participants perceived acceptable forms of bodily interaction to vary across local sociocultural environments. Physical contact was therefore experienced not simply as an individual pedagogical decision, but as a practice negotiated within differing regional, cultural, and community expectations.

Religious and school-based normative expectations were also described as influential in shaping interactional boundaries. Participants working in Imam Hatip school contexts more frequently referred to heightened sensitivity regarding gender interaction and bodily proximity. Okan described this experience as follows:

Our society is rooted in religious principles. When I worked at an Imam Hatip school, I couldn’t even arrange extra classes, let alone have physical contact. The parents didn’t want it, and neither did the school administration. Certain values have become taboo. These taboos also define the boundaries of physical contact.

Similarly, Gamze stated: “After all, we live within a moral framework. This framework exists because it is accepted by society, due to the fact that we are a Muslim society, or because of teachings passed down from the past.”

Participants did not describe these expectations as uniform across all educational or regional contexts. These accounts suggest that some participants perceived cultural and religious expectations as influential in shaping the boundaries of acceptable bodily interaction within their professional settings. However, participants described these expectations differently depending on school context, parental profile, and local community dynamics.

One of the most frequently emphasized forms of pressure involved concerns regarding social interpretation and community judgment rather than direct institutional sanctions. Male participants in particular more frequently described anxiety regarding the possibility of their actions being misunderstood. Selim described this situation as follows:

When I’m helping the child do a somersault, the child touches my knees, does the somersault, and I support their back with my hand. It’s a perfectly normal movement for me… But when a parent from the village comes and sees this, they comment to the teacher, “Look, she’s touching the child’s back.” What’s routine for me can be seen as inappropriate by them. That’s when the fear of “what will people say” comes into play.

This account illustrates how the pedagogical meaning of touch became shaped not only through teachers’ intentions but also through anticipated social interpretation. Participants described modifying their behavior according to the possibility of being observed, judged, or misinterpreted by parents and community members, even in the absence of formal monitoring mechanisms.

Media discourse was also described as contributing to increased professional caution. Participants frequently referred to media representations of abuse cases as shaping broader public suspicion surrounding teacher-student contact. Işıl stated, “Physical contact is not considered appropriate because we hear about so many cases of abuse. Even the possibility of being misunderstood over time is enough to discourage physical contact.”

Similarly, Selim explained:

You’re watching TV in the evening: a teacher is sexually harassing a student. Millions of people are watching. In the morning, you go to school, and your student wants to run up and hug you, but you can’t. Because everyone watched that news story last night. Inevitably, there’s a sense of distance.

Unlike direct institutional regulation, participants described media discourse as producing a broader climate of suspicion in which pedagogical touch increasingly became associated with potential risk and misinterpretation.

Participants also distinguished digital visibility from face-to-face social scrutiny. Digital platforms were described not simply as spaces of observation but as environments in which images and interactions could rapidly circulate beyond their original context.

Gamze described this pressure, saying, “Even the slightest snapshot can be used as blackmail material. As social media grows, the teacher’s room to maneuver shrinks.”

Similarly, Selim stated: “Social media… the interaction you have there in that moment might seem very innocent. But a photo is taken, it spreads, and takes on a life of its own. That’s why I have to be careful to maintain physical distance.”

Işıl explained this growing sense of visibility as follows: “With the media around, teachers have to be more careful, as if they were constantly being watched by a camera. Whereas we used to pay 10% attention, now we pay 70–80% attention. Because when one teacher makes a mistake, it reflects poorly on all teachers.”

Yavuz also emphasized the speed at which information circulated online: “It gets shared immediately in a parent group: ‘A teacher at that school did this.’ When a parent sees this, they start to doubt their own child. And once you’re aware of that possibility, you inevitably become more cautious.”

Overall, these findings suggest that participants experienced visibility through multiple and overlapping forms of social scrutiny, including parental interpretation, media discourse, digital circulation, and community expectations. Rather than describing a single form of surveillance, participants portrayed pedagogical touch as negotiated within broader environments of visibility, interpretation, and anticipated judgment.

Subjectification

This theme demonstrates that teachers construct their practices through ethical principles, professional experience, and collective memory. Participants’ statements reveal that, in a domain where the boundaries of their practice are not defined by clear and formal rules, teachers regulate themselves through self-regulation and internalized.

ethical frameworks. Participants clearly stated that, in the absence of a written guide, they set the boundaries themselves. One participant described this situation as follows:

It seems to me that it comes down to moral standards. Because the Ministry of National Education never tells me, “You can’t touch the child here, but you can touch their hand—for three minutes.” There’s no such guideline. Here, it’s entirely our own values that come into play. In other words, we establish the ethical boundaries within ourselves (Yavuz).

It seems to me that it comes down to moral standards. Because the Ministry of National Education never tells me, “You can’t touch the child here, but you can touch their hand—for three minutes.” There’s no such guideline. Here, it’s entirely our own values that come into play. In other words, we establish the ethical boundaries within ourselves (Yavuz).

Similarly, Samet made the following statement: “Unfortunately, there is no written guide on this matter. These matters are entirely determined by our ethical principles. I will continue to act in the way I have internalized them. Whatever interaction I have with a student that is morally appropriate will yield the corresponding outcome. In other words, it all comes down to the teacher.”

These statements indicate that decisions regarding physical contact are not explicitly determined by an external normative framework but are instead grounded in the teacher’s own internal ethical framework. The absence of written rules does not imply that physical contact is unrestricted. On the contrary, it creates a space for self-regulation within which the teacher governs their own behavior. These findings suggest that, in the absence of explicit institutional guidelines, participants relied on personal ethics and professional judgment when determining appropriate forms of physical contact.

Professional experience also appeared to shape how participants interpreted and managed bodily interaction over time. Işıl described the difference between her early years in the profession and later periods in the following words: “In the early years, there was no such thing as ‘connecting’ with new students. I was very cautious. But as I gained experience, I now know what I can do and when. I’m still cautious during initial interactions, but my confidence has grown.”

Yeşim, however, noted that experience has not diminished her connection but has made it more conscious: “The Yeşim at the start of my career is not the same as who I am now. Many people think that experience diminishes that connection, but for me, it has grown. Because now I know how to touch someone and with what words. I am aware of how it reflects back to the child.”

In contrast, Selim expressed that he had gradually adopted a more reserved stance as follows:

I’m 34 years old now. When I was 22, I didn’t think about any of this. Because I had just graduated from college. I was eager to teach. I was in a different mindset… As time went on, as I saw and experienced things, and as I witnessed the negative examples around me, I told myself, “You shouldn’t do this. You shouldn’t get this close…” There’s a world of difference between when I first became a teacher and now. That’s something that comes with life experience.

These differing approaches demonstrate that subjectification is not a one-sided process. While the experience fosters confidence and pedagogical awareness regarding physical contact in some teachers, it leads others to adopt a more cautious and reserved approach.

It is evident that collective memory also plays a decisive role in decision-making. One participant described how an incident experienced by a colleague transformed their own practice in the following words:

We haven’t made an official decision on this matter as a group, but we generally discuss it in light of negative examples from the past. For instance, a physical education teacher committed suicide following an incident involving a misunderstanding with a student. After hearing about that case, I no longer bring students into my office alone. My door is always open (Yavuz).

When I first started working, a senior teacher told me, “You’re in high school—be careful. Don’t let the students get too close to you. Especially the male students who are close to your age—they might look at you differently. Keep a distance of about an arm’s length between you.” I’ve never forgotten that advice; it’s always been my boundary.

I’ve never received any complaints. But I’m careful to take precautions before things get out of hand. Because we have colleagues who’ve been through this. Seeing what happened to them makes us take precautions ourselves.”

Overall, these findings suggest that participants’ self-regulation emerged through a complex interaction between professional ethics, experiential learning, collective memory, and broader normative expectations. Participants did not describe themselves merely as passive recipients of external control; rather, they portrayed themselves as actively negotiating appropriate forms of pedagogical contact within contexts shaped by uncertainty, visibility, and professional vulnerability. In this sense, Foucauldian concepts of self-regulation and subjectification help illuminate how participants internalized broader professional and social expectations while simultaneously exercising professional judgment within their everyday pedagogical practices.

Maintaining and reorganizing pedagogical contact

This theme demonstrates how participants described attempting to maintain pedagogical effectiveness while simultaneously negotiating concerns related to visibility, social interpretation, and professional risk. Rather than completely abandoning physical contact, participants described reorganizing pedagogical interaction through alternative strategies that reduced direct bodily contact while allowing them to continue fulfilling instructional responsibilities. These strategies included the use of objects, peer support, gender-based pairing, and technological tools. The findings suggest that teachers did not respond uniformly to normative pressures; while some participants prioritized distance and indirect instruction, others continued to view physical contact as pedagogically necessary under specific conditions.

Several participants described developing indirect instructional strategies in order to minimize the possibility of misunderstanding while still supporting student learning. Some female participants more frequently emphasized mediated forms of interaction designed to reduce ambiguity surrounding bodily contact. Yeşim described the use of objects during instruction as follows:We’re working on posture. I have a stick in my hand, and I’m pressing it against their back like this… Normally, I could correct their posture by touching their back, but that could be misinterpreted. That’s why I use the stick… It puts my mind at ease and doesn’t leave the student with any doubts.

In this account, the use of an object functioned not only as a pedagogical tool but also as a strategy for managing professional vulnerability and reducing the possibility of social misinterpretation.

Similarly, Yavuz explained how he adapted his teaching practice during table tennis instruction:When I’m teaching table tennis, if the child’s arm isn’t at the angle I want, I place a plastic bottle between their arms. That way, the child’s arm is forced to assume that angle. There’s no need to touch the child… This way, I achieve my pedagogical goal while also avoiding unnecessary physical contact.

These accounts suggest that participants did not necessarily reject pedagogical touch entirely; rather, they described restructuring bodily interaction through indirect and mediated forms of instruction.

The use of technological tools was described in a similar way. Gamze stated: “If I need to demonstrate a skill and my student doesn’t want me to make physical contact, I use videos… That way, I can ensure the child learns without any physical contact.”

In this context, participants described adapting their instructional methods according to both student comfort and broader social sensitivities surrounding bodily interaction. Physical contact was therefore repositioned as one possible pedagogical tool among several alternatives rather than as an unavoidable component of teaching practice.

Peer support and gender-based instructional arrangements also emerged as strategies for negotiating bodily boundaries. Participants working in more conservative school environments referred more frequently to the use of indirect assistance and same-gender interaction. Okan explained this approach as follows: “Especially when teaching something that requires physical contact, I ask students who are good at it in class to help out. They support their classmates, and I can resolve the issue without direct physical contact.”

These findings suggest that participants did not necessarily withdraw from pedagogical responsibility under conditions of caution and visibility. Instead, many teachers described reorganizing the pedagogical environment in ways that reduced direct bodily interaction while still attempting to maintain instructional effectiveness.

At the same time, not all participants viewed the avoidance of physical contact as pedagogically appropriate. Several participants emphasized that instructional necessity, student safety, and health concerns could override fears related to social interpretation. Male participants in particular more frequently described physical contact as unavoidable in situations involving movement learning or injury prevention. Samet stated:“When I do something that is inherent to my lesson, I can’t be held back by anyone’s approval… If physical contact is necessary, I’ll do it. I don’t even consider the reactions that might come from outside.”

Similarly, Selim emphasized pedagogical responsibility in relation to skill learning:“If it’s necessary for the child to learn, I’ll make physical contact. If I don’t, they’ll never learn that movement… So, if it’s necessary to make contact in the right way and at the right moment, I’ll do so.”

These accounts demonstrate that participants did not uniformly position physical contact as inherently risky or inappropriate. Instead, teachers described continuously weighing pedagogical necessity against concerns regarding visibility and interpretation.

Participants also emphasized that concerns regarding social judgment became secondary during moments involving student health and safety. Samet stated:“If physical contact is necessary, I will definitely make contact because there could be an injury.”

Similarly, Işıl described responding to a medical emergency during class: “A child fainted during class. I immediately picked them up and took them to the infirmary… I took off their sock and checked their temperature. In that situation, you don’t consider the guidelines at all. The child’s health becomes the priority.”

These accounts suggest that participants viewed care, safety, and immediate pedagogical responsibility as capable of temporarily overriding normative concerns regarding bodily boundaries. In situations involving injury, health, or student well-being, participants described prioritizing professional and ethical responsibility over fears of possible misinterpretation.

Overall, the findings indicate that participants did not simply abandon pedagogical touch under conditions of visibility and social pressure. Rather, they described selectively reorganizing, limiting, or strategically maintaining bodily interaction according to context, perceived risk, pedagogical necessity, and student needs. In this sense, participants portrayed pedagogical contact not as a fixed practice but as a continuously negotiated aspect of professional judgment shaped by broader sociocultural expectations, institutional uncertainty, and ethical responsibility. Foucauldian perspectives on power and self-regulation help illuminate how these adaptive strategies emerged not only through restriction, but also through the ongoing reconfiguration of pedagogical practice under conditions of visibility and normative pressure.

Discussion

The findings suggest that participants experienced pedagogical touch as negotiated within broader conditions of visibility, social interpretation, and normative expectations. Participants referred to multiple forms of scrutiny, including parental interpretation, media discourse, digital circulation, and community expectations, while making decisions regarding bodily interaction in everyday professional practice. From a Foucauldian perspective, these findings can be interpreted in relation to the disciplinary effects of visibility and anticipatory self-monitoring described within panoptic forms of power [11]. Fletcher’s [7] study reveals that discussions regarding physical contact in school settings have created a panoptic sensitivity, leading teachers to adjust their behavior cautiously in anticipation of potential criticism and scrutiny. Similarly, the findings from this study indicate that the climate of fear surrounding physical contact serves as a factor shaping teachers’ behavior. Lang’s [28] study on swimming coaches shows that coaches position themselves in particularly visible areas to reduce the risk of being misunderstood. In this context, visibility becomes an indicator of safe and legitimate behavior. Similarly, in this study, teachers report that they carefully regulate their behavior and develop constant self-monitoring due to social media circulation and the potential for misunderstanding.

At the same time, the findings indicate that different forms of visibility and regulation should not be treated as analytically identical. Participants distinguished between institutional expectations, parental interpretation, media discourse, digital circulation, and broader cultural-religious norms. While some participants referred to formal school expectations and administrative caution, many more frequently described concerns regarding anticipated social judgment and community interpretation. Thus, the findings suggest that teachers’ self-regulation emerged not solely through direct institutional monitoring, but also through perceived normative climates surrounding morality, professionalism, gender, and child protection.

Studies on panoptic surveillance in education provide a useful theoretical framework for understanding how teachers may regulate themselves under conditions of perceived visibility. Perryman [29] demonstrates that school monitoring systems create a sense of constant surveillance among teachers and produce a panoptic effect that disciplines teacher behavior. Proudfoot [30] notes that while the panopticon metaphor remains valid for explaining teacher surveillance, additional conceptual frameworks are needed to understand not only teachers’ visible behaviors but also the invisible strategies they develop under surveillance. For this reason, the researcher proposes a “searchlight” metaphor to complement panoptic surveillance. The present study extends these discussions by showing how physical education teachers in Türkiye negotiated bodily interaction not only through institutional accountability but also through socially distributed forms of visibility shaped by media narratives, parental interpretation, digital circulation, and local moral expectations.

The findings also suggest that bodily interaction in Turkish physical education settings is negotiated within culturally specific understandings of modesty, gendered boundaries, and moral appropriateness. However, participants did not describe these expectations as uniform or homogeneous. Rather, perceptions regarding acceptable bodily contact appeared to vary across regions, school contexts, parental profiles, and professional experiences. Participants working in more conservative or Imam Hatip school environments more frequently referred to concerns regarding bodily proximity, gender interaction, and community interpretation, whereas participants working in western urban settings described relatively greater flexibility regarding physical contact. In this sense, the study contributes primarily through cultural contextualization by demonstrating how pedagogical touch is negotiated differently within varying sociocultural environments in Türkiye.

Another important finding of the study concerns how teachers negotiated ethical boundaries in the absence of clear institutional guidelines. Participants frequently stated that decisions regarding physical contact were shaped through professional ethics, experiential learning, and collective professional memory rather than through explicit formal rules. From a Foucauldian perspective, these accounts may be interpreted as reflecting processes of self-regulation in which participants actively monitored and evaluated their own conduct through internally constructed ethical boundaries [17, 19]. However, the findings also suggest that teachers’ self-regulation cannot be reduced solely to disciplinary submission or internalized surveillance. Participants simultaneously framed their decisions through pedagogical care, student well-being, professional responsibility, gendered vulnerability, and practical risk management.

Importantly, participants did not respond to these pressures in uniform ways. Some teachers described becoming increasingly cautious and professionally distant over time, whereas others emphasized developing more conscious and ethically negotiated forms of pedagogical contact through experience. Similarly, while some participants reorganized their practices through indirect strategies such as peer support, objects, and technological mediation, others continued to view physical contact as pedagogically unavoidable in situations involving learning, safety, or care. These differing trajectories highlight the ambivalent and context-dependent nature of self-regulation within physical education settings.

Collective professional memory also appeared to shape participants’ decision-making processes. Participants frequently referred to stories involving colleagues, rumors, or previous incidents within the profession as influencing their own preventive strategies and professional caution. Rather than functioning solely through direct institutional discipline, self-regulation often emerged through collectively shared narratives of reputational vulnerability and professional risk.

Another finding of the study indicates that participants did not completely abandon pedagogical contact under conditions of caution and visibility. Instead, many teachers described reorganizing bodily interaction through alternative pedagogical strategies such as the use of objects, peer support, gender-based pairing, and technological mediation. Similarly, Varea and Öhman [6] found that physical education teachers engage in a constant negotiation between pedagogical necessity and risk perception regarding physical contact. The study reveals that while teachers acknowledge the pedagogical necessity of physical contact, they are compelled to reorganize their professional practices due to increasing social and institutional discourses on risk. Similarly, Öhman’s [5] study shows that physical education teachers view physical interaction as pedagogically necessary and important, yet they address the issue of physical contact in different ways. The study reveals that teachers have developed different forms of self-regulation in response to this dual situation. In this context, while some teachers develop strategies to avoid contact, others continue to engage in physical contact by downplaying or normalizing it within the pedagogical context. The researcher conceptualizes this process along an axis of resistance arising from avoidance-oriented adaptation and the downplaying of contact.

However, the present findings suggest that these adaptive strategies should not be interpreted solely as products of disciplinary pressure. Participants also framed these practices through pedagogical care, ethical responsibility, student comfort, and child safety concerns. Thus, self-regulation appeared simultaneously disciplinary, ethical, relational, and pedagogical rather than reducible to a single form of power or control.

At the same time, the findings demonstrate that disciplinary caution was not absolute or uninterrupted. Participants frequently described situations involving student safety, injury, or health in which pedagogical and ethical responsibility temporarily overrode fears regarding social interpretation. These moments are important because they reveal tensions between professional caution and pedagogical care. Rather than simply withdrawing from physical contact, participants continuously negotiated competing responsibilities involving safety, learning, ethics, and self-protection. This finding complicates interpretations that reduce teachers’ practices solely to disciplinary compliance or defensive professionalism.

The findings also support critiques of rigid “no-touch” approaches within child protection discourse. Öhman and Quennerstedt [10] argue that approaches focused exclusively on avoiding physical contact may unintentionally narrow pedagogical relationships and overlook children’s broader educational and emotional needs. Similarly, the present findings suggest that overly restrictive or prohibition-oriented guidelines may intensify defensive teaching practices and reinforce cultures of fear surrounding bodily interaction.

Conclusion

This study explored how physical education teachers in Türkiye negotiate pedagogical touch within broader conditions of visibility, social interpretation, and normative expectation. The findings suggest that decisions regarding physical contact were shaped not only by formal institutional expectations, but also by parental interpretation, media discourse, digital visibility, community judgment, and culturally situated understandings of appropriate bodily interaction. Participants described anticipating possible misunderstanding, parental interpretation, media discourse, and digital circulation while making decisions regarding bodily interaction in everyday teaching practice.

Rather than presenting surveillance as a single or uniform mechanism, the study demonstrates that teachers negotiated physical contact within multiple and overlapping forms of visibility and normative pressure. Importantly, participants did not respond to these pressures in identical ways. While some teachers became increasingly cautious and reorganized their pedagogical practices through indirect strategies such as peer support, objects, and technological mediation, others continued to view physical contact as pedagogically necessary in situations involving learning, safety, care, and injury prevention. These findings suggest that self-regulation cannot be understood solely as a form of disciplinary submission. Participants also framed their decisions through professional ethics, pedagogical responsibility, child protection concerns, emotional care, and practical risk management.

The study contributes to the literature in three main ways. First, it provides an empirical contribution by documenting how physical education teachers in Türkiye experience and interpret pedagogical touch within differing sociocultural and institutional contexts. Second, it contributes theoretically by extending Foucauldian discussions of self-regulation and visibility through engagement with social norms literature and by analytically distinguishing between formal institutional monitoring, social interpretation, media discourse, digital visibility, and broader normative expectations.Third, the study offers practical implications by highlighting the need for context-sensitive guidance that supports pedagogical judgment, ethical reflection, and child protection awareness without reinforcing defensive “no-touch” cultures. Rather than rigid prohibition-oriented rules, future teacher education and professional development programs may benefit from reflective and scenario-based approaches that help teachers navigate uncertainty, negotiate appropriate bodily boundaries, and make context-sensitive pedagogical decisions within differing sociocultural environments.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study involved a relatively small and gender-imbalanced sample, and therefore the findings should not be interpreted as representative of all physical education teachers in Türkiye. In addition, the study relied on self-reported experiences rather than observational data and did not include the perspectives of students, parents, administrators, or policy actors. The findings therefore reflect participants’ interpretations of professional and social pressures rather than direct evidence of institutional surveillance practices. Future research may benefit from comparative studies involving different stakeholder groups, observational approaches, and analyses of institutional policies related to bodily interaction and child protection in educational settings.

Overall, the study suggests that pedagogical touch in physical education has not disappeared but has become increasingly negotiated within broader relations of visibility, ethics, social norms, and professional responsibility. In this context, teachers’ decisions regarding physical contact emerge not as fixed or universal practices, but as contextually situated and continuously negotiated forms of pedagogical judgment.
