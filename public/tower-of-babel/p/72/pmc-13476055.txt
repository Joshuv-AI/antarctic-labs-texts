Emergency Department Visits after Radiotherapy in Patients with Solid Malignancies: Analysis of Causality and Severity
Authors: Sunmin Park, Sungwoo Moon, Jong-Hak Park, Jungsun Kim, Jungyoon Choi, Chai Hong Rim, Won Sup Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to characterize emergency department (ED) visits after radiotherapy (RT) and to assess the causality and severity of these visits.MethodsPatients who received RT for solid malignancies from 2016 to 2021 were retrospectively analyzed. The study period for ED visits was defined from the start of RT to 3 months after the completion of RT. Causes of ED visits were classified as (1) cancer treatment-related causes, (2) RT-related causes, (3) cancer-related causes, and (4) other medical conditions, and the degree of relevance for each cause was assessed as (1) certain, (2) probable, (3) possible, or (4) unlikely. Severity was graded using the Korean Triage and Acuity Scale (KTAS). Acuity at ED presentation was categorized by KTAS grade: grades 1–2 as high, grade 3 as moderate, and grades 4–5 as low. Predictors related to strong relevance (certain and probable) for each cause and to severe events were examined using binary logistic regression. A p value <0.05 indicated statistical significance.ResultsA total of 177 patients presented to the ED, and 192 ED visits were evaluated. The thoracic site was the most frequent primary site, and 67.2% of cases were stage III/IV. KTAS 3, indicating urgency, was the most common, accounting for 64.1%. The degree of relevance to RT-related causes was positively correlated with KTAS grade (r = 0.253, p < 0.001). The proportions of ED visits with strong relevance for cancer treatment-related, RT-related, cancer-related, and other medical conditions were 62.5%, 41.1%, 16.1%, and 38.0%, respectively. Among causes with strong relevance, concurrent chemoradiotherapy (OR: 4.24, p < 0.001) and curative RT (OR: 3.23, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with cancer treatment-related and RT-related causes, respectively. Severe events were more frequently observed with thoracic primary tumors (OR: 3.01, p = 0.005), intervals >30 days after RT (OR: 2.60, p = 0.016), and age ≥65 years (OR: 2.37, p = 0.022).ConclusionPatients with identified risk factors require close and continuous follow-up after RT to reduce severe ED presentations.

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) is a principal treatment modality for cancer, used either alone or in combination with surgery and chemotherapy. Curative RT may target the primary tumor alone or include regional lymph nodes depending on the treatment strategy. In South Korea, the proportion of cancer patients receiving RT has increased from 24.5% in 2010 to 31.6% in 2019, partly due to patient-oriented cost-reduction policies for severe illnesses [1]. In addition, reimbursement by the national health insurance system in 2015 has contributed to the widespread adoption of intensity-modulated radiation therapy. Acute adverse effects of RT may occur during treatment and persist for up to 3 months after completion, requiring close clinical monitoring. The likelihood of these toxicities may increase, particularly with combined-modality therapy.

In our previous investigations, we assessed RT tolerance in a cohort including palliative cases and found that approximately 13% of patients experienced treatment interruption or could not complete RT [2]. Further research focusing on elderly patients receiving curative RT suggested that poor performance status and low serum albumin were associated with reduced treatment tolerance, while patients aged ≥75 years were more susceptible to treatment-related factors such as concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) or larger RT fields [3]. During these investigations, we frequently observed emergency department (ED) visits among patients undergoing RT.

Understanding the characteristics of ED visits during RT may help reduce unnecessary visits through improved outpatient management and timely interventions and prevent presentations in critical condition. However, limited studies have specifically examined ED visits during intensive cancer therapies. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the characteristics and causes of ED visits during or after RT and to identify patient groups at risk for more severe conditions. These findings may help guide preventive strategies and optimize patient management.

Methods

We retrospectively enrolled adult patients aged ≥19 years with solid tumors who underwent RT with curative intent at our institution between January 2016 and December 2021. Patients who presented to the ED during or within 3 months after completion of RT were included. Additional inclusion criteria were: (1) a planned radiation dose with a biologically effective dose of ≥50 Gy10; (2) RT administered to either the primary tumor or regional lymph nodes, and (3) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2 at the initiation of radiotherapy. The exclusion criteria comprised: (1) benign or in situ disease; (2) distant metastases; (3) incomplete medical records due to transfer from another hospital for RT only; (4) re-irradiation of a previously treated RT field; (5) a diagnosis of double primary cancer or another malignancy within 5 years, except for early-stage skin or thyroid cancer, and (6) ED visits related to COVID-19. Revisits for different causes were counted separately, whereas repeated visits for the same cause were recorded only once based on the most severe episode. These criteria were applied to define a homogeneous, curative-intent population and to allow reliable causality assessment: benign or in situ disease differs in disease behavior and treatment intent from invasive solid malignancy; distant metastases were excluded to avoid confounding by metastatic disease burden; transfer-only cases lacked adequate longitudinal records; re-irradiation carries non-comparable cumulative-dose toxicity; a second malignancy within 5 years (other than early-stage skin or thyroid cancer) precludes unambiguous attribution of an ED visit; and COVID-19-related visits reflected pandemic-era pathways unrelated to cancer treatment.

The causes of ED visits were categorized as follows: (1) cancer treatment-related causes (e.g., chemotherapy or CCRT), (2) RT-related causes, (3) cancer-related causes, and (4) other medical conditions, including comorbidities and acute intercurrent illnesses not directly attributable to cancer or its treatment. Because multiple causes could be assigned to a single ED visit, these categories were not mutually exclusive, and each cause was evaluated independently with respect to its relevance. The relevance of each cause was evaluated according to the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality assessment categories, which include the following: (1) certain, (2) probable, (3) possible, and (4) unlikely [4]. Our multidisciplinary team, comprising 2 radiation oncologists, 2 medical oncologists, and 2 emergency physicians, determined the classification of these causes after comprehensive case review and discussion.

We assessed whether each ED visit was expected by reviewing patient tolerability, disease status, and comorbid conditions. An expected ED visit was defined as one that was clinically predictable based on prior evaluation, including known treatment-related toxicity, disease status, or comorbid conditions documented during routine follow-up visits. Emergency severity was determined using the Korean Triage and Acuity Scale (KTAS), defining critical emergencies as resuscitation (grade 1) or emergency (grade 2), suspected critical emergencies as urgency (grade 3), non-critical emergencies as semi-urgent (grade 4), and non-urgent cases as grade 5 [5]. Severe events were identified as those meeting 1 or more of the following criteria: (1) KTAS grade 1 or 2, (2) admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), or (3) death occurring within 2 months of the ED visit. These 3 criteria were selected to define clinically meaningful severe outcomes using objective and reproducible markers: KTAS grades 1–2 denote the highest-acuity triage categories (resuscitation and emergency) on the validated KTAS scale; ICU admission indicates the need for organ support or critical deterioration; and death within 2 months identifies short-term mortality after ED presentation. The associations between disease extent, including T and N stage, and treatment-related factors, including CCRT and RT field extent, were assessed using the Spearman correlation analysis.

Demographic and clinical data were systematically collected for all patients. For ED visits, additional data regarding the condition at the time of visit and subsequent follow-up were incorporated. The degree of relevance for each cause was analyzed in relation to KTAS grade using Spearman correlation analysis. “Strong relevance” for each cause was defined as cases classified as “certain” or “probable,” and this variable was used as the primary outcome in the analysis. In the univariate analyses, the chi-square test assessed associations between strong relevance and categorized variables related to demographic and clinical factors. The same statistical approach was used to evaluate severe events. For multivariate analyses, binary logistic regression was performed to estimate the probability of strong relevance for each cause and the occurrence of severe events, based on independent variables with a p value <0.1 from the univariate analyses. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 22; IBM, Armonk, NY).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 177 patients who underwent RT subsequently visited the ED during or within 3 months after RT. The cohort had a median age of 63 years (range, 26–92 years), and 58.2% were female. The median body mass index (BMI) was 22.5 kg/m2 (range, 15.3–35.9 kg/m2). The most frequent primary sites included the thorax (27.1%), abdomen (19.8%), breast (19.2%), head and neck (15.3%), and pelvis (15.3%). According to AJCC staging (8th ed.), 67.2% of patients were classified as stage III–IV. Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) scores were distributed as follows: 0, 46.3%; 1, 26.0%; and ≥2, 27.7%. RT intent was classified as curative (49.2%), adjuvant (35.4%), preoperative (6.8%), and salvage (7.9%). The median biologically effective dose was 72.0 Gy10 (range, 4.8–119.6 Gy10). RT encompassed both the primary tumor and regional lymph nodes in 65.5% of cases. Regarding combined treatment modalities, CCRT and surgery were administered in 56.5% and 53.1% of patients, respectively. Table 1 summarizes the baseline clinicopathological characteristics. Distant metastasis was a pre-specified exclusion criterion; therefore, the stage III–IV proportion reflects locoregionally advanced, non-metastatic disease rather than metastatic disease. CCRT use increased across T categories (T1, 20.0%; T2, 44.7%; T3, 74.5%; T4, 71.4%; Spearman r = 0.355, p < 0.001). Higher N stage was associated with more frequent CCRT (N0, 36.5% vs. node-positive, 72.9%; r = 0.329, p < 0.001) and with regional nodal irradiation (N0, 31.8% vs. node-positive, 97.2%; r = 0.648, p < 0.001). Among the 94 patients classified as having surgery, radiotherapy was delivered postoperatively in the adjuvant setting in most cases (73 patients, 77.7%), while 9 patients (9.6%) received preoperative radiotherapy and 7 patients (7.4%) were treated in the salvage setting. The remaining 5 patients (5.3%) underwent supportive surgical procedures, such as tracheostomy or gastrostomy, during the treatment course. Among the 122 patients who received chemotherapy, 100 (82.0%) were treated with CCRT, and the remaining 22 (18.0%) received sequential chemotherapy, administered either before (neoadjuvant) or after (adjuvant) radiotherapy.

Characteristics of ED Visits

During the study period, approximately 3,320 adult patients received RT for malignant solid tumors at our institution; the cohort size decreased to approximately 2,040 when breast cancer was excluded. If an estimated 3–7% of these patients were excluded based on the study criteria, the incidence of ED visits was approximately 5.5–5.7% in the overall cohort, and about 7.2–7.5% in the subset excluding breast cancer.

A total of 192 ED visits were included in this analysis. Table 2 provides a detailed overview of the visit characteristics. The median interval from completion of RT to ED presentation was 36.4 days. The majority of patients used personal transportation (67.7%) to arrive at the ED, whereas 26.0% utilized the national emergency ambulance service, and 6.3% relied on other forms of medical transport.

KTAS 3, indicating urgent status, accounted for the largest portion (64.1%) of visits. Hemodynamic instability was observed in 32.3% of cases. Cancer treatment-related causes were the most frequent, with strong relevance observed in 62.5% of visits, followed by RT-related (41.1%), other medical conditions (38.0%), and cancer-related causes (16.1%). Overall, 67.7% of ED presentations were considered expected based on prior outpatient assessments. Nearly half of the patients required hospital admission, including 9.4% admitted to the ICU. Among those admitted to the ICU, only 27.8% fully recovered, while 61.1% died. Dyspnea was the most frequently reported chief complaint, followed by generalized weakness and chest pain.

Causality of ED Visits

The degree of relevance to RT-related causes showed a correlation with KTAS grades (r = 0.253, p < 0.001), and a higher urgency was associated with a greater likelihood that ED visits were expected (r = 0.162, p = 0.025). Figure 1 presents the distribution of ED visits after radiotherapy, stratified by the cause of ED visit and acuity as defined by KTAS grade. As shown in Figure 1a, across all causes of ED visit, KTAS grade 3 (moderate acuity) was the most frequent category. ED visits attributed to cancer treatment-related or RT-related causes showed a high proportion of expected visits, whereas those attributed to other medical conditions showed a substantially lower proportion despite a similar acuity distribution. Specifically, among KTAS grade 3 visits, expected ED visits accounted for 93.3% (70/75) in cancer treatment-related visits, 98.1% (51/52) in RT-related visits, and 91.7% (22/24) in cancer-related visits. In contrast, only 29.2% (14/48) of KTAS 3 visits related to other medical conditions were considered expected. Expected visits were less frequent in lower acuity categories (KTAS grades 4–5) across causes. In Figure 1b, the distribution of severe events differed from that of expected ED visits. Severe events were observed across all causes of ED visit and were strongly associated with higher acuity. Notably, all KTAS grade 1–2 visits were classified as severe events across all causes (100%), whereas the proportion of severe events decreased in KTAS grade 3 and KTAS grade 4–5 visits, with variation according to the cause of ED visit. Together, these findings indicate that while most ED visits after RT present with moderate acuity, predictability (expected ED visits) and clinical severity (severe events) diverge substantially according to both acuity and the cause of ED visit.

The appendix provides the univariate analysis results. It is important to note that this study examined predictors of strong relevance within the cohort that actually presented to the ED, not among all patients receiving RT. As such, any predictors that lacked a logical causal relationship were excluded from the multivariate analysis, regardless of whether they had a p value <0.1 in the univariate analysis. Multivariate analysis indicated that strong relevance to cancer treatment was associated with CCRT (OR: 4.24, 95% CI: 2.16–8.34, p < 0.001), adjuvant aim (OR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.22–0.86, p = 0.016), and N2-3 disease (vs. N0–1) (OR: 2.19, 95% CI: 1.03–4.66, p = 0.042). For RT, curative intent (OR: 3.23, 95% CI: 1.69–6.17, p < 0.001) and CCRT (OR: 2.19, 95% CI: 1.09–4.40, p = 0.027) were significant. After the exclusion of breast cancer in the context of RT, thorax site (OR: 2.33, 95% CI: 1.14–4.74, p = 0.020) and CCRT (OR: 2.43, 95% CI: 1.15–5.16, p = 0.021) were determined as predictors. Abdominal site (OR: 2.53, 95% CI: 1.06–6.05, p = 0.037) was identified as a significant predictor for cancer-related causes. For other medical conditions, a CCI ≥1 with curative aim (OR: 6.67, 95% CI: 2.90–15.3, p < 0.001) and adjuvant aim (OR: 6.40, 95% CI: 2.78–14.72, p < 0.001) were significant (Table 3). ED visits in surgical patients were less likely to show strong relevance to cancer treatment-related causes (53.4% vs. 74.4%; OR: 0.39, p = 0.003), RT-related causes (29.1% vs. 55.8%; OR: 0.33, p < 0.001), and cancer-related causes (OR: 0.39, p = 0.023). Conversely, the proportion of visits with strong relevance to other medical conditions was higher among surgical patients, although this did not reach statistical significance (43.7% vs. 30.2%; OR: 1.79, 95% CI: 0.98–3.27, p = 0.058). Surgery was also associated with fewer severe events (30.1% vs. 44.2%; OR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.30–0.99, p = 0.046). However, surgical patients were predominantly treated in an adjuvant, preoperative, or salvage setting (88.3%) and received CCRT less frequently (45.6% vs. 69.8%, p = 0.001), whereas non-surgical patients were mostly treated with curative RT (95.3%; p < 0.001).

Severity of ED Visits

Severe events were more frequently observed in patients with a thoracic primary site (OR: 3.01, 95% CI: 1.40–6.47, p = 0.005), an interval after RT >30 days (OR: 2.60, 95% CI: 1.20–5.66, p = 0.016), and age ≥65 years (OR: 2.37, 95% CI: 1.13–4.94, p = 0.022). In subgroup analyses with strong relevance to RT-related causes, an interval after RT >30 days (OR: 6.32, 95% CI: 2.11–18.96, p = 0.001) and a thoracic primary site (OR: 2.78, 95% CI: 0.97–7.93, p = 0.056) showed marginal significance (Table 4).

Discussion

Several studies have investigated ED visits among cancer patients; however, most relied on large-scale databases that primarily describe demographic and socioeconomic characteristics such as race, age, income, and insurance payer, without detailed clinical information [6, 7]. Our study provides an advantage by utilizing individual patient data collected up to 3 months after RT, allowing a more detailed evaluation of ED visits among patients undergoing RT.

After completion of curative RT, patients are typically followed for several months to evaluate treatment response and treatment-related toxicity. In our study, approximately 5–6% of patients visited the ED during this observation period, exceeding 7% when breast cancer patients receiving adjuvant RT were excluded. Among these patients, approximately 80% required urgent or emergent care, nearly half required hospitalization, and 14.6% died within 2 months after the ED visit. According to a South Korean database study, cancer patients were more likely than non-cancer patients to have a KTAS grade of 3 or lower, had extended ED stays, and faced higher admission rates [8]. Another report noted that cancer patients constituted 6.8% of all ED patients, with 57% requiring admission, and 16.1% experiencing in-hospital death [9]. In an Austrian single-institution study, 69% of cancer patients were admitted and 12.1% of cases followed unplanned ED visits. Lung cancer was the most prevalent primary cancer and pain was the most common chief complaint [10].

Strong relevance was most frequent in cancer treatment-related causes (62.5%), followed by RT-related (41.1%), other medical conditions (38.0%), and cancer-related causes (16.1%). The degree of relevance to RT-related causes was correlated with KTAS grade. Identifying high-risk patients and implementing proactive strategies, including patient education and early clinical intervention, may help reduce emergency presentations. Clinical factors reflecting treatment intensity, particularly CCRT and curative RT, were strongly associated with cancer treatment-related ED visits in our cohort. Previous studies have likewise shown that chemotherapy is a major cause of ED visits and that a substantial proportion of cancer treatment-related visits may be potentially preventable [11]. Treatment intensity has also been associated with ED utilization in head and neck cancer [12].

Severe events were more frequently associated with RT delivered to thoracic primary sites and tended to occur more than 1 month after treatment, highlighting the importance of continued monitoring during follow-up. This finding is consistent with our previous study demonstrating lower tolerance to thoracic RT, likely due to radiation exposure to organs such as the lungs and esophagus [2]. Older age was also associated with severe events. Dyspnea was the most common reason for ED visits in our cohort, which may reflect differences in tumor distribution and observation periods across studies. Previous studies have reported that the most common causes of hospitalization related to treatment toxicity vary by cancer type and healthcare setting, including radiation cystitis, colitis, esophagitis, septicemia, and intestinal obstruction [13, 14].

The role of surgery in our cohort should be interpreted in the context of treatment strategy and patient selection. Patients who had undergone surgery were predominantly treated in an adjuvant, preoperative, or salvage setting, whereas non-surgical patients were mostly treated with curative RT and more frequently received CCRT. Thus, the observed association between surgery and ED-visit outcomes may primarily reflect differences in treatment intensity rather than an independent effect of surgery itself.

Tumor and nodal extent were closely related to treatment intensity and RT field extent. Advanced T and N categories were associated with more frequent CCRT, and nodal disease was strongly associated with inclusion of regional nodal irradiation. Because distant metastasis was excluded, the high proportion of stage III–IV disease in this cohort reflects locoregionally advanced, non-metastatic disease. These relationships provide clinical context for our findings that CCRT and curative RT were associated with treatment-related and RT-related ED visits and that N2-3 disease was associated with strong relevance to cancer treatment-related causes.

The site distribution among ED visitors differed from that among all patients treated with radiotherapy during the study period (online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553522). Breast cancer was the most frequently treated site in the institutional registry (36.6%) but accounted for only 19.2% of ED visitors, indicating relative under-representation among ED presentations; pelvic primaries were similarly under-represented. In contrast, head and neck, thoracic, and abdominal primaries were over-represented among ED visitors compared with our institutional treatment volume. This pattern suggests that ED presentations were more frequent in disease sites often requiring intensive or combined-modality treatment and is consistent with the independent association between a thoracic primary site and severe events.

Several studies focusing on patients receiving RT have also reported substantial rates of ED visits or unplanned hospitalization. Among patients with head and neck cancer receiving CCRT, 34.7% required hospitalization, with comorbidity burden and radiation dose identified as significant factors [15]. Similar findings were reported in another head and neck cancer cohort, in which advanced stage, comorbidities, CCRT, smoking history, and marital status were associated with hospitalization risk [16]. In nasopharyngeal cancer, ED visits were associated with older age, higher comorbidity burden, and advanced T stage [17]. In colorectal cancer, ED utilization within the first year after diagnosis was also substantial, particularly among patients with greater comorbidity and treatment exposure [18].

Because the observation period in our study was limited to 3 months after RT, the proportion of cancer-related ED visits was relatively high at 16.2%. Many visits were classified as KTAS grade 4 or 5, suggesting that anxiety related to cancer diagnosis and treatment may contribute to ED visits for relatively mild symptoms. Another Korean study has also reported that a proportion of cancer-related ED visits may be potentially non-emergent [19].

In our study, 15 patients were analyzed for repeat ED visits due to different reasons; however, considering cases with overlapping identical reasons, a considerable proportion of patients experienced revisits. In a US database study, 17.9% of patients had revisits within 7 days, a rate higher than the 13.2% observed in non-cancer patients. Moreover, this study highlighted the importance of implementing ongoing management programs [20]. One report demonstrated that weekday urgent care clinics led to a reduction in weekday ED visits. Notably, there was no change observed on weekends when the urgent care clinic services were unavailable [21]. Additionally, another study found that early palliative care referral in patients with pancreatic cancer resulted in decreased ED visits compared to those referred at a later stage [22]. There is a need to establish multiple safety nets, not only to ease the societal burden of ED visits but also to minimize acute deterioration and ensure the safety of cancer therapy. The implementation and evaluation of support programs are warranted.

This study has several limitations. First, the predictors for strong relevance and severe events were analyzed only within the patient cohort who visited the ED, rather than among all patients who underwent RT. Consequently, as described in the results, clinical variables that lacked a clear logical rationale were omitted from the multivariate analysis. Second, as a single-institution study, sampling bias cannot be excluded. Although causality assessments were determined through multidisciplinary consensus, these evaluations may not fully reflect broader clinical perspectives. Furthermore, this study focused primarily on the post-RT period, excluding cancers not indicated for RT. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to the entire cancer population, and interpretation must account for the patterns of cancer incidence and the specific health care system in South Korea.

Conclusions

The proportion of ED visits during RT or within 3 months following RT exceeded 5%. When excluding breast cancer, this rate increased to over 7%, highlighting a common issue that requires substantial attention. Most ED visits are primarily attributable to cancer treatments, including RT. Importantly, the level of relevance to RT tends to correspond with the severity of the presenting emergency. It may be feasible to stratify patient populations by major risk factors, assess their radiation dose distributions, and forecast possible adverse outcomes to inform clinical recommendations. Thus, developing a clinical program for rigorous post-RT surveillance could be valuable.
