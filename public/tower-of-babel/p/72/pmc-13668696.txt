7-ketocholesterol contributes to microglia-driven increases in astrocyte reactive oxygen species in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Kayalvizhi Radhakrishnan, Yiyu Zhang, Oluwaseun Mustapha, Thaddeus K Weigel, Clint M Upchurch, Xiaodong Tian, Franklin Herbert, Wenyuan Huang, Norbert Leitinger, Ukpong B Eyo, Huiwang Ai, Heather A Ferris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxidative stress is a prominent feature of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Within this context, cholesterol undergoes oxidation, producing the pro-inflammatory product 7-ketocholesterol (7-KC). In this study, we observe elevated levels of 7-KC in the brains of the 3xTg mouse model of AD. To further understand the contribution of 7-KC on the oxidative environment, we developed a method to express a genetically encoded fluorescent hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) sensor in astrocytes, the primary source of cholesterol in the brain. With this sensor, we showed that 7-KC increases H2O2 levels in astrocytes in vivo, but not when directly applied to astrocytes in vitro. When 7-KC was applied to a microglia cell line alone or mixed astrocyte and microglia cultures, it resulted in microglia activation and increased oxidative stress in astrocytes. Depletion of microglia from 3xTg mice resulted in reduced 7-KC and reduced reactive oxygen species in astrocytes. Taken together, these findings suggest that 7-KC, via microglia activation, contributes to increased astrocyte oxidative stress in the 3xTg mouse model of AD. Introduction

Astrocytes are the main source of cholesterol in the brain1. Astrocyte-derived cholesterol can be used in cell membranes and organelles of the astrocyte, stored in lipid droplets, or transported to other cells of the brain. A variety of metabolic stressors, including excess lipid accumulation2, amyloid beta (Aβ) exposure3 and cytokine exposure4, can result in increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) in astrocytes. Increased oxidative stress in astrocytes has been implicated in the pathogenesis of AD3. 7-ketocholesterol (7-KC) is a cholesterol derivative produced as a result of non-enzymatic ROS-induced oxidation of the cholesterol molecule at the C-7 position. Elevated levels of 7-KC have been detected in atherosclerotic plaques, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) brains, the serum of individuals with diabetes5–8, and the retina of individuals with age-related macular degeneration9. 7-KC is known to trigger inflammatory pathways and induce apoptosis in neurons and glial cells10–13. As a toxic component, 7-KC promotes oxidative stress, resulting in an amplification of ROS production, which can damage proteins, lipids, and DNA. There are limited studies of 7-KC in microglia9, the impact on astrocytes has not yet been assessed.

Direct measurement of ROS in biological systems presents significant challenges. The most common techniques involve the quantification of oxidized lipids or proteins, the end products of oxidative stress. These measures cannot dynamically assess ROS levels but instead provide a snapshot of accumulated exposure. Furthermore, it is often challenging to gain spatial and cell-specific information from the analyzed samples. Recently, a group of genetically encoded fluorescent redox indicators (GERIs) have been developed for the spatial and temporal monitoring of ROS14–17. They are now being increasingly utilized to study oxidative stress in disease models18–20. In a previous study, we fused a mitochondrial targeting sequence to a ratiometric hydrogen peroxide sensor, HyPerGRT21. HyPerGRT is comprised of a green-fluorescent hydrogen peroxide sensor, HyPer716, and a red fluorescent protein, tdTomato22. While HyPer7 is intrinsically an excitation-ratiometric hydrogen peroxide indicator, reliable 405/488-nm excitation-ratiometric imaging of HyPer7 in mammalian tissues is challenging given the overlap with tissue autofluorescence23–25. To overcome this, fusion with tdTomato enabled robust ratiometric imaging of mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide in a variety of preparations, including cultured HeLa cells, primary mouse neurons, Aβ-treated organotypic brain slices, and brain slices from Aβ-treated mice21.

Our previous study validated the response of this sensor to hydrogen peroxide and generated serotype 9 AAVs with an hSyn promoter for imaging mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide in neurons21. In this study, we expanded the use of HyPerGRT, expressing the sensor specifically in astrocytes for the detection of astrocyte ROS. We coupled this sensor with other techniques, including mass spectrometry, in vitro astrocyte and microglia cultures, fluorescence imaging, and AD mouse models to study the relationship between 7-KC and ROS in astrocytes and mouse models of AD.

We identified that both astrocyte ROS and 7-KC are elevated in the brains of the 3xTg mouse model of AD. We further show that 7-KC, but not cholesterol, increases astrocyte hydrogen peroxide levels in vitro and in vivo. To determine if the elevation in astrocyte ROS was a direct effect of 7-KC on astrocytes, we applied 7-KC to primary astrocyte mixed glia cultures. Our findings revealed a limited direct impact of 7-KC on astrocytes in the absence of microglia. In contrast, 7-KC was able to activate microglia in vitro. Finally, depletion of microglia from 3xTg mice reduced 7-KC in the cortex of these animals and reduced cytosolic ROS in astrocytes. Overall, this study shows that 7-KC is increased in the 3xTg mouse brain and can contribute to increased astrocyte ROS by activating microglia. In addition, this study introduces an astrocyte-specific AAV redox indicator as a convenient tool for studying ROS specifically in astrocytes both in vitro and in vivo.

Results

7-KC and ROS are mutually inducible

Previous studies have implicated the oxidized cholesterol species 7-KC as a driver of oxidative stress in various diseases5. Reactive oxygen species oxidize cholesterol to form 7-KC and increased 7-KC in turn increases ROS (Fig. 1a) by activating enzymes like NADPH oxidase and by interfering with mitochondrial function26–28. Elevated levels of 7-KC have been seen in postmortem brains of individuals with AD13, so, we assessed whether this phenomenon is also present in a mouse model of AD. We collected brains from 7–8-month-old 3xTg and control mice. 3xTg mice express mutated forms of both human Aβ and tau and show evidence of neuroinflammation and at 7-8-months demonstrate early amyloid plaque formation, but not tau pathology29,30. Lipid samples were prepared from these brains and analyzed using mass spectrometry to determine the levels of 7-KC. The concentration of 7-KC was higher in the brains of 3xTg mice compared to controls (Fig. 1b). Elevated 7-KC levels in the brains of 3xTg mice relative to controls, indicated heightened oxidative stress, consistent with the increased 7-KC observed in humans with AD. Given that ROS is a driver of 7-KC formation, we set out to quantify ROS in 3xTg mouse brains.

Whole brain was isolated from 7–8-month-old 3xTg and age-matched control mice. 7-KC was measured by mass spectrometry and normalized to starting tissue weight. The mean is +/- SEM. N = 4–7 animals per group.

Astrocyte-specific expression of AAV HyPerGRT

Previous studies have highlighted a pathogenic association between 7-KC accumulation and inflammation during disease progression in AD brains of humans and mouse models of AD13,31. To better understand this process, we applied a genetically encoded ratiometric hydrogen peroxide sensor, HyPerGRT, to examine hydrogen peroxide levels in astrocytes. In the presence of oxidation, HyPerGRT exhibits an increase in green fluorescence, while maintaining relatively stable red fluorescence. This characteristic enables ratiometric calibration, which takes into account the different expression levels of the sensor in individual cells. To enable the expression of the sensor in astrocytes, we generated a new AAV plasmid containing HyPerGRT under a truncated GFAP promoter32(GfaABC1D) to direct astrocyte-specific expression (Fig. 2a). Next, we utilized the newly developed plasmid along with packaging plasmids to generate AAVs with serotype 5.

To validate the virus in vivo, we performed intracranial injections of the virus into the hippocampus of C57BL/6J mice. After 2–3 weeks of viral expression, strong fluorescence was observed in the acute brain slices obtained from these animals. The cells expressing the sensor exhibited morphological characteristics of astrocytes (Fig. 2b). The majority of the sensor expression was in the cytoplasm and overlapped with GFAP antibody staining (Fig S1). Next, we transduced C6 astroglioma cells with the virus. After 72 h of viral expression, cells were treated with 10 µM hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. Strong expression of the virus was observed in the C6 cells (Fig. 2c) and the green-to-red fluorescence ratio increased by approximately 1.3-fold following hydrogen peroxide treatment in fixed cells and 1.6-fold in live cells (Fig. 2d). While HyPerGRT live-cell imaging provides superior sensitivity for detecting rapid and reversible hydrogen peroxide dynamics, fixation-based analysis still captures significant and measurable redox increases and offers a convenient workflow that allows imaging to be performed after fixation. To further compare the HyPerGRT emission-ratiometric readout with the intrinsic HyPer7 excitation-ratiometric readout, we performed time-lapse imaging of AAV-transduced C6 cells in response to 10 µM hydrogen peroxide (Fig S2). HyPer7 excitation-ratiometric imaging produced the largest apparent response, as expected for the parent sensor, whereas the HyPerGRT green-to-red emission ratio showed a robust increase with similar response kinetics. Single-channel green fluorescence also increased after hydrogen peroxide addition, but the ratiometric readouts provide normalization against sensor expression and imaging variability. These results indicate that HyPerGRT preserves the hydrogen peroxide responsiveness of HyPer7 while enabling emission-ratiometric imaging that avoids reliance on UV or near-UV excitation, which is particularly advantageous for tissue imaging. Together, these results support the robust astrocyte-specific expression achieved by AAVs carrying the GFAP promoter, consistent with previous findings33, and confirm the functionality of the sensor.

Next, we asked whether 7-KC can independently induce ROS in astrocytes in vivo. For this, we injected C57BL/6J mice in the hippocampus with the astrocyte-specific AAV HyPerGRT virus. Following a 3-week period for viral expression, we performed stereotactic injections of 1 µl of 5 µM 7-KC or cholesterol (as a control) into the hippocampus. After 3 days, we imaged acute brain slices and observed a notable elevation of cytosolic ROS in astrocytes from the brains of mice injected with 7-KC, compared to those injected with cholesterol (Fig. 2e, f). This suggests that 7-KC acts as a key upstream lipid mediator that promotes astrocytic ROS generation and may trigger downstream inflammatory signaling.

7-ketocholesterol increases astrocyte ROS and activates astrocytes in a mixed glial culture system

To better understand the source of increased ROS in astrocytes we switched to an in vitro culture system. Astrocyte-enriched primary mixed glial cultures were transduced with astrocyte-specific AAV HyPerGRT virus. After one week of virus expression, cells were treated with 10 μm 7-KC or cholesterol for 24 h and then imaged or collected for qPCR (Fig. 3a). ROS was quantified using the green to red fluorescence ratiometry. We observed that astrocytes treated with 7-KC showed significantly higher ROS compared to cholesterol-treated cells (Fig. 3b, c). In addition to this, we performed qPCR for cytokines and activation markers of astrocytes and microglia. Transcripts of the widely used astrocyte activation marker, Gfap, were significantly increased with 7-KC, as were Gbp2, a gene implicated in neuroinflammation34, Ptgs2 (Cox2), a gene important in inflammation and Aβ deposition35 and Ptx3, shown to be involved in neuroinflammation36,37(Fig. 3d). We observed low, but detectable transcripts of Iba1, indicating a low level of microglia in the cultures. Transcripts of the cytokine Tnfα and the microglia phagocytosis marker, Cd68, were significantly increased with 7-KC. In contrast, transcripts of P2ry12, whose expression often decreases with microglia activation, showed a trend towards decreased expression with 7-KC treatment (Fig. 3e). These data indicate that 7-KC could be acting either directly on astrocytes or through microglia to increase astrocyte ROS.

7-ketocholesterol does not directly increase astrocyte activation markers

We next assessed if the increase in astrocyte activation observed in response to 7-KC was due to a direct effect of 7-KC on astrocytes. For this we treated primary mixed glial cultures with 5 µM PLX3397 to significantly reduce microglial populations from the culture. After five days of PLX3397, the cells were treated with 7-KC or cholesterol for 24 h and qPCR was performed (Fig. 4a). In contrast to the mixed glial cultures, in the absence of microglia, Gfap, Gbp2, Ptgs2 and Ptx3 were not increased in response to 7-KC (Fig. 4b). Expression of the microglia-specific Iba1, Cd68 and P2yr12 were very low or undetectable, indicating effective elimination of microglia from the culture (Fig. 4c). Both the microglia-specific transcripts and Tnfα transcripts, which can come from both astrocytes and microglia, did not change in response to 7-KC treatment (Fig. 4c). These results indicate that 7-KC does not influence astrocytes directly, but rather that microglia have a key role to play in astrocyte activation by 7-KC.

(b, c) qPCR of mixed glial cultures showing absence of changes in astrocyte-associated gene transcripts and loss of microglia-associated gene transcripts. N = 3 independent cultures. All p-values were obtained from two-tailed unpaired t-test. The mean is +/- SEM.

7-ketocholesterol increases microglial activation markers in SIM-A9 cells

Given that we did not see significant activation of astrocytes with 7-KC when microglia were depleted from primary cultures, we investigated the activation of microglia by 7-KC directly. To test this, primed SIM-A9 microglia-derived cells were treated with 12 µM of either 7-KC or cholesterol as a control (Fig. 5a). In response to 7-KC, transcripts of microglia activation markers, Il1β and Iba1 were significantly increased (Fig. 5b). Tnfα transcript showed an increasing trend without statistical significance. Therefore, our results demonstrate that 7-KC is able to activate microglia directly.

Depletion of microglia from 3xTg mice is accompanied by reduced 7-KC

Microglia are the immune cells of the CNS and are activated in AD. Because 7-KC is generated in the presence of oxidative stress, we hypothesized that microglia would be a major source of 7-KC in AD mouse models. In order to investigate if increased 7-KC in 3xTg brains is initiated by microglia, we depleted microglia in these mice by feeding with 660 mg/kg PLX3397. Following microglia depletion, brains were extracted and prepared lipid samples were analyzed for total cholesterol and 7-KC. As anticipated, 7-KC levels decreased in 3xTg brains upon microglia depletion, without impacting total cholesterol (Fig. 6a, b). Thus, microglia are required for the elevations in 7-KC seen in 3xTg brains.

Microglia are required for 7-KC increase in 3xTg brain. (a) Hippocampi were isolated from 12-month-old female 3xTg and control mice fed either a control diet or PLX3397 for 4 weeks. There was no difference in total cholesterol between groups. (b) However, there was a reduction in 7-KC in the PLX3397-fed 3xTg mice compared to chow-fed 3xTG mice. The mean is +/- SEM. N = 4–5 animals per group.

ROS in 3xTg is reverted to baseline with elimination of microglia

Given the increase in 7-KC in 3xTg mice and its reversal with PLX3397, we tested if this result correlated with ROS in these mice. For this, the AAV HyPerGRT sensor with astrocyte-specific expression was stereotactically injected into the hippocampi of 3xTg and B6129SF2/J control animals. After 3 weeks, mice were fed PLX3397 for 10 days to deplete microglia. IBA1 staining confirmed the depletion of microglia with PLX3397 (Fig. 7a, S3). Imaging of the sensor demonstrated that hippocampal astrocytes of 3xTg mice showed a statistically significant 1.5-fold increase in ROS compared to control mice (Fig. 7b-d), while both the control and 3xTg mice treated with PLX3397 demonstrated similar levels of ROS to untreated controls. These data show that ROS in astrocytes is driven by microglia in 3xTg mice.

In conclusion, our data supports a vicious cycle in AD mouse models, wherein 7-KC elicits astrocyte activation in a microglia-dependent manner. This microglia activation triggers an increase in astrocyte ROS, providing a source for the generation of additional 7-KC. Interrupting this cycle may prove an important target for reducing neuroinflammation in diseases such as AD.

Discussion

7-Ketocholesterol increases in Alzheimer’s disease

7-KC is a toxic derivative of cholesterol mainly produced by auto-oxidation during adverse physiological changes and pathological conditions38,39. 7-KC is elevated in brain tissues13, cerebrospinal fluids and plasma of patients with neurological disorders such as AD40 and it is a biomarker of oxidative stress41–43. While 7-KC is produced by the interaction of ROS with cholesterol, it also furthers the stimulation of ROS production. Since inflammation is a prominent feature of AD, we examined the levels of 7-KC in 3xTg mouse brains. As determined by mass spectrometry, the levels of 7-KC were higher in 3xTg compared to control mouse brains. The magnitude of this increase is similar to what is seen in human AD brains13.

7-Ketocholesterol and reactive oxygen species

7-KC, an important oxidized form of cholesterol, has been shown in several studies to increase inflammation and reactive oxygen species production. For example, 7-KC treatment induces hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anion production in microglial BV2 cells44. These reactive species can cause cellular dysfunction and in immune cells this can lead to inflammation. In macrophages, NADPH-oxidase plays a significant role in elevated ROS production in response to oxysterols, including 7-KC27. In human aortic smooth muscles cells 7-KC induced apoptosis and ER stress mediated by NOX4, which has a pivotal role in ROS generation26. 7-KC also increased myocardial infarction size by activating macrophage-mediated inflammation45. In PC12 cells ROS levels increased due to 7-KC-induced apoptosis46, while in N2a neuronal cells 7-KC induced ROS and mitochondrial dysfunction12. Thus, 7-KC induces biologically significant ROS that induces inflammatory responses in multiple disease states.

Of note, the magnitude of the HyPerGRT ratio increase observed after 7-KC treatment was similar to that observed after direct application of H2O2 onto cells. This similarity in amplitude should not be interpreted as indicating that 7-KC produces hydrogen peroxide concentrations equivalent to those achieved by exogenous H2O2 application. Exogenous H2O2 exposure represents an acute perturbation, and hydrogen peroxide that does not react with the sensor is rapidly consumed or neutralized by cellular antioxidant systems. In contrast, 7-KC treatment produces a slower and more sustained biological response involving cellular redox regulation, antioxidant buffering, and cell-cell signaling. Therefore, HyPerGRT measurements in these experiments are best interpreted as relative changes in the H2O2-sensitive cellular redox state rather than as absolute measurements of H2O2 concentration. Also, because HyPer7-based sensors are reversible redox probes, fixed-cell HyPerGRT imaging should not be interpreted as a cumulative or irreversible measurement of hydrogen peroxide exposure. Rather, fixation was only used to limit post-treatment thiol redox changes and provide a convenient endpoint comparison across experimental conditions. We therefore use HyPerGRT to compare sensor responses between groups within the same experimental context, rather than to make quantitative comparisons across different experimental paradigms.

Microglia drive 7-ketocholesterol-mediated increases in astrocyte ROS

There are several studies examining the impact of 7-KC on microglia, however its influence on astrocytes had not previously been examined. In this study, using a genetically modified AAV GFAP-HyPerGRT ROS sensor, we showed that astrocyte ROS increases in the hippocampus of C57BL/6J mice upon 7-KC injection. This suggested that 7-KC by itself can cause activation of astrocytes. Similar results were obtained in vitro in primary mixed glial cultures where the astrocytes showed activation. However, 7-KC treatment had no effect on microglia-depleted cultures. In contrast, the microglial cell line SIM-A9 responded to 7-KC treatment, implicating microglia in 7-KC-mediated increases in astrocyte ROS. Our data supports recent findings that chronic activation of microglia by oxysterols, including 7-KC, is linked to the ongoing neuroinflammation seen in AD47. This inflammatory response exacerbates neuronal damage, contributing to AD pathology.

This was further confirmed by in vivo depletion of microglia in 3xTg, where 7-KC levels decreased with the depletion of microglia. This also indicates that microglia could be involved in the production of 7-KC and contribute to a vicious activation cycle, where microglia induce 7-KC production, which in turn activates microglia and causes subsequent inflammatory responses in astrocytes.

This study further demonstrates the bidirectional relationship between 7-KC and ROS, by showing elevated astrocyte ROS in 3xTg mice along with increased 7-KC levels. 7-KC exacerbates ROS, causing an increase in brain 7-KC levels. Elimination of the increased ROS seen in 3xTg with microglia depletion indicates the pivotal role of microglia in 7-KC-mediated astrocyte ROS.

This study identifies 7‑KC as a contributor to microglial activation and subsequent astrocytic ROS production in mouse models, implicating this pathway in inflammatory processes observed in experimental models of neurodegeneration. Further investigations are needed to study the mechanism of activation of microglia by 7-KC and the mechanism by which 7-KC-stimulated-microglia cause astrocyte activation that add up to disease progression.

Integrating the fluorescent biosensor with AD mouse models will enable future longitudinal in vivo imaging of ROS. This approach can be used to further investigate the complex interactions among microglia, 7‑KC, and astrocytic ROS in vivo, providing insight into disease‑associated processes within experimental models.

Methods

General animal information

All experiments were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations of the United States federal government and with the approval of the University of Virginia Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Institutional Biosafety Committee. 3xTg experiments were conducted with 7–14-month-old female 3xTg29(B6;129-Psen1tm1Mpm Tg(APPSwe, tauP301L)1Lfa/Mmjax, Jackson Laboratory #034830), and age-matched B6129SF2/J (Jackson Laboratory #101045) females as wild-type controls. Only female mice were used given the higher expression of the transgenes in female mice and the female sex predominance of AD in humans. C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (#000664) and maintained as pure colonies and females were used at 9 months.

Depletion of microglia in animals

For microglia depletion experiments mice were given chow formulated with the CSF-1R inhibitor PLX3397 (660 mg/kg) or control diet48–50. They were maintained on PLX3397 treatment or control diet for four weeks.

Measurement of 7-KC levels by mass spectrometry

For 7-KC measurements, mice were first anesthetized with a ketamine-xylazine solution and transcardially perfused with chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). All mice were sacrificed between Zeitgeber time 5–9. The brain region of interest (whole brain or hippocampus) was dissected out, weighed, and flash frozen. Samples were homogenized in water with 1.2% butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) (Sigma-Aldrich) to prevent oxidation. Sterols were extracted using the modified Bligh-Dyer method. Samples were mixed with deuterated standards (cholesterol-d7 and 24-dehydro cholesterol-d6, Cayman Chemical Company) to control for differences in extraction efficiency. Samples were further extracted with chloroform, dried under nitrogen gas, and resuspended in methanol. Sterols were separated using binary reverse-phase ultra-high performance Vanquish liquid chromatography (Phenomenex Reverse Phase C18, 3 mm particle size, 2 mm diameter, 250 mm length) using Solvent A (methanol; 5 mM ammonium acetate) and Solvent B (15% water; 85% methanol; 5 mM ammonium acetate) with a flow rate of 0.200 mL/min and the following gradient: 0 to 2 min, 0% B; 2 to 8 min, 0-100% B; 8–32 min, 100% B, 32 to 35 min, 100-0% B; 35 to 40 min 0% B. Column temperature of 35 °C was used, and sterols were quantified by electrospray ionization (ESI) spectrometry and selective ion monitoring (SIM) using a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive mass spectrometer. The mass spectrometer was operated with the following settings: Resolution – 35,000; AGC Target – 5e5; Scan Range – 150–2000 m/z; Isolation window – 2.0 m/z; and Runtime – 10 to 22 min.

Mouse primary cultures

Astrocyte-enriched mixed glial cultures were prepared from cortices of P1 to P3 C57BL/6J mouse pups. The brains were dissected in ice-cold Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and the cortices were separated from other tissues with meninges removed completely. Isolated cortices were dissociated with trypsin and DNase in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min. The enzyme reaction was stopped by adding fetal bovine serum (FBS)-containing media to the dissociated tissue. The contents were centrifuged, and the cell pellet was resuspended and plated onto T75 flasks with DMEM containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 1.25 mM L-glutamine. After 7 days, the flask was shaken in an orbital shaker at 37 °C at 500 RPM for 3 to 4 h to remove most microglia and oligodendrocyte precursor cells. The astrocytes were detached using trypsin and seeded onto 6-well plates for qPCR or 0.1 mg/ml poly-D-lysine coated glass chambers for imaging. To deplete remaining microglia in designated experimental groups, 5 µM PLX3397 in DMSO (Cat. # HY-16749, MedChem Express) was added at the time of cell seeding and replenished with each media change. For 7-KC treatments, 10 µM 7-KC (Cayman Chemicals, Cat # 16339) or cholesterol (Cayman Chemicals, Cat # 9003100) dissolved in ethanol prepared as a 1000x stock was added to the culture media. The final concentration of ethanol in the media was around 0.1%. 24 h after the treatment, the cells were harvested for RNA extraction.

In vitro imaging of astrocytes

Astrocytes were transduced with the AAV- HyPerGRT and allowed one week for viral expression. The transduced cells were treated with 10 µM 7-KC or cholesterol for 24 h. The cells were then washed three times with PBS containing 20mM N-ethylmaleimide and blocked with 4% paraformaldehyde containing 20mM N-ethylmaleimide followed by three PBS washes. The cells were then mounted using Invitrogen’s Prolong Gold mounting media. The images were taken using a Keyence slide scanner with standard wavelengths 488 nm for green and 561 nm for red channels within one week of fixation. The green and red intensities of individual cells were measured using Fiji / ImageJ and the green to red ratio was calculated. As many as 100 cells from several fields taken from 3 to 4 different cultures for each condition were used for quantification.

SIM-A9 microglia cells

SIM-A9 is a microglia-derived cell line51. SIM-A9 cells were cultured in DMEM-F12 containing 5% horse serum, 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells require priming with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as previously described9. SIM-A9 cells were primed with 0.5 µg/ml LPS for 16 h prior to experiments.

C6 rat glioma cells

C6 cells are widely used in vitro model for astrocyte studies. C6 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. C6 cells were transfected with HyPerGRT plasmid and 72 h post transfection, they were treated with 10 µM hydrogen peroxide followed by fixation with 4%PFA containing 20 mM NEM or live cell imaging. In case of fixation, the C6 cells were incubated with hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. For live cell imaging, the culture media was washed off with live cell imaging buffer three times and live imaging was performed with 10 µM of hydrogen peroxide.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) followed by cDNA synthesis using an iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad). qPCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad). The primer sequences for qPCR are listed in Table 1. The mRNA expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene TATA binding protein. The relative gene expression was calculated using 2(−delta Cq).

Plasmid construction and viral packaging

The construction of pAAV-hSyn-HyPerGRT was described previously21. To use it for AAV packaging and achieving astrocyte-specific expression, a truncated version of the glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) promoter (GfaABC1D) was amplified from pTYF-1xGfaABC1D-EGFP (Addgene plasmid #19974)32 and used to replace the hSyn promoter in pAAV-hSyn-HyPerGRT, resulting in a new plasmid which we named pAAV-GFAP-HyPerGRT. This plasmid was combined with pDeltaF6 (Addgene plasmid #112867) and pAAV2/5 (Addgene plasmid #104964) for viral packaging and purification, following a described protocol52. The viral titers were assessed using a qPCR method provided by Addgene. The AAV titers employed in this study were approximately 2 × 1012 genome counts (GC) per mL.

Hippocampal injection

The AAV virus was administered to mice via stereotaxic injection. Briefly, animals were anesthetized using isoflurane in an induction chamber, before being transferred to a Stoelting stereotaxic apparatus where there was a continuous flow of isoflurane. 3 mg/kg of ketoprofen was given subcutaneously. The surgical site was shaved, disinfected with betadine and ethanol, and then infiltrated with bupivacaine (2.4 mg/kg). Craniotomies were done using a dental drill at the stereotaxic coordinates appropriate for the hippocampus; AP, − 1.7 mm; ML, ± 1.2 mm; DV, -1.5 mm relative to bregma53. 1 µl of the virus was injected at a rate of 0.1 µL/min. After withdrawing the needle, the skin was sealed with tissue adhesive. Injected animals were monitored and given ketoprofen for five subsequent days for pain relief. The animals were allowed to express the virus for 3 weeks before collecting the brains. In experiments with 7-KC injections, 1 µl of 5 µM 7-KC or cholesterol or 1 µl of methyl-beta-cyclodextrin (MBCD) was injected as described above. In microglia depletion experiments, the mice were injected with 1 µl AAV HyPerGRT virus (~ 1013 vg/ml), and then 10 days after the injections, the mice were fed either PLX3397 diet or chow. 10 days after the diet change, the animals were euthanized for brain collection.

Brain slice imaging and quantification

Brains of injected mice were collected for either acute or frozen sectioning. In the case of frozen sections, the animals were perfused with ice-cold PBS and 4% PFA both containing 20mM N-ethylmaleimide. The brains were isolated and fixed for an hour in 4% PFA containing 20mM N-ethylmaleimide and frozen. The frozen brains were embedded in OCT and sectioned for imaging using a Keyence slide scanner with standard wavelengths 488 nm for green and 561 nm for red channels. Astrocytes in the hippocampus region were imaged and analyzed.

Imaging of prepared acute brain slices was done on a Scientifica SliceScope Pro 1000 equipped with a Photometrics Prime 95B scientific CMOS camera, a CoolLED Enhanced pE-300white LED light source, and a 40× (NA 0.8) water immersion objective lens. The green and red channels were imaged using a FITC filter cube (470/40 nm excitation and 525/50 nm emission) and a TRITC filter cube (545/25 nm excitation and 605/70 nm emission). Astrocytes in the hippocampus region were imaged and analyzed.

To process the ratios, individual cells were circled as regions of interest (ROIs) in ImageJ, and their intensities were measured in both channels. The green/red intensity ratio was then computed. Approximately 100 cells across several acute brain slices for each condition were analyzed.

Immunostaining

30 μm thick sections of HyPerGRT injected mouse brains frozen in OCT were stained using standard immunostaining protocol. Primary antibody, IBA1 (Wako # 019-19741) was used in a dilution of 1:1000 to show depletion of microglia and GFAP (Millipore 345860) staining was done with a dilution of 1:1000 to show astrocyte specific expression of HyPerGRT virus.

Statistical quantification

For qPCR and in vitro fluorescent ratiometric quantification experiments, a two-tailed unpaired t-test was performed to measure statistical significance. For ROS measurements from acute brain slices in vivo, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was performed for statistics. For mass spectrometry, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc analysis was performed to measure significance. Error bars represent the standard error of the mean.
