Safety and Efficacy of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 in Malignant Solid Tumors: Phase 1/2a Results and Translational Insights Into Observed Hepatotoxicity

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHexaBody‐DR5/DR5 (GEN1029) is a death receptor 5 (DR5) agonist composed of two humanized immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) antibodies, Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05, that bind non‐overlapping DR5 epitopes with nanomolar affinity. HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 is designed to activate the TRAIL pathway through DR5 clustering. This phase 1/2a, open‐label, multi‐center study aimed to determine the safety, pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics, and preliminary antitumor activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 in patients with advanced solid tumors. In the study, 46 patients received biweekly intravenous injections of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 at doses ranging from 0.1 to 3.0 mg/kg, including patients with colorectal, gastric, non‐small‐cell lung, pancreatic, and triple‐negative breast cancer. Safety evaluations revealed that 76.1% of patients experienced at least one grade (G) ≥ 3 treatment‐emergent adverse event (TEAE), and 45.7% had drug‐related G ≥ 3 events. The most frequent G ≥ 3 drug‐related TEAEs were elevations of liver transaminases and diarrhea. According to RECIST criteria, 32.6% of patients showed stable disease. No patients achieved a partial or complete response. Exploratory assessments of cleaved cytokeratin 18 (cCK18) plasma levels indicated apoptotic pathway activation upon treatment. Reductions in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) levels and tumor metabolic activity according to PERCIST criteria suggested tumor‐targeted biological activity. Nonclinical studies in vitro using human liver models demonstrated that HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 directly inhibits viability of hepatocytes, but not Kupffer cells. The non‐clinical liver toxicity was driven by dual epitope binding and hexamerization features, independent of FcγR‐mediated crosslinking. Overall, these findings suggest that the TRAIL pathway has potential as a target for cancer therapy, provided tumor‐specificity is sufficiently high to achieve an acceptable therapeutic index.

Introduction

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 (GEN1029) is a death receptor 5 (DR5) agonist consisting of a mixture of two non‐competing humanized immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) DR5 antibodies with an E430G hexamerization‐enhancing mutation (Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05). Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05 recognize non‐overlapping epitopes and can simultaneously bind DR5 with nanomolar affinity. The E430G Fc mutation enhances hexamer formation upon binding to DR5 on the cell surface. DR5, like DR4, is a member of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily. Crosslinking of DR5 activates the TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand (TRAIL) pathway, which is of interest for cancer therapy due to its preference for killing malignant cells [1, 2]. Based on this, multiple monoclonal antibodies targeting DR4 and DR5 have been explored in the clinic. These first‐generation DR targeting antibodies showed limited antitumor efficacy in clinical trials, most likely because conventional IgG1 molecules depend on secondary crosslinking by FcγR‐bearing cells to drive sufficient DR‐mediated apoptosis and a low frequency of FcγR‐bearing cells in the tumor environment may thus limit biological activity in the tumor [3, 4, 5]. Researchers have since explored alternative methods to elicit DR aggregation and clustering independent of FcγR binding, including the development of bispecific and/or multivalent antibody variants [4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Despite these efforts, limited clinical success has been achieved due to lack of clinical activity, occurrence of adverse events or both [9, 13, 14].

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5's dual epitope targeting together with enhanced IgG1 hexamer formation upon binding to DR5 on the cell surface induces highly potent DR5 agonist activity [15]. Preclinical studies demonstrated that complement component C1 acts as a structural component in the formation or stabilization of antigen‐bound hexameric antibody ring structures on the cell surface. The formation of the C1q‐antibody‐DR5 complex has been shown to activate DR5 outside‐in signaling and caspase‐dependent apoptosis in various cancer cell lines in vitro, independent of FcγR‐mediated secondary crosslinking. In vivo, HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 has shown potent antitumor activity in a range of patient‐derived xenograft models representing various solid cancers including colorectal, gastric, and urothelial cancer.

Here, we report the first‐in‐human (FIH), open‐label, phase 1/2a study, which aimed to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD), recommended Phase 2 dose (RP2D), safety, PK, pharmacodynamics, and preliminary antitumor activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 as monotherapy in advanced solid tumors (NCT03576131). Clinical safety and efficacy findings for patients treated with biweekly intravenous (IV) injections of 0.1 (n = 10), 0.2 (n = 7), 0.3 (n = 4), 1.0 (n = 11), 2.0 (n = 7), or 3.0 mg/kg (n = 7) HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 are presented, supported by exploratory pharmacodynamic biomarker analyses including ctDNA and cCK18 data. Additionally, we used 2D and 3D human liver models to elucidate the mechanisms underlying HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced liver toxicity.

Methods

Clinical

Overview

This open‐label, multicenter FIH trial in patients with malignant solid tumors (NCT03576131) was sponsored by Genmab. It was conducted between 30 April 2018 and 12 October 2021 at different study sites in the UK, Spain, and the US, in compliance with the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) E6 (R2) guideline for Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and applicable local regulations, and in concordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol and amendments were approved by an independent ethics committee or institutional review board at each participating site (Table S1).

Patients

Patients aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with advanced cancer having measurable disease according to Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, for whom no standard therapies are available were eligible. Key inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Supporting Information. All patients provided written informed consent.

Dose Regimen

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 was administered by IV infusions on Cycle 1 Day 1 (C1D1) of a 14‐day cycle regimen (Q2W). A modified Bayesian optimal interval (mBOIN) design was used to make dose recommendations for the next cohort. The planned full starting dose of 0.3 mg/kg was considered to be safe and at the lower end of the potential pharmacologically active dose range, based on considerations from nonclinical pharmacology, PK, and toxicology studies. The clinical trial enrolled 48 patients, and results from 46 subjects analyzed in the biweekly regimen across six ascending dose cohorts are presented here (Table S2).

Study Assessments

The primary objectives of this phase 1/2a, FIH, open‐label, multi‐center study were to determine the MTD/RP2D of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 and the safety profile of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 in patients with advanced solid cancer. The secondary objectives were to establish the PK profile and immunogenicity after single and multiple infusions and to evaluate the antitumor activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5. In addition, exploratory objectives included the assessment of pharmacodynamic parameters in association with PK, safety, and efficacy.

Safety Evaluations

Routine safety evaluations were performed by the investigator and AE severity was assessed via the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Event NCI‐CTCAE v4.03. The dose‐limiting toxicity (DLT) assessment period was defined as beginning on the first day of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 dosing and continuing to 28 days after the first dose. AE analyses were based on TEAEs, that is, those AE with onset on/after the day of first dose of study drug and no more than 70 days after the last dose of study drug. Further details on DLT criteria are presented in the Supporting Information.

PK and Immunogenicity

Blood samples for assessment of the PK profiles of Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05 were collected at screening, before infusion and 15 min, 2 and 4 h after infusion on Day 1 of Cycles 1–3, on Day 2 and Day 8 of Cycles 1–3, and before and after infusion on Day 1 of Cycle 4. For a subset of patients, a sample was taken on Day 3 of Cycles 1 and 2. Plasma concentrations were established using validated electrochemiluminescent assays (ECLIA) (ICON, The Netherlands).

Blood samples for assessment of anti‐drug antibodies (ADA) against Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05 were collected at screening, on Day 1 of Cycles 1–4 and at the end of treatment. Plasma concentrations were measured using validated Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05 specific titer assays (ICON, The Netherlands).

Antitumor Activity

Antitumor activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 was evaluated based on RECIST v1.1 criteria [16]. Computed tomography (CT) scans were performed at baseline (within 21 days prior to C1D1), every 6 weeks (±7 days) until Week 50, and every 12 weeks (±7 days) thereafter. Tumor response was evaluated through best overall response (BOR) rate based on confirmed SD, PR, or CR, and through objective response rate (ORR) based on confirmed complete response (CR) or partial response (PR).

Exploratory Assessments

Tumor Metabolic Activity

Tumor metabolic activity was assessed using positron emission tomography (PET) response criteria (PERCIST) v1.0 [17, 18]. PET‐CT scans were taken at baseline and, if this scan showed avidity, at C2D2, and Week 12 (±7 days). Metabolic activity was presented as standardized uptake volume corrected for body lean mass (SUL). Metabolic response was classified as complete metabolic response (CMR), partial metabolic response (PMR), stable metabolic disease (SMD), and progressive metabolic disease (PMD).

Circulating Tumor DNA (ctDNA)

Blood samples for ctDNA levels from a subset of 19 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients were collected before infusion of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 on Day 1 of Cycle 1, on Day 8 of Cycles 1, 3 and 6, and at the end of treatment. The level of ctDNA was determined using the Tempus xF liquid biopsy assay, which detects single nucleotide variants, insertions/deletions, copy number variants, and chromosomal rearrangement in a targeted panel of 105 genes by next‐generation sequencing (NGS) [19]. Data were presented as mean Variant Allele Frequency (VAF; %), the arithmetic mean of the VAF of all somatic mutations detected in the specific sample, and as the ratio of mean VAF (rVAF), representing the mean VAF on treatment (both baseline‐specific mutations and new mutations) divided by the mean VAF at baseline [20]. Patients with an rVAF < 1 were considered to show biological activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 on the tumor.

Cleaved Cytokeratin 18 (cCK18)

Blood samples for cCK18 analysis were collected from 35 patients before and 4 h after infusion of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 on Day 1 of Cycles 1–3, on Day 2 and Day 8 of Cycles 1–3, and on Day 3 of Cycles 1 and 2. The level of cCK18 in plasma was quantified using the cCK18 (M30) Apoptosense sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (DiaPharma, Prod. No. P10011). Experimental details are provided in the Supporting Information. The amount of cCK18 in the samples (U/L) was presented as maximum fold change compared to baseline (maxFC) and as fold change (FC) on C1D2.

Statistical Analysis

Data Analysis Sets

The full analysis set (FAS), used for safety and efficacy evaluations, consisted of all patients who received at least one dose of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5. PK was analyzed in all patients who had at least one dose of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 and who had at least one post‐dose PK measurement. Immunogenicity was analyzed in the patients in the FAS who had at least one immunogenicity measurement taken. Within the FAS, tumor metabolic activity as determined by PERCIST could only be determined in patients that had an avid PET scan at baseline. Similarly, data analysis sets for ctDNA and cCK18 levels were based on the availability of evaluable patient samples for these biomarkers.

Statistical Methods

ORR and BOR (based on RECIST v1.1 criteria) were summarized for each treatment arm with two‐sided 95% exact confidence intervals (CIs) calculated using the Clopper‐Pearson method in SAS (version 9.4).

Pharmacodynamic biomarker analyses were conducted using the R programming environment (version 4.5.2). Changes in ctDNA mean rVAF between paired samples (baseline versus on treatment) were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Comparisons between two unpaired groups, such as cCK18 FC by response status or transaminase elevation group, were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Data cleaning and visualization were performed using tidyverse (version 2.0) and ggplot2 (version 4.0) packages.

Immunogenicity, PK and tumor metabolic activity data were summarized using descriptive statistics and visualized in R.

Nonclinical

Effect of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 on Monocultures of Human Primary Hepatocytes and Kupffer Cells

The effect of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 on viability of human primary liver cells was investigated in 2D monocultures of healthy Kupffer cells or hepatocytes, and in 3D hepatocyte spheroids. Cells were incubated with a concentration range of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 or non‐binding control antibody IgG1‐b12, supplemented with purified human C1q (2.5 μg/mL final concentration). Cell viability was determined by quantifying the level of ATP in the cells, as described in the Supporting Information.

Effect of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 on Stressed Cocultures of Human Primary Hepatocytes, Kupffer Cells and Endothelial Cells

The effect of inflammatory stress conditions on sensitivity to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced liver toxicity was investigated by pre‐incubating 3D cocultures consisting of ~60% pooled healthy human donor primary hepatocytes, ~20% Kupffer cells, and ~20% endothelial cells with LPS for 24 h before incubation with a concentration range of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 or DR5‐specific control antibody IgG1‐cona for 48 h. Cell viability, cytotoxicity, and inflammation in these cocultures was determined by quantifying ATP in the cells and LDH and cytokine levels in the supernatant, respectively, as described in the Supporting Information.

Mechanism of Action Underlying HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐Mediated Reduction in Hepatocyte Viability

The contribution of the dual‐epitope binding of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 to the observed hepatotoxicity was studied in the 3D coculture of human primary hepatocytes, Kupffer cells and endothelial cells. The coculture was incubated with a concentration range of the HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 antibodies or with the single DR5‐targeting antibodies Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05. In addition, to assess the involvement of Fc‐mediated effector functions of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5, 3D cocultures were incubated with three variants of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5. In these variants, the Fc‐domain of the antibody mixture components, Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05, was modified as follows: WT‐DR5‐01/05: The E430G mutation, responsible for hexamerization enhancement, was removed to abrogate this function.Hx‐DR5‐01/05‐G237A: The G237A mutation was added, resulting in a lack of FcγR binding while retaining enhanced hexamerization and C1q binding.Hx‐DR5‐01/05‐P329R: The P329R mutation was added, leading to a lack of both FcγR and C1q binding while preserving enhanced hexamerization.

Results

Clinical

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

Between 2018 and 2021, 48 patients with solid tumors were enrolled in the phase 1 dose escalation part of the FIH study. The present report describes outcomes for 46 subjects who were allocated or reallocated to the biweekly HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 dosing regimen, including patients with CRC (n = 35), gastric cancer (n = 3), non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC; n = 2), pancreatic cancer (n = 3), urothelial cancer (n = 1), and triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC; n = 2). Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table S2.

Exposure

Dose escalation was initiated at a dose level of 0.3 mg/kg and escalated to a maximum dose level of 3.0 mg/kg, administered in a Q2W regimen. Thereafter, lower Q2W dose levels were explored due to DLTs indicative of liver toxicity (Figure S1). All patients in the FAS received one or more HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 doses of 0.1 mg/kg (n = 10), 0.2 mg/kg (n = 7), 0.3 mg/kg (n = 4), 1.0 mg/kg (n = 11), 2.0 mg/kg (n = 7) or 3.0 mg/kg (n = 7). The median duration of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 exposure was 41.5 days (range: 7–408 days), out of a median study follow‐up of 98 days (range: 19–565 days) in the trial. Reasons for HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 discontinuation were disease progression (n = 32; 69.6%), AEs (n = 11; 23.9%), withdrawal by patient (n = 1; 2.2%), or other (n = 2; 4.3%).

Pharmacokinetics and Immunogenicity

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 exposure peaked at or shortly after the end of infusion followed by a two‐phase decline (Figure S2). No relevant differences were observed between the PK profile of clones Hx‐DR5‐01 and Hx‐DR5‐05. Both clones showed a fast clearance with a geometric mean of 77.75 mL/h (n = 44) for Hx‐DR5‐01 (CV% 55.3%) and 88.52 mL/h (n = 44) for Hx‐DR5‐05 (CV% 54.1%) in Cycle 1, without an apparent association with dose (Table S3). The maximum concentration (C max) and the area under the concentration‐time curve (AUC0‐inf) increased approximately proportional to dose, suggesting no major impact of target‐mediated drug disposition on PK in the dose range evaluated. No accumulation of exposure was seen upon repeated administration.

Of the 46 patients, 44 (95.7%) were ADA‐negative at baseline, and two patients (4.3%) had a missing ADA status at baseline. On treatment, 26 patients (56.5%) were ADA‐positive, 13 patients (28.3%) remained ADA‐negative, and seven patients (15.2%) had a missing ADA status. The impact of ADA on PK varied between patients from no clear impact, impact on one of the clones only (Hx‐DR5‐05 more commonly than Hx‐DR5‐01), to an accelerated clearance of both clones.

Efficacy

According to RECIST criteria, the BOR was stable disease (SD) in 15 of 46 (32.6%) patients; progressive disease (PD) in 20 (43.5%) patients; and 11 (23.9%) patients were not evaluable (NE). None of the patients achieved partial or complete response (Table 1). The disease control rate based on a BOR of CR, PR, or SD among all dose levels was 15 (32.6%) patients (95% CI: 19.5%; 48.0%).

Safety

Out of 46 patients, 43 were DLT‐evaluable, of which 10 experienced DLTs. The DLTs reported were: drug‐induced liver injury (DILI), ALT increased, AST increased, diarrhea, and enteritis. The MTD was not formally established.

A total of 35 (76.1%) patients experienced at least one G ≥ 3 treatment‐emergent adverse event (TEAE) (Table S4). HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐related TEAEs G ≥ 3 occurred in 21 (45.7%) patients. Serious TEAEs were reported in 27 (58.7%) patients, while 17 (37.0%) patients had serious TEAEs that were deemed HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐related. No trial‐drug‐related fatal TEAEs were reported.

The most frequent HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐related G ≥ 3 TEAEs were elevations of transaminases (87 events in 27 patients) and diarrhea‐presenting events (25 events in 16 patients). TEAEs leading to drug discontinuation were reported in 11 (23.9%) of the patients. The most common TEAEs leading to discontinuation were increased ALT (four patients, 8.7%), increased AST (four patients, 8.7%), and diarrhea (three patients, 6.5%). TEAEs leading to dose reduction were reported in 9 (19.6%) patients, with the most frequent TEAEs being increased ALT (five patients, 10.9%), increased AST (three patients, 6.5%), and diarrhea (two patients, 4.3%). All DLTs were also reported within these two categories.

Transaminase elevations were noted at all dose levels without an obvious dose‐related threshold. Out of the 87 events of transaminase elevation, 79 (90.8%) resolved within follow‐up time. The median time to onset of the first occurrence was 2 days from initial dosing (range: 2–91 days), and the median time to event resolution was 9 days (range: 2–67 days). Patients who continued trial drug after elevation of transaminases either did not experience reoccurrence of this event, or had reoccurrence of less severity. A dose‐dependency was observed in diarrhea‐presenting events. Out of 25 diarrhea‐presenting events, 22 (88.0%) resolved with a median time to onset of the first occurrence of these events of 14.5 days (range: 4–55 days), and a median time to event resolution of 5 days (range: 2–71 days). Recurrence of diarrhea‐presenting events after continuation of trial drug treatment occurred in two cases. TEAEs related to elevation of transaminases and diarrhea are presented by grade in Table S5.

Comparison of ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative patients showed no apparent relationship between incidence of ADA and the occurrence of TEAEs (Table 2).

ctDNA

ctDNA was analyzed to explore biological activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 in a more homogenous subpopulation of 18 evaluable CRC patients. In this subpopulation, ctDNA levels reduced significantly in patients showing SD according to RECIST (n = 10 across all dose levels), as presented by decreased mean VAF (%) in the on‐treatment samples (C3D8) compared with the respective baseline samples (C1D1). A significant reduction in ctDNA level was not observed in CRC patients showing PD (n = 8) (Figure 2A). Among patients categorized as SD and PD, a proportion demonstrated a rVAF < 1; 70% in the SD group (7/10) and 50% in the PD group (4/8) (Figure 2B). Overall, these findings are indicative of biological activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 across the tested dose levels.

cCK18

cCK18 levels increased over time for the majority of the 35 analyzed patients dosed at 0.2–3.0 mg/kg HexaBody‐DR5/DR5, with a maxFC around C1D2 compared with C1D1 predose (Figure S3). The maxFC between the different HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 doses showed no significant dose‐dependent relationship. However, at HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 dose levels ≥ 0.2 mg/kg the proportion of patients showing cCK18 elevations was higher compared with the group treated with 0.1 mg/kg (Figure 3A), supporting the mechanism of action and biological activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5. The maxFC cCK18 was significantly higher in patients with G ≥ 2 transaminase elevations compared with patients with no/Grade 1 transaminase elevations (Figure 3B).

Nonclinical Evaluation of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐Induced Hepatotoxicity

In response to reported safety events, the sensitivity of liver cells to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced cytotoxicity and the contribution of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5's dual epitope binding and binding of C1q and/or FcγRs to the antibody Fc‐domains were investigated using in vitro liver models consisting of 2D and 3D human primary liver cell mono‐ and cocultures.

Hepatotoxicity in Human Primary Liver Cell Monocultures

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 induced dose‐dependent reduction of viability of primary human hepatocytes but had no effect on primary Kupffer cells (Figure 4A,B). 2D‐cultured hepatocytes were more sensitive to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced inhibition of viability than 3D‐cultured primary hepatocytes, as reflected by a lower average IC50 (IC50 ± standard deviation; 2D culture 0.109 μg/mL±0.024; 3D culture 1.269 μg/mL±0.456) and higher maximal percentage kill in the 2D hepatocyte monocultures (Max kill ± standard deviation; 2D culture 48.1% ± 5.3; 3D culture 39.5%±7.8) (Figure 4B,C).

The percentage viable cells was calculated relative to no antibody control samples. Hepatotoxicity in Human 3D Primary Liver Cell Cocultures Under Stress Conditions

The sensitivity of human liver cells for HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced cytotoxicity under stress conditions was explored to emulate the liver condition of patients who have received prior treatments. The conditions were simulated using a 3D coculture model with primary human hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and Kupffer cells as an immune component that can be stimulated by LPS to induce inflammatory stress [21]. The LPS‐induced stress condition of the cocultures was confirmed by an increased release of cytokines IL‐8, IL‐6, and TNF‐α (Figure S4) and was associated with approximately 25% decreased cell viability (Figure 4D). The sensitivity to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced hepatotoxicity under LPS‐induced stress conditions was increased compared with conditions without LPS, as shown by decreased cell viability and increased LDH in the supernatant (Figure 4E,F).

Role of Epitope‐Binding, Hexamerization, and Fc Binding of C1q and FcγR

The contribution of the intrinsic characteristics of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 to hepatotoxicity was investigated in vitro in the 3D liver cell coculture model. Hepatotoxicity was only observed when targeting the two different DR5 epitopes with HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 and not when only targeting one of the epitopes using the single antibodies (Figure 5A). In addition, HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced hepatotoxicity was completely abolished by removing the hexamerization‐enhancing mutation in the Fc domain (Figure 5B). With FcγR‐expressing cells present in the 3D coculture liver model, it was shown that FcγR‐mediated crosslinking did not contribute to hepatotoxicity by using the HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐based variant with an additional G327A mutation that inhibits FcγR binding (Figure 5C). In contrast, hepatotoxicity was completely abolished when the P329R mutation was introduced to inhibit both FcγR binding and C1q binding, indicating that HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced hepatotoxicity depends on C1q binding in this in vitro coculture model. This observation was further supported by the loss of hepatotoxicity when the cocultures were incubated with HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 in absence of purified C1q (data not shown).

Discussion

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 activates the TRAIL pathway by DR5 hyperclustering through dual epitope targeting combined with enhanced IgG1 hexamer formation upon binding to DR5 on the cell surface. This interaction results in caspase‐mediated death of malignant cells, as shown in preclinical studies in vitro and in vivo [15].

In this open‐label, multi‐center safety clinical trial of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5, patients with malignant solid tumors did not respond to treatment with HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 according to RECIST criteria at dose levels up to 3 mg/kg. The safety profile of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 revealed hepatotoxicity‐related TEAEs that were not dose‐dependent, resulting in a narrow therapeutic window and ultimately leading to the discontinuation of the clinical trial.

To explore whether HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 exhibited biological activity, we analyzed a subset of patients by assessing metabolic changes in their tumors using PERCIST criteria [18]. PERCIST allows for the detection of functional changes in tumors, which may occur before any changes in size, potentially offering an earlier indication of treatment response. In a subset of 18 patients for whom a PET scan was performed at screening and on treatment, decreased tumor metabolic activity was observed in 10 patients, suggesting the biological activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5.

Additionally, tumor‐specific and non‐tumor‐specific pharmacodynamic biomarkers were analyzed to confirm the biological activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5. HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 treatment did not modulate peripheral C1q and C2 levels, confirming that treatment did not result in complement consumption (data not shown). ctDNA levels, a tumor‐specific pharmacodynamic biomarker [22], remained stable or decreased in a subset of CRC patients treated with HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 whose clinical outcomes were classified as SD according to RECIST. This further suggests that the biological activity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 may have contributed to tumor control in this population. cCK18 levels, a non‐tumor‐specific indicator of DR5‐induced outside‐in signaling and caspase‐mediated apoptosis [23] increased in the plasma of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐treated patients compared with baseline samples. These data confirm the hypothesized main mechanism of action of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 to be DR5 activation and apoptosis induction rather than complement activation.

Since cCK18 is not a tumor‐specific biomarker, the changes observed may reflect apoptosis of tumor cells and/or apoptosis in other DR5‐expressing tissues, including the liver. The observed association between cCK18 and transaminase elevations (liver enzymes indicative of liver toxicity) suggested that increased cCK18 levels were at least partially derived from HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced apoptosis in liver tissue.

Significant hepatotoxicity was also observed in other clinical trials investigating DR5‐targeting compounds. For example, G3 or G4 elevations in liver enzyme levels were reported in participants of Phase 1 trials on the DR5‐targeting monoclonal antibodies lexatumumab [24] and drozitumab [25]. In addition, a Phase 1 clinical trial investigating a tetravalent compound targeting DR5 receptors (TAS266) in participants with advanced solid tumors was terminated early due to unexpected significant hepatotoxicity [9]. The underlying mechanism was not fully understood, but was suggested to involve the compound's high potency, immunogenicity and/or increased DR5 expression on hepatocytes leading to enhanced DR5 clustering and hepatocyte apoptosis [26]. For HexaBody‐DR5/DR5, no apparent relationship was observed between ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative participants and the occurrence of TEAEs, suggesting the observed hepatotoxicity was not related to immunogenicity to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5.

HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐dependent hepatotoxicity was not observed in nonclinical toxicology studies with HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 in cynomolgus monkeys (unpublished study). The affinity of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 to human and cynomolgus monkey DR5 was comparable and HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 was well tolerated up to the highest dose tested in vivo (50 mg/kg by IV infusion once weekly for 5 weeks). Ex vivo, HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 induced a dose‐dependent reduction in viability of primary human 2D‐cultured hepatocytes, whereas no effect was observed in primary 2D‐cultured cynomolgus monkey hepatocytes, both conditions in the presence of human C1q. These findings suggest differences in sensitivity to caspase‐mediated apoptosis between human and cynomolgus monkey hepatocytes. The HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 starting dose for the clinical trial was set to 0.3 mg/kg, which was in the lower end of the predicted pharmacologically active dose range determined in nonclinical pharmacology studies in vivo [15], and much lower than a starting dose based on the no‐observed‐adverse‐effect‐level (NOAEL) observed in the pivotal toxicology study in cynomolgus monkeys, in which no toxicity occurred even at the highest tested dose level of 50 mg/kg. In addition, clinical evaluation of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 was advanced with close monitoring for potential hepatotoxicity.

To understand the factors contributing to the HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐mediated hepatotoxicity observed in the clinic, we conducted additional nonclinical studies in vitro using different human primary liver cell models. HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 decreased cell viability in a 2D monoculture of primary hepatocytes, whereas no cytotoxicity was observed in Kupffer cells, suggesting that the observed hepatotoxicity is due to a direct effect on the hepatocytes. The IC50 for HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced decrease in cell viability was in the same range for hepatocytes and tumor cells [15], indicating a narrow therapeutic window. In a 3D coculture of ~60% primary human hepatocytes and ~40% non‐parenchymal liver cells, sensitivity to HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced cytotoxicity increased under in vitro conditions simulating stress in the liver micro‐environment. These findings suggest that HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced liver cytotoxicity may be enhanced under conditions in which stress is induced in the liver micro‐environment, for example in patients who have received prior treatments. Furthermore, the presented work shows the translational value of the ex vivo 3D spheroid models with primary hepatocyte to evaluate liver safety liabilities for future death receptor programs and other therapeutic candidates with similar risk profiles.

DR5 clustering at the cell membrane is a key determinant to induce efficient DR5 outside‐in signal transduction and apoptosis [27, 28]. Various anti‐DR5 IgG antibodies depend on extrinsic secondary antibody crosslinking via FcγR‐expressing cells for optimal DR5 clustering [29, 30]. In contrast, HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 induces clustering of DR5 and subsequent apoptosis through dual epitope targeting, enhanced IgG hexamerization and C1q binding. To determine HexaBody‐DR5/DR5's features driving the observed hepatotoxicity, we compared different variants of the antibodies in 3D liver cocultures. We demonstrated that HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐induced cytotoxicity was driven in part by dual‐epitope targeting. Moreover, our studies demonstrated that the hexamerization‐enhancing mutations in the Fc domains of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 were responsible for decreased viability in the 3D liver cocultures when compared to a variant without the hexamerization‐enhancing mutation. Using specific loss‐of‐binding Fc mutations we also demonstrated that HexaBody‐DR5/DR5‐mediated DR5 agonism in liver cultures occurred independent of FcγR binding but was dependent on the presence of C1q, which is hypothesized to stabilize the hexameric ring structures on the cell surface of the hepatocytes rather than inducing CDC, similar to the observations on tumor cells with endogenous DR5 expression [15]. Together, these nonclinical mechanistic studies revealed that the effect of HexaBody‐DR5/DR5 on liver cells was mediated by the same mechanism of action as previously shown to enhance agonist activity of the DR5 antibodies in tumor cells [15].

In conclusion, despite the lack of an objective response rate at the tested dose levels up to 3 mg/kg HexaBody‐DR5/DR5, the observed clinical tumor‐specific biological activity confirms that the TRAIL pathway has potential as a target for cancer therapy, provided that the balance between antitumor activity and liver toxicity can be managed to achieve an acceptable therapeutic index.
