Three-dimensional evaluation of lip changes before and after bracket debonding in patients with different vertical skeletal patterns
Authors: Yongjie Fan, Qiang Wang, Xin Lv, Le An, Junting Wei, Bingle Wang, Gebing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe objective of this study was to quantitatively evaluate changes in lip morphology before and after bracket debonding in patients with different vertical skeletal patterns using 3D structured light scanning.Methods285 patients (110 males, 175 females) who completed orthodontic treatment and debonding at an orthodontic clinic in China between June 2024 and September 2025 were classified into hypodivergent (FH-MP ≤ 22°), normodivergent (22°< FH-MP < 32°), and hyperdivergent (FH-MP ≥ 32°) groups based on the mandibular plane angle (FH-MP). Three-dimensional facial scans were obtained before (T0) and after (T1) debonding, and 17 lip-related parameters were measured. Linear and angular changes were compared within and between groups.ResultsWithin-group analysis showed that all skeletal pattern groups exhibited significant changes in the nasolabial angle, the mentolabial angle, the upper and lower lip prominence, the upper and lower lips to the E-line, and the commissure depth after bracket debonding. The upper lip position relative to the true vertical line changed significantly in the normodivergent and hyperdivergent groups, whereas the lower lip position relative to the true vertical line changed significantly only in the hyperdivergent group (q < 0.05, FDR-adjusted p value). No other parameters showed significant within-group changes (q > 0.05). Intergroup comparisons revealed statistically significant differences in the magnitude of change for the nasolabial angle, the mentolabial angle, the upper and lower lip prominence, the upper and lower lips to the E-line, the lower lip position relative to the true vertical line, and the commissure depth (q < 0.05). In particular, between the hyperdivergent and hypodivergent groups, the nasolabial angle was approximately 1.67° and 0.90°, respectively, while the mentolabial angle was approximately 1.78° and 0.94°, respectively. No significant differences were observed for the remaining parameters (q > 0.05).ConclusionsLip changes following bracket debonding varied according to vertical skeletal pattern. Frontal analysis showed stable morphology, while lateral analysis revealed lip retrusion, which was greatest in hyperdivergent patients. After bracket debonding, the lower lip had a numerically larger retrusion than the upper lip.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-09119-3.

Background

With the increasing emphasis on facial aesthetics, soft tissue changes following orthodontic treatment have attracted considerable attention [1–4]. Fixed labial brackets are a common concern among patients and may influence clinical treatment planning. Previous studies on soft tissue changes before and after bracket debonding have mainly focused on morphological and thickness alterations [5, 6], while overlooking potential contributing factors such as the vertical skeletal pattern [7–9]. Understanding how the vertical skeletal pattern influences soft tissue changes before and after bracket debonding is clinically valuable, facilitating more accurate patient counselling regarding expected soft tissue changes. It further assists clinicians in formulating more accurate diagnoses and treatment plans. With advances in three-dimensional (3D) imaging technology, structured light scanning systems now enable noninvasive, rapid, and high-resolution acquisition of facial soft tissue color and texture [2, 10, 11]. Previous studies have confirmed that this system is accurate and reliable for 3D facial reconstruction in orthodontic patients, with the highest reproducibility in the lower facial third [12]. In recent years, these systems have seen widespread application in evaluating soft tissue changes after orthodontic treatment.

Therefore, the present study employed a structured light scanning system to analyze soft tissue changes before and after bracket debonding, and to evaluate the effects of vertical skeletal pattern and sex, aiming to provide more reliable guidance for both patients and clinicians.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This retrospective cohort study included 285 patients (110 males, 175 females) who had completed orthodontic treatment and were scheduled for bracket debonding at Qingzhimei Dental Clinic, Inner Mongolia, China, between June 2024 and September 2025. Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software (Faul et al., 2007, 2009) with parameters set for a two-tailed test (α = 0.05), a statistical power of 0.80 (1-β), and a medium effect size. Sample sizes for different statistical tests were calculated, with the largest estimate (n = 178) selected as the minimum theoretical sample size. To ensure the robustness of multiple statistical analyses and to account for potential non-normal data distributions, the sample size was increased, yielding a final cohort of 285 patients aged 12–25 years (mean ± SD: 16.78 ± 2.60 years). Patients were categorized into hypodivergent, normodivergent, and hyperdivergent groups based on the mandibular plane angle, with baseline characteristics summarized in Table 1. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (Approval No. 2025-IRB-AF/CZ-01/40.0). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Completed orthodontic treatment with well-aligned dentition and stable occlusion. (2) Well-proportioned facial features without noticeable asymmetry, with normal lip morphology and function. (3) Complete three-dimensional facial images and lateral cephalometric data available. (4) BMI was assessed according to age-specific criteria: participants under 18 years used age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles, while those aged 18 years and older were classified using adult BMI standards (18.5–24 kg/m²). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). (5) Voluntary participation, with signed informed consent obtained. (6) Use of a standardized self-ligating bracket system (Damon Q; Ormco, USA), with an upper central incisor bracket thickness of 0.091 inches (≈ 2.31 mm) as specified by the manufacturer.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) History of maxillofacial trauma or surgery intervention. (2) Previous combined orthognathic-orthodontic treatment. (3) Lip dysfunction or marked facial asymmetry. (4) Pathologies affecting normal facial morphology (e.g., swelling). (5) Incomplete three-dimensional image data for measurement.

Because this study focused on immediate lip changes after bracket debonding rather than long-term growth, age and developmental stage were not considered exclusion criteria.

Scanner specifications

A DS-FSCAN+ structured light scanner (Sinlin 3D, China) was used to capture three-dimensional facial images before (T0) and immediately after (T1) bracket debonding. The system includes three depth cameras for capturing spatial information, a texture camera for acquiring color data, and a projector that enhances feature recognition by projecting laser lines. This configuration enables three-dimensional data acquisition in approximately 10 s. Detailed parameters of the scanner are presented in Table 2.

3D scanning procedure

Patients were seated in a backrest chair against a black backdrop under natural lighting. Any glasses were removed, and hair was secured with clips to fully expose the face and forehead. Patients were instructed to maintain a natural head position according to their own perception of balance. During scanning, they were asked to relax their lips and surrounding muscles, keep a neutral facial expression, look straight ahead, and lightly occlude their teeth to minimize motion artifacts. A trained operator held the scanner and activated its automatic brightness adjustment to optimize facial texture capture, preventing over- or underexposure. When the distance indicator signaled an optimal scanning range and the facial recognition frame turned green, the operator performed a smooth, continuous scan of the patient’s face. The resulting three-dimensional images were saved in STL format for subsequent analysis.

Cephalometric measurement and analysis

On the day of bracket debonding, all patients underwent standardized lateral cephalometric radiographs using the Rayscan α system (Samsung RIL, Korea), performed by the same radiologist. Patients were positioned in a natural head posture, looking straight ahead, with posterior teeth lightly occluded in centric relation and lips relaxed. The cephalograms were analyzed by the same operator using Dolphin Imaging software (version 11.95; Dolphin Imaging & Management Solutions, USA) at a standardized resolution. Patients were classified into three groups based on the angle between the mandibular plane (MP) and the Frankfort horizontal plane (FH): hypodivergent (FH-MP ≤ 22°, Fig. 1, A), normodivergent (22° < FH-MP < 32°, Fig. 1, B), and hyperdivergent (FH-MP ≥ 32°, Fig. 1, C).

Construction of the 3D coordinate system and lip measurements

The three-dimensional images were imported into FScan software (version 2.1.0.3; Sinlin 3D, China), carefully checked for data completeness, and any extraneous regions outside the facial area were manually removed. Subsequently, T0 and T1 images were registered in Geomagic Design X (version 2020.1.1; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC) following the method described by Qiao et al. [3]. Considering potential global pose differences introduced during handheld scanning, all registrations were performed within a unified 3D reference framework constructed from each patient’s anatomical structures. First, the lower face of the T0 model (lip, chin, and cheek regions) was removed. An initial registration of the T1 model to the T0 model was performed using eight anatomical landmarks, with the T0 model as the reference, including the glabella, bilateral tragions, bilateral outer canthi, bilateral inner canthi, and the soft-tissue nasion. This was followed by a best-fit alignment of the midface region. The complete T0 model was subsequently restored while preserving the established registration relationship. After registration, a unified 3D coordinate system was established. The soft-tissue nasion (Ns) was defined as the origin, with three orthogonal planes intersecting at this point. The sagittal plane (YZ) was determined based on facial symmetry. The horizontal plane (XZ) was constructed using the right ala–tragus line to define the Camper plane, which was rotated upward by 7.5° and then translated to pass through Ns. The coronal plane (XY) was defined as orthogonal to the other two planes. The coordinate axes were defined as follows: positive X (+) to the left and negative X (−) to the right; positive Y (+) upward and negative Y (−) downward; positive Z (+) forward and negative Z (−) backward (Fig. 2).The true vertical line passing through Sn was defined as a line that passes through Sn, is perpendicular to the horizontal plane (XZ), is parallel to the Y-axis, and serves as the spatial reference for true vertical line (TVL)-related measurements.

Data collection and quality control

A single examiner with a background in orthodontics, who had undergone standardized calibration training for landmark identification, performed all landmark identifications over a four-week period. Intraobserver reliability was assessed by three repeated measurements of 100 three-dimensional images obtained at T0 and T1 from 50 patients by the same observer, with a measurement interval exceeding two weeks. All data were recorded using Microsoft Excel (version 2021; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Measurement reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with all values exceeding 0.85 (α = 0.05), indicating good repeatability and consistency (Supplementary Table 1). Prior to statistical analysis, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Levene’s test were used to assess data normality and homogeneity of variance, respectively. According to these results, normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data are reported as median and interquartile range. Statistical inference for within-group changes in lip parameters before and after bracket debonding was performed using paired t-tests. Between-sex comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests after confirming homogeneity of variance. For comparisons among the three vertical skeletal pattern groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied when the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met, followed by Tukey post hoc tests. When this assumption was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was used, followed by Games–Howell post hoc comparisons. Changes were considered clinically significant if linear measurements were ≥ 1.5 mm or angular measurements were ≥ 2° [16, 17]. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05. Given the involvement of multiple outcome measures and multiple levels of comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR). FDR correction was performed separately for the predefined between-group main-effect analyses and within-group pre–post comparisons. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Adjusted p-values were reported as q-values, with statistical significance set at q < 0.05. In this framework, q-values indicate FDR-controlled multiple-comparison adjusted results, with statistical significance set at q < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 shows the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the 285 included patients, stratified by vertical skeletal pattern into the hypodivergent (n = 70), normodivergent (n = 126), and hyperdivergent (n = 89) groups. No significant differences were observed among the three groups in sex distribution (P = 0.962), age (P = 0.119), extraction versus non-extraction treatment modality (P = 0.078), ANB angle (P = 0.070), overbite (P = 0.233), overjet (P = 0.493), U1-SN angle (P = 0.070), or L1-MP angle (P = 0.055). These findings indicate that the three groups were well balanced at baseline and were comparable for subsequent analyses.

Table 4 shows the within-group changes by sex. Paired comparisons were performed separately for each sex. After Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction, in males, significant changes were observed in the nasolabial angle, mentolabial angle, upper and lower lip prominence (to the Sn-Pog’ line), upper and lower lips relative to the E-line, upper and lower lip positions relative to the true vertical line, and commissure depth (q < 0.05). No significant changes were found in upper and lower lip vermilion height, mouth width, Cupid’s bow width, Cupid’s bow angle, commissure-to-Cupid’s bow angle, Cupid’s bow depth, or lip depth (q > 0.05). Female patients showed a similar pattern of changes.

Discussion

This study utilized structured light 3D scanning to investigate the effects of vertical skeletal patterns on three-dimensional lip changes before and after bracket debonding. Considering the potential influence of sex, analyses were first stratified by sex. The results indicated that male and female patients exhibited similar patterns of change, and the magnitude of these changes did not differ significantly between sexes (q > 0.05), consistent with the findings of Kim et al. [5].

Our data indicate that, in the frontal view, lip morphology remained stable across different vertical skeletal pattern groups before and after bracket debonding. This is consistent with Kim et al. [5], who, using 3D stereophotogrammetry to analyze 20 patients, found no significant horizontal or vertical changes in frontal lip measurements after debonding. Similarly, Jeon et al. [6] reported no significant displacements of lip landmarks in the frontal dimension following bracket removal using 3D laser scanning. Although these studies suggest frontal lip stability after bracket debonding, their small sample sizes may have limited the statistical power for subgroup analyses. In contrast, our study employed a larger cohort and stratified patients by vertical skeletal pattern, confirming not only the stability of frontal lip morphology but also its generalizability across skeletal patterns. Overall, the evidence suggests that bracket removal minimally affects frontal lip morphology, suggesting limited impact on patients’ frontal facial aesthetics.

In the lateral view, hyperdivergent patients exhibited significantly greater lip retrusion after bracket debonding compared to hypodivergent patients. For instance, changes in the nasolabial angle were more pronounced in hyperdivergent patients (-1.67 ± 2.00°) than in hypodivergent patients (-0.90 ± 1.30°) (P = 0.010). Furthermore, the changes in the nasolabial angle, mentolabial angle, and commissure depth in hyperdivergent patients were very close to the predefined and commonly used clinical thresholds, but did not reach these thresholds, and the effect sizes were also small. Given that orthodontic patients often prioritize facial esthetics and are particularly sensitive to subtle changes in lip morphology, these changes may still have some clinical relevance, although their clinical significance requires further validation. Lip retrusion after bracket debonding is more pronounced in hyperdivergent patients, which may be attributed to the combined effects of hard and soft tissue characteristics. This pronounced retrusion could be related to excessive vertical growth, which increases lower facial height and leads to stretched and thinned soft tissues with increased tension, often accompanied by lip incompetence; together, these factors predispose the lips to greater retrusion. In contrast, hypodivergent patients, with shorter lower facial heights, thicker lip tissues, and lower soft tissue tension, as well as inherent contact between the upper and lower lips, may partially buffer the retrusive effect. These characteristics are consistent with those reported by Alhazmi et al. [8] and Fang et al. [18]. Furthermore, Togninalli et al. [19] reported that hyperdivergent patients exhibited significantly reduced masseter muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, and volume relative to hypodivergent and normodivergent patients, indicating a potential overall deficiency in perioral soft tissue support associated with this vertical skeletal pattern. Previous studies investigating lip retrusion after bracket debonding have produced inconsistent results, often attributable to differences in measurement techniques. Lee et al. [20] reported posterior sagittal displacement of multiple lip landmarks relative to a perpendicular drawn from the soft tissue glabella to the S-line (subnasale to pogonion) following bracket removal. Using conventional lateral profile photographs, Abed et al. [21] found no statistically significant changes in lip posture before and after bracket debonding. The greater retrusion reported in Lee’s study may be explained by the hyperdivergent sample (mean SN-MP = 38.4°, posterior/anterior facial height ratio = 39°), which aligns with our findings of pronounced lip retrusion in hyperdivergent patients. Although 3D scanning has improved measurement precision compared with 2D analysis, discrepancies among studies still persist. Jeon et al. [6] reported generalized posterior displacement of the lips and perioral soft tissues exceeding 1 mm at the vermilion border and approximately 2 mm at the commissures. Kim et al. [5] observed minor posterior shifts ranging from 0.44 to 0.56 mm using high-precision 3D image registration. Eidson et al. [16] directly measured linear displacement using 3D facial scans and concluded that the changes were not clinically significant. Variations in study findings may be attributable to differences in sample composition (e.g., vertical skeletal pattern), bracket thickness, or registration errors. In clinical practice, secondary lip retrusion may still occur after bracket debonding, particularly in hyperdivergent extraction cases. Therefore, the immediate soft-tissue profile should not be regarded as the treatment endpoint during the retraction phase. Instead, a prospective allowance for compensatory changes should be incorporated, and the amount of retraction should be carefully evaluated, particularly in extraction-related decisions, to maintain optimal long-term profile stability.

Furthermore, within each vertical skeletal pattern group, lower lip retrusion appeared to be slightly greater than that of the upper lip. For instance, mean retrusion of the lower lip prominence was 0.27, 0.45, and 0.74 mm in the hypodivergent, normodivergent, and hyperdivergent groups, respectively, compared to 0.22, 0.34, and 0.60 mm for the upper lip. However, no direct statistical comparison between upper and lower lip changes within the same group was performed; therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. This finding aligns with previous studies [5, 6, 20], indicating that after bracket debonding, changes are generally more pronounced in the lower lip when assessed from a lateral soft tiss ue perspective. Volumetric analyses also support this observation, as Yook et al. [22] reported greater post-debonding volume reduction in the lower lip (513 mm3) compared to the upper lip (431 mm3). Anatomical and functional characteristics of perioral muscles may account for the upper-lower lip differences, with the lower lip relying more on underlying support structures [23, 24]. Clinically, in Class II skeletal patients, excessive lower lip retrusion after debonding may accentuate mandibular prominence, deepen the mentolabial fold, flatten the lower facial third, and impart an aged or stern appearance, highlighting the need for careful treatment planning.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single-center Chinese population. Given the known ethnic variations in lip morphology, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other racial or regional populations, which may limit the external validity of the results [25, 26]. In addition, a single bracket system was used in this study. Differences in bracket design, size, and volume across systems may influence changes in lip support and subsequently result in different patterns of soft-tissue response, which may further limit the generalizability of the findings.Third, the present results reflect the immediate post-debonding changes in the lips and may not fully represent the long-term stable soft-tissue condition; therefore, further longitudinal studies are required for validation. Moreover, only static lip measurements were performed in this study. However, the lips undergo dynamic changes during speech and smiling; future studies incorporating 4D dynamic capture technology may provide a more comprehensive characterization of these features [27].Certain variables not included in this study, such as initial malocclusion characteristics, the amount of incisor retraction, and treatment duration, may also influence lip retrusion; therefore, potential residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Although false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, the possibility of residual type I errors cannot be fully ruled out given the large number of statistical tests performed.We plan to address these limitations in future studies and further refine and strengthen the conclusions of this work.

Conclusions

After bracket debonding, the frontal lip morphology remained relatively stable overall. Vertical skeletal pattern was associated with lateral lip retrusion, with hyperdivergent patients showing relatively greater retrusion; however, the magnitude of change was limited and should be interpreted with caution in terms of clinical significance. Differences in upper and lower lip retrusion were observed, with the lower lip showing a numerically greater change; however, given the exploratory nature of some comparisons, these findings should be considered preliminary. Overall, vertical skeletal pattern was associated with lip retrusion after bracket debonding; however, its clinical significance and potential application value require further investigation and validation.
