Targeting the Hippo pathway in pulmonary arterial hypertension: emerging pharmacological strategies
Authors: Danning Zheng, Yanzi Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a severe, progressive disease characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling and increased resistance, ultimately leading to right heart failure and high mortality. The Hippo signaling pathway has emerged as a key regulator of PAH development by influencing cell proliferation, apoptosis, and phenotypic changes in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), endothelial cells, and adventitial fibroblasts. Disruption of this pathway – especially involving its effectors YAP1 and TAZ – contributes to excessive cell growth, migration, endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), metabolic reprogramming, and inflammatory responses, collectively driving pathological vascular remodeling. Recent studies have highlighted the complex interaction between the Hippo pathway and signaling axes such as Notch, Transforming growth factor (TGF-β), Akt/mTOR, and integrin-linked kinase, emphasizing its extensive influence on disease progression. Emerging therapies targeting Hippo components and new small molecules, including natural products such as luteolin and resveratrol, offer promising options for reversing remodeling and treating PAH. However, the potential for Hippo-focused therapies is limited by the pathway's diverse roles and context-dependent effects. This narrative review covers recent progress in understanding Hippo signaling in PAH vascular remodeling, molecular mechanisms, and therapeutic prospects, laying a foundation for future targeted treatments.

INTRODUCTION

Pulmonary hypertension is a progressive pulmonary vascular disease characterized by increased pulmonary vascular resistance, elevated pulmonary arterial pressure, and pulmonary vascular remodeling, ultimately leading to right ventricular hypertrophy, right heart failure, and death. Epidemiological studies indicate a minimum prevalence of 127 cases per 100 000 individuals, affecting approximately 0.127% of the global population [1]. Pulmonary hypertension is defined by a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) at least 20 mmHg at rest [2]. Exercise-induced pulmonary hypertension, defined by an mPAP/cardiac output slope greater than 3 mmHg/(l min) between rest and exercise, represents another form of abnormal hemodynamic response [3]. Due to its high incidence and poor clinical outcomes, pulmonary hypertension is often referred to as the ‘cancer of cardiovascular diseases’ [4]. The 7th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension (2024) classified pulmonary hypertension into five categories according to pathology, clinical presentation, and therapeutic strategies: pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), pulmonary hypertension associated with lung diseases/hypoxia, pulmonary hypertension due to left heart disease, pulmonary hypertension caused by pulmonary artery obstruction, and pulmonary hypertension with unclear or multifactorial mechanisms [5].

Although the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved five classes of drugs for pulmonary hypertension [6], these agents primarily induce pulmonary vasodilation and offer symptomatic relief without reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling – the central driver of disease progression. The lack of effective therapies targeting the structural and cellular changes underlying remodeling reflects an incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved [7]. Nevertheless, pulmonary hypertension remains a complex disorder shaped by genetic predisposition and environmental factors[8]. As vascular remodeling is fundamental to all pulmonary hypertension subtypes, identifying novel pathways and therapeutic targets capable of modifying this process remains a priority [7].

Pulmonary vascular remodeling involves pathological changes across all three layers of the vessel wall – including endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and adventitial fibroblasts – leading to luminal narrowing and increased vascular resistance [1,9]. Endothelial dysfunction plays a pivotal role, characterized by loss of endothelial identity, impaired intercellular junctions, enhanced platelet activation, in-situ thrombosis, and dysregulated immune responses, collectively promoting a pro-inflammatory environment that accelerates remodeling [10].

Among emerging molecular targets for vascular remodeling, the Hippo signaling pathway has attracted particular attention due to its central role in regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and phenotypic plasticity across multiple vascular cell types [11]. Growing evidence demonstrates its involvement in pulmonary hypertension pathogenesis [12]. In particular, the Hippo pathway influences pulmonary vascular remodeling by regulating PASMC proliferation and migration, as well as endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) in pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (PAECs) [13]. Despite its relevance, the specific mechanisms through which Hippo signaling contributes to pulmonary hypertension and its interplay with vascular cells, extracellular matrix remodeling, and inflammation remain incompletely defined.

This review summarizes current research on Hippo pathway regulation in pulmonary hypertension-associated vascular remodeling, emphasizing its roles in PASMCs, PAECs, extracellular matrix dynamics, and inflammatory processes. By clarifying these mechanisms, we aim to highlight unresolved knowledge gaps, stimulate new research directions, and support the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

HIPPO PATHWAY

The Hippo pathway is an evolutionarily conserved signaling cascade composed of an upstream kinase module and downstream effector components, primarily transmitting signals through phosphorylation-mediated cascades [14–17]. Under physiological conditions, activated MST1/2 phosphorylates and activates its substrates LATS1/2, which in turn directly phosphorylate the downstream effector YAP1. Phosphorylated YAP1 binds to 14-3-3 proteins in the cytoplasm, sequestering it from nuclear translocation and thereby inhibiting its transcriptional coactivator function [18–20]. Under stress or hypoxic conditions, Hippo signaling is suppressed, leading to YAP1 dephosphorylation and increased nuclear translocation. Nuclear YAP1 then acts as a transcriptional coactivator, collaborating with multiple transcription factors to regulate downstream target genes (Fig. 1) and drive the pathogenesis of various diseases [18–20].

The Hippo pathway plays pivotal roles in regulating cell proliferation, survival, organ size, tissue development, repair, and regeneration [21]. Dysregulation of this pathway is implicated in a wide spectrum of diseases, including pulmonary hypertension [13,22], cardiac dysfunction [23], hepatic ischemia–reperfusion injury [24], gastric cancer [25], colorectal cancer [26], ischemic stroke [27], and asthma [28] (Fig. 2). Mounting evidence indicates that the Hippo pathway actively contributes to pulmonary hypertension pathogenesis and progression [29–31].

THE HIPPO PATHWAY IN PULMONARY HYPERTENSION-ASSOCIATED VASCULAR REMODELING

The hippo pathway and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell phenotypic switching

Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) normally regulate vascular tone and blood flow, exhibiting limited proliferation and migration under physiological conditions [32–35]. Pathological stimuli such as hypoxia induce a transition from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype, characterized by enhanced proliferation, migration, apoptosis resistance, extracellular matrix secretion, and morphological changes from spindle-shaped to epithelioid or rhomboid forms. This shift is accompanied by decreased contractile markers, including α-SMA and SM22α, and increased synthetic markers such as proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and cyclin D1, contributing to vascular wall thickening and remodeling in proliferative vascular diseases [34,36].

The Hippo pathway – particularly the effector Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) – is a central regulator of VSMC and PASMC phenotypic behavior. YAP1 acts as a transcriptional coactivator with TEAD factors to regulate downstream genes, including CTGF, CYR61, cyclins A1, D1, and E1, and SOX9 [37–42]. Through this activity, YAP1 suppresses contractile gene expression while promoting synthetic, proliferative, and migratory phenotypes in smooth muscle cells [43,44]. Consequently, interventions that inhibit YAP1-mediated phenotypic switching may help counteract vascular remodeling in occlusive vascular diseases and pulmonary hypertension [45].

Multiple regulatory mechanisms modulate YAP1 activity. HOXA4 interferes with YAP1/TEAD binding, reducing YAP1-dependent transcription and limiting VSMC switching; its deletion leads to aggravated remodeling [46–48]. YAP1 additionally promotes myocardin degradation, impairing smooth-muscle differentiation [49], while BACH1 cooperates with G9a and YAP1 to suppress VSMC marker gene promoters, an effect reversed upon silencing these factors [50]. In PASMCs, YAP1/TAZ inhibit COX-2 and prostaglandin signaling, driving phenotypic switching and vascular stiffness; conversely, YAP1/TAZ inhibition restores COX-2 expression and reduces proliferation and migration in pulmonary hypertension models [51]. These regulatory partners collectively highlight therapeutic avenues aimed at the YAP1/TEAD axis (Fig. 3).

Excessive PASMC proliferation, a hallmark of pulmonary hypertension, is tightly regulated by Hippo pathway components [34]. MST1/2 kinases activate the Akt/mTORC1 pathway to promote PASMC proliferation; their deletion in pulmonary hypertension mouse models suppresses PASMC growth and remodeling, suggesting MST1/2 as viable targets [52]. The E3 ubiquitin ligase Siah2 destabilizes LATS1/2, enhancing YAP1 activation in pulmonary hypertension; pharmacological Siah2 inhibition stabilizes LATS1/2, suppresses YAP1, and mitigates vascular remodeling and right-ventricular pressure elevations [53,54].

Upstream signals, including sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), further modulate Hippo/YAP1 signaling. S1P promotes PASMC proliferation by activating STAT3 and upregulating miR-135b, which suppresses β-TrCP-mediated YAP1 degradation, thereby enhancing Notch3 activity [55]. In idiopathic PAH, reduced LATS1 and elevated nuclear YAP1 support mTOR-AKT signaling, HIF-1α stabilization, increased Notch3 and β-catenin, and suppression of the pro-apoptotic regulator Bim, collectively sustaining PASMC proliferation and survival [56].

Extracellular matrix signaling also intersects with the Hippo pathway. Fibronectin upregulates integrin-linked kinase 1 (ILK1), and YAP1 stimulates fibronectin synthesis, forming a feed-forward loop that drives ILK1-dependent LATS1 inactivation and YAP1 nuclear translocation. ILK1 inhibition restores LATS1 signaling, reduces PASMC proliferation, and enhances apoptosis; treatment with the ILK1 inhibitor Cpd22 in pulmonary hypertension models suppresses remodeling and improves outcomes [56].

Metabolic disturbances in pulmonary hypertension are also linked to aberrant YAP1 activation. Galectin-3 (Gal-3) enhances YAP1 dephosphorylation and nuclear localization, increasing FOXM1 and cyclin D1 expression and promoting PASMC proliferation in a dose-dependent and time-dependent manner. AMPK counteracts this pathway by inhibiting YAP1/FOXM1/cyclin D1 signaling, thereby attenuating vascular remodeling [57,58].

Posttranslational modification through ubiquitination further shapes Hippo pathway dynamics. E3 ligases such as β-TrCP ubiquitinate YAP1/TAZ, whereas others like ITCH target LATS1/2. Deubiquitinating enzymes including USP9X and USP10 remove ubiquitin moieties, providing additional control over pathway components [59–63]. PASMCs from pulmonary hypertension patients exhibit reduced LATS1 and increased nuclear YAP1/TAZ accumulation, supporting proliferative and antiapoptotic phenotypes. Bioinformatic studies reveal extensive crosstalk between Hippo and Akt, Notch3, Wnt, and HIF-1α pathways, reinforcing the pathway's central role in pulmonary vascular remodeling and its potential as a therapeutic target [64].

Hippo pathway and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells

EndMT was initially observed during embryonic development and is now known to contribute to various diseases, including pulmonary hypertension, atherosclerosis, cardiac fibrosis, and myocardial infarction [65]. Analogous to epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), EndMT enables endothelial cells to lose polarity and cell–cell adhesion, adopting mesenchymal-like phenotypes with enhanced migratory and invasive capacities [66,67]. Under pathological stimuli, EndMT disrupts intercellular junctions, downregulating endothelial markers such as VE-cadherin, Tie-1/2, VEGFR1/2, and PECAM-1/CD31, while upregulating mesenchymal and smooth muscle markers like vimentin and α-SMA [68]. This process contributes significantly to vascular remodeling and disease progression in pulmonary hypertension [69].

YAP1/TAZ interactions with transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) play critical roles in promoting fibrosis and tumorigenesis [70,71]. TGF-β1 regulates downstream EndMT-related genes, including Snail and SLUG, by phosphorylating Smads or activating non-Smad pathways, thereby promoting EndMT, pulmonary vascular remodeling, and pulmonary hypertension [72]. YAP1 is closely linked with Smad proteins – Smad7, for example, activates YAP1/Notch signaling to induce EMT in cholangiocytes and promote cholangiocarcinoma [73]. In oral submucous fibrosis (OSF), YAP1 is highly expressed and mediates EndMT, correlating with disease stage. Arecoline enhances reactive oxygen species and PERK signaling, promoting YAP1 nuclear translocation and EndMT progression, while the YAP1 inhibitor verteporfin suppresses these effects and alleviates fibrosis in mice [74,75].

YAP1 also interacts with microRNAs (miRNAs) in regulating EndMT [72]. miR-200c-3p, for instance, targets the 3’-UTR of Smad7, increasing its expression and enabling Smad7 to bind YAP1, thereby inducing YAP1 nuclear translocation and EndMT in human umbilical vein endothelial cells [44]. Whether YAP1 cooperates with other miRNAs to modulate pulmonary hypertension progression remains to be explored.

Recent findings highlight exosomes as regulators of Hippo pathway-mediated EMT. Exosomes, secreted by nearly all cell types, carry proteins and miRNAs that influence recipient cells and participate in diseases such as pulmonary hypertension, myocardial infarction, and cancer. For instance, exosomal miR-665 suppresses PTPRB expression in hepatocellular carcinoma, inhibiting Hippo-mediated EMT and enhancing invasiveness. Given the parallels between EMT and EndMT, it is plausible that exosomes could regulate Hippo-mediated EndMT during pulmonary vascular remodeling in pulmonary hypertension. However, this remains unreported and warrants further investigation.

Hippo pathway and pulmonary vascular adventitial fibroblast proliferation

Pulmonary artery adventitial fibroblast cells (PAAFs) are the most abundant cellular component of the pulmonary vascular adventitia, regulating pulmonary vascular structure and function from the outside in [76]. Overactivation and proliferation of PAAFs lead to their infiltration into the vascular media and release of growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), thereby promoting PAAF proliferation, increased extracellular matrix production and deposition, and ultimately contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling and pulmonary hypertension [76]. Recent studies have shown that SOX9 expression in endothelial cells is significantly upregulated in bleomycin-induced, choline-deficient diet-induced, and high-fat diet-induced mouse fibrosis models [77]. To investigate whether endothelial SOX9 upregulation alone is sufficient to induce disease, endothelial cell-specific SOX9-overexpressing mice were generated, demonstrating that SOX9 overexpression promotes fibrosis in multiple organs and leads to heart failure. In two heart failure mouse models and lung/liver fibrosis models, endothelial cell-specific SOX9 knockout prevented fibrosis and organ dysfunction. Whole transcriptome and single-cell RNA sequencing of mouse endothelial cells revealed that SOX9 induces expression of extracellular matrix, growth factor, and inflammation-related genes, increasing endothelial cell matrix deposition [77]. Furthermore, activated endothelial cells stimulated adjacent fibroblasts, promoting their proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix deposition – partially mediated by secreted growth factor CCN2, a direct SOX9 target. Endothelial cell-specific SOX9 knockout reversed these changes [77]. As SOX9 functions as a pro-fibrotic factor across multiple mouse organs, it may serve as a key regulator of endothelial cell-mediated fibroblast fibrosis. Given that SOX9 is a target gene of the YAP1 effector, whether the Hippo/YAP1 pathway in endothelial cells also mediates SOX9 regulation of PAAFs warrants further investigation.

The Hippo pathway is closely associated with PAAF proliferation. Evidence includes: in pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary artery adventitial PAAFs; interleukin-6 (IL-6) stimulation significantly upregulates MST1/2 expression, which, in turn, suppresses FOXO3, driving excessive PAAF proliferation and thereby promoting pulmonary vascular remodeling and pulmonary hypertension. Specific inhibition of MST1/2 effectively blocks FOXO3-induced PAAF overproliferation, attenuates vascular remodeling, and alleviates pulmonary hypertension [52]. In summary, MST1/2 promotes aberrant proliferation and survival of both PASMCs and PAAFs through distinct mechanisms, collectively contributing to vascular remodeling and pulmonary hypertension. The novel mechanistic link between MST1/2 and the abnormal proliferation/survival of PASMCs versus PAAFs warrants further investigation. The proliferative function of MST1/2 in PAAFs is IL-6-dependent. As IL-6 is a well known critical pro-inflammatory mediator, whether the Hippo pathway interacts with inflammatory cell infiltration in pulmonary hypertension – and how this crosstalk is coordinated – remains to be explored. Hippo pathway and pulmonary arterial inflammatory cell infiltration

Pulmonary vascular inflammatory cell infiltration is a major pathological hallmark of pulmonary hypertension. Clinical and experimental studies consistently demonstrate varying degrees of perivascular immune cell accumulation in both pulmonary hypertension patients and animal models [78]. Inflammation typically precedes vascular remodeling in experimental pulmonary hypertension, indicating that immune dysregulation is a driver, rather than a secondary consequence, of vascular disorder[78]. Elevated cytokines, chemokines, and inflammatory mediators correlate with disease severity and outcomes. Diverse immune cells – including neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, mast cells, and T and B lymphocytes – localize around pulmonary vessels [78]. Phenotypic alterations in endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts further enhance their responsiveness to inflammatory stimuli, amplifying cytokine and chemokine production. These mechanisms collectively sustain pulmonary hypertension-associated inflammation.

Inflammation itself is an evolutionarily conserved protective process mediated by coordinated activation of immune and nonimmune cells [79]. The Hippo signaling pathway, another conserved regulatory cascade, governs cell proliferation, survival, differentiation, and tissue homeostasis. Recent evidence indicates that Hippo signaling extensively interfaces with inflammatory and immune responses triggered by tissue injury or pathogen exposure [79–81].

A key inflammatory mediator linked to the Hippo pathway is the NLRP3 inflammasome, which responds to microbial components, endogenous danger signals, and environmental stressors. Upon activation, NLRP3 promotes caspase-1 activation and the maturation of IL-1β and IL-18, driving pyroptosis [82]. Dysregulated NLRP3 signaling contributes to pulmonary hypertension [83] and to other inflammatory and metabolic disorders such as atherosclerosis [84], gout [85], and type 2 diabetes [86].

The regulatory influence of YAP1 on NLRP3 appears context-dependent. In ischemia–reperfusion-induced liver inflammation, ischemia–reperfusion stress enhances MST1/2 and LATS1 phosphorylation, reducing YAP1 activity. Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy reverses these effects, increases YAP1 nuclear translocation, and attenuates liver inflammation [87]. MSC-mediated Hippo pathway inactivation promotes YAP1–β-catenin interaction, which activates NLRP3 but paradoxically shifts macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype by upregulating Arg1 and IL-10 while reducing TNF-α and IL-1β [87].

Conversely, in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced systemic inflammation and monosodium urate (MSU)-triggered peritonitis, YAP1 stabilizes NLRP3 by preventing its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby enhancing pro-inflammatory responses [37]. YAP1 knockdown diminishes NLRP3 activity, lowers IL-1β release, and reduces neutrophil infiltration [37].

Tissue specificity may underlie these divergent outcomes. In models of acute lung injury and fibrosis, YAP1 silencing reduces inflammatory infiltration and collagen deposition [88]. However, YAP1 inhibition simultaneously exacerbates oxidative stress – elevating IL-6, TNF-α, and ROS while depleting SOD2 and GSH-Px – thereby worsening diaphragm dysfunction [88]. Thus, YAP1 activity can exert protective or detrimental effects depending on the cellular environment.

In endothelial cells, YAP1 directly regulates inflammatory cell recruitment. YAP1 dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation enhance inflammatory activation and monocyte adhesion, whereas YAP1 inhibition or YAP1/TAZ knockdown reduces TNF-α-induced VCAM1 expression and attenuates endothelial inflammation [90]. Given that inflammation is a major driver of pulmonary hypertension progression [89] and the Hippo pathway is intertwined with inflammatory regulation, delineating the molecular basis of Hippo-mediated inflammatory signaling may facilitate the development of safe and effective immunomodulatory therapies for pulmonary hypertension.

Hippo pathway-targeted pharmacological therapies for pulmonary arterial hypertension

Despite significant progress in pulmonary hypertension prevention and treatment over recent decades – driven by advances in basic research and clinical experience – current therapeutic outcomes remain suboptimal, with poor patient prognosis and substantial drug-related side effects (e.g. liver injury, dyspnea, diarrhea, nausea, and systemic pain). Thus, there is an urgent need for safer and more innovative pulmonary hypertension treatment strategies [7]. In recent years, deepening research on the Hippo pathway has identified multiple Hippo-targeted drugs or compounds with promising potential for pulmonary hypertension therapy (Table 2). Studies have shown that the Hippo pathway and its downstream effectors YAP1 and TAZ are dysregulated in multiple cancer types and are essential for various cancer cell phenotypes [90]. Most available YAP1/TAZ inhibitors – including SWTX-143 – have been developed and validated primarily in oncology settings, where they demonstrate strong suppression of YAP1/TEAD-driven tumor growth. For example, SWTX-143 inhibits YAP1/TAZ-TEAD transcriptional activity, reduces mesothelioma growth in xenograft models, and suppresses renal cancer cell proliferation [90]. Although these findings do not originate from pulmonary hypertension models, they provide proof-of-mechanism for pharmacologically targeting YAP1/TAZ and thereby inform pulmonary hypertension drug development, given that similar Hippo dysregulation drives PASMC proliferation and vascular remodeling in pulmonary hypertension.

Since 2018, numerous publications, patents, and first-in-human phase 1 trials have further validated the therapeutic potential of targeting the Hippo pathway, particularly the YAP1/TAZ-TEAD transcription complex [91]. While these inhibitors were originally designed for cancer, their mechanisms – such as blocking TEAD palmitoylation or disrupting TEAD-YAP1/TAZ interactions – are mechanistically relevant to pulmonary hypertension, where excessive YAP1 activation underlies pathological remodeling. Current anticancer drugs such as doxorubicin also modulate Hippo signaling by upregulating MST1 and altering YAP1 activity [92], although these effects, too, have been observed exclusively in cancer cells rather than pulmonary hypertension models. Thus, cancer-derived mechanistic insights highlight potential therapeutic angles but require targeted evaluation in pulmonary hypertension contexts.

Verteporfin is the first identified small-molecule YAP1-TEAD inhibitor and is widely used in preclinical studies across diseases [93]. Its direct relevance to pulmonary hypertension has been demonstrated: S1P or hypoxia activates YAP1 in PASMCs, promoting proliferation and nuclear translocation; verteporfin inhibits this response and attenuates S1P-mediated PASMC proliferation, thereby suppressing vascular remodeling and pulmonary hypertension progression [94]. This stands in contrast to many YAP1 inhibitors whose effects are limited to non-pulmonary hypertension disease models. Recent work also noted that verteporfin exhibits off-target effects in functional YAP1-TEAD assays [53]. To address this, Tang et al.[13] identified small-molecule TEAD palmitoylation inhibitors (VT103 and VT104) with high potency and selectivity. Although their demonstrated efficacy is currently restricted to tumor models, the nanomolar potency, oral bioavailability, and ability to modulate TEAD – an essential mediator of YAP1 activity in pulmonary hypertension – suggest promising translational potential for future pulmonary hypertension applications.

The identification of TEAD-selective inhibitors provides a foundation for designing Hippo-targeted modulators with improved specificity and lower toxicity for pulmonary hypertension. Such translational opportunities parallel the recent success of sotatercept, approved in 2024 as the first novel pulmonary hypertension therapy in over a decade. As an activin signaling inhibitor, sotatercept restores proliferative balance in pulmonary vascular cells by binding TGF-β superfamily ligands and blocking downstream EpoR-β signaling, thereby mitigating vascular remodeling [95]. Although sotatercept does not directly target Hippo components, its mechanism emphasizes the therapeutic value of modulating dysregulated proliferative pathways – an approach highly relevant to Hippo-targeted drug strategies.

Luteolin, a natural flavonoid, represents one of the few Hippo-targeting compounds with direct evidence in pulmonary hypertension models. Beyond its known clinical applications [96], luteolin promotes YAP1 degradation and inhibits EMT, proliferation, and migration in non-pulmonary hypertension disease settings [97]. Importantly, pulmonary hypertension-specific studies show that luteolin inhibits YAP1-induced PASMC proliferation through the Hippo/YAP1/PI3K/AKT axis, thereby reducing pulmonary vascular remodeling and improving pulmonary hypertension outcomes [98]. Thus, luteolin provides one of the clearest examples of a compound whose Hippo-modulating effects translate directly to pulmonary hypertension pathobiology.

Resveratrol is a bioactive polyphenol with extensive pharmacological activities. In pulmonary hypertension, resveratrol alleviates endothelial dysfunction and inhibits arteriolar remodeling through multiple non-Hippo mechanisms, including NF-κB, BMP/SMAD, and PI3K/AKT pathways [99]. Resveratrol also targets Hippo/YAP1 signaling in non-pulmonary hypertension contexts, including gastric cancer cells [100] and hepatic stellate cells in liver fibrosis [101], where it activates the Hippo pathway and suppresses YAP1/TAZ expression. However, its Hippo-specific actions have not yet been demonstrated in pulmonary hypertension models, making this an area requiring further investigation to determine whether its YAP1-directed effects can contribute to pulmonary hypertension therapy.

Overall, Hippo pathway inactivation (e.g. via hypoxia) reduces YAP1 phosphorylation, increases nuclear translocation, and activates downstream transcription, driving PASMC proliferation, migration, and pulmonary vascular remodeling [56,58]. This pathway is implicated across endothelial, smooth muscle, and fibroblast dysfunction in pulmonary hypertension [10]. Therefore, while several Hippo-targeting compounds have been validated primarily in cancer and other disease contexts, a subset – including verteporfin and luteolin – demonstrate direct mechanistic and therapeutic relevance in pulmonary hypertension models. Continued refinement of selective Hippo modulators offers promising opportunities to develop more specific, targeted, and low-toxicity therapies capable of transforming currently unsatisfactory pulmonary hypertension outcomes (Table 2).

In conclusion, the Hippo signaling pathway is increasingly recognized as a central regulator of PAH pathogenesis, influencing PASMC proliferation, EndMT, adventitial fibroblast activation, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Multiple Hippo components – especially YAP1/TAZ – drive maladaptive vascular remodeling, and preclinical studies demonstrate that targeted modulation through agents such as verteporfin, ILK1 inhibitors, luteolin, and resveratrol can attenuate these processes. Despite these advances, significant unanswered questions remain. Key challenges include defining the context-dependent effects of Hippo signaling across different vascular cell types, establishing reliable biomarkers of pathway activation in human PAH, and understanding how Hippo-targeted therapies might integrate with or enhance current vasodilator-based treatments. Furthermore, the broad regulatory scope of the Hippo pathway raises concerns regarding off-target effects and long-term safety. Future research should prioritize mechanistic clarification, optimization of compound specificity, and rigorous translational studies to determine whether Hippo modulation can meaningfully alter disease progression. Addressing these gaps will be essential for advancing Hippo-targeted therapies toward clinical adoption and achieving durable improvements in PAH outcomes.
