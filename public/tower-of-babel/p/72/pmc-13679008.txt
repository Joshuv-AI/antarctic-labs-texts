Characterizing the spatiotemporal displacement of fiducial markers for cyberknife radiosurgery in liver cancer and identifying predictive factors
Authors: Miaomiao Zhang, Lei Nie, Dengcui Zhao, Hui Xu, Guihua Zhang, Bo Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFiducial markers are essential for accurate image-guided stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, post-implantation positional variation introduces geometric uncertainty. This study aims to quantify the spatiotemporal observed displacement of fiducial markers in HCC patients treated with CyberKnife SBRT and to identify predictive factors for significant displacement.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed data from 185 HCC patients who underwent percutaneous implantation of 518 fiducial markers between January 2018 and December 2022. Serial CT scans were acquired at baseline (Day 1) and on days 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 post-implantation. Fiducial displacement was measured in three dimensions (craniocaudal, anteroposterior, mediolateral) relative to the baseline scan after rigid registration to vertebral bodies. Significant displacement was defined as a 3D vector displacement > 3 mm, encompassing true migration and geometrical uncertainties. To account for data clustering (multiple markers per patient), univariate and multivariable Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) models were used to identify predictors of significant displacement. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling. Furthermore, a dosimetric analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical impact of significant marker displacement.ResultsThe mean 3D vector displacement was greatest at Day 3 (2.8 ± 1.5 mm) and progressively decreased, plateauing after Day 7 (Day 7: 1.9 ± 1.1 mm; Day 14: 1.8 ± 1.0 mm; p < 0.001 for trend). The craniocaudal axis exhibited the largest magnitude of displacement. By Day 7, 88.6% of markers achieved stability (displacement ≤ 3 mm). Dosimetric evaluation revealed that simulated displacement > 3 mm significantly degraded target coverage (PTV V100%: 98.5% vs. 92.1%, p < 0.001). Multivariate GEE analysis identified tumor location within 2 cm of the diaphragm (Odds Ratio [OR] = 3.05, 95% CI: 1.75–5.20, p < 0.001) and tumor diameter > 5 cm (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.15–3.30, p = 0.012) as independent predictors for significant fiducialisplacement. The optimism-corrected area under the curve (AUC) for the predictive model was 0.76.ConclusionFiducial markers in HCC patients undergo significant early displacement, primarily within the first week, and stabilize thereafter. Proximity to the diaphragm and larger tumor size are associated with a higher risk of displacement, which can negatively impact delivered dosimetry in simulated scenarios. These findings support a recommended 7-day waiting period between implantation and CT simulation as a reasonable safety guideline and suggest that patients with high-risk features may benefit from extended monitoring or adaptive radiotherapy strategies.Keywords: Hepatocellular Carcinoma, Stereotactic Body Radiotherapy (SBRT), CyberKnife, Fiducial Markers, Marker Displacement, Image-Guided Radiotherapy (IGRT)

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a particularly high prevalence in East Asia [1, 2]. While surgical resection and transplantation offer curative potential for early-stage disease, a large proportion of patients present with unresectable tumors due to advanced stage, multifocality, or underlying liver dysfunction [3]. In this context, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), also known as stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR), has emerged as a highly effective and non-invasive local therapy, delivering ablative doses of radiation with high precision over a few fractions [4, 5].

The success of SBRT is contingent upon sub-millimeter accuracy in radiation delivery to maximize tumor control while minimizing toxicity to the surrounding normal liver parenchyma and adjacent organs-at-risk [6]. The liver, however, is a mobile organ, subject to significant respiratory-induced motion, which poses a major challenge to treatment accuracy [7]. To overcome this, advanced image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) techniques are employed. For systems like the CyberKnife, which uses real-time tumor tracking, the implantation of intra-tumoral or peri-tumoral radiopaque fiducial markers is the gold standard for robust and accurate target localization [8–10]. These markers serve as internal surrogates, allowing the system to track and compensate for tumor motion throughout the treatment fraction [11].

Despite their critical role, the positional integrity of fiducial markers is not absolute. Following percutaneous implantation, markers can shift from their initial position due to various factors, including local tissue reactions like hemorrhage and edema, resolution of inflammation, respiratory mechanics, and changes in tumor volume [12]. This observed displacement introduces a geometric uncertainty that can compromise the entire treatment chain, from CT simulation and treatment planning to final dose delivery. A discrepancy between the marker’s position at simulation and its position during treatment can lead to a geographic miss of the target and unintended irradiation of healthy tissue [13]. To mitigate this risk, a common clinical practice is to implement a waiting period, typically one week, between marker implantation and the planning CT scan to allow for stabilization [14]. However, this practice is largely based on empirical evidence and institutional experience rather than robust quantitative data. The optimal waiting interval remains poorly defined, and factors that might predict greater marker instability have not been systematically investigated for liver SBRT.

A comprehensive understanding of the spatiotemporal dynamics of fiducial marker displacement is crucial for optimizing clinical workflows and enhancing the safety and efficacy of liver SBRT. Therefore, this study was designed to address these knowledge gaps. The primary objectives were: (1) to quantitatively characterize the three-dimensional (3D) displacement of fiducial markers over a two-week period following implantation in HCC patients; (2) to determine the time required for markers to achieve positional stability; and (3) to identify patient-, tumor-, and procedure-related factors that predict significant marker displacement.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This single-center, retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital, which waived the need for individual patient consent due to the retrospective nature of the analysis. We reviewed the records of all patients with HCC who underwent CyberKnife SBRT with implanted fiducial markers at our institution between January 2018 and December 2022. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of HCC confirmed by pathology or typical imaging features according to international guidelines; (3) treatment with CyberKnife SBRT; (4) implantation of at least one gold fiducial marker; and (5) availability of a complete series of CT scans, including a baseline scan within 24 h of implantation and at least three follow-up scans at predefined intervals. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior liver radiotherapy; (2) incomplete imaging data; (3) significant artifacts on CT scans precluding accurate marker localization; (4) subsequent local treatments (e.g., TACE, RFA) between marker implantation and SBRT; and (5) extreme fiducial displacement preventing the successful delivery of SBRT. The patient selection process is detailed in the study flow diagram (Fig. 1). A sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate potential selection bias due to patient exclusion. This research was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [15].

Fiducial marker implantation and imaging protocol

All fiducial implantations were performed by experienced interventional radiologists under CT guidance. Typically, 2 to 4 sterile gold markers (1.0 mm diameter, 5 mm length) were implanted within or immediately adjacent to (≤ 2 cm) the tumor. A baseline 4D-CT simulation scan (Philips Brilliance Big Bore CT, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands) was performed within 24 h of implantation (defined as Day 1). Patients were immobilized in a supine position with their arms raised above their heads, using a customized whole-body vacuum cushion. The baseline 4D-CT was utilized to define the internal target volume (ITV). To ensure phase consistency and minimize radiation exposure, the end-exhale phase was extracted from the 4D-CT for baseline registration, matching the subsequent fast 3D non-contrast CT scans which were all acquired at the end-exhale phase. Subsequent non-contrast CT scans, using the same positioning and scanning parameters, were acquired on days 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 post-implantation for marker stability assessment. All scans were performed during the end-exhale phase of the respiratory cycle, guided by a respiratory monitoring system (Varian RPM, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA).

Image registration and displacement measurement

All CT image sets for a given patient were imported into the Accuray PrecisePlan treatment planning system (Version 2.0, Accuray Inc., Sunnyvale, CA). To establish a consistent frame of reference, each follow-up CT scan was co-registered with the baseline (Day 1) CT scan using a rigid registration algorithm based on the thoracic vertebral bodies (T10-L2), which are considered stable anatomical structures. The centroid of each fiducial marker on the baseline scan and on each subsequent scan was automatically identified by the planning software and manually verified by an experienced medical physicist. The displacement of each marker at each time point was calculated as the difference in centroid coordinates relative to baseline in the mediolateral (ML, X-axis), craniocaudal (CC, Y-axis), and anteroposterior (AP, Z-axis) directions. Data collection, covariates, and dosimetric evaluation

We collected data on patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and tumor features from electronic medical records. Variables included: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), presence of cirrhosis, Child-Pugh class, tumor diameter (largest dimension on baseline CT), tumor volume, tumor location (categorized as subdiaphragmatic [within 2 cm of the diaphragm] vs. non-subdiaphragmatic), number of implanted markers, and marker position (intratumoral vs. peritumoral). Significant marker displacement was defined a priori as a 3D vector displacement > 3 mm. This specific threshold was selected because it corresponds to the typical 3–5 mm Planning Target Volume (PTV) margins employed in liver SBRT and represents the limit of acceptable geometric uncertainty for CyberKnife tracking algorithms [11, 16].

To assess the clinical relevance of fiducial displacement, a dosimetric evaluation was conducted on a randomly selected subset of 30 patients who exhibited significant marker displacement (> 3 mm) and 30 matched control patients with stable markers (≤ 3 mm). The original treatment plans (created on the baseline CT) were recalculated by artificially shifting the isocenter to reflect the average displaced 3D coordinates observed on Day 7. Dosimetric parameters, including PTV coverage (V100%), Gross Tumor Volume (GTV) coverage (V100%), and Mean Liver dose (Liver-GTV), were compared between the original plans and the simulated displacement plans.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.2.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. A linear mixed-effects model was used to analyze the trend of 3D vector displacement over time, accounting for the correlation of repeated measurements within each marker. The proportion of stable markers (displacement ≤ 3 mm) at each time point was calculated. To account for the clustering effect of multiple markers nested within individual patients, univariate and multivariable analyses were conducted using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure [17]. Ten candidate variables were initially considered. With 112 significant displacement events, the events-per-variable ratio exceeded 10, ensuring model stability. A backward stepwise selection method based on the Quasi-likelihood Information Criterion (QIC) was utilized to construct the final multivariable model. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. The results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated to evaluate the performance of the final predictive model. To prevent overfitting, internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to calculate the optimism-corrected area under the curve (AUC) and the Brier score for calibration assessment [18]. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 296 patients who underwent fiducial implantation for liver SBRT were screened for eligibility. After excluding 111 patients for reasons detailed in Figs. 1 and 185 patients with a total of 518 implanted fiducial markers were included in the final analysis. Of the 111 excluded patients, 23 had prior liver radiotherapy, 38 had incomplete imaging data, 38 underwent subsequent local treatments, and 12 were excluded specifically because extreme fiducial displacement precluded SBRT delivery. To address potential selection bias, a sensitivity analysis comparing baseline characteristics between included (n = 185) and excluded (n = 111) patients was performed (Table 1). No significant differences were observed, suggesting our final cohort remains largely representative. However, it is noteworthy that among the 12 patients excluded due to extreme migration, 9 (75.0%) had subdiaphragmatic tumors, further supporting the risk association. The baseline characteristics of the included patient cohort are summarized in Table 2. The median age was 62 years (IQR, 54–69), and 78.4% were male. Most patients had underlying cirrhosis (74.1%) and were Child-Pugh class A (81.1%). The mean tumor diameter was 4.8 ± 2.1 cm, and 41.6% of tumors were located in a subdiaphragmatic position.

Spatiotemporal displacement patterns of fiducial markers

The observed displacement of fiducial markers was most pronounced in the early phase after implantation and gradually stabilized over the two-week observation period. The mean 3D vector displacement was 2.8 ± 1.5 mm at Day 3, which significantly decreased to 2.2 ± 1.3 mm at Day 5, and further to 1.9 ± 1.1 mm at Day 7 (p < 0.001, linear mixed-effects model). After Day 7, the displacement remained relatively stable, with a mean of 1.8 ± 1.0 mm at both Day 10 and Day 14 (Fig. 2A).

When analyzed by direction, the CC (craniocaudal) axis showed the greatest magnitude of displacement at all time points, followed by the AP (anteroposterior) and ML (mediolateral) axes (Fig. 2B). The mean CC displacement was 2.1 ± 1.6 mm at Day 3 and stabilized at approximately 1.2 mm after Day 7. The proportion of markers considered stable (3D displacement ≤ 3 mm) increased over time: 61.2% on Day 3, 79.5% on Day 5, 88.6% on Day 7, and reached 91.3% by Day 14 (Fig. 3).

On Day 7, 59 markers (11.4%) remained unstable (> 3 mm). Further analysis revealed that these persistently unstable markers were predominantly associated with high-risk features: 48 (81.4%) were in tumors either subdiaphragmatic or > 5 cm. In tracking individual markers, 3 markers exhibited continued incremental migration between Day 7 and Day 14. Additionally, a “stable-then-migrated” pattern was observed in 2 markers, which met stability criteria on Day 5 but subsequently displaced > 3 mm by Day 10. At the patient level, 22 of 185 patients (11.9%) harbored at least one unstable marker on Day 7.

Dosimetric impact of significant marker displacement

The dosimetric simulation comparing planned versus actual delivered doses (accounting for displacement > 3 mm) demonstrated significant degradation in treatment quality (Table 3). For patients with significant marker displacement, the target coverage was markedly compromised, with PTV V100% dropping from 98.5% ± 1.2% to 92.1% ± 4.5% (p < 0.001), and GTV V100% decreasing from 99.5% ± 0.8% to 95.4% ± 3.2% (p = 0.021). Furthermore, the unintended irradiation of healthy tissue increased, as evidenced by the Mean Liver Dose rising from 13.5 ± 2.1 Gy to 14.8 ± 2.4 Gy (p = 0.038). In contrast, the control group with stable markers (≤ 3 mm) showed no significant dosimetric variations.

Performance of the predictive model

A predictive model incorporating the two independent risk factors (subdiaphragmatic location and tumor diameter > 5 cm) was developed. The ROC and calibration curves for this model are shown in Fig. 5. Following internal validation with 1000 bootstrap resamples, the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.76 (95% CI: 0.72–0.81), indicating robust discriminative ability (Fig. 5A). The Brier score was 0.12, and the calibration plot demonstrated excellent agreement between the predicted probabilities and actual observed frequencies of marker displacement (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The precision of SBRT for HCC relies heavily on the stability of implanted fiducial markers. This study provides a detailed quantitative analysis of the spatiotemporal displacement of these markers and identifies key predictors of instability. Our principal finding is that fiducial markers undergo a dynamic period of positional variation, predominantly within the first week post-implantation, after which they achieve a state of relative positional stability. We also identified that tumors located near the diaphragm and those of larger size are significant risk factors for greater marker displacement. These findings have important implications for optimizing the clinical workflow of liver SBRT.

The observed pattern of early displacement followed by stabilization is consistent with findings from studies in other anatomical sites, such as the prostate and lung [19–21], and is likely driven by the resolution of acute tissue responses to the implantation procedure. The initial trauma of needle insertion can cause localized hemorrhage, edema, and inflammation, which can displace the marker. As these acute effects subside over several days, the marker settles into a more permanent position within the liver parenchyma [12]. Our data suggest that this stabilization process is largely complete by Day 7, as evidenced by the plateauing of the mean displacement curve and the fact that nearly 90% of markers had displaced ≤ 3 mm by this time point. This provides strong quantitative support for the common clinical practice of waiting approximately one week before performing the planning CT scan [21]. Shortening this interval, for instance to 3–5 days, could introduce significant geometric errors, as a substantial proportion of markers are still unstable during this period. However, it is vital to contextualize this finding at the patient level. In our cohort, nearly 12% of patients still had at least one unstable marker on Day 7. In clinical CyberKnife practice, a tracking constellation generally relies on multiple fiducials. If a single marker remains unstable on Day 7, it can often be masked in the tracking algorithm, allowing precise treatment delivery as long as the remaining stable markers provide adequate geometry.

A key finding of our study is the identification of strong predictors for marker instability. The most significant factor was a subdiaphragmatic tumor location. This is clinically intuitive, as tumors in the liver dome are subject to the greatest shear forces and excursion from respiratory motion transmitted by the diaphragm [21]. This constant mechanical stress may hinder the fibrotic anchoring of the marker, leading to greater and more prolonged displacement. Similarly, larger tumors were associated with increased marker displacement. This could be due to several mechanisms, including greater internal heterogeneity, potential for central necrosis leading to less stable tissue for anchoring, or larger respiratory-induced deformation in bulkier tumors [22].

The clinical implications of these findings are twofold. First, they reinforce the necessity of a standardized waiting period. Based on our results, a minimum 7-day interval between fiducial implantation and CT simulation appears to be a safe and evidence-based standard for the majority of HCC patients. Crucially, our dosimetric analysis confirms that ignoring this waiting period and proceeding with markers that subsequently exhibit displacement > 3 mm leads to a severe drop in PTV coverage and an increase in healthy liver irradiation, highlighting the dosimetric peril of geometric uncertainty. Second, our predictive model provides a tool for risk stratification. For patients with high-risk features (e.g., a large tumor in the liver dome), a more cautious approach may be warranted. This could involve extending the waiting period to 10–14 days, performing an additional verification CT scan immediately before treatment planning to confirm stability, or employing daily image guidance with cone-beam CT (CBCT) prior to every fraction during the treatment course to detect and correct for any interfractional marker shift [23]. Furthermore, these patients may be ideal candidates for adaptive radiotherapy (ART) strategies, where the treatment plan is modified during the course of therapy to account for anatomical changes [24, 25].

This study has several strengths, including a large cohort of patients and markers, a standardized imaging protocol with multiple follow-up scans, and a robust statistical analysis employing GEE and internal validation to identify independent predictors. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design is subject to inherent biases. Furthermore, as a retrospective cohort of patients who successfully completed SBRT, extreme cases of marker displacement that resulted in the cancellation or significant delay of SBRT were not captured, potentially introducing a selection bias that underestimates the overall rate of instability. Although our sensitivity analysis indicated no major baseline differences between included and overall excluded patients, high-risk features were highly prevalent in extreme cases. Second, the definition of our endpoint, initially termed ‘marker migration’, encompasses not only the true movement of the gold fiducials within the liver parenchyma but also inherent geometrical uncertainties. These include uncorrected liver deformations, slight variations in respiratory phase matching despite end-exhale gating, and inherent errors of rigid bone-based registration. Therefore, ‘observed marker displacement’ is a more precise characterization of our metric. Third, this was a single-institution study, and our findings may not be generalizable to centers with different implantation techniques or patient populations. Fourth, our dosimetric evaluation has notable limitations. This simplified manual translation of the isocenter only estimates translational errors and fails to capture the intricate realities of CyberKnife tracking, such as rotational shifts, intrinsic liver deformations, inter-marker geometric distortions, and the dynamic dose delivery process over multiple respiratory cycles. Therefore, our dosimetric findings should be interpreted with caution as indicative estimates rather than exact reflections of delivered doses. Consequently, while our data strongly support a 7-day waiting period, further rigorous dosimetric studies are required to establish this as an absolute safety standard. Fifth, we did not analyze the impact of inter-marker drift (changes in distance between markers), which can affect rotational setup corrections, although this is a less common issue with modern 6D couch systems [26].

In conclusion, this study provides a detailed characterization of fiducial marker displacement in HCC patients undergoing SBRT. Markers are most unstable within the first week post-implantation, with stability largely achieved by Day 7. Subdiaphragmatic tumor location and larger tumor size are independent predictors of significant displacement. These data support a recommended standard 7-day waiting period before simulation and highlight a subset of high-risk patients who may require more intensive image guidance and monitoring to ensure the precision, dosimetric integrity, and safety of liver SBRT.
