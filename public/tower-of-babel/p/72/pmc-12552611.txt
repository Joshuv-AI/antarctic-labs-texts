Scour depth estimation using standalone metaheuristic algorithms and their combinations with CatBoost
Authors: Nasrin Eini, Saeid Janizadeh, Sayed M Bateni, Changhyun Jun, Essam Heggy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractScouring around bridge piers poses a critical threat to structural integrity, leading to costly damage and safety risks. Traditional equations often fail to accurately predict equilibrium scour depth (Seq) due to the complexity and nonlinearity of the underlying hydraulic processes. This study proposes two approaches for estimating Seq: (1) optimizing a Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) machine learning model using five metaheuristic algorithms—Harris Hawk Optimization (HHO), Moth–Flame Optimization (MFO), Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA), Pelican Optimization Algorithm (POA), and Fox Optimization Algorithm (FOX); and (2) using the abovementioned optimization methods (i.e., HHO, MFO, WOA, POA, and FOX) to derive explicit equations. Among the hybrid models, HHO–CatBoost achieved the highest accuracy, with a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 0.0286 m, mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.0178 m, and coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9670 during testing. Among the explicit formulations, the HHO-based model excluding the Reynolds number outperformed 18 existing equations, achieving an RMSE of 0.066 m, an MAE of 0.043 m, and an R2 of 0.828. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis identified pier diameter as the most influential factor and critical velocity as the least, while sensitivity analysis highlighted the ratio of pier diameter to flow depth as most important and Reynolds number as the least significant under turbulent flow conditions.Keywords: Scour depth, Bridge pier, Explicit equation, SHAP, CatBoostSubject terms: Civil engineering, Hydrology

Introduction

Scouring is a natural phenomenon caused by the erosive power of water as it flows over riverbeds. From a hydraulic perspective, three mechanisms dictate the formation of scour holes around bridge piers: downflow, horseshoe vortices, and wake vortices1.Water flow encountering a pier generates a stronger pressure gradient, which results in downflow in the area upstream of the pier. The boundary layer of the approaching flow swirls around the tip of the pier, causing three-dimensional separation and generating a horseshoe-shaped vortex near the pier. Additionally, as the flow narrows along the pier’s sides, the shear stress on the riverbed intensifies. This causes the boundary layer to separate near the pier, generating downstream lee-wake vortices. Consequently, scouring is initiated along the edges of the pier1,2 The scouring process reaches equilibrium when the rate of sediment removal matches the rate of sediment deposition. The scour depth at this point is referred to as the equilibrium scour depth (Seq)3. Clear-water scouring typically occurs when the movement of bed materials is absent.

Due to its impact on the stability of a bridge, scouring around bridge piers—specifically the Seq around the bridge foundations—requires comprehensive and accurate evaluation. According to the Federal Highway Administration, over a quarter of the bridges in the United States are structurally and/or functionally obsolete1. Additionally, Wardhana et al.4 who investigated more than 500 bridge collapses that occurred between 1989 and 2000, found that approximately half of these failures were attributable to flood-induced scouring.

Several studies investigated protection performance through experimental approaches5–7. Further research examined the use of hooked-collar devices8–10 and optimized collar configurations to reduce scour around single piers11–13. These works, while primarily centered on protection methods, provide valuable context for understanding broader efforts to address bridge scour.

When designing bridges to avoid such failure, the overestimation of Seq can result in excessive construction costs, whereas underestimation can lead to bridge collapse14. However, the prediction of Seq around bridge piers is complicated due to the numerous factors involved, including the pier geometry, the size and density of the bed materials, the sediment transport mechanisms, the turbulent boundary layer, and the temporally varying flow patterns. Hence, formulating a mathematical equation for the accurate estimation of scour depth is difficult15.

Numerous laboratory experiments have been undertaken to simulate the scour depth around bridge piers16–25. These studies have yielded various regression-based equations for determining Seq26–29. However, Seq and its driving factors share complex and nonlinear relationships, which—when combined with the intrinsic uncertainty of the input data—are difficult for conventional regression-based models to capture30. Additionally, these equations are often fine-tuned through small-scale laboratory experiments conducted in controlled settings involving uniform bed materials and consistent flow depths. Consequently, they may not accurately represent real-world conditions31.

To address the limitations of traditional regression-based equations and predict scour depth more accurately, various machine learning (ML) methods—such as support vector machine (SVM)32, shallow artificial neural networks (ANN)33,34, optimized extreme gradient boosting35 adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems (ANFIS)15,31, gradient tree boosting36,37, CatBoost38,39—have been tested. While these ML models have demonstrated strong predictive capabilities, each presents trade-offs that limit its practical use in engineering design28. ANN and gradient boosting methods such as XGBoost often achieve high accuracy by capturing nonlinear relationships between input variables and the output35,40,41. However, they typically require large datasets, involve considerable computational cost, and lack interpretability42. SVM performs well with smaller, well-structured datasets, but is sensitive to parameter selection and tend to underperform in high-dimensional problems43. ANFIS offers better interpretability due to its rule-based structure, yet it is limited by computational inefficiency and scalability issues when handling numerous input variables44,45. This study aims to overcome the limitations of standalone ML models by integrating CatBoost, a fast, efficient gradient boosting algorithm, with five novel metaheuristic optimization techniques to develop interpretable, high-performance models for estimating Seq.

Although such ML models can capture the complex nonlinear connections between Seq and its causative factors, they cannot produce user-friendly equations for estimating Seq. This is undesirable because simple, explicit equations offer two major advantages over ML-based models: (1) they provide transparent and interpretable insights into the underlying relationships, enhancing the understanding of the factors that influence scour depth, and (2) they are straightforward to use in practical settings. Therefore, several studies have used gene expression programming, genetic functions (GFs), and multiple linear regression (MLR) to develop explicit equations for estimating Seq around circular bridge piers46–48.

Metaheuristic optimization algorithms, such as moth–flame optimization (MFO), Harris hawk optimization (HHO), the whale optimization algorithm (WOA), the pelican optimization algorithm (POA), and the fox optimizer (FOX), can develop explicit equations for the estimation of Seq. They are being increasingly used in engineering projects because they rely on straightforward principles, are simple to execute, do not require information regarding the objective function gradient, and do not become trapped in local minima when searching for global optima49. MFO draws inspiration from the navigational tendencies of moths, particularly their transverse orientation behavior. Notably, MFO has been used to optimize extreme learning machines for estimating daily reference evapotranspiration50 and soil erosion51. The WOA, introduced by Mirjalili and Lewis49, imitates the communal conduct of humpback whales, drawing inspiration from their bubble-net hunting approach. It has been used to improve the accuracy of support vector regression in estimating evapotranspiration52 as well as that of a multilayer perceptron in predicting rainfall53. HHO is an innovative population-driven optimization technique introduced by Heidari et al.54 This algorithm is inspired by the collaborative hunting approach employed by Harris hawks, which involves a surprise pounce. HHO has been used to predict droughts55 and runoff56. Inspired by the hunting behavior of pelicans, the POA alternates between broad exploration and focused exploitation. This dual strategy allows it to efficiently converge to optimal solutions while maintaining diversity in the search process57. The POA has been successfully applied to high-dimensional optimization problems, such as cascade reservoir management58. The FOX algorithm is a novel optimization method inspired by the hunting strategies of foxes. It models the process through which foxes gauge the distance to their prey, enabling precise and effective jumps during the search for optimal solutions59.

The concept of Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) was introduced by Lundberg and Lee60 to enhance the interpretability of ML models. This method involves calculating Shapley values, which quantify the contribution of each input variable to the model’s predictions. SHAP also provides insights into whether each input factor has a positive or negative influence.

This study advances the literature in four major directions. First, we use MFO, WOA, HHO, POA, and FOX to develop explicit equations for estimating Seq around circular bridge piers. These equations leverage a comprehensive dataset containing field and laboratory data. Second, we optimize the hyperparameters of the CatBoost model using the abovementioned optimization algorithms. CatBoost is a fast and efficient boosting algorithm, developed by Yandex61. Third, we conduct a sensitivity analysis to assess the relative importance of different input variables on Seq estimates. Finally, we use SHAP values to interpret the optimized CatBoost model’s behavior and explain the influence of individual features.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to formulate explicit equations for predicting Seq around circular bridge piers using MFO, the WOA, HHO, the POA, and FOX, while also employing hybrid ML models (HHO–CatBoost, MFO–CatBoost, WOA–CatBoost, POA–CatBoost, and FOX–CatBoost), and SHAP for model interpretation. These algorithms were selected for their complementary strengths in global optimization and proven success in related hydraulics applications, providing a robust and novel framework for scour depth prediction. It is worth mentioning that this study focuses on the estimation of Seq around circular bridge piers. The readers are referred to41,62 for the estimation of Seq around non-circular bridge piers.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section “Materials and methods” describes the dataset; the CatBoost, HHO, MFO, WOA, POA, and FOX approaches; the formulation of the equations; and the evaluation metrics. Section “Results and Discussion” then discusses the findings. Finally, the conclusions are provided in Section “Conclusions”.

CatBoost

CatBoost is an advanced ML algorithm within the gradient-boosting decision tree family, renowned for its exceptional ability to process categorical features directly during training, which eliminates the need for extensive preprocessing steps63. This capability enables CatBoost to achieve high efficiency, particularly when applied to datasets containing a mix of numerical and categorical variables. Thus, it frequently outperforms other gradient-boosting algorithms, such as XGBoost and LightGBM, in terms of accuracy and computational performance64.

Traditional gradient-boosting methods often struggle with categorical variables as they require these features to be converted into numerical representations, which can exponentially increase the number of features. CatBoost addresses this challenge by employing a technique known as target statistics65, which converts categorical features into numerical values based on the target variable while using a random permutation of the dataset to prevent target leakage. The process is mathematically expressed as

The encoded value represents a smoothed target statistic for the i-th categorical feature of the q-th instance. It is calculated by averaging the target values across all training examples j for which the feature value matches as indicated by the binary indicator function. To address the issue of data sparsity, especially in low-frequency categories, a prior target value commonly set to the global mean of the target, is introduced into the numerator, weighted by a smoothing parameter ϑ > 0.

This approach minimizes noise, particularly for low-frequency categories, and enhances the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data 63,65. CatBoost also represents a novel method for combining categorical features during the tree-building process. This technique allows the model to capture complex interactions between features, thereby improving its predictive accuracy61. For each split in the decision tree, CatBoost considers all possible combinations of categorical features, enabling the model to uncover intricate patterns and relationships within the data66. To mitigate gradient bias, which is a common problem in traditional gradient-boosting methods, CatBoost employs a technique known as ordered boosting. This method uses a random permutation of the training data to compute gradients, ensuring that the model does not overfit to the training data. By maintaining multiple models for each permutation, CatBoost reduces the risk of overfitting and enhances the model’s stability and accuracy61. The base predictors of CatBoost are oblivious trees, which are balanced decision trees having the same splitting criterion across all levels. This structure reduces the susceptibility of the trees to overfitting and accelerates the computation for predictions. Each leaf in an oblivious tree is encoded as a binary vector, which simplifies the prediction process and improves computational efficiency61.

In this study, we optimized three hyperparameters of CatBoost—iterations, L2 leaf regularization, and learning rate—using five metaheuristic algorithms, namely HHO, MFO, the WOA, the POA, and FOX, as described in the following subsections. To ensure robustness and reduce the risk of overfitting in tuning the hyperparameter of CatBoost, we applied the fivefold cross-validation approach. Each set of potential hyperparameters (iterations, learning rate, and L2 regularization) was evaluated based on the average RMSE across the five validation folds, offering a more reliable estimate of model performance. Prior to model training, all input features were normalized to a [0, 1] range using Min–Max scaling based on the training set statistics. This preprocessing step was critical for maintaining balanced influence among input variables with differing scales and for improving the convergence behavior of ML models. The same scaling parameters were applied to normalize the test set.

Results and discussion

In this study, the Seq around bridge piers was estimated using two approaches: (1) optimizing the CatBoost model using the five abovementioned metaheuristic algorithms (i.e., HHO, MFO, WOA, POA, and FOX), and (2) deriving explicit equations using the same optimization techniques. The following subsections present and compare the results of these methods.

Performance evaluation of optimized CatBoost models

To identify the most suitable ML algorithm for predicting Seq around circular bridge piers, we evaluated several widely adopted models, including LightGBM, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, Random Forest, and CatBoost. Among these, CatBoost achieved the best overall performance, yielding the lowest RMSE (0.0605 m), the highest R2 (0.8527), and the lowest MAE (0.0245 m) on the test dataset (Table 2). Based on these results, CatBoost was selected as the base model for further improvement using metaheuristic optimization techniques.

The hybrid MFO–CatBoost, POA–CatBoost, FOX–CatBoost, and WOA–CatBoost models also yielded robust results, even though they exhibited slightly higher RMSE and MAE values than HHO–CatBoost. In contrast, the baseline CatBoost model, which was not optimized using any of the aforementioned methods, performed very poorly, particularly in the testing phase. It had an RMSE of 0.0605 m, an MAE of 0.0245 m, and a notably low R2 of 0.8527. These results emphasize that integrating optimization methods with the baseline CatBoost model improves its performance.

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied to the R2 values obtained from five-fold cross-validation to compare all models. For the ANOVA test, we compared the mean R2 values of the five-fold cross-validation for all hybrid models (HHO–CatBoost, MFO–CatBoost, POA–CatBoost, WOA–CatBoost, FOX–CatBoost). The resulting F-statistic was 0.131, with a p-value of 0.969. This indicates that the differences in the performance of various hybrid models are not statistically significant, suggesting similar predictive capability from all models. To further evaluate whether HHO–CatBoost significantly outperformed other models on a fold-by-fold basis, we applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. We compared HHO–CatBoost with other models. The resulting p-values ranged from 0.1875 to 0.4375, all are above the 0.05 threshold61. This further supports the conclusion that the differences in R2 of various models, are not statistically significant. Together, these tests suggest that while HHO–CatBoost achieved the highest average R2 across the five-fold cross-validation, the performance margin relative to other models are not significant statistically. However, all hybrid models outperformed the standalone CatBoost model by a substantial margin in terms of average R2 and error metrics, reinforcing the practical value of metaheuristic-based optimization.

Figure 5 presents the SHAP analysis results for HHO–CatBoost, highlighting the contributions of the input features to the Seq prediction. SHAP is a method derived from cooperative game theory that explains the predictions of ML models by quantifying the average marginal contribution of each feature to the output60. The bar chart in Fig. 5 ranks the input features based on their mean absolute SHAP values, which represent their importance in predicting Seq. Pier diameter exhibited the highest mean SHAP value, which indicates that it is the most influential factor in determining scour depth. Flow velocity and flow depth followed as other significant contributors, although their impact is considerably less pronounced than that of pier diameter. Median sediment size and critical velocity displayed lower mean SHAP values, which suggests that their influence on the predictions is minor. Based on this analysis, geometric and hydraulic parameters, particularly D and V, are critical for accurately modeling Seq using HHO–CatBoost. This inference aligns with previous studies on the critical role of pier diameter in Seq prediction33,35,41,74,75.

Notably, points with higher flow depth (marked in red) amplify this effect, particularly at intermediate-to-large diameters. This interaction reveals that the combined increase in pier diameter and flow depth strongly contributes to scour risk, which aligns with physical expectations in hydraulic behavior around bridge piers. The consistency in feature importance and interaction patterns provides physically meaningful insights that align with established hydraulic theory. For instance, the strong influence of pier diameter and its interaction with flow depth corresponds to the known relationship between increased pier obstruction and intensified vortex-induced scour. These insights support practical implications for design, suggesting that piers with larger diameters and/or located in high-flow regimes require greater consideration for scour countermeasures, especially during design-stage assessments when site-specific flow characteristics are uncertain.

As shown in Table 7, M2, which excludes Re, outperformed the other models (i.e., M1 and M3–M6) under all five optimization methods, which suggests that Re has a negligible impact on Seq prediction. This could be because Re represents the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces, with the latter being relatively negligible due to the high turbulence levels in most rivers76. This observation concurs with the findings of33,35,75,77. Eliminating Re reduces the number of unknown coefficients, allowing the optimization algorithms to estimate fewer calibration coefficients more robustly. Regarding the optimization strategies for M2, HHO produced the most accurate Seq estimates (RMSE = 0.066 m, MAE = 0.043 m, and R2 = 0.828), with the RMSE being 13.55%, 13.55%, 10.00%, 14.68%, and 80.96% lower than those for M1-HHO, M3-HHO, M4-HHO, M5-HHO, and M6-HHO, respectively. Additionally, the RMSE of M2-HHO is 21.79%, 19.29%, 17.88%, 16.84%, and 81.84% lower than those of M1-WOA, M3-WOA, M4-WOA, M5-WOA, and M6-WOA; 23.60%, 24.48%, 18.89%, 23.60%, and 81.49% lower than those of M1-MFO, M3-MFO, M4-MFO, M5-MFO, and M6-MFO; 23.26%, 18.52%, 18.52%, 23.26%, and 81.46% lower than those of M1-POA, M3-POA, M4-POA, M5-POA, and M6-POA; and 24.14%, 16.46%, 14.29%, 14.29%, and 80.12% lower than those of M1-FOX, M3-FOX, M4-FOX, M5-FOX, and M6-FOX, respectively.

In contrast, M6—where D/Y is removed—exhibited the weakest performance, which emphasizes the significance of D/Y in estimating Seq around circular bridge piers. This is because the pier diameter and flow depth both affect scour depth more than other variables33,35,75,78. M6-HHO exhibited RMSE, MAE, and R2 values of 0.345 m, 0.133 m, and 0.662, respectively.

As observed in Fig. 7, the Seq estimates of M2 exhibited lower dispersion around the 45° line, which highlights the superior performance of this model. Similarly, the wider distribution of the Seq predictions of M6, particularly for Seq > 0.8 m, underscores the importance of D/Y in Seq estimation. With all five optimization methods, M1, M3, and M5 tended to underestimate Seq when it exceeded 0.6 m, while M6 overestimated it significantly in such cases. This confirms that M6 would be unreliable and economically infeasible as a predictive model.

The superiority of HHO over the WOA, MFO, the POA, and FOX in formulating equations to predict Seq around circular bridge piers highlights the usefulness of incorporating a search technique modeled on the cooperative hunting strategy of Harris hawks. Specifically, HHO effectively balances exploration and exploitation in landscapes with multiple variables54, thus avoiding premature convergence and stagnation in local optima. By incorporating a dynamic and randomized escape energy parameter and various mechanisms based on the mean position of the hawks, HHO promotes exploration in the early stages54. It also uses adaptive and time-varying parameters, which allow it to manage complications within the exploration area, including multiple modes and misleading optima54.

Figure 8 shows the variation in the RMSE of Seq/Y estimates from the five optimized versions of M2 versus the number of iterations. HHO outperformed the WOA and MFO, exhibiting a higher convergence rate. Specifically, the fitness functions of MFO, FOX, and the WOA reached an asymptotic state after approximately 300, 300, and 700 iterations, respectively, while the asymptotic state occurred for HHO in fewer than 50 iterations. The faster convergence of HHO to the optimal solution is attributable to the dynamic and randomized nature of its escape energy parameter, which enhances its exploration and exploitation strategies and allows it to seamlessly shift between exploration and exploitation phases54. The POA also exhibited faster convergence than MFO, the WOA, and FOX. This algorithm balances the trade-off between exploration and exploitation effectively, which is essential for efficient optimization and fast convergence57.

Conclusions

In this study, we present the application of five metaheuristic optimization algorithms—HHO, MFO, WOA, POA, and FOX—for estimating Seq around circular bridge piers, both by deriving explicit equations, and optimizing the CatBoost model to enhance its predictive accuracy. A dataset comprising 841 datapoints from laboratory and field experiments under clear-water conditions was used for training and testing. The results demonstrated that both approaches provide significant improvements in Seq prediction accuracy over traditional methods. Among six models (M1–M6) tested, M2—which excludes Re—consistently achieved the highest accuracy across all five optimization methods. The explicit equation derived for M2 using HHO (M2-HHO) performed the best, with an RMSE of 0.066 m, an MAE of 0.043 m, and an R2 of 0.828. Additionally, sensitivity analyses revealed the pier diameter–flow depth ratio (D/Y) to be the most influential variable in Seq prediction, with Reynolds number (Re) having minimal impact. The dynamic nature of HHO enables convergence to optimal solutions at a lower iteration number compared with other optimization methods.

In addition to formulating explicit equations, we integrated the metaheuristic optimizations (HHO, WOA, MFO, FOX, and POA) with CatBoost to create hybrid models. The HHO–CatBoost model achieved superior performance, with an RMSE of 0.0286 m, an MAE of 0.0178 m, and an R2 of 0.9670 during testing. A SHAP analysis confirmed that pier diameter is the most critical factor influencing Seq predictions in the HHO–CatBoost model. Furthermore, Taylor diagrams and violin plots verified that both M2-HHO and HHO–CatBoost outperform traditional regression-based studies such as, such as Wilson, Melville, HEC-18, and Ghumman et al. in terms of accuracy and variability representation. These findings highlight the effectiveness of combining advanced ML techniques with metaheuristic optimizations for Seq prediction.

The explicit equations constitute user-friendly tools for practical engineering applications, while the hybrid ML models offer unparalleled accuracy. Thus, this work can contribute to improving public safety by enabling reliable bridge designs while optimizing construction costs through accurate scour depth predictions.

While the proposed hybrid models offer high accuracy and interpretability, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the dataset used includes both laboratory and field measurements, it is still limited in size relative to the variability seen in natural river systems. This may affect the generalizability of the results when applied to bridge sites with highly different hydraulic or sediment conditions. Expanding the dataset to include more diverse field cases and pier geometries would further validate the applicability of the models across broader engineering scenarios. Second, the optimization algorithms used can be sensitive to initial parameters and search space boundaries. While we used consistent parameter settings across all methods for fairness, further tuning might reveal additional performance difference.
