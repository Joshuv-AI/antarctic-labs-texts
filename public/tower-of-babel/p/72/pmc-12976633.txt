Dynamics of Varicella Zoster Virus-Specific Immune Reconstitution and Impact of Varicella Vaccination in Pediatric Allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplant Recipients
Authors: Gahee Kim, Sanghoon Lee, Yonghee Lee, Reenar Yoo, Jung Hwa Kim, Sung Han Kang, Hyery Kim, Kyung Nam Koh, Ho Joon Im, Jina Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaricella-zoster virus infection poses a significant risk to pediatric hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients owing to compromised T-cell-mediated immunity. Although varicella-zoster virus-specific T-cell-mediated immunity is crucial for preventing reactivation, its reconstitution following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant in pediatric patients remains poorly characterized.MethodsThis prospective study assessed varicella-zoster virus-specific T-cell-mediated immunity in 32 pediatric allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients pre-transplant and 1 and 3 months post-transplant using enzyme-linked immune absorbent spot assays. An extension study assessed varicella-zoster virus-specific T-cell-mediated immunity 2 years post-transplant in 15 recipients, including 8 after a single dose of varicella vaccination.ResultsAmong the 32 recipients (median age, 10.5 years), only one experienced post-hematopoietic stem cell transplant varicella-zoster virus infection. Median varicella-zoster virus-specific spot-forming cells were low during the first 3 months but increased significantly at 2 years (0 vs. 5.0/2.0 × 105 peripheral blood mononuclear cells; P = 0.005), more evident in recipients with pre-hematopoietic stem cell transplant varicella-zoster virus R+ serostatus (P = 0.006) and no prior varicella-zoster virus infection (P < 0.001). The presence of varicella-zoster virus-specific T-cell-mediated immunity (≥ 1.0 spot-forming cells/2.0 × 105 peripheral blood mononuclear cells) did not differ significantly by pre-hematopoietic stem cell transplant T-cell-mediated immunity status, serostatus, or vaccination history. Among seven recipients assessed at all five time points, recovery-level responses (≥ 5.0 spot-forming cells) increased from 28.6% pre-vaccination to 71.4% post-vaccination.ConclusionVaricella-zoster virus-specific T-cell-mediated immunity in pediatric hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients remained low in the first 3 months but recovered significantly by 2 years post-hematopoietic stem cell transplant, with a boosting effect from varicella vaccination. Further studies are needed to assess the clinical significance of T-cell-mediated immunity and to guide personalized prevention strategies.Keywords: Varicella-Zoster Virus, T Cell Immunity, Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation, Children, Varicella Vaccines, Acyclovir Prophylaxis

INTRODUCTION

Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infection occurs in 13–55% of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) recipients when prophylactic acyclovir is not administered, with approximately 25% of pediatric HSCT recipients experiencing VZV infection within the first year post-transplantation.1,2,3 Although most VZV infections, primarily resulting from reactivation, occur within 3–12 months after HSCT, approximately one-third may be late-onset, occurring between 1 and 3 years post-HSCT.2,4 Antiviral prophylaxis may reduce VZV disease incidence by 2–21%.5 Although acyclovir prophylaxis is recommended for VZV-seropositive (VZV R+) HSCT recipients, its implementation and duration vary across institutions.6,7,8,9 Moreover, evidence on whether discontinuation of prophylaxis impacts the rebound of VZV infections is conflicting.10,11

VZV-specific T-cell-mediated immunity (CMI) is essential for controlling VZV reactivation, and its impairment may cause severe VZV infections despite antiviral therapy.12,13 In allogeneic HSCT recipients, VZV-specific CMI remains suppressed, and recovery is influenced by VZV reactivation and vaccination.14,15,16 Recipients who experience VZV reactivation have lower pre-HSCT T-cell counts, especially in the CD4+ subset, than those without reactivation. Thus, pre-HSCT immunosuppression may contribute to post-HSCT VZV reactivation.3 However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have specifically investigated the recovery of VZV-specific T-cell immunity before and after HSCT in pediatric recipients.

We evaluated the VZV-specific CMI reconstitution using an interferon (IFN)-γ enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) assay at pre-HSCT, 1, and 3 months post-HSCT, to investigate whether VZV-specific CMI within the first 3 months could predict the risk of VZV infection and guide personalized prophylaxis strategies in pediatric allogenic HSCT recipients. Additionally, the study was extended to assess VZV-specific CMI at 2 years post-HSCT, primarily before varicella vaccination and in some patients after a single vaccine dose.

METHODS

Study population

This prospective study was conducted at Asan Medical Center Children’s Hospital, a 270-bed tertiary referral hospital in Seoul, Republic of Korea. Patients aged ≤ 19 years who underwent allogeneic HSCT between April 2019 and February 2020 were enrolled and followed-up for at least 2 years post-transplantation, or until recurrence or death. The exclusion criteria were refused consent, multiple HSCTs, and engraftment failure within 3 months post-HSCT.

Clinical and demographic data including age; sex; underlying disease; donor type (matched sibling, matched unrelated, and haploidentical); conditioning regimen (myeloablation vs. reduced intensity); prophylaxis for graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD); pre-transplant recipient/donor VZV serostatus; duration of acyclovir prophylaxis; and clinical outcomes, such as PTLD, GVHD, disease recurrence, and death, were extracted from electronic medical records. Information on prior varicella infection or VZV vaccination before and after post-HSCT VZV was also collected.

At our center, rabbit antithymocyte globulin has been used for conditioning regimen since 2005.17 For haploidentical HSCT recipients, rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, was administered on the day following HSCT as a PTLD prophylaxis. T-cell depletion was performed via negative selection using the CliniMACS® system (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch-Gladbach, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.18 GVHD prophylaxis consisted of mycophenolate mofetil for haploidentical recipients and a combination of cyclosporine and methotrexate for matched recipients.

VZV serostatus and disease

VZV serostatus pre-HSCT was assessed using a chemiluminescence immunoassay (DiaSorin, Saluggia, Italy), with IgG titer ≥ 150 mIU/mL considered positive.19 Recipients aged < 1 year were considered seronegative regardless of the assay results owing to the potential persistence of transplacentally transferred maternal antibodies up to 12 months of age. At 2 years post-HSCT, the VZV serostatus was re-evaluated in a subset of patients, as only a portion of the original cohort participated in the extended follow-up beyond 3 months post-transplantation.

VZV disease incidence was monitored by attending physicians for up to 2 years post-HSCT or until the patients received varicella vaccination, experienced disease recurrence, or died, whichever occurred first. VZV disease was diagnosed based on characteristic clinical manifestations, with or without confirmation by VZV-specific polymerase chain reaction or histopathological examination.

Antiviral prevention strategy

Acyclovir was administered for 1 year to haploidentical HSCT recipients and to VZV R+ recipients with a prior history of VZV infection for the prophylaxis of herpes simplex virus (HSV) and VZV. Matched HSCT recipients who were VZV R+ without prior VZV infection received acyclovir until day 100 post-HSCT. Recipients who were HSV-seronegative and VZV-seronegative (HSV R− and VZV R−) received acyclovir for 30 days post-HSCT. In cases where universal cytomegalovirus (CMV) prophylaxis was provided—using ganciclovir or foscarnet—for CMV R+ haploidentical HSCT recipients, no additional anti-VZV agents were administered.

Varicella vaccination after HSCT

Pediatric HSCT recipients received two doses of live attenuated varicella vaccine (Sky Varicella; SK Bioscience, Incheon, Korea) during the study period. Each dose, containing 2,400–40,000 plaque-forming units of the attenuated Oka/SK strain, was administered at least 3 months apart, beginning 2 years after HSCT, provided that the patient was not receiving immunosuppressive therapy.

Evaluation of VZV-specific CMI

VZV-specific CMI was evaluated at a minimum of three time points: before HSCT and at 1 and 3 months post-HSCT. In an optional extension study, VZV-specific CMI was additionally assessed 2 years post-HSCT during routine follow-up visits, primarily before varicella vaccination. In some patients, testing was performed after a single varicella vaccination. The CMI responses following the second varicella vaccine dose were not included in the present study.

Peripheral venous blood samples (up to 5 mL) were collected to detect VZV-specific IFN-γ-producing T cells using an ELISPOT assay (MABTECH, Nacka Strand, Sweden). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were stimulated with phytohemagglutinin (positive control; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), Medical Research Council cell strain 5 (MRC-5) control antigen (Microbix Biosystems Inc., Mississauga, Canada), VZV grade 2 antigen (Microbix Biosystems Inc.), and medium only (negative control), and then incubated for 12 hours. The resulting spots were counted by a trained researcher using an automated microscope. The number of spots in the VZV antigen-stimulated wells was calculated by subtracting the spot counts in MRC-5 and negative control wells. Results were expressed as spot-forming cells (SFCs) per 2.0 × 105 PBMCs. VZV-specific CMI was arbitrarily defined as present at ≥ 1.0 SFCs/2.0 × 105 PBMCs and as recovered at ≥ 5.0 SFCs/2.0 × 105 PBMCs.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for repeated measures, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between independent groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A linear-by-linear association was used to assess trends.

Subgroup analyses—such as those based on pre-HSCT factors, antiviral prophylaxis, and donor type—were analyzed using nonparametric tests. However, only descriptive statistics were provided for the subgroup evaluating the impact of varicella vaccination due to its small sample size. All statistical tests were two-sided, and significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (2019-0173 and 2021-0996). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of HSCT.

RESULTS

Study population

Between April 2019 and February 2020, 49 recipients aged ≤ 19 years underwent allogeneic HSCT (Fig. 1). Of these, 17 were excluded because of lack of consent (n = 4), multiple HSCTs (n = 7), or engraftment failure within 3 months (n = 6). The remaining 32 patients were included, with a median follow-up duration of 29 months (IQR, 24.0–33.0). All 32 recipients underwent VZV-specific CMI analysis pre-HSCT and at 1 and 3 months post-HSCT. In an extension study, 15 recipients were evaluated for VZV-specific CMI at 2 years post-HSCT, prior to varicella vaccination, and among them, eight underwent an additional test at least 1 month after a single dose of varicella vaccine.

The median age of the 32 enrolled recipients was 10.5 years (IQR, 5.9–15.2) (Table 1). A total of 53.1% (17/32) of patients underwent HSCT for malignant diseases, and 37.5% (12/32) underwent haploidentical HSCT. Before HSCT, 90.6% (29/32) had received varicella vaccination, and 18.8% (6/32) had a history of natural VZV infection. Of the 26 recipients without confirmed VZV infection, 19 reported no prior history and seven had an unknown status; thus, all were classified as having no definite history of prior VZV infection. Antiviral prophylaxis was administered to all recipients, with 37.5% (12/32) receiving it for up to 3 months and the remaining 62.5% (20/32) continued prophylaxis for up to 1 year. Acute GVHD occurred in 53.1% (17/32) of the patients, and the overall mortality rate was 12.5% (4/32). Only one case of VZV infection occurred after HSCT, with no associated mortality.

VZV serostatus pre- and post-HSCT

Prior to HSCT, 81.3% (26/32) of the recipients were VZV R+, including five with a history of both natural VZV infection and varicella vaccination, and 19 with a history of vaccination alone (Fig. 1). Two seropositive recipients had no documented history of natural infection or vaccination but had recently received red blood cell transfusions. Donor VZV serostatuses were available for 25 of the 32 donor–recipient pairs (Table 1).

At 2 years post-HSCT, 14 recipients underwent VZV IgG testing. Among the 11 recipients who were seropositive before HSCT, only three remained seropositive—including one who developed herpes zoster—while the remaining eight became seronegative. None of the three initial VZV R− recipients tested at 2 years post-HSCT underwent seroconversion. Among them, two had received transplants from VZV R+ donors (D+/R−), yet both remained seronegative at that time.

Six recipients underwent post-vaccination IgG testing, five of whom had paired pre- and post-vaccination samples and all seroconverted. The remaining recipient had no pre-vaccination IgG testing and was seronegative after vaccination.

Changes in VZV-specific CMI over time following HSCT

The presence of VZV-specific CMI (≥ 1.0 SFC/2.0 × 105 PBMCs) was observed in 46.9% (15/32) of recipients at pre-HSCT, 50.0% (16/32) at 1 month, and 46.9% (15/32) at 3 months, increasing to 80.0% (12/15) at 2 years post-HSCT prior to vaccination (Table 2; P for trend = 0.123). Recovery of VZV-specific CMI (≥ 5.0 SFC/2.0 × 105 PBMCs) was initially low—12.5% (4/32) within the first 3 months—but increased to 53.3% (8/15) at 2 years post-HSCT (P for trend = 0.062).

Subgroup analysis by pre-HSCT factors, antiviral prophylaxis, and donor type

Considering pre-HSCT factors, such as VZV serostatus, prior VZV infection, and presence of pre-HSCT VZV-specific CMI, increases in SFCs appeared more pronounced among VZV R+ recipients and those without prior VZV infection, whereas no clear difference was noted based on pre-HSCT CMI status (Fig. 2B-D).

No significant differences in the presence of VZV-specific CMI were observed at any time point based on pre-HSCT factors, including VZV serostatus, history of VZV infection, presence of pre-HSCT VZV-specific CMI, or varicella vaccination status (Table 3). However, at 2 years post-HSCT, prior to vaccination, the proportion of recipients showing the presence of VZV-specific CMI was higher among recipients without a history of VZV infection than among those with a prior infection (100% [10/10] vs. 40% [2/5], P = 0.022).

Impact of a single dose of varicella vaccination

Among the eight recipients who underwent CMI testing at least 1 month after a single dose of varicella vaccine, seven had paired pre- and post-vaccination results, and one was tested only after vaccination. In the seven recipients with paired data, VZV-specific CMI remained largely undetectable during the first 3 months post-HSCT, with a median SFC of 0.0 (IQR, 0.0–4.0), and none achieved recovery by that time (Table 4).

By 2 years post-HSCT, prior to vaccination, a modest increase in CMI was observed (median SFCs 1.0 [IQR, 0.0–5.0]), with 57.1% (4/7) of recipients exhibiting CMI presence and 28.6% meeting the recovery threshold. Following a single dose of varicella vaccine, all seven recipients (100.0%) demonstrated VZV-specific CMI, and 71.4% (5/7) recovered, with a marked increase in median SFCs to 5.0 (IQR, 2.0–17.0).

Among the same seven recipients who underwent VZV serologic testing 2 years post-HSCT, six were seronegative regardless of their pre-HSCT VZV serostatus, and one did not undergo IgG testing at that time. Of these, five underwent antibody testing following a single dose of the varicella vaccine, and all showed seroconversion. The remaining recipient who underwent antibody testing only after vaccination was seronegative.

VZV-specific CMI in a recipient with VZV reactivation

VZV reactivation occurred in only one patient. A 12-year-old male recipient who underwent fully matched HSCT for aplastic anemia developed herpes zoster at 23 months post-HSCT. The patient received immunosuppressive therapy for 19 months post-HSCT for acute and chronic GVHD and PTLD, along with 12 months of antiviral prophylaxis. No known exposure to varicella was identified.

This recipient, classified as VZV R+ because of a history of both chickenpox and varicella vaccination, had VZV-specific SFCs of 0.0, 1.0, and 0.0 per 2.0 × 105 PBMCs at pre-HSCT, 1 month, and 3 months post-HSCT, respectively.

At 3 months after the herpes zoster episode, his VZV-specific CMI increased to 2.0 SFCs/2.0 × 105 PBMCs, and he remained VZV seropositive.

DISCUSSION

In this study, VZV-specific CMI in pediatric allogeneic HSCT recipients demonstrated a delayed yet significant recovery. CMI remained low until 3 months post-HSCT, but increased by 2 years, even without symptomatic VZV infections.

Immunocompromised patients, including HSCT and solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients, generally exhibit reduced VZV-specific SFCs compared with that exhibited by healthy individuals.15,20,21,22,23 Among SOT recipients, adult kidney transplant recipients showed low VZV-specific CMI up to day 60, with recovery observed by day 180,21 whereas pediatric SOT recipients maintained low levels for over a year.15 Adults who undergo T-cell-depleted allogeneic HSCT have persistently low VZV-specific T-cell levels within the first year after HSCT, although data on pediatric HSCT recipients are limited.14

In line with previous findings, our study observed low VZV-specific CMI levels during the early post-HSCT period, with a modest increase in SFCs by 2 years post-HSCT, even in patients who did not receive varicella vaccination or experienced clinical VZV infection after HSCT. VZV-specific CMI did not increase significantly compared to CMV-specific CMI following HSCT.24

Although VZV-specific T-cell immunity, particularly through CD4+ cells, plays a protective role,25,26 its impairment does not always result in symptomatic disease. In this study, symptomatic VZV reactivation was absent during the initial 12-month period despite subdued VZV-specific CMI. This paradox mirrors the findings in patients with HIV, where severe CD4 depletion was inversely correlated with herpes zoster.27 Thus, caution should be exercised in using VZV-specific CMI alone as a predictive marker for reactivation.

No significant difference in the presence of VZV-specific CMI was observed between recipients who received prophylaxis until day 100 and those who received prophylaxis until day 365 post-HSCT. However, the impact of antiviral duration on immune reconstitution remains unclear. Acyclovir effectively prevents symptomatic VZV reactivation without significantly impairing VZV-specific T-cell immunity reconstitution.28 This underscores the potential benefits of antivirals combined with systematic CMI monitoring to support personalized prevention strategies.

VZV-specific immune reconstitution remains insufficient in certain populations, such as VZV R− and those with prior VZV infection. As reactivation or persistence of latent antigens from prior infection or vaccination may facilitate the expansion of VZV-specific T cells, the VZV R− group may delay CMI recovery. However, delayed recovery in patients with prior VZV infection should be interpreted cautiously because of the possibility of underdiagnosis or recall errors of prior mild or paucisymptomatic varicella. At 3 months post-HSCT, the proportion of VZV-specific CMI was lower in haploidentical HSCT recipients than in matched recipients. This may reflect delayed immune reconstitution, potentially because of intensified GVHD and PTLD prophylaxis, including the use of mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab, as well as T-cell depletion, all of which were common in the haploidentical group.

The current guidelines recommend varicella vaccination for pediatric HSCT recipients who are seronegative for VZV and lack a history of chickenpox or prior vaccination because HSCT does not eliminate latent VZV in the sensory nerve ganglia.29 Varicella vaccination supports the reconstitution of VZV-specific T-cell immunity even in immunocompromised patients.5,14,26,28,30,31 In our study, 72.7% of VZV R+ recipients who underwent serologic testing 2 years post-HSCT were seronegative, despite a history of varicella or prior vaccination. However, all recipients exhibited a substantial immunological response to a single dose of the varicella vaccine, both in terms of cellular and humoral immunity. Thus, the broader application of varicella vaccination may have a boosting effect, regardless of serostatus or vaccination status. Future studies should assess the effect of two doses of varicella vaccination on enhancing VZV-specific CMI and to determine whether broader varicella vaccination strategies could improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

This study had some limitations. First, the questionnaire-based assessment of prior varicella infections and vaccinations may have been inaccurate, especially in older children, potentially affecting the interpretation of factors associated with VZV-specific CMI. Second, this single-center study as well as the extended study had small sample sizes, restricting the generalizability of the findings. The original study focused on changes in VZV-specific CMI during the first 3 months post-HSCT. However, owing to the minimal early recovery of CMI, the study period was extended to 2 years post-HSCT to further assess the dynamics of varicella-specific CMI. Nevertheless, only approximately half of the initial participants were enrolled in the 2-year follow-up analysis, and approximately half of them underwent evaluation of immune responses after a single dose of the varicella vaccine. Although two doses of varicella vaccine are generally recommended for HSCT recipients, data following the second dose are not available, limiting the conclusions that can be drawn regarding vaccine-induced CMI. Third, we used ELISPOT, a widely adopted and sensitive method for measuring VZV-specific CMI. However, it is not standardized across laboratories and does not allow immunophenotyping of responding T-cell subsets.15 Complementary approaches, such as flow cytometry, can elucidate the functional characteristics of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells involved in VZV-specific immune reconstitution. Lastly, the lack of a control group, such as age-matched healthy individuals, and the low VZV disease incidence hinder the definition of a threshold for clinically significant VZV-specific immune recovery. Nevertheless, this study offers valuable insights into VZV-specific T-cell immune recovery in pediatric HSCT recipients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to longitudinally monitor VZV-specific CMI reconstitution in pediatric HSCT recipients over an extended period.

This study highlights the dynamics of VZV-specific T-cell immune reconstitution in pediatric allogeneic HSCT recipients. Although VZV-specific CMI remained low during the first 3 months post-HSCT, significant recovery by 2 years was observed, with an expected boost effect from varicella vaccination. Large-scale studies are needed to clarify the clinical significance of VZV-specific CMI and to guide effective prevention strategies including antiviral therapy and vaccination.
