Genome-resolved gut microbial guild and fecal metabolic signatures associated with post-weaning estrus return in sows
Authors: Pan Jiang, Mengqing Zhou, Yang Wen, Zikang Hu, Yongqiang Hu, Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-weaning estrus return is critical for sow reproductive efficiency. The gut microbiota is associated with post-weaning estrus of sows, potentially through effects on nutrient utilization and metabolic regulation. However, current microbial signatures associated with estrus return remain poorly resolved at the strain-level. Here, we explored the relationship between the gut microbiome and post-weaning estrus in sows using metagenomics and metabolomics profiling of 85 fecal samples. From 2,704 non-redundant metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), 608 estrus-associated MAGs were identified by LEfSe analysis. Among these, 48 high-quality MAGs were selected for co-abundance network analysis, which revealed two competing microbial functional guilds. Guild 1 was significantly enriched in the normal group, harboring more β-glucosidase and folate biosynthesis genes, but fewer antibiotic resistance genes and virulence factors than Guild 2. A random forest model based on these 48 MAGs demonstrated excellent performance in distinguishing between the normal and non-return sows (AUROC = 0.946) and was validated in an independent dataset (n = 29, AUROC = 0.818). Additionally, the guild-level microbiome index (GMI) derived from abundance differences between the two guilds also showed good discriminatory power (AUROC = 0.799). Integrated multi-omics analysis revealed alterations in fecal bile acid metabolism in non-return sows, characterized by a significantly increased ratio of secondary to primary bile acids and the accumulation of specific secondary bile acids. Notably, the enrichment of the Clostridia strain SFHK01 sp016296675, a member of Guild 2, and its encoded 12α-HSDH gene was positively associated with specific secondary bile acids, suggesting that this specific strain is involved in the distinct metabolic alterations observed in non-return sows. Introduction

Sow reproductive performance is a critical determinant of economic profitability in pig farming, with the weaning-to-estrus interval (WEI) serving as a key indicator of breeding efficiency. The majority of sows return to estrus within 7 days after weaning [1, 2]. Prolonged WEI not only reduces pigs per sow per year (PSY) but also increases non-productive days (NPD) and feeding costs, resulting in significant economic losses to the swine industry [3]. Post-weaning estrus return is tightly regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis, which remains suppressed during lactation [4]. After weaning, declining prolactin levels restore gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion, which triggers the release of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), promoting follicular development and estradiol synthesis for estrus behavior [5].

Recent advances in microbiome research have revealed the critical role of the gut microbiota in regulating host metabolism and physiological functions. Mounting evidence demonstrates that the gut microbiota can significantly influence reproductive function through the modulation of hormonal metabolism [6, 7]. In post-weaning sows, gut microbiota dysbiosis is associated with impaired nutrient utilization, disrupted energy metabolism, and hormone imbalance, which are linked to a delayed return to estrus. Studies have reported that Bacillus supplementation can modulate serum reproductive hormone concentrations, the gut microbiota and its metabolites, significantly shortening the WEI [8, 9]. Our previous studies revealed that bacterial species Lactobacillus reuteri and Prevotella spp. affect the estrus return of weaned sows the via regulation of sex steroid hormone metabolism [10]. These findings provide a foundation for understanding the associations between the gut microbiota and post-weaning estrus return in sows at the species level.

However, species-level resolution may be insufficient to fully capture the intraspecific functional diversity in microbial communities. As we learn more about the gut microbiome, it has become clear that even strains within the same species can exhibit substantial differences in metabolic and physiological functions. For instance, Prevotella copri exhibits strain-level diversity with distinct genetic profiles and carbohydrate metabolism capabilities that are associated with dietary patterns [11, 12]. Similarly, Klebsiella pneumoniae displays remarkable strain-level heterogeneity in virulence factors and antibiotic resistance gene profiles [13]. Traditional analytical approaches rely on reference databases for taxonomic annotation and often fail to capture these strain-specific functional capability differences, leading to contradictory research findings. This is exemplified by Prevotella copri, which has been inconsistently associated with disease outcomes across studies [14].

To address these limitations, the metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) approach provides a database-independent strategy that not only identifies novel or understudied disease-associated microbes, but also enables strain-level functional characterization and metabolic network reconstruction [15–17]. Given the complexity of the gut microbiota, microbial functional modules (guilds) based on co-abundance patterns enable community-level analysis of ecological interactions and functions [18, 19], providing comprehensive insights into the mechanisms underlying microbiota-mediated regulation of host physiological functions. Importantly, while previous studies have identified specific bacterial species influencing post-weaning estrus in sows through steroid hormone metabolism [10], whether other gut microbes are involved in this process via additional metabolic pathways remains largely unexplored.

This study employed genome-based analyses to identify the microbial signatures associated with post-weaning estrus return in sows at both strain and community levels. Specifically, we de novo assembled 2,704 non-redundant MAGs (nrMAGs) and identified two competing guilds consisting of 48 high-quality MAGs (HQMAGs) that were significantly associated with estrus return. These 48 HQMAGs demonstrated good and robust discriminatory performance in both the discovery and independent validation cohorts, highlighting their potential as microbial biomarkers. Furthermore, by integrating metagenomics and metabolomics, we identified that gut microbiota-mediated alterations in fecal bile acid metabolism are associated with the failure to return to estrus. These findings provide a valuable foundation for future causal validation and the development of microbiota-targeted strategies to improve sow reproductive performance.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and sample collection

A total of 114 Landrace × Large White sows with an average parity of 5.54 ± 1.85 were used in this study. The animals were obtained from two independent standardized commercial farms in Fujian Province, China, comprising a discovery cohort (n = 85) and a validation cohort (n = 29). Both farms maintained similar management and environmental conditions. All sows were provided with a standardized commercial diet consisting of 67% corn, 26% soybean meal, 1% soybean oil, and 6% lactating sow premix (nutrient composition detailed in Table S1) and had ad libitum access to water via nipple drinkers. All animals were healthy and had not received antibiotics or probiotics for at least two months before sampling. The housing facilities at both sites were enclosed barns equipped with mechanical ventilation systems. Sows were housed in individual farrowing crates during lactation and were weaned at day 28, after which they were moved to group-housed gestation pens. On the day of weaning, a total of 114 fecal samples were collected from the two cohorts. Sampling was performed as previously described [20]. Briefly, approximately 2 g of feces was manually collected from the rectum of each sow into a 2 mL sterile centrifuge tube. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C for DNA extraction and metabolomic analyses. Estrus was monitored twice daily (08:00 and 16:00) in all sows beginning on the day of weaning by observing vulvar swelling, redness, and the standing reflex in response to direct back pressure. Based on the WEI, sows were categorized into two groups: the normal group, comprising sows that exhibited estrus within 7 days post‑weaning (discovery cohort, n = 45; validation cohort, n = 18), and the non‑return group, consisting of sows that failed to return to estrus within 14 days post‑weaning (discovery cohort, n = 40; validation cohort, n = 11). This behavior-based grouping was further verified by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) based on fecal hormone and hormone-related metabolite profiles, as shown in Figure S7A.

DNA extraction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Microbial DNA extraction and quality assessment were performed as described previously [10]. Briefly, DNA from fecal samples was extracted using an E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), and DNA quality and concentration were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop® spectrophotometry, respectively. Genomic DNA was fragmented to an average size of ~ 400 bp using a Covaris M220 instrument (Gene Company Ltd., Hong Kong, China) for paired-end library construction. Libraries were prepared using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions to obtain 150 bp paired-end reads.

Metagenomic sequencing analysis

Quality control and assembly

Raw reads were processed using fastp [21] (v0.20.0) with parameters ‘--cut_by_quality3 -W 4 -M 20 -n 5 -c -l 50 -w 3’ to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. Host DNA contamination was removed by aligning filtered reads to the Sus scrofa reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) using BWA-MEM2 [22] (v2.2.1), and discarding mapped sequences with SAMtools [23] (v1.16). The high-quality clean reads from each sample were subsequently assembled using MEGAHIT [24] (v1.1.3) with parameters ‘--min-count 2 --k-min 27 --k-max 127 --k-step 20 --min-contig-len 1000’ to generate contigs.

MAGs reconstruction, quality control, and abundance quantification

Single-sample binning was performed using MetaBAT 2 [25] (v2.12.1, parameters: -t 16 -m 1500 -v) and VAMB [26] (v3.0.2, parameters: -m 1500 -p 16 --minfasta 200000). The resulting bins were analyzed using CheckM [27] (v1.1.3, parameters: -t 32 -x fa -pplacer_threads 1) to evaluate genome completeness and contamination. Only putative bins with completeness ≥ 50% and contamination ≤ 5% were retained. These medium- and high-quality MAGs were subsequently dereplicated using dRep [28] (v2.6.2) with parameters ‘-p 16 -comp 50 -con 5 -nc 0.25 -pa 0.9 -sa 0.99’, resulting in 2,704 non-redundant genomes. The abundance of each MAG across samples was quantified using the quant_bins module integrated with Salmon in metaWRAP [29] (v1.2.1).

Taxonomic annotation and functional analysis of MAGs

The 2,704 non-redundant MAGs were taxonomically classified using the Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-Tk) [30] (v2.4.0) with GTDB release 220. Gene prediction and genome annotation were performed using Prokka [31] (v1.14.5) with parameters ‘-metagenome -kingdom Bacteria -cpus 8 -force’. Predicted proteins from each MAG were annotated against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [32] database (released on October 31, 2022) using KofamScan [33] (v1.3.0, parameters: -E 1e-5 -f mapper), and the resulting KEGG Orthologs (KOs) were mapped to KEGG pathways. Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) within MAGs were identified using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI v5.2.1) [34] with default parameters against the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD v4.0.0) [34]. Virulence factors (VFs) in MAGs were characterized by BLASTp (v2.16.0) alignment against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [35] (downloaded on February 12, 2025). Only the best hit with an E value < 1e − 5, identity ≥ 80%, and query coverage ≥ 70% was retained for each query.

Gene and pathway abundance quantification

Gene and pathway abundance quantifications were performed as described previously [10]. Briefly, protein-coding genes were predicted from assembled contigs using Prodigal (v2.6.3) [36] and clustered at 90% amino acid sequence identity and 80% alignment coverage using CD-HIT (v4.8.1) [37] to construct a non-redundant gene catalog. Reads from each sample were mapped to the gene catalog using BWA-MEM2 (v2.2.1), and gene abundances were quantified using featureCounts (v1.6.2) [38] and normalized as fragments per kilobase of gene sequence per million mapped reads (FPKM). Genes were searched against the KEGG database using DIAMOND, with KOBAS (v3.0) employed for KOs assignment and pathway mapping. KOs and pathway abundances were determined by summing the FPKM abundances of all genes assigned to each functional category. The protein sequences for bile acid metabolism genes (12α-HSDH, 3α-HSDH, 7α-HSDH, 7β-HSDH, baiB and BSH) were obtained from the NCBI database with a text search. The assembled gene catalog and open reading frames (ORF) predicted from MAGs were aligned to these sequences using BLASTp (best-hit with an E-value < 1e-5; identity > 70% and coverage > 70%). The abundance of each bile acid metabolism gene was calculated as the sum of the FPKM abundances of all matching genes.

Gut microbiota co-abundance network construction at the strain level

Pairwise correlations among MAGs were calculated using the Sparse Correlations for Compositional data (SparCC) [39] algorithm implemented in Fastspar (v1.0.0) [40], with 50 iterations and 1,000 bootstrap permutations based on abundance data. The resulting correlation matrix was converted to a distance matrix (1- correlation value) and subjected to hierarchical clustering using the Ward’s linkage (ward.D2) method in weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) (v1.73) [41]. This approach identified two distinct microbial guilds. Network visualization was performed using Cytoscape (v3.10.3) [42], displaying only significant correlations (P < 0.05) between MAGs.

Random forest (RF) model for biomarker selection

A random forest classification model based on the abundance data of 48 high-quality MAGs from the two guilds was constructed to distinguish sows with normal return to estrus from those that failed to return to estrus. Three validation strategies, including leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), 5-fold cross-validation, and random 70/30 train/test split were employed to evaluate the predictive performance. Leave-one-out cross-validation was ultimately selected due to its optimal predictive accuracy. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) via the pROC package (v1.18.5) in R.

Validation in an independent dataset

The quality control procedures for the 29 metagenomic samples in the validation dataset were identical to those described above. The relative abundances of the 48 HQMAGs in the validation dataset were subsequently calculated using CoverM [43] (v0.7.0, parameters: --min-read-percent-identity 95 --min-read-aligned-percent 75 --min-covered-fraction 0 --exclude-supplementary). The random forest model described above was then applied to evaluate its generalizability to the independent validation dataset.

Widely targeted metabolomics and lipidomics profiling of fecal samples

Widely targeted metabolomics and lipidomics profiling were performed as described previously using fecal samples from the discovery cohort (n = 85). Briefly, for metabolomics analysis, 50 mg (± 1 mg) of each fecal sample was homogenized with 500 µL of ice-cold methanol/water (70:30, v/v) containing internal standards, followed by vortexing, sonication, and centrifugation. For lipidomics analysis, 20 mg of the fecal sample was extracted with 1 mL of methyl-tert-butyl ether (MTBE)/methanol (3:1, v/v) containing an internal standard mixture. Both extracts were analyzed by LC–ESI–MS/MS using UPLC coupled to an AB Sciex triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (QTRAP 6500; AB Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Metabolite identification was performed based on retention time (RT) and mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) using an in-house MWDB database (Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) and publicly available databases including MassBank (https://massbank.eu/MassBank/), Metlin [44], HMDB [45], and KNApSAcK. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by mixing aliquots from each sample and were injected every 10 samples to monitor system performance. The relative concentration of metabolites was evaluated based on the integrated peak area of each chromatographic peak. Metabolites with coefficient of variation (CV) values greater than 0.5 were excluded from further analysis.

Data statistical analysis

Gut microbiota diversity analysis

Alpha diversity indices, including Shannon, Simpson, and beta diversity based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity were calculated using the vegan R package (v2.7.1). Statistical differences between the normal and non-return groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All visualizations were generated using with ggplot2 (v3.5.2) in R.

Differential MAG and function analysis

Differentially abundant MAGs between the normal and non-return sows were identified using Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) [46] analysis (LDA score ≥ 2, FDR < 0.05). For these MAGs, differential KOs were identified using Fisher’s exact test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction at FDR < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was subsequently conducted on the differential KOs using blitzgsea package (v1.3.34) in Python and the official KEGG pathway database (https://rest.kegg.jp/list/pathway; accessed on 19 July 2023).

The index was calculated based on the average difference in the abundances of HQMAGs between the two guilds, reflecting the structural predominance of the functional communities within each sample. To assess the discriminatory power of the GMI in distinguishing between the normal and non-return groups, ROC analysis was performed using the GMI values derived from the two microbial guilds.

Fecal metabolite data analysis

Metabolomic and lipidomic data preprocessing and auto-scaling (mean-centered and divided by the standard deviation of each variable) were performed using MetaboAnalyst (v5.0) before statistical analysis. Differential metabolites and lipids between the normal and non-return groups were identified using Student’s t-test with FDR correction (FDR < 0.05). Subsequently, pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to identify significantly enriched metabolic pathways based on these differential metabolites and lipids. The abundance of each bile acid was normalized using total sum scaling (TSS). Total primary and secondary bile acid levels were then calculated by summing the normalized abundances of the respective compounds, and the ratio of secondary to primary bile acids was determined for two group comparison.

Association between metabolite levels, gut microbiota, and functional genes

To explore the relationships between the gut microbiota and metabolism, Spearman rank correlations were performed among MAGs, finctional gene abundances, and metabolite levels. Statistically significant correlations were defined as those with P-values < 0.05. All visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.2) in R.

Results

Changes in gut microbiota composition are associated with post-weaning estrus return in sows

In this study, shotgun metagenomic sequencing was conducted on fecal samples from 85 weaned sows. We reconstructed a total of 11,114 medium- and high-quality MAGs and obtained 2,704 nrMAGs with strain-level resolution. Based on these MAGs, the gut microbial diversity was compared between the normal and non-return sows. We found that both the Shannon and Simpson diversity indices were significantly higher in the normal group relative to the non-return group (Fig. 1A and B, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05). Additionally, Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity distances showed a clear distinction of the gut microbiota between the normal and non-return sows, and the statistical significance was further confirmed by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) (Fig. 1C, P = 1 × 10⁻⁴). Collectively, these results demonstrate significant differences in alpha-diversity and overall gut microbial community structure between the normal and non-return groups.

LEfSe was used to identify key gut microbial features associated with the estrus status of sows after weaning. A total of 608 MAGs exhibited significant differential abundances between the normal and non-return groups (LDA score ≥ 2, FDR < 0.05), including 476 MAGs enriched in the normal group and 132 MAGs enriched in the non-return group (Table S2). The top 20 enriched MAGs in the normal group included Lactobacillus (e.g., Lactobacillus sp945980025, Lactobacillus sp947568765), Prevotella sp002251245, Clostridium sp000435835, Treponema_D sp016293915, as well as other MAGs primarily affiliated with Oscillospiraceae (e.g., F23-B02 sp016292445), Lachnospiraceae (e.g., UBA2868 sp004552595) and Muribaculaceae (e.g., SFTJ01 sp004563195). Conversely, the top 20 enriched MAGs in the non-return group included Streptococcus faecavium, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, Bacteroides fragilis, Clostridium sp900759995, as well as MAGs belonging to Christensenellales and Anaerovoracaceae (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that specific MAGs are associated with post-weaning estrus return in sows.

Significant shifts in functional profiles of the gut microbiome between normal and non-return sows

Considering that alterations in microbial community structure are often accompanied by changes in potential functional capacity, we further annotated the functional genes carried by the 608 differentially abundant MAGs using the KEGG database, and identified a total of 4,161 KOs. Based on the Jaccard distance derived from the copy numbers of these KOs across MAGs, we observed significant shifts in the functional profiles of the gut microbiome between the normal and non-return sows (Fig. 2A, PERMANOVA P = 0.001). A total of 1,883 KOs exhibited significantly different distributions across these genomes, of which 1,392 were more broadly distributed in 476 MAGs enriched in the normal group, while 491 were more prevalent in 132 MAGs enriched in the non-return group.

Based on the distribution of these KOs in the genomes, GSEA was performed to assess the enrichment of KEGG pathways. The results indicated that pathways including cell growth, quorum sensing, ABC transporters, DNA repair and recombination proteins, beta-lactam resistance, antimicrobial resistance genes, and glycerolipid metabolism were significantly enriched in the non-return group. Conversely, pathways such as lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis, glycosyltransferases, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions were significantly enriched in the normal group. Other pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, glutathione metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism pathways (e.g., fructose and mannose metabolism, galactose metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism), and folate biosynthesis, also showed significant enrichment (Fig. 2B). Notably, we observed that key genes folK (K00950), folP (K00796), and folA (K00287) were more widely present in MAGs enriched in the normal group (Fig. 2C-E), suggesting that these MAGs have a higher functional potential for folate synthesis. This trend was further supported by fecal metabolomic analysis, as dihydropteroate (C00921), a key intermediate in tetrahydrofolate biosynthesis, was significantly elevated in the normal group (Fig. 2G; Fig. S1, FDR = 0.003). Furthermore, we found that MAGs enriched in the normal group carried a higher copy number of the K05349 gene, which encodes β-glucosidase (Fig. 2F).

Two competing microbial guilds are associated with post-weaning estrus in sows

Among the 608 MAGs significantly associated with post-weaning estrus in sows, 48 HQMAGs were filtered based on the Minimum Information about a MAG (MIMAG) standards [48] (LDA score ≥ 2, FDR < 0.05, completeness > 90%, contamination < 5%), which spanned 34 different genera and showed markedly distinct patterns of variation in abundance between the normal and non-return groups (Fig. 3A). Among the 48 HQMAGs, 18 were significantly enriched, while 30 were significantly depleted in the normal sows (Fig. S2).

To investigate the potential interactions among these HQMAGs, we constructed a co-abundance network based on their abundances, retaining only significant correlations regardless of direction, and found that the 48 MAGs formed a highly connected unit. To further identify potential functional group structures, WGCNA and hierarchical clustering were applied (Table S3). Notably, these 48 HQMAGs were divided into two distinct guilds. Guild 1 comprised 18 HQMAGs enriched in the normal group, including four from Prevotella, three each from Treponema_D and Lachnospiraceae, and two each from Muribaculaceae, Oscillospiraceae and RF39. Guild 2 contained 30 HQMAGs enriched in the non-return group, consisting of seven from Christensenellales, three each from Bacteroides and Anaerovoracaceae, two each from Enterococcus_E cecorum, Phocaeicola, and Ruminococcus, and one each from Streptococcus faecavium, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, Corynebacterium xerosis, and Ligilactobacillus ruminis (Fig. 3B; Table S4). In contrast to the positive correlations observed within each guild, 98.02% of the associations between the two guilds were significantly negative, indicating a potential competitive relationship. These findings suggest that the two competing guilds are associated with estrus in post-weaning sows.

Functional potentials of two competing guilds are significantly distinct

To gain functional insights into the potential mechanisms underlying the association between the two guilds and return to estrus, we characterized the genomic functional potential of each genome within the two guilds using the KEGG, CARD, and VFDB databases. A total of 2,241 and 3,310 KOs were identified in Guild 1 and Guild 2, respectively. PCoA based on Euclidean distance calculated from KO copy numbers showed a clear separation in functional potential between the two guilds (Fig. 4A, PERMANOVA, P = 0.009). Notably, K05349 (β-glucosidase) and the key folate synthesis genes were significantly enriched in Guild 1, including folK (K00950) and folP (K00796), while folA (K00287) showed a similar trend but was not significant (Fig. 4B; Fig. S3). The KOs were further mapped to KEGG modules, and a total of 213 and 257 modules were identified in Guild 1 and Guild 2, respectively. Guild 1 contained two unique modules, including M00055 (N-glycan precursor biosynthesis). Conversely, 46 modules were uniquely present in Guild 2, including four antibiotic resistance-related categories, such as multidrug resistance and tetracycline resistance, two cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) resistance-related modules, and modules involved in the biosynthesis of sphingosine and staphylopine (Fig. S4). The distribution of ARGs across these MAGs within the two guilds provided additional evidence for this functional differentiation, with Guild 1 containing 6 ARGs across 3 genomes compared to 16 ARGs distributed among 6 genomes in Guild 2 (Fig. 4C; Table S5). Similarly, VFs annotation demonstrated that 22.22% of HQMAGs in Guild 1 encoded 5 VFs limited to a single virulence category, whereas 26.67% of HQMAGs in Guild 2 harbored a broader repertoire of 28 VFs spanning six different virulence categories, indicating a higher pathogenic potential in Guild 2 (Fig. 4D; Table S6).

Two microbial guilds as potential biomarkers for post-weaning estrus in sows

To evaluate whether the members in the two guilds could accurately distinguish normal sows from non-return sows, a RF classifier was constructed using the 48 HQMAGs from both guilds and then evaluated using LOOCV, five-fold cross-validation, and train/test split, respectively (Fig. 5A). In the study cohort, the classifier evaluated by LOOCV showed the best discriminatory performance with the AUROC of 0.946 and an accuracy of 0.859 (Fig. 5B and C). We then determined whether these microbial features were applicable to other cohorts. Specifically, the 48 MAGs were used as a reference to calculate their abundances via read recruitment analysis in an independent dataset consisting of 19 normal sows and 10 non-return sows. The model demonstrated robust classification performance in the validation set with an AUROC of 0.818 (Fig. 5D), which supports the association between these microbial features and estrus in sows after weaning.

To further simplify and quantify the community-level attributes of the two guilds, we introduced a GMI that assesses the dominance of Guild 1 over Guild 2 in terms of abundance. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further demonstrated that the GMI could distinguish normal from non-return sows with good accuracy (AUROC = 0.799) (Fig. 5E). The optimal discrimination threshold for the GMI was determined to be 1.2535, which corresponds to an overall classification accuracy of 0.8 (Fig. S5). Furthermore, the difference in GMI between the two groups was statistically significant (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05), supporting the feasibility of using GMI as a simplified microbial diagnostic metric (Fig. 5F). Overall, both the RF classifier and the derived GMI showed strong discriminative power between the normal and non-return sows, suggesting that the two guilds and their representative strains in the gut ecosystem hold promise as microbial biomarkers for distinguishing estrus return in post-weaning sows.

Fecal bile acid metabolism involving the gut microbiota is associated with estrus return

Given that cholesterol serves as the common precursor for steroid hormone biosynthesis, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and steroid degradation pathways, we investigated the alterations in these functional pathways between the normal and non-return sows. Metagenomic functional profiling revealed that the non-return sows exhibited a significant depletion in the functional potential for steroid hormone biosynthesis, coupled with a significant enrichment of the steroid degradation pathway (Fig. 6A). Simultaneously, metabolite enrichment analysis indicated that the primary bile acid biosynthesis pathway was upregulated in the non-return sows (Fig. 6A), which was accompanied by significantly increased concentrations of chenodeoxycholic acid (Fig. 6B).

To further evaluate the shift in the bile acid pool, we compared the relative abundances of primary and secondary bile acids between the two groups. The non-return sows were characterized by significantly higher total relative abundances of both primary and secondary bile acids, along with a significantly elevated ratio of secondary to primary bile acids (Fig. 6C and D). This reflects an altered microbial biotransformation of bile acids in the gut of non-return sows. Specifically, several secondary bile acids and their derivatives, such as 12-ketolithocholic acid, 12-oxochenodeoxycholic acid, 6,7-diketolithocholic acid, 7-ketolithocholic acid, dehydrolithocholic acid, isolithocholic acid, lithocholic acid and norcholic acid, were also significantly enriched in the non-return sows (Fig. 6E). This metabolic divergence was consistent with the functional potential of the gut microbiota. We compared the abundance of key genes encoding enzymes (12α-HSDH, 3α-HSDH, 7α-HSDH, 7β-HSDH, baiB, and BSH) involved in bile acid metabolism between the two groups and found that only 12α-HSDH showed a significant enrichment in the non-return group (Fig. 6F; Fig. S6A). Genomic analysis revealed that 12α-HSDH was present in the MAG annotated as SFHK01 sp016296675, a Clostridia strain that was significantly enriched in the non-return sows. Moreover, the MAG abundance showed a significant positive correlation with 12α-HSDH abundance (Fig. 6G). To further explore the associations between this strain and secondary bile acid transformation, we assessed the correlations of the strain and gene abundances with secondary bile acid levels. SFHK01 sp016296675 showed significant positive correlations with 12-oxochenodeoxycholic acid and 12-ketolithocholic acid (Fig. 6G), with 12α-HSDH also showing similar positive trends, although these did not reach statistical significance (Fig. S6B). These findings indicate that the enrichment of SFHK01 sp016296675 and its encoded 12α-HSDH gene, along with the accumulation of specific secondary bile acids, are collectively associated with the failure to return to estrus in post-weaning sows.

Discussion

This study reveals a close association between estrus return in post-weaning sows and the gut microbiota based on genome-resolved metagenomic analysis. Building upon our previous findings that species-level microbial signatures are associated with estrus return and sex steroid hormone metabolism [10], the present work further characterizes the microbial signatures associated with estrus return at both the strain and community levels. Specifically, we conducted de novo assembly of MAGs and identified two competitive bacterial guilds composed of 48 high-quality MAGs that were closely associated with estrus return. By constructing a random forest model integrated with the gut microbiome index (GMI), we demonstrated the strong discriminatory power of these signatures in distinguishing between normal and non-return sows, which was further validated in an independent dataset. Furthermore, integrating metagenomic and metabolomic analyses revealed alterations in fecal bile acid metabolism in non-return sows. Notably, the enrichment of the Clostridia strain SFHK01 sp016296675 and its encoded 12α-HSDH gene in the non-return group was consistent with the significant accumulation of specific secondary bile acids in these sows. These findings provide complementary insights into the microbial and metabolic signatures associated with sow estrus return.

In the present study, sows were categorized into normal and non-return groups based on rigorous behavioral estrus monitoring. Although direct serum hormone measurements were not performed, we conducted an internal validation using fecal hormone and hormone-related metabolite profiles. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) based on these detected metabolites revealed a distinct separation between the two groups (Fig. S7A). Specifically, the normal group showed a significant enrichment of estrogen metabolites (e.g., estrone 3-sulfate) and phytoestrogens (e.g., daidzein and genistein) (Fig. S7C), while the non-return group was characterized by a marked accumulation of testosterone and progesterone-related derivatives (e.g., pregnenolone and allopregnan-20α-ol-3-one) (Fig. S7B). Based on this framework, a key advantage of this study is the application of genome-resolved analysis, a reference-free approach that enabled strain-level characterization of the gut microbiota and revealed functional heterogeneity [49, 50]. Specifically, we identified a total of 608 MAGs that exhibited significant differences in abundance between the normal and non-return groups. In the normal group, microbial strains such as Lactobacillus sp945980025, Lactobacillus sp947568765, and Prevotella sp002251245 were significantly enriched. Our previous research has shown that Lactobacillus (such as L. reuteri) and Prevotella spp. are involved in sex steroid hormone metabolism [10]. This suggests that they may play a positive role in estrus return. Conversely, the strain Bifidobacterium pseudolongum and the progesterone-related metabolites were both enriched in the non-return group. Previous research has reported that the abundance of Bifidobacterium in pregnant individuals is significantly positively correlated with progesterone levels [51]. This is consistent with our findings, which showed significantly positive correlations between Bifidobacterium pseudolongum and the progesterone-related metabolites, such as pregnenolone (Fig. S6C). Given that high levels of progesterone can inhibit the secretion of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and follicular development [52], this may be associated with the delayed estrus return observed in non-return sows. From a functional perspective, the MAGs enriched in the non-return group exhibited a higher encoded capacity for pathways related to cell proliferation, quorum sensing, antibiotic resistance, and glycerolipid metabolism. In contrast, the MAGs in the normal group were significantly enriched in pathways related to folate biosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, and energy metabolism (such as oxidative phosphorylation), indicating that these bacteria may be beneficial for the recovery of estrus in weaned sows.

Gut bacteria do not exist in isolation but rather have complex interactions and form functional groups known as “guilds” that are associated with host physiology and health [18]. Our guild-based analysis revealed two competing guilds exhibiting distinct abundance patterns between normal and non-return groups. Guild 1 was significantly more abundant in the normal group and was characterized by MAGs possessing a higher genomic functional potential for folate metabolism, including the key genes folK (K00950) and folP (K00796). Correspondingly, metabolomic profiling revealed that dihydrofolate, an intermediate in folate metabolism, was also significantly elevated in the normal group. Folate serves as an essential cofactor in nucleotide and amino acid synthesis, playing a crucial role in reproductive function [53, 54], and folate intervention can improve estrous cycles and follicular development [55]. Notably, MAGs in Guild 1 also harbored a higher copy number of the β-glucosidase gene (K05349), which hydrolyzes conjugated estrogens to increase free estrogen reabsorption [56]. In line with this, reduced β-glucosidase activity in women with PCOS has been associated with increased testosterone levels [57]. This genomic potential aligns with the enrichment of estrogen metabolites and the lower levels of testosterone observed in the normal group. These findings collectively support the potential beneficial role of Guild 1 in normal sows. In contrast, Guild 2 was enriched in non-return sows and harbored a broader repertoire of encoded virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes. After weaning, sows experience multiple stressors including metabolic stress from the transition out of lactation, oxidative stress, and hormonal fluctuations [58–60], which can disrupt gut microbiota homeostasis and intestinal barrier function [59, 61]. Under these stressful conditions, bacterial communities with higher drug resistance and virulence potential may gain selective expansion advantages and suppress the growth of functionally beneficial bacteria, which may be associated with estrus return failure in post-weaning sows.

Cholesterol serves as the common precursor for both the steroid hormone biosynthesis and primary bile acid biosynthesis pathways. In the present study, an integrated analysis of metagenomics and metabolomics revealed a significant enrichment of primary bile acid biosynthesis and a depletion of the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway in non-return sows. Our findings suggest a shift in cholesterol-related metabolism in these sows. To further characterize this metabolic divergence, we performed a detailed analysis of the fecal bile acid pool. Studies have shown that approximately 95% of primary bile acids are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum via the enterohepatic circulation, while the remaining 5% enter the colon where they are partially metabolized by gut microbes into secondary bile acids [62]. In this study, non-return sows exhibited a significant enrichment of both primary and secondary bile acids. More importantly, the significantly elevated ratio of secondary to primary bile acids in the non-return group reflects an altered microbial biotransformation process. Notably, the enrichment of the strain SFHK01 sp016296675 and its encoded gene 12α-HSDH aligns with the accumulation of specific products, including 12-oxochenodeoxycholic acid and 12-ketolithocholic acid. The 12α-HSDH enzyme specifically catalyzes the transformation of the hydroxyl group to a keto group at the C-12 position of the bile acid nucleus [63, 64]. This consistency between the genomic potential and the observed metabolic products provides strong evidence for the active role of this microbial pathway in vivo. Although we did not measure ovarian tissues, growing evidence supports the involvement of the gut microbiota-bile acid axis in the regulation of ovarian function. For instance, gut microbiota-mediated bile acids can modulate ovarian function by stimulating the secretion of interleukin [65]. Additionally, specific bile acids have been reported to exert direct inhibitory effects on ovarian steroidogenesis and follicular development through farnesoid X receptor (FXR) signaling [66]. Collectively, these findings identify a distinct fecal bile acid signature associated with estrus return failure. However, identifying the specific bile acid components and establishing their relationships with ovarian function will require further tissue-level analysis and functional validation. Nevertheless, this provides a robust foundation for future research to evaluate the systemic impact of these microbial-derived metabolites on the host reproductive axis.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the absence of direct serum hormone measurements represents a limitation of the present study. Future research incorporating standardized hormone assays and time-resolved sampling strategies will be essential to further validate and refine the research model. Second, genome-based functional predictions do not unequivocally demonstrate in vivo enzymatic activity. Consequently, the proposed functional mechanisms remain speculative and require further validation using metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics, or functional assays with isolated strains. Third, although diet composition, weaning age, and housing conditions were consistent across all sows, individual variations in feed intake and Body Condition Score (BCS) during lactation were not recorded. These may represent potential confounding factors for the observed microbial and metabolic differences. Finally, this study focused exclusively on the bacterial kingdom, while the gut microbiome is a complex microbial ecosystem comprising not only bacteria but also fungi, viruses, and archaea [67]. Accumulating evidence has highlighted the importance of these non-bacterial components in host health, as they play pivotal roles in nutrient absorption, metabolic regulation, and immune response [68–71]. These components may be significantly associated with post-weaning estrus return in sows. Therefore, future studies should investigate the contributions of non-bacterial microbiota and their inter-kingdom interactions to sow physiological and reproductive health. Despite these limitations, our study identifies robust microbial and metabolic signatures associated with estrus return, providing a foundation for future research to understand the gut-reproductive axis.

Conclusion

This study employed genome-resolved and guild-level metagenomic analysis to identify two competing microbial guilds associated with post-weaning estrus return in sows, with Guild 1 possessing potentially beneficial effects and Guild 2 showing a higher encoded pathogenic potential. We identified specific bacterial strain associated with alterations in fecal bile acid metabolism, particularly the accumulation of secondary bile acids in non-return sows. Importantly, these genome-based guild-level signatures hold promise as biomarkers for distinguishing estrus return, providing a basis for the development of potential microbiota-targeted interventions in post-weaning sows.
