Availability and readiness of healthcare services at primary healthcare centers during the Sudan war: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mustafa Rabih, Ahmed Balla M. Ahmed, Hafeia A. Abdelgyoum, Suodad Elhassan, Elalim Eltayeb Abduelrhman Mohamed, Fatima Mohamed Osman Yasin, Gasm Alseed Abdelmonim Gasm Alseed Fadlalmoula, Magrabia Arbab, Mohanned Abdalsalam Mohammed Salih, Rana Adam Abdallah Ahmed, Muhannad Bushra Masaad Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth services in Sudan have been severely disrupted during the ongoing war, leading to a decline in the availability and readiness of services at the primary care level. This study aimed to evaluate the availability and readiness of healthcare services at Sudanese primary healthcare centers (PHCCs) during the current conflict.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in four out of seven safe states in Sudan. Using the Service Delivery Indicator (SDI) survey, the availability and readiness of 15 medical equipment items, 15 vaccines, and 36 essential drugs were assessed. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare indices across states, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test to identify significant differences. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsAmong the 50 PHCCs included, the mean equipment availability index was 63.6%, while the overall vaccine availability index was 48.0%, with 80% of facilities providing vaccination services. The overall drug availability index was 54.4%, with River Nile State having the highest availability at 67.6%. Vaccine availability indices in Red Sea State (mean = 29%) and River Nile State (mean = 24.4%) were significantly lower than in Gadarif State (mean = 80.7%) (p < 0.001). Additionally, there was a significant difference in drug availability across the four states (p = 0.015).ConclusionPrimary healthcare centers in Sudan face significant challenges during the war, with shortages in medical equipment, vaccines, and essential drugs. Addressing these gaps is critical to ensure service functionality and prevent disease outbreaks. Strengthening supply chains and prioritizing healthcare support are urgently needed.

Background

Primary healthcare centers (PHCCs) serve as the first point of contact for patients within the healthcare system [1]. Since The Alma-Ata Declaration of 1978, which laid the groundwork for primary healthcare (PHC) principles, to The Astana Declaration of 2018, unanimously supported by all World Health Organization (WHO) Member States, primary healthcare has been consistently emphasized as vital for improving health and health outcomes. Both declarations recognize primary healthcare as a cornerstone of an efficient and responsive healthcare system [2].

In Sudan, the national health programming process initiated in 1975 led to the development of a countrywide primary healthcare program with WHO assistance [3]. Despite this, the health system continues to face challenges, and ensuring access to high-quality primary healthcare (PHC) services remains a significant issue [4]. Currently, Sudan has one primary healthcare facility for every 10,000 people [5].

On April 15, 2023, violent clashes erupted between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF), resulting in at least 15,550 reported fatalities and over 12.5 million people affected [6, 7]. The conflict has severely damaged Sudan’s health infrastructure, with the WHO reporting that 70% of public and private healthcare facilities in war-affected states had closed by the end of 2023 [8]. Critical shortages of medicines, medical supplies, and equipment, coupled with insufficient funding for operational costs, have forced hospitals and health facilities to shut down in most states [9]. Primary healthcare has also been heavily impacted, with significant losses in access to essential medical supplies and equipment, including medicines [10]. In response, the WHO has provided direct support to 60 primary healthcare centers across 10 states [9].

Service availability and readiness refer to the ability of a healthcare facility to provide safe, high-quality, and respectful care, ensuring that patients encounter competent and motivated providers who can build trust [11]. While studies measuring essential service availability and readiness in Sudan are limited, one study conducted in October 2022, just before the current conflict, found that the overall availability of essential medicines for adults at primary healthcare centers in Khartoum State was only 36.8% [12]. Assessing the availability and readiness of healthcare facilities is critical to understanding their capacity to respond to the challenges posed by the ongoing war.

To our knowledge, no studies have yet examined the services provided by primary healthcare centers during the current conflict. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the availability and readiness of healthcare services offered at Sudanese primary healthcare centers amid the ongoing armed conflict.

Methods

Study design and settings

This study is an observational, descriptive, cross-sectional investigation conducted between August 1 and November 15, 2024, during the ongoing war in Sudan. The study focused on primary healthcare centers located in four relatively safe states: Red Sea, River Nile, White Nile, and Gadarif. The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure rigorous reporting standards.

Study population

All government-run primary healthcare centers managed by physicians in the four selected states, which had on-site pharmacies or medicine dispensaries, were included in the study. Centers categorized as below the primary care level in the healthcare pyramid were excluded. These excluded facilities included PHC units staffed by community health workers, dressing stations managed by nurses, and dispensaries overseen by medical assistants.

Sampling

A multi-stage cluster sampling approach was used to select the facilities. In the first stage, four states were chosen from the seven safest states in Sudan: Red Sea, River Nile, White Nile, and Gadarif. In the second stage, main localities within each of these four states were selected, followed by the identification of the central city within each locality. The selected cities were Port Sudan in Red Sea State, Shendi in River Nile State, Ad-Dueim in White Nile State, and Gadarif in Gadarif State. Finally, total coverage of all operational primary healthcare centers in each selected city was achieved.

Data collection tools and techniques

The Service Delivery Indicator (SDI) survey was adopted for data collection. The SDI is a nationally representative, facility-based survey designed to assess the delivery of primary healthcare services as experienced by the average citizen. Its standardized indicators allow for comparisons between countries, across regions, and over time [13]. The SDI survey evaluates the performance and quality of healthcare service delivery systems, collecting data on diagnostic accuracy, adherence to basic clinical guidelines, caseloads, staff absenteeism, and the availability of drugs, medical equipment, and infrastructure [14]. For this study, the modules on drugs, basic medical equipment, sterilization equipment, and vaccinations were utilized [15].

The medical equipment, vaccines, and essential drugs included in the questionnaire were selected based on the Federal Ministry of Health guidelines for PHC centers. The list of essential drugs was derived from the Sudan National Essential Medicines List [16]. Specifically, the study assessed the availability and quality of 15 medical equipment items, 15 vaccines, and 36 essential drugs.

Nine medical students were recruited as data collectors based on their geographical proximity to the target centers. They underwent comprehensive training on the study’s objectives, the questionnaire, and the data collection process. Additionally, they were trained to use Kobotoolbox, an offline mobile data collection app for epidemiological surveys. Data collection involved face-to-face interviews with the medical director, immunization officer, and pharmacy staff, as well as direct observation of physicians and laboratory workers using equipment. Expiration dates of medications and vaccines were also verified.

Data analysis and management

All analysis was conducted using R (4.2.2). Descriptive statistics were calculated for numerical variables using mean and standard deviation, and for categorical variables using frequencies and percentages. Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted to compare indexes across states, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test to identify states with significant differences. These tests were performed using the FSA package in R [17]. In the Kruskal-Wallis test for vaccine availability, White Nile State was excluded from the analysis due to having only one observation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

General characteristics, equipment’s availability and functionality

The study included 50 primary healthcare centres distributed across four states in Sudan, namely Gadarif (N = 20), Red Sea (N = 20), White Nile (N = 4), and River Nile (N = 6) States.

Equipment availability indices of different states ranged from 55.3 to 72.7%, mean index was 63.6% (SD = 20.8). Red Sea State reported the highest equipment availability (72.7%, SD = 26.5%), followed by White Nile (65.0%, SD = 11.4%), River Nile (60.0%, SD = 8.4%), and Gadarif (55.3%, SD = 14.5%). Diagnostic tools such as stethoscopes (97%) and microscopes (95%) were mostly available, while sterilization equipment showed low availability; autoclaves were present in only 37% of facilities overall, and electric dry heat sterilizers in 23%. Furthermore, they were functional in only 64% and 50% of centres when available, respectively. Thermometers were available in less than half (47%) of the facilities (Table 1). However, There was no significant difference in mean availability indices between different states (p-value = 0.14) (Table 2) (Fig. 1).

Availability and functionality of immunization services

Overall, 80% of all facilities provided vaccination services. The percentage of vaccination coverage ranges between 25% (N = 1) (White Nile State) and 100% (N = 20) (Gadarif state) (Table 3). Mostly, The PHCCs owned refrigerators and vaccines were stored at the same facility 88% of the time. However, refrigerator temperature was observed to be higher than 8 Celsius in 18% of the surveyed facilities (Table 3).

The overall vaccine availability index across all surveyed facilities was 48.0% (SD = 42.5%). No state achieved a total coverage of all essential vaccines (Table 2). Diphtheria, Poliomyelitis, Tetanus, and BCG vaccines were the most commonly readily available vaccines (70–73%). In contrast, somewhere only available in 50–55% of the sampled facilities (namely: Yellow Fever, Hepatitis B, Haemophilus Influenza B, Rotavirus, Mumps, and Rubella). Coverage of the rest of the vaccines ranged between 60 and 65% (Table 4).

Vaccine availability indices of Red sea State (mean = 29%) and River Nile State (mean = 24.4%) are significantly lower than vaccine availability index of Gadarif State (mean = 80.7%) (P- value < 0.001) (Table 2).

Drugs and medications availability

Upon questioning and observing The available essential drug, the overall drug availability index was 54.4%. River Nile State had the highest availability at 67.6% while White Nile had the lowest at 38.2% (SD = 25.9) (Table 2).

Among antibiotics, metronidazole (88%) and amoxicillin 500 mg capsules (77%) were the most readily available, with overall availability rates of 77% and 88%, respectively. Paracetamol 500 mg tablets were widely available (88%), while aspirin 100 mg/75 mg tablets were available in only 57% of facilities. Oral rehydration salts (ORS) and ringer’s lactate were highly available at 69% and 88%, respectively. Anthelmintics and gastrointestinal drugs showed moderate availability, ranging from 38 to 57%. However, promethazine (20%), tetanus antitoxin (28%), and diazepam (34%) showed low availability (Table 5).

There was a significant difference in drug availability across the four states (p = 0.015). However, Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Holm adjustment did not identify statistically significant pairwise differences. Unadjusted p-values suggested that Gadarif State had a significantly lower drug availability than Red Sea State (p = 0.017), and River Nile (p = 0.028). It also suggested that White Nile State had a significantly lower index than River Nile (p = 0.028), and Red Sea (p = 0.037).

Discussion

This study seeks to evaluate the availability and readiness of health services delivered by primary healthcare centers (PHCCs) in four Sudanese states during the current war, with a particular focus on medical equipment, drugs, and vaccination services.

The mean index of equipment availability was 63.6%, consistent with findings from other conflict-affected regions such as Syria and Yemen, where essential medical equipment is often scarce due to disrupted supply chains and damaged infrastructure [18, 19]. Red Sea State reported the highest availability, while Gadarif State reported the lowest. This disparity is unsurprising, as Red Sea State has become the new administrative capital following the war, with the government and critical services relocating there. Notably, sterilization equipment, such as autoclaves and electric dry heat sterilizers, showed particularly low availability and were among the most non-functional devices. This situation heightens the risk of infections and potential epidemics across the country. It is important to highlight that the shortage of medical equipment in PHCCs has been a longstanding issue in Sudan’s health sector, predating the current conflict [20], largely due to the government’s disproportionate focus on hospitals, which has created an imbalanced health system [4]. The ongoing armed conflict has further exacerbated this problem, deepening the challenges faced by the healthcare sector.

Regarding vaccination Services, 80% of the surveyed facilities provided immunization services. However, according to the Sudan Federal Ministry of Health, 22 localities remain entirely inaccessible for vaccination programs after the war, while 51 others are only partially accessible [21]. This low accessibility demands urgent attention from authorities to prevent outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases. Vaccination coverage varied significantly across states, ranging from 100% in Gadarif State to as low as 25% in White Nile State. White Nile State’s fragile health system, which was ill-prepared to handle such challenges, was evident in the recent cholera outbreak, resulting in a total of 2,718 admissions to the cholera treatment center. Unfortunately, 92 of these patients died [22]. This situation is primarily due to the state being under siege by the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, unlike the other surveyed states, which has severely hindered access and exacerbated the challenges in White Nile State. The overall vaccine availability index across all facilities in our study was 50%. This Aligns with UNICEF reports indicating that national vaccination coverage in Sudan has dropped from 85% before the war to approximately 50%. In active conflict zones, which were not included in our study, a study reported that coverage rates are even lower, averaging around 30%—a critically low level [23]. A study conducted in Sudan at the end of 2023 identified several war-related factors contributing to low immunization coverage, including reduced federal healthcare spending, disruptions to the central cold chain in Khartoum, shortages of refrigerated vehicles and transport fleets, poor road conditions, and interruptions in health facility operations [24].

In 2018, only 1% of children in Sudan had never received a single dose of basic vaccines (“zero-dose” children). However, this figure has risen dramatically due to vaccine shortages, with 43% of children now missing out on essential immunizations [25]. This decline has contributed to disease outbreaks, such as the polio outbreak in 2024 [21].

Among the vaccines assessed, Diphtheria, Poliomyelitis, Tetanus, and BCG vaccines were the most readily available, with availability rates ranging between 70 and 73%. Pertussis vaccines, however, were slightly less available. During the Syrian war, the availability of DTP-containing vaccines fell to 41% in 2015 [26], which is lower than the rates observed in this study. Similarly, in Sudan, the availability of these vaccines declined to 57% in 2023, also lower than the findings reported here [27]. This discrepancy may be due to the exclusion of active conflict zones from our study. The relatively high availability of polio vaccines could reflect a proactive response by authorities to prevent polio outbreaks, given documented cases in Sudan [21]. In contrast, Yellow Fever, Hepatitis B, and Haemophilus Influenza B vaccines were less available, mirroring the general situation in the country in 2023 [28]. Notably, the vaccine availability indices in Red Sea State and River Nile State were considerably lower compared to Gadarif State, underscoring the necessity for targeted interventions in these two states. This situation may bring positive news for Gadarif State, as it is a region frequently affected by outbreaks and epidemics and hosts a significant population of internally displaced persons [29, 30]. In most cases, PHC facilities were equipped with refrigerators, and vaccines were stored on-site 88% of the time. However, in 18% of the surveyed facilities, refrigerator temperatures were observed to exceed 8 °C, deviating from the standard range of + 2 °C to + 8 °C [31]. This issue is concerning and necessitates immediate action to prevent vaccine spoilage and ensure their efficacy. Strengthening temperature monitoring systems and providing regular maintenance for refrigeration units are essential steps to maintain proper storage conditions and safeguard vaccine quality.

The overall drug availability index was 54.4%. Notably, a 2009 study conducted in 36 primary healthcare centers across six states found that 82% of the core medicines surveyed were available [32], a figure far higher than our current findings and clearly reflecting the severe deterioration in medicine availability caused by the ongoing war. While our figure is higher than that reported in Khartoum State before the war [12], this does not necessarily reflect improved availability. Local pharmaceutical factories have been destroyed, supply chains disrupted, priority given to military needs, and medication imports halted at the onset of the conflict. This suggests a reliance on humanitarian aid, as significant quantities of medicines have been delivered through these channels [33], further highlighting the deteriorating situation in Khartoum State even prior to the war. Our findings align with those from other conflict zones, such as Ukraine, where the physical availability of medicines declined during the war. This reduction was closely linked to the decrease in the number of operational pharmacies and the destruction of their infrastructure [34]. White Nile State recorded the lowest drug availability, consistent with its fragile health system and the blockade imposed during the conflict [35]. Gadarif also showed significantly lower drug availability than Red Sea and River Nile, yet had the highest vaccine availability—opposite to the River Nile pattern. These variations suggest possible differences in supply chain priorities, distribution challenges, or state-level decision-making, and warrant further investigation, particularly given Gadarif’s ongoing outbreak.

In general, medical supplies such as syringes and water for injection had the highest availability, followed by electrolytes and IV fluids, then analgesics and antipyretics. At the bottom of the list were antihelminthics beside anesthetics and emergency drugs. The limited availability of anesthetics and emergency drugs is particularly concerning, given the increased risk of injuries during wartime and the heightened need for surgical interventions. Antibiotics showed moderate availability, with metronidazole 500 mg tablets being the most available and cotrimoxazole 240 mg/5 ml suspension the least. The availability of antibiotics requires greater attention due to the elevated risk of infections during the war and the outbreaks of diseases [36]. Notably, promethazine 50 mg injection was the least available drug among all surveyed medications.

Although this study was conducted in safer states due to the inaccessibility of active conflict zones, the war has had a severe impact across the entire country. Pharmaceutical manufacturing has largely ceased, vaccination coverage has fallen from 85% before the war to around 50% nationally, with rates dropping to about 30% in conflict-affected areas. Supply chain disruptions have affected all sectors of the health system [37, 38]. Therefore, findings from safer states still provide a meaningful reflection of the broader impact of the war on Sudan’s health system. This study provides valuable insights into the health services delivered by primary healthcare centers across various Sudanese states, with the strength of including diverse geographical representation. This study’s cross-sectional design provides important descriptive insights at one point in time but limits the ability to infer causality. The exclusion of rural areas and regions experiencing active conflict also limits the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights the severe disruptions in health services across Sudanese primary healthcare centers (PHCCs) due to the ongoing conflict, with significant shortages in medical equipment, vaccines, and medications. To address these challenges, urgent interventions are needed to restore and strengthen healthcare infrastructure, particularly, White Nile State, which is under siege and blockade due to the war. Prioritizing the repair of damaged facilities, ensuring consistent supply chains for essential medical equipment and drugs, and enhancing vaccination programs are critical. Immediate actions should include improving vaccine storage conditions, increasing the availability of emergency drugs and anesthetics, and expanding humanitarian aid to address medication shortages. Additionally, targeted support for states with the lowest service readiness, such as Gadarif and Red Sea States, is essential to mitigate the risk of epidemics and ensure equitable healthcare access. Strengthening collaboration between government authorities, international organizations, and local communities will be vital to rebuilding Sudan’s healthcare system and safeguarding public health during and after the conflict.
