Persistent cutaneous lupus erythematosus: A pathway toward systemic disease?
Authors: Qi Wang, Mengjiao Chen, Hui Jin, Jingyu Xiao, Yamin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) is an inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by specific skin lesions, differing from systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) which affects multiple organs widely. Although CLE has traditionally been regarded as a cutaneous-limited disorder, increasing evidence suggests that a subset of CLE patients—particularly those with persistent, refractory, or relapsing disease courses—face a heightened risk of systemic progression. The heterogeneous transition rates among CLE subtypes and the increasing frequency of treatment-resistant cases highlight unmet needs in predicting disease evolution and refining therapeutic strategies.This review synthesizes current knowledge on the clinical characteristics and management challenges of refractory and recurrent CLE, while analyzing epidemiological evidence and risk factors associated with progression to SLE. We further delineate shared and distinct immunopathogenic pathways linking CLE and SLE, emphasizing the roles of type I interferon signaling, neutrophils, autoreactive B cells and T cell–mediated immunity. By integrating clinical insights with emerging mechanistic data, this review highlights the importance of early risk stratification and targeted immunomodulation to prevent systemic involvement in high-risk CLE patients, offering new perspectives for improving long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) is a chronic autoimmune dermatosis with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 4 per 100,000 individuals worldwide [1,2]. Clinically, CLE presents with diverse manifestations and is classified into four major subtypes: acute CLE (ACLE), subacute CLE (SCLE), intermittent CLE (ICLE), and chronic CLE (CCLE) [3]. Among these, CCLE is the most prevalent, encompassing discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE), verrucous lupus erythematosus (VLE), tumid lupus erythematosus (TLE), lupus erythematosus profundus (LEP), and chilblain lupus erythematosus (ChLE). Although CLE has long been regarded as a predominantly skin-limited disease, accumulating clinical and epidemiological evidence challenges this concept.

Longitudinal studies indicate that approximately 20% of CLE patients eventually develop systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) within 3 years, with higher risk observed in smokers, African American individuals, and patients with SCLE [[4], [5], [6]]. This progression underscores that CLE is not merely a cutaneous disorder but also a potential harbinger of systemic autoimmunity. However, the biological and clinical determinants that distinguish patients with stable, skin-limited CLE from those who subsequently develop SLE remain poorly defined.

An important and underrecognized challenge in CLE management is the subgroup of patients with refractory or recurrent disease, characterized by resistance to conventional therapies, multiple treatment failures, and frequent relapses [7]. Real-world cohort studies reveal 13-19% of CLE patients exhibit treatment resistance, often requiring prolonged or intensified immunosuppressive therapy that may paradoxically exacerbate comorbidities such as infections [8]. Strikingly, longitudinal data indicate that refractory CLE confers a high risk of SLE compared with treatment-responsive cases, yet the immunological mechanisms underlying this progression remain largely unexplored [8].

Mechanistically, CLE pathogenesis is driven by ultraviolet-induced keratinocyte apoptosis and dysregulated type I interferon (IFN) signaling, which promotes dendritic cell activation and autoantigen presentation [9,10]. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies identify a pathologic axis involving CXCR3+ cytotoxic CD8+ T cells that infiltrate CLE lesions and CD11c+ tissue-resident memory B cells producing anti-Ro/La autoantibodies [3,11,12]. These cells exhibit metabolic reprogramming (e.g., enhanced OXPHOS) and secrete interleukin- (IL)-23/IL-17, creating a feedforward loop that bridges localized inflammation to systemic autoimmunity. Despite increasing evidence of shared immune features between cutaneous and systemic lupus, how localized skin inflammation in CLE contributes to systemic autoimmunity in SLE remains unclear.

Given the clinical heterogeneity of CLE and its variable risk of systemic progression, a comprehensive synthesis of epidemiological, clinical, and mechanistic evidence is needed to clarify the CLE–SLE relationship. In this review, we performed a narrative synthesis of clinical and mechanistic studies of CLE, including large lupus cohort studies and representative reports of CLE progression to SLE, retrieved from PubMed and Web of Science over the past decade. We aim to: (i) synthesize epidemiological evidence defining the incidence, timing, and predictors of CLE-to-SLE transition; (ii) characterize the clinical burden and systemic risk associated with refractory and recurrent CLE; (iii) integrate emerging mechanistic insights to delineate shared and divergent immunopathogenic links between CLE and SLE; and (iv) discuss therapeutic implications and emerging strategies aimed at informing strategies to modify disease course and potentially mitigating systemic progression.

Epidemiology of CLE-to-SLE progression

The concept of “lupus” (Latin word for wolf) has evolved from a description of destructive skin lesions in the Middle Ages to the recognition of SLE as a distinct clinical entity in the 19th century [13]. Within this spectrum, CLE represents a heterogeneous group of skin-limited manifestations [14]. Crucially, a subset of patients with CLE progress to SLE, underscoring the importance of epidemiologic studies in defining risk, clinical course, and management strategies.

Cutaneous involvement is among the most common presentations for patients with SLE, including both LE-specific lesions and LE-nonspecific lesions [15,16]. Approximately three-quarters of SLE patients develop skin lesions during the disease course, and about one-quarter have skin manifestations as their first symptom [17]. Some patients with CLE also develop extracutaneous involvement over time, reflecting progression toward systemic disease. Clinical studies have reported that up to 42% of adult CLE patients progress to SLE within 3-5 years, with the proportion reaching up to 31% in children [18].

We searched PubMed and Web of Science for cohort studies on the progression of CLE to SLE in the past 15 years. Long-term cohort studies provide further insights into the timing of this transition (Table 1). Two studies from Texas, USA, reported that patients with CLE had a 10-17.4% probability of developing SLE within 11 years, with a median progression time of 7.8 years [4,19]. In Denmark, a cohort of 2380 CLE patients reported a median progression time of 2.05 years [20], and a national study from Korea reported a mean time to progression of 1.53 years [21]. However, data from China and many other regions remain unavailable, and no global estimates of CLE-to-SLE progression time have yet been established. Overall, both the reported proportion and the estimated time of CLE patients progressing to SLE vary widely across studies. Clinical challenges of refractory and recurrent CLE

Clinical features of refractory and recurrent CLE

Refractory and recurrent CLE present a critical management challenge, as persistent disease activity leads to irreversible, debilitating, and disfiguring lesions. Although no universally accepted diagnostic criteria exist, extensive clinical observations and literature proposed that refractory CLE generally persists despite failure of at least two standard regimens—including antimalarials and/or topical corticosteroids—for three months or longer [6,7,22]. Notably, the absence of standardized criteria has led to considerable heterogeneity across studies in terms of prior treatment lines, treatment duration, and response endpoints (e.g., CLASI-A), which limits the comparability of clinical outcomes and the generalizability of evidence. Patients with refractory CLE frequently have a long disease duration and a history of both CLE and/or SLE [23].

Several factors influence treatment response in CLE. DLE typically responds less well to therapy compared with other subtypes, both in localized and systemic treatment contexts [24]. In one study of thalidomide for refractory CLE, DLE (42%) and SCLE (30%) were the most frequent subtypes [25]. Patients without DLE and with milder baseline activity are more likely to achieve remission [24]. Smoking is more prevalent among patients with refractory disease and is associated with reduced efficacy of antimalarial therapy and lower likelihood of achieving long-term remission [26,27]. Racial background may also affect disease outcomes: black individuals often exhibit higher lupus activity at baseline, are less likely to achieve complete and long-term remission, and frequently require the addition of second-line agents.

Clinically, higher-risk profiles are associated with specific CLE subtypes, female sex, lupus non-specific skin lesions, younger age at disease onset, a greater number of SLE classification criteria fulfilled at baseline, and higher disease activity scores. Laboratory predictors include various serological abnormalities, such as autoantibody profiles and complement levels. Clinical features and laboratory markers associated with CLE progression to SLE.

The risk of systemic involvement varies substantially across CLE subtypes, following the order: ACLE > SCLE > CCLE > ICLE [28]. Among the subtypes of DLE, generalized DLE is more likely to progress to SLE than localized DLE [4].

Age at disease onset also plays a significant role, as early-onset CLE has a higher probability of developing SLE and is often characterized by higher systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity index (SLEDAI) and cutaneous lupus erythematosus disease area and severity index (CLASI) scores [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. In contrast, late-onset CLE tends to present with milder symptoms and a lower risk of systemic progression.

Although both SLE and CLE predominantly affect females, female predominance is more pronounced in SLE, suggesting a higher risk of progressing to SLE [20]. Interestingly, CLE incidence in males increases significantly after the age of 50, peaking between 55 and 64 years, whereas SLE in females peaks earlier, between 30 and 50 years [2,34,35].

Race-related differences further contribute to heterogeneity in progression risk. Black and Asian populations exhibit a higher incidence of CLE compared to Caucasians [4,28,36]. Among CLE patients, Black individuals are more likely to present with earlier disease onset, higher CLASI scores, coexisting SLE, and more severe systemic manifestations, and they more frequently report a family history of CLE and/or SLE [[37], [38], [39]].

Disease activity is another key determinant of systemic progression. Higher CLASI damage or other severity scores are associated with increased risk of progression from CLE to SLE [4,19,40]. Patients with extensive CLE, particularly those with skin lesions extending beyond the head and neck region, show a higher likelihood of systemic dissemination compared with those whose disease is confined to these areas [41].

Furthermore, certain lupus non-specific cutaneous features, such as non-scarring alopecia and periungual telangiectasia, have been linked to extracutaneous involvement, and may serve as clinical indicators of increased systemic risk [42,43].

Cytokine profiles have shown promise in predicting systemic progression of CLE. Serum and skin-lesion IL-9 levels are higher in SLE than in CLE and correlate positively with SLEDAI scores [44]. Elevated IL-9 has also been detected in CLE patients who subsequently developed SLE, suggesting it may serve as a biomarker of transition. Similarly, the serum CXCL13 level is higher in SLE than in CCLE and appears independent of CLE disease activity, indicating SLE specificity [45]. Isolated cases of SCLE or CCLE with markedly elevated CXCL13 (>500 pg/mL) have been associated with subsequent SLE development. In addition, skin lesions from CLE+/SLE + patients show higher IL-1β expression alongside increased cytotoxic markers (granzyme B, perforin) compared with CLE+/SLE− patients [46]. Conversely, IL-17 expression is enriched in CLE+/SLE− patients and correlates with Foxp3+ regulatory T cell and CD8+ T cell densities, suggesting a predominant role in localized cutaneous inflammation [47]. IL-34 has also been proposed as a potential marker of SLE disease activity [48]. Collectively, these findings highlight cytokine signatures as potential predictors of systemic evolution.

High-titer antinuclear antibodies (ANA) are the most consistently reported risk factor for CLE progression to SLE [41,[49], [50], [51]]. More than half of DLE patients with concomitant SLE are ANA-positive, whereas ANA is typically absent in isolated DLE [52]. Changes in ANA immunofluorescence patterns, specifically from homogeneous or speckled to discrete speckled, may signal disease exacerbation and increased risk of organ involvement, particularly lupus nephritis [53]. Anti-dsDNA antibodies are highly specific for SLE and are associated with lupus nephritis. Elevated anti-single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) antibody levels have been reported in DLE patients with persistent cutaneous activity, some of whom later developed disseminated lesions, new-onset SCLE, or proteinuria [54]. Additional predictors include anti-Sm, anti-Ro-52, and anti-U1-RNP antibodies, all of which have been linked to a higher likelihood of systemic transition [55].

Low complement levels, particularly C3 and C4, are associated with systemic involvement in CLE. In CCLE patients, reduced complement was linked to poorer prognosis [55,56]. Beyond conventional complement measurements, elevated erythrocyte-bound C4d level (mean fluorescence intensity >20) was associated with a 2.61-fold increased hazard of progression to SLE [57,58]. The multi-analyte assay panel (MAP) score, which integrates cell-bound complement activation products with anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm antibodies, has shown superior predictive accuracy [57]. MAP score >0.8 was a strong predictor of progression from probable to classified SLE, with a hazard ratio of 3.11, sensitivity of 40%, and specificity of 96%, outperforming low complement or anti-dsDNA testing alone.

A positive cutaneous lupus band test (LBT) can effectively differentiate lupus from non-lupus skin diseases, though it cannot reliably distinguish CLE from SLE. However, when combined with serological markers such as anti-chromatin antibodies and/or complement levels, a positive LBT may indicate increased risk of systemic evolution in CLE patients [59].

Immunopathogenic links between CLE and SLE

The pathogenesis of lupus erythematosus, encompassing both CLE and SLE, is driven by a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers. While substantial clinical evidence supports the progression of CLE to SLE, the underlying mechanism remains incompletely understood. Upstream determinants of immune dysregulation

Immune-related genetic susceptibility

Lupus erythematosus is widely recognized as a polygenic autoimmune disease with substantial heritability. Family and twin studies estimate the heritability of SLE at approximately 55-77% and of DLE at 34-54%, while up to 12% of first-degree relatives of SLE patients are also affected [60,61]. Genetic association studies have consistently identified risk loci across both systemic and cutaneous forms of lupus, particularly interferon-related genes and members of the interferon regulatory factor (IRF) family [[62], [63], [64], [65], [66]].

Among the strongest associations are variants within the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) [67,68]. Specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) loci confer disease susceptibility and influence autoantibody profiles. For instance, HLA-DQ1/DQ2 alleles are enriched in anti-Ro-positive patients, whereas HLA-DR3 is associated with anti-dsDNA seropositivity [69,70]. This association is thought to reflect the role of MHC genes in shaping B-cell antigen presentation and autoantibody production in SLE [71,72]. Genome-wide association studies have further confirmed strong correlations between HLA haplotypes (e.g., A1, B8, DR3, DQ2, DRw52, C4null) and SCLE, particularly in anti-Ro–positive individuals [73,74]. Functionally, circulating anti-Ro antibodies can bind keratinocytes expressing surface Ro antigen, leading to basal keratinocyte damage and pathology.

Beyond HLA, non-HLA loci also contribute to cutaneous involvement. A key example is the ITGAM polymorphism, which is strongly associated with CLE and cutaneous manifestations in SLE [[75], [76], [77]]. The rs1143679 variant is linked to zygomatic rash and discoid lesions [78], and impaired ITGAM function may reduce clearance of apoptotic debris, thereby amplifying immune activation [79]. Recent work has also identified PD-1H as a potential shared regulator: it is highly expressed in both SLE and DLE lesions, while PD-1H-deficient mice develop overlapping systemic and cutaneous autoimmune features, suggesting convergent immune dysregulation [80].

Environmental triggers in the progression

Environmental exposures contribute to the onset and progression of lupus in genetically susceptible individuals, including infections, smoking, ultraviolet light, air pollution, chemicals, and drugs [81]. These factors can cause alterations in DNA methylation and histone modifications, thereby increasing the immune system's propensity to mount autoimmune responses [82,83].

Photosensitivity is a crucial diagnostic criterion for both SLE and CLE [84,85]. High doses of ultraviolet radiation (UVR) can trigger or worsen cutaneous and mucosal lesions and, in some cases, is associated with subsequent systemic flares [54,86,87]. Mechanistically, UVR induces the release of proinflammatory mediators such as IFN-Ⅰ, TNF-α, and IL-6, along with increased cell recruitment. Clinically, lupus patients often experience exacerbation of skin lesions during summer months, sometimes preceding an increase in systemic disease activity [88,89]. These findings suggest a potential link between UV-induced cutaneous injury and systemic progression.

Smoking is another well-established environmental risk factor associated with both SLE and CLE, including DLE, TLE, and SCLE [90]. Components of tobacco smoke enhance oxidative stress and myeloperoxidase activity, which can diminish the efficacy of antimalarial therapy and worsen cutaneous disease [91,92]. Despite some inconsistencies across studies, smoking is overall associated with increased lupus activity, and the precise mechanisms remain to be clarified.

Dysregulated innate immune responses

Interferon signaling represents a central immunopathogenic link between CLE and SLE [[93], [94], [95]]. SLE patients with CLE exhibit significantly higher expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and non-lesion skin compared with those without CLE [96]. In the early stages of CLE, keratinocytes and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) secrete type I interferons in response to external stimuli, activating innate immune cells such as neutrophils, and promoting adaptive immune responses [40]. Such interferon-driven cascades closely parallel the immune dysregulation characteristic of SLE, supporting a mechanistic continuum between cutaneous and systemic disease.

Plasmacytoid dendritic cells

The hyperactivation of pDCs and the consequent overproduction of IFN-Ⅰ are central to lupus immunopathology [[97], [98], [99]]. In CLE, pDCs infiltrate skin lesions and produce IFN-α/β via Toll-like receptor (TLR) 7/9 recognition of self-nucleic acids, promoting keratinocyte apoptosis and interface dermatitis. Despite an unexpected decrease of circulating pDCs, single-cell analyses reveal robust ISG expression, suggesting that pDCs migrate from the blood to inflamed tissues, thereby contributing to systemic damage [100]. In the kidney, pDC infiltration appears de novo as disease progresses, highlighting their role in systemic involvement [101]. In CLE, higher pDC density in skin lesions positively correlates with serum IFN-α levels, whereas lower skin pDC scores have been linked to systemic manifestations, possibly reflecting redistribution to internal organs [100]. Therapeutically, Litifilimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting Blood Dendritic Cell Antigen 2 (BDCA2), effectively inhibits IFN production by pDCs and alleviates skin and joint symptoms in lupus patients [[102], [103], [104]]. Together, these findings underscore the pivotal role of pDCs in bridging local cutaneous inflammation and systemic autoimmunity, and identify them as promising targets for preventing CLE-to-SLE transition.

Keratinocytes

Skin inflammation secondary to epidermal injury is increasingly recognized as a contributor to systemic autoimmunity in lupus [105]. Keratinocytes sustain IFN-I signaling, undergo aberrant apoptosis, and release autoantigens and proinflammatory mediators that amplify immune activation [106]. UV induces keratinocyte apoptosis through RIG-I/MDA5/IRF7 and Hippo pathways, accelerating IFN production and cutaneous inflammation [107,108]. In SLE, circulating IFN activity correlates with cutaneous disease activity, and keratinocytes from lesional skin exhibit constitutive expression of IFN-κ even without exogenous stimuli [109,110]. In CLE, p21^WAF1/CIP1-high senescent basal keratinocytes produce abundant IFN-I and upregulate HLA class I on neighboring cells, fostering CD8+ T cell–mediated cytotoxicity and a self-amplifying loop of antigen release and inflammation [111]. Apoptotic keratinocytes further release nuclear autoantigens (e.g., Ro/SSA) that may deposit at the dermo-epidermal junction (“lupus band”) and promote autoantibody formation. UV-induced Z-DNA exposure in keratinocytes has also been linked to anti-Z-DNA antibody emergence [[112], [113], [114]]. Notably, keratinocytes from patients with lupus nephritis exhibit IFN-responsive transcriptional programs that correlate with renal tubular IFN scores, providing evidence that epidermal IFN pathways mirror systemic organ involvement [115]. Recent findings reveal that mitochondrial stress and mtDNA release can activate innate immune sensors, while nucleic acid transfer to plasmacytoid dendritic cells amplifies IFN production [116]. These observations position keratinocytes as a central hub linking cutaneous injury to systemic immune dysregulation and underscore their importance in the transition from CLE to SLE.

Neutrophils

Neutrophils in lupus exhibit abnormal phenotypes and function, characterized by enhanced cell death and excessive neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation [117,118]. NETosis releases DNA-protein complexes, histones, and granular enzymes (e.g., myeloperoxidase), which expose nuclear autoantigens and promote autoantibody production [119,120]. Additionally, NETs can directly damage endothelial cells through reactive oxygen species and neutrophil elastase, contributing to vasculopathy, thrombosis, and lupus nephritis [121,122]. Indirectly, NETs amplify inflammation by stimulating macrophage production of IL-1β and IL-36 cytokines, driving further neutrophil recruitment [123,124]. Additionally, NETs upregulate BAFF to enhance B cell proliferation and autoantibody generation [125].

Among these dysregulated neutrophils, a distinct subset of low-density neutrophils has been identified in lupus blood and tissues, characterized by heightened IFN-I signaling and exaggerated NETs formation, making them a primary pathogenic neutrophil subpopulation in lupus [126,127]. Ultraviolet radiation further promotes NETosis, and UV-injured neutrophils contribute to renal inflammation through IL-17A/G-CSF–dependent pathways, driving neutrophilia and tissue homing [46,128,129]. Subsets with high CXCR4 or ICAM1 expression may migrate from the skin to the blood and infiltrate the kidney, causing transient inflammatory injury and proteinuria [87,130]. Moreover, UV-induced skin injury may lead to NET-associated transport of alarmins such as IL-33 to the kidney, activating plasmacytoid dendritic cells via the suppression of tumorigenicity 2 pathway and enhancing IFN-α responses [131]. In addition, cutaneous colonization with Staphylococcus aureus has been shown to accelerate systemic autoimmunity by promoting neutrophil activation [129].

Collectively, these findings indicate that neutrophils are not merely effectors of tissue damage but central mediators linking cutaneous injury to systemic inflammation, making them a promising focus for investigating the systemic spread of CLE.

Basophils

Accumulating evidence implicates basophils as potential mediators linking cutaneous inflammation in CLE to systemic immune dysregulation in SLE. Chemokine-driven recruitment may constitute an early event in this process: CCL17 is highly expressed in inflamed human skin and promotes the migration of memory CD4+ T cells and dendritic cells to lesional skin and draining lymph nodes, providing a conceptual basis for immune dissemination from the skin in which basophils may participate [132]. Consistent with this notion, basophil infiltration has been directly demonstrated in SLE skin lesions but not in healthy skin, with upregulation of CCR1 and CCR2 enabling migration toward RANTES and MCP-1, both elevated in lupus-associated cutaneous inflammation [133].

Despite increased tissue infiltration, multiple clinical studies consistently report reduced circulating basophil counts in active SLE. Peripheral basophil reduction correlates negatively with global disease activity, complement consumption, and histopathologic severity of lupus nephritis in both adult and pediatric cohorts, supporting a redistribution of basophils from blood to inflamed tissues during disease flares [[134], [135], [136]]. Mechanistic studies in lupus-prone mouse models further implicate that basophils amplify autoantibody production and organ damage through IL-4 secretion, IgE-dependent pathways, and promotion of Th2-skewed immunity, whereas basophil depletion ameliorates disease severity [137]. Notably, basophils in SLE exhibit an activated phenotype despite reduced numbers, highlighting functional activation rather than cell abundance as the critical determinant of pathogenicity [137].

Together, these findings suggest that basophils may participate in the immunological continuum linking cutaneous inflammation to systemic immune dysregulation. Although their role in CLE remains insufficiently explored, existing data support the hypothesis that basophils are potential contributors to CLE-to-SLE progression and warrant further investigation into their utility as biomarkers of disease activity and as modulators of skin-to-systemic immune crosstalk.

Dysregulated adaptive immune responses

In both SLE and CLE, adaptive immunity is an important factor that should not be overlooked. Abnormal T cell function impairs immune tolerance and promotes B cell activation, and B cells secrete autoantibodies that exacerbate the inflammatory response. Together, these abnormalities in immune cell function contribute to the progression of CLE to SLE.

T cells

Recent studies have identified T cell subsets in CLE lesions that mirror those found in the peripheral blood of SLE patients, suggesting an immunological link between cutaneous and systemic disease [138]. Mendelian randomization analyses further implicate skin-derived T cell chemokines, particularly IL-17, in systemic progression, underscoring the contribution of cutaneous inflammation to broader immune dysregulation [139]. Transcriptomic profiling further reveals that CLE lesions display strong upregulation of IFN-Ⅰ inducible and T cell chemotactic genes, distinct from the signatures observed in lupus nephritis [140].

Among helper T cell populations, peripheral helper and follicular helper T cells are present in CLE skin, expressing high levels of ICOS, TIGIT, and MAF—molecules known to promote B cell activation and autoantibody production in SLE [125,141]. Although their frequency in CLE lesions is low, they may provide a mechanistic link to systemic autoimmunity under permissive conditions. Other effector subsets include γδ T cells, enriched in CLE lesions and positively correlated with SLE activity [142], and CCR6+ Th22 cells, which are elevated in the blood of SLE patients with skin or renal involvement and may promote CLE-to-SLE transition via IL-22–mediated inflammation and pathogenic T cell trafficking.

Tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) exhibit disease-specific patterns: ACLE lesions show low TRM density and stronger systemic associations, consistent with their role as markers of systemic lupus, whereas DLE and SCLE display higher TRM burden, supporting a locally driven, chronic course [143]. In addition, murine CLE models demonstrate that skin-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells upregulate HIF-1, conferring potent cytotoxic capacity that parallels HIF-1-dependent effector functions of kidney-infiltrating T cells in lupus nephritis [144,145].

Together, these findings indicate that cutaneous T cell subsets—through B cell help, proinflammatory cytokine release, and metabolic reprogramming—may acquire effector functions that extend beyond the skin, thereby bridging local inflammation with systemic autoimmunity.

B cells and autoantibodies

B-cell dysregulation is a hallmark of both CLE and SLE [146]. Analysis of skin lesions shows that DLE patients exhibit stronger B-cell enrichment and transcriptional activity compared with SLE skin, highlighting differences between localized and systemic manifestations [147]. In parallel, loss of B-cell tolerance and activation of autoreactive B cells underlie autoantibody production, a central driver of lupus pathogenesis. Although >100 autoantibodies have been described in SLE, their direct links to tissue injury remain incompletely defined. Anti-dsDNA antibodies, for example, can deposit at the dermal-epidermal junction and contribute to cutaneous inflammation, yet they are typically systemic rather than lesion-specific [11]. While anti-dsDNA antibodies are rarely detected in isolated CLE, elevated ANA, anti-dsDNA, and anti-RNP antibodies are common in SLE and correlate with systemic activity, including lupus nephritis [[148], [149], [150], [151]]. These findings suggest that anti-dsDNA and related antibodies may also mediate systemic manifestations in patients with CLE.

Importantly, approximately 42% of primary CCLE patients (CCLE+/SLE−) display a B-cell dysregulation profile characterized by reduced unswitched memory B cells and expansion of effector B cells, including double-negative B cells and plasmablasts [152]. This immunophenotype overlaps with B-cell abnormalities commonly observed in SLE and is associated with broader cutaneous involvement and increased serological activity, including higher positivity for anti-Sm, anti-RNP, and anti-Ro52/60 antibodies. However, it remains unclear whether this B-cell profile represents a CLE-specific immunological state associated with heightened inflammatory burden, or reflects an early or subclinical phase along the spectrum of systemic lupus erythematosus. While such observations are consistent with reports that 20% of CCLE patients eventually develop SLE, current evidence is largely cross-sectional [152]. Therefore, longitudinal studies are required to determine whether this B-cell signature precedes, predicts, or merely parallels systemic disease transition, and to validate its potential utility for risk stratification in CLE patients.

Beyond conventional IgG autoantibodies, recent studies have implicated that IgE-class autoantibodies may act as potential amplifiers of B-cell–driven systemic autoimmunity. In SLE, elevated anti-dsDNA IgE levels correlate with disease activity and can engage plasmacytoid dendritic cells via TLR9-dependent pathways, enhancing type I interferon production [153]. In parallel, IgE engagement of basophils promotes IL-4 secretion, thereby facilitating B-cell differentiation toward antibody-secreting plasma cells [154]. Notably, IgE autoantibodies against dsDNA and SSA/Ro52 are enriched in SLE patients with cutaneous involvement and correlate with complement consumption and systemic activity [155]. Although direct data in CLE are limited, these findings suggest that IgE-associated B cell responses may represent an additional, yet still underexplored, immunological axis linking persistent cutaneous inflammation to systemic immune activation.

From a therapeutic perspective, targeting B cell survival and function has shown promise. Treatment with belimumab significantly reduced flare rates in CLE patients, regardless of systemic involvement [51], highlighting the relevance of B cell–directed therapies in modifying the cutaneous–systemic disease continuum.

Cytokine and biomarker networks

Inducible IFN-α expression alone can reproduce hallmark features of SLE in healthy mice, including immune complex deposition, glomerulonephritis, and alopecia, driven in part by double-negative T cells producing IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-17, and TNF-α [156]. In CLE patients, serum IFN-α bioactivity correlates with cutaneous disease severity and predicts subsequent SLE development [157].

Keratinocytes from CLE lesions exhibit a pre-activated state, releasing more IL-6 than controls in response to TLR stimulation or ultraviolet B exposure, with marked IL-6 upregulation in lesional epidermis [158]. IFN-κ further amplifies this response in an autocrine loop, whereas IFN-κ blockade reduces IL-6 production, highlighting an interferon–IL-6 axis as a driver of chronic skin inflammation. In lupus-prone MRL-lpr mice, IL-6 deficiency delays renal injury and reduces immune complex deposition and leukocyte infiltration [159], providing mechanistic evidence that IL-6 accelerates systemic organ damage. Consistent with these observations, multi-omics profiling has identified concurrent upregulation of IFN- and IL-6–responsive genes in CLE skin, with strong correlations to systemic activity scores [1].

IL-4 may contribute to CLE-to-SLE progression as a context-dependent immune modulator rather than a primary driver. Skin tissues from SLE patients with concomitant cutaneous involvement, including DLE and SCLE, show significantly higher proportions of IL-4+ CD4+ T cells compared with SLE patients without cutaneous disease and healthy controls [160]. Beyond the skin, IL-4 signaling influences autoreactive B-cell fate. Although naïve B cells in SLE exhibit impaired IL-4 responsiveness, residual IL-4–STAT6 signaling can restore B-cell receptor recycling, reverse functional anergy, and promote differentiation of pathogenic B-cell subsets [161,162]. In parallel, IL-4 derived from basophil–T cell interactions enhances Th2 responses and autoantibody production, linking local immune cues to systemic humoral activation [163].

Collectively, current evidence positions type I interferon and IL-6 signaling as core drivers of the cutaneous–systemic interface in lupus, while IL-4 functions as an immunological modulator that modulates activation of pathogenic immune cells. Additional cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-17, may further reinforce the network. Together, these interconnected cytokine pathways underscore the value of integrated cytokine profiling as a predictive biomarker strategy.

For patients with localized or treatment-resistant cutaneous lesions, topical and local interventions remain important adjunctive options. Intralesional injections of triamcinolone are beneficial in refractory localized DLE [8,164]. Topical retinoic acid is an option for hyperkeratotic or recurrent lesions, with reported improvements in pigmentation, erythema, and atrophy [165,166]. Pulsed-dye laser therapy has shown efficacy in recurrent but non-progressive CLE by disrupting lesional vasculature [167].

When antimalarials and corticosteroids are insufficient, several conventional immunosuppressive and immunomodulatory agents have been employed as second-line agents. These include lenalidomide, thalidomide, retinoids, dapsone, methotrexate, and mycophenolate [6,26]. Thalidomide and lenalidomide are effective in DLE and SCLE but are limited by cumulative toxicity and are therefore recommended at low doses, often in combination with antimalarials [168,169]. Retinoids are useful in hyperkeratotic or verrucous CLE, especially when antimalarials alone are insufficient [170]. Dapsone remains first-line for bullous SLE and may benefit DLE and SCLE refractory to conventional treatment [171]. Low-dose methotrexate with folate supplementation provides modest efficacy [172], while mycophenolate is an option in multi-refractory cases [173,174]. Lenalidomide appears ineffective for lupus chorioretinitis, which may respond better to thalidomide and methotrexate [[175], [176], [177]].

Biologic therapies targeting distinct immune pathways have also been employed in the treatment of CLE. B-cell directed therapies such as Belimumab and Rituximab have shown benefit in subsets of CLE patients, particularly those with refractory disease [3,178]. The T cell co-stimulation modulator abatacept has also been evaluated, with modest efficacy in cutaneous manifestations [3]. More recently, the interferon receptor antagonist Anifrolumab was approved for SLE and demonstrated improvement in skin disease activity, supporting its potential role in CLE ,[[22], [179], [180], [181]]. A number of novel biologics are under active investigation for CLE. Anti-BDCA2 antibody Litifilimab (BIIB059) and IFN-γ antagonist AMG811 are designed to modulate interferon signaling and have shown promising early results [3,104]. Ustekinumab, targeting the IL-12/23 pathway, was tested in CLE, especially those with concurrent psoriasis [[182], [183], [184]]. In a phase II SLE trial, Ustekinumab showed significant skin improvement, with ≥50% CLASI reduction achieved in 79% of treated patients versus placebo [182]. However, subsequent phase III trials failed to meet primary endpoints, leading to early termination. In contrast, Secukinumab (IL-17A blockade) has been linked to lupus flares and is therefore not recommended for CLE management [185].

Beyond interferon- and B cell–targeted approaches, emerging data suggest that dysregulation of the Th2 axis, particularly IL-4/IL-13 signaling, may contribute to B-cell activation and tissue involvement in lupus, including cutaneous manifestations [186,187]. Although anti-Th2 biologics have not been systematically evaluated in CLE or SLE, their shared receptor pathways and mechanistic relevance support further exploration in selected patient subsets, particularly those with refractory cutaneous disease or Th2-skewed immune signatures.

In parallel, small-molecule inhibitors have gained increasing attention. Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors have shown favorable responses in refractory CLE. Agents such as Upadacitinib and Filgotinib (JAK1), Tofacitinib (JAK1/3), Baricitinib (JAK1/2), and Ruxolitinib (topical JAK1/2 inhibitor) all demonstrated effective lesion clearance with a low incidence of mild infections and no reports of serious adverse events [[188], [189], [190]]. The latest research indicate that Deucravacitinib (tyrosine kinase 2 inhibitor) can significantly improve the Physician Global Assessment scores and the quality of life of patients with refractory CLE [191]. Although not yet approved for CLE, JAK inhibition appears to be a promising avenue, especially for refractory and recurrent CLE cases [[192], [193], [194]].

Several cytokine-based therapeutic strategies have also been proposed based on emerging immunopathogenic insights. IL-2 is a crucial cytokine for the growth and survival of Treg cells, and low-dose IL-2 has shown potential in restoring Treg homeostasis and improving outcomes in SLE, though its role in CLE remains less defined [195,196]. IL-6 and IL-17 blockade represent additional targets under investigation, but further validation is required before clinical application.

Toward targeted strategies to prevent systemic transition

A major challenge in CLE management is the early identification of patients at risk of progressing to SLE. Clinicians need to be vigilant when patients with CLE present known risk factors or fulfill items of SLE classification criteria. Evidence for strategies that effectively prevent systemic transition remains limited. Retrospective studies suggest that hydroxychloroquine may delay progression to SLE, as patients not treated with hydroxychloroquine were diagnosed with SLE earlier than those receiving the drug [41]. Unfortunately, there is no clear evidence to confirm these conjectures.

Therapeutic approaches for CLE largely overlap with those for SLE, including antimalarials, glucocorticoids, and immunosuppressants. Once systemic involvement develops, treatment escalation should be individualized according to disease severity and organ involvement. In contrast, systemic immunosuppressants, such as azathioprine, cyclosporine, cyclophosphamide, and biologics, are generally not recommended for isolated CLE in order to avoid unnecessary adverse effects.

Future directions

Despite growing recognition of the CLE-SLE transition, several key knowledge gaps remain. First, the ability to reliably predict which patients with CLE will progress to SLE is still limited. Most available biomarkers are derived from cross-sectional studies and lack prospective validation, underscoring the need for longitudinal cohorts integrating clinical, serological, and immunological profiling. Second, the precise mechanistic pathways by which persistent CLE contributes to systemic immune dysregulation remain incompletely defined. In particular, how skin-derived immune signals influence distant target organs such as the kinder and cardiovascular system warrants further investigation. Finally, management of refractory CLE continues to pose clinical challenges. Current therapies often provide limited efficacy and increased risks of side effects. Future studies should focus on identifying novel therapeutic targets and optimizing treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes. There is also a need for comprehensive studies that explore the full spectrum of factors influencing CLE progression, as well as the relationship between cutaneous and systemic manifestations of lupus.

Conclusion

This review synthesizes current evidence supporting a continuum between cutaneous and systemic lupus erythematosus (Fig. 2), highlighting CLE as a heterogeneous condition with variable risk of systemic progression. A more precise understanding of the CLE–SLE interface may ultimately enable earlier intervention and more personalized approaches to prevent systemic disease evolution.
