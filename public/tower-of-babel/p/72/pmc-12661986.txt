Long-term follow-up study on the impact of a gut microbiota-supportive diet on heart failure and mortality outcomes
Authors: Minghui Xu, Xiaohan Zhang, Haiping xin, Li Daoming, Xuanjian Xiao, Xuecong Zhang, Shilin Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiet-driven modulation of gut microbiota may influence heart failure (HF) risk and mortality, but evidence in large populations is limited. We evaluated the association between a Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) and prevalent HF.MethodsDI-GM scores were derived from two 24-h dietary recalls. Logistic regression assessed odds of HF, diabetes, stroke, kidney failure, hypertension, and rheumatoid arthritis across DI-GM categories (1–3, 4, 5, ≥6), adjusting for demographics, lifestyle, BMI, and comorbidities. Restricted cubic splines examined dose–response. Mediation analysis quantified the roles of systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and C-reactive protein (CRP). Kaplan-Meier and Cox models evaluated all-cause and cardiovascular mortality by DI-GM (>4 vs 1–4).ResultsCompared to DI-GM 1–3, scores of 5 and ≥6 were associated with 21–40 % lower odds of HF (adjusted OR 0.79, p = 0.034; OR 0.60, p = 2.3 × 10−5). Similar inverse associations were observed for diabetes, stroke, and kidney failure. Spline analysis revealed a nonlinear HF risk decline beyond scores of 4–5. Component-level analyses showed fiber and coffee were linked to significant reductions in CRP, SII and heart failure odds (fiber OR 0.85; coffee OR 0.77). Mediation by SII and CRP accounted for 26 % and 32 % of the DI-GM–HF association. Higher DI-GM (>4) predicted lower all-cause (HR 0.83; p < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.71; p < 0.001).ConclusionsGut-microbiota–supportive diet (DI-GM ≥ 5) is linked to substantially lower prevalence of HF and related conditions, partly via reduced inflammation, and confers long-term survival benefits.Keywords: C-Reactive protein, Dietary index for gut microbiota, Gut microbiota diversity, Heart failure, Mortality, Systemic immune-inflammation index

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains one of the foremost public health challenges globally and continues to impose substantial healthcare costs [1]. Between 2007 and 2017, the global number of CVD-related deaths rose by approximately 21 %, mainly driven by aging populations [2], and predictions suggest CVD could cause around 23 million fatalities annually by 2030 [3]. Heart failure (HF), a chronic and progressive form of CVD characterized by impaired cardiac function and elevated intracardiac pressures, typically represents the final stage of multiple cardiovascular disorders [4].

Emerging evidence indicates a significant role for gut microbiota diversity and microbial metabolites in the pathogenesis and progression of heart failure [5]. Mechanistically, alterations in gut microbiota can affect cardiac function by disrupting intestinal barrier integrity, increasing bacterial translocation, and elevating inflammatory responses [6]. Dietary interventions represent a promising avenue for modulating gut microbiota, suggesting potential new strategies to reduce heart failure risk [7]. Although traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia contribute substantially to the progression of CVD, evidence supports dietary improvements as effective preventive measures against these risk factors [[8], [9], [10]]. Indeed, poor dietary habits alone have been linked to approximately 72 % of CVD mortality [11]. Furthermore, plant-based dietary patterns characterized by higher plant food intake and lower animal food consumption have demonstrated protective associations against cardiovascular diseases and even certain cancers [12,13].

To specifically evaluate dietary influence on gut microbiota, the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) was developed for their potential effects on microbial health [14]. Through a systematic review of 106 intervention and longitudinal studies, 13 dietary components (Tea is unavailable) were identified as having consistent “beneficial” (e.g., fermented dairy, fiber) or “unfavorable” (e.g., red meat, processed mea) effects on the gut microbial ecosystem. Each component is scored 0 or 1 based on whether intake falls on the healthier side, 1 point each if intake “beneficial” foods ≥ the sex-specific median, 1 point each if intake “unfavorable” foods < the median or fat <40 % energy. These are summed to yield a total DI-GM ranging from 0 (least favorable) to 13 (most favorable). A higher DI-GM score indicates a dietary pattern predicted to foster a richer, more diverse microbial community.

However, the association of DI-GM with heart failure risk, its impact on cardiovascular-related and all-cause mortality, and the mediating role of systemic inflammation remain inadequately explored. This study thus aims to assess the relationship between DI-GM and heart failure risk, its influence on mortality outcomes, and to investigate whether C-reactive protein (CRP) and Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) mediates these associations.

Methods

Study design and population

We performed a prospective analysis using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [15], acccess in January 21st, 2025. All NHANES data are de-identified and publicly accessible. The study did not involve direct contact with participants. Participants aged ≥40 years with complete 24-h dietary recalls and available CRP measurements were included. Individuals with a prior diagnosis of heart failure at baseline, implausible energy intakes, or missing key covariate information were excluded. After excluding individuals with unavailable data, a total of 23,835 participants remained in the analysis, of whom 1111 had heart failure. Mortality follow-up was obtained through the NHANES Linked Mortality File, which links participants to the National Death Index. The follow-up period from 1999 to 2019. Fig. 1 shows the detailed flow chart. This study used publicly available, de-identified data. All NHANES participants provided informed consent at the time of data collection, and the survey protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/erb.html#print. No additional consent was required for this analysis.

Study Population Selection Process for NHANES Data Analysis. Initial Sample: NHANES 2007–2018 & 2021–2023 (n = 71,775); The 2019–2020 NHANES cycle was not included because field operations were suspended in March 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic, and data collection for that cycle was not completed. Exclusions: Age<40, missing demographic/lifestyle data, diet, heart failure, or comorbidities. Final Sample: 23,835 participants, including 1111 with heart failure.

DI-GM calculation

Dietary information was collected through two separate 24-h dietary recalls, employing the standardized United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) dietary assessment protocol. Specifically, the DI-GM includes ten beneficial components (fermented dairy, chickpeas, soybean, whole grains, fiber, cranberries, avocados, broccoli, coffee, and green tea) and four unfavorable components (red meat, processed meat, refined grains, and high-fat diet, defined as fat derived enery≥40 % of total energy intake). For each food or nutrient, sex-specific median intakes were calculated (except for the high-fat diet, which used a fixed 40 % cutoff). Participants received 1 point if their intake was above the median for beneficial components, or below the median (or cutoff) for unfavorable components. Otherwise, they received 0 points. Scores were summed across all components, yielding a total DI-GM score ranging from 0 to 13, with higher scores indicating a more microbiota-supportive diet. The detailed dietary components used for scoring are provided in Supplement Table 1. Higher DI-GM scores—calculated reflect greater microbiota-supportive diet.

Outcome

Heart failure was defined by self-reported physician diagnosis confirmed through available medical records or adjudication protocols within NHANES. The date of first diagnosis or hospitalization was recorded as the event time. Mortality data were obtained through linkage with the National Death Index. Heart disease–specific mortality was identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes corresponding to cardiovascular causes, while all-cause mortality encompassed any death recorded during the follow-up period.

Measurement of CRP and SII

Baseline blood samples were analyzed for high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP) using standardized immunoassays. CRP (mg/L) values were log-transformed for all statistical analyses and used as a continuous variable in mediation models. The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) was calculated as (platelet count × neutrophil count)/lymphocyte count, using absolute cell counts.

To minimize confounding from conditions that elevate inflammatory markers, we excluded participants with acute infection/inflammation—defined as hs-CRP >10 mg/L, white blood cell count (WBC) > 11,000/μL, or antibiotic use within the prior 30 days—and those with chronic inflammatory conditions including asthma, chronic bronchitis/COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease), gout, celiac disease, and psoriasis.

Covariates

Potential confounders included age, race, education level, poverty–income ratio (PIR), marital status, alcohol use, smoking status, physical activity, BMI, comorbidities. Poverty–income ratio (PIR) is a measure of a family's income in relation to the U.S. federal poverty threshold for that survey year, adjusted for family size. A value of 1.0 indicates income exactly at the poverty level, values below 1.0 indicate income below poverty, and values above 1.0 indicate income above poverty (top-coded at 5.0) Marital status include Married/Living with partner or Widowed/Divorced/Separated/Never married, alcohol use include never drink and drinl now and before. Smoking status include current smokers, former smokers (100 cigarettes or more but no longer smoked), never smokers (less than 100 cigarettes but no longer smoked). Physical activity was classified into three groups: inactive (<1 h/week of moderate or vigorous activity), moderately active (neither inactive nor active), and vigorous (>2.5 h/week or > 1 h/week). BMI, comorbidities (anemia, diabetes, kidney failure, liver disease, rheumatoid arthritis, stroke, or hypertension). These variables were selected based on prior evidence linking them with both dietary patterns and cardiovascular outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Participant baseline characteristics were reported as mean ± standard deviation(SD) for continuous data and as percentages for categorical data. We utilized Cox regression to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). To investigate whether CRP mediated the association between DI-GM scores and heart failure risk, a mediation analysis based on regression modeling was performed using the R package mediation (4.5.0). The total association was decomposed into a direct pathway (not mediated by CRP) and an indirect pathway mediated by CRP levels. Odds ratios (OR) are used for binary outcomes, whereas β‐coefficients (Beta) are used for continuous outcomes.” Confidence intervals (CI) for mediation effects were computed using a bootstrap approach with 5000 iterations. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of <0.05. All statistical procedures were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Among 23,835 participants, 1111 were diagnosed with heart failure (Table 1). As shown in the table, participants with heart failure were significantly older and had a higher mean BMI. They were also more likely to have a lower education level and a lower mean poverty–income ratio, were less likely to be married or living with a partner, and were more likely to be non-drinkers. They tended to be less physically active and had substantially higher rates of comorbid conditions. Finally, those with heart failure had a lower mean total score of DI-GM compared to those without heart failure.

Higher DI-GM scores (≥5) are associated with lower odds of heart failure

In unadjusted logistic models (Model A; Table 2), compared with the reference group (DI-GM 1–3), a DI-GM score of 5 was associated with 30 % lower odds of prevalent heart failure (OR 0.70; p = 6.8 × 10−4), and a score ≥6 with 47 % lower odds (OR 0.53; p = 4.0 × 10−9). A DI-GM score of 4 showed no significant association (OR 1.03; p = 0.756). After adjustment for age, race, education, poverty–income ratio, marital status, alcohol use, smoking, physical activity, BMI, and comorbidities (Model B), the protective associations persisted for DI-GM = 5 (OR 0.79; p = 0.034) and DI-GM ≥ 6 (OR 0.60; p = 2.3 × 10−5), while DI-GM = 4 remained non-significant (OR 1.06; p = 0.630). Thus, only participants with DI-GM scores ≥5 exhibited a statistically significant 20–40 % reduction in odds of heart failure.

Higher DI-GM scores (≥5) are associated with lower odds of diabetes, stroke, and kidney failure

In adjusted models (Table 3), a DI-GM score of 5 (versus 1–3) was linked to significantly lower odds of diabetes (OR 0.80, p = 3.4 × 10−4), stroke (OR 0.81, p = 0.046), and kidney failure (OR 0.67, p = 5.7 × 10−4). Participants with a DI-GM ≥6 experienced even greater reductions: diabetes OR 0.72 (p = 1.5 × 10−7), stroke OR 0.73 (p = 3.6 × 10−3), and kidney failure OR 0.57 (p = 2.8 × 10−6). In contrast, DI-GM = 4 showed no significant associations for any outcome There were also no significant associations at any score for hypertension or rheumatoid arthritis (p > 0.17).

Differential dietary components across DI-GM score and associated with heart failure

Participants with DI-GM scores >4 differed sharply from those with scores ≤4 across both beneficial and unfavorable components (Fig. 2). Among beneficial foods, prevalence in the >4 group versus = 4 and < 4 was: fiber (70.5 % vs 31.4 % vs 11.3 %), fermented dairy (59.7 % vs 30.0 % vs 13.8 %), coffee (72.0 % vs 43.9 % vs 23.2 %), broccoli (6.9 % vs 1.4 % vs 0.4 %), whole grains (2.2 % vs 0.4 % vs 0.05 %), and smaller but consistent gains in avocado (4.6 % vs 0.7 % vs 0.1 %), soybean (2.3 % vs 0.3 % vs 0.15 %), chickpeas, and cranberries. Conversely, intake of red meat (30.6 % vs 60.0 % vs 83.3 %), processed meat (0.24 % vs 0.89 % vs 4.12 %), and refined grains (3.34 % vs 4.68 % vs 5.70 %) fell progressively from <4 to >4, while the fat-to-total-energy ratio declined from 40.1 % (<4) to 35.7 % (=4) and 33.4 % (>4). These shifts—especially large increases in fiber, fermented dairy, and coffee, and sharp decreases in red meat—highlight the component differences driving the protective effect at DI-GM > 4.

Dietary component distribution and fat intake across DI-GM score groups.

Next, to assess component-level associations with heart failure risk, we first stratified participants by sex and calculated the median intake of each dietary component separately for men and women. Individuals whose intake exceeded the sex-specific median were coded as 1 (higher intake), and those at or below the median were coded as 0 (lower intake). In our adjusted analysis (Table 4), four dietary factors emerged as significant, independent predictors of lower heart failure risk. Participants whose avocado intake exceeded the sex-specific median had 48 % lower odds of heart failure (OR 0.52, p = 0.0368). Similarly, individuals with higher intakes of coffee, fermented dairy, and total dietary fiber—defined as consumption above the sex-specific median for each component—also showed a 15–23 % lower risk (coffee OR 0.77, p = 0.0004; fermented dairy OR 0.84, p = 0.016; fiber OR 0.85, p = 0.0168). By contrast, other “beneficial” components (broccoli, cranberries, whole grains) did not reach significance (all p > 0.37), nor did any “unfavorable” items—red meat, refined grains, total fat share, or processed meat—although processed meat trended toward increased risk (OR 3.13, p = 0.0541). These findings indicate that among the 13 DI‐GM constituents, higher intakes of avocado, coffee, fermented dairy, and fiber have a key protective effect on heart failure.

Mediation analysis of SII and CRP in the DI-GM to heart failure relationship

Considering infection and chronic inflammation conditions that could affect CRP and SII, we do a mediation analyses after excluding 2315 participants with hs-CRP >10 mg/L, WBC >11,000/μL, antibiotic use in the past 30 days, or chronic inflammatory diseases (asthma, chronic bronchitis/COPD, inflammatory bowel disease, gout, celiac disease, psoriasis). In adjusted mediation models (Table 6), higher DI-GM scores were linked to lower systemic inflammation—both SII and CRP played significant intermediary roles. Specifically, each one-point increase in DI-GM corresponded to a 0.0255-unit decrease in log SII (β = −0.0255; p = 3.43 × 10−4), and elevated log SII, in turn, increased the log-odds of heart failure (β = 0.5395; p = 2.96 × 10−3). The average causal mediation effect (ACME) through SII was −0.0150 (p = 1.11 × 10−2), while the direct effect of DI-GM on heart failure (ADE) controlling for SII was not significant (β = −0.0425; p = 0.0688), the SII-mediated proportion was 26.1 %. These indicating that reduced SII mediated important protective effect.

Dietary components associated with systemic inflammation

Higher intakes of fiber and coffee were the most consistent anti-inflammatory predictors, each reducing both CRP (mg/L) and SII. Every additional gram of fiber was linked to a 0.0200-unit decrease in log(SII) (p = 1.73 × 10−4) (Table 7) and a 0.0597-unit decrease in log(CRP) (p = 2.43 × 10−6) (Table 8). Similarly, each extra 1g of coffee corresponded to a 0.0089-unit decline in log(SII) (p = 4.84 × 10−2) and a 0.0457-unit reduction in log(CRP) (p = 5.67 × 10−4).

Discussion

In this large cohort of 23,835 U S. adults, higher DI‐GM was robustly associated with lower odds of prevalent heart failure and low mortality. In adjusted models, participants scoring ≥5 points on the 13‐item DI‐GM experienced 20–40 percent lower odds of HF than those scoring 1–3. These protective associations extended to other cardio‐metabolic outcomes, including diabetes, stroke, and chronic kidney disease—yet not hypertension or rheumatoid arthritis—underscoring that a microbiota‐friendly diet may particularly mitigate HF and certain comorbidities.

Similarly, Jin et al. reported that every additional DI-GM point corresponded to a 4 % lower odds of prevalent cardiovascular disease, and those in the highest tertile (scores 6–12) had a 13 % lower odds compared to participants scoring 0–3 [16]. In contrast, we found that each one-point rise in DI-GM corresponded to a around 7 % reduction in incident heart failure risk, with those scoring ≥6 experiencing a 40 % lower odds of heart failure even after covariate adjustment. The differences may be attributable to their inclusion of younger individuals, such as those aged 18 to 40, who were not excluded from the their analysis. Liu et al. found a one‐point rise in DI-GM was associated with a 7 % lower odds of prevalent stroke [17] and prevalent diabetes in study of Huang Y et al. [18], while individuals scoring 5 or ≥ 6 exhibited approximately 20 % lower stroke prevalence and participants scoring 5 had significantly reduced risk of diabetes versus the 0–3 reference group. Moreover, 41 % of that association was mediated through phenotypic age and 26 % through body mass index between DI-GM and diabetes. A diverse gut microbiota enhances functional redundancy, ensuring that critical processes including dietary fiber fermentation and beneficial short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) synthesis—remain stable during environmental fluctuations [19]. SCFAs, in turn, support intestinal barrier integrity, modulate immune responses, and reduce systemic inflammation, which are all essential for metabolic regulation and disease prevention [20,21]. Furthermore, a diverse microbial community can outcompete pathogens for resources and niches, thereby reducing the risk of infections and related inflammatory conditions. The DI-GM, designed to capture the diet–gut microbiota interface, appears to more precisely reflect gut microbial health than broader indices such as the HEI-2015 (Healthy Eating Index-2015; 0–100 overall diet-quality score reflecting adherence to U.S. Dietary Guidelines) and MDS (Mediterranean Diet Score; 0–9 index of adherence to a Mediterranean-style dietary pattern) [22]. Unlike these conventional indices that assess overall diet quality and have shown inconsistent associations with microbiota diversity [23,24], the DI-GM focuses on specific foods with documented beneficial or adverse effects. Similar to the DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) - which emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy, and lower sodium, while limiting red/processed meats and sweets - a high DI-GM diet may reduce oxidative stress in heart failure patients, possibly due to a high intake of antioxidant-rich foods (e.g., fruits and vegetables) [25] and nutrients like magnesium and vitamin C, which have been shown to enhance total antioxidant capacity in healthy young adults [26]. These antioxidant compounds are believed to preserve endothelial function [27] and thereby contribute to improved cardiac performance. Adherence to a Mediterranean diet has also been associated with better quality of life in individuals with heart failure [28], a finding supported by observations in a North American cohort [29] and a large Spanish study involving over 11,000 participants that reported significant improvements in physical and mental health [30].

In line with this, our analysis identified coffee, fermented dairy, and dietary fiber as particularly protective against incident heart failure and as robust modulators of systemic inflammation. Among these, dietary fiber stands out for its multifaceted, gut‐mediated anti‐inflammatory actions: by reshaping bile acid pools, both fermentable and insoluble fibers activate FXR and TGR5 signaling to strengthen the intestinal barrier and curb endotoxemia [[32], [33], [34]], while enriching cellulose‐ and pectin‐degrading taxa that foster anti‐inflammatory microbial communities [35]. Further, fermentation of soluble fibers generates SCFAs that engage GPR41/43 on immune and adipose cells to restore immune homeostasis [36,37], and promotes fecal excretion of branched‐chain amino acids to dampen mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin)‐driven inflammation [38]. Soluble fibers also directly activate toll‐like receptors to trigger anti‐inflammatory cascades in murine models [39], and by suppressing postprandial GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) secretion they reduce ectopic lipid deposition and hypothalamic inflammation [40,41]. Together, these mechanistic insights help explain why, within a high‐quality diet—alongside components like coffee and fermented dairy—ample fiber intake emerges as a cornerstone for reducing heart‐failure risk through inflammation attenuation.

Similarly, coffee-another key food component identified in our analysis—exerts potent anti‐inflammatory effects through both direct bioactive actions and modulation of the gut microbiome. Its diverse array of compounds—caffeine, chlorogenic acid (and its roasting‐derived pyrocatechol, trigonelline, melanoidins, the diterpenes kahweol and cafestol and caffeic acid [42]—work in concert to quell inflammation: they scavenge free radicals, inhibit NF‐κB (nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells), activate Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2) [43], sirtuin-1, and peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor-gamma [44], suppress pro‐inflammatory cytokines, inhibit matrix metalloproteinases, and modulate T helper type 1/type 2 cells balance [45]. Collectively, these actions underpin moderate coffee consumption as an effective dietary means to mitigate inflammatory signaling. Moreover, coffee's benefits extend beyond its bioactive phytochemicals to reshaping gut microbial composition and diversity [46]. Habitual coffee consumers exhibit higher levels of Bacteroides, Porphyromonas, and Prevotella compared to non‐drinkers [47], and caffeine intake correlates with increased alpha diversity and greater abundances of the anti‐inflammatory genera Faecalibacterium and Alistipes in the colonic mucosa [48]. This evidence suggests that coffee's bioactive compounds are both transformed by—and concurrently remodel—the gut ecosystem, fostering taxa linked to metabolic and immune health. By integrating these mechanistic pathways with our stage‐specific “heart nutrition” approach, we highlight how high‐quality components like dietary fiber and coffee synergize to attenuate systemic inflammation and thereby protect against the progression of heart failure.

Building on its microbiota‐mediated benefits, fermented dairy also delivers potent immunomodulatory effects that can translate into cardiovascular protection. By selectively enriching Bifidobacterium spp. (notably B. infantis and B. breve) and boosting anti‐inflammatory taxa such as Faecalibacterium and Alistipes, fermented milks reshape the gut ecosystem toward a milieu less prone to endotoxemia and systemic inflammation [49]. Beyond this, bioactive peptides and short‐chain fatty acids generated during fermentation directly engage the host immune system: supernatants from B. breve C50–fermented milk uniquely activate and mature human dendritic cells to favor regulatory cytokine profiles [50], while murine and human trials have shown that daily fermented‐milk intake increases secretory IgA (immunoglobulin A), expands interleukin-10–producing regulatory T cells, and enhances macrophage phagocytosis—actions that collectively suppress pro‐inflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-1 beta [51]. These combined effects on barrier integrity and immune homeostasis are precisely the mechanistic pathways by which fermented dairy intake is believed to mitigate low‐grade inflammation, a key driver of myocardial remodeling and heart‐failure progression. While targeted approaches such as fermented dairy or high‐fiber diets can enrich specific beneficial taxa (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium) and dampen systemic inflammation, attempts to restore gut health using only a handful of probiotic strains have often produced inconsistent outcomes [52]. Indeed, although genera like Bifidobacterium and Blautia are broadly regarded as “good” microbes [53,54], elevated Blautia has been linked to inflammatory disorders [55], and some studies have even found a surprising positive association between Bifidobacterium abundance and coronary heart disease [56]. Such contradictions likely stem from substantial functional variation among species within these genera—for example, B. animalis, B. keratocidum, and B. adolescentis each differ markedly in their metabolic and immunomodulatory capacities [57]. In light of these complexities, interventions that boost overall microbial diversity—rather than introducing a small number of strains—may offer greater promise. Fecal microbiota transplantation, which transfers a full, complex community of microorganisms, has demonstrated sustained colonization of beneficial species (as shown in the “Repopulate” study's 33‐species consortium) and is now recommended by recent guidelines for restoring ecosystem balance [58]. By prioritizing diverse, resilient microbial assemblies over narrow, single‐strain supplements, such strategies aim to more effectively recalibrate gut ecology and support cardiovascular as well as metabolic health.

There are several limitations. First, our analysis relies on a single baseline DI‐GM score derived from two 24‐hour recalls, so we cannot account for any subsequent dietary changes that participants may have made, which mean may not accurately reflect long-term eating habits. Unlike broader indices such as the HEI-2015 and MDS, which evaluate overall diet quality, the DI-GM focuses specifically on microbiota-related dietary components. While this provides mechanistic insight, it may overlook other important aspects of diet. Second, the DI-GM is a novel index may not fully reflect the complexity of gut microbial composition. Moreover, these data were obtained from a US population; additional studies are needed to explore these associations in other populations.

In summary, participants with a DI-GM score ≥5 had 20–40 percent lower odds of prevalent heart failure and, when stratified by DI-GM >4, experienced a 29 percent reduction in cardiovascular mortality and a 17 percent reduction in all-cause mortality. Approximately 30–35 percent of the DI-GM–heart failure association was mediated by lower systemic inflammation, as reflected in reduced SII and CRP. Future intervention trials should test whether increasing intake of fiber, coffee, fermented dairy, and avocado can replicate these anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects.
