Demographically adjusted norms for a French-Quebec version of the face–name associative recognition test
Authors: Joël Macoir, Mariane Landry, Carol Hudon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Face–Name Associative Recognition Test (FNART) is a recognition-based measure of associative episodic memory designed to reduce retrieval demands while preserving sensitivity to hippocampal-dependent binding processes. The absence of normative data currently limits its clinical use. This study aimed to establish normative data for a Quebec French version (FNART-Q).MethodFNART-Q performance was examined in a large sample of cognitively healthy French-speaking Quebec adults, and percentile-based normative data were developed accounting for demographic influences.ResultsA demographically stratified normative framework was developed using an overlapping stratification approach, and percentile ranks were derived to support clinical interpretation of individual scores.ConclusionsThe FNART-Q provides clinically interpretable normative data, supporting its use as a demographically adjusted measure of associative episodic memory.

Introduction

Associative memory has received considerable attention because of its sensitivity to age-related and neurological changes (Rubiño & Andrés, 2018). Face–name associative memory tasks are of particular interest because they place substantial demands on hippocampal-dependent binding processes while maintaining strong ecological validity (Sperling et al., 2003). However, the clinical usefulness of such measures depends not only on their theoretical relevance, but also on the availability of robust normative data allowing accurate interpretation of individual performance. Although episodic memory impairment is a hallmark feature of neurodegenerative conditions, traditional memory tests often lack sensitivity in the early phases of the disease (Jutten et al., 2021), in part because they allow for compensatory strategies or exhibit ceiling effects in cognitively intact individuals (Rabin et al., 2009; Torrealba et al., 2023).

Associative episodic memory has been proposed as a particularly vulnerable cognitive domain in the early stages of Alzheimer's disease (AD) (Bastin et al., 2014; Naveh-Benjamin et al., 2004). Tasks requiring the binding of unrelated pieces of information, especially cross-modal associations, place greater demands on hippocampal and medial temporal lobe structures than standard item memory paradigms. Experimental and neuroimaging studies indicate that the hippocampus is critically involved in maintaining high-resolution associative bindings and in integrating elements from distinct sensory or conceptual domains into coherent event representations (Borders et al., 2017; Horner et al., 2015; Yonelinas, 2013). Among these, face–name associative memory tasks have attracted considerable interest because they combine high ecological validity with strong theoretical relevance to hippocampal binding processes (Klamer et al., 2017; Sperling et al., 2003). Difficulties in remembering names associated with faces are among the most frequent memory complaints reported by older adults and have been shown to relate to early Alzheimer’s pathology (Kormas et al., 2020; Rentz et al., 2011). However, despite their increasing use in research and clinical contexts, the interpretation of performance on such tasks remains limited by the lack of well-characterized normative data.

Several studies have examined the psychometric properties and clinical sensitivity of Face–Name Associative Memory Exam (FNAME) variants, including large-scale initiatives such as the Assessing Reliable Measurement in Alzheimer's Disease and Cognitive Aging study (ARMADA) as well as recent work demonstrating reliability and validity using digital assessment platforms (e.g., Amariglio et al., 2012; Papp et al., 2014; Rentz et al., 2023, 2025). However, relatively few studies have focused on the development of normative frameworks suitable for individual-level interpretation (e.g., Alviarez-Schulze et al., 2022; Rubiño & Andrés, 2018). In neuropsychological assessment, normative data are essential for distinguishing expected variation associated with demographic characteristics from potentially clinically meaningful impairment. Without appropriate demographic adjustment, the interpretation of individual performance may be inaccurate, particularly in older adults where age, education, and sex frequently influence cognitive test scores.

The FNAME was originally developed to assess cross-modal associative memory, combining visual and verbal elements in a demanding paired-associate recall paradigm (Amariglio et al., 2012; Rentz et al., 2011). Face–name associative memory tasks have attracted considerable interest in aging and memory research because they place substantial demands on associative binding processes while maintaining strong ecological validity. Previous studies have demonstrated the clinical relevance of these paradigms in both cognitively healthy older adults and individuals experiencing memory difficulties (Macoir et al., 2024b; Rentz et al., 2011; Sanabria et al., 2018). However, a comprehensive review of face–name associative memory tests highlighted important variability across studies and emphasized the need for well-characterized normative data to support clinically meaningful interpretation of performance (Rubiño & Andrés, 2018).

To address some of the limitations of recall-based paradigms, the Face–Name Associative Recognition Test of Quebec (FNART-Q) was developed as a recognition-based adaptation of the FNAME (Polcher et al., 2017). By requiring participants to identify the correct name associated with each face rather than to freely recall it, the FNART-Q reduces task difficulty and stress while preserving the associative binding demands critical for detecting subtle memory impairment (Macoir et al., 2024b). Despite its potential clinical relevance, the absence of normative data currently limits its use in applied neuropsychological settings.

Although several validation and adaptation studies have been published for the FNAME, only a limited number provide normative data suitable for clinical use. To date, comprehensive normative data for the FNAME have been reported primarily for the Spanish version (Alviarez-Schulze et al., 2022), while other studies in English (Amariglio et al., 2012; Papp et al., 2014), Spanish (Sanabria et al., 2018), Latin American Spanish (Vila-Castelar et al., 2020) and Greek (Kormas et al., 2020) have focused on adaptation, psychometric properties, biomarker associations, or clinical discrimination rather than norm development. In contrast, the FNART has been investigated almost exclusively in clinical and at-risk populations, without the publication of normative data. Consequently, despite growing interest in face–name associative memory paradigms, the absence of population-specific normative data has limited the clinical interpretability of FNART-Q performance. The present study directly addresses this gap by providing the first demographically adjusted normative data for the FNART-Q in a French-speaking population.

The primary objective of the present study was therefore to establish normative data for the FNART-Q in a sample of cognitively healthy French-speaking adults, using a percentile-based approach to account for demographic influences. By providing demographically adjusted norms, this study aims to support the accurate and clinically meaningful interpretation of FNART-Q performance in neuropsychological assessment.

Methods

Participants

Various channels were used to recruit participants on a voluntary basis, including online announcements (such as social media and the Université Laval email list), public events like the Salon Alzheimer (Alzheimer’s Awareness Day), and the pool of potential participants of Dr. Hudon and Dr. Macoir.

All participants were native French speakers residing in Quebec (Canada), having acquired French as their first language in childhood and maintaining its use up to the time of data collection (Statistique Canada, 2022). Individuals were excluded if they met one or more criteria that could impact cognition, including: (1) a z-score of −1.33 or lower on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; Nasreddine et al., 2005), as per research team standards (Larouche et al., 2016); (2) cognitive complaints suggestive of subjective cognitive decline (Jessen et al., 2020); (3) an active and unstable psychiatric condition; (4) history of moderate or severe traumatic brain injury; (5) history of stroke; (6) history of intracranial surgery; (7) neurological disorder; (8) unstable or untreated medical or metabolic condition; (9) delirium within the past 6 months; (10) cancer treatment within the past year; (11) general anesthesia within the past 6 months; (12) history of psychotic symptoms or manic episodes; (13) history of substance abuse; and (14) uncorrected hearing or visual impairments. These exclusion criteria were assessed based on self-report during structured interview.

The participants ranged in age from 50 to 91 years and had between 7 and 23 years of education. Educational attainment was treated as a continuous variable but was interpreted within the framework of the Quebec education system: 6 years for elementary school, 11 years for a high school diploma, 13 years for a college diploma (or 14 years for technical degrees), 16 years for a bachelor’s degree, 18 years for a master’s degree, 21 years for a doctoral degree, and 23 years for medical or postdoctoral fellowships. When individuals had multiple degrees, only the highest level attained was considered. If participants held duplicate degrees at the same level (e.g., two master’s degrees), education was recorded at that level without aggregation. Special cases, such as professional training completed with or without a high school diploma, were assessed individually.

Recruitment and assessments took place between June 2021 and December 2025. Approval for the study (Project No. 2019-1541) was granted by the Comité d’Éthique de la Recherche Sectoriel en Neurosciences et Santé Mentale [Ethics Committee for Sectorial Research in Neurosciences and Mental Health], and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Material

For this study, a Quebec version of the FNART (FNART-Q) adapted from Polcher et al. (2017) was used. A total of 32 neutral facial photographs were chosen from the Minear and Park database (Minear & Park, 2004), with an equal distribution between male (n = 16) and female (n = 16) faces, spanning four age groups: 18–29 years, 30–49 years, 50–69 years, and 70–94 years. Each photograph was paired with a two-syllable first name chosen to be typical of the corresponding historical period and age cohort, based on its frequency of use in Quebec at that time. For individuals aged 18 to 49, name frequency was determined using the first name database compiled by Retraite Québec (2024). As comparable official data were unavailable for those born before 1980, names for individuals aged 49 and older were identified using genealogy websites.

Procedure

As a component of a broader validation and normative project involving multiple cognitive and language assessments, this study required participants to attend two evaluation sessions, each lasting 90 min. During the initial session, participants signed a written informed consent form and filled out a sociodemographic questionnaire. The MoCA was then administered to evaluate cognitive health. During this session, participants also completed the DDQ-30, a naming-from-definition test (Macoir et al., 2024a), the symbol digit modalities test (Bouchard et al., 2025; Smith, 1982) and the Test d’apprentissage verbal de Québec—9 items, an episodic memory measure adapted from the nine-word Philadelphia (repeatable) Verbal Learning Test (Libon et al., 1996). The FNART-Q was administered during the second session, immediately after the Grooved Pegboard Test (Lafayette Instrument, 2023) and before a number processing and calculation battery (Macoir et al., 2025), the simplified Krikorian version of the Tower of London test (Krikorian et al., 1994) and the Five Digit Test (Sedó et al., 2007). To minimize potential interference, the tests followed a predetermined sequence.

The FNART-Q paradigm comprised three successive phases: encoding, interference, and recognition (see Fig. 1 for illustrative examples). During the encoding phase, 32 face–first name pairings were displayed on a computer screen for 5 s each. Each pair appeared twice across two randomized blocks. Participants were instructed to read each first name aloud and to actively form an association between the face and the corresponding name in preparation for later retrieval. The instructions, delivered in French, informed participants that they would view photographs of male and female faces paired with first names, each presented twice, and that they should focus on memorizing the association between each face and its corresponding name because they would later be asked to identify the correct name linked to each face. The interference phase followed immediately and consisted of a 30-s backward counting task starting from 100. In the recognition phase, the 32 previously presented faces were shown again individually, each accompanied by two first names: the correct name and a foil name drawn from another face, matched for biological sex and approximate age. Participants were asked to indicate, without time constraints, which name corresponded to the displayed face. Across the task, each first name appeared twice, once as the target and once as a distractor. The spatial position of target and distractor names was counterbalanced across trials. Stimuli were presented in a pseudorandom sequence such that the same first name did not recur within six consecutive trials. Instructions (provided in French) explained that participants would again see each face along with two candidate names and should select the name correctly associated with the face.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi (version 2.6.45; The Jamovi project, 2025). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < .05. Descriptive statistics were computed to characterize the normative sample in terms of age, sex, education, cognitive functioning and FNART-Q performance. The distribution of FNART-Q scores was examined visually and statistically and was found to deviate from normality. No transformation was possible to normalize the distribution of scores. Consequently, non-parametric statistical methods were used for inferential analyses.

Associations between FNART-Q total scores and continuous demographic variables (age and years of formal education) were examined using Spearman rank-order correlation coefficients. Variables showing significant or meaningful associations with FNART-Q performance were subsequently incorporated into the normative framework. For the purpose of normative data presentation, these variables were operationalized in a categorical format to enhance clinical usability and facilitate the interpretation of individual scores. Group differences according to sex were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Effect sizes were reported where appropriate to facilitate interpretation independently of sample size.

Given the primary objective of the study, namely, the development of normative data, analyses were not intended to test specific hypotheses but rather to identify demographic variables relevant for stratification in the normative framework. Variables showing meaningful associations with FNART-Q performance were therefore considered for inclusion in the normative stratification procedure.

Normative data were established using an overlapping stratification approach as described by Pauker (1988). This method was selected to reduce artificial discontinuities associated with fixed age cut-offs and to provide smoother transitions across adjacent age ranges, thereby improving the clinical interpretability of normative estimates. Age was stratified using overlapping windows spanning approximately 20 years, with midpoint intervals incremented by about 10 years. For each overlapping age cell, FNART-Q performance was further stratified according to sex and education level. Education was dichotomized at 12 years, corresponding approximately to the completion of secondary education within the Quebec educational system. This approach was selected to balance demographic specificity, clinical usability, and the need to maintain adequate sample sizes within normative strata. Percentile ranks were calculated within each cell to support clinical interpretation of individual scores. Percentile-based norms were prioritized because they rely on minimal distributional assumptions, preserve the empirical score distribution of the FNART-Q, and are consistent with established clinical practices in neuropsychological assessment.

Results

The normative sample initially comprised 412 participants; however, 41 individuals were excluded for the following reasons: below-average performance on the MoCA (Nasreddine et al., 2005), defined as scores more than 1.33 SD below the mean according to age-, sex-, and education-adjusted norms (Larouche et al., 2016) (n = 25); unstable medical condition (n = 10); uncorrected hearing or visual impairment (n = 4); history of traumatic brain injury (n = 1); and a mother tongue other than French (n = 1). The final sample comprised 371 community-dwelling individuals, including 246 women and 125 men, ranging in age from 50 to 91 years (M = 67.6, SD = 7.59). Their years of formal education ranged from 7 to 23 (M = 15.5, SD = 3.15). Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the normative sample, including age, sex, and educational level.

Demographic factors of sex, age, and education were considered as potential influencers of FNART-Q performance. Spearman correlation analyses revealed a significant positive association between years of education and FNART-Q total score (r = 0.16, p = .002), indicating that individuals with higher educational attainment achieved better performance. In contrast, age was significantly and negatively correlated with FNART-Q scores (r = −0.31, p < .001), reflecting lower performance with increasing age.

Group differences according to sex were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test, which showed a significant effect of sex on FNART-Q performance (U = 11,366, p < .001), with women obtaining higher scores than men overall.

Given the significant associations observed between FNART-Q performance and age, education, and sex, these variables were incorporated into the normative framework to optimize the clinical interpretability of test scores.

Normative data were established using an overlapping strata procedure to estimate the percentiles for the FNART-Q total score while accounting for age, education level, and sex. Age was organized into overlapping windows spanning 20 years, with midpoint intervals incremented by 10 years, in order to reduce discontinuities associated with arbitrary age cutoffs and to improve the stability of percentile estimates across adjacent age ranges. To ensure the stability and interpretability of normative estimates, percentile reporting was limited to clinically meaningful values. Extreme percentile ranks were excluded in subgroups with small sample sizes, where such estimates may be unstable and potentially misleading.

Consistent with guidance from the American Academy of Clinical Neuropsychology (Guilmette et al., 2020), performance at or below the 8th percentile was used as the principal threshold for identifying statistically low performance, reflecting a widely adopted compromise between sensitivity and specificity in clinical neuropsychological evaluation. As emphasized in AACN recommendations, percentile classifications describe the rarity of a score within a normative distribution and must be interpreted in conjunction with broader clinical information.

Discussion

The present study aimed to establish normative data for the FNART-Q in a French-speaking population, using a percentile-based approach to account for demographic influences.

The normative analyses revealed that FNART-Q performance is influenced by demographic variables, including age, education, and sex, highlighting the necessity of demographic adjustment for accurate clinical interpretation. While previous studies of face–name associative memory using recall-based paradigms have consistently reported age-related effects on performance (Amariglio et al., 2012; Enriquez-Geppert et al., 2021; Naveh-Benjamin et al., 2004, 2009), the present findings extend this literature by demonstrating additional contributions of education and sex to associative recognition performance. The inclusion of sex in the normative framework was supported by the observed performance differences between men and women and is consistent with previous reports of female advantages on verbal and associative memory tasks.

The availability of normative data now allows clinicians to interpret FNART-Q performance within an appropriate demographic context, enhancing its utility for individual assessment. The FNART-Q may serve as a useful adjunct to standard neuropsychological measures for the assessment of associative memory deficits in adults and older individuals. It can also be contextualized within a broader set of memory measures designed to assess hippocampal-dependent processes. For instance, the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (Grober & Buschke, 1987) is widely used to evaluate associative memory and controlled encoding processes, while the Mnemonic Similarity Task (Stark et al., 2019) assesses the ability to discriminate highly similar stimuli, a function linked to pattern separation mechanisms. Although these paradigms differ in format and cognitive demands, they share a common focus on associative and relational memory processes. In this context, the FNART-Q offers a complementary approach by targeting associative binding through a recognition-based format that may facilitate performance in populations for whom recall-based tasks are more demanding.

Although the present study focused on normative data, the cognitive processes assessed by the FNART-Q are supported by neural networks that are commonly implicated across a range of clinical conditions. Associative memory relies primarily on the integrity of hippocampal and medial temporal lobe structures, which support the binding of disparate elements into unified representations (Eichenbaum et al., 2007; Kim & Lee, 2023; Mayes et al., 2007). In addition, successful performance involves broader distributed networks, including prefrontal regions supporting strategic processing and attentional control, as well as posterior cortical regions involved in perceptual integration (Rolls, 2022; Rolls et al., 2024). Given this network-level involvement, alterations in FNART-Q performance may reflect disruptions affecting these systems, suggesting potential applicability across conditions characterized by memory and network dysfunction. Future studies will be required to determine the sensitivity and specificity of the FNART-Q in different clinical populations. Moreover, the applicability and feasibility of the FNART-Q across different levels of cognitive impairment remain to be established, particularly in more advanced stages where task demands may exceed patients’ capacities.

Furthermore, recognition-based associative paradigms may be especially advantageous in populations for whom recall-based tasks are poorly tolerated or disproportionately influenced by non-memory factors such as anxiety, stress, or increased executive demands. Experimental and clinical studies indicate that recall tasks are more vulnerable than recognition tasks to stress and attentional disruption (Gagnon & Wagner, 2016; Shields et al., 2017), whereas recognition paradigms reduce retrieval demands while preserving sensitivity to underlying memory impairment (Lowndes & Savage, 2007; Vyhnálek et al., 2019). In the context of associative memory assessment, recognition-based face–name paradigms have been shown to remain sensitive to pathological memory changes while minimizing task burden (Macoir et al., 2024b; Polcher et al., 2017). The normative framework established here provides a foundation for future investigations of FNART-Q performance in various clinical populations, including individuals with mild cognitive impairment and AD, for whom sensitive yet well-tolerated cognitive measures are particularly needed (Belleville et al., 2017; Petersen et al., 2018).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the present study establishes normative data for a French-speaking population, cultural and linguistic factors may influence face–name associative memory performance. Cross-cultural validation and the development of population-specific norms in other languages remain important objectives. A further limitation concerns the representativeness of the normative sample. Although the overall sample size was adequate, the sample was characterized by an overrepresentation of women and individuals with higher levels of education relative to the Quebec population. This imbalance likely reflects the voluntary recruitment strategy, which may preferentially attract individuals who are healthier, more cognitively engaged, and more willing to participate in research. As a result, the normative data may not fully capture the variability present in the general population, particularly among underrepresented groups such as individuals with lower levels of education or different sociodemographic profiles. Consequently, the resulting normative estimates are likely to be more robust for demographic groups that are well represented in the sample and comparatively less stable for underrepresented strata. This limitation is especially relevant given the stratified nature of the normative framework, designed to balance demographic specificity, clinical usability, and statistical stability. Although more finely graded educational classifications may increase demographic precision, they would also have resulted in substantially smaller normative cells when combined with age and sex stratification, thereby reducing the stability of percentile estimates. In these cases, percentile estimates may be less stable and subject to greater sampling variability than estimates derived from larger strata. Accordingly, percentile classifications obtained from smaller demographic cells should be interpreted with greater caution. Future studies should aim to replicate and extend these norms in more representative samples to enhance their generalizability and clinical applicability. An additional limitation concerns the absence of formal psychometric indices in the present study. Reliability measures such as internal consistency and test–retest stability were not assessed, and convergent validity with established episodic memory measures was not examined. These aspects should be addressed in future research to further support the robustness and construct validity of the FNART-Q. Finally, although a pronounced ceiling effect was not observed in the present normative sample, the recognition-based, forced-choice format of the FNART-Q may inherently constrain score variability in cognitively intact individuals, leading to a relative clustering of scores at the upper end of the distribution. As a result, relatively small differences in raw scores may occasionally correspond to larger percentile differences than would be expected in measures with a broader score distribution. This characteristic should be considered when interpreting high-range percentile estimates. Future studies should further examine the distributional properties and sensitivity of the FNART-Q across a broader range of cognitive profiles.

In summary, the present study provides the first normative data for the FNART-Q. The FNART-Q emerges as a clinically valuable tool for the assessment of associative episodic memory, offering meaningful interpretation through demographically adjusted norms. These findings support the inclusion of recognition-based face–name associative memory measures in the neuropsychological evaluation of older adults and contribute to ongoing efforts to improve the detection and characterization of episodic memory impairment.
