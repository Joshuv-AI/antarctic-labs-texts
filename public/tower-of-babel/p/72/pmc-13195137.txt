Disulfide tethering reveals cryptic pockets in oncogenic KRAS
Authors: Trent E. Balius, Marcin Dyba, Vandana Kumari, John-Paul Denson, Ming Yi, Alok K. Sharma, Priyanka Prakash, Emily Alberico, Simon Messing, Vanessa Wall, Albert H. Chan, Dhirendra K. Simanshu, Dwight V. Nissley, Frank McCormick, Dominic Esposito, Anna E. Maciag, David M. Turner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disulfide tethering is a site-directed method of drug discovery used to identify hits for challenging targets. We applied tethering to target oncogenic KRAS, a small GTPase once considered undruggable due to its high nucleotide affinity and a perceived absence of binding sites. We prepared a library of 2160 disulfide-containing fragments. We screened over 1000 compounds against a panel of 83 engineered cysteine mutants of KRAS G12D in the active conformation and screened the full library for a subset of 30 mutants. For select mutants and hits, we performed 2-mercaptoethanol competition assays (βME-50) to prioritize ligands. Ligandability analysis comparing hit rates across mutant residues enabled the identification of druggable hot spots. Our studies confirmed known binding sites, including the Switch-II / α-helix 3 pocket. In addition, we identified previously undescribed cryptic pockets and validated select hits using computational chemistry and NMR spectroscopy. These pockets represent promising opportunities for future drug discovery campaigns.

Introduction

Somatic mutations of the RAS oncogenes are among the most common drivers of human cancer1. RAS proteins are small GTPases that function as a molecular switch alternating between an active GTP-bound “ON” state and an inactive GDP-bound “OFF” state to regulate downstream signaling. In cells, nucleotide cycling is regulated through guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), which facilitate the exchange of GDP to GTP, and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), which enhance GTP hydrolysis. In tumor cells, RAS-activating mutations perturb nucleotide hydrolysis, resulting in persistent activation and continual downstream signaling. KRAS, the most frequently mutated RAS isoform, accounts for approximately 85% of RAS-driven cancers2–4 and was previously considered “undruggable” due to a perceived absence of small molecule binding sites and picomolar nucleotide affinity—making the advent of direct GDP/GTP competitive inhibitors unlikely5. However, in 2013, Shokat and colleagues used disulfide tethering to target KRAS G12C, resulting in the discovery of irreversible covalent inhibitors that bound within a cryptic pocket located between Switch II and α-helix 36,7. This breakthrough sparked a resurgence of KRAS drug discovery and the FDA approval of sotorasib (AMG510) and adagarasib (MRTX849) as targeted therapies for the treatment of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) harboring a KRAS G12C mutation. Despite this success, KRAS G12C inhibitors that target the OFF state of KRAS have shown only modest activity in the clinic due to resistance mechanisms such as adaptive feedback8–11. Next-generation KRAS G12C inhibitors, including BBO-8520, which target both active and inactive KRAS conformations, are expected to improve target coverage and efficacy12. However, these G12C inhibitors target only a subtype of KRAS mutations, leaving many other common oncogenic mutations largely untreated. To address this, highly potent KRAS G12D and pan-KRAS inhibitors have been developed, some of which are currently undergoing clinical evaluation13–17. Although there is great progress, at the time of publication, there are just two FDA-approved KRAS therapeutics, both covalent G12C inhibitors. Therefore, there remains an unmet clinical need for new KRAS therapeutics.

The value of non-traditional screening methods for addressing difficult-to-target proteins has become increasingly evident. To date, the majority of known KRAS binding compounds are localized to a few small regions of the protein leaving much of the surface unexplored (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Inspired by the success of Shokat and colleagues, we conducted a disulfide tethering campaign to evaluate all surface exposed amino acid residues of oncogenic KRAS G12D with the goal of discovering cryptic pockets that may be the subject of future drug discovery campaigns.

Fragment-based drug discovery (FBDD) is a powerful screening tool that has found widespread application throughout industry and academia18. The binding of fragments to their target protein is typically weak, making detecting these interactions challenging. Sophisticated biophysical techniques such as NMR-based screening, protein crystallography, and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) have seen growing application in the field of FBDD due to increased automation and equipment sensitivity18. Despite these advancements, low screening throughput and the challenging detection of such weak binding events remain significant obstacles to successful FBDD campaigns19.

To address these issues, mass spectrometry-based covalent screening of electrophilic fragment libraries was developed20. This irreversible screening approach is used to rank compounds by percent labeling, typically to a native or induced cysteine residue. However, although the non-covalent (van der Waals and electrostatic) interactions between fragments and a target protein contribute to binding, reactivity-driven labeling gives rise to false positives. To avoid this, we applied disulfide tethering (Fig. 1), which relies upon the reversible disulfide bond formation between a fragment and a targeted cysteine residue21. Screens are performed under thermodynamic equilibrium in the presence of 2-mercaptoethanol (βME), and hits are ranked by percent labeling. Tethering has been successfully applied to challenging drug discovery targets where traditional approaches have failed6. A barrier to the wider application of tethering is the requirement for a custom library of disulfide-containing fragments. To overcome the lack of commercially available libraries, we created a library using fragment-based drug-discovery principles and screened this library against the panel of KRAS cysteine mutants.

Herein, we describe a tethering-based screening approach for the discovery of fragment-binding pockets in KRAS G12D (full screening data provided in Supplementary Data 1 and 2). We apply ligandability to score pockets and highlight a previously undisclosed cryptic binding site, which we validate through NMR and molecular dynamics simulation. In addition, we performed βME-50 assays for a subset of mutants and compounds to prioritize hits. Data from these studies are provided to inspire the development of targeted therapeutics for the treatment of KRAS-driven cancers.

Results

KRAS4b (1-169) G12D tethering screens

We performed tethering screens for the 83 engineered cysteine mutants in KRAS G12D against an initial library of 1,161 disulfide fragments and compared the levels of labeling (Fig. 2, Supplementary Note 2, and Supplementary Data 1). We then applied the concept of protein ligandability (Supplementary Note 3), which is a measure that allows ranking of mutants based upon the likelihood of finding developable hits22, to evaluate each cysteine mutant and identify binding hot spots22. Several cysteine residues showed high hit rates, suggesting proximity to a fragment binding site. An example was residue E37C, which demonstrated an average labeling of 41% and maximal labeling of 94%. This residue is found in an established binding pocket located at the KRAS/SOS1 binding interface23. Other examples of high labeling residues include residues D69C, M72C, and Q99C, which form part of the Switch II / α-helix 3 pocket—an allosteric binding site targeted by Shokat and colleagues6. We also observed cysteine mutants showing low levels of labeling, for example, residues K101C and S106C, which demonstrated a maximal labeling of 22% (mean = 3%) and 24% (mean = 5%), respectively. Pocket-by-pocket analysis

To further characterize these hits, all residues presented in the heatmap analysis (Figs. 2 and 3) were grouped into pockets (Fig. 4). For each pocket, we presume that ligands bound within a single site adjacent to a mutated cysteine residue. Through visualizing KRAS crystal structures, we grouped cysteine-mutation residues based upon their proximity to a likely binding site (Fig. 4B–D and Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). Further, we generated a ligandability score to compare residues based on hit rate (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table 9). The goal of this work was to identify binding sites in KRAS G12D and encourage the discovery of KRAS-targeted therapeutics. To this effect, we found several pockets, some unexplored, that we consider therapeutically interesting.

Pocket 1

Pocket 1 is formed between Switch-II and α-helix 3 and is an allosteric binding site for multiple compounds, including KRAS G12C covalent inhibitors sotorasib and adagrasib (Supplementary Fig. 3A). This pocket is a cryptic binding site, and in most structures of KRAS, the pocket appears shallow, unless occupied by a small molecule. Importantly, compounds occupying this pocket can be RAS isoform-specific due to residue 95 differing among KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS (amino acids H, L, and Q, respectively)24.

We determined ligandability, average labeling, and standard deviation of labeling (Supplementary Table 9A). The ligandability score for mutants associated with Pocket 1 is among the highest examined in this study and ranges from 0.05 (N86C) to 0.75 (D69C). Q99C (which has the second-highest ligandability and average labeling values) has the highest standard deviation value of 15. Tethering hits for Q99C and D92C are of particular interest as these compounds may demonstrate KRAS selectivity over H/NRAS through engagement of H95. In future studies, we plan to follow up on the top hits through counter screening for these residues against other RAS isoforms. In addition, we investigated the compounds that rank in the top ten for the pocket mutants to illustrate examples of the screening hits for this site (Supplementary Note 8, Supplementary Tables 10 and 11, and Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Pocket 2

Pocket 2 is an established fragment binding site and represents the first targetable pocket identified on RAS23,25,26. The site is located adjacent to Switch-I and Switch-II and consists of a small lipophilic cavity surrounded by polar residues. The pocket is known to be inducible through compound binding and is conserved among RAS isoforms; however, differences in small-molecule binding affinity among RAS mutants have been reported. This site forms part of the RAS/SOS binding interface, and compounds that bind within this pocket are known to prevent GDP/GTP cycling through the inhibition of SOS-mediated activation26. We determined that the ligandability is also high for this site, ranging from 0.01 to 0.74 (Supplementary Table 9B). The maximum average labeling is for E37C at 47.1%, and the data has a high standard deviation at 17.3%. The minimum average labeling is G75C at 15% with a standard deviation of 6.9%.

G75C is on the edge of this site, located between the RAS/SOS binding interface pocket (Pocket 2) and Switch-II/α-helix 3 pocket (Pocket 1). We observed that this residue appears somewhat inaccessible in published crystal structures, which correlates well with the ligandability value of 0.01 and low average labeling of 15%. Interestingly, G75C also has the tightest distribution (standard deviation is 6.9, Supplementary Table 9). For discussion of the chemical preferences of this site see Supplemental Note 9, Supplementary Tables 12 and 13, and Supplementary Fig. 4. Several molecules are known to bind to this site including DCAI23, BI-285227,28, compound 1327,28, and compound 3629 (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Strikingly, hit DL0813 has an indole ring, which is a common functionality in established binders (Supplementary Fig. 4B). In addition, DL1954 (benzimidazole) and DL1611 (oxindole) contain structurally similar groups (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Pocket 2 shares residues with the binding site of RAS(ON)/CYPA tricomplex inhibitors RMC-6291 (elironrasib) and RMC-6236 (daraxonrasib)16,17,30,31, which target KRAS G12C and pan-RAS, respectively, in the ON conformation (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Pocket 3

We define RAS Pocket 3 as a site between helices H4 and H5 (residues 120–158). There is no established pocket at these residues, but ligand binding could suggest the formation of a cryptic pocket. Structural studies indicate that this region mediates interactions with the CRD of RAF1, which are necessary for full RAS-driven RAF activation32. Therefore, compounds targeting Pocket 3 are expected to disrupt the RAS–CRD interaction, reducing RAF activation. This site can also serve as a potential binding platform for secondary functionalization (e.g., degrader or molecular glue). To our knowledge, there are no known small molecules that bind to this site. However, there are engineered proteins that bind to this site, for example, the synthetic binding protein (monobody) NS1, which seems to interfere with RAS-RAS interactions, which in turn impacts downstream signaling33. The ligandability ranges from 0.10 to 0.66 for this site (Supplementary Table 9C), with D154C having the best overall ligandability (0.66) and an average labeling of 41.4%. The amino acid sequence at this site differs significantly between KRAS4b, NRAS, and HRAS, allowing for possible isoform specificity. We plan to follow up on these hits through counter screens of NRAS and HRAS in future studies. For further discussion of the screening hits to this site, see Supplementary Note 10, and Supplementary Tables 14–16.

For Pockets 2 and 3, we have included computational results that predict binding modes for select compounds (see Fig. 5 A and B, Supplementary Note 11 and Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). We include βME-50 determinations and dose response data for these two pockets as well, Supplementary Figs. 6A and 8. Additionally, we have performed 2D 1H-15N HSQC NMR experiments for protein tethered to DL0993 at P121C (Fig. 5 C, Supplementary Note 12, and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). The disulfide hits that label P121C may bind to a site with isoform specificity due to amino acid differences in residues 121 and 122 between RAS isoforms. Specifically, P121 in KRAS and NRAS is alanine in HRAS, and S122 in KRAS is alanine in HRAS and threonine in NRAS. DL0993 was initially docked into a small pocket located near the nucleotide. We selected this pocket for docking studies due to the likelihood of the site binding ligands, indicated by the presence of a glycerol molecule in the crystal structure PDB 5US434. However, during molecular dynamics simulations, DL0993 moved away from the initial docking site into a cryptic pocket located between loop 8, α-helices 4 and 5, and the top of β-sheet 6. To determine the P121C binding site of DL0993, we carried out NMR experiments for backbone residue assignments of the protein (Supplementary Fig. 9). Covalent modification of C121 by DL0993 resulted in a significant residue perturbation as demonstrated by the overlay of 2D 1H-15N HSQC spectra of KRAS P121C in the absence and presence of the compound (Supplementary Fig. 10), confirming the computationally determined binding site of DL0993. Interestingly, G-domain residues I84 and select residues within G115-V125, F141-S145, and R149-A155 elicited significant CSPs (Fig. 5C) and apparently play an important role in defining the binding pocket. In the molecular dynamics simulations of the predicted complex, DL0993 makes a sidechain hydrogen bond with T148, stacks with Q150, and wedges between Q150 and the rest of the protein (Fig. 5B). We note that this binding site contains residues G151 and D153, which are unique to KRAS4b and may afford isoform specificity. KRAS4a contains R151, which extends into Pocket 3, forming interactions with neighboring residues Q150 and D154 that restrict flexibility and occlude this pocket. Other pockets

Briefly, for Pocket 4 residues, we ran the full library of 2,160 disulfides. For both S136C and T87C, the second half of the library demonstrated higher labeling (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2B, D), which we attribute to greater structural diversity between compounds. By contrast, Pocket 5 showed poor ligandability (Figs. 2 and 4) and as such we chose not to run the full library screen.

The residues of Pocket 6 are located on Switch-I and represent a key effector binding region with high flexibility and therapeutic potential. In our studies, this site had ligandability ranging from 0.36 to 0.62 and average labeling ranging from 33.0% to 39.0% (Supplementary Table 9D). See Supplementary Note 13, Supplementary Tables 17 and 18 for a discussion of the tethering hits of this site. Few compounds are reported to bind in this site; however, a discovery by Mathieu et al.36 led to the identification of compound 4 (Supplementary Fig. 11)36, which occupies this site to covalently modify Cys12. For follow-up dose response determinations for hits for Pocket 6 with GppNHp or GDP, see Supplementary Table 19 and Supplementary Fig. 12.

Pocket 7 is likely a cryptic binding site located between α-helix 1, β-sheet 2, and α-helix 5. It may have therapeutic potential and is discussed in the literature37–39. We have targeted this site in the screens of the first half of the library (Fig. 2), and we did not pursue this site further. Ligandability ranges from 0.0 to 0.98, average labeling ranges from 4.1 to 48.7, and standard deviation ranges from 3.7 to 13.4 (Supplementary Table 9E).

Pocket 8 is located at the end of α-helix 5 and is a well-formed, solvent accessible site that is present in many crystal structures. The site is well-solvated with crystallographic waters and contains several charged residues. The pocket was identified through computational studies by Gorfe and colleagues40 and is also discussed by Lehner and colleagues41—identified in both papers as Pocket 3. We have targeted this site in the screens of the first half of the library, but because of poor ligandability and poor labeling (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 9F) we did not pursue this site further. Of the pocket residues, D105C and E168C have the best ligandability and labeling (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 9F).

For all of these pockets, there are molecules that could provide useful starting points for chemical programs (see Supplementary Data 1 and 2). However, we anticipate that the development of certain pockets may be more challenging due to low ligandability scores. This is particularly true for small, isolated pockets where binding affinity is likely low.

Hit and target validation by βME-50 determination and time-course analysis

To assess the relative binding strength of tethering hits, we determined βME-50 values for the top five labeling molecules across each of the 30 mutants shown in Fig. 3 (see Supplementary Note 14 and, Supplementary Table 20 and Supplementary Fig. 13). βME-50 is the concentration 2-mercaptoethanol required to reduce disulfide labeling of the molecule to 50%. This value provides a quantitative measure of binding-driven disulfide stabilization and is used to guide selection of molecules for further development42,43.

As further validation of our approach, we conducted time-course experiments on a subset of 34 mutants, using common hitter DL1880 as a control compound due to its broad labeling profile (Supplementary Note 15, Supplementary Tables 3 and 21). For most mutants, only minimal changes in percent labeling were observed over time, indicating that tethering equilibrium had been achieved. Exceptions to this behavior included M72C, G75C, E76C, N86C, and K88C, which exhibited large increases in labeling and up to three modifications, which we attribute to protein unfolding (Supplementary Fig. 14).

G75, which resides in pocket 2 (on the switch-II α2 helix), participates in an allosteric network with M72, R73, and K104 that is critical for KRAS activation. Yang et al.44 showed that the G75A substitution disrupts this network and remodels the α2-helix conformation. In our time-course studies, we observed marked protein instability for G75C, which is likely due to a similar perturbation of this allosteric network and consequent intrinsic destabilization of the protein. Under these conditions, covalent ligand engagement at G75C would be expected to further compromise structural integrity, promoting progressive, time-dependent unfolding and sequential exposure of additional cysteines, thereby accounting for the three covalent adducts observed in our experiments.

Discussion

We screened a custom library of 2,160 disulfide fragments against KRAS G12D and identified cryptic binding pockets. Specifically, we screened over 1000 compounds from this library against a panel of 83 engineered cysteine mutants, and expanded screens to include the full library for a subset of 30 mutants that were selected due to strong labeling or high interest level in the site. Some of these pockets exist at known interfaces for RAS-binding proteins. Thus, the development of hits that bind in these pockets is of therapeutic interest and could result in biological effects through allostery45.

The following key findings have emerged from this work:

Cysteine mutagenesis (83 mutants) combined with disulfide tethering screens (over 2,000 compounds) provides an effective strategy to discover pockets on the KRAS protein—both known and cryptic—and hits that bind these pockets. We anticipate that this approach would yield similar results if applied to other proteins, particularly GTPases such as RAS, Rho, and Rab families, which share sequence similarity with KRAS and exhibit common structural features46.

MALDI-TOF MS, together with assay miniaturization using Echo sonic liquid handling, proved invaluable for the success of this project by facilitating rapid and efficient screening. Additionally, by applying disulfide tethering as a reversible covalent screening technique, we were able to avoid reactivity-driven labeling, which is a caveat of some irreversible screening approaches6,21,47–49. Furthermore, βME-50 experiments served as an effective tool for the selection of hits for further development.

The two pockets most characterized in the literature, Pocket 1 and Pocket 26,25,27,50–52, were also the two pockets with the highest ligandability scores in our study and are the most promising pockets from a drug discovery perspective. Other pockets, less studied, also show promise, as discussed below. We speculate that small molecules binding to these pockets may have surprising and varying biological effects, acting through allostery or disruption of effector binding45,53. There are a number of other studies that discuss pockets on RAS; however, our work is the first to include a site-directed covalent binding approach38,40,54–60. The cryptic pockets that we have identified throughout KRAS G12D (GppNHp) may be amenable to therapeutic intervention, as evidenced by Pocket 1, which is the established binding site for clinically approved drugs sotorasib and adagrasib in KRAS G12C.

Certain pockets and mutants stand out with strong labeling. As discussed in the Methods, we quantified and analyzed our data using multiple complementary metrics, including raw labeling data, ligandability, average labeling, and standardized values. Through these standardized values, we see that despite poor overall labeling for some of the mutants, there are ligands that label above the mean. We speculate, however, that such hits may be more challenging to develop than those targeting mutants with high ligandability. We also grouped the mutants by “pocket”. These pockets are selected by the spatial proximity of residues and allow for the examination of the chemical preference of the site from different vectors of approach. These pockets have been discussed in the literature, indicating that computational and other screening methods have also implicated these as possible small molecule binding sites38,40,54–59.

Few molecules in the screening library seem to be promiscuous. Although some ligands during initial evaluation appear to label highly across the board, these molecules typically bind to one mutant with statistically stronger labeling (standardized value, see Supplementary Note 6 for some examples).

Subsequent computational studies of hit mutant complexes, in some cases, indicated a consistent binding mode prediction. In the case of DL0993 with P121C, the computational prediction and NMR data both indicate binding in the same pocket.

Although we are confident in the validity of our results, there are a few caveats in this screening approach that warrant mentioning. Firstly, a requirement for tethering is the introduction of cysteine mutations at target sites. These modifications may alter the physical properties of the protein, changing the nature of the target site41,61. Mutations that altered protein stability were removed during QC; however, more subtle changes may occur. For example, mutation of Tyr to Cys will alter the volume of the pocket, while mutations of Lys to Cys or Asp to Cys change the charge and electrostatic potential of the site. This type of change will impact how the protein interacts with itself and with the binding ligands. Other mutations, such as Pro to Cys or Gly to Cys could impact secondary structure. A further consideration is the influence of GppNHp on our results. GppNHp served as a non-hydrolysable analog of GTP, but it is established that this nucleotide analog does not fully replicate GTP’s behavior62. Additionally, our pocket-by-pocket analysis grouped residues into discrete pockets; however, some target residues exist at the interface of two pockets. For example, D154C is positioned such that molecules labeling this mutant may bind within either of two adjacent pockets. Moreover, ligands may not bind as anticipated and could instead engage a different pocket or an induced pocket. Finally, conversion of disulfide hits into non-covalent inhibitors for drug discovery purposes remains challenging due to the weak binding affinity of fragment hits. Disulfide probes are also not stable in cellular environments, and further development is required to evaluate in vitro effectiveness.

These caveats do not alter our key findings: disulfide tethering is a powerful screening approach for identifying binding sites in challenging drug targets. The MALDI-TOF MS assay development was key to the success of this endeavor, and through the labeling data, ligandability, quantification, and βME-50 experiments, we identified targetable pockets and high labeling molecules suitable for further development. We provide this screening dataset as a resource to the cancer research community, in hopes of nucleating the discovery of RAS therapeutics and enhancing predictive models.

Methods

Construction and screening of a disulfide tethering library

We designed our tethering fragment library using the cheminformatics package Canvas (Schrödinger, LLC) with the goal of maximizing structural diversity while ensuring good fragment-like properties63,64. The library was constructed in two phases. The first half was prepared from ~1100 carboxylic-acid fragments, whereas the second half was prepared from ~1000 primary and secondary amines (Supplementary Fig. 16), resulting in a final library of 2160 compounds. We selected fragments for synthesis from a database of approximately 39,000 commercially available building blocks. To simplify compound selection, we applied a fragment weight cutoff of 358 Da and removed functionalities associated with problematic compound behavior using REOS (rapid elimination of swill)65 and PAINS (pan assay interference compounds) filters66. To maximize structural diversity, we applied hierarchical clustering techniques using 2D linear and 3D pharmacophore-based fingerprints. K-means clustering using Lloyd’s algorithm67 was applied during the initial diversity-based selection. The centroid from each cluster was further filtered using diversity-based selection (Soergel distance with 0.5 exclusion sphere size) and hierarchical clustering based on Tanimoto similarity, followed by manual shortlisting of hits for synthesis. Purchased compounds were prepared through custom synthesis as the corresponding TFA salts using a modified one-pot parallel synthesis approach that included an initial amide coupling step followed by phosphine-catalyzed disulfide metathesis68. A 90% success rate was achieved, with an acceptance criterion of greater than 95% purity. An overview of the physicochemical properties of our library is shown in Supplementary Table 22. For further details, see Supplementary Note 16 and Supplementary Figs. 16–18.

Creation of a KRAS G12D cysteine mutagenesis library

To explore potential surface pockets on the G-domain of KRAS4b (1-169), a collection of mutants was generated in which a single surface residue was mutated into cysteine for use in tethering screens. We generated the initial library in the G12D oncogenic mutant background and engineered the C118S mutation to prevent off-target labeling. The G-domain of KRAS includes only three cysteine residues: C51, C80, and C118 – of these, only C118 was expected to be near enough to the surface to permit labeling. The reactivity and accessibility of these cysteines were evaluated for labeling susceptibility to maleimide-PEG-biotin and Ellman’s reagent (5,5’-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid)). No labeling was observed with either reagent on C51 or C80 unless the protein was denatured. However, C118 could be labeled at some frequency with both reagents, justifying mutation to serine for the library constructs. Serine was chosen as a surrogate for cysteine due to the close structural similarity between residues; however, it should be noted that these residues differ in polarity and hydrophobicity69. Regardless of these differences, previous work has shown that the C118S mutation has little impact on KRAS structure or function, and in fact results in better structural determination by NMR and X-ray crystallography70,71.

To predict surface residues suitable for tethering, crystal structures of KRAS4b (1-169) G12D were examined. All 169 residues were scored, and a threshold for surface accessibility was determined, leading to 102 potential mutants. After removing several residues where previous protein production efforts had failed, a final list of 98 mutants was chosen, along with the parental G12D protein as a negative control (Supplementary Note 17 and Supplementary Table 23). All cysteine mutant proteins underwent GppNHp nucleotide exchange, and the level of exchange was assessed by HPLC (Supplementary Table 24). Most cysteine mutants achieved > 90% GppNHp exchange except for D38C, which was exchanged to 74%. This residue is located at the nucleotide-binding site adjacent to magnesium. We speculate that the switch from the negatively charged polar sidechain of D38 to a neutral and more hydrophobic sulfhydryl group altered the local environment enough to destabilize magnesium and affect nucleotide exchange. It should, however, be noted that our exchange data is N = 1, and one must be cautious not to overinterpret this result. All 98 mutants were subjected to differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF)72 to analyze their melting temperature (Supplementary Fig.19), and then further validated through treatment with maleimide-PEG-biotin and Ellman’s reagent to determine cysteine accessibility. Of the 98 mutants, 83 passed DSF and cysteine accessibility quality control criteria and were selected for tethering screening efforts (Supplementary Table 23).

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry as a high-throughput disulfide tethering screening platform

We developed a semi-automated Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization-Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS)-based tethering screen for high-throughput analysis of disulfide fragment binding (Supplementary Methods). We further optimized screening using acoustic liquid handling (Echo 555/655), which allowed reactions to be efficiently performed in 384-well plates at a volume of 6 µL per well with concentrations of 5 µM protein, 500 µM βME, and 500 µM of disulfide fragment (see Supplementary Methods). We developed an automated bioinformatic pipeline to process the mass spectra data collected by the MALDI-TOF in multiple steps with R scripts (https://cran.r-project.org) and to return the percent labeling for each mutant-small molecule pair. These scripts take as input two sets of options for allowed Dalton differences in peaks of protein and protein-small molecule. Intermediate files, for the purpose of quality-assessment and cross-validation, and final result files are generated automatically for each set of raw data files. Potential errors are tracked in log files, and re-runs of the pipeline can be easily performed with modification of the processing parameters. A representative screening experiment is illustrated (Fig. 1C), where compounds labeling over three times the standard deviation (σ) were considered hits and selected for further validation through resynthesis and dose-response. For this study, we focused on the primary screening data. Follow-up dose response, βME-50 determination, NMR studies, and molecular models and simulations are discussed for select hits.

NMR experiments for the binding-site determination

NMR data were collected on a Bruker 700 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5-mm TCI cryoprobe. All data were collected at 298 K with gradient-selected sensitivity-enhanced pulse programs73 using nonuniform sampling (NUS) with sampling rates of approximately 30%74. Data were processed using NMRPipe/NMRDraw75 and SMILE for NUS reconstruction76 and visualized/analyzed in CCPNMR analysis77. Backbone residue assignments were carried out using 2D 1H-15N HSQC, HNCACB, CBCA(CO)NH, HNCA, and HN(CO)CA spectra. The 1H, 13C, and 15N chemical shifts were referenced to the internal standard DSS using IUPAC-IUB recommended protocols: https://bmrb.io/ref_info/cshift. Assignments were made manually. Chemical shift perturbation (CSP) analysis was carried out by determining the differences in 1HN and 15N chemical shifts from 2D 1H-15N HSQC spectral overlay of protein in the absence and in presence of DL0993 using the weighted-average chemical shifts (∆δweighted) from the equation: [∆δweighted = {(∆H)2 + (∆N/5)2}0.5].

13C/15N-labeled samples of 0.8 mM concentration were prepared for KRAS P121C-GppNHp and KRAS P121C-GppNHp in complex with DL0993 (compound is tethered to C121 of KRAS P121C-GppNHp and sample was purified). Samples were prepared in a buffer that contained 20 mM MES-d13 (pH 6.5), 100 mM KCl, 50 mM NaCl, and 2 mM MgCl2, 250 μM DSS (internal standard), and 0.02% sodium azide in a solvent composition of 93% H2O and 7% D2O. Protein sequence (aa 1-169) included G12D and C118S mutations.
