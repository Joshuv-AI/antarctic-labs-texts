Computational Design of a TCR-Based Bispecific Engager Targeting Cancerous KRAS G12V Mutations
Authors: Nisarg Shah, Gargi Sharma, Vihaan Patel, Melinda Wu, Anishreddy Mummadi, Anya Taneja, Moksh Shah, Gia Bao Le, Madhav Krishna, Harman Brah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) therapy is a type of immunotherapy that redirects cytotoxic T cells to target tumor cells. Traditionally, a BiTE structure dual binds to the CD3 receptor on the T-cell surface and to the mutated antigen presented on the surface of the cancer cell as an extracellular protein. An example of this is blinatumomab, an FDA-approved BiTE structure for the treatment of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL). However, traditional BiTE complexes are at risk of off-tumor effects due to their targeting of cell surface antigens, which are not specific to certain cancer variants. In this study, we utilized a computational approach, an area that remains underdeveloped in current immunotherapy design, to design the initial structures of two TCR-BiTEs that consist of a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) in conjunction with a T-cell receptor (TCR) that binds specifically to the KRAS G12V neoantigen, which currently does not have any FDA-approved targeted therapy treatments. Ultimately, we present two promising TCR-BiTEs that are specific to two different HLAs (HLA:03:01 and HLA:11:01) targeting KRAS G12V, serving as valuable starting points for further evaluation and design in vitro. We validated the design of our TCR-BiTE structures through AlphaFold tools, free energy estimation methods, and molecular dynamics analysis, thereby also providing a potential computational pipeline that can be applied in the design of TCR-BiTE structures targeting other mutations in addition to KRAS G12V.

Introduction

KRAS is one of the most frequently mutated oncogenes in colorectal cancer, nonsingle cell lung cancer, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Unfortunately, the successful development of KRAS small-molecule inhibitors was reliant on specific factors such as the high GTPase activity of KRAS G12C, which is not fully present in other KRAS mutant types, such as KRAS G12V. As a result, the use of targeted therapy is limited in scope, and the need to develop treatments for the KRAS G12V mutation remains pressing.

In response to this challenge, many alternatives to small-molecule drugs are currently being investigated for KRAS treatment. One particular avenue that is being explored is immunotherapy, which utilizes the patient’s own T cells to eliminate malignant cells. Specifically, bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) therapy has received acclaim due to its ability to enhance immune response by retargeting T cells to kill tumor cells via engineered bispecific antibodies. This unique ability of BiTEs to activate T cells bypasses the need to bind directly to the KRAS protein and also promotes T-cell activity in cancers that are naturally immunosuppressive due to the tumor microenvironment (TME).

Despite these advantages, traditional BiTEs are limited by their structure. Composed of two single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) bound through a peptide sequence linker, BiTEs function by redirecting T cells to kill malignant cells, engaging both the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) and CD3 molecules on immune receptors, and reducing its specificity. For instance, studies assessing the clinical efficacy of Blinatumomab, an FDA-approved BiTE for the treatment of B-acute lymphoblastic leukemia, found that blinatumomab tends to bind to noncancerous CD19 on healthy cells, pointing to a lack of specificity in binding.

As a potential solution, BiTEs have been modified to contain a T-cell receptor (TCR). This allows them to attach directly to the HLA by replacing the second scFv that typically connects to surface proteins in cancer cells. As seen in Figure , the TCR is able to bind to an antigen housed in a defined pocket of the HLA on the tumor cell surface, called the neoantigen. TCRs provide more specificity than an scFv because they contain accessible complementarity-determining region (CDR) loops (most notably CDR3) that can bind in many conformations to neoantigens. Moreover, because of the HLA’s specificity to antigen presentation and the TCR’s selective binding of the neoantigen, the TCR-modified BiTE can excel at targeting specific cancer-inducing mutations expressed by the neoantigen, as highlighted by an in vivo study aimed at developing TCR-modified BiTEs for KRAS G12D.

TCR-BiTEs function to redirect T cells to mutant-specific neoantigens, essentially modulating the cancer-immune interaction in such a way as to allow cytotoxic T cells to specifically target cancerous cells. The idea of modulating cancer-immune interactions has been highlighted by Wang et al. 2023, who examine why ovarian cancer remains resistant to immunotherapy despite successes in other malignancies, identifying it as a “cold” tumor due to a suppressive TME. The authors analyze the microenvironment and propose approaches to increase the efficacy of immune treatment, such as PARP inhibitors, chemotherapy, etc. The overall idea behind their approach to increase ovarian cancer’s sensitivity to immunotherapy is similar to that of TCR-BiTEs, by modifying/activating the immune environment to target cancer cells, thereby highlighting the biological reasoning behind the function of TCR-BiTEs.

Recent research has produced BiTEs for specific KRAS mutations. Specifically, research efforts have targeted KRAS G12D and G12V due to their inaccessibility for direct treatment by small-molecule inhibitors. In 2022, researchers at Immunocore successfully designed a TCR-BiTE for the treatment of KRAS G12D. This BiTE exhibited potent and selective binding to HLA-A*11-KRASG12D in vitro. Further, the constructed BiTE was able to selectively bind to KRAS G12D, reducing the possibility of any off-target effects on wild-type KRAS.

More recently, a TCR-BiTE for the treatment of KRAS G12V cancers was developed. The constructed TCR engager targeted HLA-A*11:01, one of the many HLA-A alleles present in KRAS-induced malignancies. The findings were promising, but the researchers noted that “future engineering of TCRs that could cross-recognize KRAS G12V peptide presented by additional HLA alleles would be valuable.” In addition, there is still a need to build upon these findings by developing and testing KRAS G12V TCR-BiTEs computationally, which could present more findings through residue interactions between the HLA and the TCR, and also between the BiTE itself.

This study aims to address current needs in BiTE research with the in silico development, testing, and analysis of multiple TCR-guided BiTEs that target the KRAS G12V mutation. A key contribution is a novel computational pipeline that integrates a composite scoring framework, multidomain AlphaFold modeling, and stability-affinity assessments to enable rational and rapid prioritization of TCR-BiTE candidates. Ultimately, this study identifies two promising TCR-BiTEs through a computational approach, using the TCRs 8rro and 8wte, for further testing and treatment development targeting KRAS G12V presented by HLA-A*03:01 and HLA-A*11:01, respectively. In silico development began with the identification of five KRAS G12V binding TCRs. Four of the TCRs targeted HLA-A*11:01, while one TCR, 8rro, targeted HLA-A*03:01. Out of the four TCRs targeting HLA-A*11:01, three were chosen for further analysis: 8wte, 8wul, and 8i5c, based on free energy and stability calculations. 8rro was also selected for further testing due to its unique binding of the HLA-A*03:01. Each of the four remaining TCRs was then linked to an anti-CD3 scFv using a standard GGGGS linker, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations followed. Results of the MD simulations were then analyzed, where the TCR-BiTEs 8wte and 8rro demonstrated structural stability in a physiological aqueous environment, demonstrating preliminary potential for the targeting of the oncogenic KRAS G12V mutation along with a feasible computational pipeline for the development and screening of TCR-BiTES in silico.

Methods

Selection of TCRs

Prior to construction of the TCR-BiTE structure, TCRs were identified, tested, and then analyzed.

Initial Identification

Five TCR-HLA complexes (PDBs: 8rro, 8wte, 8i5c, 8i5d, 8wul), as shown in Table , were downloaded from RCSB. Each of these TCRs is murine and was developed from collecting CD8+ T cells from transgenic mice primed against the G12V KRAS HLA and crystallizing the TCR. − 8wte is an altered version of 8wul with residue mutations in the α and β chains of TCR with the aim of enhancing its affinity to the HLA-bound G12V peptide.

After selecting the five TCRs, the native structures were analyzed visually on UCSF Chimera to assess whether the biologically relevant diagonal binding of the TCR and HLA-bound neoantigen was present Then, the Clashes and Contacts tool was utilized in order to confirm van der Waals interactions between the TCR and the HLA complex. For each TCR-HLA complex, six models were observed. These included the CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3 loops for both the α and β chains of each TCR. CDR1 and CDR2 were tested against the residues of the α helices of the HLA, while CDR3 was tested against the 9 amino acid peptide of the neoantigen. This decision was made because existing literature indicates that the germline CDRs (CDR1 and CDR2) bind to the HLA, while CDR3 binds to the peptide. During the analysis, atoms were found with a VDW overlap of −0.4 Å, and intramolecular bonds were ignored. This way, nonbonding interactions could be totaled and the compatibility between the TCR and the HLA could be modeled.

MM/GBSA Binding Free Energy

To further analyze binding interactions between the 5 crystal structures, we utilized Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) analysis, a comprehensive tool that utilizes a generalized Born model to predict residue attraction. MM/GBSA has been used in the past to predict binding free energies of a ligand and receptor and identify biologically relevant binding conformations. We utilized the Hawkdock web tool to calculate MM/GBSA due to its computational efficiency and specific residue calculations. Within Hawkdock, the PDB file of the docked TCR-HLA complex from RCSB was input, with HLA designated as the receptor and TCR as the ligand. During the procedure, trajectories were calculated by the ff02 force field, the implicit solvent model, and the GBOBC1 model (interior dielectric constant = 1). The receptor–ligand system was minimized for 5000 steps with a cutoff distance of 999 Å for van der Waals interactions (2000 cycles of steepest descent and 3000 cycles of conjugate gradient minimizations). Total binding free energy scores as well as the top 10 residues in terms of the binding free energy score were documented.

Hydrogen Bond Count

Hydrogen bond count was another metric used to test the binding affinity of the protein–protein complex. Hydrogen bonds were incorporated into the TCR selection metric because they contribute highly to binding affinity and can be predictors of binding affinity. Using UCSF Chimera, intermodel H bonds between each HLA and TCR were enumerated. The H bond constraint was relaxed by 0.4 Å and 20.0° in order to document weaker H bonds that might have been offset by the flexibility of the structure. Following enumeration, the H bonds were inspected in the 3-dimensional space to ensure correct positioning between the TCR and the HLA.

AlphaFold

In order to create the TCR-BiTE structure in silico, ColabFold, an optimized AlphaFold2-multimer software for protein structure prediction was used. , The software was able to predict interdomain contracts that focused on the TCR variable regions and fusion components. The structure prediction workflow consisted of the following steps: Automated Sequence Search: Multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) were generated using MMseqs2, with automated searches against the UniRef100 and environmental sequence databases. , Both paired and unpaired MSAs were constructed to capture a broad range of evolutionary signals. Predictive Modeling: Five structural models were produced for each input sequence, with ranking based on two key confidence metrics: the predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score, which evaluates residue-level accuracy, and the predicted TM-score (pTMscore), which assesses the accuracy of global domain packing and interdomain contacts. Comprehensive Output Analysis: The ColabFold pipeline generated a complete output package including relaxed and unrelaxed PDB structures, confidence metrics (mean pLDDT and pTMscore), MSA coverage plots, Predicted Alignment Error (PAE) matrices, and detailed JSON-formatted scoring files.

Modeller

In order to analyze the stability of our 5 constructed TCR-BiTE structures, we first utilized AlphaFold to concatenate the sequences of the TCR α and β chains with the variable heavy and light chain regions (VH and VL) of the anti-CD3 single-chain fragment variable with three short GGGGS linkers, as described in the previous subsection. The decision to utilize this linker is based on existing BiTE research. For example, blinatumomab, an FDA-approved BiTE structure, utilizes GGGGS linkers due to their flexibility. ,

The structures were energy minimized through UCSF Chimera, with 100 steps of steepest descent at 0.02 Å step size, and 10 steps of conjugate descent with a 0.02 Å step size as well. The structures were then submitted to the ModEval web-based application, which assesses the stability of the structure compared with existing experimental structures. The following metrics were analyzed: z-DOPE score (normalized Discrete Optimized Protein Energy) and z-combi (which is a combination of z-surf and z-pair).

The z-DOPE score is a normalized version of the Discrete Optimized Protein Energy (DOPE) score, which is a statistical potential derived from a data set of high-resolution crystal structures from RCSB PDB. The score evaluates the quality of the input structure by comparing its DOPE score to similar native structures from the data set. A lower score generally depicts a more native-like structure, with values of −1 or lower depicting high native-likeness.

z-surf and z-pair are statistical potentials that analyze the solubility accessible surface area of the structure as well as pair-based interactions within the protein’s residues in a similar manner as z-DOPE. Lower/more negative values of these potentials are favorable. z-combi is a linear combination of the two statistical potentials, and this is the metric, in addition to z-DOPE, which was factored into the scoring of the TCRs.

Composite Score Calculation

Once all the binding affinity and stability data for each TCR was collected, linear normalization was applied to each of the five TCR performance criterions (docking score, top 10 residue interaction, H-bond count, Z-DOPE, and z-combi scores). We used standard max–min normalization formulas, which took an input value of any range and outputted a value from 0 to 1. The normalization formula is below: Xnorm=X−XminXmax−Xmin 1 where X is the original value, X min is the minimum value in the data set for that metric, and X max is the maximum value in the data set for that metric.

Once we had a normalized value for each of the five categories, the averages of the free energy calculation and binding score were computed. The denominator of the free energy average formula is 3 because there were three criteria that pertained to free energy, and only 2 that pertained to stability. The two averaging formulas are as follows: Avg1=Norm.DockingScore+Norm.Residues+Norm.H‐bondcount3 2 Avg2=Norm.Z‐Dope+Norm.z‐combi2 3

The above formulas outputted two average scores for each TCR: one for free energy and the other for stability. In order to compile each of these scores into a final normalization, the two scores were averaged. Then, the final composite value was multiplied by 100 for a better interpretation of the results. MD via GROMACS

In order to test the stability of the TCR-BiTEs, multiple MD simulations were conducted using GROMACS. GROMACS was the software of choice due to its parallelization capabilities and ideal calculations for nonbonding interactions. GROMACS was run on a Vast AI instance with the following NVIDIA GPUs rented: 1× RTX 5090, 1× RTX 4090, and 2× RTX 4090. The structures of the TCR-BiTE were first processed through USCF Chimera 1.18 software, where the structures were added charges and energetically minimized with the steepest descent method with 100 steps of 0.02 Å steepest descent step size as well as 10 conjugate gradient steps. Then, GROMACS was used to conduct the MD simulation for minimized structures, where Optimized Potential for Liquid Simulation (OPLS) All Atom force field was used. The structure was solvated in a cubic water box, aiming to create an aqueous environment, neutralized with counterions, and its potential energy was minimized using the steepest descent method. After that, the system was processed through NVT and NPT equilibrations, which aim to stabilize the system’s pressure and temperature to 1 bar and 310 K, respectively. The mdp parameter files utilized for the MD run are provided in the Supporting Information section. The system then underwent a 100 ns production MD simulation, where atom trajectories were predicted and documented. Following the completion of the simulation, the data analysis methods were used: Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD): RMSD measures the average distance between atoms in the structure. A steady RMSD demonstrates the stability of the structure. The TCR-BiTE structures evaluated with MD as described earlier were shortlisted based on their composite scores consisting of the MM/GBSA values as well as the ModEval stability metrics (see Composite Score Calculation above). Based on the results of these dynamics simulations, the BiTE structure that was interpreted to be the most stable based on RMSD and radius of gyration metrics was further tested in an MD simulation for its docking stability to CD3 and the HLA displaying the G12V neoantigen.

CDR Loop Identification and HADDOCK Three-Body Docking

Prior to conducting a three-body docking of the full TCR-BiTE, CDR loops were identified on the 8wte TCR and the anti-CD3 scFv. CDR loops are the regions of the anti-CD3 scFv and the TCR that contain the residues that primarily interact with their targets. In the case of the anti-CD3 scFv, it interacts with CD3 present on the T-cell surface, and the TCR interacts with the HLA displaying the KRAS neoantigen. Each chain (variable heavy and light chain domains of the scFv, and TCR α and β chains) has three CDR loops, so each structure in total consists of six CDR loops. The CDR loop sequences for the anti-CD3 and TCRs were identified using the ANARCI Web server. For the CD3 structure, solely the CD3 epsilon chain was utilized as it is the primary interacting chain of the CD3 with the anti-CD3 scFv.

ANARCI utilizes an IMGT numbering scheme to identify the CDR loops when given a chain sequence as input. , CDR loops for each are found in the following residue ranges: CDR1 (27–38), CDR2 (56–65), and CDR3 (105–117). The residues identified through this were input as active residues for the anti-CD3 and TCR chains in HADDOCK. In order to identify the active interacting residues for CD3, HLA, and the G12V neoantigen, residue interactions within 4 Å of the anti-CD3 for CD3 and HLA and G12V neoantigen for TCR were identified through PyMOL from the PDB structures they were derived from (1XIW for anti-CD3, and the other TCR PDBs are specified in the Initial TCR Identification section). The residues found within 4 Å of the anti-CD3, and the TCR, were classified as active residues. The docked structure consisted of 4 chains: CD3 epsilon chain, HLA sequence, G12V neoantigen peptide, and the TCR-BiTE engager. This was then utilized to run the MD simulation using the same parameters described for the TCR-BiTE structure stability. In addition to radius of gyration values for the docking, solvent-accessible surface areas (SASAs) of the proteins were also analyzed, as described below.

T-Cell Activation

The C-ImmSim Web server was used to estimate the CD8+ T-cell activation against MHC-1 peptide complexes derived from KRAS G12V and KRAS WT peptides. − To do so, MHC-1 complexes were created by pairing each KRAS peptide side with the HLA:A*11:01 and HLA:A*03:01 alleles. That is, the KRAS G12V peptide (VVGAVGVGK) was combined with HLA:A*11:01 and HLA:A*03:01 to create two distinct MHC-1 peptide complexes. An identical procedure was followed to create MHIC-1 peptide complexes for KRAS WT. Antigen injections were scheduled at simulation times of 10, 110, and 210, aiming to study the primary, secondary, and tertiary immune exposures. The number of antigens to inject was set to 1000, while the type of injection was set to “vaccine (no LPS)” module with an adjuvant parameter of 100. The simulation proceeded with a simulation volume of 10 with 1000 simulating steps. At the end, the estimated concentrations of CD8+ and cytokines and interleukins were collected for analysis.

Immunogenicity

Following antigen immunogenicity evaluation, the in silico analysis of CD4+ T-cell epitope content for both TCR-BiTEs (8wte and 8rro) was conducted. The amino acid sequences were taken from the corresponding PDB structures and converted to FASTA format using UCSF Chimera’s sequencing tool. These FASTA files were then submitted to the NetMHCIIpan-4.x web server developed by DTU Health Tech. Each sequence was processed as overlapping 15-mer peptides against a representative subset of common HLA-DRB1 alleles (DRB1*01, DRB1*03, DRB1*04, DRB1*07, DRB1*08, DRB1*11, DRB1*13, and DRB1*15). These alleles were selected because they encompass the major class II specificities present in diverse human populations. For every pair of peptide/allele, the server reported a percentile rank (%Rank). In accordance with previously established literature on therapeutic protein risk assessment, peptides with %Rank <1 were classified as strong binders (and thus T-cell hotspots), whereas peptides with %Rank ≥1 were classified as moderate or nonbinders and were therefore not factored into the immunogenicity evaluation. For each TCR-BiTE, the amino acid positions and number of strong binders (%Rank < 1) were documented. Following this, the quantitative measurements were reclassified into epitope density based on total sequence length, which was used as an indicator of the intrinsic class II-mediated immunogenicity risk of the drug candidates.

Results

AlphaFold Structural Prediction of TCR-BiTEs

To build and test the structural feasibility of our TCR-BiTE molecules, we predicted the tertiary structures using AlphaFold2 via the ColabFold multimer pipeline. , For all five TCR-BiTE constructs, AlphaFold predicted and outputs five ranked structural models. Each model included predicted PDB files and interpretive graphs such as per-residue confidence scores (as seen in Figure ), interdomain predicted alignment error (as depicted in Figure ), and multiple sequence alignment (MSA) coverage plots (as shown in Figure ).

Residue-level confidence traces (pLDDT scores) for the five TCR-BiTE constructs, including (a) 8i5c, (b) 8i5d, (c) 8rro, (d) 8wte, and (e) 8wul predicted by AlphaFold2. Each plot overlays the pLDDT scores for the top five ranked structural models per construct. The predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score is a per-residue metric ranging from 0 to 100, estimating AlphaFold’s confidence in the atomic position of each amino acid. Scores above 90 indicate highly reliable predictions (typically structured domains such as TCR or scFv regions), while lower scores (<70) reflect flexible or uncertain regions, often corresponding to synthetic linker regions or interdomain junctions.

Predicted alignment error (PAE) matrices for the five TCR-BITEs, (a) 8i5c, (b) 8i5d, (c) 8rro, (d) 8wte, and (e) 8wul, as calculated by AlphaFold2. Each heatmap visualizes the predicted pairwise positional error between residues (in Ångströms), with blue indicating high-confidence relationships and red indicating uncertainty. Diagonal blocks represent well-folded intradomain regions, while off-diagonal regions reflect domain–domain confidence. Modular designs like TCR-BiTEs often exhibit strong intradomain structure (blue blocks) and weaker interdomain anchoring (red zones), particularly at flexible linker regions.

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) coverage plots for (a) 8i5c, (b) 8i5d, (c) 8rro, (d) 8wte, and (e) 8wul. The red lines represent homologous sequences aligned to the query, while the black trace indicates the per-position alignment depth. High coverage in scFv and TCR domains suggests confident evolutionary constraints, whereas sparse coverage near linker junctions and novel fusions reflects limited homologous supportpotentially impacting AlphaFold2’s confidence and structural accuracy in those regions.

8i5c TCR-BiTE

The 8i5c construct shows high pLDDT scores across both the anti-CD3 scFv and the TCR α domains, with some localized drops in confidence that are centered around the GGGGS linker region (residues 100–120 and 480–510). The PAE heatmap shows a high intradomain confidence in specific areas like the scFv and individual TCR chains, while interdomain interactions exhibit higher predicted alignment error. MSA coverage is dense in the scFv and TCR regions but lower in the linker regions, which is expected in BiTE-like structures.

8i5d TCR-BiTE

In 8i5d, AlphaFold2 predicts a similarly high-confidence structure in the scFv and TCR domains (pLDDT > 90), with moderate confidence (60–70) at the GGGGS linker interfaces. These are shown in both the pLDDT trace and the interdomain zones of the PAE plot. The MSA coverage plot also shows the same interpretation, with strong sequence identity in structured regions and minimal coverage near the synthetic junctions.

8rro TCR-BiTE

The 8rro construct demonstrated a strong structured TCR connection with a high pLDDT and dense MSA coverage. The anti-CD3 scFv also exhibits strong confidence, while the GGGGS linker connecting them shows decreased pLDDT and elevated PAE, which suggests either flexibility or a lack of defined orientation between domains. Compared with other constructs, the linker region in 8rro is underrepresented in homologous sequences.

8wte TCR-BiTE

For the 8wte construct, the AlphaFold2 output predicted a high-confidence folding of the scFv and TCR chains, with pLDDT scores > 90, but the linker region again introduced moderate-to-low pLDDT values and diffuse PAE signal in the off-diagonal regions. The modularity of the construct is visually apparent in both the PAE and MSA plots, with well-folded blocks separated by flexible joints.

8wul TCR-BiTE

The 8wul construct preserves structural integrity in the scFv and TCR regions, with pLDDT scores > 90 in these domains similar to the others. The GGGGS linker region shows a broad confidence trough in the 60–75 range, consistent with flexible or disordered behavior. The PAE map supports a strong internal consistency within each domain and looser packing between them. The MSA profile again shows a strong alignment in natural modules but sparse representation at engineered junctions.

The pLDDT confidence profiles, PAE heatmaps, and MSA coverage plots collectively provide structural insights into the reliability of each TCR-BiTE model. Structured regions such as the TCR variable domains and scFv moieties consistently demonstrated strong evolutionary support and high AlphaFold confidence, while engineered linker regions showed lower certainty, as expected for non-native flexible regions. For each TCR-BiTE, we selected the top-ranked AlphaFold2 model (Rank 1) based on these confidence metrics for downstream structural refinement. These initial structures were then used to calculate a composite biophysical score and conduct MD simulations to evaluate and identify the most promising TCR-BiTE design.

MD Results

TCR-BiTE Stability

Based on the composite score rankings, 8i5c performed the best out of the five TCRs. However, 8wte and 8wul had similar composite scores (within 6 points of each other), so MD simulations were also run on these two TCRs. In addition, MD was performed on 8rro because of its distinct ability to bind to HLA-A*03, as explained in the Initial Identification of TCRs.

After the MD simulations were complete, the first metric observed was the RMSD. RMSD fluctuation was calculated by the command gmx rms, which generated the multicolumn data file for RMSD calculation; one file was created for each TCR-BiTE structure complex. Then, all four of the files were plotted using XMGrace, which generated the result observed in Figure . Among the four complexes, 8wul had the greatest RMSD value, peaking at 2.4 nm and stabilizing at 2 nm. This RMSD value is almost two times as great as the three other TCRs and is therefore considered to be indicative of excessive protein flexibility. RMSD guidelines were adapted from Figure in Carugo & Ponggor’s study for our 650 residue BiTEs. 8wul also experienced a rapid spike in its RMSD from 30 to 40 ns, depicting a large structural change in the protein. Both these qualities (high RMSD and spike in RMSD) make 8wul an unattractive complex for a TCR-BiTE structure. In contrast, 8wte displayed the lowest RMSD score (stabilized at 0.6 nm) and demonstrated the greatest stability (fluctuation radius < 0.1 nm). These two characteristics are desirable because they represent a stable conformation between the TCR and HLA, which is of utmost necessity for the TCR-BiTE structure to bind to the CD3 and HLA proteins.

RMSD value of structure backbone through 100 ns viewed with XMGrace. RMSD values fluctuate from 0 to 1 nm throughout the simulation, indicating significant but not erratic structural change.

Following the analysis of the RMSD value, the radius of gyration was tested as another stability metric. The same process described above for RMSD, with the exception of gmx gyrate, was used for the radius of gyration calculation. Results can be viewed in Figure . Within the set of TCRs, 8wte experienced the smallest radius (3 nm at 100 ns) and the most stability (radius of fluctuation < 0.1 nm). This is favorable because it shows that 8wte had tight packing of atoms, depicting a stable and compact conformation. For the other TCRs, 8i5c and 8rro demonstrated similar radius values to 8wte (3.1 and 3.25 nm), but they had constant fluctuations up to 0.25 nm throughout the 100 ns. In contrast, 8wul experienced the largest radius at 4 nm stabilization. Alone, this is not necessarily an issue, but in combination with its high RMSD value, this confirms that the backbone of 8wul expanded significantly during the simulation.

Radius of gyration of the structure backbone viewed with XMGrace.

These results indicate that 8wte is the most stable TCR complex under aqueous conditions. Its conformational stability, paired with its relatively compact structure, makes it a favorable candidate for incorporation into a TCR-BiTE.

TCR-BiTE Docked to CD3 and HLA-Bound Neoantigen

Since the TCR-BiTE construction containing the 8wte TCR appeared the most stable in initial simulations, as described previously, in terms of RMSD and Rg, which is why it was tested for its stability in docking to its targets, CD3 and the HLA-bound G12V neoantigen. Through HADDOCK, as described in the Methods, the three structures were docked. HADDOCK output three clusters, of which the nr1 structure from the highest scoring cluster (cluster 3 with a score of −98.4 kcal/mol ± 13.7) was utilized as input into the MD setup. The residues that were specified as active were found to be interacting, with the exception of the anti-CD3 VL chain, which was not within a range of 4 Å for any of the residues on CD3, indicating that it could not form many interactions. The nr1 structure from the cluster with the lowest docking score was selected for input into MD. Two main metrics were evaluated: radius of gyration of the backbones of each of the three structures and SASA of the CDR loops on the TCR-BiTE, before and after docking.

Figure depicts the radii of gyration of each of the three docked structures. As seen for CD3, the radius of gyration decreases from the start of the simulation up until approximately 20 ns and then primarily fluctuates around 1.3 nm by 0.25 nm. The 8wte TCR-BiTE observed relatively higher observed fluctuations in its radius, with two major observable drops, one at approximately 3 ns to 3.2 nm and the other at around 20 ns to 3 nm. After a slight increase in radius at approximately 30 ns, the structure’s radius then primarily fluctuates around 3.05 nm ± 0.05 nm for the remaining duration. The HLA-bound neoantigen experienced relatively extreme fluctuations in radius throughout the simulation, with fluctuations around 2.5 nm by ±0.1, with a particularly sharp spike to 2.7 nm at approximately 37 ns.

Figure depicts the SASA values of all of the CDR loops present in the constructed 8wte TCR-BiTE structure. All four chains maintained relatively stable fluctuations in SASA values for the duration of the simulation, with the docked 8wte beta chain CDRs displaying slightly steeper fluctuations. The average change in SASA was calculated for each chain by assessing the average SASA value of the undocked chain, and the average SASA of the docked chain, and found the difference of the docked value from the undocked to identify the change. Generally, if a structure is bound to another structure, through intermolecular contacts, it is expected that the SASA values for it would be lower since fewer residues would be fully exposed to the solvent. Therefore, in our case, this would be signified by a calculated negative change in SASA. The anti-CD3 chains were interacting with CD3, and the 8wte TCR chains were interacting with the HLA-bound neoantigen.

The average change in SASA for the anti-CD3 VH chain was calculated to be −0.056 nm (the average SASA for the docked structure was 30.44 and 30.496 nm for the undocked structure). With the same method of calculation, the average change in SASA for the anti-CD3 VL chain was −0.15 nm (SASA for the docked structure was 21.7 nm and 21.85 for undocked), −0.88 nm for the 8wte α chain (average SASA for docked was 27.49 and 27.58 nm for undocked), and 2.638 for the 8wte beta chain (average SASA was 27.75 nm for docked and 25.12 nm for undocked). The study that designed the crystal structure for 8wte identified that the CDR3 loop of the TCR beta chain primarily interacts with the neoantigen, so the increase in SASA for the docked structure could potentially point to a lack of interaction with the neoantigen for the duration of the MD simulation, evidenced by an increased level of exposure to the solvent.

Immunogenicity

As shown in Table , 8wte and 8rro, despite their large sizes of 663 and 686 amino acids, contained 77 and 75 strong binders, respectively, which corresponds to a low density of high-affinity class II epitopes when normalized to sequence length and distributed over multiple alleles. Moreover, the fact that these epitopes occur as discrete hotspots rather than extended clusters suggests that neither construct is heavily enriched for CD4+ T-cell epitopes, consistent with a relatively modest intrinsic risk of T-cell-mediated antidrug antibody responses. Discussion

This study outlines the initial computational design of two TCR-BiTEs that show promise for further in vitro experiments and treatment development for KRAS G12V mutations. The first, 8wte, is an HLA-A*11:01 targeting TCR that demonstrated exceptional binding to the KRAS G12V neoantigen during MM/GBSA free energy calculation and H-Bond analysis but comparatively poor structural stability during Modeler structural analysis. The second TCR, 8rro, demonstrated comparatively low binding free energy but sufficient Modeler stability. However, both of the TCRs exhibited structural stability in aqueous environments (in silico) during MD simulation. The structural stability makes each TCR-BiTE promising for distinct reasons, 8wte for its combination of adequate stability and high specificity to the KRAS G12V HLA-A*11:01 and 8rro for being the first TCR-BiTE to possess the unique ability to bind to HLA-A*03:01. Both TCR- BiTEs also serve as a proof-of-concept for the computational development of a TCR-BiTE, as our study also presents a computational pipeline for the design of a TCR-BiTE using open-source software.

In order to evaluate the structural feasibility of each of the TCR-BiTE designs, we used AlphaFold2 via the ColabFold multimer pipeline, which enabled us to achieve in silico predictions of the tertiary structures of the 5 TCR-BiTEs. The pLDDT profiles, PAE heatmaps, and MSA coverage plots confirmed strong and accurate predictions with folding in the anti-CD3 scFv and the various TCR regions paired with the linkerable junctions. High plDDT values (>85) in the anti-CD3 and TCR variable domains resulted in high structural confidence, proving for downstream binding and functional prediction. Even though regions of the GGGGS linkers achieved a lower confidence, it was expected due to a flexible design, making the TCR-BiTE improve in antigen engagement geometry.

More importantly, existing literature has shown that pLDDT tends to reflect flexibility rather than inaccuracy, and mobile or hinge-like regions such as the GGGGS linker often exhibit a lower confidence without misfolding. Our PAE heatmaps and MSA coverage plots affirm these calims, and both revealed a strong consistency within the scFV and TCR domains and a lower constraint in the GGGGS linkers.

The stable functional domains connected by flexible linkers follow a core design principle seen in many engineered immunotherapies. BiTE molecules are built to physically connect a cancer cell and a T cell, and that connection needs to be both strong and flexible to work well at the immunological synapse. Our AlphaFold predictions reflect this balance: they show high confidence in the folded scFv and TCR regions and expected lower confidence in the linker regions, which is not due to poor modeling but because those areas are meant to be flexible. This supports a broader view of AlphaFold in which it is able to handle highly complex structures such as TCR-BiTEs, and it could, as it continues to improve, become a central tool in the rational design of even more advanced multispecific immunotherapies.

Analysis of the five original TCRs using MM/GBSA and UCSF Chimera revealed several parallels with previous research. First, we found that 8wte performed the best out of the five TCRs in (1) its high-affinity residue interactions and (2) its hydrogen bonds and was only −2 kcal/mol off of 8wul in terms of MM/GBSA binding free energy. The TCR also achieved an average free energy composite score of 0.974 on a scale of 0–1, the highest of any TCR by >0.2. This aligns with the research that developed the 8wte TCR, which detected that 8wte was highly specific to the KRAS G12V peptide and had no detectable interaction with the KRAS WT peptide. This finding validated our 3-fold method of evaluating binding affinity and also suggested that 8wte was a strong TCR candidate for further analysis. Second, our results align with previous affinity assessments in that 8wte demonstrated a greater binding capacity to its HLA compared to 8wul. In our composite metric, 8wte scored 0.974, and 8rro scored 0.743 for free energy. This aligns with the research of Zhang et al., who mutated the 8wul TCR in 2 amino acid locations to develop the more optimized 8wte TCR.

While our results align with previous research, we would like to utilize more precise methods when it comes to the free energy calculation. While MM/GBSA has shown to be more accurate than traditional docking scoring functions in reaching a near native binding pose and also accurately predicting binding affinity, its accuracy can be improved by conducting it throughout an MD simulation in various snapshots, or using VD-MM/GBSA to incorporate residue specific dielectric constants. Future research will explore these 2 methods to understand TCR interactions with HLAs further.

Previous studies have noted that MD simulations are computationally and molecularly complex for BiTEs. However, we were able to model four TCR-BiTEs over biologically relevant time scales. Our MD result indicates 8wte as a promising candidate for future optimization. However, all 4 of the constructed BiTE structures: 8i5c, 8rro, 8wte, and 8wul established relatively stable binding conformations in the aqueous environment established by the MD simulation. Two established MD analysis techniques, RMSD and radius of gyration, were used in order to examine this.

While most current literature outlines acceptable RMSD values to be 1–2 Å, our TCR-BiTEs achieved stabilizing values of 10 Å. While high, this is not irregular for large complexes such as BiTEs because they have flexible regions such as CDR loops on the anti-CD3 scFv and TCR and are prone to atom fluctuations. Moreover, 8i5c, 8rro, and 8wte all achieved similar stabilizing RMSD scores (1.0 ± 0.3 nm), so it is highly likely that they stabilize at higher values. The differential between the TCRs was noted by their radius of fluctuation, which is the average fluctuation over the 100 ns simulation. The results indicate that 8wte had the lowest fluctuation radius (<0.1 nm), and was therefore the most stable TCR according to the RMSD result.

Expectedly, the radius of gyration followed suit from the RMSD, depicting a similar set of results. 8wte demonstrated the greatest stability, stabilizing at 30 nm with fluctuation radius >0.1. Additionally, the radius of gyration values of all of the four TCRs were comparable to the radius of gyration from similar BiTEs in documented literature. The literature cites the average radius of gyration for similar complexes as around 4.8 nm, which was slightly above the average of each of the TCRs that we observed. This finding validates the overall radius of the gyration result. However, similar to the RMSD, the facilitating factor that guided us to choose 8wte for further analysis was its stability radius, which remained consistently stable after 30 ns.

Referring to the docked structure of the CD3–8wte TCR-BiTE-HLA-bound neoantigen MD analysis, the Rg values of each BiTE were in a similar range compared to radius of gyration values from similarly sized structures in documented literature. The Rg values of all three structures, as shown in Figure , reached eventual stable values, signifying a stable, compact structure. In terms of the SASA of the CDR loops of the TCR-BiTE structure, the average changes in SASA were marginally negative for three of the CDR loop regions, with the exception of those of the 8wte beta chain. Generally, negative values of the SASA indicate that the docked structure is interacting with the residues of its target compared to its undocked structure.

However, the positive change in SASA value for the 8wte beta chain CDRs signifies that it may not be interacting with the residues on the HLA-bound neoantigen sufficiently. Based on existing literature surrounding linker design of such biomolecular conjugate designs, we hypothesize that a possible explanation for the limited interaction during binding could be explained by the linker lengths. In this paper, we followed the same linker design convention as the Blinatumomab structure, which contains GGGGS linkers. However, our BiTE contains two TCR chains instead of the anti-CD19 scFv, which could require different linker designs, potentially longer linkers, in order to facilitate a stable interaction with the target. Furthermore, we observed that the VL chain of the anti-CD3 scFv was not effectively interacting with the CD3 in the docked structure, which could also potentially be explained by the relatively short linker we utilized. Therefore, a possible avenue of exploration to expand on the work presented here would be to experiment with different linker designs and analyze how that affects the stability of TCR-BiTE, as well as its interactions with its two targets. Additionally, there is currently a sparsity of analysis of three-body docked systems in MD simulations, which also limits the analysis of our TCR-BiTE. In order to understand how this TCR-BiTE functions within a biological context, in vivo and in vitro experimentation would be required.

On the other hand, the fact that MHC-1 peptide complexes for KRAS G12V and WT peptides demonstrate similar results on the CD8+ T-cell recognition test highlights an important consideration approach. Because C-ImmSim uses a generic CD8+ T cell to predict the MHC-1 complexes’ T-cell recognition rather than TCR specificity, the lack of differential activation between KRAS G12V and WT suggests that natural antigens alone may not be sufficient to elicit mutation-selective recognition. Therefore, our proposed TCR-BiTE is designed to overcome this limitation in the nature of endogenous MHC-1 complex, through facilitating proximity between T cells and tumor-bearing G12V mutation. Ultimately, it aims to amplify functional immune activation beyond baseline recognition of the natural human immune system.

In tandem with antigen immunogenicity, the immunogenicity of the amino acid sequences of the TCR-BiTEs themselves was evaluated by predicting CD4+ T-cell epitopes. As described in the methodology of Drug Immunogenicity, a class II MHC binding predictor is used on overlapping 15-mers across common HLA-DRB1 alleles. Upon observation of binding hot spots, both constructs (663 and 686 aa) showed similar numbers of strong binders (%Rank < 1; 77 and 75, respectively), which appeared as discrete hotspots as opposed to dense clusters. These results indicate a modest overall class II epitope load and that the TCR-BiTE designs are not heavily enriched for CD4+ T-cell epitopes, suggesting a relatively low risk of T-cell-mediated antidrug antibody response. The true immunogenicity of the TCR-BiTEs will still need to be confirmed experimentally through PBMC-based assays and antidrug antibody monitoring.

Future development focused on the two TCR-BiTEs described in this study would involve isolation of TCRs from HLA donors (KRAS G12V-specific cell lines) and phage display methodologies with NNK randomization of CDRs to create variant TCRs with the goal of enhancing their affinities to the G12V neoantigen. For structural characterization, TCRs and pHLA complexes were mixed at specific determined concentrations and crystallized using sitting drop vapor diffusion. These methods were identified from Poole et al., who designed a TCR-BiTE-like complex targeting KRAS G12D

In order to comprehensively evaluate the feasibility of these TCR-BiTEs beyond their structural features, it is vital to evaluate their affinity to the TCRs, and their possible toxicity and immunogenicity. The TCRs we selected from RCSB are known to have interactions with their specific HLAs (HLA-A*11:01 for 8wte and HLA-A*03:01 for 8rro), but validating this in vitro through surface plasmon resonance will provide insights into their affinity. In reference to possible toxicity, bispecific antibody structures such as blinatumomab have been found to result in several dose-limiting toxicities and cytokine release syndrome, due to excessive T-cell activation, providing evidence that this is a possibility for the TCR-BiTEs presented in this paper. As a result, in vitro techniques including cytotoxicity and immune cell activation assays such as activation marker assays would be essential in determining toxicity, immunogenicity, and T-cell activation in vitro.

Additional considerations include how the metabolic environment could influence the activity of the described TCR-BiTEs, as highlighted by Shende et al. Through a review of existing literature on the effect of metabolism of cancer cells on T-cell activity, the researchers identified that characteristics such as dysregulated lipid metabolism, lactate buildup, and increased glycolysis result in suppressed immune activity and, as a result, impaired T-cell activation. This highlights potential limitations with TCR-BiTEs since their activity could be limited by the metabolic TME. Additional limitations could include issues with drug transport due to the size of the TCR-BiTE structures, which combine the T-cell receptors and the anti-CD3 scFv. Further analysis would require an in vitro evaluation of these characteristics.

In summary, we present the TCR-BiTEs 8wte and 8rro for future in vitro testing for KRAS G12V-induced cancers on HLA-A*11:01 and HLA-A*03:01, respectively. Our computational framework serves as a promising model for the future construction of TCR-BiTEs in silico.
