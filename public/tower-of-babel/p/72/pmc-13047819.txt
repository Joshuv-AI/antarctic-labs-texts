Conflict-driven antenatal care interruption in conflict zones of Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amare Simegn, Yitayal Ayalew Goshu, Temesgen Lingerh Endalew, Yeshiambaw Eshetie, Yirgalem Abere, Yohannes Tesfahun, Temesgen Birlie Asmare, Negesse Zurbachew Gobezie, Habtie Bantider Wubet, Kumlachew Geta Belete, Belayneh Jejaw Abate, Getachew Mekete Deress, Biruk Demissie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe delivery of maternal health services is compromised by conflict’s substantial disruption of health systems. Antenatal care (ANC) continuity in Ethiopia has been jeopardized by ongoing armed hostilities in conflict areas of Ethiopia. The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to determine the pooled prevalence of ANC interruption and investigate its drivers in war affected regions of Ethiopian.MethodsWe systematically researched articles published between 2015 and 2025 in PubMed, Embase, CINAHL and Scopus. Grey literatures (International Humanitarian Organizations, United Nations and International Monitoring Agencies like Offices of Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs-OCHA) were used to support completeness of the sources. Studies that reported ANC utilization or disruption in Ethiopia’s conflict-affected areas were included. Information about ANC results, study parameters, and contextual factors were extracted. I² statistics were used to evaluate heterogeneity, and pooled prevalence was estimated using random-effects model meta-analysis.ResultsEight peer-reviewed studies were included for the review. The pooled prevalence of ANC interruption was 54.3% (95% CI: 39.2–69.4). Facility destruction, insecurity, and population displacement were among the determinants. These results were supported by grey literature, which stated that maternal services were extremely limited and most health institutions were non-operational. Grey literature corroborated these findings, reporting that the majority of health facilities were non-functional and maternal services severely limited.ConclusionANC interruption in conflict-affected Ethiopia is high, affecting more than half of pregnant women. Immediate interventions are required, including health facility rehabilitation, mobile outreach services, workforce strengthening, and supply chain restoration to mitigate maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13031-026-00790-5.Keywords: Antenatal care, Interruption, Ethiopia, Conflict, Maternal health, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

In this review, ANC interruption is defined as the partial or complete discontinuation of recommended antenatal care contacts during pregnancy, relative to the minimum schedule recommended by the World Health Organization, as a result of conflict-related barriers such as insecurity, population displacement, and damage to health facilities, disruption of health services, and restricted physical or financial access to care [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends at least eight antenatal care (ANC) contacts to lower perinatal mortality and enhance women’s experience of care [2]. Ethiopia represents a critical case study for conflict-maternal health research because of the multi-regional nature of recent conflicts, large-scale internal displacement, and prolonged disruption of primary health-care delivery across several administrative regions. The direct impact of war on maternal healthcare access was demonstrated by a geographic modeling research from the Tigray War, which found that 24.8% of pregnant women had ANC interruption [3]. In a similar vein, just 36.5% of women received optimum ANC, according to a sizable community-based survey carried out during the conflict [4]. Skilled birth and postnatal care were also greatly reduced. Even while ANC initiation has improved, post-war evaluations continue to reveal that ANC completion and effective coverage are still incredibly low; in post-war Tigray, effective coverage was only 3.8% [5]. Similarly in Amhara region, before the onset of the war, the monthly average number of women who used antenatal care services at least once was 28,891. After the conflict expanded, the monthly average dropped to 10,895, representing a 62.3% reduction in ANC utilization in the Amhara region. Additionally, only 46.9% of health facilities continued offering the continuum of care (ANC, delivery, postnatal) in these areas during the conflict [6]. Significant disruption of maternal health services is also confirmed by information from hospitals. In comparison to pre-war data, a comparative analysis from a teaching hospital in Tigray revealed significant increases in maternal morbidity and decreased access to critical maternal health care during the war [7]. These quantitative results are contextualized and supported by reports in the grey literature. Widespread disruption of maternal health care, including broken referral systems and a shortage of trained birth attendants in war regions, was initially observed by UNFPA [8].The majority of health institutions in Tigray became non-operational, which directly limited women’s access to ANC, according to UNOCHA situation reports [6]. The destruction of facilities, shortages of supplies, and relocation of health workers in conflict-affected areas were also reported in WHO public-health bulletins [9]. In the districts most severely impacted, UNICEF reported significant drops in the availability of maternal and newborn health services [10]. Women were unable to get routine ANC visits or emergency obstetric treatments due to the looting, destruction, or inaccessibility of numerous health facilities, according to field evaluations by Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) [11]. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) found significant obstacles to access for displaced women, such as insecurity, a lack of transportation, and the breakdown of primary healthcare services [12].

These interruption of ANC has important public-health consequences, including delayed detection and management of pregnancy complications, reduced uptake of preventive interventions such as tetanus toxoid vaccination and iron folate supplementation, and increased risks of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [13, 14]. In conflict-affected areas, disruption of more than 50% probably raises the risk of hypertensive disorders, obstructed labor, stillbirth, and maternal death [15]. When combined, peer-reviewed research and humanitarian field reports consistently show that ANC disruption in Ethiopia’s conflict areas is severe, pervasive, and multifactorial caused by supply shortages, displacement, insecurity, health system collapse, and limited access to operational facilities. Although several national situation reports and narrative reviews have documented the disruption of maternal health services during conflict in Ethiopia, there is currently no systematic and quantitative synthesis of the magnitude of conflict-driven antenatal care (ANC) interruption across conflict-affected areas. Existing reports primarily describe service disruptions qualitatively or focus on specific facilities or regions. Despite the substantial contextual heterogeneity of conflict-affected settings, meta-analysis remains valuable for estimating the overall average burden of ANC interruption and for formally quantifying between-study variability. The findings of this review are intended to inform humanitarian response planning, prioritization of maternal health interventions, and policy decision-making for service restoration and protection of maternal health Programmes in fragile and conflict-affected settings by estimating the pooled prevalence of ANC interruption and synthesizing knowledge on the drivers of ANC disruption.

Methods

Protocol registration and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis will be conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The protocol is registered with registration number CRD420251250011 in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) prior to initiating the review. No substantive deviations from the registered protocol were made.

Review questions

To estimate the magnitude and determinants of antenatal care (ANC) interruption among women living in conflict-affected zones of Ethiopia.

Eligibility criteria

Studies among women living in conflict-affected areas of Ethiopia who are either pregnant or of reproductive age (15–49) were included. Areas officially recognized as experiencing armed conflict, insecurity, or forced displacement are referred to as conflict-affected areas. The review’s findings included: Missed ANC visits; reduced ANC contacts in comparison to WHO or national guidelines; delayed ANC initiation because of conflict; and total lack of ANC throughout pregnancy due to conflict, displacement, or insecurity. Cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, and mixed-method studies (qualitative studies will be narratively described but not included in statistical pooling, and the quantitative component will only be used for meta-analysis) were all included.

Only English-language studies carried out in Ethiopia’s conflict-affected regions were taken into account. However, studies carried out outside of Ethiopia, studies that evaluate ANC independently of conflict, editorials, commentary, opinion papers, case reports, and studies lacking extractable data were not included.

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, CINAHL databases. Grey literatures were identified via UN, WHO, UNICEF and others. In addition to database searches, the first 150 records from Google Scholar sorted by relevance were screened to identify additional eligible studies. A structured search strategy combining MeSH terms, keywords, and Boolean operators was used. The search string included: (“antenatal care“[MeSH] OR “prenatal care” OR ANC OR antenatal OR prenatal) AND (interrupt OR disrupt OR discontinuation OR “service interruption” OR missed OR “reduced attendance”) AND (conflict OR “armed conflict” OR war OR insecurity OR displacement OR “internally displaced persons” OR IDP) AND (Ethiopia OR Ethiopian). The search strategy was adapted appropriately for each database.

Study selection

All identified records were exported to EndNote for deduplication. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by three reviewers (YE and YT, TLE). Full texts were retrieved and screened using predefined eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. All steps were detailed in a PRISMA flow diagram, including reasons for excluded records at full-text stage.

Quality review of the included studies

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists were used to evaluate the included studies’ methodological quality. The quantitative studies were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies, and the qualitative studies were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Study quality did not determine statistical weighting in the meta-analysis. To reduce bias and guarantee dependability, three reviewers (BD and TBA, NZG) independently carried out the quality appraisal. The listed studies were evaluated by each reviewer based on the pertinent checklist criteria.

When reviewers couldn’t agree, they worked together to reach a consensus. A third reviewer (TBA) was consulted to make the final decision if there was no consensus. Each of the checklist’s eight items was assessed and assigned a rating of “Yes,” “No,” or “Unclear.” The studies were classified as high quality if there were seven to eight “Yes” responses, moderate quality if there were four to six “Yes” responses, and poor quality if there were or were 0 to three “Yes” responses. This grading scheme is in line with the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis, which suggests that in order to guarantee uniformity and openness in critical evaluation, overall quality levels should be assigned based on the number of criteria satisfied.

Data synthesis and statistical analysi

After being exported into Microsoft Excel, the data were imported into STATA 17.0 for additional analysis. Based on the research characteristics, a pair of reviewers (YT and YE) coded the data. All included studies were summarized narratively based on region, study year, sample size, types of study design and prevalence of ANC interruption, and determinants. Pooled prevalence with 95% CI and Odds Ratios were used as an effect measures as most primary studies applied logistic regression models and reported associations using odds ratios. To ensure consistency and to avoid inappropriate transformations across heterogeneous study designs and models, effect measures were synthesized as reported by the original studies. Random-effects model was used and heterogeneity were evaluated using I² statistics. Subgroup Analyses was conducted where data permit. When quantitative pooling of determinants was not feasible because of heterogeneity in exposure definitions, outcome measures, or analytical approaches, determinants were synthesized narratively by summarizing the direction and consistency of reported associations.

Data extraction

A standardized data-extraction tool was developed and pilot-tested. Two reviewers were independently extract data covering: Study characteristics: author, year, region, and study design, sampling method, Participants: sample size, sociodemographic characteristics, Determinants, proportion of ANC interruption, effect sizes, and confidence intervals.

Publication bias

Because fewer than 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis, formal tests for publication bias (e.g., funnel plot asymmetry, Egger’s regression) were not performed, consistent with Cochrane guidance. Instead, we qualitatively assessed the likelihood of publication bias by examining study characteristics, effect size patterns, and the comprehensiveness of our search strategy, including grey literature. Although formal quantitative assessment was not possible, the risk of publication bias was judged as [low/moderate/high] based on these considerations.”

Ethics and dissemination

Since this review uses already published data, ethical approval is not required. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication, conference presentations, and policy briefs for organizations working in conflict-affected maternal health.

Results

Out of 506 records screened, eight studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing a total of 12,745 participants (Fig. 1). The pooled prevalence of antenatal care (ANC) misuse or interruption across these studies was 54.3% (95% CI: 39.2–69.4), indicating that over half of the participants experienced ANC discontinuity in the included settings (Fig. 2). At the full-text screening stage, studies were excluded primarily because they did not report ANC interruption as an outcome, were not conducted in conflict-affected settings in Ethiopia, lacked extractable quantitative data, or employed ineligible study designs.

Characteristics of the included studies

All studies included in the review employed a cross-sectional design. The regional distribution of included studies was imbalanced, with a substantial proportion conducted in Tigray Region in which four studies undertaken in the Tigray region, and one study each conducted in Amhara, Afar, Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples’ (SNNP), and Somali regions. This distribution reflects geographic coverage across northern, northeastern, southern, and eastern parts of the country.

Regarding the year of data collection, two studies were conducted in 2024 and three studies in 2025, indicating recent evidence. The sample sizes of the included studies varied considerably, ranging from 357 to 4,151 participants, indicating substantial differences in study scale. Across included studies, the prevalence of ANC interruption ranged widely from 24.8% to 84.5%, representing a major finding and indicating marked variation in the severity of service disruption across conflict-affected settings (Table 1).

Publication bias, small-study effects, and heterogeneity

Formal assessment of publication bias and small-study effects, including funnel plot inspection and statistical tests such as Egger’s or Begg’s tests, was not undertaken because the number of included studies (n = 8) was below the recommended threshold for reliable evaluation. With fewer studies, these methods lack sufficient statistical power and may yield misleading inferences, particularly in the presence of considerable heterogeneity.

The very high heterogeneity observed (I² = 99.8%) is likely attributable to substantive different contextual differences across studies rather than random error alone. The pooled prevalence of ANC interruption should be interpreted as an average estimate across highly heterogeneous conflict contexts rather than as a precise or directly generalizable population value. The very high heterogeneity (I² = 99.8%) indicates substantial contextual variation between studies.

The included studies were conducted in diverse conflict-affected and fragile settings, encompassing different regions, population characteristics, levels of insecurity, health system functionality, and time periods. Additional sources of heterogeneity include variability in sample size, measurement of ANC misuse or interruption, and access to maternal health services across regions.

To minimize the risk of biased or spurious pooled estimates, several methodological measures were implemented. A random-effects model was applied to account for both within-study and between-study variability. Sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out procedures (Fig. 3), were conducted to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate, with results demonstrating stability in the direction of the association.

Sensitivity analysis (Leave-One-Out Analysis)

Given these considerations, pooled estimates are presented as summary measures reflecting average effects across heterogeneous contexts, and confidence intervals were conservatively interpreted. The transparent acknowledgment of heterogeneity and analytical constraints strengthens the validity of the findings and provides an appropriate basis for interpretation in settings characterized by conflict, displacement, and health system disruption. The limited number of available studies highlights a clear need for further high-quality primary research to strengthen the evidence base and support more robust synthesis in future analyses.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimate by sequentially excluding individual studies from the analysis. The results demonstrated that the overall effect size remained essentially unchanged following the removal of each study. This indicates that no single study exerted a disproportionate influence on the pooled estimate. Moreover, variations in study characteristics, including differences in sample size, methodological quality, and analytical assumptions, did not materially affect the overall effect estimate. These findings suggest that the pooled result is stable and robust, and that the observed effect is not driven by any individual study (Fig. 3).

Determinants of ANC misuse or Interruption

Several factors were identified across the included studies as determinants of antenatal care (ANC) misuse or interruption. These determinants include individual, household, community, and health system related factors, as well as contextual factors related to conflict and insecurity. Quantitative meta-analysis of the determinants was not feasible because only two studies [4, 16] reported statistically measurable associations, whereas the remaining studies described determinants qualitatively as barriers. This limitation constrains the ability to estimate the strength and consistency of associations across studies and should be considered when interpreting the evidence (Fig. 3).

Women’s ability to attend routine ANC visits was hampered by limited access to health facilities, place of residence, and cultural competency skills of providers, political and economic instability, and providers’ attitudes. These factors also had an impact on the quality of care received, which in turn affected women’s willingness to seek maternal health services [17]. ANC utilization was further limited by geographic disparities, having more children under five, and lack of information access. Women living in remote or underserved areas had to deal with long travel distances, logistical difficulties, and conflicting childcare responsibilities, and their lack of information limited their understanding of the significance and timing of ANC visits [3].

Additional characteristics included younger age, education, and the respondents’ attitudes; younger and less educated women frequently had less awareness of maternal health issues, and those who had unfavorable opinions of healthcare services were less likely to attend ANC [16]. Because many pregnant women did not see the need for preventative visits when they felt well, routine care was discouraged due to lack of awareness and the absence of reported health issues [18]. This reduced adoption of crucial ANC interventions. Lastly, many women were unable to safely access health facilities due to restricted movement brought on by insecurity in conflict-affected or unstable areas, which exacerbated already-existing barriers and ultimately contributed to suboptimal antenatal care utilization [19].

Overall, these findings indicate that ANC misuse or interruption is influenced by a combination of measurable socio-demographic factors in some studies and descriptive barriers related to health systems, household, and conflict contexts in others, highlighting the complex determinants of maternal healthcare access in fragile and conflict-affected settings.

Discussions

This study offers thorough data on the extent of ANC disruption caused by conflict in Ethiopia’s conflict areas. During times of armed conflict, 54.3% of pregnant women reported discontinuation or non-utilization of ANC services, according to the pooled estimate, underscoring a significant decline in vital maternal health treatments in vulnerable environments. The findings indicate that ANC interruption arises from the combined effects of conflict-related insecurity, population displacement, and health-system collapse, including damaged facilities, disrupted supply chains, and shortages of health workers, which together undermine both access to and availability of maternal health services. The extent of ANC disruption found in this study is in line with results from other low- and middle-income nations affected by violence. ANC usage has been found to decline by 40% to 70% during times of active conflict in South Sudan, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Somalia [3, 20, 21]. However, given the scope, duration, and geographic dispersion of the conflict as well as the deliberate destruction of civilian infrastructure, including medical institutions, Ethiopia’s disruption seems especially severe [5, 12]. This result highlights the extreme susceptibility and limited resilience of maternal health systems to conflict. This is consistent with more extensive data from humanitarian settings where regular maternal services are among the first to be interrupted during emergencies [3, 17]. ANC utilization has gradually but unevenly improved before the onset of violent conflicts in Ethiopia’s northern, western, and central regions. Although completion of four or more ANC visits remained suboptimal, especially in rural and pastoralist regions, nationally representative surveys showed a continuous increase in at least one visit, climbing from 62% in 2016 to almost 74% by 2019 [18, 19]. The decentralization of primary health care services, community mobilization, and the growth of the Health Extension Program (HEP) were all major factors in these improvements [22]. However, structural disparities continued even in the pre-war era, with lower utilization among women living in rural regions, those with low levels of education, and households experiencing poverty [23]. The Ethiopian context is distinguished by the multi-regional and prolonged nature of the conflict, extensive internal displacement, and sustained disruption of primary health-care delivery across several administrative regions, rather than localized or short-term crises. This study’s pooled ANC disruption estimate of 54.3% shows a sharp reversal of pre-war advances. Following the start of conflicts, ANC attendance consistently drops sharply in conflict-affected areas of Ethiopia [17, 24, 25]. Humanitarian evaluations and facility-based reports revealed extensive damage to medical facilities, theft of medical supplies, and disruption of referral networks, making ANC services either unavailable or non-operational [26]. The effects of conflict on ANC interruption may differ between internally displaced women and women residing in host communities; however, most included studies did not disaggregate findings by displacement status. In several locations, more than 50% of maternity care facilities were either totally or partially damaged, leaving women to either completely forgo care or rely on risky substitutes [27]. Service disruption was made worse by population displacement. Significant obstacles experienced by internally displaced pregnant women included financial difficulty, insecurity during transit, loss of documentation, and ignorance of service availability [28]. Even in cases where services ostensibly continued to operate, facility attendance was discouraged by military checkpoints, road blockades, and fear of violence [29]. These conflict-related obstacles signal a significant shift from pre-war determinants of ANC utilization, with security-driven and systemic flaws replacing socioeconomic and cultural considerations as the main hurdles. Maternal and newborn health are seriously affected by the disruption of ANC services at the level seen in this study. ANC is essential for tetanus toxoid immunization, early identification of pregnancy-related problems, control of infections and anemia, and connection to expert birth attendance [14]. In conflict-affected areas, disruption of more than 50% probably raises the risk of hypertensive disorders, obstructed labor, stillbirth, and maternal death [15]. Maternal health care are not very resilient to long-term warfare, as seen by the sharp difference between pre-war advancement and post-war breakdown and can be interpreted as a policy and system-level failure to protect essential services during large-scale crises. Ethiopia’s primary health care system proved to be quite vulnerable to labor relocation, supply chain disruptions, and insecurity, even if it showed efficacy in stable settings. Therefore, post-conflict recovery initiatives must emphasize building the conflict-sensitive health system over returning to pre-war service levels. In order to mitigate future disruptions, strategies including mobile ANC clinics, integration of services within humanitarian operations, protection of health institutions under international humanitarian law, and focused outreach to internally displaced women are essential [4, 16]. Recommended actions should be implemented through existing humanitarian coordination mechanisms, including health cluster response platforms coordinated by the Global Health Cluster and national coordination structures. Priority research gaps should include longitudinal studies to assess service recovery trajectories, disaggregated analyses by displacement status and conflict exposure intensity, and evaluations of alternative service-delivery models for sustaining ANC during insecurity.

Strengths and limitations

The pooled estimate from this study offers a solid summary of the data on ANC disruption in Ethiopian conflict areas. However, the accuracy of the pooled estimate may be impacted by variation between study contexts, conflict phases, and assessment techniques. Furthermore, disruption among highly mobile or inaccessible individuals may be underestimated if facility-based and cross-sectional statistics are relied upon. Notwithstanding these drawbacks, the conclusion that violence has caused significant and widespread ANC disruption is strengthened by the uniformity of findings across regions.

Conclusions

This study shows that ANC services in Ethiopia’s conflict areas have been severely disrupted by armed conflict, which is a widespread and serious setback to the improvements in maternal health made before the conflict. This review advances the evidence base beyond fragmented facility-based and single-region studies. The results highlight the critical need for conflict-responsive maternal health interventions, emergency health information and monitoring systems and ongoing funding for robust health systems that can continue to provide critical services in times of emergency. Immediate priorities should focus on humanitarian actions such as mobile and outreach ANC services and rapid restoration of essential supplies, while longer-term recovery must prioritize system rebuilding, workforce recovery, infrastructure rehabilitation, and improved health-system resilience.
