Genomic structural equation modeling decodes skeletal aging: novel loci discovery and multisystem genetic crosstalk
Authors: Yunqiao Zhou, Jian Huang, Leqin Xu, Fan Zhang, Chunxiao Bai, Fangyang Fan, Yuquan Wang, Bixuan Fang, Tian Wang, Xiaohong Mu, Jinyu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSkeletal aging, a core determinant of systemic aging, poses a global public health challenge due to its association with chronic diseases and functional decline. This study aimed to decode the genetic architecture of skeletal aging by identifying novel loci and multi-system crosstalk using genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic SEM).MethodsWe integrated genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from five musculoskeletal-related traits (osteoporosis [OP], osteoarthritis [OA], lumbar spinal stenosis [LSS], telomere length [TL], and low back pain [LBP]) across 462,933 to 472,174 European individuals. Genomic SEM, FUMA, FUSION, and fine-mapping tools (SuSIE/FINEMAP) were applied to model latent skeletal aging ("mvSAge") and identify causal variants, enriched pathways, and tissue-specific gene expression.ResultsThe latent factor model (CFI = 0.993, SRMR = 0.065) revealed shared genetic architecture among OP, OA, LSS, TL, and LBP. We identified 514 lead SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻12), including 136 novel loci enriched in regulatory regions (e.g., brain putamen, frontal cortex). Fine-mapping prioritized causal variants (posterior probability > 0.95) near MTPAP, GRAMD4, and DPP8, implicating mitochondrial function and immune regulation. Transcriptome-wide analyses highlighted PPP6R3 (bone mineral density) and SLC33A1 (anticancer target) as key genes. Enrichment analyses linked mvSAge to Wnt signaling, ER stress, and Mendelian disorders (e.g., ALS). Chromosomes 1, 2, and 4 showed elevated heritability contributions, driven by conserved regulatory elements (RUNX2, SP7) and chromatin accessibility hotspots.ConclusionThis study establishes mvSAge as a genetically cohesive construct and uncovers novel loci, pathways, and multi-system interactions underlying skeletal aging. These findings advance precision medicine strategies for aging-related musculoskeletal disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07104-y.Keywords: Skeletal aging, Genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic SEM), Genome-wide association study (GWAS), Multi-system crosstalk, Wnt signaling

Introduction

As Hippocrates once stated, "Bones are the foundation of health; when they begin to age, the bedrock of life silently crumbles." Aging, characterized by the breakdown of physiological homeostasis [1], age-related diseases, and mortality, represents not merely a multifaceted decline in human biological functions [2, 3] but also an urgent global public health challenge due to escalating societal aging. The rising prevalence of age-associated chronic conditions—including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, and osteoarticular degenerative diseases—imposes substantial socioeconomic burdens [4, 5]. Skeletal aging has been identified as a central mediator of systemic aging [6], transcending mere musculoskeletal functional decline to encompass a complex, irreversible biological process marked by deteriorating physiological resilience and diminished bone remodeling capacity [7–9], profoundly influenced by genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors [10–12]. With accelerating global aging [13], the rapid increase in skeletal senescence and its associated comorbidities, such as frailty, poses critical challenges to medical and socioeconomic systems [13–15]. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study [16], musculoskeletal disorders now affect 1.71 billion individuals worldwide, equating to 21 cases per 100 people. Despite recent advances in osteoarthritis (OA) research [17–20], the genetic interactions and biological crosstalk underlying multisystem pathologies in skeletal aging remain poorly understood. Elucidating shared genetic architectures and cross-system interactions between skeletal and other physiological systems is imperative for developing targeted public health interventions and precision clinical strategies [21]. These genetic modeling findings ​​will enable the development of novel diagnostic models and therapeutic tools​​ for future disease intervention [22–24].

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have advanced our understanding of skeletal health by linking single-phenotype traits to sociodemographic, pathological, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Evidence suggests that air pollution [25] and smoking [26] may accelerate skeletal aging, while emerging research explores systemic crosstalk through circulating biomarkers [27] and brain-tissue [28] interactions. However, single-phenotype approaches inadequately explain interindividual variability in aging trajectories and fail to address complex traits such as senescence, frailty, and metabolic syndromes. To address these limitations, this study integrates advanced genetic analytical tools and robust correlation frameworks to uncover molecular mechanisms and expand associations with multifactorial pathologies. We specifically focus on genomic loci and chromosomal regions linked to skeletal aging, aiming to identify therapeutic targets. Our work not only deepens the mechanistic understanding of skeletal senescence but also provides theoretical and practical foundations for precision medicine.

To overcome the challenge of imprecise measurement in skeletal aging mechanisms, we designed a latent GWAS targeting unobserved skeletal aging phenotypes. Leveraging genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic-SEM), we analyzed summary statistics from published GWAS on skeletal aging-related diseases and biomarkers. This approach enabled the derivation of SNP associations with latent skeletal aging traits, forming a novel GWAS for a previously unmeasured construct termed "mvSAge" (multivariate skeletal aging). Building on systems biology principles, we defined residual genetic variations in mvSAge unexplained by known biomarkers as potential genetic signatures, subjecting them to multi-tiered GWAS analyses. While this method does not fully disentangle the intricate interplay of genetic, environmental, and stochastic factors driving skeletal aging, it mitigates confounding biases from aging biomarkers, facilitating analysis of otherwise intractable data. Finally, from a translational perspective, we conducted over 10,000 correlation analyses to construct an accessible multisystem crosstalk map for non-bioinformaticians (e.g., clinicians, nurses), highlighting clinically actionable "bone-system" associations to guide preventive and therapeutic strategies. Our study pioneers a streamlined pathway bridging genomic statistics, basic research, and precision clinical implementation.

Single-input GWAS data sources

The individual GWAS datasets for skeletal aging were derived from five musculoskeletal-related phenotypes: osteoporosis (OP), osteoarthritis (OA), lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), telomere length (TL), and low back pain (LBP). All input GWAS studies obtained ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards, with written informed consent provided by all participants and rigorous quality control procedures applied.

The OP, OA, LSS, TL, and LBP datasets were sourced from publicly available GWAS summary statistics. Specifically, the OP dataset (IEU GWAS ID: ukb-b-12141) included 7547 cases and 455,386 controls; the OA dataset (ukb-b-14486) included 38,472 cases and 424,461 controls; the LSS dataset (ukb-d-M13_SPINSTENOSIS) was obtained from the Neale Lab and included 1910 cases and 359,284 controls; the TL dataset (ieu-b-4879) was derived from 472,174 European individuals; and the LBP dataset (ukb-b-1557) included 2439 cases and 460,571 controls. Detailed GWAS metadata are provided in Table 1.

Sample overlap in single-input GWAS

The input GWAS datasets were obtained from distinct genomic repositories with non-overlapping participant cohorts. We comprehensively evaluated potential sample overlaps across studies to ensure analytical robustness and generalizability while accounting for statistical impacts of residual overlaps.

Genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic SEM)

We implemented Genomic SEM using the GenomicSEM [29] R package (v0.0.5) to perform multivariate GWAS analyses on OP, OA, LSS, TL, and LBP, investigating the shared genetic architecture underlying mvSAge-related traits. Genomic SEM is a novel multivariate method that enables the exploration of latent structures across complex phenotypes. Detailed specifications are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Genomic SEM is robust to sample overlap (e.g., UK Biobank participants appearing in multiple input GWAS) and sample size imbalances. It facilitates the identification of variants affecting subsets rather than all traits, thereby excluding variants with broad cross-trait effects.

The analysis comprised two stages:

Stage 1: Estimation of the empirical genetic covariance matrix and corresponding sampling covariance matrix. We prepared summary statistics for OP, OA, LSS, TL, and LBP and applied multivariate extensions of cross-trait LD score regression to generate an empirical genetic covariance matrix for the six traits, which served as input for the SEM common factor model.

We prepared summary statistics for OP, OA, LSS, TL, and LBP and applied multivariate extensions of cross-trait LD score regression to generate an empirical genetic covariance matrix for the six traits, which served as input for the SEM common factor model.

Stage 2: Specification of an SEM model to minimize discrepancies between the hypothesized covariance matrix and the empirically derived matrix. Our primary objective was to identify genetic factors shared across the six skeletal aging-related traits; thus, we tested a single-factor model. Model fit was evaluated using standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), χ2 statistics, Akaike information criterion (AIC), and comparative fit index (CFI) (Supplementary Table 2). By incorporating SNP associations into the genetic and residual covariance matrices under the common-factor SEM framework, we generated multivariate mvSAge GWAS results encompassing shared covariance across 2,026,842 input SNPs.

SNP heterogeneity in genomic SEM

To assess whether mvSAge-associated SNPs were appropriately modeled within the multivariate SEM framework, we computed SNP heterogeneity statistics (QSNP). The null hypothesis posited that SNP associations from individual GWAS could be fully mediated by mvSAge. Significant QSNP (P < 0.05) indicated SNPs exerting effects through pathways independent of the shared mvSAge genetic architecture.

Multi-tiered evaluation of genomic SEM

We implemented a hierarchical adjustment strategy for the Genomic SEM model, including:

Correction of correlation P-values at two stringency thresholds (P < 5 × 10⁻1⁶ and P < 5 × 10⁻12) to identify novel SNPs across analytical tiers.

Two-step LD score regression for genomic control parameters. Specific steps included: no removal of SNPs with missing values; no exclusion based on INFO scores < 0.9, MAF < 0.01, or invalid P-values; no removal of non-SNPs or ambiguous strand orientations; exclusion of partitioned LD scores with zero variance; and use of a two-step estimator with a cutoff of 30 SNPs.

Definition of genomic loci and novelty assessment

We employed FUMA [30] (Functional Mapping and Annotation of Genome-Wide Associations) to define genomic loci and identify lead SNPs associated with mvSAge. Lead SNPs were defined as genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) variants in low linkage disequilibrium (LD; r2 < 0.1) with other SNPs. We compared these loci against those identified in original univariate GWAS and classified loci as novel if located > 1 Mb away from known associations.

We utilized the ​​Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) version 6.8​​ (http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/) [31] to obtain ​​updated Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotations​​ and ​​Gene Ontology (GO) annotations​​, which served as the ​​reference background​​. Genes were mapped against this background gene set, and ​​functional enrichment analysis​​ was performed using the ​​R package clusterProfiler (v3.21)​​ to identify enriched pathways and biological functions among genes proximal to novel loci. GO annotation analysis encompassed ​​three categories​​: ​​Cellular Component (CC)​​, ​​Biological Process (BP)​​, and ​​Molecular Function (MF)​​. Resulting P-values were ​​adjusted for multiple testing​​ using the ​​default Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate method​​. ​​Adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

To evaluate pleiotropy among 37 mvSAge lead SNPs, we cross-referenced published associations (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) in the GWAS Catalog. Additionally, we conducted risk locus analysis using FUMA under a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) and performed gene-level association testing via MAGMA (Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation), which aggregates SNP-level signals into gene-level associations. Gene-level significance was defined as false discovery rate (FDR) P < 0.05.

Fine-mapping

To identify the most probable causal variants for mvSAge, we implemented SuSIE (Sum of Single Effects) and FINEMAP [32] within the R package echolocatoR (v2.0.3). Key steps included:

Regional analysis: A 250-kb window centered on each lead SNP was analyzed to compute posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) for causal variants.

Credible set definition: Variants with PIP ≥ 0.95 were classified as causal.

Consensus SNP identification: echolocatoR defined "consensus SNPs" as variants overlapping between SuSIE and FINEMAP results. For these SNPs, average PIPs were calculated, and a final credible set was determined by thresholding average PIPs at ≥ 0.95.

Transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS)

Following causal variant prioritization, we conducted TWAS [33] using FUSION with precomputed expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) weights from GTEx v8 (37,920 gene-tissue pairs). Of these, 36,149 features were analyzable in mvSAge data, indicating high data quality. Genes with nominal significance (P < 0.05, uncorrected) were advanced to FOCUS fine-mapping, which estimates posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) to prioritize causal genes. Genes showing TWAS significance and consistent FOCUS evidence (PIP ≥ 0.95) were classified as high-confidence candidates.

Gene set and disease ontology enrichment

We performed gene set enrichment analysis using MAGMA and GeneCards, followed by protein–protein interaction network construction via GeneMANIA [34]. MendelVar [35] (https://mendelvar.mrcieu.ac.uk/) was employed to assess overlaps between mvSAge loci and Mendelian disease genes. MendelVar integrates 12,344 gene-disease associations (80.2% with disease descriptions, 82.8% annotated with Human Phenotype Ontology terms, 53.2% with Disease Ontology terms) and ∼130,000 ClinVar rare variants, enabling GWAS-compatible enrichment testing of Mendelian and other gene ontology terms.

Cellular annotation

To identify cell types etiologically linked to mvSAge, we applied CELLECT (Cell-type-specific expression integration for complex traits) to single-cell RNA-seq data from Tabula Muris (100,000 cells across 20 murine tissues). Data were preprocessed and normalized using CELLEX to compute expression specificity scores. Cell type-specific enrichment was assessed via LD score regression with an FDR threshold of 0.05.

Partitioned heritability across genomic regions

We calculated partitioned heritability using LD score regression (LDSC), which allocates heritability to predefined genomic annotations (e.g., genes, enhancers, repressors). LDSC utilizes weighted LD matrices, genotype frequency files, and summary statistics to estimate the contribution of each genomic region to phenotypic heritability.

Polygenic risk score (PRS) construction

We computed PRS using PRS-CS (Polygenic Risk Score with Continuous Shrinkage), which employs Bayesian regression to estimate SNP effect sizes from GWAS summary statistics and external LD reference panels. This method enables the assessment of chromosome-specific genetic contributions to disease risk.

Results

Construction of structural equation model (SEM) statistical metrics

LD Score regression analysis revealed heritability contributions of 8.25, 14.3, 5.41, 9.02, and 2.16 for the five single-input GWAS of OP, OA, LSS, TL, and LBP, respectively. Pairwise genetic covariances between OA and other traits were -0.0016 (OP), 0.0062 (LSS), -0.0046 (TL), and 0.0047 (LBP) (detailed single-factor genetic parameters in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2; Fig. 2). During model preparation, SEM analysis demonstrated excellent fit for the common factor model between the genetic covariance matrix of the five input GWAS and the empirical covariance matrix (Comparative Fit Index [CFI] = 0.992951246385486; Standardized Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR] = 0.0649947874130541) (model stability assessments in Supplementary Table 3 parameters of the latent factor [F1] and univariate SEM in Supplementary Table 4), providing robust evidence for the shared genetic factor mvSAge.

Hierarchical assessment of genomic SEM GWAS

By extending the SEM framework to incorporate individual variants, we generated an indirectly measured GWAS estimating associations between 3,195,766 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and mvSAge. At a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻12, we identified 514 lead SNPs among 3,195,766 loci; under a more stringent threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻1⁶), 327 lead SNPs were identified (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). These SNPs were entirely novel, distinct from loci reported in the five single-input GWAS used to construct mvSAge, highlighting the enhanced resolution of genomic SEM.

Genomic control evaluation of genomic SEM via LD score regression

After removing 2,449,951 SNPs through parameter-based filtering and retaining regression coefficients, 745,815 SNPs were retained for analysis. The mean chi-square value across all SNPs was 0.169, with a genomic inflation factor Lambda GC = 1.462. Extreme values reached a maximum chi-square of 913.763. Genome-wide significance was observed at 278 loci. Heterogeneity testing passed (P > 0.05). Total observed-scale heritability (h2) was 1.2318 × 10⁻11 (SE = 1.3599 × 10⁻12), with a genetic-to-environmental variance ratio < 0. The regression model intercept was 0.1206 (SE = 0.0036). These estimates collectively demonstrate that latent inflation in the SEM arises from polygenic heritability signals rather than population stratification or pleiotropic biases.

FUMA-based evaluation of mvSAge structural equation model

FUMA analysis identified 735 risk loci (Supplementary Table 7) and annotated 791 lead SNPs, predominantly located in intergenic regions (Supplementary Table 8; Fig. 3B). Gene expression mapping revealed significant upregulation of lead SNP-associated genes in the brain putamen (basal ganglia) and brain frontal cortex BA9 (Fig. 3D and E). Additionally, six potential mvSAge-related genes were identified under genome-wide significance control (significance threshold = 5 × 10⁻⁸, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3C, Table 2). ​​Furthermore​​, we identified ​​136 novel SNP loci​​. Genes proximal to these loci ​​were significantly enriched​​ in the Thyroid hormone signaling pathway, GnRH secretion, and Insulin secretion pathways, and ​​showed significant associations​​ with biological processes including ​​catabolic process​​, ​​dephosphorylation​​, and ​​histone modification​​ (Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables 9, 10). Notably, GWAS subtraction identified 17 loci (e.g., rs12131, rs565896; Table 3) unreported in prior studies, suggesting these loci may act as mediators rather than direct causal factors for mvSAge. Fine-mapping analyses identified strong associations at multiple genomic loci: chromosome 1 (rs411499), chromosome 3 (rs4676473 and rs784517), chromosome 5 (rs11134816), chromosome 7 (rs34813026), chromosome 10 (rs61841153), chromosome 13 (rs376834), chromosome 15 (rs34121578), chromosome 18 (rs8089504), and chromosome 22 (rs2542035). Chromosomal fine-mapping of mvSAge

Transcriptome-wide predictions

Transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) using FUSION identified eight genes surpassing multiple comparison correction thresholds (Table 4, Fig. 7B). Subsequent FOCUS fine-mapping of genomic SEM data implicated 845 genes as potential causal signals (Supplementary Table 12). Intersection analysis prioritized eight high-confidence genes: MRPL55, ARF1, GJC2, CHKA, DHX32, MRPL10, PPP6R3, and SLC33A1. MRPL55, PPP6R3, and CHKA exhibited positive TWAS Z-scores (Z > 0), suggesting their upregulation correlates with increased mvSAge. Conversely, ARF1, GJC2, SLC33A1, DHX32, and MRPL10 showed negative Z-scores (Z < 0), indicating downregulation associates with mvSAge progression (Fig. 7C; TWAS-FOCUS intersection results in Supplementary Table 13).

Pathway, cell type, and Mendelian disease gene enrichment

Multi-marker genomic annotation analysis (MAGMA) mapped two genes (Supplementary Table 14). GLIPR2 showed high expression in lung, small intestine, heart, and brain tissues (Fig. 8A). Protein–protein interaction networks constructed using GeneCards and STRING (experimental score-based) revealed interactions between COLEC10 and COLEC11, MASP2, and MASP1 (Fig. 8B; Supplementary Table 14), while GLIPR2 interacted with BECN1 (Fig. 8C; Supplementary Table 15). These findings were corroborated by GeneMANIA (Fig. 8C; Supplementary Table 16). MendelVar enrichment analysis (Supplementary Table 17) demonstrated significant associations between mvSAge and congenital nervous system abnormalities, microcephaly, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Fig. 8E). GO pathway analysis highlighted enrichment for cell–cell signaling by Wnt, fibronectin binding, Wnt signaling pathway, and cellular response to misfolded protein (Fig. 8F). GO-Slim results implicated mitotic cell cycle, membrane organization, mitotic nuclear division, and vitamin metabolic process (Fig. 8G). HPO testing associated mvSAge with multiple exostoses, metaphyseal dysplasia, and hypochromic anemia (Fig. 8H). Cell-type enrichment analysis identified six cell types surpassing significance after multiple comparison correction (Supplementary Table 18), with the top two being lung epithelial cells, heart endothelial cells, and brain non-myeloid endothelial cells (Fig. 9).

Cell subpopulations significantly associated with mvSAge

Heritability contributions across genomic regions

Partitioned heritability analysis revealed significant enrichment of skeletal aging GWAS signals in evolutionarily conserved regions (Conserved_LindbladToh), DNase I hypersensitivity site (DHS) extended regions (DHS_Trynka.extend.500), chromatin accessible regions (DGF_ENCODE.extend.500), DHS core peaks (DHS_peaks_Trynka), and transcription factor binding sites (TFBS_ENCODE). These regions correspond to evolutionarily constrained regulatory elements, open chromatin-mediated transcriptional hubs, and key nodes in transcription factor networks. Cross-species conserved regions may influence aging via ancient pathways regulating skeletal development and metabolism (e.g., BMP/WNT signaling). DHS/DGF enrichment suggests genetic variants preferentially target dynamically open chromatin hotspots, potentially disrupting enhancer-promoter 3D interactions or altering chromatin compartmentalization, leading to osteoblast differentiation defects or bone matrix imbalance. Strong associations at transcription factor binding sites implicate functional perturbations of osteogenic master regulators (e.g., RUNX2, SP7), whose motif variations may impair transcriptional activation of target genes (COL1A1, ALPL), accelerating bone density loss. Additionally, DHS core peak enrichment underscores the role of skeletal-specific enhancers in age-related epigenetic remodeling via distal gene regulation (Supplementary Table 19).

Chromosome-level results

Our analysis demonstrated that PRS-prioritized variants are strongly associated with disease risk, with significant heterogeneity in genetic contributions across chromosomal regions. Notably, chromosomes 1, 2, and 4 exhibited the highest contributions, likely harboring critical genes and regulatory elements influencing disease susceptibility.

Discussion

Aging, defined as a biological process involving mechanisms such as DNA damage [36–38], epigenetic modifications [39, 40], inflammation [41, 42], and metabolic dysregulation [43, 44], is characterized by systemic multi-organ and multi-system functional decline. With the continuous development of systems biology and genome-wide association studies (GWAS), there is increasing awareness that aging is a systemic disease rather than a degenerative process confined to a single organ or system. The mechanisms by which multiple organs and systems contribute to aging progression have become a major focus of research [45].

In contrast to the limited perception of the musculoskeletal system as merely a "structural system" decades ago, bones are now recognized as the largest endocrine organ in the human body. The interactions between bones and muscles extend beyond mechanical roles to encompass biochemical dimensions [46, 47], playing a central role in systemic aging [48, 49]. A growing body of clinical and experimental evidence demonstrates that exercise, particularly high-intensity exercise [50, 51], significantly reduces aging biomarkers, further underscoring the critical role of the musculoskeletal system in aging. Additionally, the musculoskeletal system exhibits complex crosstalk with multiple systems, organs, and cellular layers during human aging, a phenomenon that has become a key research priority. For instance, the gut-bone axis has been shown to critically influence musculoskeletal diseases by regulating metabolism [52], hormonal balance, and immune responses. Furthermore, the paradigm-shifting concept of the bone-brain axis is increasingly emphasized, with mounting evidence highlighting strong associations between skeletal and neural systems in development and disease manifestations [53]. Understanding the profound impact of multi-organ-system interactions on pathological processes and their shared genetic architecture is crucial for the clinical management and prevention of musculoskeletal aging.

As a research strategy, GWAS has gained prominence in single-phenotype disease studies [54, 55]. Over the past decade, large-scale GWAS have identified numerous genetic variants associated with skeletal traits. Concurrently, the application of methods such as Mendelian randomization (MR) has revealed protective and risk factors for skeletal aging-related diseases, providing valuable evidence for understanding pathogenesis and improving preventive strategies. For example, childhood obesity [56] and elevated serum LDL levels [57] have been established as significant risk factors for osteoarthritis (OA). However, the limitations of single-phenotype data and MR methodologies hinder precise investigation of complex traits such as aging, frailty, and metabolic syndrome.

Genomic Structural Equation Modeling (Genomic-SEM)​​, as an emerging strategy in GWAS research, ​​provides capabilities​​ for modeling and characterizing complex traits ​​that surpass​​ conventional GWAS approaches. This represents a ​​conceptually transformative advance​​ for elucidating fundamental and systemic frameworks underlying human diseases and health. ​​For instance​​, a study by Park et al.​​ [58] leveraged​​ Genomic-SEM to investigate the ​​shared genetic architecture​​ of Metabolic Syndrome (MetS), ​​revealing in depth​​ the connections between MetS and multiple health outcomes ​​through the identification​​ of 11 potential therapeutic target genes. ​​Moreover​​, downstream analyses enabled by Genomic-SEM, coupled with the discovery of ​​key biological targets, pathways, susceptibility genes, and contributing factors​​, ​​offer expanded opportunities​​ for translational medicine. ​​In another application​​, a cross-trait multivariate GWAS study ​​constructed​​ a GWAS model of puberty timing using Genomic-SEM, ​​uncovering​​ genes associated with pubertal onset (KDM4C, LEPR, CCNC, ACP1, and PCSK1) [59]. This ​​enhanced​​ the biological understanding of puberty and ​​improved prospects​​ for developing clinical interventions. ​​Collectively, these applications demonstrate that implementing​​ Genomic-SEM ​​within the musculoskeletal field​​ will ​​significantly advance​​ our understanding of its ​​systemic role​​ in overall human health.

In this study, we integrated genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic SEM), summary-data-based polygenic risk scoring (PRS), MR-IEU, fine-mapping, and transcriptomic analyses to investigate the shared genetic basis of OA, osteoporosis (OP), lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), and low back pain (LBP). Through joint analysis of these complex traits, we identified multiple novel genetic markers. Our findings demonstrate that genetic factors not only determine mvSAge but also exert profound effects on skeletal health through genes such as GLIPR2, as well as cardiac, intestinal, and neural pathways. This study provides new theoretical foundations for understanding how genetic loci shape skeletal aging and offers critical insights for implementing precision medicine and public health interventions.

Discovery of genetic architecture and shared genetic factors

Our Genomic SEM analysis revealed genetic covariances among OA, OP, LSS, and LBP, indicating that these phenotypes share genetic factors, with OA, LSS, and LBP playing pivotal roles. The association between LBP and OA—two of the most common causes of pain [60, 61], functional impairment, and disability—has been previously explored. Our results align with the hypothesis proposed by Amarasinghe et al. [62] that LBP exacerbates knee OA (KOA) pain intensity in clinical patients. Furthermore, the review by Staud et al. [63] corroborates shared pain mechanisms between OA and LBP, including central sensitization and aberrant pain modulation. From a therapeutic perspective, translational research has demonstrated that selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors effectively alleviate chronic pain in both LBP and OA [64], highlighting the clinical utility of targeting shared pain pathways. Our SEM also revealed genetic links between OA and LSS, consistent with large-scale observational studies. A cross-sectional study of 10,234 patients found that individuals receiving OA treatment frequently self-reported LSS symptoms, suggesting that musculoskeletal comorbidities (e.g., OA and LSS) are clinically common [65]. Compared to single-phenotype GWAS data (e.g., LBP and OA), mvSAge provides a more comprehensive measure of skeletal aging, and our SEM further confirms intricate genetic interconnections among OA, OP, LSS, and LBP, implying that these traits are not isolated but rather interwoven through shared genetic mechanisms.

Novel SNPs and their relationship to complex traits

Post-hoc analyses based on Genomic SEM identified 136 novel SNPs, 791 lead SNPs, and 735 risk loci. Notably, these SNPs were not emphasized in prior studies. FUMA analysis revealed that novel SNPs were significantly associated with tissues such as the brain putamen (basal ganglia) and brain frontal cortex BA9. Most of these SNPs reside in gene regulatory regions, suggesting that non-coding regions may play critical roles in genetic mechanisms. As demonstrated by Kellis et al. [66], non-coding variants may influence complex trait formation by modulating gene expression. The enrichment of novel SNPs in brain and other tissues provides potential genetic targets for future research. Additionally, we identified six mvSAge-associated genes (LRRC31, WNT16, GLIPR2, OR1S1, PPP6R3, and COLEC10) under genome-wide significance control. Consistent with our preliminary findings, WNT16 overexpression induces osteoblast senescence in ankylosing spondylitis [67], while TWAS has localized PPP6R3 as a genome-wide significant gene for human bone mineral density [68]. Moreover, a GWAS meta-analysis established a potential association between PPP6R3 and hip bone geometry [69]. Critically, enrichment framework analysis of genes proximal to novel SNP loci revealed significant enrichment of skeletal aging-associated novel loci in the Thyroid hormone signaling pathway, GnRH secretion, and Insulin secretion pathways. These loci were also closely associated with key biological processes, including ​​hormone catabolic process, dephosphorylation​​, and ​​histone modification​​. As explored in prior research​​, ​​thyroid hormone (TH)​​ plays a ​​critical role​​ in vertebrate development and in maintaining the homeostasis of adult tissues, including bone [70]. Studies indicate that a ​​thyroid state within the upper normal reference range​​ is associated with ​​low bone mineral density (BMD)​​ in postmenopausal women. Research by Mazziotti et al. [71] ​​further identified​​ that ​​TSH levels at the lower limit of the normal range​​ correlate with an ​​increased prevalence of vertebral fractures​​ in this population. While these earlier studies demonstrate a ​​clinical observational association between TSH and skeletal health​​, the findings present ​​somewhat contradictory evidence​​. ​​Our research provides more refined genetic evidence​​ to elucidate this relationship.

​​Furthermore​​, the observed ​​significant enrichment for gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pathways underscores the critical influence of reproductive hormones on bone homeostasis [72]​​. ​​Previous in vitrostudies indicate​​ that ​​GnRH exerts direct effects on osteoblast-like cells​​. GnRH expression ​​has been detected​​ in canine osteosarcoma cell lines (COS) and normal canine osteoprogenitor cells [73], with ​​measurable GnRH concentrations​​ confirmed in COS using ​​radioimmunoassay (RIA) [74]​​.

​​Regarding clinical implications​​, GnRH agonists serve as a ​​cornerstone of management​​ for prostate cancer patients; however, the ​​potential impact of this therapeutic strategy on skeletal health warrants attention​​. One clinical study ​​demonstrated a significant association between​​ treatment with the ​​GnRH agonist leuprolide​​ and ​​reduced BMD at lumbar spine and total hip​​ sites in prostate cancer patients [75]. Collectively, these pathways and biological processes outline a ​​complex systemic interplay​​ whereby the organism regulates bone homeostasis and aging ​​through endocrine networks​​. This provides ​​novel genetic evidence​​ elucidating the ​​system-level biological role​​ of bone as both a ​​target site and effector organ​​ within the endocrine system. Furthermore, it ​​offers robust systemic support​​ for the involvement of these loci in skeletal aging progression ​​via regulation​​ of an ​​energy-endocrine-epigenetic network​​, underscoring the ​​inherent multi-system crosstalk dynamics​​ characteristic of skeletal aging. The co-enrichment of novel loci within interconnected pathways suggests these genetic variants may influence ​​communication hubs or nodal points across hormonal axes​​, potentially exerting ​​additive or synergistic effects​​ on the ​​integrated regulation of bone metabolism​​. The discovery of this enrichment pattern across gene targets, pathways, and biological functions provides ​​preliminary evidence​​ for targeting bone aging therapeutically and ​​offers novel avenues​​ for uncovering ​​genetic links​​ underlying skeletal aging traits.

Key genetic loci revealed by genomic stratification and fine-mapping

Stratified analysis and fine-mapping identified 679 critical SNPs located in gene regions associated with mitochondrial function, neurodevelopment, and immune response. For example, rs61841153 (chromosome 10) is proximal to MTPAP, which is essential for synthesizing poly(A) tails of mitochondrial transcripts and critically regulates mitochondrial gene expression and nuclear-mitochondrial interactions [76]. Dysregulation of MTPAP expression has been shown to mediate defects in mitochondrial transcript maturation, driving clinical disease progression [77]. Rs2542035 (chromosome 22) neighbors GRAMD4, which Oliveira et al. [78] demonstrated regulates dendritic cell homeostasis in murine bone marrow, indicating its importance in immune system function. Rs34121578 (chromosome 15) is located near DPP8, a dipeptidyl peptidase considered an ideal therapeutic target for human diseases and validated as a treatment target for multiple myeloma [79]. Through precise localization of these SNPs and their neighboring genes, we gained refined insights into how these genes influence individual health and disease susceptibility by modulating mitochondrial dynamics, immune homeostasis, and other processes. Notably, genetic markers in regions linked to mitochondrial function and neurodevelopment suggest these loci may play key roles in shaping skeletal aging-related phenotypes.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals causal genes

Through FUSION-based transcriptomic analyses and TWAS intersection testing, we further identified potential causal genes associated with these SNPs, including MRPL55, ARF1, GJC2, CHKA, DHX32, MRPL10, PPP6R3, and SLC33A1. Consistent with preliminary findings, PPP6R3 was highlighted as critical for bone mineral density and geometric shaping. SLC33A1, defined as an environment-specific essential gene, has emerged as a promising anticancer target [80], with its mutations or duplications linked to hereditary spastic paraplegia [81]. Notably, ARF1 regulates fatty acid metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis. Enkler et al. [82] demonstrated that the ARF1 pool within lipid droplets modulates fatty acid metabolism and acetyl-CoA transfer to mitochondria, thereby controlling ATP production. Furthermore, ARF1 plays a pivotal role in immune responses; Wang et al. [83] revealed that ARF1-mediated lipid metabolism sustains cancer cell viability, while its ablation enhances antitumor immunity in mice. Collectively, these genes predominantly implicate immune responses and mitochondrial stability, providing novel insights into how immune regulation and mitochondrial homeostasis may orchestrate skeletal aging and disease progression. These genes likely underpin the genetic basis of skeletal aging phenotypes. Notably, our TWAS assessment utilized the comprehensive, multi-tissue GTEx v8 dataset. While this approach provides broad transcriptomic context, it does not constitute a bone tissue-specific evaluation. Emerging methodologies, particularly single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) with its advanced capability for spatial transcriptomic mapping, hold significant promise [84]. Future research employing cutting-edge approaches—including genetic information-based spatial mapping (gsMap) of complex trait-relevant cells—to explore spatially resolved cellular mapping specific to bone aging carries substantial translational value and potential [85].

Cellular and disease annotation insights

MAGMA-based genomic mapping identified two core genes: GLIPR2 and COLEC10. GLIPR2, previously characterized as a negative regulator of autophagy [86], has been implicated as a potential biomarker in lung adenocarcinoma [87]. Intriguingly, GLIPR2 exhibits high expression in lung, small intestine, heart, and brain tissues, suggesting that skeletal aging correlates with multi-tissue interactions and emphasizing the tissue-crosstalk nature of aging progression. Given the critical role of autophagy-lysosomal degradation in aging, pharmacological agents targeting GLIPR2 to induce autophagy hold broad clinical promise and may represent a novel intervention strategy for skeletal aging [88].

MendelVar enrichment analysis revealed significant associations between mvSAge and congenital nervous system abnormalities, microcephaly, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). This evidence further supports the comorbid relationship between neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) and skeletal degeneration, as well as the close interplay between neural and skeletal systems. Recent studies implicate ferroptosis as a key mechanism in ALS and musculoskeletal diseases. ALS patients exhibit elevated transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) and free iron levels alongside high serum ferritin expression [89, 90]. Investigating ferroptosis in neuro-skeletal crosstalk is thus crucial for developing targeted preventive strategies. Consistent with our findings regarding the impact of the energy-endocrine-epigenetic network on skeletal aging, NDDs and metabolic diseases may exert influence on bone homeostasis through shared nodal points and genetic architectures. Notably, the novel loci identified in our study remain largely unexplored within prior research domains of NDDs and metabolic diseases. Future investigations should prioritize deciphering the genetic architecture of multi-system crosstalk relevant to skeletal aging, thereby maximizing the potential to unlock the translational value embedded within these novel loci.

Pathway analysis highlighted significant enrichment for cell–cell signaling by Wnt, fibronectin binding, Wnt signaling pathway, and cellular response to misfolded protein. These findings align with prior research establishing the Wnt pathway as central to skeletal development and homeostasis, with emerging therapeutic strategies targeting Wnt signaling. For example, viridicatol, derived from deep-sea fungi, ameliorates bone loss via Wnt pathway modulation [91], while quercetin-capped selenium nanoparticles promote bone regeneration through Wnt and BMP signaling [92]. Notably, the association between cellular response to misfolded protein and skeletal aging underscores the role of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. The ER is responsible for protein synthesis, folding, and maturation [93], and ER stress leads to accumulation of misfolded proteins [94]. As ER stress critically determines cellular fate, it has become a focal point in aging and disease research [95]. Our results suggest that misfolded proteins significantly contribute to skeletal aging. Furthermore, cell-type enrichment analysis identified six cell types—including lung epithelial cells, heart endothelial cells, and brain non-myeloid endothelial cells—as strongly associated with skeletal aging, reinforcing the importance of inter-tissue crosstalk. Future studies should integrate genetic, multi-cellular, multi-organ, and multi-system interactions to comprehensively unravel skeletal aging mechanisms. Such integrative research will not only enhance our understanding of complex diseases but also inform personalized prevention and intervention strategies.

Genomic elements and risk chromosome identification

Partitioned heritability analysis revealed systemic enrichment of skeletal aging-associated genetic signals in evolutionarily conserved regions (Conserved_LindbladToh), open chromatin hubs (DHS/DGF_ENCODE), and transcription factor binding networks (TFBS_ENCODE). These regions correspond to core regulatory elements under evolutionary constraint, gene expression hubs mediated by open chromatin, and key nodes in transcription factor networks. Cross-species conserved regions likely influence aging by regulating ancient pathways such as BMP/WNT signaling [20, 96], which govern skeletal development and metabolism. The enrichment of DHS hypersensitive sites and chromatin-accessible regions suggests that genetic variants preferentially target dynamically open regulatory hotspots, potentially disrupting 3D enhancer-promoter interactions or altering chromatin compartmentalization, thereby impairing osteoblast differentiation or bone matrix homeostasis [97, 98]. Notably, strong associations at transcription factor binding sites implicate functional perturbations in osteogenic master regulators such as RUNX2 and SP7. Variants in their binding motifs may directly reduce transcriptional activation efficiency of target genes (e.g., COL1A1, ALPL) [99, 100], accelerating age-related bone density loss. Furthermore, the robust enrichment of DHS core peaks pinpoints skeletal-specific enhancers that may regulate distal gene expression during epigenetic remodeling in aging [101].

Chromosome-specific analysis further identified chromosomes 1, 2, and 4 as core carriers of genetic risk networks. These chromosomes not only harbor the aforementioned regulatory elements but also contain key bone homeostasis genes such as BGLAP (osteocalcin) and SOST (sclerostin) [102, 103]. Coordinated variants in these regions may trigger cascading transcriptional dysregulation through epigenetic remodeling mechanisms, such as enhancer hijacking or insulator disruption. More critically, from a clinical translation perspective, the global demographic shift towards an aging population renders the development of intelligent prediction tools for identifying bone aging-related risks an imminent requirement. PRS-derived findings on risk chromosomal loci identified through the mvSAge model can be integrated into clinical risk stratification or preventive screening frameworks for bone aging. Leveraging PRS for risk estimation will enable targeted early-stage interventions against bone aging. Future studies are warranted to determine the clinical utility of such approaches.

Challenges and future directions

While this study provides novel insights into the genetics of skeletal aging, several limitations remain. First, our cohort primarily comprises individuals of European ancestry, lacking validation in other populations. Future studies must expand sample diversity, particularly across ethnic and geographic groups, to ensure the broad applicability of these findings. Second, given the distinctive nature and multifactorial complexity of skeletal aging traits, ensuring adequate model fitness and quality control may preclude the inclusion of all relevant GWAS datasets (e.g., data concerning traits such as handgrip strength). Thus, despite employing multiple methodological approaches including on-overlapping sample sources and Genomic SEM to minimize sample overlap, residual sample-induced bias may still exist and could not be entirely ruled out. Furthermore, although we identified multiple skeletal aging-associated genetic loci through fine-mapping and transcriptomic analyses, and subsequently linked these genes to specific biological pathways, elucidating the system-level role of the energy-endocrine-epigenetic network in skeletal aging remains an outstanding challenge in the field. Subsequent research should elucidate how genetic variants influence skeletal homeostasis through gene expression, neurodevelopment, and metabolic pathways. Clinical cohort strategies leveraging datasets including the UK Biobank and real-world data offer significant translational potential and scientific value for investigating the clinical relevance of these genetic variants [104, 105]. Third, while our work highlights the importance of genetic factors, environmental contributors (e.g., nutritional status, mechanical loading) cannot be overlooked. Investigating gene-environment interactions will be essential to fully understand phenotypic variability and advance personalized interventions.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into the genetic underpinnings of skeletal aging. By integrating Genomic SEM, fine-mapping, and transcriptomic analyses, we identified novel genetic loci and delineated their roles in mediating gene expression and complex trait associations. Our findings deepen the understanding of skeletal aging mechanisms and offer a foundation for precision medicine and public health strategies. Future research will validate these genetic markers and explore gene-environment interplay to enhance healthspan and functional longevity globally.
