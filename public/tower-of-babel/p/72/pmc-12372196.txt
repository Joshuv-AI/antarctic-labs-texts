Repeated chip analysis for reducing the effect of genotyping errors on gene mapping for two recombinant inbred line populations in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)
Authors: Hengyi Zhang, Jiankang Wang, Shuanghe Cao, Yuanfeng Hao, Xianchun Xia, Xiaowan Xu, Lingli Li, Luyan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Genotypic data has been applied in multiple research fields, such as molecular biology, genetics, and breeding. However, due to various reasons, genotyping data inevitably contains a certain percentage of errors, which affects the reliability of gene localization. This study conducted quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping for some yield related and disease resistant traits, using repeated genotyping data of two wheat RIL populations derived from Yangxiaomai × Zhongyou 9507 and Jingshuang 16 × Bainong 64, and non-erroneous data consisting of consistent genotypes between the two replications. Mapping results were compared with reported QTL for the corresponding traits. Then error correction methods implemented in software packages QTL IciMapping (EC), Genotype-Corrector (GC) and R/qtl were applied to these datasets, followed by QTL mapping. Simulation study was performed by randomly adding five levels of genotyping errors to investigate the effect of genotyping errors on QTL mapping and the efficiency of error correction methods. The results indicated that the impact of genotyping errors on QTL mapping was mainly reflected in the decrease in QTL detection power and accuracy of estimation for QTL positions and effects. The impact was increased with the increasing of errors. EC, GC, and R/qtl improved the mapping accuracy, but all of them cannot eliminate the negative effect of errors completely. Considering detection power, false discovery rate, estimation of QTL position and effect, correction accuracy, and running time, EC was better than the other two methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07184-4.

Introduction

Genotyping is the process of classifying individuals based on two or more alleles at locus to determine the linkage combination of genes, Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) sequences, or genetic markers on chromosomes. With the development of new technologies, marker number and density increase rapidly in the past decade, which provides new opportunity to uncover the genetic variation of complex traits [1]. At the same time, more and more researchers have realized that each step and various factors in genotyping process may lead to genotyping errors, for example, characteristics of gene sequence itself, small sample size, sample contamination, taq polymerase errors, poor equipment precision or reliability, human factors, and so on [2, 3].

Construction of high-quality genetic linkage maps is the basis for precisely identifying QTL associated with target traits. But genotyping errors have a negative impact on the construction of genetic linkage maps, as they may mask the true segregation of alleles [3, 4]. Zhang et al. [5] found that the more genotyping errors in a population, the lower accuracy of genetic marker ordering in a genetic map. Because genotyping errors can cause inaccurate estimation of recombination frequency between markers, and concomitantly have a significant negative effect on linkage analysis and subsequent map construction. Cartwright et al. [6] found that every 1% error in markers increases the map distance by 2 cM. Hackett et al. [7] studied the effects of missing values, genotyping errors, and segregation distortion in molecular marker data on the construction of linkage maps. The results showed that errors in the data affected the accuracy of marker ordering.

Quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping refers to the determination of gene position on their related chromosomes or linkage groups, which is a crucial component and fundamental task of genetic research, and plays an important role in breeding [8]. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) between QTL and markers or linear combinations of markers is an important factor affecting the accuracy of QTL mapping. Akey et al. [9] performed theoretical studies and simulation experiments to investigate the effects of genotyping errors on four commonly used parameters in LD studies, i.e., D’, r, Q, and d. Their findings revealed that approximately 3% genotyping error rate could have significant impacts on LD measurements.

Therefore, identification of genotyping errors is a necessary step in genetic analysis. The simplest way to detect genotyping errors is to evaluate them based on the consistency of repeated genotypes. Inconsistent genotypes between repeated genotyping data are considered as incorrect. Software packages GIMLET, ConGenR, etc [10, 11]. were developed based on this method. However, the cost of repeated genotyping is high. For family data, genotyping errors can be identified by examining whether parental and offspring data conform to the Mendelian inheritance, or if double recombination events of closely linked markers happen, using software PREST and LINKPHASE 3 [12, 13].

Some experiments have shown that the testing for Mendelian inheritance can identify errors in single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) data to some extent [14, 15]. However, not all genotyping errors are inconsistent with Mendelian inheritance, and not all genotypes are family data. Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) in genotypes may be caused by genotyping errors [16, 17]. For non-familial data, genotyping errors can be evaluated by comparing whether the genotypes deviate from HWE, such as using software MICRO-CHECKER [18]. In addition to these methods, some researchers have proposed algorithms based on maximum likelihood to estimate the error rate of genotypes and identify genotyping errors, represented by software CERVUS 3.0 and RelioType [19, 20].

This study conducted QTL mapping on repeated genotyping data of two wheat recombinant inbred line (RIL) populations derived from Yangxiaomai × Zhongyou 9507 (YZ) and Jingshuang 16 × Bainong 64 (JB), and non-erroneous data composed of consistent genotypes between the two replicates. Mapping results were compared with reported QTL for the corresponding traits in wheat database. Then three error correction methods were applied to the repeated genotyping data, and QTL mapping were also performed based on corrected genotypes for comparison. The first error correction method is implemented in QTL IciMapping V4.2 (EC) [21] based on the conditional probability of marker type at each locus under given flanking marker types. The second method is implemented in software Genotype-corrector (GC) [22], which is applicable in RIL and F2 populations using an improved sliding window approach. The third method is the error correction program in R/qtl, with the command calc.errorlod [23]. Simulation studies were performed by randomly adding five levels of genotyping errors to demonstrate the impact of genotyping errors and error correction methods.

These findings not only point out the impact of genotyping errors on QTL mapping, but also provide effective instruction and approach to reduce the adverse effects of genotyping errors on the accuracy and reliability of QTL mapping.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials and trait measurement

Two wheat populations were used in this study to investigate the effect of genotyping errors on QTL mapping. The first population was Yangxiaomai × Zhongyou 9507 F6 RILs (YZ) with population size of 193 [24–26], planted in Beijing and Shijiazhuang during 2011 and 2012, Gaoyi during 2018 and 2020, Xinxiang during 2019 and 2021. Five traits were measured, i.e., grain number per spike (GNS), thousand grain weight (TGW), grain length (GL), grain width (GW), and pre-harvest sprouting (PHS). The second population was Jingshuang 16 × Bainong 64 F6 RILs (JB) with population size of 181 [27], planted in Beijing, Gaoyi and Shijiazhuang from 2019 to 2020. Plant height (PH) was measured. Heritability and BLUE (best linear unbiased estimation) values of the traits was calculated across environments by the AOV functionality in software package QTL IciMapping V4.2 [20]. Genotyping by 15 K SNP array

Each population was genotyped twice at the same time with the 15 K wheat Affymetrix SNP array at China GoldenMarker (Beijing) Biotech Co., Ltd. (http://www.cgmb.com.cn/). Heterozygous and non-polymorphic markers in parents and non-polymorphic markers in progenies were filtered, remaining 4273 and 4497 SNP markers in the YZ and JB populations, each with two replications of genotyping. Inconsistent data points between the two replications were regarded as genotyping errors, and replaced by missing values, and therefore the non-erroneous data was generated. In other words, the non-erroneous data consisted of consistent genotype calls between replicates. Missing rate of the YZ population was 1.18%, and the estimated error rate was 0.35%; missing rate of the JB population was 1.42%, and the estimated error rate was 8.47%.

QTL mapping in the two RIL populations

QTL mapping in this study was performed for the multi-environmental phenotypes and BLUE values of the traits in the two RIL populations using the inclusive composite interval mapping (ICIM) method [28] implemented in the BIP functionality of QTL IciMapping V4.2. Missing values were imputed by ICIM before QTL mapping. The scanning step size was set at 1 cM, and the LOD threshold was set at 2.5. Probability of marker variables entering the model was set at 0.001. QTL mapping was performed on the twice genotyping data and non-erroneous data of the two populations, respectively. The linkage map used for QTL mapping was constructed using the non-erroneous data and reported by Wang et al. [29]. Detected QTL were compared with those that have been reported for the corresponding traits listed in the wheat database (http://wheatqtldb.net/). If the distance of physical locations between the closest left or right markers of detected QTL in this study and reported QTL was smaller than 10 Mb, the two QTL were considered to be overlapped. QTL detected in multiple environments were considered as environment-specific instances. By this way, consistently detected QTL in different environments can be reflected in the mapping results.

Three commonly used methods were adopted for error correction of the two RIL populations, i.e., error correction method implemented in software package QTL IciMapping V4.2 (EC), methods in Genotyping-corrector (GC) [21], and R package R/qtl [30]. No parameters need to be set during the application of EC. For GC, error rate was predefined based on the actual information of the populations, i.e., 0.35% and 8.47%, and the length of sliding window was set by default at 11. For R/qtl, error rate was set as 0.35% and 8.47% for the two populations. Then QTL mapping was performed using the corrected genotypes. QTL detected from different genotyping datasets or methods were regarded as overlapped, if their distance was smaller than 10 cM.

Simulation study

Two simulation models were considered to investigate the effects of different error rates on QTL mapping. In Model I, population size, linkage map, heritability of the five traits, QTL number, locations and effects were consistent with those from the YZ population based on the non-erroneous genotypes and BLUE values of traits. One thousand RIL populations were generated by QTL IciMapping V4.2, each with population size of 193 and marker number of 4273. In Model II, chromosome, and marker information was the same as the immortalized F2 (IF2) maize population derived from Zong 3 × 87 − 1 [31, 32]. QTL information was consistent with those affecting ear length (EL) [31], which is shown in Table S1. Error variance was set at 2.00. One thousand F2 populations were simulated by genetic breeding simulation platform ISB based on Blib [33, 34], each with population size of 166 and marker number of 3184. Fortran code was written to randomly select 0.5%, 1%, 3%, 5%, and 8.47% of the genotypes in each population, which were then replaced by errors. Percentages of 0–5% were the common range of error rate in actual populations, and 8.47% was the error rate of the JB population. In RIL population, original genotypes A and B were replaced by each other. In F2 population, original genotype A had a probability of 1/3 to be replaced by B, 2/3 to be replaced by H; B had a probability of 1/3 to be replaced by A, 2/3 to be replaced by H; H had equal probability to be replaced by A and B. Genotypes after the replacement were errors.

The three methods of error correction were also applied for the simulated populations, and the detected errors were replaced by missing values. No parameters need to be set during the application of EC. For GC, error rate was predefined based on the simulated error rate, and the length of sliding window was set by default at 11. For R/qtl, error rate was set as the simulated error rate. True positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative rates of error correction were calculated for each method. True positive rate was defined as the proportion of true errors to the number of total errors identified by the error correction algorithm. False positive rate was the proportion of false errors to the number of total errors identified by the error correction algorithm. True negative rate was the proportion of correct genotypes that were also considered as correct by the error correction algorithm to the total number of correct genotypes reported by the algorithm in the population. False negative rate is the proportion of incorrect genotypes that were considered as correct by the error correction algorithm to the total number of correct genotypes reported by the algorithm in the population.

QTL mapping was performed on the simulated populations with and without error correction by the ICIM algorithm. Length of the support interval was set at 20 cM. Detected peaks out of the support interval were considered to be false positives. When more than one peak occurred within the same interval, only the one with the highest LOD score was counted. Power of each predefined QTL was the ratio of true positives to 1000 simulation runs [35]. False discovery rate (FDR) was defined as the proportion of false positives to the total number of true and false positives [36].

Results

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) in phenotype

From ANOVA in the YZ population, the broad-sense heritability of grain number per spike (GNS), thousand grain weight (TGW), grain length (GL), grain width (GW), and pre-harvest sprouting (PHS) was 0.56, 0.74, 0.85, 0.69, and 0.60 on the plot level, and 0.83, 0.92, 0.96, 0.90, and 0.85 on the mean level. GL had the highest heritability in the five traits, and GNS had the lowest heritability. For all traits, proportion of environmental variance was lower than that of genotypic variance. Proportion of environmental variance was the highest for GL, and proportion of genotypic variance was the highest for TGW (Table S2). There was no replication in each environment of the YZ population, so the genotype-by-environment interaction variance cannot be estimated. In the JB population, the heritability of plant height (PH) was 0.77 on the plot level and 0.94 on the mean level. For PH, proportions of genotypic variance, environmental variance and genotype-by-environment interaction were 71.14%, 7.53% and 0.23% (Table S2).

Detected QTL in the two wheat populations

The likelihood of odds (LOD) profiles for different traits in the two wheat RIL populations were shown in Figs. S1 and S2. In the first genotyping of the YZ population, a total of 120 QTL were detected, 25 for GNS, 22 for TGW, 33 for GL, 16 for GW, and 24 for PHS. Among them, 66 QTL were reported in the wheat database (http://wheatqtldb.net/), 10 for GNS, 18 for TGW, 22 for GL, 8 for GW, and 8 for PHS (Table 1). In the second genotyping of the YZ population, a total of 103 QTL were detected, 19 for GNS, 17 for TGW, 32 for GL, 14 for GW, and 21 for PHS. Among them, 61 QTL were reported in the database, 8 for GNS, 17 for TGW, 22 for GL, 6 for GW, and 8 for PHS. In the non-erroneous data of the YZ population, a total of 125 QTL were detected, 18 for GNS, 25 for TGW, 36 for GL, 25 for GW, and 21 for PHS. Among them, 88 QTL were reported in the database, 9 for GNS, 24 for TGW, 30 for GL, 17 for GW, and 8 for PHS. Detected QTL from the non-erroneous data using BLUE values were shown in Table S3. The overlapping rate of QTL detected in the twice genotyping and the non-erroneous data with the reported QTL was 55.00%, 59.22% and 70.40%, respectively (Table 1). QTL mapped from the non-erroneous data were more accurate than those form the twice genotyping data. The number of consistent QTL in the twice genotyping and non-erroneous data was 8, 10, 15, 5, and 10 for GNS, TGW, GL, GW, and PHS, respectively (Figs. S3A-E).

Detected QTL in the two wheat populations after error correction

Three error correction methods were applied to the two wheat RIL populations, and then QTL mapping was conducted on the corrected datasets. In the first genotyping of the YZ population, after EC correction, 14, 17, 23, 15, and 19 QTL were detected for GNS, TGW, GL, GW, and PHS, 6, 14, 19, 7, and 7 of which were reported in the wheat database. By GC, 14, 11, 15, 10, and 8 QTL were detected for GNS, TGW, GL, GW, and PHS, 6, 5, 5, 3, and 0 of which were reported. By R/qtl, 19, 17, 33, 10, and 17 QTL were detected for GNS, TGW, GL, GW, and PHS, 7, 16, 16, 6, and 6 of which were reported (Table S4). The number of QTL were the largest in the data from R/qtl, and the smallest in the data from GC. The overlapping rate with the reported QTL were the highest in the data from EC, and the lowest in the data from GC. Similar results were observed in the second genotyping. The number of consistent QTL from the three correction methods and non-erroneous data was 4 and 2 for GNS in the twice genotyping, 1 and 4 for TGW, 2 and 2 for GL, 4 and 2 for GW, and 0 and 1 for PHS, respectively (Figs. S4A-J).

In the first genotyping of the JB population, after EC correction, 8 QTL were detected for PH, 4 of which were reported in the wheat database. By GC, 9 QTL were detected for PH, 4 of which were reported. By R/qtl, 11 QTL were detected for PH, 4 of which were reported. The overlapping rate with the reported QTL was the highest in the data from EC, followed by that from GC and R/qtl. In the second genotyping, the overlapping rate with the reported QTL was the highest in the data from GC, followed by that from R/qtl and EC. The number of consistent QTL from the three correction and non-erroneous data was 5 and 4 for PH in the twice genotyping (Figs. S4K-L). Generally speaking, EC achieved better QTL mapping results, compared with GC and R/qtl, considering the two mapping populations comprehensively.

In model II, power of qEL-5.2 was 81.20%, 81.10%, 79.00%, 76.50%, 75.30% and 71.50%, and FDR of EL was 31.01%, 31.91%, 34.06%, 40.85%, 45.97%, and 52.77%, respectively (Fig. 1C).

Mapping results in simulated populations after error correction

Detection powers and FDR of QTL mapping after error correction were shown in Figs. 2 and 3 for model I and Fig. 4 for model II. Most QTL had a significant improvement in detection power after error correction by all the three methods. In model I, the power from R/qtl after error correction was higher than that of EC and GC, but still lower than that from populations without errors (Fig. 2). Error correction also reduced FDR of all traits significantly. FDR from R/qtl was lower than that of EC and GC, but still higher than that from populations without errors (Fig. 3). In model II, QTL detection power and FDR were also improved by each error correction method. Results from EC was better than those from R/qtl, followed by GC, but still worse than those from populations without errors (Fig. 4).

The estimated locations and effects of predefined QTL were given in Tables S7-S9 for EC, GC and R/qtl in model I, and Tables S10-S12 for the three methods in model II. In both models, each correction method cannot achieve more accurate estimation of QTL locations and effects than those from non-erroneous datasets (Tables S5 and S6). In model I, EC and R/qtl improved the estimation of QTL locations and effects under all levels of errors. R/qtl had smaller deviation and standard error of estimates in both QTL positions and additive effects than those by EC and no-correction, followed by GC. For example, under the error rate of 3%, the average deviation in position across all QTL was 0.60, 0.59, 0.63, and 0.44 cM from no correction, EC, GC, and R/qtl. The corresponding standard error was 1.55, 1.47, 1.68, and 1.38. In model II, EC improved the estimation of QTL locations, additive and dominant effects under all levels of errors, but results from GC and R/qtl were a little worse than those from no correction and EC. For example, under the error rate of 8.47%, the average deviation in dominant effect across all QTL was 0.066, 0.061, 0.077, and 0.068 cM from no correction, EC, GC, and R/qtl.

Accuracy of error correction by EC, GC and r/qtl methods

True positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative of error correction under different levels of genotyping errors were shown in Fig. S5 for model I, and Fig. S6 for model II. True positive and true negative rates were reduced with the increasing of errors, no matter which error correction was applied. Similarly, false positive and false negative rates were increased. In model I, R/qtl achieved lower false positive and false negative rates, followed by EC and GC. For example, under the error level of 0.50%, the false positive was 6.29%, 10.88%, and 3.36% by EC, GC and R/qtl, and the corresponding false negative was 0.03%, 0.19%, and 0.02%. Similarly, R/qtl also achieved higher true positive and true negative rates, followed by EC and GC (Fig. S5). In model II, EC achieved lower false positive and false negative rates, followed by R/qtl and GC. For example, under the error level of 2%, the false positive was 6.95%, 14.28%, and 10.27% by EC, GC and R/qtl, and the corresponding false negative was 0.07%, 2.91%, and 0.32%. Similarly, EC also achieved higher true positive and true negative rates, followed by R/qtl and GC (Fig. S6).

Comparison of running time among different error correction methods

Running time of different correction methods was given in Table 2 for models I and II averaged from 1000 simulated runs, using a ThinkPad X1 laptop. With the increasing of error rate, the running time was also increased, especially by GC and R/qtl. Owing to the larger population size and marker number in model I, error correction took longer time in model I compared with model II. EC was the fastest among the three methods. Discussion

Analysis of QTL mapping results in two wheat RIL populations

The non-erroneous method detects genotyping errors by comparing genotypes among repeated genotyping data, and replaces the errors by missing values. The error rate is reduced, which will facilitate the detection of QTL. As shown in Table 1, in most cases, the non-erroneous datasets based on repeated genotyping detected more reported QTL compared with the twice genotyping, reflecting its higher mapping accuracy. This conclusion was consistent with previous studies [10, 11]. However, the missing rate is increased by the non-erroneous method, which is adverse for QTL mapping. Most of the QTL detected by Li et al. [24] for the five traits of GNS, TGW, GL, GW, and PHS in the YZ population were re-located in this study. The consistent QTL include the QTL where Li et al. [24, 25] discovered the gene TaGL3-5 A associated with grain size and the gene WAPO-A1 which acted as an important regulator of spikelet number per spike [24, 37]– [38]. In the JB population, based on the physical location of markers, Rht-D1b, one of the major semi-dwarfing genes in wheat, was mapped to the qPH-4D genetic interval which was consistently detected from the first genotyping, second genotyping or non-erroneous data, no matter error correction was applied [39, 40].

Impact of genotyping errors on QTL mapping

From the QTL mapping results of the two RIL populations and simulated experiments, it was concluded that the impact of genotyping errors on QTL mapping was mainly reflected in the decrease in QTL detection power and accuracy of estimation for QTL positions and effects. And the impact was increased with the increasing of errors. Generally speaking, the impact was more serious for QTL with minor effects. For example, in model I, when the missing rate increased from 0 to 0.5%, power of qGL-7 A.1 decreased from 52.00 to 12.60%, whose PVE was 3.50%, while power of qGL-7 A.2 decreased from 100.00 to 99.90%, whose PVE (phenotypic variance explained) was 11.85% (Fig. 1, Table S3). QTL with large effect is more likely to be consistently detected no matter genotyping errors appear or not. In other words, QTL which can be consistently detected among repeated genotyping has higher probability to be a true QTL. For example, qTGW-5 A related to gene TaGL3-5 A and qGNS-6B related to gene WAPO-A1were repeatedly detected in the twice genotyping and the non- erroneous data of the YZ population. Detection power of the two QTL in simulation was higher than 75%, even when the error rate was 8.47%. And QTL which can only be detected under high error rate has higher probability to be a false QTL.

The effectiveness of error correction algorithms

From comparison among the three error correction algorithms by simulation experiment, R/qtl outperformed EC in RIL populations in terms of higher detection power, lower FDR, more accurate estimation of QTL positions and effects, and higher true positive and true negative rates, while EC outperformed R/qtl in F2 populations. The GC method was the worst in both populations (Figs. 2, 3 and 4, S3, and S4, Tables S7-S12). Considering the running speed, EC was much faster than GC and R/qtl. Overall, EC achieves relatively accurate mapping results in the shortest time, which is recommended among the three methods. The results of comparison among the three methods were summarized in Table S13. However, no matter which correction method was applied, the mapping accuracy was still worse than that from the data without any errors (Tables S5 and S6). With the increasing of errors, the correction effect will be reduced (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). When encountering datasets with extremely poor quality, the effect of error correction will be very limited. Although a series of error correction methods have been proposed, the quality control during generating genotypes is still quite important.

There are some limitations in the current error correction software packages. For example, some correction algorithms require the inputting of error rate in the datasets before running, which is hard for the users, like GC and R/qtl. Studies for comparison of correction accuracy among the methods are limited. Some researchers are developing new error correction software and introducing new algorithms, such as Markov chains [41, 42], Monte Carlo random algorithms [43], Bayesian theorem [44], machine learning algorithms [45], and so on. We will study the effect of these new algorithms on QTL mapping in the future. Integration with high-throughput genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) or pangenome-based genotyping may also reduce the impact of genotyping errors on genetic studies.

The impact of genotyping errors in natural populations

This study explored the impact of genotyping errors on QTL mapping using RIL and F2 populations as examples. The three algorithms of error correction can only be applicable for genetic populations with controlled crosses. Natural population is widely used in genetic studies. Genotyping errors will also reduce the mapping accuracy of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and genomic selection (GS) in natural populations [46]. However, there is still few algorithms of error correction for natural populations. We are developing an algorithm of error correction based on linkage disequilibrium between markers. The impact of the new method on GWAS and GS will be investigated by simulation in the near future.

Conclusion

Genotyping errors can significantly affect the accuracy of gene mapping. With the increasing of error rate, mapping accuracy was decreased. Non-erroneous method based on repeated genotyping improved the mapping results. Three error correction methods, EC, GC, and R/qtl, were compared, and all the three methods significantly increased the accuracy of QTL mapping. Extensive simulation experiment was conducted to compare the effect of different correction methods. EC was more efficient than GC and R/qtl, by considering detection power, FDR, estimation of QTL position and effect, correction accuracy, and running time. We suggest the researchers conduct error correction before QTL mapping using the non-erroneous or EC methods, which will improve the reliability of mapping results.
