Corticosteroids for hospitalized patients with severe/critical COVID-19: a retrospective study in Chongqing, China
Authors: Rongjuan Zhuang, Hongli Xia, Li Xu, Zhiqiang Liu, Kaican Zong, Hailang Peng, Bin Liu, Huizi Wu, Lan Huang, Hongwei Yang, Chun Luo, Yuting Yin, Shuliang Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corticosteroids have always been recommended for severe cases of COVID-19. However, the efficacy of treatment with corticosteroids for COVID-19 during the SARS-CoV-2 omicron outbreak in China has not been reported. Clinical data from 406 patients hospitalized for severe/critical COVID-19 from December 2022 to January 2023 at six hospitals in Chongqing were retrospectively analyzed. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 28 days in the groups with and without corticosteroids treatment after propensity score matching (PSM). Secondary outcomes were to compare in-hospital mortality and length of survival time with corticosteroids and those without corticosteroids. This study included 406 patients with severe or critical COVID-19, divided into the corticosteroids group (231, 56.9%) and non-corticosteroids group (175, 43.1%). After PSM, the use of corticosteroids did not reduce all-cause mortality at 28 days (42.5% vs. 39.1%). Univariate analysis showed that corticosteroids were not associated with improved all-cause mortality at 28 days [hazard ratio (HR), 1.019; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.639–1.623; p = 0.938]. Multivariate analysis showed similar results (HR, 1.047; 95% CI, 0.633–1.732; p = 0.858). Among non-survivors, the survival time was significantly larger in those who received corticosteroids compared with the non-corticosteroid users [median 13 (IQR 6.5–15.5) vs. 6 (4-11.25), p = 0.007]. The use of systemic corticosteroids in severe/critical COVID-19 may provide certain potential survival benefits but does not improve prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75926-9.

Introduction

Since November 2021, Omicron (Pango lineage B.1.1.529), the current WHO-designated variant of concern (VOCs) of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has caused a worldwide pandemic of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)1. Omicron is more infectious than ancestors of SARS-CoV-2 VOCs (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta) and could evade immunity established by previous vaccinations or infections2. Because “dynamic zero-case policy” were adopted to prevent large outbreaks, only a small portion of the people in the Chinese mainland were exposed to SARS-CoV-2 before December 2022, leading to the unsuccessful development of herd immunity. After removing the control policy, there was a sharp increase in severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infections in China, bringing new challenges to the public health system. In particular, the majority of the Chinese population experienced first-time infections within a few months. Given the rapid transmission of Omicron infection, a gradual increase of severe/critical cases reported from hospitalized symptomatic COVID-19 patients. In China, there have been over 99 million confirmed cases and over 121,000 deaths reported3.

Corticosteroids were identified as the first drug able to reduce mortality in severe or critically ill patients with COVID-194,5. Various randomized controlled studies and meta-analysis have shown the benefits of using steroids in hospitalized COVID-19 patients6–8. Corticosteroids could enter the cytoplasm and act on the nuclear receptors which lead to the synthesis of specific mRNA and protein. It downregulates the hyperactivation of the components of both innate (neutrophils) and acquired (T and B lymphocytes) immune system and the cytokine storm that characterizes severe cases of COVID-199. It was widely used in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, while its efficacy and optimal dose remain controversial10–12. Several observational studies suggested that corticosteroid treatment may increase mortality to some extent13,14. In China, corticosteroids have always been recommended for severe cases of COVID-1915–17. However, the efficacy of treatment with corticosteroids for severe/critical COVID-19 during the SARS-CoV-2 omicron outbreak in China has not been reported. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective analysis in Chongqing City, China to estimate the association between the administration of corticosteroids and all-cause mortality of hospitalized patients with severe/critical COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Setting

We conducted this study at six tertiary hospitals in the area of Chongqing, China. Clinical records from all consecutive adult subjects admitted with SARS-CoV-2 infection from 1 November 2022 to 20 January 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. All patients were diagnosed by positive SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR assay in nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swabs. Data were manually extracted from the electronic medical and hospital radiology records.

From 22 March 2022, all patients admitted to the hospital with severe pneumonia were treated according to the ninth edition of the COVID-19 diagnosis and treatment protocol15. This protocol recommended that in severe and critical cases with progressive deterioration of oxygenation parameters, rapid imaging progress, and over-activation of the inflammatory response, corticosteroids should be used for a short course (no more than 10 days) as appropriate, with dexamethasone 5 mg/day or methylprednisolone 40 mg/day recommended15. No other interventions were introduced in the clinical management of patients during the study period.

Study design

In this multiple-center retrospective study, we compared patients who were treated with corticosteroids with patients who were not treated with corticosteroids. According to their conditions, patients received appropriate antibiotic/antiviral agents as well as standard care measures (supplemental oxygen, invasive or non-invasive mechanical ventilation and other organ function support, at the discretion of the responsible clinical team).

Definitions

Confirmed COVID-19 was defined as the presence of clinical manifestations of COVID-19 and one or more of the following etiology or serological findings: (1) positive RT-PCR SARS-CoV-2 in nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swabs; (2) positive antigen test for SARS-CoV-2; (3) positive cultured for SARS-CoV-2; (4) four times or more elevated levels of SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG antibodies in the recovery period than in the acute period. COVID-19 is divided into four clinical classifications as following mild, moderate, severe and critical according to its severity15,16.

Severe COVID-19 was defined as any criteria of the following is met and cannot be explained by a cause other than SARS-CoV-2 infection: (1) shortness of breath, RR ≥ 30 times/min; (2) resting oxygen saturation ≤ 93%; (3) partial pressure of arterial blood oxygen (PaO2)/ fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ≤ 300 mmHg; (4) chest imaging showing obvious lesion progression > 50% within 24–48 h15,16.

Critical COVID-19 was defined as any criteria of the following: (1) respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation; (2) shock; (3) other organ failure requiring intensive care unit (ICU) monitoring15,16.

Pulmonary inflammation index (PII) was used to quantify lung CT lesions. It has been proved that the PII can be used as an independent indicator for the disease progression of COVID-1918. A modified semi-quantitative scoring system based on commercial Lung Quantitative Software (Siemens) was used to quantitatively estimate the pulmonary involvement of all patients according to the distribution and size of the lesions. The lesion distribution score was based on the segment of the lung where the lesion was located. There were 20 segments in total for the left and right lobes, 1 point for each segment of lung, and 20 points for the left and right lungs. The lesion size score was based on whether the lesion occupied > 50% of the lung segment. The score was 1 for ≥ 50% involvement and zero for < 50%, with a maximum score of 20. The PII scores were distributed between zero and 100%. A larger value indicated a more severe inflammatory load. PII = lesion distribution score + lesion size score/total score (40 points) x 100%18.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were that all adult patients were admitted to the hospital with confirmed severe/critical COVID-19. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients without a positive etiology or serological findings of SARS-CoV-2 ; (2) Patients who received methylprednisolone at lower doses than 0.5 mg/kg/d or any corticosteroid at equivalent dose19; (3) patients who received corticosteroids less than 3 days; (4) patient died within 72 h of hospitalization; (5) patients whose clinical classification for COVID-19 was mild or moderate; (6) patient whose clinical data were incomplete.

Variables and treatment groups

The following variables were recorded for each patient: age, gender, days from the onset to admission, comorbidities and current diagnoses, clinical classification, laboratory and chest CT findings, treatment administered, Invasive/non-invasive ventilator ventilation, ICU admission and outcomes. For patients with less than 28 days of hospitalization, we telephoned to obtain the follow-up data of the time of death. The study population was categorized into the corticosteroids and non-corticosteroids groups according to corticosteroids treatment status after oxygen therapy started.

Outcome

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 28 days in the groups with and without corticosteroids treatment after propensity score matching (PSM). Secondary outcomes were to compare inhospital mortality and length of survival time between patients with and without corticosteroids treatment. All-cause mortality at 28 days in the subgroups (grouped according to the ventilator usage) with and without corticosteroids treatment after PSM were also been analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were presented as percentages in the case of categorical variables and as median with interquartile range (IQR) in the case of continuous variables. The baseline characteristics of corticosteroids and non-corticosteroids groups were evaluated by Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables. All-cause mortality at 28 days was calculated from the day of oxygen therapy initiation. The propensity score was used to account for baseline differences in the probability of receiving or not corticosteroids treatment based on observed covariates.

Confounders, including gender, age(years old), comorbidities (obesity, mycobacteria infection, diabetes, chronic pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, chronic renal disease, chronic liver disease and human immunodeficiency virus), current smoking, long-term inhaled ICS, days from the onset to admission, clinical classification (mild, moderate, severe, critical)15,16, PII, partial pressure of arterial blood oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaFi) ratio, laboratory tests (neutrophil count, lymphocytes count, platelets count, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, D-dimer), ventilator ventilation and drug usage were taken into PSM. We used a 1:1 matching algorithm between the corticosteroids group and the non-corticosteroids group within a calliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Values of p were determined for all baseline variables before and after matching to evaluate the imbalance. Kaplan–Meier survival curve and log-rank test were used to assess the relationship between corticosteroids use and 28-day death. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated by using a Cox regression model, which examined the relationship between corticosteroids use and the risk of all-cause mortality at 28 days.

Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS version 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All analyses were carried out as two-tailed tests. A p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Population characteristics

Clinical records of 1320 patients admitted to six hospitals with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection were reviewed. 406 cases fulfilled all the inclusion and none of the exclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Of them, 231 patients with COVID-19 underwent corticosteroids treatment, which was defined as the corticosteroids group. Moreover, 175 patients did not undergo corticosteroids treatment, which was defined as the non-corticosteroids group.

Details in corticosteroid use

231(56.9%) patients receive corticosteroids treatment during hospitalization. Of these, 17(7.4%) patients received methylprednisolone plus dexamethasone, 186(80.5%) patients received methylprednisolones alone and 28(12.1%) patients received dexamethasones alone. The doses of all-type corticosteroids were uniformly converted into methylprednisolone and the median daily dose of corticosteroids was 40 (IQR 40–75) mg. The duration of treatment with corticosteroid was 6 (IQR 4–8) days (Table 2).

Primary outcomes

The all-cause mortality at 28 days was 43.3% in the corticosteroids group and 30.9% in the non-corticosteroids group (Table 3). Univariate analysis showed that the 28-day mortality in the corticosteroids group was significantly higher than that in the non-corticosteroids group (HR, 0.706; 95% CI, 0.507–0.983; p = 0.039). When multivariate analysis was further used to control confounders, the results showed that corticosteroids were not associated with improved 28-day mortality in the overall population (HR, 1.013; 95% CI, 0.713–1.492; p = 0.872) (Table 3). Probability scores were next calculated. The 87 patients who received corticosteroids were matched to 87 patients who did not receive corticosteroids by PSM. The imbalance between the two groups was significantly reduced after PSM (Table 1). In the matched cohort, the 28-day mortality was 42.5% in the corticosteroids group and 39.1% in the non-corticosteroids group. Univariate analysis did not reveal a significant difference in the 28-day mortality between the two groups (HR, 1.019; 95% CI, 0.639–1.623; p = 0.938). Similar results were obtained when multivariate analysis was further used to control confounders (HR, 1.047; 95% CI, 0.633–1.732; p = 0.858) (Table 3). Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes are shown in Tables 4 and 5. After PSM, in-hospital mortality (32.2% vs. 34.5%; p = 0.748) did not differ between patients treated with corticosteroids and untreated patients. No differences were found in median survival time at 28 days between the two groups. However, among non-survivors, the survival time was significantly larger in those who received corticosteroids compared with the non-corticosteroid users (median 13 [IQR 6.5–15.5] vs. 6 [4-11.25], p = 0.007) (Table 4). Of the 174 patients in the matched cohort, 76 did not receive mechanical ventilation but received nasal catheter oxygen inhalation, 34 received invasive mechanical ventilation and 64 received non-invasive mechanical ventilation. After PSM, the results showed that corticosteroids were not associated with improved 28-day mortality in patients with or without mechanical ventilation (All p>0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the effect of corticosteroids on the outcome of patients with severe/critical COVID-19 in China in the autumn/winter 2022 Omicron wave. In this study, most of the hospitalized patients with severe/critical COVID-19 in Chongqing were observed elderly, male gender and comorbidities. Older age, male gender, comorbidities, critically ill cases and longer days from the onset to admission were significantly more common in the corticosteroids group. The in-hospital mortality of hospitalized patients with severe/critical COVID-19 was 32.8%, and the 28-day mortality was 37.9%. Corticosteroids treatment did not reduce in-hospital and 28-day mortality of hospitalized patients with severe/critical COVID-19. Subgroup analysis yielded the same conclusion. However, a larger length of survival was observed with corticosteroids among non-survivors.

At the very beginning of the Omicron outbreak in Chongqing City, measures like suspension of public events, increasing lockdown areas and traffic restrictions were promptly taken by the government. Nevertheless, SARS-CoV-2 infection cases surged in a short time. In less than half a month, more than 8,000 new COVID-19 cases were reported in Chongqing, and the number was reported as more than 59,000 ten days later. According to the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, this wave of epidemic in China peaked in late December 2022 and then declined. By late January, the overall epidemic had dropped to a low level. From September 26, 2022, to January 23, 2023, a total of 18,906 indigenous cases of novel coronavirus genome were submitted nationwide, all of which were Omicron variants, 69 evolutionary branches, the main prevalent strains were BA.5.2 (70.8%) and BF.7 (23.4%)20.

Although Omicron’s symptoms are relatively mild compared with Delta’s symptoms21, in this study, the in-hospital mortality of hospitalized severe/critical patients was 32.8%, and the 28-day mortality rate was 37.9% which was relatively higher. The reason may be that most study patients were observed as elderly, male and comorbidities. The elderly have poorer basal lung function and immunity than younger people, resulting in an equivalent viral load that will cause far more damage in this group than in younger people. In England, a large cohort study including 17.2 million adults indicated that males, greater age, deprivation and various other medical conditions were associated with COVID-19-related death22. The high death rates observed in study patients might also be partly attributed to the older population remaining largely unvaccinated and being first-time-infection. A study during the 2022 Hong Kong Omicron COVID-19 outbreak indicated that the risk for COVID–19–associated death among unvaccinated persons was higher than among recipients of 2–3 doses in the elderly (aged ≥ 60)23. Previous studies have also found that even if the vaccine is protective, it is still less effective in the elderly than in the young24, possibly because of poor basic lung function in the elderly24.

In the corticosteroids group, a worse baseline respiratory situation was observed. Older age, male gender, comorbidities, critically ill cases and longer days from the onset to admission were significantly more common. This could be the reason why the length of hospital stay was longer in the corticosteroids group. Corticosteroids exposure increased with the severity of COVID-19 pneumonia. After adjusting by baseline characteristics, corticosteroids treatment was not associated with lower mortality than non-corticosteroids treatment.

Corticosteroids have broad, non-specific anti-inflammatory effects, which may affect mRNA transcription, thereby reducing the production of inflammatory mediators25. However, the immunosuppressed state of the respiratory epithelium may lead to delayed clearance of the virus, secondary infections and deterioration of disease. This is why the WHO recommends against the routine use of corticosteroids in the treatment of patients with severe COVID-19 in 202026.

Some studies showed that corticosteroids use was not associated with reduced mortality in patients with COVID-1927,28 and even led to an increased risk of death29,30. Evidence for a low dose of methylprednisolone has been limited to date. A large, double-blind, randomized controlled trial allocated (1:1 ratio)patients randomly to receive either intravenous methylprednisolone (0.5 mg/kg) or placebo (saline solution)31. And the study suggested that a short course of methylprednisolone in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 did not reduce mortality in the overall population which was consistent with our study. A similar conclusion was also obtained in a retrospective study of prospectively collected data, which included 1835 patients. The study analyzed the prognosis of patients with mechanically ventilated COVID-19-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) treated with short courses of corticosteroids. Their results also suggested that corticosteroid treatment may present a biphasic, time-dependent effect on survival, with an initial protective effect in the first two weeks of critical illness, but long-term adverse effects may occur after this period32. In our study, we similarly observed a larger length of survival among non-survivors with corticosteroids but mortality was not reduced in all patients. Perhaps the use of corticosteroids therapy before the onset of disease progression or early in the severe stages may reduce mortality rates.

In 2020, dexamethasone was the first drug to be identified which was able to reduce mortality in severe or critically ill patients with COVID-19 who received oxygen therapy4. Subsequent research found that methylprednisolone could be used for treating severe COVID-1933–36. However, the application and dose of methylprednisolone are still controversial11,30,37–39. The efficacy and safety of high versus low doses of corticosteroids for the treatment of patients with COVID-19 have shown mixed outcomes in controlled trials and observational studies6,39–41. There was also some research suggests that low-dose corticosteroids (<15 mg/d dexamethasone,<400 mg/d hydrocortisone, and<1 mg/kg/d methylprednisolone) are beneficial in the treatment of patients with severe COVID-19 pneumonia28,42 or even reduced mortality in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia4,8,43.

Currently, in China, low doses and a short course of corticosteroids are recommended in severe and critical cases of COVID-1944,45. Almost all of the study patients in this study had corticosteroid treatment for less than 10 days which was similar to Jeronimo’s study31. In 2021, a systematic review and meta-analysis published in Intensive Care Med found that patients who received a longer course of corticosteroids (over 7 days) had higher rates of survival compared to a shorter course44. As is known to all, different times and duration of corticosteroids administration might have affected the observed outcome. Thus the shorter course of corticosteroids use may be one of the reasons for the higher mortality rate of the patients in this study. However continuous administration of corticosteroids can significantly suppress the immune system, making patients more susceptible to infections. This includes bacterial, viral, and especially fungal infections such as Aspergillosis and Candidiasis46. Such infections are typically controlled for a healthy immune system but can be serious and difficult to treat in immunosuppressed patients47. And mixed fungal infections exacerbate COVID-19 severity46,47.

When viral replication is active in vivo, the human immune system can provide beneficial protection by killing the virus. But in some cases, the human immune system can also cause harmful damage by infinitely activating local inflammatory reactions48. The choice of corticosteroids is to inhibit the body’s immune system, so the decision requires full consideration. It is necessary to assess whether the virus is replicating and whether there is secondary damage caused by inflammation. Theoretically, patients with no active virus in vivo but a significant inflammatory response are most appropriate to receive corticosteroids. And the meantime effective antiviral therapy is also important.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective study in six medical centres. The relatively small sample size may lead to an underestimation of the efficacy of corticosteroids. Second, data on the history of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination were not analyzed in this study because it has been a long time since the last vaccination in this study population, making these data difficult to analyze retrospectively. Third, we had no chance to evaluate serious adverse events of corticosteroids in our study although the probability of occurrence is small.

This is the first report evaluating the efficacy of low doses of corticosteroids among hospitalized patients with severe/critical COVID-19 in China’s autumn/winter 2022 Omicron wave. We ensured strict quality control, including data collection, internal quality control, and external supervision. All information regarding cases and controls was extracted retrospectively from the medical records of patients by trained physicians.

Conclusion

In this study, we observed that a short course and low dose of corticosteroids in hospitalized patients with severe/critical COVID-19 did not reduce mortality in the overall population. The use of systemic corticosteroids in severe COVID-19 may provide certain potential survival benefits but does not improve prognosis. Subsequently, we will conduct more to examine the risk factors and protective factors for the deaths of patients with COVID-19. More well-designed studies are needed to evaluate the impact of the type of corticosteroids, the timing of initiation, dosage, duration and mode of administration on the outcomes of patients.
