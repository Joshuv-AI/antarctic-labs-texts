Development and evaluation of a comprehensive Patient for Medication Safety (PFMS) intervention programme to improve participation in medication safety behaviours among older adults with chronic disease during hospital-to-home transition: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Weixi Xu, Xiaoyan Lin, Huiqi Lai, Yaqin Ren, Shanling Peng, Ting Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedication safety management during the hospital-to-home transition for older adults with chronic disease is a key focus in public health. This study aims to develop and evaluate the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of a personalized Patient for Medication Safety (PFMS) intervention programme for older adults with chronic disease during this transition.MethodsA parallel, two-arm, randomized controlled trial was conducted. The PFMS intervention included medication reconciliation before discharge, two bedside education sessions, a portable pillbox, and six remote motivational interviews over six weeks following discharge. Both the intervention and the control groups received usual care. Feasibility was assessed using recruitment rates, retention rates, intervention adherence, data collection completion rates, satisfaction surveys, and post-intervention interviews. Outcome measures (patient participation in medication safety, health literacy, healthcare worker support, and self-efficacy) were evaluated at baseline and post-intervention. The incidence and frequency of medication discrepancies were evaluated at one and six week post-discharge. Generalized estimating equations were used to examine within-group, between-group, and time-related changes in outcomes. Thematic analysis was used to analyse the qualitative data.ResultsA total of 30 patients were enrolled in the feasibility study, with 27 completing the study. The recruitment rate was 83.3%, retention rate 90%, and intervention adherence rate 81.3%. No adverse events occurred during the intervention period. Most participants reported high satisfaction. At the end of the intervention, significant differences were observed between the two groups in patient participation in medication safety (time effect), health literacy (between-group effect), medication discrepancies (time and interaction effects), and perceived healthcare provider support (between-group, time, and interaction effects) (all P < 0.05). No significant difference was found in self-efficacy (P >0.05). Patient feedback identified: (1) facilitators for accepting the PFMS intervention: care from healthcare workers and could benefit; (2) barriers: lack of comprehension and knowledge, lack of time, concern about bothering healthcare workers, negative attitudes associated with aging, sense of self-fulfillment, and intervention fatigue; (3) suggestions for improving the PFMS programme: increased identity credibility and provision of personalized support.ConclusionsThe PFMS intervention is feasible and shows preliminary efficacy in certain aspects during the hospital-to-home transition for older adults with chronic disease. Future research should refine the intervention to better enhance patient participation in medication safety behaviours and self-efficacy.Trial registrationThis trial was retrospectively registered on 18 April 2024 in the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400083215).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06563-8.

Background

As one of the countries experiencing the fastest population ageing, over 78% of older adults in China live with at least one chronic disease [1]. Medication safety management during the hospital-to-home transition has become an increasing public health priority for older patients. This transition refers to the period in which patients continue their recovery at home after hospital discharge, and in this study, it is defined as the time from pre-discharge to three months post-discharge [2]. For patients with chronic diseases requiring long-term treatment, hospitals can only provide limited acute-phase treatment, while long-term treatment needs must rely on out-of-hospital care for continuity. However, after discharge, older patients often face challenges managing complex medication regimens, including polypharmacy [3, 4], inaccurate medication information transmission [5], poor doctor-patient communication [5], and decreasing self-discipline. These challenges further increase the risk of medication safety in the home environment, making older patients particularly vulnerable post-discharge. Research indicates that older patients are most likely to encounter medication-related problems during this transition [6], experiencing an average of 2.7 to 9.3 medication discrepancies [7, 8], as well as engaging in unsafe practices such as medication misuse, non-adherence, and overuse [9]. These issues not only heighten the risks of adverse drug reactions, re-hospitalisation, falls, and frailty, leading to poorer health outcomes, but also directly affect the rational allocation of medical resources and control of healthcare costs [10, 11].

Patient participation in medication safety refers to behaviours by individuals capable of making independent decisions to promote the safety and effectiveness of their own medication use before, during, and after formal medical care [12]. It can enhance patients’ control over their health status, reduce medication errors, and improve treatment outcomes [13, 14]. However, older patients generally exhibit low levels of engagement and willingness to participate, limiting the effectiveness of medication safety management measures [15]. Factors influencing older patients’ participation in medication safety behaviours are complex and varied, mainly including health literacy [16], healthcare worker support [17], and self-efficacy [18, 19]. Particularly in the older population, declining memory and comprehension abilities, combined with the coexistence of multiple chronic diseases, make medication safety management more challenging. Therefore, it is particularly necessary to tailor a patients participation in medication safety intervention programme for older adults with chronic disease during the hospital-to-home transition to address these controllable factors.

Patient for Medication Safety (PFMS) intervention has the potential to enhance participation through strategies such as medication reconciliation, education, and motivational interviewing [20, 21]. However, few studies focus on such interventions for older patients’ participation in medication safety during the hospital-to-home transition. Existing interventions mostly focused on a single aspect and rarely form a comprehensive intervention system targeting influencing factors. This study aims to develop a customised comprehensive PFMS intervention programme and evaluate its feasibility and preliminary efficacy through a small-scale pilot trial, identifying and addressing potential issues to lay the foundation for a large-scale randomised controlled trial.

Objectives

To develop and validate a customised PFMS intervention programme for older adults with chronic disease during the hospital-to-home transition, aiming to improving patient participation in medication safety behaviours. The pilot study will evaluate the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of the programme and make necessary refinements to support future large-scale implementation.

Methods

Development of the PFMS intervention programme

Guided by the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) [22], the research team used the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behaviour model (COM-B) [22], in earlier work to identify the key determinants of patient participation in medication safety: capability (health literacy), opportunity (healthcare worker support), and motivation (self-efficacy). These components were then mapped to appropriate intervention functions—namely education, persuasion, incentivisation, training, and enablement—and corresponding behaviour change techniques [23]. Based on evidence from a systematic review [24] and prior qualitative research [25] conducted by the team, a preliminary version of the PFMS intervention programme was developed. This draft was subsequently revised through expert consultation (see Supplementary file 1 for revision details). The PFMS intervention content

Pre-discharge period (two days before to the day of discharge):

• Clinical pharmacist conducts medication reconciliation to create an individualized optimal discharge medication plan.

• Researchers conduct two bedside medication education sessions (30–60 min each) using a tablet in the patient’s ward.

• Education content is uploaded to a WeChat public account in a combined text and image format for repeated review.

• Participants receive a portable pillbox and a “5 Moments for Medication Safety” engagement tool.

Post-discharge period (six weeks):

• Weekly remote motivational interviews via WeChat video calls (10–20 min each).

During the pre-discharge period, from two days before to the day of discharge, a clinical pharmacist conduct medication reconciliation to form an individualized optimal discharge medication plan. At the same time, the researchers use a tablet to conduct two bedside medication education sessions in the patient’s ward, each lasting 30–60 min. To avoid older patients forgetting the information, the education content is uploaded to a WeChat public account in a combined text and image format, allowing participants to review or listen to it repeatedly as needed. Additionally, a portable pillbox and a “5 Moments for Medication Safety” engagement tool are provided. During the six weeks post-discharge, the researcher conducts weekly remote motivational interviews via WeChat video calls, each lasting 10–20 min, for a total of six times, to reinforce patient participation in medication safety behaviours.

To ensure participant adherence, the researchers build trust relationships during the hospital stay, fully inform participants of the research arrangements, obtain informed consent, maintain regular online and offline interactions, and offer small gift rewards.

Feasibility and preliminary efficacy assessment of the PFMS intervention programme

Trial design and setting

A two-arm, parallel randomized controlled trial design was conducted at a tertiary hospital in Fuzhou, China, and reported in strict accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement [26].

Sample size and participants

The study included 30 older adults with chronic disease, meeting the typical feasibility study sample size of 10–20 participants per group [27]. Patients discharged from the cardiology, neurology, and respiratory departments of a tertiary hospital in Fuzhou from April 24 to 30, 2024, who met the study criteria were invited to participate through healthcare provider recommendations and on-site mobilisation. Researchers explained the study’s purpose, voluntary participation, and confidentiality of information. If patients agreed to participate, they were asked to sign an informed consent form.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 60 years, (2) diagnosed with at least one chronic disease such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or respiratory disease at a secondary or higher hospital [28], (3) long-term use of at least two medications, (4) permanent resident of Fuzhou, (5) hospitalised for ≥ 3 days, (6) owning a smartphone and using WeChat, and (7) no communication barriers. The exclusion criteria were: (1) cognitive impairment(SPMSQ ≥ 3 points), (2) being critically ill or in the terminal stage of disease, (3) having severe mental illness such as schizophrenia, delusions, or bipolar disorder, (4) having previously received or currently receiving similar interventions, (5) inability to independently perform basic daily activities, (6) having severe chronic diseases such as malignant tumors [29], and (7) re-hospitalisation within 3 months of discharge.

Interventions

Intervention Group (IG): Received the PFMS intervention and usual care.Control Group (CG): Received usual care, including medication reconciliation, discharge education, and one telephone follow-up one week after discharge.

Randomisation, allocation concealment, implementation and blinding

Randomisation was conducted before baseline assessment. Eligible participants were numbered in order of enrolment, and a random number keeper not directly involved in the study used ResearchRandomizer (https://www.randomizer.org/) to generate a set of random numbers from 1 to 30. These numbers were placed in sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque, and identical envelopes, which were properly stored. Researchers inquired about the group assignment via WeChat. If the random number in the envelope was between 1 and 15, the participant was assigned to the intervention group; if between 16 and 30, to the control group. The study was blinded to participants, data collectors, and data analysts.

Outcomes

Outcomes for feasibility criteria

To assess the feasibility of the trial and to determine whether adjustments to the main trial procedures were required, we established progression criteria for recruitment rate, intervention adherence, and outcome data collection. Intervention adherence was defined as the ratio of the total number of sessions actually completed by all participants to the total number of sessions scheduled for all participants; in addition, following common practice in the literature, participants who completed ≥ 6 out of 8 sessions were considered adherent at the individual level. The specific progression criteria were [30]: (1) 20% of older adults meeting the eligibility criteria; (2) 80% of intervention participants attending at least six sessions; and (3) 80% of participants completing both baseline and post-intervention data collection.

Acceptability and feedback of intervention

A self-designed 16-item evaluation form assessed participant satisfaction. Participants rated the intervention on a Likert 5-point scale, from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Feedback on the PFMS intervention was collected through approximately 15-minute semi-structured interviews. The form began with an open-ended question asking, “What are your experiences and feelings during the intervention? What suggestions or opinions do you have about the intervention? What factors helped you adhere to the intervention? What factors hindered your adherence to the intervention?” Permission was sought to record oral responses during the interview.

Data collection

Data were sourced from patients, family members, and their electronic medical records. Three investigators distributed questionnaires bedside, explaining filling instructions. Patients completed the questionnaires by themselves. Quantitative data at the end of the intervention were collected through Questionnaire Star, and qualitative data were collected face-to-face during follow-up appointments or via telephone for those who did not attend. Investigators checked the completed questionnaires on-site, correcting any missing, incorrect, or logically erroneous entries immediately.

Analytical methods

Quantitative data were analysed using IBM SPSS 26.0. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-sided), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptions used Mean ± SD, the median (P25, P75), frequency, percentage, or proportion. T-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and Fisher’s exact test were performed to investigate the balance between the two groups’ baseline demographics and outcome variables. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used to analyse the impact of the PFMS intervention on the outcome variables. In the GEE model, group and time were treated as covariates. For outcome variables measured as continuous data, the identity link function was applied; for outcome variables measured as count data, the log link function was used. An unstructured working correlation matrix was specified for all analyses. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion.

Qualitative data were managed using NVivo 11 software, and thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach [39]. To ensure analytical rigor, investigator triangulation was applied. Two researchers (XWX and LHQ) independently and repeatedly read the transcribed texts, extracted key statements, and performed coding. Relationships and potential contradictions within the data were then identified to refine the codes and develop candidate categories, subthemes, and themes.

Finally, a deductive analysis was conducted using the COM-B model, with themes and codes classified into the most relevant domains (capability, motivation, or opportunity), and the results were returned to participants for validation. During the processes of inductive coding and deductive categorization, any discrepancies between the two researchers were resolved through discussion with a third team member (LT) until consensus was reached.

Results

Recruitment, attrition, and adherence

The flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 30 participants agreed to join the study (recruitment rate 83.3%) and were randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group in a 1:1 ratio. In the intervention group, one participant lost contact due to change contact details, and two participants were excluded after re-hospitalization (retention rate 90%). By the end of the study, 10 participants completed the PFMS intervention (overall adherence rate 81.3%, individual adherence rate 83.3%), while two did not meet the required number of interventions due to personal or family matters, such as taking grandchildren to school, grocery shopping, or attending church services as practising Christians. All participants completed the questionnaires at baseline and at the 6-week follow-up (100% response rate). No adverse events occurred during the intervention period.

Participant characteristics

The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 2. All were aged between 60 and 79 years. The average length of hospital stay was 7.93 days. Participants had between 1 and 7 chronic conditions, with an average of 5 medications. The duration of medication use ranged from 0.5 to 38 years, with a mean of 11.59 years. In total, 40.7% required medication three or more times per day, and 14.8% had experienced adverse drug reactions. There were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups in terms of demographic characteristics and baseline outcome variables, making them comparable.

Acceptability

Most participants provided positive feedback on the PFMS intervention, expressing satisfaction with the overall programme and its components, as indicated by the statements “strongly agree” or “agree” on the evaluation form (see Fig. 2). Among the 12 participants, all found the form and duration of health education satisfactory, and the practicality of the portable pillbox was fully recognized. The relevance and usefulness of the intervention content were also highly praised. However, 16.7% were dissatisfied with the practicality of the “5 moments for medication safety” tool, and 8.3% were dissatisfied with the themes, content, and duration of post-discharge interventions.Fig. 2Bar graph of participants’ satisfaction with the PFMS intervention (n = 12)

Bar graph of participants’ satisfaction with the PFMS intervention (n = 12)

Semi-structured interviews were conducted from the intervention group to explore the facilitators and barriers to completing the PFMS intervention, as well as their feedback and suggestions regarding the program. In total, 12 interviews were conducted, with an average length of 18.95 min. Themes were mapped using the COM-B model, resulting in ten identified themes: (1) Capability: lack of comprehension and knowledge, intervention fatigue. (2) Opportunity: lack of time, concern about bothering healthcare workers, care from healthcare workers, increased identity credibility. (3) Motivation: could benefit, sense of self-fulfillment, negative attitudes associated with aging, provision of personalized support.

Capability

Capability is defined as an individual’s ability to engage in a particular activity. It includes psychological capability (such as the knowledge and mental skills required to engage in necessary cognitive processes, including comprehension and reasoning) and physical capability (such as physical skills, strength, or stamina).

Opportunity

Opportunity encompasses all external factors beyond the individual that make a behaviour possible or prompt it to occur. This includes physical opportunity (the physical environment, such as time, resources, location, cues, and materials) and social opportunity (the social environment, including language, social norms, interpersonal influences, and cultural practices).

Motivation

Motivation refers to all brain processes that energise and direct behaviour. It comprises automatic motivation (including emotional reactions, desires, impulses, inhibition, drive states, and reflex responses) and reflective motivation (such as planning and evaluation).

Discussion

This pilot study evaluated the feasibility of the PFMS intervention and its preliminary effects on hospital-to-home transition for older adults with chronic disease.

Feasibility of the PFMS intervention

The recruitment rate (83.3%), retention rate (90%), and intervention adherence rate (81.3%) were all high, and patient satisfaction was positive, indicating that the PFMS intervention is highly feasible and acceptable among older adults with chronic disease. To ensure intervention fidelity, the research team provided standardised training to all staff, established a standardised intervention protocol, and used online checklists to document the delivery of each session. The strong feasibility of the PFMS intervention may be attributed to several factors: (1) its content was tailored to individual differences and addressed the real-world challenges of medication safety in this population; (2) the intervention process was simple and of moderate duration, aligning with older adults’ preferences for receiving information; (3) patient participation was central to the programme, encompassing pre-discharge medication reconciliation and teach-back health education, as well as post-discharge motivational interviewing via WeChat video calls, emphasising collaboration and primarily verbal communication, which suits older adults’ preferences [40]; and (4) regular interaction and follow-up were maintained throughout the study, enhancing participants’ sense of engagement and trust.

Preliminary effects of the PFMS intervention

The results showed that, over time, patient participation in medication safety significantly improved in both groups (P < 0.001), suggesting that follow-up itself may have contributed to promoting such behaviours. However, the between-group difference was not statistically significant, possibly due to the relatively short follow-up period. During the transitional phase from hospital to home, older adults with chronic disease need to rapidly adapt to new medication routines, yet their cognitive and comprehension abilities may limit the early acquisition and application of new knowledge and skills.

Compared with usual care, the PFMS intervention significantly reduced the incidence of medication discrepancies in older adults during the transition period (P < 0.05). This was achieved by encouraging patients to provide feedback on medication effects, express needs and questions, and develop habits of proactive communication with healthcare providers, which not only enhanced their awareness of participation but also ensured continuity of care and improved adherence. For older adults undergoing long-term polypharmacy, the PFMS intervention enabled them to better understand medication information, master correct administration techniques, and promptly report issues during the medication process, providing critical information for healthcare teams to identify and correct potential risks [40, 41], thereby reducing discrepancies caused by misunderstanding or incorrect execution.

The PFMS intervention also significantly improved patients’ health literacy and perceived healthcare provider support (P < 0.05). The improvement in health literacy is likely attributable to enhanced understanding and mastery of disease and medication management, which helped patients better comprehend and follow treatment plans [42, 43]; the increase in perceived support may be related to more frequent and higher-quality interactions with healthcare providers and influenced by factors such as provider attitude [44], communication quality [45], and patients’ trust in their providers [17].

However, the PFMS intervention did not significantly improve patients’ self-efficacy (P > 0.05). Self-efficacy is influenced by factors such as cognitive ability, psychological state, health beliefs, and social support [46]. In this study, some older adults had limited comprehension and knowledge and held negative health attitudes, which may have partly reduced the intervention’s effectiveness. The short follow-up period may also have failed to capture the gradual development and maintenance of self-efficacy over time. Future studies should consider the cognitive and behavioural characteristics of older adults, provide personalised interventions, strengthen medication education, extend follow-up, and encourage active engagement to promote medication safety behaviours and enhance self-efficacy.

Barriers to accepting the PFMS intervention and implications

Based on the COM-B model, this study identified the following barriers to older adults’ acceptance of the PFMS intervention: lack of comprehension and knowledge, lack of time, concern about bothering healthcare workers, negative attitudes associated with aging, sense of self-fulfilment, and intervention fatigue.In terms of capability, some participants, due to low literacy and limited medical knowledge, struggled to understand the intervention content, which reduced their motivation to participate. This suggests that intervention delivery should use plain, accessible language and translate abstract knowledge into familiar, everyday analogies to ensure comprehension.In terms of opportunity, many older adults juggle family responsibilities and social roles, making it difficult to commit fixed time slots for participation. This highlights the importance of agreeing on intervention times in advance and offering more flexible formats. Concerns about “bothering healthcare workers” reflect both respect for professional boundaries and fear of causing annoyance, particularly during remote interventions. Healthcare providers should proactively reach out to patients, encourage participation in medication safety, and reduce social barriers through regular follow-up.

In terms of motivation, some participants attributed health problems to the inevitability of aging, leading to negative health beliefs (e.g., “At our age, it’s useless even if you listen carefully”). This underscores the need to provide emotional support, foster a positive health outlook, and ensure intervention content is practical and relevant so that patients perceive tangible benefits and understand the long-term health impact of participating in medication safety. Additionally, patients whose symptoms had “resolved” or who experienced intervention fatigue were prone to losing motivation. This indicates that healthcare providers should emphasise the importance of continuous medication safety management, reinforce the long-term benefits of the intervention, and establish mechanisms for positive feedback.On this basis, the intervention process and content should be streamlined to avoid unnecessary components. For example, the “Five Moments for Medication Safety” tool could be removed, as it imposed additional burdens on participants with lower literacy and had little appeal to those with higher literacy. Similarly, motivational interviewing on “medication storage” and “accessing medication information” could be omitted, as these are skill-oriented topics for which pre-discharge education already achieves satisfactory behavioural conversion rates. Resources could then be focused on topics requiring dynamic adjustment, with flexibility in pacing and duration, and delivered in smaller, spaced segments to sustain long-term engagement.

Limitations

The limitations of this study should be acknowledged and addressed in future research:

(a) This was a single-centre study with a relatively small sample size, conducted in a tertiary hospital located in an urban area. These factors may limit the generalisability of the findings to broader populations. (b) The outcome measures in this study were primarily based on patient self-report, which may be subject to subjective judgement and social desirability bias. In addition, some older adults were unable to complete electronic questionnaires after discharge, requiring data to be supplemented via telephone interviews. Variations in data collection methods may have affected the accuracy and consistency of the results. (c) Potential confounding factors need to be considered, such as differences in routine care or follow-up frequency between the intervention and control groups, which may have influenced the outcomes.Future studies should aim to refine the intervention protocol and adopt a multi-centre, large-sample design to validate its effectiveness in more diverse populations, including those in rural areas or with cognitive impairment.

Conclusions

The PFMS intervention for the hospital-to-home transitions in elderly patients with chronic diseases is feasible. Future research should optimize the intervention programme, increase patient participation and compliance, and extend follow-up periods with additional support forms to comprehensively improve patient participation in medication safety and self-efficacy, laying the foundation for large-scale application.
