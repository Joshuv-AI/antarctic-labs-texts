Ultrasound-assisted extraction of polyphenols from Phyllanthi Fructus: Comprehensive insights from extraction optimization and antioxidant activity
Authors: Haixia Che, Rui Zhang, Xianyao Wang, Hong Yu, Xuepeng Shi, Jie Yi, Jiawen Li, Qi Qi, Ruizhi Dong, Qian Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

For centuries, PF has been utilized in traditional medicinal systems, including Tibetan, Indian, and Chinese herbal medicine, either alone or in combination with other traditional herbs for treating Diabetic, digestive disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and so on [1], [2]. Due to its medicinal and culinary value, PF is recognized as one of the three health-promoting plants designated by the United Nations World Health Organization for global cultivation and promotion [3], [4]. Extracts derived from PF have been studied extensively for their antioxidant and hepatoprotective properties, as well as their potential hypoglycemic effects, owing to their rich content of polyphenols and other active constituents [5], [6]. Nowadays, PF is adopted as a health product, which has been overwhelming the most of consumers [7]. Therefore, there is a pressing need to optimize extraction methods to enhance both the yield and activity of bioactive compounds, ensuring the advancement of functional food products derived from PF.

Currently, a variety of traditional extraction methods, such as hot water bath, maceration, and heating reflux, as well as modern auxiliary techniques like ultrasound-assisted and microwave-assisted extraction, have been utilized to isolate bioactive compounds from natural sources [8], [9], [10]. Among various auxiliary extraction methods, heat reflux is included in Chinese pharmacopeia (2020) to extracting PF. However, traditional methods are often time-consuming, yield low extraction rates, and require large quantities of organic solvents, posing significant environmental risks and impeding the widespread use of natural active ingredients [11]. In contrast, ultrasound-assisted extraction has emerged as a promising green, high-efficiency and non-thermal technology. In 2014, Tsai, et al. first employed UAE to extract PF, finding that the yield of polyphenols was higher than conventional solvent extraction, but the polyphenol yield was still not ideal [12]. Therefore, we aim to propose a more efficient and environmentally friendly PF extraction method with higher polyphenol yield, promoting the application of PF.

Process optimization is essential for maximizing the yield and improving the activity of extracts obtained through UAE, thereby minimizing material and solvent waste [13]. The RSM stands out as a powerful statistical optimization tool widely employed across diverse fields such as agriculture, biology, and chemistry. This approach enables the determination of optimal results with high accuracy and efficiency, often in a minimal number of iterations [14], [15]. In recent years, the RSM model has found extensive application in designing experiments for natural product extraction [16]. However, to date, there has been a notable absence of research utilizing PBD combined with RSM to optimize the extraction of polyphenols from PF using ultrasound.

In this study, we commenced by investigating the impact of various extraction methods and conditions on the yield of polyphenols from PF. Firstly, we employed PBD to screen significant factors, followed by the utilization of RSM to optimize the UAE process. Then, we assessed the antioxidant activity in vitro to validate the efficacy of method optimization. Furthermore, we characterized the polyphenol extracts obtained through the optimal method with UPLC-Q-TOF MS. Finally, we utilized network pharmacology and molecular docking to elucidate the key antioxidant polyphenols and potential target proteins in PF. This comprehensive approach aims to optimize the parameters of ultrasound-assisted polyphenols extraction, thereby enhancing both the polyphenols yield and antioxidant activity of PF extracts. The findings of this study offer valuable insights into the future utilization of PF in the realms of food and pharmaceuticals.

Materials and methods

Materials

PF was collected from Sankeshu Chinese Medicine Market (Harbin, China). Gallic acid and 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). BCA protein assay kit and Follin- Ciocalteu’s Phenol Reagent (2 N) was bought from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Na2CO3 and ethanol were bought from Tianjin Chemical Regent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China), while HPLC grade acetonitrile was from MREDA (Beijing, China). Formic acid was bought from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). 2,2-Azino-bis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP), MDA and SOD assay kits were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Extraction of polyphenols from PF

Preparation of PF

Dried PF was ground by a high-speed crusher (DFY-1000C, Bilang Instruments Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) and sieved with a 24-mesh sieve. The powder (0.05 g) was placed in a tapered bottle with a plug and weighed after accurately adding 50 % ethanol (v/v, 50 mL).

Heating reflux extraction (HRE)

HRE was conducted according to Chinese pharmacopeia (2020 Edition) with some modifications. The sample was transferred to reflux apparatus and refluxed for 1 h. The extracts were weighed again and made up the lost weight with 50 % ethanol. Afterward, the extracts were centrifuged using a high-speed centrifuge (H1650R, Xiangyi Instruments Co., Ltd, Changsha, China) at 8000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was collected for further study.

Ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE)

The UAE of polyphenols from PF was performed using an ultrasonic cleaner (KS-250DE, Kunshan Jielimei Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd, Suzhou, China). The samples were extracted using ultrasonic treatment at 200 W of power for 1 h at 40 ℃. The extracts were then weighed and made up the lost weight with 50 % ethanol. After centrifuging (8000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was obtained for further study.

Analysis of polyphenols yield

The yield of polyphenols in the PF was determined by Folin-phenol colorimetry method [17]. After mixing samples with Folin- Ciocalteu reagent and 7.5 % Na2CO3 solution (1: 5: 4, v/v/v) and storing in the dark for 1 h, the absorbance of mixture was detected by an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (T6-1650E, Purkinje GENERAL Instrument Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) at 760 nm. The polyphenols yield was calculated according to gallic acid standard curve [C = 10.279A + 0.0073, R2 = 0.9999] and equation (1), expressed in mg of gallic acid equivalent per g dry weight (mg/g).(1)Yield (%) = (C × V × N)/M × 100where C is the concentration of polyphenols in the PF crude solution (mg/g), V is the volume of PF crude solution (mL), and N is the dilution ratio, M is the weight of PF powder (g).

Single-factor test

The single-factor experiments were performed by controlling a single variable. Five potential factors affecting the polyphenols yield including liquid-to-solid ratio (5: 1–30: 1), ethanol volume fraction (30–80 %), ultrasonic time (15–90 min), ultrasonic temperature (30–80 ℃) and ultrasonic power (150–250 W) were investigated. The specific experiment design was shown in Table S1.

Plakett-Burman design (PBD)

According to the results of single-factor test, PBD was employed to screen the main factors affecting the extraction yield of polyphenols from PF using Design-Expert 13.0 analysis software. For PBD experiments, the relative significance of five parameters that could affect the polyphenols yield including liquid-to-solid ratio (X1), ethanol volume fraction (X2), ultrasonic time (X3), ultrasonic power (X4) and ultrasonic temperature (X5) were tested. The design matrices and related levels were shown in Table S2.

Box-Behnken design (BBD)

A three-factor, three-level response surface method, also known as BBD, was conducted to further optimize parameters screened by PBD using Design-Expert 13.0 analysis software. Three factors, including liquid-to-solid ratio, ethanol volume fraction and ultrasonic temperature, were selected as independent variables, and the polyphenols yield, DPPH scavenging ability, ABTS+ scavenging ability and Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) were taken as the response value. The levels of independent variables were displayed in Table S3.

Assays of antioxidants

DPPH scavenging ability

The DPPH scavenging ability of PF crude extract was measured based on the method proposed by Martín-García et al with slight adjustments [18]. Briefly, the mixture of 100 μL PF crude extract solution and 100 μL DPPH solution (50 μmol/L) were incubated for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance values were measured at 517 nm and DPPH scavenging ability was calculated using Eq. (2).(2)DPPH scavenging ability (%) = [1 − (A1 − A2)/A0] × 100 %where A0 was the absorbance of DPPH solution, A1 was the absorbance of mixture solution, A2 was the absorbance of sample.

ABTS+ scavenging ability

ABTS+ scavenging ability was assessed using a total antioxidant capacity assay kit (A015-2–1) and determined according to instructions. 10 μL samples were combined with 170 μL ABTS working fluid and 20 μL application solution. Then the absorbance of mixed solutions was measured at 405 nm after reacting for 6 min (at room temperature), and results were presented as Trolox Equivalent Antioxidant Capacity (TEAC).

FRAP assay

The ability of PF extracts to remove Fe3+ was estimated by the FRAP method following the instructions of the assay kit (A015-3–1). PF extracts (5 μL) and FRAP working fluid (180 μL) were mixed and incubated at 37 ℃ for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm. FeSO4 was used as a reference to establish a standard curve for quantifying the antioxidant capacity of PF extracts.

The antioxidant activity of Phyllanthi Fructus extraction in cells

Cell culture

PASMCs were obtained from the Otwo biotech (Shenzhen, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) with 15 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin under 5 % CO2 at 37℃. For vitro experiments, PASMCs that reached 60–70 % were treated with PF extracts and placed under hypoxia conditions (3 % O2, 5 % CO2 and 91 % N2) for 24 h.

Cell viability assay

After seeding in 96-well plates with 2000 cells per well and adhering for 24 h, cells were treated with different concentrations (10–100 µg/mL) of PF extraction dried powder and incubated for 24 h. 20 µ L MTT at 5 mg/mL was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 ℃. The violet crystal was redissolved with 150 µL DMSO and the absorbance was measured at 492 nm.

Intracellular MDA assay

According to the MDA assay kit (A003-1), MDA levels in PASMCs were determined. PASMCs were seeded at a density of 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. After treatment of hypoxia and PF extracts, cells were disrupted and centrifuged. The supernatant was incubated with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 95 ℃ for 40 min and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm.

Determination of intracellular SOD activity

The superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity in cells was detected using xanthine oxidase method kits (A001-3). PASMCs (105/well) were incubated in 6-well plates and treated with PF extracts for 24 h under hypoxia. After homogenizing and centrifuging, the sample was combined with enzyme reaction solution and reacted for 20 min at room temperature. The results were measured at 450 nm and SOD activity was expressed as an active unit per milligram protein.

Qualitative analysis of polyphenols extracts by UPLC-Q-TOF MS

UPLC-Q-TOF MS analysis was conducted using an AcquityTM UPLC system coupled with LCT premier XE time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Waters, MA, USA). The chromatography separation was performed on ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) column with acetonitrile (A) and 0.1 % formic acid water (B) as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 40 ℃ and injection volume was 5 μL. The linear gradient elution was applied and shown in Table S4. Mass spectrometer was operated with an electrospray ionization ion source in the positive and negative ion modes. The scan range was m/z 100 ∼ 1100, source temperature was 110 ℃, capillary voltage was 2500 V and the cone voltage was 30 V.

Network pharmacology analysis

On the basis of the components retrieved using UPLC-Q-TOF MS, the TCMSP database (oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30 %, drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18) and literatures were used to screen pharmaceutical ingredients. The targets were obtained from SEA (https://sea.bkslab.org/) and SwissTargetPrediction (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) databases with probability more than 0. In addition, GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM database (https://www.omim.org/) were applied to collect antioxidant targets. Subsequently, the potential active ingredient targets and antioxidant targets were imported to Venn database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/) to collect the co-targets. The common targets were then imported into STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org) to obtain protein–protein interaction (PPI) and visualize by Cytoscape 3.9.1. DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp) was employed to perform GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis. Finally, “component-target-pathway” network of network pharmacology was constructed using Cytoscape 3.9.1.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of AKT1 (PBD ID: 6HHJ), EGFR (PBD ID: 2RGP), TNF (PBD ID: 2E7A), etc. were acquired from protein databank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/). The 3D structure of small molecule compounds were downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). After the pre-treatment of ligand removal, dehydration, and hydrogenation of targets and converting the molecules of ligands and receptors to pdbqt format using Autodock 1.5.7. Molecular docking was performed by AutoDock Vina to obtain affinity values between active ingredients and key targets. Finally, PyMOL 4.6.0 and Discovery Studio 4.5 were applied to visualize the results.

Statistical analysis

The data were conducted in triplicates and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was identified by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1.

Results and discussions

Influence of different extraction methods on polyphenols yield

To evaluate the impact of extraction methods on the yield of natural polyphenols, we conducted a comparative study between traditional HRE and UAE (as shown in Fig. 1A). The HRE procedure adhered to guidelines outlined in Chinese pharmacopeia (2020 Edition) with some modification. In consideration of the toxicity of methanol, ethanol was chosen as a modifier due to its eco-friendly, non-toxic and recoverable properties. In contrast, the UAE process was performed according to preliminary experiments and previous research [12]: liquid-to-solid ratios (50: 1), ultrasonic temperature (40 ℃), ethanol volume fraction (50 %), and ultrasonic power (200 W) for 60 min. As depicted in Fig. 1B, the polyphenols yield with UAE significantly surpassed that of HRE. This superiority can be attributed to markable capacity of ultrasonication approach to disrupt the external structure of the raw materials, facilitating enhanced solvent penetration. The single-factor analysis of PF extraction

Effect of liquid-to-solid ratio

The liquid-to-solid ratio stands as a pivotal factor influencing the extraction process by regulating the driving force of the mass transfer (the concentration difference between the solid phase and the liquid phase) [19]. As shown in Fig. 1C, the polyphenols yield exhibited a notable increase with the escalation of liquid-to-solid ratio from 5: 1 to 20: 1 mL/g, but, beyond this threshold, a visible decline in yield was observed. This phenomenon can be explained that increased solvents improving contact area, heightening osmotic pressures, and reducing viscosity, thus, accelerating the mass transfer rates, and enhancing the ultrasonic cavitation effect. Yet, an excessive liquid-to-solid ratio may reduce ultrasonic penetration, potentially hindering the extraction procedure [20]. Taking into account comprehensively, 20: 1 was selected as the optimal liquid-to-solid ratio.

Effect of ethanol volume fraction

Ethanol, as a polar solvent, could effectively dissolve and extract polyphenols from the plant matrix [21]. The ethanol volume fraction is a crucial factor in the extraction experiment. Here, we explored the impact of varying ethanol volume fractions (ranging from 30 % to 80 %) on polyphenols yield. As shown in Fig. 1D, the yield of polyphenols exhibited an upward trend from 30 % to 70 %, peaking at 70 %, then declining from 70 % to 80 %. Two reasons contributed to this trend: one was a concentration gradient between cellular interior and exterior in the presence of ethanol, facilitating the diffusion of the polyphenols into the solvent. the other was that ethanol disrupted the interactions between polyphenols and plant matrix. However, it is important to note that excessive ethanol volume fraction may alter to solvent polarity unfavorably for the dissolution of polar polyphenols [22], [23]. Therefore, 70 % ethanol was chosen as optimal, consistent with previous study [12].

Effect of ultrasonic time

Fig. 1E demonstrated the significant impact of ultrasonic time on the yield of polyphenols from PF, highlighting a key role of ultrasonic time in the extraction process. As the ultrasonic time increased, the polyphenols yield initially rose, reaching its peak value at 30 min, however, beyond this point, this yield exhibited a slight decline after 30 min and a pronounced downward trend after 60 min. These results implied that while ultrasonic enhanced the diffusion and release of polyphenols within an appropriate time frame, prolonged ultrasonic time led to decomposition of certain polyphenols due to the enhancement of ultrasonic thermal effect and mechanical effect [24]. Considering the importance of energy efficiency and cost-effectiveness, an ultrasonic time of 30 min was selected for extraction.

Effect of ultrasonic temperature

According to Fig. 1F, the polyphenols yield displayed a temperature-dependent response. From 30 to 60 ℃, a positive correlation between the temperature and the polyphenols yield was observed. It maybe attributed to that elevated temperature reducing physical adsorption and chemical interaction, therefore accelerating the movement of the polyphenols compound and the solvent molecule, and ultimately improving the extraction proficiency [25], [26]. However, excessive temperature could lead to the structural damage of target ingredients and the solvent volatilization, adversely affecting the extraction process. Thus, 60 ℃ was chosen as the subsequent experiment’s ultrasonic temperature.

Effect of ultrasonic power

Results showed a positive correlation between polyphenols yield and ultrasonic power, reaching a maximum yield 213.69 ± 2.39 mg/g at 250 W (Fig. 1G), highlighting the significant role of ultrasonic power in enhancing the release of polyphenols. Such enhancement maybe partly due to increased acceleration of mass transfer and cell fragmentation caused by bubble collapse [27]. However, it was crucial to note that 250 W was the maximum power utilized in this experiment due to instrument constraints, making it the optimal choice.

Screening of extraction parameters using PBD

PBD is recognized as a valuable statistical optimization tool for prioritizing the prioritization of variables based on their overall importance to system performance [28]. It is commonly employed to identify “key factors” among multiple influential variables in complex processes, thereby facilitating efficient optimization and waste reduction [29]. In this study, we utilized PBD to assess the effects of various factors on the extraction of polyphenols from PF. The results were summarized in Table 1, Table 2 (variance analysis (ANOVA)). The model p-value = 0.0013 < 0.05 and Adeq Precision = 14.9368 > 4 indicated that the model terms were significant [30]. Furthermore, the yield of polyphenols was well-fitted to the least square linear regression model with the coefficient of determination (R2) exceeding 0.90. According to the ANOVA, the liquid-to-solid ratio, ethanol volume fraction and ultrasonic temperature (all with P < 0.05) exerted significant effects on the yield of polyphenols, while other factors had no prominent impact. When optimizing with ABTS+ scavenging activity as the response value, it was observed that increasing the liquid-to-solid ratio and ethanol volume fraction led to elevated ABTS+ scavenging activity of extracts (Fig. 4A), indicating a significant interaction effect. However, this trend was not consistent for ultrasonic temperature, it displayed an indistinctive interaction effect with liquid-to-solid and a pronounced interaction effect with ethanol volume fraction, as evidenced by a gentle response surface for the former and a steep slope for the latter (Fig. 4B, C).

The steep gradients depicted in Fig. 5 underscored the substantial impact of the three factors on FRAP. The sparsely distributed contour lines illustrating the interaction between X1 and X2 suggested a comparatively lower significance than interactions involving other factors. Furthermore, when holding other factors constant, the solid–liquid ratio and ethanol concentration exhibited a more pronounced effect on FRAP values compared to ultrasonic temperature. These observations aligned with the findings presented in Table 4.

Combining the regression equation and response surface results, the optimal extraction conditions for high achieving polyphenols yield, DPPH scavenging ability, ABTS+ scavenging ability and FRAP were determined as follows: liquid-to-solid ratio (22.856: 1), ethanol volume fraction (60 %), ultrasonic temperature (52.939 ℃). Under these conditions, the predicted polyphenols yield was 211.326 mg/g, and the DPPH scavenging ability, ABTS+ scavenging ability and FRAP were 84.547 %, 2.131 mmol/g and 2.421 mmol/g, respectively.

Verification of the extraction conditions given by the predictive model

To validate the reliability of optimal extraction conditions predicted by BBD experiment, we conducted a validation experiment using adjusted predicted extraction conditions. These adjustments were made based on instrument performance to ensure practicality. The modified optimal experimental conditions obtained from the response surface model were as follows: liquid-to-solid ratio (23: 1), ethanol volume fraction (60 %) and ultrasonic temperature (53 ℃). Three extraction experiments were conducted under these conditions, and the actual value was 213.49 mg/g for polyphenols yield, 76.95 % for DPPH scavenging ability, 2.24 mmol/g for ABTS+ scavenging ability and 2.34 mmol/g for FRAP. These values were in close agreement with the predicted values, indicating good adequacy and reliability of the model. The polyphenols yield was higher than the 55.34 and 114.01 mg /g obtained by Tsai, et al. and Wu, et al [12], [33], respectively, indicating that the UAE method optimized by PBD-RSM technology has significant potential in promoting the extraction and application of beneficial polyphenols from PF.

Validation of PF extract’s antioxidant activity in cells

To validate the results of method optimization, we conducted a study on the antioxidant capacity of PF extract at cellular level, focusing primarily on its impact on the activity of the key antioxidant enzyme SOD and the level of MDA (Fig. 6A). Previous studies have reported that hypoxia could induce severe oxidative stress in PASMCs [34], rendering this model ideal for our investigation. Initially, we assessed the effects of HRE, UAE and optimized ultrasound-assisted extraction (OUAE) on cell viability. Fig. 6B illustrated that cell viability remained at approximately 80 % when exposed to a drug concentration of 20 μg/mL. Consequently, we selected this concentration for subsequent experiments. Next, as shown in Fig. 6C, there was a significant decrease in SOD activity under hypoxia, which was notably restored following treatment with PF extracts, and the order was OUAE > UAE > HRE. Additionally, the level of MDA in PASMCs significantly increased under hypoxia, but was downregulated by PF extracts (Fig. 6D), exhibiting a consistent trend with SOD activity. These findings suggested that ultrasound could effectively enhance the yield of polyphenols in PF extract, thereby boosting its antioxidant capacity. Construction of “component-target-pathway” network by network pharmacology

After characterizing polyphenol components extracted via ultrasound assistance in PF extracts, we performed network pharmacology analysis to elucidate its possible antioxidant targets and pathways. Among the 26 polyphenols ingredients identified in PF extracts, 24 potentially active ingredients were selected based on TCMSP database and literature for further research [46], [47]. Utilizing SwissTargetPrediction and SEA database, a total of 417 target genes for 24 polyphenol compounds were obtained after merging and deduplicating. In addition, 4889 targets were screened using “antioxidant” as search terms in OMIM and GeneCards database. By intersecting them with compound targets, we identified 292 intersecting genes as potential targets for polyphenol ingredients in PF extracts against oxidative stress (Fig. 8A). Fig. 8B visually displayed the interaction among the target proteins in the network. The larger node represented the higher degree value, suggesting their importance as the core targets. Results showed that AKT1, IL6, TNF, EGFR, BCL2, STAT3, HIF1A, CASP3, SRC were centrally located in the PPI network, indicating their involvement in the antioxidant process of polyphenol compounds in PF. GO enrichment analysis showed that this process mainly encompasses biological processes such as protein phosphorylation and negative regulation of apoptosis, as well as molecular functions including enzyme binding and protein kinase activity (Fig. 8C). Furthermore, through the enrichment analysis of the KEGG pathway, decisive pathways of polyphenol components against oxidative stress were screened, as shown in Fig. 8D, which also illustrated the top 20 pathways including HIF-1 signaling pathway and NF-κB signaling pathway. Molecular docking of polyphenol components to antioxidant targets

Molecular docking is an in-silico technique utilized to determine the interactive mode and binding affinity of a ligand to a receptor, making it a promising tool for predicting the target proteins of natural active ingredients [48]. In this study, we employed AutoDock Vina to clarify the interaction between polyphenol components in PF and their target proteins identified through network pharmacology. It is well-established that the lower binding energy indicating a stronger binding ability, and a binding power less than −5.0 kcal/mol−1 implying good bonding, and below −7.0 kcal/mol−1 representing a powerful binding activity [49], so the degree of matching between the top 10 core components and proteins were evaluated by the size of the binding energy. Fig. 9A showed the heat map of the binding energy and the value of binding energy were listed in Table S5. Results showed that most binding energies were less than −5.0 kcal/mol−1, and more than half fell below −7.0 kcal/mol−1, suggesting the strong binding of polyphenol components in PF with the antioxidant proteins. Notably, AKT1, EGFR and TNF emerged as promising targets due to their low binding affinity with most of the compounds. Subsequently, Pymol and Discovery Studio were employed to further characterize interactive forces and visualize the molecular models of the top three combinations with the lowest binding energies. As shown in Fig. 9B-D, ellagic acid (−10.3 kcal/mol−1), myricetin (−9.9 kcal/mol−1) and eriodictyol (−9.6 kcal/mol−1) interacted with the active site of AKT1 mainly through hydrogen bonds, Van der waals and Pi-Alkyl, facilitated by the multi-hydroxyl and benzene ring structure in the compounds. Given that the top three combinations were all concentrated on AKT1, a crucial protein kinase partaking oxidative stress processes associated with the progression of cardiovascular disease such as atherosclerosis, which implied that AKT1 may be a valuable target for further research in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases with PF [50]. Conclusion

This study introduced a green and efficient strategy for UAE of polyphenols from PF, optimizing UAE parameters using a combination of PBD and RSM for the first time. Results indicated a significant enhancement in polyphenol yield from PF extracts, which exhibited robust antioxidant activity, at both chemical and cellular level. This antioxidant activity was likely attributed to abundant polyphenols in PF’s extracts, which focused on target proteins, such as AKT1, IL6, EGFR. Notably, ellagic acid, myricetin, and eriodictyol emerged as key compounds potentially interacting with AKT1. These findings provided theoretical and technical supports for the further development and utilization of PF in pharmaceutical, functional food or cosmetic industry. Additionally, this economically and green chemical extraction approach opens new avenues for extraction of numerous plant components, stimulating further research into the biological activity and underlying antioxidant mechanisms of PF extracts.
