Introduction

The work has been reported in line with the Surgical Case Report (SCARE) criteria [1].

Large bowel obstruction (LBO) due to volvulus presents a critical, life-threatening condition requiring immediate intervention. Sigmoid colon volvulus is the most common (43–75%), followed by cecal volvulus (10–52%) [2, 3, 5–8]. Synchronous double colonic volvulus is exceptionally rare, with fewer than 10 cases documented in academic literature; this case marks the first reported instance in Uganda.

Diagnosing these conditions is challenging due to overlapping clinical and radiological features, potentially leading to missed diagnoses. Timely resuscitation and prompt surgical management are crucial, as untreated cases can rapidly progress to severe morbidity or mortality.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old male of the Toro tribe from Fort Portal city in western Uganda came to our emergency department with acute abdominal pain, distension, and complete constipation persisting for 2 days. He reported five episodes of non-bloody feculent vomiting and reduced appetite over the preceding day. This was his first instance of abdominal distension, and no changes in bowel habits or rectal bleeding were noted. He had no significant medical history except for occasional intermittent constipation.

During the physical examination, the patient appeared unwell with an oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 95% on room air, pulse rate of 110 beats per minute, and respiratory rate of 21 breaths per minute, while other vital signs were normal. His abdomen was noticeably distended and mildly tender throughout, without any palpable masses or hernias. Bowel sounds were hyperactive upon auscultation.

During digital rectal examination, the rectal vault was found to be empty, with no palpable masses or signs of bleeding noted on inspection of the examining finger.

Investigations

Blood test: complete blood count showed leucocytosis. Electrolyte levels, liver function tests, and renal function tests all showed results within normal ranges. Blood gases were not analyzed.

Radiographs: Erect abdominal radiograph (Fig. 1) showed significant dilation of the sigmoid colon without pneumoperitoneum. Differential diagnosis

A diagnosis of sigmoid volvulus was made, and the patient, with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score of III, was taken to the operating room after undergoing appropriate preoperative optimization.

Treatment

Due to the unavailability of flexible sigmoidoscopic derotation and decompression, laparotomy by general surgeon was performed. Findings during surgery revealed dilated loops of the large bowel, with a 180-degree clockwise sigmoid volvulus and a 180-degree anticlockwise cecal volvulus (Fig. 2). The small bowel appeared collapsed without visible abnormalities. On the basis of these findings and concerns for recurrence, a total colectomy and end ileostomy were performed due to the rectum being too dilated and edematous for a safe primary anastomosis. The ileostomy was intended as temporary, with plans for later restoration of bowel continuity.

Outcome and follow-up

Postoperatively, the patient was managed with analgesia and received intravenous antibiotics and proton-pump inhibitors (PPI) for 3 days. On the second day, oral sips were initiated. The patient was discharged home on the 5th day and was reviewed on the 14th postoperative day in the outpatient clinic, with no reported complications. Discussion

“Volvulus” comes from the Latin volvere, meaning to twist, referring to the axial rotation of a segment of the alimentary tract around its mesentery [2, 3]. It commonly affects the colon [4], with the cecum (10–52%) and sigmoid colon (43–75%) being the most frequently involved sites for colonic volvulus [3, 5–8], particularly prevalent in low and middle-income settings [3].

Untreated volvulus can quickly lead to bowel ischemia, necrosis, and perforation, significantly increasing morbidity and mortality risks [3].

A synchronous double volvulus affecting both the cecum and sigmoid colon is exceedingly rare, with only a few cases documented worldwide. This case marks the first reported instance in Uganda.

Sigmoid volvulus, typically seen in older adults with neurological or psychiatric conditions [8], is relatively prevalent among middle-aged individuals in Uganda, often with a history of chronic constipation [3, 9]. In younger populations, such as children, sigmoid volvulus is linked to conditions such as Hirschsprung’s disease and Chagas disease [10–14].

Cecal volvulus involves the rotation or torsion of a mobile cecum and ascending colon, contributing to 1–3% of all large-bowel obstructions [3, 15]. Type I and II, primarily due to torsion, account for 80% of cases, while Type III, characterized by cecal bascules (upward folding of the cecum), constitutes the remaining 20% of cases [3].

Several theories have been proposed to explain colonic torsion leading to volvulus, including mesenteric stretching and lengthening due to chronic constipation, anatomical anomalies for example, absence of gastrophrenic, gastrosplenic, and splenorenal ligaments, presence of elongated mesentery and redundant sigmoid colon, colonic dysmotility, and partial intestinal malrotation [3].

Diagnosing colonic obstruction entails a comprehensive evaluation comprising detailed history, thorough physical examination, and radiological investigations. Physical manifestations can vary significantly; individuals with bowel ischemia or perforation may manifest signs of septic shock and peritonitis, while others may present with stable vital signs. Typically, the abdomen exhibits distension and tympany upon percussion, though some cases may display asymmetrical distension with tympany localized to specific abdominal regions [3].

Upon admission, our patient presented with stable vital signs and a mildly tender, tympanic distended abdomen characterized by absent bowel sounds.

Laboratory tests are not diagnostic for colonic volvulus; however, significant leukocytosis or metabolic acidosis may suggest bowel ischemia, necrosis, or perforation. Hypochloremia, hypokalemia, and other electrolyte imbalances can occur from prolonged vomiting in bowel obstruction [3].

Plain radiographs generally show large-bowel obstruction, but synchronous sigmoid and cecal volvulus can mask each other’s appearance. Computed tomography (CT) imaging may miss the diagnosis due to the mass effect and rarity of the condition. Contrast enema can aid diagnosis but poses a higher perforation risk [3, 16].

In this case, plain radiographs demonstrated dilated large-bowel loops and the characteristic “coffee bean” sign of sigmoid volvulus. The cecal volvulus, however, was not visible, likely obscured by the sigmoid volvulus. CT and contrast studies were not performed due to their unavailability in our setting.

The primary objective in treating colonic obstruction is to alleviate torsion and prevent recurrence. Standard treatment includes colonic decompression via rectal tube or colonoscopy, followed by definitive surgery. However, urgent laparotomy is warranted for patients with peritonitis or failed endoscopic detorsion [3].

For isolated sigmoid volvulus, surgical options include sigmoid resection with primary anastomosis or a Hartmann’s procedure [17]. Cecal volvulus typically necessitates surgery, as non-operative reduction is ineffective in over 95% of cases and carries a high perforation risk [3]. Additionally, cecal necrosis may be missed in 20–25% of endoscopically managed cases, leading to perforation [8, 15, 18–20]. Surgical intervention for cecal volvulus involves right hemicolectomy, with or without ileostomy, based on intraoperative findings and patient stability.

In our patient, the lack of flexible sigmoidoscopic derotation and decompression capabilities necessitated immediate laparotomy, leading to the intraoperative diagnosis of synchronous double volvulus. Given the findings and recurrence risk, a total colectomy with end ileostomy was performed. Ileorectal anastomosis was deemed unsafe due to significant rectal dilation and edema.

Conclusion

Synchronous double colonic volvulus, a rare condition, is frequently overlooked clinically. Timely recognition and intervention are crucial to address diagnostic challenges and prevent potentially fatal outcomes.
