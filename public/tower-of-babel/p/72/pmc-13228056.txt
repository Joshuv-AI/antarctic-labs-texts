Seasonal green space, air pollution, and temperature in the United States: Spatial disparities among people with disabilities
Authors: Sandy Wong, Arrianna Marie Planey, Johnathan Rush, Allan C Just
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper advances research on environmental justice through analysis of multiple concurrent environmental exposures (green space, air pollution, temperature) among overlooked marginalized populations (people with disabilities) during both summer and winter seasons in the year 2021. To date, environmental justice research and policy in the United States and other parts of the world have traditionally focused on how low-income communities and racialized minorities navigate higher levels of environmental hazards, and the adverse consequences to health and quality of life. Much less attention has been paid to environmental injustices among people with disabilities. This study contributes to this knowledge gap by (1) identifying seasonal neighborhood clusters with high environmental exposures and predominantly socially marginalized groups; and (2) quantifying the distributions of green space, air pollution, and temperature among disabled residents in the contiguous United States. Using local join count statistics for the first objective, 169 clusters were detected in 21 cities. These locations are where environmental risks are highest and environmental justice interventions are most needed. Philadelphia is notable for having the most hotspots, and for being the only city to have clusters in both summer and winter. Using Kruskal-Wallis tests and Generalized Additive Models for the second objective, the results suggest that neighborhoods with more residents with disabilities have more green space. The air pollution and temperature findings are inconclusive at the national scale, indicating a need for further investigation in regions or cities. This study contributes much needed empirical insights on environmental exposures among disabled populations as part of a larger endeavor to better understand how changing environmental conditions impact marginalized communities.Keywords: Environmental justice, Air pollution, Temperature, Green space, Disability, Cluster analysis

Introduction

Since the 1980s, the environmental justice scholarship has generated increasing evidence and recognition of the unequal allocation of environmental hazards and benefits among different social groups in diverse regions of the world. In the United States, environmental justice research and policy have traditionally focused on how racialized minorities and low-income communities navigate higher levels of environmental hazards, and the consequent implications for public health, quality of life, and social equity (Holifield et al., 2017). In terms of environmental burdens, hazardous wastes and toxic air pollutants have been historically concentrated in predominantly Black and impoverished neighborhoods (Liu et al., 2021; Mohai et al., 2009). As for environmental benefits, majority White and wealthy neighborhoods have greater access to parks of better quality and to green spaces more broadly (Dai, 2011; Rigolon, 2016). Environmental racism and classism have predisposed marginalized communities – those who are positioned lower in the social hierarchy (Trudeau and McMorran, 2011) – to sub-standard living conditions and poor health outcomes while also reducing their access to health-enabling therapeutic spaces and resources (Beard et al., 2024).

In environmental justice, much less attention has been given to ecoableism, which is discrimination against and exclusion of disabled individuals in environmental spaces, planning, and policy; and there is a need to consider disparities in environmental exposures borne by disabled groups (Chakraborty and Koul, 2025). Disability comprises a diversity of physical and mental conditions that affect people’s response to environmental threats as well as their needs for environmental benefits. Furthermore, disability impacts where and how people live, the environments that they are exposed to, and the resources they have access to. While the research on environmental exposures and disability is limited and has mixed results, the existing evidence suggests that in certain geographies, there are environmental inequities between disabled and non-disabled populations. Disabled residents in many urban areas experience less access to residential green space (Wong et al., 2023). Neighborhoods with more disabled residents have a higher concentration of air pollution (Chakraborty, 2022) and heatwaves (Chakraborty, 2024).

There is a need for more consideration of and investigation into disability in environmental justice (Chakraborty and Koul, 2025). To date, previous environmental justice studies largely examined the spatial distributions of one environmental exposure. In research focusing on disability, green space, air quality, and temperature were analyzed independently (Chakraborty, 2022; Chakraborty, 2024; Wong et al., 2023). For green space, the study area was the contiguous United States, the time period was around the year 2010, and variables included Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) (annual average), tree canopy (annual average), total population with disability, and relevant sociodemographic characteristics (Wong et al., 2023). The main findings were that greener census tracts generally have a higher proportion of residents with disabilities; and that there were significant clusters of low green space and high disability in urban neighborhoods (Wong et al., 2023). For air quality, the study area was the contiguous United States, the time period was from 2011 to 2015, and variables included PM2.5 (annual average), total population with disability, and relevant sociodemographic characteristics (Chakraborty, 2022). One main finding was that tracts with a higher proportion of residents with disabilities had greater PM2.5 exposure (Chakraborty, 2022). For temperature, the study area was the U.S. South (16 states and Washington, D.C.), the time periods were 2005–2022 for heatwaves and 2017–2021 for population estimates, and the variables were heatwaves (total number), total population with disability, and pertinent sociodemographic characteristics (Chakraborty, 2024). One main finding was that tracts with more residents with disabilities had higher heatwave frequency (Chakraborty, 2024).

Our paper contributes to the existing research by investigating updated population-level disparities in concurrent exposures to green space, air pollution, and temperature during summer and winter seasons among disabled people in the contiguous United States. Given that all three are related – green spaces help regulate temperatures and enhance air quality (United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA), 2024) – this paper examines green space, air quality, and temperature in relation to each other and concurrently in space. This project also uses the latest available data in 2021 and is sensitive to time by considering seasonal differences. Prior research relied on annual averages of environmental exposures in the years 2010 (Wong et al., 2023) and 2015 (Chakraborty, 2022). The use of annual averages obscures seasonal extremes in summer and winter, and overlooks time-dependent interventions. Moreover, distinguishing summer from winter is important because both extreme hot and cold temperatures can impair human health and exacerbate pre-existing conditions (Seltenrich, 2015). Furthermore, our analysis also considers the social intersection of disability, race, and class. We continue the historical tradition in environmental justice scholarship of analyzing race and class, while also expanding our investigation to include disability. In summary, our project seeks to highlight disability in the intersectional conversation on environment and social equity, and to identify where and when interventions are most needed to improve environmental conditions among socially marginalized populations.

The eco-ableism scholarship largely focuses on recreational green spaces, such as parks. In the United States, many recreational green spaces are located far away from (Lasky et al., 2023) or are inaccessible to disabled visitors (Younker, 2010), despite the 1990 Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) that aims to safeguard individuals with disabilities from discrimination. In Denmark and England, more ‘natural’ spaces have uneven terrain that is physically difficult to navigate for those with mobility and vision disabilities (Corazon et al., 2019; Macpherson, 2017). In China and the United Kingdom, people with developmental and learning disabilities have felt excluded in green spaces due to off-putting sensory and social interactions (Li et al., 2019; Mathers, 2008). Like non-disabled populations, disabled people value green spaces for their therapeutic uses (Corazon et al., 2019; Macpherson, 2017; Mathers, 2008). Green spaces promote mental health as well as social interaction among disabled children and adults (Jakubec et al., 2016; Li et al., 2019). If people with disabilities are excluded from green spaces, then they are unable to access such therapeutic and social benefits that contribute to a better quality of life.

There is much less research on residential green spaces, the everyday green spaces that disabled people more routinely have access to. Green spaces offer shade and cool areas when it is hot outside, and filter polluted air (US EPA, 2024), thus reducing health risks from extreme heat and air pollution. Furthermore, people may experience indirect benefits from viewing green spaces from a window (Keniger et al., 2013). One previous study based in the United States on residential green space among disabled individuals found that while greener neighborhoods across the country have higher ratios of residents with disabilities, there are also hotspots in urban neighborhoods of low green space, high disability, and are largely socioeconomically disadvantaged (Wong et al., 2023). Another study focusing on the city of Los Angeles found that neighborhoods with more green space have a lower prevalence of disability (Xu and Wang, 2025).

Compared to green space, the research on outdoor air pollution and temperature among disabled individuals is relatively sparse. Studies find that disabled individuals have higher exposure to outdoor air pollutants compared to those without disabilities (Emerson et al., 2019). Neighborhoods in the United States with greater ratios of disabled residents have higher levels of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) (Chakraborty, 2022). This may be due to disabled residents living near pollution sources (Chakraborty, 2019; Chakraborty, 2020). In the United Kingdom, children with intellectual disabilities have a greater likelihood of residing in environments with high concentrations of multiple harmful air pollutants compared to children without intellectual disabilities (Emerson et al., 2019). Elevated exposures to air contaminants are linked to harmful health consequences, including disability progression (Weuve et al., 2016).

With outdoor temperature, both extreme heat and cold have harmful impacts on people with disabilities. Studies find that extreme heat exacerbates morbidity and mortality among disabled people (Jehn et al., 2013; Tian et al., 2021). Heat stress and acute temperature variability have been found to exacerbate chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and increase hospitalization rates in Germany and China, respectively (Jehn et al., 2013; Tian et al., 2021). A study on an ice storm in Canada found that the storm worsened people’s chronic illness and pain (Gigac et al., 2003). As for population-level research, one study found that neighborhoods with more disabled residents experience more heatwaves in the U.S. South (Chakraborty, 2024). To our knowledge, there are no studies on seasonal temperatures among people with disabilities in the contiguous United States.

Green space, air pollution, and heat are important considerations in the context of a changing climate and growing urbanization. In urban municipalities, green infrastructure is increasingly sought to reduce the impacts of and build resilience against climate change as green spaces regulate heat and humidity, and filter polluted air (US EPA, 2024). As such, environmental justice is linked to climate change, particularly in amplifying social inequities. People with disabilities are acknowledged as a group vulnerable to climate change (US EPA, 2024), yet there is relatively little research investigating the climate change experiences of disabled individuals (Stein et al., 2024) compared to people from other social axes of difference. A recent scoping review of climate change experiences among disabled individuals found that extreme weather events worsened people’s mental and physical health, disrupted children’s education, forced workers to lose or change employment, and decreased access to health and community services (Lindsay et al., 2022). The exposures examined in the 45 reviewed studies included, but were not limited to, hurricanes, extreme winter weather, extreme heat, floods, wildfires, dust storms, typhoons, and droughts (Lindsay et al., 2022).

Spotlighting disability is critical for recognizing and responding to eco-ableism in research and policy. This paper examines the seasonal distribution of green space, air quality, and temperature for individuals with disabilities in the contiguous United States, contributing to previous scholarship that investigated each environmental characteristic in isolation (e.g., Chakraborty, 2022; Chakraborty, 2024; Wong et al., 2023). The results will therefore offer a more comprehensive understanding of the natural environments where disabled people live, their everyday environmental exposures, and their risk from climate change impacts. We investigate residential environments as people with disabilities have an increased likelihood of allocating a greater proportion of their time within the immediate vicinity of their home (Myers and Ravesloot, 2016). There is also a need to use spatial analytic methods to identify localities for intervention. As such, we also utilize multivariate cluster detection to identify significant hot spots at the intersection of multiple environmental risks (low green space, high air pollution, high temperature) and social marginalization (high disability, low non-Hispanic White, low income). In doing so, this research reveals where environmental justice and climate change adaptation are most needed.

Materials & methods

Study area

The study area is the contiguous United States, which includes the lower 48 states and Washington, D.C. All census tracts in the contiguous United States across the rural-to-urban continuum were considered for data analysis. Over 329 million people reside in the contiguous United States (United States Census Bureau, 2024). Most inhabitants (80%) live in urban areas (United States Census Bureau, 2023). Nearly 13% live with a disability (Leppert and Schaeffer, 2023), compared to 16% globally (World Health Organization, 2023).

As a large region covering nearly eight million km2 of land, the contiguous United States has spatially heterogeneous environmental patterns. Compared to urban areas, rural regions have more abundant green spaces (Wong et al., 2023), cooler temperatures (US EPA, 2024), and lower concentrations of air pollutants (Kilpatrick et al., 2024). In recent decades, increased urbanization has reduced green spaces and increased impervious surface area, exacerbating the increased surface temperatures that result from climate change (US EPA, 2024). In contrast, air quality has largely improved since 1980, in part due to state and national regulations on emissions (US EPA 2025).

Data

We obtained and processed secondary demographic and environmental data dated around the year 2021. Data were summarized by census tracts, which are commonly used to assess contextual factors among populations (Wong et al., 2023). Each tract was categorized by one of the following Köppen-Geiger climate classes (Beck et al., 2018): temperate, tropical, arid, or cold. Our dataset includes 82,635 tracts after excluding tracts with 0 people and missing data.

Demographic data from the Census Bureau’s 2017–2021 American Community Survey (ACS) and 2020 decennial census were downloaded from the National Historical Geographic Information System (Manson et al., 2023). The decennial census asks a short set of questions that provides an official count of the population. The ACS asks a sample of the population a longer set of questions on topics not covered in the decennial census. The following ACS data were downloaded along with their margin of error: disability, race/ethnicity, household income, sex, age, and employment. Urban residence data came from the decennial census. The Census Bureau considers a person to have a disability if they have one or more of the following conditions: cognitive, hearing, ambulatory, vision, independent living, or self-care limitations. The ACS data have margins of error and to consider these errors in our analyses, we followed guidance from Jurjevich et al., 2018 to calculate the coefficient of variation (CV).

Environmental data were obtained from different sources. NDVI (250 m) came from the United States Geological Survey (n.d.). Using the population-weighted centroids of census tracts, daily temperature and PM2.5 predictions were generated with machine learning models (Just et al., 2025a; Just et al., 2025b). Temperature models included temperature observations from ground stations for training and cross-validation, and 13 variables as predictors, including remote sensing data (Just et al., 2025a). PM2.5 is fine inhalable particulate matter that is harmful to human health. PM2.5 models included PM2.5 observations from ground stations for training and cross-validation, and 22 predictors (Just et al., 2025b). Zonal statistics were used to compute mean NDVI during the summer and winter for each census tract (Wong et al., 2023). For the same summer and winter time periods, mean temperature and PM2.5 were calculated from daily predictions. Summer was defined as May to September 2021 and winter was defined as November 2020 to March 2021. Seasonal averages were calculated rather than an annual average to differentiate hot and cold months, and to distinguish areas that experience more extreme or moderate temperatures.

Methods

Data rescaling: NDVI, PM2.5, temperature

For NDVI, PM2.5, and temperature, the Empirical Cumulative Distribution Function (ECDF) was computed for tracts by Köppen-Geiger climate class (temperate, tropical, arid, or cold). This calculation re-scales values to range from 0 to 1 within each climate class so that values between climates are easier to compare. For instance, NDVI is generally higher in the temperate Southeast than in the arid Southwest. With the rescaling of the data, the same value in different climates (e.g., 0.5) is of equivalent rank.

Cluster analysis: Co-location multivariate local join count statistics

Using co-location multivariate local join count statistics in GeoDa (Anselin, 2019), we identified the co-occurrence of clusters of environmental risk (low NDVI, high PM2.5, high temperature in summer or low temperature in winter) and predominantly socially marginalized groups (high disability, low income, and low non-Hispanic White). Local join count statistics generate clusters of rare events when applied to the upper or lower quantile of variables (Anselin, 2019). Compared to more commonly used measures of local spatial autocorrelation that evaluate values around the mean, this statistic detects clusters of extreme values (Anselin, 2019). We sought to identify clusters where most residents experience higher social marginalization (disabled, low-income, and non-White) and greater environmental risks (low green space, high air pollution, and extreme heat or extreme cold). We coded the following observations as 1: fourth quartile for disability, first quartile for mean NDVI, fourth quartile for mean PM2.5, fourth quartile for mean temperature in summer or first quartile for mean temperature in winter, first quartile for median household income, and first quartile for non-Hispanic White. This statistic detects the co-location of multivariate observations with values of 1 adjacent to neighboring co-located observations coded as 1. Neighbors were defined using queen contiguity spatial weights. For the detection of statistically significant clusters, we used the following recommended parameters: pseudo p-value≤0.01 and 99,999 permutations (Anselin, 2019). We also conducted a sensitivity analysis to compare the cluster results using quartiles versus terciles, quintiles, and deciles.

Statistical analysis: Generalized additive models (GAMs)

To measure the correlation between proportion disability (outcome) and environmental and demographic covariates (predictors), we generated two Generalized Additive Models (GAMs). Disability prevalence was designated as the outcome variable because the analysis foregrounds the residential circumstances of people with disabilities, thus examining factors correlated with disability prevalence. Previous studies on green space and disability included disability prevalence as the outcome variable in statistical models (Wong et al., 2023; Xu and Wang, 2025). Research on the relationship between environmental exposures and health conditions modeled the health condition as the outcome (Gronlund et al., 2015; Son et al., 2020). Furthermore, studies that investigated multiple exposures included them as independent variables (Shi et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2022). Our GAMs therefore examine all three environmental exposures concurrently and simultaneously, and separate their independent associations with disability while holding the other exposures constant. One GAM includes environmental exposure covariates for the summer and the other GAM includes them for winter. The ECDF is calculated for the environmental exposures, specifically for mean NDVI, mean PM2.5, and mean temperature within their respective season. For both GAMs, the demographic covariates are: proportion age 65 years and older, proportion non-Hispanic white, proportion female, proportion employed, proportion urban residents, and median household income. Similar variables have been previously included in statistical analyses with disability (Wong et al., 2023).

Following guidance from Jurjevich et al. (2018), we excluded tracts with low reliability in any of the ACS variables (CV > 0.3). As a result, 22,394 tracts were excluded (27%). A total of 60,241 tracts were included in statistical analyses.

The GAMs were finalized after checks for high concurvity (≥0.80), which is akin to collinearity in linear relationships. To account for spatial dependence, the models initially included an interaction term for the latitude and longitude of the tract centroid. However, there was high concurvity with NDVI, indicating that NDVI largely captured the data’s spatial structure. Therefore, the interaction term was excluded.

Results

Clusters of disability & environmental exposures

Co-location multivariate local join count statistics were used to identify significant cases of high disability, low NDVI in summer or winter, high PM2.5 in summer or winter, high temperature in summer or low temperature in winter, low income, and low non-Hispanic White. The variables were coded as low or high based on whether they were in the upper or lower quartile. This clustering technique detected 78 significant (pseudo p-value≤0.01) clusters in the summer and 91 clusters in the winter (Fig. 1). The main difference is that summer clusters include significantly high temperatures while winter clusters include significantly low temperatures. While geographically dispersed, many of the clusters are concentrated in the Mid-Atlantic Region, which has a temperate climate with distinct summer and winter seasons. Approximately 36% of the summer clusters (n = 28) and 70% of the winter clusters (n = 64) are located in the Mid-Atlantic.

Table 1 provides descriptive statistics (median and standard deviation) on the environmental and demographic attributes of all census tracts considered in the study (n = 82,635) alongside the summer and winter clusters. When comparing median values, the environmental exposures are generally worse within the clusters relative to all census tracts. NDVI is lower in the clusters (0.39 in summer and 0.24–0.25 in winter) compared to all tracts (0.58 in summer and 0.39 in winter). NDVI values between 0.2 and 0.5 are considerate moderate levels of greenness and indicate shrubs, grasslands, and similar vegetation. Greater values (0.6 and higher) signify dense vegetative cover, such as tropical and temperate forests. PM2.5 is higher in the clusters (8.9–9.9 μg/m3) compared to all tracts (7.5–8.3 μg/m3). The regulatory National Ambient Air Quality Standard set in 2024 is an annual mean of 9.0 μg/m3, averaged over three years (United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA), 2025). In the clusters, summer temperatures are higher (23–24 °C), and winter temperatures are lower (5.5 °C) than in all tracts (22 °C in summer and 5.8 °C in winter).

We conducted a sensitivity analysis to compare cluster results using varying quantile thresholds: tercile, quartile, quintile, and decile. Tables S1 and S2 present the descriptive statistics for summer and winter clusters, respectively. Decile results are not included because the cluster analysis generated 0 clusters. Most quintile clusters were included in the quartile results, and most quartile clusters were included in the tercile results. This paper focuses on the quartile results because the clusters include predominantly socially marginalized populations with elevated environmental exposures and strike a good balance between the tercile results that generated hundreds of clusters and quintile results that detected very few clusters.

In assessing median values, there is generally a higher proportion of socially marginalized groups in the clusters compared to all census tracts (Table 1). Disability is much higher in the clusters (23%) relative to all tracts (12%). The clusters have lower proportions of non-Hispanic White (4–7% versus 68% in all tracts) and employed residents (87–90% versus 95% in all tracts); and median household income is nearly half that of all tracts ($31,667–$32,436 versus $65,780). To sum up, the clusters largely have much worse environmental exposures (low green space, high air pollution, high temperature in the summer or low temperature in the winter) coupled with a higher proportion of socially marginalized residents (low income, lower employment, more residents who are racialized minorities).

The clusters are located in or near 21 cities (Table 2). Cities with at least five significant census tracts in the summer include: San Antonio (n = 26), Philadelphia (n = 23), Houston (n = 7), Chicago (n = 6) and St. Louis (n = 6). Cities with at least five significant census tracts in the winter include: Philadelphia (n = 36), Baltimore (n = 14), Louisville (n = 12), Camden (n = 11), and Minneapolis (n = 5).

Philadelphia is notable for being the only city to have both summer (n = 23) and winter clusters (n = 36) (Table 2). These clusters are concentrated in North Philadelphia, with a handful of clusters in West Philadelphia (Fig. 2). Residents in these areas face considerable economic, environmental, and health challenges (Center of Excellence in Environmental Toxicology, n.d). Philadelphia has been previously identified as having significant clusters of high disability and low greenness in the year 2010 (Wong et al., 2023), and these findings reveal that these patterns have persisted nearly a decade later. Distributions of disability & environmental exposures

Disability quartiles

Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted to evaluate group differences between quartiles of disability. The results indicate that for all attributes, the population median for one quartile is significantly different (p < 0.001) from at least one other quartile (Table 3). Compared to the census tracts in the lowest quartile (≤8% disability), the tracts in the highest quartile (≥21% disability) are greener, have lower levels of air pollution, and are warmer. Disabled populations are concentrated in rural areas, many of which experience lower levels of air pollution relative to urban areas (Kilpatrick et al., 2024). Disability is also concentrated in the Southeast where it is relatively greener (Wong et al., 2023) and hotter (Chakraborty, 2024). In the summer, median NDVI is 0.64 in the highest quartile versus 0.58 in lowest quartile, median PM2.5 is 8.05 μg/m3 in the highest quartile versus 8.31 μg/m3 in the lowest quartile, and median temperature is 22.4 °C in the highest quartile versus 21.5 °C in the lowest quartile. In the winter, median NDVI is 0.43 in the highest quartile versus 0.39 in lowest quartile, median PM2.5 is 7.04 μg/m3 in the highest quartile versus 7.63 μg/m3 in the lowest quartile, and median temperature is 5.93 °C in the highest quartile versus 4.95 °C in the lowest quartile.

Population distributions

Two GAMs were produced to measure the correlation between disability prevalence (outcome) and environmental and demographic characteristics (covariates). One model includes the average environmental factors in the summer and the other includes mean environmental characteristics in the winter. For NDVI, PM2.5, and temperature, the ECDF of the tract-level mean was computed within each climate class. For ease of reference, ECDF values are described as mean NDVI, PM2.5, and temperature in the subsequent text. In the tables and figures, they will occasionally be described as quantiles by climate class.

For the GAM representing average environmental factors in the summer (Fig. 5), the trend line illustrates that tracts with more disabled residents generally have higher NDVI. As NDVI values get larger, there is a slight increase in disability prevalence. From 0 to 100% NDVI, disability increases 2.0%, with the remaining covariates at their mean values (Fig. 5A). The trend lines for PM2.5 (Fig. 5B) and temperature (Fig. 5C) are relatively flat. In other words, there is not a clear positive or negative relationship between disability and mean PM2.5 and temperature.

GAM results with proportion disability as outcome, mean environmental covariates in summer.

In comparison, the GAM characterizing mean environmental covariates in the winter (Fig. 6) portrays slightly different results from the summer. In the winter, as NDVI increases from 0 to 100, the trend line is relatively flat with a very small increase in disability (0.3%; Fig. 6A). In contrast, the increase in summer is 2.0% (Fig. 5A). The trend lines for winter PM2.5 (Fig. 6B) and temperature (Fig. 6C) are relatively flat, with a similar interpretation to summer PM2.5 and temperature. As PM2.5 and temperature increase from 0 to 100, there is a very small decrease in disability (both 0.5%).

GAM results with proportion disability as outcome, mean environmental covariates in winter.

The GAM results represent population averages, and the findings are both consistent and inconsistent with the existing evidence based in the United States. One similarity is with summer green space – prior research has indicated that disability prevalence and green space have a positive association (Wong et al., 2023). On air pollution and temperature, our findings differ from other studies. One study detected a positive correlation between disability and PM2.5 (Chakraborty, 2022) and another found a positive relationship between disability and heatwaves (Chakraborty, 2024). In contrast, our study finds that there is a relatively flat trend line between disability and mean PM2.5 and temperature, both in summer and winter.

For the demographic covariates in both GAMs (Fig. 3D–I & Fig. 4D–I), their associations are similar. Census tracts with higher disability prevalence also have a higher proportion of inhabitants who are non-Hispanic White (Fig. 3G & Fig. 4G), 65 years of age or older (Fig. 3D & Fig. 4D), and have lower income (Fig. 3F & Fig. 4F). These findings are consistent with patterns identified nearly a decade earlier (Wong et al., 2023).

Discussion

Our ecological study of the contiguous United States reveals a dual narrative of extremes and averages. In the case of extremes, multivariate cluster statistics uncover localities where disabled residents experience high social marginalization and environmental risk. These clusters are important to examine because these are neighborhoods and communities that would be otherwise overlooked had we only focused on averages alone. Furthermore, these clusters in summer and winter pinpoint exactly where and when interventions are most needed. Regarding averages, population distributions confirm that census tracts with more disabled residents are greener. These results are consistent with previous trends from nearly a decade earlier, which are in part attributable to disability prevalence being highest in rural areas, where land is less developed and greener (Wong et al., 2023). In contrast, the findings for air pollution and temperature are not substantial, illustrating that these environmental exposures have less clear relationships with disability at the scale of the contiguous United States and indicating a need for further investigation at regional and local scales.

For detecting extremes, we used co-location multivariate local join count statistics to identify cases of high disability, low NDVI, high PM2.5, high temperature in summer or low temperature in winter, low income, and low non-Hispanic White in 21 cities. Cities with at least five significant clusters in the summer include: San Antonio (n = 26), Philadelphia (n = 23), Houston (n = 7), Chicago (n = 6) and St. Louis (n = 6). Cities with at least five significant clusters in the winter include: Philadelphia (n = 36), Baltimore (n = 14), Louisville (n = 12), Camden (n = 11), and Minneapolis (n = 5).

Philadelphia is notable for being the only city to have both summer and winter clusters (23 and 36, respectively). Philadelphia has relatively new initiatives for climate resilience, including the 2021 Climate Action Playbook (Office of Sustainability, 2025) and 2023 Philly Tree Plan (Philadelphia Parks and Recreation, 2023). To reduce air pollution, goals include increasing green acreage and expanding and establishing an all-electric fleet for public transit by 2040. To address extreme heat, a cool pavement pilot project was launched in 2024 around Hunting Park Recreation Center, which is in North Philadelphia and is among the summer clusters detected in this study. These strategies should be expanded to all other clusters identified in this paper. Furthermore, while these plans outline climate resilience strategies that increase green space, reduce air pollution, and mitigate against extreme heat, there is no discussion about resilience against extreme cold. Consideration of extremely cold temperatures would inspire initiatives to construct indoor green spaces and greenhouses, and provide additional justification for tree plantings. These initiatives would improve air quality, buffer against wind in the winter, and provide protection against cold temperatures. In terms of long-term land use changes to motivate climate resilience, zoning codes could be changed to increase the proportions of open areas, specify planting types for open areas (e.g., trees, perennials), and reduce barriers to the creation and preservation of community gardens and open spaces.

Cities across the United States, in varying capacities, are looking to green spaces to counteract urban heat island impacts in addition to providing recreational areas for their residents. This study reveals that strategies to mitigate against environmental exposures in the winter are also needed. Our findings also complement and expand on the urban neighborhoods being targeted by city governments for intervention and improvement. In doing so, our intention is to motivate policymakers to consider the marginalization of people with disabilities and to include them as meaningful stakeholders so that a growing and vital constituent of the population can develop strategies to become increasingly resilient amid ongoing environmental and climate challenges.

The clusters identified in this paper are important case studies for future health research. People with disabilities (e.g., with autoimmune disease, decreased lung function, dementia) are more susceptible to heat, air contamination, and negative health outcomes, including death (National Council on Disability, 2023). For many, their susceptibility is linked to poor housing conditions, financial barriers to obtaining air conditioning and air filters, along with insufficient and inaccessible cooling centers (Vasquez, 2021). Mapping relevant data to account for such susceptibilities would identify key areas for intervention. Yet, governments often do not compile statistics and track disabled people before, during, and after weather disasters (Engelman et al., 2022). This project contributes to this effort by estimating and mapping environmental disparities among disabled populations.

For computing averages, we used multivariable GAMs to quantify the association between disability and environmental exposures, along with Kruskal-Wallis tests to measure the equality of distribution between quartiles of disability. We identified two distinct findings between the different methods. One is that summer NDVI increases with disability across census tracts. The other is that there is not a meaningfully clear positive nor negative relationship between disability and mean temperature and PM2.5.

Previous studies based in the United States have found that neighborhoods with more disabled residents have higher levels of PM2.5 (Chakraborty, 2022) and experience more heatwaves (Chakraborty, 2024). These findings differ from ours, but they also utilize different data, methods, and times. Additionally, one study focused on the U.S. South (Chakraborty, 2024) whereas our project’s geographic coverage spans the contiguous United States. Given the sparse research and our mixed findings, additional investigation is needed. Future studies should focus on specific regions or cities and conduct sensitivity analyses using different data sources for the same environmental exposure.

While this study has generated new empirical insights on disability and environmental exposures, it has a few limitations. First, we focus on environmental exposures based on residential location. While this may be where many disabled people spend much of their time, some individuals may spend a portion of their time elsewhere for work, leisure, or other purposes. Further inquiry is needed on how activity spaces influence personal exposure to environmental factors. Second, we include all individuals with disabilities. Additional investigation is needed to identify differential exposure based on disability type, as people with physical disabilities may have different experiences than those with cognitive impairments. At the same time, the ACS data have varying degrees of error. To explore this direction, census geographies may need to be aggregated from tracts to counties (Jurjevich et al., 2018). Third, the data we used likely underestimates disability prevalence as the Census Bureau measures disability among the noninstitutionalized population and individuals with disabilities are a hard-to-reach demographic. Lastly, our data and analyses only reveal cross-sectional trends and correlations in the year 2021. Future studies should compare trends between different time periods or leverage longitudinal designs to better understand changes and causal relationships over time.

This paper contributes much needed investigation into disability and environmental justice. In environmental justice research and policy, eco-ableism and consideration of disabled groups in environmental injustices are noticeably absent (Chakraborty and Koul, 2025). This study adds to the sparse and mixed evidence by identifying specific geographies and seasons in the contiguous United States where disabled communities are experiencing serious social and environmental inequities. Given the diminishing availability of green space throughout the country, heat waves tied to climate change, and worsening air quality in many places, there is a growing imperative to analyze and gain insight into how these changing environmental conditions impact disabled individuals and other disadvantaged groups. Including and centering disability in environmental justice will generate more inclusive practices, policies, and spaces that improve the well-being and resilience of marginalized communities.
