Revisiting the impact of cathepsin Z in prostate cancer: concerns and insights
Authors: Zhiguo Zhu, Huisheng Qin, Zesong Jiang, Zhonghai Li
Published: 2025

Dear editor,

I read Saipeng Chen et al.’s study [1] with great interest. In this study, authors identified Cathepsin Z (CTSZ) as a key cathepsin linked to prostate cancer (PCa) progression through bioinformatics analysis. Interesting, CTSZ overexpression dose not directly facilitate invasion/migration capacities of PCa cells in Transwell assays. They found that CTSZ promotes PCa metastasis by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through the TRA2A/IL-32/ITGA5 axis, thereby inducing M2 macrophage infiltration. The study also proposes a promising therapeutic strategy, showing that blockade of ITGA5 suppresses metastatic progression and reprograms the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Despite the strengths of this study, several underlying concerns require further elucidation.

Firstly, the identification of molecular drivers of PCa metastasis is based on three GEO datasets; however, the absence of patient demographic and clinical information, particularly in GSE32269 and GSE101607, limits the clinical interpretation of the results. Providing a clearer clinical description of the cohorts would strengthen the relevance of the findings, for example, specifying whether the patients had hormone-sensitive PCa (HSPC) or castration-resistant PCa (CRPC) and whether the samples were derived from prostate or metastatic site (bone, lung, or brain). GSE32269 includes 22 primary PCa (HSPC) and 29 bone metastatic PCa (CRPC). Bone metastatic CRPC samples were obtained through CT-guided bone marrow biopsies and were confirmed by microscopic examination to primarily consist of tumor tissue. Primary samples were extracted from frozen biopsy samples using laser capture microdissection. However, all 48 samples in GSE101607 were derived from spinal metastases (40 CRPC and 8 untreated). Therefore, the group comparison in GSE32269 corresponds to “primary HSPC versus bone metastatic CRPC”, whereas in GSE101607 it corresponds to “untreated versus CRPC”. This grouping strategy appears somewhat difficult to reconcile with the stated analytical objectives. Nevertheless, it does not diminish the important role of CTSZ.

Secondly, the authors demonstrated, using both tumor tissues and PCa cell lines, that CTSZ overexpression is associated with metastasis (Fig. 1A–D). They further analyzed TCGA-PRAD data and found that CTSZ overexpression correlates with lymph node metastasis (Normal versus N1), higher Gleason scores (Normal versus Gleason score 10), and reduced DFS (HR = 2.7) (Fig. 1E, F, I). However, the expression pattern of CTSZ in PCa tissues compared with normal prostate tissues was not described. Although the study primarily focuses on the role of CTSZ in PCa metastasis, providing a comprehensive overview of its expression profile would enhance our understanding of its functional significance. Using the UALCAN database employed in this study, we found no significant difference in CTSZ expression between PCa tissues and adjacent normal tissues. We believe it would be interesting to include this result in the discussion.

In conclusion, this study identifies CTSZ as a key driver of prostate cancer metastasis via the TRA2A/IL-32/ITGA5 axis by driving M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages recruitment. The results suggest that ITGA5 blockade could be a promising therapeutic strategy, offering valuable insights for future research into PCa metastasis and immune interactions. We raise some concerns for improvement in the study to help readers better understand the findings.
