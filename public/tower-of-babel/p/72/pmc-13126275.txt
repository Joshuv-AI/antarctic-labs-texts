Multidisciplinary approaches to coexisting Glanzmann thrombasthenia and juvenile polyposis syndrome
Authors: Sondus Alsharidah, Eman Almatter, Hana' Nofal, Maha Bourusly, Mohammad Obaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlanzmann thrombasthenia (GT) is a rare autosomal recessive bleeding disorder from defects in the αIIbβ3 integrin receptor, impairing platelet aggregation and often overlooked due to normal platelet counts. Juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS) is an autosomal dominant disorder with multiple gastrointestinal polyps and increased colorectal cancer risk. Their co-occurrence is exceedingly rare and challenging. We report a 19-year-old male who was diagnosed with GT at age 5 after rectal bleeding from a gastrointestinal polyp requiring polypectomy and transfusions; platelet aggregation studies confirmed GT. At age 9, genetic testing established JPS. In 2014 he developed recurrent severe bleeding—hematochezia, melena, and hemarthrosis—managed with recombinant activated factor VII (rFVIIa), blood transfusions, and supportive care including vitamin D. Additional findings included duodenal lymphangioma requiring resection, vitamin D deficiency with bone pain and complex regional pain syndrome, and chronic idiopathic hypertension treated with antihypertensives. This case highlights rFVIIa effectiveness and the need for multidisciplinary, long-term follow-up and care.Keywords: Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Juvenile polyposis syndrome, Bleeding disorders, Multidisciplinary care, VII (rFVIIa)

Introduction

Glanzmann thrombasthenia (GT) is a rare autosomal recessive bleeding disorder of the megakaryocyte lineage, caused by defects in the ITGA2B or ITGB3 genes which encode the αIIbβ3 integrin receptor [1]. This receptor is essential for platelet aggregation, and its dysfunction leads to impaired platelet function despite a normal platelet count and morphology [[1], [2], [3]]. Common manifestations of GT include mucocutaneous bleeding, such as epistaxis, gum bleeding, and menorrhagia, while severe bleeding episodes, such as large muscle hematomas and hemarthrosis, are less frequently observed [[4], [5], [6]].

Juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS), on the other hand, is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by the presence of multiple juvenile polyps in the gastrointestinal tract, which pose a significant risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and an increased likelihood of colorectal cancer development [7]. JPS is most commonly associated with mutations in the SMAD4 and BMPR1A genes, which are involved in the TGF-β signaling pathway and play a crucial role in gastrointestinal tract development and cellular regulation [7].

To date, there are no studies proving a common underlying etiology for GT and JPS, so they may be considered distinct disorders. To the best of our knowledge, no previous cases describing the co-occurrence of GT and JPS have been reported in the literature, highlighting the exceptional rarity of this case. This case report highlights the diagnostic challenges, clinical management complexities, and therapeutic interventions required for a 19-year-old man with a 14-year history of GT, who later developed concurrent JPS. Understanding the interplay between these two rare conditions provides valuable insights into patient care. While the co-occurrence has not been previously reported, existing literature emphasizes the complexity of managing GT [5,6], the surveillance needs in JPS [7], and underscores the need for multidisciplinary management approach in rare bleeding disorders [5,8].

Case report

A 19-year-old male, diagnosed with GT at 5 years of age, presented with rectal bleeding and pallor suggestive of anemia.

He was born at full term by normal vaginal delivery, achieved normal developmental milestones, and followed the recommended vaccination schedule. Family history revealed that one sibling had been diagnosed with GT at 8 months of age. The patient’s parents were not affected by any bleeding disorder. There was no other significant family history of bleeding diathesis, clotting disorders, or recurrent blood transfusions, and no history of consanguinity.

His symptoms first came to medical attention at 5 years of age, when rectal bleeding was attributed to a gastrointestinal polyp. Emergency investigations identified the polyp, which was excised by polypectomy. He received blood and platelet transfusions, and a nuclear scan ruled out Meckel’s diverticulum.

Further evaluation was prompted by a history of significant bruising after intramuscular vaccinations, which had worsened with age. Platelet aggregation studies showed normal aggregation in response to high ristocetin concentrations but markedly reduced aggregation in response to collagen, ADP, and epinephrine, confirming the diagnosis of GT at 5 years of age.

At 9 years of age, he was diagnosed with juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS). Since then, he has remained under regular follow-up with the Gastroenterology Department and has undergone periodic upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy with polypectomies as needed.

The lesion was subsequently surgically resected with appropriate peri-procedural monitoring given the patient’s underlying Glanzmann thrombasthenia and increased risk of bleeding. Current status

At 19 years of age, recent laboratory investigations showed a normal complete blood count, including hemoglobin of 145 g/L and platelet count of 370 × 10⁹/L. He is currently stable and adheres to regular follow-up. Current recommendations include periodic upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy, continuation of antihypertensive treatment, and regular monitoring of vitamin D levels with supplementation as needed.

This case highlights the challenges of managing GT, particularly when compounded by JPS and other comorbidities, and emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to care.

Discussion

This case illustrates the unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with managing GT complicated by JPS. The co-occurrence of these rare conditions creates a complex clinical scenario that necessitates individualized, multidisciplinary management strategies.

Glanzmann thrombasthenia

GT is a rare autosomal recessive platelet disorder caused by mutations in ITGA2B or ITGB3 genes, which encode the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa complex critical for platelet aggregation [1,2]. The clinical manifestations predominantly include mucocutaneous bleeding such as purpura, epistaxis, and gingival bleeding, with occasional severe bleeding episodes, including hemarthrosis or gastrointestinal hemorrhage [[4], [5], [6]]. The diagnosis of GT is established by abnormal platelet aggregation studies showing defective aggregation in response to ADP, collagen, and epinephrine while maintaining normal aggregation with ristocetin, as seen in this patient [5].

Management of bleeding episodes in GT often involves platelet transfusions and antifibrinolytics. However, repeated exposure to platelet transfusions carries a risk of alloimmunization [5,6].

VII (rFVIIa) has emerged as an effective alternative for managing severe bleeding in GT, with a favorable safety profile [8,9]. In this case, rFVIIa was consistently used to manage episodes of hematochezia, melena, and hemarthrosis, demonstrating its efficacy in controlling severe bleeding when conventional treatments, including platelet transfusions, were inadequate.

Juvenile polyposis syndrome

JPS is a distinct autosomal dominant condition characterized by multiple juvenile polyps throughout the gastrointestinal tract, often presenting with rectal bleeding and anemia [7]. While JPS primarily increases the risk of colorectal cancer, it also poses challenges in patients with concurrent bleeding disorders such as GT. For this patient, the initial presentation of rectal bleeding at the age of 5 led to the diagnosis of a gastrointestinal polyp, which was managed with polypectomy. Periodic surveillance via endoscopy and colonoscopy is critical in JPS to monitor for new polyps and prevent complications such as obstruction or malignancy [7].

Unique challenges in co-occurrence

The coexistence of GT and JPS amplifies bleeding risks, complicating the management of gastrointestinal polyps and requiring precise coordination between hematology and gastroenterology teams [5,8]. In this case, the need for regular endoscopic surveillance and occasional surgical interventions introduced significant bleeding risks. The use of rFVIIa before and after procedures helped mitigate these effectively.

This patient’s history of recurrent severe bleeding episodes also highlights the importance of addressing associated complications, such as vitamin D deficiency and complex regional pain syndrome [7]. The occurrence of hypertension adds another layer of complexity, necessitating ongoing antihypertensive therapy.

Management and future directions

The management of this patient underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach involving hematologists, gastroenterologists, and other specialists. Long-term follow-up, routine endoscopic surveillance, and individualized bleeding management strategies are essential.

In addition to supportive and pharmacologic therapies, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) has been reported as a potential curative option for patients with severe or refractory GT. However, its use is limited by significant risks, including graft-versus-host disease, transplant-related morbidity, and mortality, and is therefore reserved for carefully selected cases.

Emerging treatments, including gene therapy and alternative hemostatic agents, may offer improved outcomes for GT patients, particularly those with complex comorbidities such as JPS [5,9].

Conclusion

The coexistence of GT and JPS is exceptionally rare and presents notable diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case emphasizes the critical role of multidisciplinary care, periodic monitoring, and tailored therapeutic strategies in managing patients with overlapping rare disorders. The effectiveness of rFVIIa in controlling severe bleeding as well as the importance of vigilance for complications reinforces the need for comprehensive and coordinated management plans.
