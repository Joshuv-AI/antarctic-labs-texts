Automated generation of structured breast ultrasound reports using BreastViT and ChatGPT

Published: 2026

Background

Breast cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors in women, with its incidence steadily rising in recent years [1]. As a safe and non-invasive imaging diagnostic tool, breast ultrasound plays a crucial role in the early screening and assessment of breast lesions [2]. This is particularly important for Asian women, in whom breast ultrasound is widely utilized due to population-specific characteristics such as higher breast density and younger age at onset [3].

The Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS), developed by the American College of Radiology (ACR), provides a standardized grading system for breast imaging results, ensuring each report contains clear grading conclusions and subsequent management recommendations [4]. However, traditional radiology reports are often written in free-text format, leading to inconsistencies in expression and reduced clinical communication efficiency. Despite the availability of BI-RADS, narrative ultrasound reports still vary considerably among radiologists and may omit critical imaging details, thereby motivating the development of automated, standardized report generation approaches.

In recent years, significant advancements have been made in the field of medical image analysis, particularly with artificial intelligence (AI) technologies [5]. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have opened possibilities for addressing these reporting challenges. Large language models (LLMs), such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT, have demonstrated outstanding performance in natural language generation tasks by leveraging vast amounts of training data, and can automatically generate high-quality text [6]. Some studies have explored the application of ChatGPT in generating radiology reports, indicating its advantages in report structuring and terminology usage, with generated reports being logically clear and fluently expressed [7]. However, LLMs still have limitations in the medical domain: performance in BI-RADS classification remains unstable, and more critically, LLMs may suffer from “hallucination” phenomena, where the generated diagnoses do not correspond with the actual images, which poses a clinical risk [8, 9]. Given that BI-RADS 4B-5 lesions pose the greatest diagnostic challenges and clinical uncertainty (intermediate-to-high malignancy risk, 10–95%), we focused on these categories to develop and validate our approach.

Therefore, this study proposes an automated method for generating structured breast ultrasound reports, combining image-text multimodal models with GPT-4o (OpenAI’s GPT-4o with multimodal capabilities, also referred to as ChatGPT in this study). To our knowledge, this is the one of the first studies to integrate a Vision Transformer (ViT)-based image encoder with a state-of-the-art large language model to generate structured ultrasound reports. First, ultrasound images are input into a Transformer-based multimodal model (BreastViT, Breast Vision Transformer, our fine-tuned VisionEncoderDecoderModel) to generate preliminary text. This initial text is then synchronized with the ultrasound image and fed into GPT-4o for linguistic refinement and structural processing: GPT-4o is responsible for terminology standardization, paragraph reorganization, and filling in missing clinical expressions without altering the key information corresponding to the original image. The study design includes both internal training validation and external independent validation, aiming to compare the differences between three approaches—“model-generated,” “model-generated + GPT-4o optimization,” and “GPT-4o direct output”—in terms of report accuracy, structural integrity, and terminology standardization, and to assess their clinical applicability and potential limitations.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This study is a retrospective diagnostic study conducted at Peking University Third Hospital. All data involved have been de-identified. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our institution, and informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

The overall research workflow is organized into three primary phases:

First, in the data preparation phase, ultrasound images of BI-RADS 4B, 4C, and 5 lesions and their corresponding reports were collected, followed by de-identification and translation.

Second, in the model development phase, the BreastViT image-to-text model was developed, incorporating GPT-4o for post-processing and report refinement.

Finally, in the evaluation phase, the proposed method was compared against two baseline models and reports generated by GPT-4o alone. Three senior radiologists performed independent clinical ratings under blinded conditions using a 10-point Likert scale, and GradCAM was utilized for interpretability analysis. Data collection and inclusion/exclusion criteria

In terms of case selection, this study specifically focused on BI-RADS 4B, 4C, and 5 lesions, which typically represent medium to high-risk malignant tumors. The main reason for selecting these cases is that these lesions have relatively high malignancy risks, especially 4B and 4C (malignancy risk between 10% and 50%), and 5 (malignancy risk greater than 95%). Therefore, they are crucial for early diagnosis and treatment decisions. Additionally, the imaging characteristics of these high-risk lesions are complex and varied, making them ideal for testing and validating the performance of the automated report generation system, especially in terms of detailed accuracy and structured report generation. By focusing on these clinically challenging cases, we can improve the model’s performance in real clinical applications, providing more valuable automated reports for radiologists. Furthermore, selecting 4B, 4C, and 5 lesions avoids interference from lower-risk categories (e.g., BI-RADS 1–3), enabling the study results to better concentrate on the accurate diagnosis of high-risk malignant lesions, thereby enhancing the clinical applicability and practicality of the model.

Given that BI-RADS 1–4A lesions generally have low risk and distinct features, and the focus of this study is on undiagnosed malignant masses rather than already diagnosed BI-RADS 6 lesions, we retrospectively collected data from patients diagnosed with BI-RADS 4B, 4C, and 5 breast masses through ultrasound from January 2019 to December 2024. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. A total of 362 patients were included, with 2,243 breast ultrasound images and their corresponding expert diagnostic reports. The dataset was randomly split into training (80%, 290 patients, 1,794 images) and internal test sets (20%, 72 patients, 449 images) at the patient level to prevent data leakage. Additionally, 49 cases collected from October to December 2024 were reserved as an independent external test set. For each patient, a single representative ultrasound image showing the lesion was selected for model training and evaluation. All cases included key information such as lesion size, echogenic characteristics, boundary conditions, and BI-RADS category. The textual reports were written by experienced clinical radiologists.

Data preprocessing

The original ultrasound reports were first desensitized by removing identifiable information such as patient names, ID numbers, and specific dates. Considering that the pre-trained model nlpconnect/vit-gpt2-image-captioning was trained on large-scale English image-text datasets, we translated the original Chinese ultrasound reports into English to ensure linguistic consistency and maximize the transfer learning effect. The translation process included: (1) initial conversion using a translation engine (DeepL), and (3) manual proofreading and terminology standardization by ultrasound imaging experts. The final English report text was then used as the training label input for the model.

Model architecture and training

The core methodology of this study is based on the Hugging Face VisionEncoderDecoderModel framework, initialized with the pre-trained nlpconnect/vit-gpt2-image-captioning weights. The model architecture integrates a Vision Transformer (ViT) as the visual encoder and a GPT-2 as the text decoder [10]. To fully adapt the model to the domain-specific semantic features of breast ultrasound, we conducted end-to-end fine-tuning on our dataset. Specifically, both the ViT encoder and the GPT-2 decoder were updated simultaneously during the training process, with no layers being frozen. This approach ensures that the visual encoder can learn to extract specialized diagnostic features from ultrasound image patches, while the decoder learns to generate corresponding medical terminology. The tokenizer was initialized with GPT-2 vocabulary and modified to include a dedicated padding token (pad_token_id = 50257) to avoid conflicts with the end-of-sequence token. Training was performed with the AdamW optimizer (learning rate = 5 × 10⁻⁵, no weight decay), batch size of 8, and for 30 epochs. Cross-validation was performed with five folds to ensure robust estimation of model performance. To minimize random variation, random seeds were fixed (seed = 5), and training was conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 (Ventus 2X, 12GB VRAM) with CUDA acceleration. Performance was evaluated using loss values and BLEU scores on both training and validation datasets after each epoch. The model achieving the best validation BLEU score or lowest validation loss was retained for final testing. The best-performing fold (fold 4) achieved a validation BLEU of 0.9187 at epoch 17 and was selected as the final model for external testing.

We fine-tuned this ViT + GPT-2 architecture on the collected domain-specific dataset, enabling it to output original report texts highly relevant to the semantic content of breast ultrasound images. To evaluate the performance of this method, we selected three commonly used image-to-text generation models as baselines for comparison: R2Gen: A CNN-Transformer model using ResNet-152 for visual extraction and a Transformer decoder for memory-driven generation [11]. CNN-Attention-LSTM: A model integrating an attention mechanism to focus on specific image regions during text generation [12]. CNN-RNN: A baseline architecture utilizing global average pooling followed by a single-layer RNN [13]. For fair comparison, all models were trained using the AdamW optimizer with a consistent batch size of 16. To account for the varying convergence rates of different architectures, the maximum number of training epochs was set to 30 for BreastViT, 60 for R2Gen, and 80 for both CNN-Attention-LSTM and CNN-RNN. An early stopping mechanism was implemented, where training would terminate if the validation loss failed to improve for 5 consecutive epochs to prevent overfitting. The initial learning rate was set at 1*10− 4 for all experiments, and the model checkpoint achieving the highest BLEU score on the internal validation set was retained for final evaluation. GPT-4o optimization

The preliminary reports generated by BreastViT were subsequently refined by GPT-4o (Fig. 2). To facilitate reproducibility, the core prompting strategy and implementation pipeline are visually detailed in Fig. 2. Specifically, the system prompt employs a multi-tiered architecture consisting of: (1) Persona Setting, which designates the LLM as an expert breast radiologist; (2) Task Definition, outlining constraints for text structuring and grammar correction; and (3) a Standard BI-RADS Template to govern output formatting. Key inference parameters were fixed at a temperature of 0.1 and a top_p of 0.95 to suppress stochastic variations and ensure high text consistency and deterministic medical output. For example, by comparing the image and report content, GPT-4o can add specific imaging feature descriptions to the report, combine simple sentences into coherent paragraphs, automatically include grading conclusions and diagnostic recommendations, and replace colloquial terms with standardized medical terminology (e.g., changing “edges relatively smooth” to “smooth borders”). This step does not alter the medical information extracted from the original text but optimizes its expression, resulting in a final report that is clear in structure and meets clinical requirements.

Evaluation framework

We selected an independent validation set consisting of 49 new BI-RADS 4B–5 breast ultrasound images from October to December 2024, which were not included in the model training. Three types of reports were generated for each case: (1) GPT-4o direct output report: The ultrasound images were directly input into GPT-4o (using the multimodal GPT-4o Vision API, accessed in December 2024) to generate report text; (2) Model-generated report: The preliminary report output directly from the deep learning model; (3) Model + GPT optimization report: The model-generated report, combined with ultrasound images, was further refined using GPT-4o. The information extraction process for all three report types was identical. Three senior ultrasound radiologists (with 8, 12, and 15 years of experience, respectively) then performed a blinded evaluation of the three report versions independently. To ensure objectivity and minimize bias, the three radiologists were blinded to the source of the generated reports. Furthermore, the presentation order of the three report versions for each case was randomized during the evaluation process. Each radiologist assessed clinical expression accuracy (consistency with imaging features), information completeness, structural integrity (whether it included standard paragraphs), and terminology standardization using a 10-point Likert scale (1 = poor, 10 = excellent). For each criterion, the average score across three radiologists was calculated. Evaluation criteria included accurate description of lesion characteristics, report logic, and use of standardized terminology.

Statistical analysis

Model performance was evaluated using BLEU scores and cross-entropy loss on training and validation sets. For external validation, three senior radiologists (8, 12, and 15 years of experience) independently rated 49 cases using a 10-point Likert scale across four criteria: clinical accuracy, information completeness, structural integrity, and terminology standardization. Given that the ratings were ordinal, paired, and derived from three related samples, we employed the Friedman test (non-parametric alternative to repeated measures ANOVA) to assess overall differences among the three report generation methods. When significant differences were detected (p < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Inter-rater reliability among the three radiologists was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement (ICC (2, k)). ICC values were interpreted as follows: < 0.5 (poor), 0.5–0.75 (moderate), 0.75–0.9 (good), > 0.9 (excellent). Descriptive statistics for Likert scores are reported as mean ± standard deviation. GradCAM localization accuracy was calculated as the proportion of cases where the heatmap overlapped with the true lesion location confirmed by radiologists. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.3) with SciPy (version 1.14.1), Pingouin (version 0.6.0), Pandas (version 2.2.3) and NumPy (version 1.26.4) libraries. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

GradCAM for model interpretability

To enhance the interpretability of the model, we employed GradCAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) to visualize the regions of interest (ROIs) that the model focused on when making predictions. For the image-to-text framework in this study, GradCAM was applied to the final normalization layer of the Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder. The gradients were computed based on the BI-RADS categories predicted by the auxiliary classification head, serving as a proxy target for the model’s diagnostic focus. Visualized heatmaps were generated for all 49 cases in the independent external test set. This analysis aims to confirm that the model’s automated reports are grounded in the lesion features of the ultrasound images rather than non-diagnostic background information.

Results

Model training results

BreastViT demonstrated excellent convergence and generalization during fine-tuning. Tables 1 and 2 summarize the performance of the four models on the training and test sets, comparing BLEU scores and loss values. In the 4th fold of 5-fold cross-validation, the best BreastViT model was obtained at the 17th epoch with a BLEU score of 0.9187 and validation loss of 0.1277 (Fig. 3A). Among the baselines, R2Gen showed the fastest loss reduction, stabilizing after ~ 60 epochs with a test BLEU of 0.7514 (Fig. 3B). CNN-Attention-LSTM [12] and CNN-RNN achieved BLEU scores of 0.7387 and 0.7872 after 80 epochs (Fig. 3C, D). R2Gen reduced loss early and generated more coherent text, while CNN-RNN tended to omit or repeat information, indicating the Transformer’s stronger image–text alignment.

In the final evaluation on the internal test set, BreastViT achieved a BLEU-4 score of 0.9187 and a loss of 0.1277, outperforming all baseline models. This confirms the advantage of the VisionEncoderDecoderModel (ViT encoder + GPT-2 decoder) in generating accurate, structured, and semantically consistent breast ultrasound reports. It is noteworthy that although R2Gen, CNN-Attention-LSTM, and CNN-RNN all achieved lower training losses than BreastViT, BreastViT yielded the minimum loss and the highest BLEU-4 score on the test set. This discrepancy indicates that the three baseline models experienced overfitting during the training process. In contrast, BreastViT, which is based on the Transformer architecture, maintained a superior balance between training loss and generation quality, demonstrating stronger generalization robustness. This characteristic is a key factor contributing to its superior performance on the independent test set.

GPT-4o optimization effects

After inputting the report text generated by the deep learning model into GPT-4o, the quality of the language and structural integrity of the reports improved markedly. GPT-4o was able to combine previously fragmented short sentences into logically cohesive paragraphs, automatically generating sections like “Impression/Diagnosis Summary” and suggested follow-up examinations or management plans. For example, reports optimized by GPT-4o often provided the BI-RADS category and recommendations clearly at the end, while the earlier sections described lesion characteristics in segments such as “History,” “Imaging Findings,” and “Impression.” In contrast, the original model texts were often fragmented lists of features. In terms of terminology, GPT-4o favored using standardized medical terms. Although the model training was conducted using English-translated reports to leverage the pretrained model, the GPT-4o post-processing was performed on the original Chinese reports to preserve clinical authenticity. For example, GPT-4o replaced colloquial expressions such as “边界比较光滑” (edges relatively smooth) with standardized terms like “边界光滑” (smooth borders), and “内部回声还行” (internal echoes are okay) with “内部回声均匀” (internal echoes are homogeneous). These improvements made the reports more standardized and readable.

External validation

To further evaluate the generalizability and robustness of BreastViT in a real-world clinical setting, we performed an independent external validation on 49 additional cases. In the report generation experiment for 49 independent cases, the outputs of the three methods were compared. GPT-4o direct output reports were fluent and natural, but reviewers found they occasionally omitted certain lesion descriptions or made unnecessary inferences. For example, in one case, the GPT-4o report did not mention a small anechoic area within the breast or mistakenly described it as a cyst. BreastViT-generated text accurately covered main features such as lesion size, echogenicity, and morphology but lacked consistent structure and smooth sentence transitions. Reports generated by combining BreastViT with GPT-4o retained the strengths of both: accurately describing imaging features while presenting a complete structure and standardized expression.

In the blinded radiologist evaluation, the “BreastViT + GPT” approach achieved the highest average scores across all criteria: clinical accuracy (7.31 ± 0.94), information completeness (7.61 ± 0.66), structural integrity (8.03 ± 0.93), and terminology standardization (8.02 ± 0.98). GPT-4o direct output scored 5.87 ± 2.10, 7.31 ± 1.09, 7.63 ± 0.83, and 7.73 ± 0.94 respectively. The BreastViT approach scored 6.78 ± 1.31, 6.29 ± 0.99, 5.82 ± 0.76, and 7.17 ± 0.86, respectively. Furthermore, the BreastViT + GPT-4o pipeline yielded the highest NLP metric scores, achieving a ROUGE-L of 0.4106 and a BERT-Score of 0.7650, which aligns with the performance observed in the radiologist assessment (Table 3). The differences between the three methods were statistically significant for all criteria (Friedman test, χ² = 11.98–71.19, all p ≤ 0.003), with post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Dunn’s test) confirming that BreastViT + GPT-4o significantly outperformed BreastViT Alone in all criteria p < 0.05. However, BreastViT + GPT-4o significantly outperformed GPT-4o Alone only in Clinical Accuracy (p < 0.05), while achieving comparable performance in the other three dimensions. For example, in the “BreastViT + GPT” reports, all cases included “Findings” and “Impression” sections and used standard terms such as “echo enhancement,” “smooth borders,” and “homogeneous internal echoes.” In contrast, GPT-4o alone omitted echogenic features in 5 of 49 cases (10.2%), and the BreastViT-only reports contained disconnected sentences in 31 of 49 cases (63.3%).

Model interpretability

To enhance the interpretability of the BreastViT, we employed GradCAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) to visualize the regions of interest the model focused on when making predictions. GradCAM was applied to the Vision Transformer encoder by computing gradients of the generated text tokens with respect to the final attention layer, producing heatmaps indicating which image regions most influenced the text generation. In the external validation set of 49 cases, GradCAM-generated heatmaps clearly highlighted the areas around breast tumors, providing a visual understanding of the BreastViT’s decision-making process (Fig. 4). These heatmaps revealed that the model concentrated on key features such as tumor boundaries and internal echoes, which are critical for malignancy assessment. In 45 of 49 cases (92%), the highlighted regions corresponded well with the true lesion locations as confirmed by radiologists. The ability to visualize and interpret the model’s attention helps validate the model’s diagnostic reasoning, offering greater confidence in its clinical utility. This analysis also helps address the common concern of “black-box” models in medical imaging by providing more transparency in how the model arrives at its conclusions.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that combining a Vision Transformer-based image captioning model (BreastViT) with GPT-4o yields structured, high-quality ultrasound reports that outperform reports generated by deep learning alone or by GPT-4o alone. The BreastViT + GPT-4o approach achieved the highest radiologist ratings for clinical accuracy (7.31 ± 0.94), information completeness (7.61 ± 0.66), structural integrity (8.03 ± 0.93), and terminology standardization 8.02 ± 0.98) in blinded evaluation of 49 external validation cases. These findings demonstrate that a hybrid AI approach can generate clinically compliant breast ultrasound reports for BI-RADS 4B-5 lesions with high inter-rater reliability.

The technical performance of BreastViT substantiates the superiority of Vision Transformer architectures over conventional CNN-based methods for medical report generation. BreastViT achieved a validation BLEU-4 score of 0.9187, substantially outperforming three baseline models: R2Gen (0.7514), CNN-Attention-LSTM (0.7387), and CNN-RNN (0.7872). This advantage stems from the self-attention mechanism in Vision Transformers, which enables more effective capture of global spatial relationships and long-range dependencies—critical for describing complex ultrasound features such as margin irregularity and posterior acoustic patterns—compared to local-receptive-field-constrained CNNs [14]. However, despite its precision in feature extraction, BreastViT alone lacked structural consistency, with 63.3% of reports containing disconnected sentences. This limitation underscores the essential role of GPT-4o in refining fragmented outputs into coherent, well-structured reports [15, 16]. By automatically organizing content into “Findings” and “Impression” sections and standardizing terminology according to the BI-RADS lexicon, GPT-4o achieves a level of linguistic flow that pure vision-language models struggle to accomplish. This synergy explains why the combined approach significantly outperformed independent components (Friedman test, p < 0.001). Nevertheless, GPT-4o’s propensity for “hallucination” remains a concern; when used alone, it omitted relevant echogenic features in 10.2% of cases [17, 18], highlighting the necessity of grounding LLM outputs in actual image-specific analysis provided by BreastViT.

Notably, the exceptional BLEU scores achieved by BreastViT not only demonstrate the model’s precision in text synthesis but also, to some extent, reflect the highly standardized nature of BI-RADS reporting. Since clinical ultrasound reports typically adhere to rigid structural templates and standardized lexicons, this high degree of linguistic consistency reduces textual entropy, making it more feasible for the model to capture N-gram overlaps with the reference text. Consequently, these scores serve not only as a testament to the superiority of the model architecture but also substantiate the feasibility of utilizing structured BI-RADS descriptors to achieve standardization in automated medical report generation.

These findings have particular relevance for breast ultrasound screening in Asian populations, where breast cancer presents at younger ages with higher breast density compared to Western populations [19], making ultrasound a critical screening modality. In 2022, approximately 43% of the world’s 2.3 million new breast cancer cases occurred in Asia, with peak diagnosis occurring roughly a decade earlier than in Western women [19]. In countries such as China, Japan, and Korea, breast ultrasound is performed by sonographers or radiologists who both conduct examinations and generate reports within integrated workflows [20, 21]. This integrated “examination-and-reporting” model places high demands on report standardization, making our hybrid BreastViT + GPT-4o approach particularly suited to improve workflow efficiency while maintaining quality.

The clinical implications of this study are significant and multifaceted. First, the automated report generation system could substantially improve workflow efficiency by providing radiologists with a structured draft report that requires only verification and minimal editing. Based on our observations, the BreastViT + GPT-4o system generates draft reports in approximately 5–10 s per case, compared to the estimated 3–5 min typically required for manual report composition. For radiologists working in high-volume breast imaging centers processing 50–100 ultrasound cases daily, this represents potential time savings of 2–4 h per day, allowing them to focus more on image interpretation and complex case consultation. Second, standardized reports ensure that important descriptors─ such as lesion size, echogenicity, margins, posterior acoustic features, and orientation─ are consistently included according to BI-RADS lexicon, which may improve diagnostic quality and reduce inter-observer variability among radiologists. Our finding that the combined approach achieved 100% inclusion of standard report sections (Findings and Impression) compared to 63.3% structural completeness for BreastViT alone demonstrates the value of LLM-based post-processing for report standardization. Third, consistent and accurate reporting can lead to more appropriate follow-up recommendations and facilitate communication with referring physicians, ultimately improving patient care pathways. This approach may be particularly valuable in three clinical scenarios: (1) resource-limited settings where radiologist availability is constrained, (2) high-volume screening programs requiring rapid turnaround times, and (3) training environments where structured AI-generated reports can serve as educational templates for radiology residents, highlighting essential descriptors and proper report organization. However, we emphasize that this system should be implemented within a human-in-the-loop framework, ensuring that critical decisions remain under meaningful human oversight [22]. The AI-generated draft serves as a structured template that radiologists must review and approve before finalization. Given the 10.2% rate of GPT-4o omitting relevant findings when used alone, physician oversight remains essential to ensure patient safety.

AI report application must clarify that the system is positioned as a supportive tool for physicians, with ultimate legal responsibility borne by the signing physician. Regarding privacy, future transition to local deployment will ensure PHI does not leave the institutional network. Additionally, prospective clinical trials are still required for regulatory certification.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the sample data were collected from a single center, and the cohort size for external validation and multi-reader evaluation remained relatively limited (n = 49). Although this cohort is modest in size, it represents a high-quality benchmark based on the consensus of two senior radiologists, and the performance improvements observed were statistically significant (p < 0.05). This may restrict the generalizability of the model to other institutions with different ultrasound equipment, imaging protocols, or patient populations. Multi-center validation is necessary before broader clinical implementation. Second, only BI-RADS 4B–5 cases were included in this study, focusing on intermediate to high-risk lesions. Therefore, the model’s performance with lower-risk lesions (BI-RADS 2–3), benign conditions, and normal examinations (BI-RADS 1) remains unclear. A comprehensive clinical tool would need to handle the full spectrum of breast ultrasound findings. Third, the model has not been piloted in a real-world clinical workflow. Fourth, this study utilized a single representative static ultrasound image per patient for model training and evaluation. In routine clinical practice, however, breast ultrasound assessment is a dynamic process that relies on multi-planar scanning and real-time evaluation of lesion vascularity or stiffness. Relying on a single static image may overlook critical spatial features or dynamic information, which could potentially limit the model’s diagnostic comprehensiveness compared to a full clinical examination. Fifth, the use of GPT-4o, a proprietary third-party model accessed via cloud API, raises practical considerations. Data privacy concerns arise from sending patient information to external servers. Additionally, ongoing API costs and potential service unavailability present operational challenges. Future clinical deployment may require on-premise language models or federated learning approaches with robust privacy safeguards. Sixth, while BLEU scores provide a measure of textual similarity, they do not directly assess clinical diagnostic accuracy or the appropriateness of management recommendations. In response to this, we incorporated ROUGE-L (0.4106) and BERT-Score (0.7650) to further validate the semantic consistency of the BreastViT + GPT-4o approach. The results indicate that while hallucination risks exist in LLMs, our hybrid framework effectively grounds the output in objective imaging features, significantly mitigating factual inaccuracies. Future studies should incorporate clinical outcome measures.

Future work could expand the dataset to include more diverse devices, lesion categories (including BI-RADS 2–3 lesions and normal examinations), and a wider range of clinical settings. Integrating pre-trained large medical models could also enhance the model’s robustness. Additionally, directly incorporating GPT-4o into the training framework, such as through visual prompting or multi-task learning, could enable end-to-end joint optimization, reducing information loss and suppressing hallucinations. The use of domain-tuned LLMs or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) methods could further improve the model’s specialization and accuracy. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that integrating a Vision Transformer-based model (BreastViT) with GPT-4o significantly improves automated breast ultrasound report generation, achieving superior performance in clinical accuracy (7.31/10), information completeness (7.61/10), structural integrity (8.03/10), and terminology standardization (8.02/10) compared to either approach alone. To our knowledge, this is the one of the first studies to combine ViT-based image encoding with large language model post-processing for structured ultrasound reporting. The proposed hybrid framework addresses the complementary limitations of deep learning (limited linguistic fluency) and LLMs (hallucination risk), offering a promising pathway toward efficient, standardized, and clinician-approved breast ultrasound reporting. Multi-center prospective validation is warranted to confirm clinical utility and safety before widespread implementation.

Conclusions

This study proposes and validates an automated breast ultrasound report generation process combining deep learning and GPT-4o. The experimental results show that this method can automatically generate clinically standardized report texts, significantly outperforming deep learning-only or GPT-only approaches in terms of linguistic fluency and structural coherence. The BreastViT demonstrated the best performance in image feature extraction and text generation, while GPT-4o further enhanced the text’s quality and professionalism. This hybrid approach offers a new perspective on radiology report automation, showing strong potential for clinical application. Future efforts will need to validate this method with more data and optimize the algorithms to overcome current limitations, making the automated report generation system more accurate and reliable.
