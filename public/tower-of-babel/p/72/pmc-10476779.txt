Association of baseline serum cholesterol with benefits of intensive blood pressure control
Authors: Xiaoqi Wang, Yingqing Feng, Li Yang, Guohui Zhang, Xiaoyuan Tian, Qianhui Ling, Jiangshan Tan, Jun Cai
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Intensive systolic blood pressure (SBP) control improved outcomes in the Strategy of Blood Pressure Intervention in the Elderly Hypertensive Patients (STEP) trial. Whether baseline serum lipid parameters influence the benefits of intensive SBP control is unclear.Methods:The STEP trial was a randomized controlled trial that compared the effects of intensive (SBP target of 110 to <130 mmHg) and standard (SBP target of 130 to <150 mmHg) SBP control in Chinese patients aged 60 to 80 years with hypertension. The primary outcome was a composite of cardiovascular disease events. A total of 8283 participants from the STEP study were included in this post hoc analysis to examine whether the effects of the SBP intervention differed by baseline low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) concentrations.Results:Regardless of the randomized SBP intervention, baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations had a J-shaped association with the hazard of the primary outcome. However, the effects of the intensive SBP intervention on the primary outcome were not influenced by baseline LDL-C level (P for interaction = 0.80) and non-HDL-C level (P for interaction = 0.95). Adjusted subgroup analysis using tertiles in LDL-C1 (hazard ratio [HR], 0.77; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.52–1.13; P = 0.18), LDL-C2 (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.55–1.20; P = 0.29), and LDL-C3 (HR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.47–0.98; P = 0.04) was provided, with an interaction P value of 0.49. Similar results were showed in non-HDL-C1 (HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.59–1.29; P = 0.49), non-HDL-C2 (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.48–1.04; P = 0.08), and non-HDL-C3 (HR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.47–0.95; P = 0.03), with an interaction P-value of 0.47.Conclusion:High baseline serum LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations were associated with increased risk of primary cardiovascular disease outcome, but there was no evidence that the benefit of the intensive SBP control differed by baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations.Clinical trial registration:ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03015311.

Introduction

Hypertension is the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and the foremost preventable cause of disability and premature mortality worldwide.[1] The China Hypertension Survey showed that from 2012 to 2015, 23.2% (approximately 244.5 million) of the adult population had hypertension and another 41.3% (approximately 435.3 million) had pre-hypertension.[2]

With aging of the population and increasing prevalence of hypertension, determination of the treatment target for systolic blood pressure (SBP) in older patients has become a focus of research. Previous randomized clinical trials have provided strong evidence that compared with standard treatment, intensive blood pressure control provides impressive cardiovascular benefits.[3–5] However, these trials and subsequent post hoc analyses ignored the potential influence of serum cholesterol.

Increased blood lipids and blood pressure are considered to be two major risk factors for CVD, and they usually co-occur. The data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys from 1988 to 2010 showed that 60.7% to 64.3% of patients with hypertension also had hypercholesterolemia.[6] More importantly, the prevalence of concomitant hypertension and hypercholesterolemia consistently increased over time. The prevalence of hypertension and elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) increased from 5.0% to 30.7%, and the prevalence of combined hypertension, elevated LDL-C, and elevated non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) increased from 1.8% to 26.9%.[6] Many studies have demonstrated a linear correlation between blood pressure and serum cholesterol.[7–9] In addition, an elevated LDL-C concentration acts as a risk factor for new-onset hypertension.[10,11]

Non-HDL-C has also been shown to influence the development, augmentation, and prognosis of hypertension.[12–14] Beginning with the 2012 guidelines, non-HDL-C has been the recommended alternate target to LDL-C in any individual with a triglyceride concentration of >1.5 mmol/L.[15,16] Additionally, non-HDL-C allows for a more comprehensive evaluation than LDL-C. Many studies have compared the clinical application value of these two parameters from various aspects, but the results are inconsistent and debates continue.[17–21]

Given the complex interactions between blood pressure and lipid parameters and their potential influence on medical therapy, the present study was performed to expand on previously reported findings by providing a more detailed analysis of primary and secondary outcomes according to baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations.

Methods

Ethical approval

The trial was approved by the Ethics Committees of Fuwai Hospital (NO. 2016-838) and collaborating centers. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study design

This study was based on the Strategy of Blood Pressure Intervention in the Elderly Hypertensive Patients (STEP) trial[22] database. The STEP trial was a prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial that compared the effects of intensive (SBP target of 110 to <130 mmHg) and standard (SBP target of 130 to <150 mmHg) SBP control at 42 clinical centers throughout China. The details of the STEP trial protocol have been published previously.[23] This article detected the interaction between blood pressure intervention and baseline lipid profiles, including LDL-C and non-HDL-C, in spline and subgroup analyses. Besides, the hazard ratios (HRs) of primary and secondary outcomes (stroke, acute coronary syndrome [ACS], death from cardiovascular causes, and death from any cause) in subgroups were provided.

Study population

Patients were recruited from January 10 to December 31, 2017. The major inclusion criteria were an age of 60 to 80 years and the presence of hypertension with an SBP of 140 to 190 mmHg during three screening visits or current treatment with antihypertensive medication. Patients with a history of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke were excluded. Further details regarding the inclusion and exclusion criteria have been published previously.[23] To ensure the optimal balance and sample size in each subgroup, the participants were divided into three subgroups using tertiles.

Intervention, follow-up, and measurement

At each center, randomization was performed using a central computerized randomization program on a Web-based interface. Eligible patients were randomized to the intensive or standard treatment arm in a 1:1 ratio with intention-to-treat analysis specified. As an open-label trial, both the investigators and patients were aware of the assignments.

After randomization, the patients were followed up monthly for the first 3 months and then every 3 months until the end of the study by trained study staff according to the requirements specified in the protocol. Blood pressure was measured by a trained trial staff member (observed) three times at 1-min intervals after the patient had been seated for 5 min of quiet rest. The mean of three blood pressure readings was used to estimate the blood pressure. This process was standardized, and the same validated office blood pressure monitor (Omron Healthcare) was used at all participating centers during all baseline and follow-up clinic visits.

Antihypertensive drugs were provided to the participants according to the results of randomization. The investigators were allowed to adjust the medications to reach the SBP target based on the office blood pressure measurements obtained during the screening phase and the entire follow-up period. Laboratory data were obtained at baseline and every year thereafter. The trial was stopped on December 31, 2020, after third and fourth interim analyses (median follow-up of 3.34 years) because the data demonstrated that the intensive treatment group had a significantly lower incidence of primary outcome events than the standard treatment group.

Clinical outcomes

Definitions and ascertainment criteria for the trial outcomes have been published previously.[22] Members of the adjudication committee were unaware of the trial group assignments. The primary outcome was a composite of stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic), ACS (acute myocardial infarction and hospitalization for unstable angina), acute decompensated heart failure, coronary revascularization, atrial fibrillation, or death from cardiovascular causes. The following secondary outcomes were studied in this post hoc analysis: stroke, ACS, death from cardiovascular causes, and death from any cause.

Statistical methods

We performed all analyses using R software (version 3.6.3; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), and we used a two-sided α level of 0.05 for hypothesis testing. We compared the baseline characteristics among the three subgroups using analysis of variance for numeric variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. When comparing the primary and secondary outcomes, the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to account for the competing risk of death. Cumulative hazard plots were created to show the treatment effects over time.

We analyzed the association of baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations with the primary outcome in all participants by fitting a Cox regression model with the randomized SBP intervention and cubic spline terms in baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations as predictor variables, respectively. We used two models to investigate whether the effects of intensive SBP control on the primary outcome differed depending on baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations. First, we provided the P values for interaction in the subgroup analysis and HRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to compare the intensive and standard SBP control within each baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C tertiles. These HRs are presented to provide a comprehensive presentation of the results; however, it is important to note that much or all of the reported variation in HRs between the subgroups is caused by chance and that, in the absence of a statistically significant interaction, the best estimate of the effect of the intervention is given by the study-wide effect estimate, including all patients. Second, we investigated the possibility of a more general interaction by fitting a Cox regression for the primary outcome with main effects for the SBP intervention and cubic spline terms for baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The mean age of the study population (n = 8283) was 66.26 ± 4.83 years, with 46.6% men. Mean ± SD baseline SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were 146.07 ± 16.66 mmHg and 82.47 ± 10.60 mmHg, respectively. Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the study population by LDL-C tertile (LDL-C1: LDL-C < 2.29 mmol/L, LDL-C2: 2.29 ≤ LDL-C < 3.02 mmol/L, LDL-C3: LDL-C ≥ 3.02 mmol/L) and non-HDL-C tertile (non-HDL-C1: non-HDL-C < 3.14 mmol/L, non-HDL-C2: 3.14 ≤ non-HDL-C < 4.02 mmol/L, and non-HDL-C3: non-HDL-C ≥ 4.02 mmol/L) are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Among the 8283 participants, the LDL-C1 subgroup comprised 2733 (33.00%) participants, and the LDL-C2 and LDL-C3 subgroups comprised 2752 (33.22%) and 2798 (33.78%), respectively. The participants in the LDL-C3 subgroup tended to be younger (LDL-C1, 2, and 3: 66.51 ± 4.91 years, 66.27 ± 4.85 years, and 66.00 ± 4.70 years, respectively; P < 0.001) and included a higher percentage of women (60.0%). More importantly, as the LDL-C concentration increased, the baseline SBP (LDL-C1, 2, and 3: 144.26 ± 16.30 mmHg, 145.91 ± 16.52 mmHg, and 147.99 ± 16.95 mmHg, respectively; P < 0.001) and DBP (LDL-C1, 2, and 3: 81.87 ± 10.53 mmHg, 82.55 ± 10.61 mmHg, and 82.99 ± 10.64 mmHg, respectively; P < 0.001) showed an increasing trend [Supplementary Table 1].

Among the 8283 participants, the non-HDL-C1, non-HDL-C2, and non-HDL-C3 subgroups comprised 2761 (32.29%), 2748 (33.14%), and 2774 (33.45%) participants, respectively. Like the results of the LDL-C stratification, participants in the non-HDL-C3 subgroup also tended to be younger (non-HDL-C1, 2, and 3: 66.68 ± 4.94 years, 66.17 ± 4.85 years, and 65.93 ± 4.66 years, respectively; P < 0.001) and included a higher percentage of women (62.2%). As the non-HDL-C concentration increased, the baseline SBP (non-HDL-C1, 2, and 3: 144.18 ± 16.26 mmHg, 145.98 ± 16.55 mmHg, and 148.04 ± 16.94 mmHg, respectively; P < 0.001) and DBP (non-HDL-C1, 2, and 3: 81.71 ± 10.40 mmHg, 82.42 ± 10.55 mmHg, and 83.28 ± 10.79 mmHg, respectively; P < 0.001) showed an increasing trend [Supplementary Table 2]. Further comparisons between the intensive and standard treatment groups were examined within each LDL-C and non-HDL-C tertile. Except for aspartate aminotransferase in LDL-C2 and hyperlipidemia history in LDL-C3, all baseline characteristics were well balanced between the two trial groups [Table 1], and there was no difference in baseline characteristics between the two trial groups by non-HDL-C tertile [Table 2]

Interactions of baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations and SBP treatment in cubic spline regression analyses

Adjusted for baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics, there was a J-shaped association of baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C with the primary outcome in cubic spline regression analyses [Figures 1A and 2A] regardless of randomized SBP intervention. The P values for interaction between baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations and SBP treatment are 0.80 and 0.95, respectively [Figures 1B and 2B].

Interactions of baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations and SBP treatment using tertile

Among the overall participants, primary outcome events occurred in 144 of 4126 patients (3.5% [1.0%/year]) in the intensive treatment group and in 191 of 4157 patients (4.6% [1.4%/year]) in the standard treatment group (HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.60–0.93; P = 0.008). These findings are consistent with the results of the STEP trial [Supplementary Table 3].

The interaction between the intensive SBP control treatment effect and baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C tertiles was analyzed. In LDL-C tertile, the interaction P = 0.49 adjusted the baseline characteristics, including age, gender, SBP, DBP, creatinine, fasting serum glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol (TC), history of diabetes mellitus, history of hyperlipidemia, and statins medication [Table 3]. In non-HDL-C tertile, the interaction P = 0.47 adjusted the baseline characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index, SBP, DBP, creatinine, uric acid, fasting serum glucose, triglycerides, TC, history of diabetes mellitus, history of hyperlipidemia, and statins medication [Table 3].

Discussion

The results of the present study indicate that high baseline serum LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations were associated with increased risk of primary CVD outcome. Intensive SBP control consistently reduced the risk of the primary CVD outcome across baseline tertiles of LDL-C and non-HDL-C. The effects of the intensive SBP control on the primary outcome were not influenced by baseline LDL-C level and non-HDL-C level both in cubic spline regression and tertile analyses.

Complex interactions exist between serum cholesterol and blood pressure, with detailed evidence as follows. A cross-sectional study showed that the serum TC and LDL-C concentrations were linearly correlated with blood pressure.[9] A secondary analysis of data from the European Study on Cardiovascular Risk Prevention and Management in Usual Daily Practice showed a statistically significant positive association between serum lipid concentrations and blood pressure.[7] According to a recent large community-based study in China, for every 1-SD increase in TC, SBP increased by 0.33 mmHg.[8] Additionally, an elevated LDL-C concentration has been shown to be associated with an increased rate of new-onset hypertension.[10,11] The possible mechanisms underlying how elevated cholesterol concentrations augment high blood pressure involve endothelial dysfunction and renin-angiotensin system activation, and the details are shown in Supplementary Table 8. However, the beneficial effects of intensive SBP control on the primary and secondary outcome were not modified by baseline LDL-C concentrations.

Although lowering the LDL-C concentration is the primary target of therapy in most guidelines, accumulating evidence indicates that LDL-C is not accurate and comprehensive enough in some circumstances. For example, patients who are treated with statins and achieve a low LDL-C concentration but have a high non-HDL-C or apolipoprotein B concentration remain at increased cardiovascular risk.[24] Beginning with the 2012 guidelines, any individual with a triglyceride concentration of >1.5 mmol/L should undergo measurement of non-HDL-C as the alternate target to LDL-C.[15,16] The rationale for this is that when the triglyceride concentration is >1.5 mmol/L, some cholesterol in LDL particles is replaced by triglycerides; this promotes production of more pro-CVD small dense LDL particles, making the amount of cholesterol in LDL-C an unreliable reflection of the LDL particle number.[25,26] The assessment of non-HDL-C, which is calculated as TC − HDL-C,[27] offers a simple way to analyze the total amount of pro-CVD lipoproteins containing apolipoprotein B.[28] Such proteins include very-low-density lipoprotein and their metabolic remnants; intermediate-density lipoproteins; lipoprotein (a); and LDL, chylomicrons, and their triglyceride-rich lipoprotein remnants.[28] For the above reasons, we used LDL-C and non-HDL-C as stratification variables in this post hoc analysis, respectively.

In one study, the non-HDL-C concentration in patients with hypertension was significantly higher than that in healthy controls, indicating the potential of non-HDL-C as a predictor of the development of hypertension.[12] Consistent with these findings, the present post hoc analysis also showed that patients with relatively high LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations tended to have higher baseline SBP and DBP. In the hypertensive population, rising non-HDL-C may result in the development of arterial stiffness and augment the associated pathophysiological process, and this has been shown to influence the prognosis of patients with hypertension.[13,14] A recent study revealed a U-shaped association between non-HDL-C and mortality among the hypertensive population.[29] The lowest risk was found at threshold values of 4.09 and 4.91 mmol/L for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, respectively, and relatively higher or lower non-HDL-C concentrations were linked to increased mortality.[29]

Hypercholesterolemia and hypertension have synergistic pro-oxidant, proinflammatory, and prothrombotic effects.[29] As mentioned above, the prevalence of concomitant hypertension and hypercholesterolemia consistently increased, acting as a “double-hit” on the progression of CVD. Lipid-lowering therapy with statins significantly decreases blood pressure via a pleiotropic effect.[10,30,31] However, there is limited evidence about the influence of serum lipids on antihypertensive treatments.

In the LDL-C3 and non-HDL-C3 subgroups rather than tertiles 1 and 2 of the present study, we observed significant risk reduction of intensive treatment on the primary outcomes. It is important to note that the most variation in HRs and CIs between the subgroups is caused by chance and that, the best estimate of the effect of the intensive SBP control is given by the study-wide effect estimate, including all patients. Additionally, the results for the secondary outcomes were also more favorable in the intensive than standard treatment arm. These results prove the beneficial effects of intensive SBP control on cardiovascular outcomes in older patients.

The HRs of death from any cause with intensive treatment in LDL-C1 and non-HDL-C1 revealed an increased all-cause mortality risk, with only the non-HDL-C1 subgroup showing statistical significance. However, it is not accurate to conclude that intensive blood pressure control increases the risk of death from any cause in patients with hypertension who have a non-HDL-C concentration of <3.14 mmol/L. The new development of cancer-related and other non-cardiovascular death events accounted for most of the causes of death in the STEP trial population aged 60 to 80 years. In addition, in patients of advanced age, lower cholesterol concentrations are associated with a poorer health status.[29] Although we excluded patients with severe somatic disease (such as cancer) during the STEP trial screening, there might have been other non-cardiovascular comorbidities or variables that indicated poor health that we did not consider.[22] Malnutrition along with inflammation leading to further aggravation in debilitated patients may explain such an inverse association.[32] With respect to the HRs of death from cardiovascular causes with intensive treatment in the LDL-C1 subgroup (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 0.38–4.24; P = 0.70) and non-HDL-C1 subgroup (HR, 1.96; 95% CI, 0.49–7.86; P = 0.34), the limited number of events (LDL-C1: 6/5, intensive vs. standard; non-HDL-C1: 6/3, intensive vs. standard) and occasional error should be considered.

A limitation inherent in the post hoc analysis is that false positive results are easy to happen. Besides, while designing this study, we had considered to stratify population according to tertiles or Chinese guidelines for the management of dyslipidemia in adults. The latter was quitted for several reasons. First, the purpose of this study is to detect whether the beneficial effects of intensive SBP control on the CVD outcome and all-cause death were modified by baseline concentration level of LDL-C and non-HDL-C, rather than dyslipidemia. Second, there exist a big gap in sample size between subgroups when stratifying population according to guidelines.

Conclusion

High baseline serum LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations were associated with increased risk of primary CVD outcome, but there was no evidence that the benefit of the intensive SBP control differed by baseline LDL-C and non-HDL-C concentrations.
