Single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy with inflexible laparoscopic instruments and laparoscopy: a single-center experience of 533 cases
Authors: Zhiquan Chen, Liufan Zha, Jinhong Wu, Huanbing Zhu, Xiang Pan, Chao Li, Yangming Hou, Dan Ye, Daren Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) has been widely performed as the gold standard for BGDs. Single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC) was considered as an option for minimizing surgical injuries and improving outcomes. However, the benefit of this novel technique, especially with conventional and inflexible instruments and laparoscopy, is still controversial.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 958 consecutive cases (533 SILC vs. 425 CLC) from January 2023 to March 2024. SILC was performed via a single transumbilical incision with straight and inflexible instruments whereas CLC with traditional three-port strategy. Information of patients’ demographic characteristics and pathological diagnoses was collected and analyzed. Comparative outcomes assessment included validated measures: SF-36 QoL indices, VAS pain scores, Vancouver Scar Scale assessments, hospitalization duration, and Clavien-Dindo complication grading.ResultsCases from two groups showed similar demographic characteristics and pathological diagnoses. They also had comparable surgical time, estimated intraoperative blood loss and hospital costs. Sixteen cases required supplementary trocars for technical challenges. The SILC group exhibited superior scar satisfaction, though no significant intergroup differences existed in hospitalization duration, postoperative pain scores, or wound infection rates. Longitudinal analysis revealed reduced chronic pain and diarrhea incidence in SILC patients. Six-month postoperative SF-36 assessments showed significant improvements in SILC recipients for Bodily Pain, Vitality, and Role-Emotional domains.ConclusionThe present study demonstrated SILC with conventional and inflexible instruments to be safe and feasible. SILC was found to be non-inferior to CLC. This technique demonstrated certain advantages, particularly in improving patient satisfaction with wound pain and appearance, while maintaining comparable surgical outcomes, hospital stay duration, and postoperative complication rates to those of CLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03124-z.Keywords: Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, SILC, Conventional instruments, Hand-crafted glove port, Postoperative outcomes

Introduction

Since being introduced by Mühe in 1985 [1], laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) has garnered global acceptance and defined as gold standard treatment for benign gallbladder diseases (BGDs) [2]. Minimally invasive surgery technique was applied in a spectrum of surgeries including LC, namely single-incision laparoscopic cholecystectomy (SILC), initially reported by Navarra in 1997 [3]. By then this technique developed dramatically in aspects of both operation and instruments. Previous experiences with SILC across various centers have consistently demonstrated its robust safety and feasibility [4, 5]. Numerous clinical trials have highlighted the benefits of SILC over conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy (CLC), particularly in terms of promoted aesthetic outcomes, reduced postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stays [6, 7]. However, discrepancies in postoperative complications including incisional hernias have sparked debate regarding the merits and drawbacks of SILC [8, 9]. Consequently, further research is urgent with larger cohorts and extended postoperative follow-up periods for a definitive assessment of SILC.

In the present study LC was performed in 958 cases from January 2023 to March 2024, aiming to compare the overall outcomes and complications between CLC and SILC. All surgical instruments and laparoscopy were introduced through a single intraumbilical incision in SILC group, allowing the resultant scar to be discreetly concealed within the reconstructed navel. Furthermore, historical implementations of SILC have predominantly utilized specialized and novel instruments [10–12]. This reliance on special equipment limited the technique’s applicability and benefits in settings with more rudimentary clinical facilities. The authors successfully applied conventional laparoscopic instruments and a 10 mm laparoscopy with straight and inflexible properties and simplified the surgical process. Moreover, the single-incision port used were made on site from basic stuffs and materials easily obtained from operation room. This study manifested SILC to be feasible and adaptive in most of BGDs with promising advantages in patients’ satisfaction and comfort.

Materials and methods

Research settings

Between January 2023 and March 2024, 533 SILC and 425 CLC cases suffering from BGDs including cholecystolithiasis with or without acute or chronic cholecystitis, gallbladder polyps and adenomyomatosis were retrospectively enrolled. In particular, cases with acute cholecystitis were classified according to Tokyo Guidelines 2018 [13]. Exclusion criteria: Concomitant liver cirrhosis (Child-Pugh class B or higher), History of upper abdominal surgery, Gangrenous or perforated gallbladder, Intraoperative conversion to alternative procedures (e.g., open laparotomy, cholangiography, biliary exploration, bile duct repair, abscess drainage, or combined organ resection), Patient or family members declined the treatment protocol, Inability to complete follow-up evaluations.

Both SILC and CLC groups were evaluated based on parameters such as estimated blood loss, operative duration, incidence of complications, Postoperative pain level and hospital stay time. Additionally, thickness of gallbladder wall in each case was determined according to imaging studies preoperatively. Patients were followed up at one and six months after surgery for further research with postoperative daily life, scar satisfaction and postoperative complications.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University (Approval No.2024 − 1092) and study was conducted under the guidance of the Declaration of Helsinki. All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the fnal manuscript. This study was preregistered in the Clinical Trials (number: NCT06708390; available at http://www.clinicaltrials.gov/. The registration date is 2024.11.25.).

Instruments and materials

In all cases a 10 mm conventional 30° laparoscopic camera (STORZ122VMK, KARL STORZ SE & Co. KG., Tuttlingen, Germany) and conventional inflexible instruments (Hangzhou Kangji Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, P.R.C.) were applied. Notably, an ultrasound activated scalpel system (USE36 (5–36 cm), Hocermed (Tianjin) Medical Technologies Co., Ltd., Tianjin, P.R.C.) and electrocautery device were used for tissue isolation, dissection and achieving hemostasis. A grasper was used for gallbladder retraction and 5/10 mm Hem-o-lok clips were strategically used for the ligation of the cystic duct and artery. (Fig. 1a, Hem-o-lok clips not presented)

Surgical instruments and intraoperative conditions. a All surgeries were performed with conventional and inflexible instruments. b Hand-made port applied in all surgeries were fabricated with surgery glove and a series of disposable ports. c A disposable wound retractor was introduced through the incision and affixed to the glove port. Laparoscopy and instruments were conducted via the three ports. d The surgeon assumed a position on the patient’s right side and assistant on the left. e Positions of laparoscopy and instruments in procedure of gallbladder isolation. f Positions of laparoscopy and instruments in procedure of hemostasis on the liver bed. g A custom-made device was manufactured by a suture and Hem-o-lok clip. h Over-sized liver’s IVb lobe led to suboptimal exposure of surgical view. i The custom-made device properly facilitated the retraction of liver’s IVb lobe

In cases of SILC a hand-crafted port was fabricated from a Single-Use Sterile Rubber Surgical Glove (Shanghai Motex Healthcare Co., Ltd., Shanghai, P.R.C.), complemented by a range of disposable ports in 5/10/12 mm diameters (Hangzhou Kangji Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, P.R.C.). The tips of thumb, middle and pinky glove fingers were excised to accommodate the attachment of correspondingly downsized 12/10/5 mm ports and secured with size-0 silk suture (Fig. 1b).

Surgical procedures of SILC

Patients were placed in reverse Trendelenburg position, adjusted to an angle of 15° to 30°. The positions of surgeon and assistant were illustrated in Fig. 1d. A 2 cm longitudinal incision was performed intraumbilically followed by a 6 cm-diameter disposable wound retractor (WP-60B, Guangzhou T.K Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, P.R.C.) introduced through the incision. The retractor’s outer ring was affixed to the glove of the surgical port assembly and pneumoperitoneum was established (Fig. 1c). A laparoscopy in companion with a grasper and the primary instruments were introduced to the abdominal cavity via the port. Positions of the instruments in this step were required in specific orders as presented in Fig. 1e. Utilizing the grasper to extend the gallbladder’s ampulla anteriorly and laterally facilitated the identification of the cystic duct in most cases.

In face of acute cholecystitis or anatomical variations such as over-sized liver’s III or IVb lobes (Fig. 1h), SILC approach might yield suboptimal exposure, increasing the risk of intraoperative complications. The authors applied a custom-made device to retract and secure the liver to promote visual access. A 0-size ETHICON® Coated VICRYLTM Plus suture (CT (40 mm 1/2c, 90 cm), Beijing AKIN AlEN International Medical Technology Co.,Ltd, Beijing, P.R.C.), 15 cm in length with a straight needle, was modified by creating a knot at the tail, with a 10 mm Hem-o-lok clip (Weck Closure Systems, Research Triangle Park, NC, USA) affixed proximal to the knot (Fig. 1g). The suture was introduced into the abdominal cavity via the port. The needle was passed through the parenchyma of liver’s III or IVb lobes and subsequently threaded through the abdominal wall from within outward and secured with a surgical clamp, facilitating the retraction of the liver anteriorly and superiorly (Fig. 1i). This maneuver ensured the subsequent isolation and dissection feasible and safe.

The cystic duct and artery were exposed with an ultrasound activated scalpel and afterwards clipped using a 5–10 mm Hem-o-lok (Weck Closure Systems, Research Triangle Park, NC, USA) and then divided by scissors via the 12 mm channel. Afterwards the gallbladder was dissected from the liver bed with the ultrasonic scalpel. In the step of hemostasis on the liver bed, positions of laparoscopy and surgical instruments were readjusted as described in Fig. 1f.

The gallbladder was removed outside the body with a disposable endoscopic specimen bag (Type III, large, Hangzhou Kangji Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, P.R.C.) and promptly examined by a pathologist. In cases of severe inflammation or at an elevated risk of bile leakage, a postoperative drainage procedure was implemented intraoperatively. The draining catheter was removed 24 h later upon situations of less than 50 ml drainage volume, no bile/blood-like ascites or no bilirubin detected in the ascites.

Surgical procedures of CLC

The CLC was performed with three channels consisting of two 10 mm and one 5 mm disposable ports strategically located in the infraumbilical, epigastric, and right hypochondriac regions, respectively. The pneumoperitoneum was established through the infraumbilical incision. The surgeon stood on the left side of the patient operating the primary instruments through 10 mm epigastric port by right hand, while manipulating a grasper or suction-hook bovie through 5 mm right hypochondriac port by left hand. The laparoscopy holder positioned to the left of patient and the foot side of surgeon. The remainder of the surgical procedure followed a protocol analogous to CILC.

Evaluation of demographic characteristics and postoperative outcomes

Data were systematically collected pre- and postoperatively, following with subsequent laboratory evaluation. Basic demographic and clinical information encompassed gender, age, body mass index (BMI) and total hospitalization costs. The general health status of patients was classified according to American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA) system. Operation time was determined from the initiation of skin incision to the completion of skin closure and the estimated blood loss was documented by the surgeon. The operative findings from the gallbladder, cystic pedicle, and associated adhesions were meticulously assessed using Nassar scale [14, 15], which scored difficulty and risk of complications on a scale from one to four. The higher scores indicated higher operative complexity and potential for adverse outcomes (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Postoperative pain level were assessed 24 h after surgery by the surgeon and nursing colleagues using a visual analog scale score from 0 to 10. The duration of hospital stay was defined as days in which patients received postoperative medical care in hospital. Postoperative complications were graded using Clavien-Dindo classification system [16, 17]. A range of complications was quantified, with those exceeding grade III on Clavien-Dindo scale classified as most severe. Specifically, incisional hernia was characterized as the hernia at the umbilical incision site. Scar assessment was conducted one month postoperatively using Vancouver Scar Scale [18].

Patients were further followed up for six months to evaluate the Quality of Life (QoL) using a questionnaire with various parameters including General Health (GH), reported Health Transition (HT), Physical Functioning (PF), Role-Physical (RP), Role-Emotional (RE), Social Functioning (SF), Bodily Pain (BP), Vitality (VT) and Mental Health (MH), in accordance with SF-36 Health Survey and International Quality of Life Assessment (IQOLA) project [19].

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized as mean values with standard deviations (Mean ± SD) while group comparisons were performed with Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (%) and comparisons were performed with Student’s t test. All statistical tests were conducted as two-tailed. Statistical significance was defined when p-value was less than 0.05. Analyses were carried out with SPSS software, version 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Preoperative clinical characteristics

The preoperative clinical characteristics of the patients in this study are presented in Supplemental Table 1. A total of 533 patients received SILC, including 199 males and 334 females; whereas 425 patients underwent CLC, comprising 178 males and 247 females. The mean age of the SILC group was 47.29 ± 14.81 years, and for the CLC group, it was 49.82 ± 14.97 years, with no significant difference in between. BMI was statistically similar with values of 24.54 ± 3.96 for SILC and 24.55 ± 3.67 for CLC (kg/m²). In SILC group 78.4% of patients received an ASA score of one, and 20.3% received a score of two, compared to 73.7% and 25.2% in CLC group, respectively, with no significant difference. Additionally, 7 patients of SILC and 5 of CLC were classified with an ASA score of three.

The pathological data documented that within SILC group, 379 patients were diagnosed with cholecystolithiasis, 76 with gallbladder polyps, and 16 with adenomyomatosis of the gallbladder. In contrast, the CLC group included 312 patients with cholecystolithiasis, 40 with gallbladder polyps, and 12 with adenomyomatosis of the gallbladder. In the SILC group, 5 patients had common bile duct stones and 9 patients had acute pancreatitis, whereas in the CLC group, these numbers were 4 and 6, respectively. Histories of previous upper abdominal surgery were confirmed in 13 cases with SILC and 15 with CLC. Additionally, the thickness of gallbladder wall were 3.12 ± 1.76 mm and 3.25 ± 1.83 mm, respectively, with no significant difference observed. (Supplemental Table 1)

In SILC group 11, 4, and 1 patients respectively underwent percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PTGBD), endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), and biliary endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST). Corresponding figures for the CLC group were 13, 7, and 4, respectively, with no statistically significant differences in between (Supplemental Table 2).

Operative and postoperative outcomes

Initially, cases in both groups were assessed using the Nassar scale, which evaluates the gallbladder, cystic pedicle, and associated adhesions. A score of one on the Nassar scale was reported by 313 patients in SILC group and 258 in CLC group. Within SILC group, 145 patients received a Nassar score of two, while 67 were given a score of three, and 8 were given a score of four. Correspondingly, the CLC group had 108, 52 and 7 patients with these respective scores.

The mean operative time was 56.11 ± 21.41 min and estimated blood loss was 11.17 ± 26.94 ml in SILC group while the operative time was 50.98 ± 21.21 min and estimated blood loss was 9.93 ± 19.55 ml in CLC group with no significant difference. The number of cases in SILC group with an obscured Calot’s triangle due to distortion of liver segments III and IVb were 61 and 22, respectively. In contrast, the CLC group had 50 and 22 cases with similar distortions, respectively. To attain CVS, which was accomplished in 493 cases with SILC and 399 with CLC, 15 patients in the SILC group required the addition of a single port, while 1 patient needed two additional ports during the procedure. Furthermore, one case in the CLC group required conversion to an open procedure due to difficulty of operation.

In SILC group, the incidences of gallbladder perforation, bile duct injury, and adjacent organ injury were 19, 2, and 2 cases, respectively. In comparison, the CLC group experienced 24, 3, and 2 cases of these complications, respectively, with no statistically significant differences between two groups. Ultimately, for safety precautions during surgery, drain insertion was performed in 8.6% of the SILC group and 12.4% of the CLC group (Supplemental Table 3).

The SILC group exhibited a mean hospital stay of 2.06 ± 2.06 days, with no significant difference with 2.25 ± 2.27 days observed in CLC group. The total cost of hospitalization was comparable, with SILC group incurring a mean expense of 1732.34 ± 636 US dollars, contrasted with 1676.21 ± 400 US dollars in CLC group. Furthermore, the visual analog scale score for pain assessed after 24 h revealed insignificant difference between the SILC and CLC group. Postoperative complications led to readmission for 5 patients in SILC group and 6 in CLC group; specifics of these complications are detailed subsequently. Additionally, 1 patient from each group required a subsequent reoperation (Supplemental Table 4).

Postoperative complications were graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification system. For Grade I complications, the SILC group reported 5 cases of wound infection, 14 of long-term pain, and 20 of long-term diarrhea. In stark contrast, the CLC group exhibited 6, 22, and 31 cases for these respective Grade I complications, indicating a significantly increased incidence of adverse outcomes. Notably, there were no cases of ileus, which is classified as a Grade II complication. For Grade III complications, the most prevalent were incisional hemorrhage, occurring in one patient in each group, bile leakage in one patient in CLC group, and incisional hernia in three patients in SILC group and one in CLC group, with no statistically significant difference observed (Supplemental Table 5). Furthermore, the pathological diagnoses, as detailed in Supplemental Table 6, demonstrate similar pathological profiles across both groups.

Assessment of postoperative quality of life and cosmetics

Scar assessment was conducted after one month using the Vancouver Scar Scale. The CLC group exhibited significantly higher scores (3.48 ± 1.60) and less favorable outcomes compared to SILC group (2.31 ± 1.82) (Supplemental Table 4).

The patients who participated in the operation answered the questionnaire questions the day before the operation, and there was no statistical difference between the two groups. At the 6-month postoperative mark, patients who underwent SILC demonstrated superior general health outcomes, particularly in the aspects of GH, RE, BP and VT, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). However, the assessments for HT, PF, RP SF and MH were comparable between two groups (Supplemental Tables 7–8).

Discussion

LC has long been recognized widely as the gold standard treatment for BGDs [2, 20]. In recent decades, the evolution of minimally invasive surgical techniques has offered promising alternatives to LC, particularly in the form of SILC. While SILC has demonstrated potential benefits, such as reduced postoperative pain and improved satisfaction with scars [21], the evidence remains equivocal [22–25]. This study was conducted to provide a more definitive assessment of SILC’s efficacy and safety comparing to traditional multi-port LC, with a large patient cohort to address the gaps in existing research.

Typically, laparoscopic surgery relies on the principle of triangulation between laparoscopy and surgical instruments to ensure smooth execution. The “chopstick” effect, characterized by instrument collisions within a confined single incision, is notably technical obstacle that would increase operative difficulty and risk of complications [26]. To mitigate the “chopstick” effect, it is essential to properly align the position and angulation of instruments. Optimally, the laparoscopy should be positioned to the left of the incision, with the primary instruments ideally operated below the grasper. Previous studies have introduced a cross-hand technique for SILC [12], which required practiced surgical skill and extended learning curve. In the current study, unlike previous researches [27, 28], the primary instruments were manipulated by the surgeon with right hand while the grasper was managed by assistant, aligning more closely with conventional LC.

Safety is always the priority considered in any surgery. This encompasses various factors including operative duration, estimated blood loss, intraoperative organ injuries, postoperative complications, and pain management. In the procedure of LC the exposure of Calot’s triangle to achieve satisfactory visualization of surgical field is critical [29]. In previous research, Um et al. [28] introduced the application of a snake liver retractor to elevate liver; however, introducing an additional instrument would increase operational complexity. Instead, a hand-crafted device was used in our study as previously described. This technique allowed to achieve satisfactory exposure of surgical field without additional incision, proving both convenient and feasible. In SILC group, additional trocars were employed in 16 cases for various reasons, such as severe adhesion due to inflammation, high risk of injury on adjacent organs and uncontrolled bleeding from the cystic artery.

Bile duct injury (BDI) remains one of the most common and serious intraoperative complications following LC, making the achievement of CVS a crucial step [30]. In the present study CVS was successfully achieved in 92.5% of SILC cases and in 94.0% of CLC cases. BDI was respectively reported in two cases in SILC group and three in CLC group. Meanwhile, cases involving gallbladder perforation, bile leakage, injury to adjacent organs, or conversion to open cholecystectomy showed comparable statistical results between two groups, suggesting similar frequency of these complications regardless of techniques used.

Following safety and feasibility established, learning curve remained a significant concern as it could substantially influence operative time. Previous reports have indicated the number of cases required to navigate the learning curve ranged from 8 to 20 cases [31–33], which depended on the surgeons’ skill and experience. However, beginners might show comparable learning curves for both SILC and CLC [34]. Our clinical experiences hinted similar results for both skilled surgeon and beginner of hepatobiliary surgery, but further research are acquired for accurate analysis. Moreover, based on our experiences, transitioning the manipulation of primary instruments from left to right hand makes SILC an approachable and manageable procedure for seasoned laparoscopic and hepatobiliary surgeons.

Postoperative pain is another critical factor impacting operation outcomes, patient satisfaction, wound healing, mortality and morbidity. Several factors, including pneumoperitoneum pressure, bile leakage, peritoneal lavage, the improper application of local anesthetics and emotional factors, might lead to detrimental and insufficient effects on postoperative pain arrangement [35, 36]. The length of the incision is widely regarded as the most significant determinant [37]. Previous studies have reported varying outcomes, with SILC causing less, equivalent, or more postoperative pain at different time points [38–40].

In this study, patients who underwent SILC reported comparable postoperative pain 24 h after surgery but less long-term pain compared to those who received CLC, likely due to reduced incisions and lower pressure exerted by single-port retractor compared to the multi-port trocars. As described above, the authors employed a hand-crafted glove port instead of a commercial port to establish the surgical access channel, which ensured consistent pressure on incision and introduced a modest expansion of the intraumbilical wound. Consequently, a 2 cm incision was adequate for the introduction of all instruments while maintaining tolerable pressure [41]. Meanwhile the hand-crafted port did not significantly intensify the financial burden in hospital while patients benefited from reduced postoperative pain and decreased analgesic requirements, although no significant difference was observed, partially attributing to comparable hospital stay time.

It is also noteworthy that conventional inflexible laparoscopic instruments were used in all surgeries, rather than the curved or novel instruments and flexible laparoscopy mentioned in other studies [10, 11, 42]. A well-trained surgeon can achieve equivalent surgical outcomes using both conventional and single-incision-specific instruments. This approach not only contributes similar clinical costs but also empowers surgeons in basic clinical settings to offer high-quality care and substantial benefits to patients.

The majority of patients requiring cholecystectomy express a strong preference for a treatment with minimal or imperceptible incisions. The SILC technique offers an alternative resulting in less incisions and scars. The specific intraumbilical incision is advantageous for concealing the surgical site, and port-site hernias occurred in both groups were observed to be comparable in this study. Similar rates of long-term incisional infection, hemorrhage and bile leakage between two groups suggest that these complications are common to both surgical techniques. Additionally, patients with SILC experienced less long-term diarrhea, which, along with the superior outcomes of the scar, contributed to superior satisfaction compared to the CLC group.

Unlike CLC, SILC has significant improvement in patients’ postoperative quality of life. According to follow-up survey analysis, the advantage might attribute to various factors. During the operation, SILC has less interference on the abdominal cavity due to less incisions, reducing postoperative adhesions and complications. Meanwhile, accurate performance of instruments required the surgeon to be more concentrated, leading to improved accuracy and safety.

This novel surgical approach not only leads to promoted postoperative experience of patients but also lays a solid foundation on their future health and quality of life. Generally, patients benefited accelerated recovery from postoperative pain and discomfort due to minimally administered analgesic drugs, and quickly returned to normal daily life. Additionally, thanks to the small incision patients’ mobility during early stage of postoperation was better preserved, contributing to reduced risk of complications caused by prolonged bedridden time. These advantages of SILC allow patients to regain self-confidence as well as work and social activities more rapidly after surgery and improve the quality of life.

This study still has limitations. The retrospective and single-center nature may introduce unavoidable bias into the findings. The follow-up period for all cases was limited to 6 months. The main limitations are that long-term follow-up studies have not been carried out, and the monitoring system for long-term complications (such as incisional hernia) is not perfect, which makes it difficult to fully assess the potential risks. It should be acknowledged that the SILC approach carries certain technical constraints. Specifically, in cases presenting with severe inflammatory conditions such as acute cholecystitis, the establishment of supplemental ports and placement of abdominal drainage tubes become necessary. Furthermore, when hepatic segments obstruct the critical view of Calot’s triangle, the implementation of liver retraction techniques, which may contribute to increased surgical trauma.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that SILC, as an innovative laparoscopic technique, offers a safe and viable alternative for patients with BGDs. This technique demonstrated certain advantages, particularly in improving patient satisfaction with wound pain and appearance, while maintaining comparable surgical outcomes, hospital stay duration, and postoperative complication rates to those of CLC. The application of a hand-crafted glove port and conventional inflexible laparoscopic instruments achieve comparable clinical costs to traditional multi-port LC, making SILC more accessible to a broader range of surgeons. Future research should include a randomized controlled trial with a larger cohort and an extended follow-up period to provide more robust evidence.
