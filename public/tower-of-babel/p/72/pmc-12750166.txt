Decoding helical dynamics in viral replication: Cryo-EM unveils conserved assembly mechanisms and broad-spectrum antiviral targets
Authors: Bo Wang, Jiwei Zhang, Xinyong Liu, Peng Zhan
Published: 2025

A recent study led by Yi-Wei Chang1, published in Science, integrates cryo-electron single-particle analysis (SPA) and cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) to elucidate the near-atomic architecture of the influenza virus ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex. The study reveals that the RNP adopts a right-handed, antiparallel double-helical configuration, in which the viral RNA is translocated via a polymerase-driven strand sliding mechanism. Dynamic tail loop interactions between adjacent nucleoprotein (NP) subunits impart structural flexibility to the RNP, enabling conformational dynamics essential for processive RNA synthesis. These findings define a structural basis for strand sliding, and guided by this insight, the authors identify novel small molecules that disrupt NP–NP interactions by targeting a conserved tail loop interface, laying a foundation for broad-spectrum influenza antivirals.

Influenza viruses continue to pose a significant global health threat, causing seasonal epidemics and occasional pandemics2. The primary targets of current vaccines and antiviral drugs, including hemagglutinin (HA), neuraminidase (NA), and polymerase acidic protein (PA), are prone to high genetic variability facilitating immune escape and drug resistance3,4, and thereby undermining clinical efficacy. In contrast, the RNP complex is highly conserved across subtypes and functions as the core transcription replication machinery, making it an attractive target for next generation antivirals5. The influenza virus RNP is composed of viral RNA, NP, and the Polymerase complex. Previous cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies6,7 on influenza RNP have revealed the double-helical architecture of the complex. However, its intrinsic flexibility hindered subunit-level modeling and left the helical handedness unresolved. Crystal structures of recombinant NP revealed a bilobed architecture capable of tail loop-mediated oligomerization into trimers or tetramers, supporting assembly of RNP-like filaments. Yet, how the polymerase coordinates with NP to enable processive RNA synthesis within native helical RNP remains elusive. As the core machinery for viral genome replication and transcription, the RNP's assembly and conformational dynamics are central to influenza virus pathogenicity. Its conformational plasticity has challenged traditional structural approaches. High-resolution structural characterization of its assembly and function is thus essential to elucidate the principles of viral RNA encapsidation and polymerase-driven transcription, and to inform the development of broad-spectrum antivirals targeting conserved RNP elements.

To address these limitations, the researchers employed an integrative cryo-EM SPA and cryo-ET/subtomogram averaging (STA), achieving 5.1 Å resolution for reconstituted rigid NS-segment RNP and 8.0–9.7 Å for native RNP. This integrative approach enabled the first precise three-dimensional reconstruction of the influenza virus RNP complex, revealing a right-handed, antiparallel double-helical architecture and conclusively resolving the previously disputed issue of its helical handedness. The study also shows that the viral RNA is encapsidated within the groove of the double helix by NP subunits, with adjacent NP molecules connected through a highly flexible tail loop. Specifically, the distal turn of one NP's tail loop inserts precisely into a conserved surface pocket on the neighboring NP, while the bridge helix engages a helical bundle within the adjacent head lobe. Together, these interactions constitute a dynamic inter-subunit interface. This tail loop-mediated linkage imparts pronounced structural plasticity to the helical RNP, enabling axial and lateral flexibility as well as local groove widening features that are essential for supporting strand sliding during processive RNA synthesis.

One of the most significant findings of the study is the elucidation of a long-standing question in the field. Namely, how the influenza polymerase (FluPol) can carry out processive RNA synthesis while preserving the integrity of the RNP's double-helical architecture, through the proposed “strand-sliding” model. The viral polymerase specifically anchors to the surface of the docking strand within the double helix and captures the 3′ end of the template strand RNA, driving the relative sliding of the two helical strands to sequentially feed the template into the catalytic center for processive synthesis. By integrating cryo-ET with graphene-coated grids, the authors captured distinct conformational states of the viral polymerase at both the pre-initiation stage, localized at the RNP terminus, and the elongation stage, located along the mid-region of the RNP, where the elongation complex adopts a more compact conformation. Further analysis revealed that NP subunits adjacent to the polymerase undergo a conformational switch from a “packing” to a “serving” conformation, characterized by minor groove widening and local subunit rotation. This transition exposes the RNA template for polymerase access, after which the newly synthesized RNA is rapidly re-encapsidated by trailing NP subunits, thereby preserving the overall double-helical architecture of the RNP.

This study not only elucidates, for the first time, the molecular mechanism underlying the dynamic coupling between FluPol and NP but also provides a structural framework for understanding the transcription-to-replication switch in influenza virus genome expression. Moreover, the observed conformational changes of NP, which facilitate access to the polymerase, provide a mechanistic basis for developing novel antivirals targeting dynamic RNP assembly.

Based on their mechanistic analysis of RNP assembly, the authors identified the tail loop binding interface mediating NP–NP interactions as a conserved and druggable antiviral target. This interface comprises two critical interaction regions: (1) the bridge helix of the tail loop engages a helical bundle in the adjacent NP head lobe; and (2) the distal turn of the tail loop inserts into a conserved pocket within the neighboring NP. The authors noted that targeting the bridge helix interface offers a greater advantage over the distal pocket, as it exhibits higher sequence conservation across influenza subtypes. To validate its functional relevance, four key residues within the bridge helix (F420S, R422A, I425S, and F429S) were mutated, resulting in disrupted NP–NP interactions, loss of oligomerization, and failure to form trimers or tetramers, thereby confirming the interface as essential for RNP assembly. Based on these findings, the authors constructed a pharmacophore model using key structural features of the bridge helix interface and performed large-scale virtual screening of over 30 million compounds. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays identified compounds that selectively bind to monomeric NP, where the interface is exposed, but not to trimeric NP, in which the interface is occluded. Cell-based antiviral assays using MDCK cells infected with influenza A/WSN/33 (H1N1) demonstrated that compounds 1 and 2 effectively inhibited viral replication, with IC50 values of 6.6 and 19.4 μmol/L, respectively, and showed no apparent cytotoxicity at 100 μmol/L. According to the docking models, these compounds bind at the entrance of the tail loop binding pocket, thereby blocking bridge helix engagement with the adjacent NP subunit and disrupting RNP assembly (Fig. 1).

The target site identified in this study, the tail loop binding interface, particularly the bridge helix region, complements and contrasts with previous work by Shen et al.8, who focused on the same general interface but specifically targeted the E339-R416 salt bridge. Compound 3 (IC50 = 2.7 μmol/L) mimics R416 to competitively bind the conserved salt bridge pocket, thereby destabilizing NP trimers and disrupting NP–polymerase interactions. In line with these findings, Zhang et al.9 demonstrated that triptolide derivatives (Compound 4) bind to the tail loop region of influenza NP and suppress its function through hydrogen bonding and aromatic stacking interactions, thereby effectively inhibiting viral replication and further underscoring the essential role of the tail loop domain in NP function. In summary, targeting the NP tail loop region, particularly the highly conserved bridge helix interface, represents a promising strategy for the development of broad-spectrum antiviral agents against influenza.

Notably, a similar structure-guided antiviral discovery paradigm has demonstrated broad applicability across viral systems, further underscoring the central role of structural biology as a foundational platform for target identification. A recent study led by Petr Chlanda10, published in Cell, applied in situ cryo-ET to visualize the dynamic structural transitions of Ebola virus replication factories. Their findings revealed that during infection, Ebola virus nucleocapsids (NCs) progressively condense from loosely coiled, left-handed helices into rigid cylindrical assemblies that further organize into highly ordered parallel bundles. Importantly, the host cytoskeletal protein vimentin was found to form intermediate filament networks closely associated with these factories, playing essential roles in nucleocapsid bundling and spatial dispersion. Cryo-ET combined with immunolabeling demonstrated that vimentin maintains the structural integrity of replication factories and facilitates nucleocapsid trafficking toward budding sites. Disruption of vimentin structure or function significantly impaired Ebola virus replication and morphogenesis, highlighting it as a host-derived, druggable factor with potential for broad-spectrum antiviral intervention (Fig. 2).

Together, the studies led by Yi-Wei Chang1 and Petr Chlanda10 represent milestone advances in both fundamental virology and antiviral drug discovery. While traditional antiviral drug development often relies on co-crystal structures11, these works collectively exemplify how high resolution structural biology can drive precision-targeted antiviral strategies. By resolving the dynamic architecture of core viral replication machinery, they identify conserved and functionally essential domains as druggable targets. Importantly, both studies converge on a common paradigm: detailed structural elucidation of viral proteins enables the discovery of structurally conserved and functionally dependent interfaces, providing a stable and reproducible molecular basis for the development of broad-spectrum antiviral agents. This emerging paradigm offers a solid foundation for the rational design of broad-spectrum antivirals targeting present and future viral challenges.
