Comprehensive basic and clinical studies are needed to improve the treatment and outcome of patients with Kearns-Sayre syndrome
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

We read with interest the article by Lu et al. about a 10-year-old girl with Kearns-Sayre syndrome (KSS) who initially presented with lower limb weakness and Fanconi syndrome at the age of 3 years, followed by growth retardation at the age of 5 years, bilateral ptosis since the age of 8 years, and diabetes and third-degree atrioventricular block (AV block III) at the age of 10 years (1). The examination at the age of 10 years also revealed sensorineural hearing loss, lactic acidosis, reduced folic acid levels, renal insufficiency and elevated proteins in the cerebrospinal fluid (1). A muscle biopsy revealed ragged red fibers (RRFs), and genetic testing revealed a single mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) deletion of 7,521 base pairs and variant c.865C>T in DNA2 (1). The patient benefited from dietary nutrition with a mitochondrial cocktail (1). The study is remarkable, but some points require further discussion.

The first point is that the heteroplasmy rate of the mtDNA variant has not been reported. Since the heteroplasmy rate considerably determines the phenotypic expression of a mtDNA variant (2), it is crucial to know the heteroplasmy rate in clinically affected and unaffected tissues for the assessment of disease progression and outcome as well as for genetic counselling. As a rule, higher heteroplasmy rates are associated with more severe phenotypes.

The second point is that the family history was not reported in detail. Since single mtDNA deletions are inherited through the maternal line in 4% of cases (2), we should know whether the mother of the index patient was also a carrier of the mtDNA deletion and, if she was a carrier, whether she manifested clinically with features of KSS. It would also be interesting to know whether both parents or other first-degree relatives were tested for the DNA2 variant and whether one of them carried the variant or whether it had occurred sporadically in the index patient.

The third point is that the patient only had a cerebral computed tomography, but no cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (1). Since stroke-like episodes (SLEs) also occur in KSS patients, especially in patients with KSS/MELAS overlap syndrome (3), it is recommended to also perform cerebral MRI or cerebral fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) to assess whether subtle morphologic or metabolic abnormalities are present.

The fourth point relates to cardiac involvement in the index patient (1). Since the patient also had cardiomyopathy with systolic dysfunction (1), we should know what kind of cardiac treatment was given. Since not only AV block III but also ventricular arrhythmias can occur in KSS patients (4), it makes sense that long-term ECG recordings are also performed in KSS patients to assess the risk of sudden cardiac death and to determine whether there is an indication not only for a pacemaker but also for an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD). Since DNA2 mutations can also manifest in the myocardium (5), it should be discussed to what extent the cardiac abnormalities of the index patient are due to DNA2 and not to the mtDNA variant. What were the results of the cardiac MRI with contrast agent?

The fifth point is that the patient carried not only an mtDNA variant but also a DNA2 variant, suggesting that the KSS phenotype was altered by the second disorder. We should therefore know which of the clinical manifestations are due to the mtDNA variant and which are due to the DNA2 variant. This is crucial as the therapeutic approach for the DNA2 variant might be different from that for the mtDNA variant. The nuclear DNA (nDNA) mutation may also have implications for genetic counselling.

The sixth point is that it is incomprehensible why the patient was diagnosed with myasthenia gravis at the age of 8 years and why steroids and tacrolimus were administered (1). We should know the results of low-frequency repetitive nerve stimulation, tests for antibodies against acetylcholine receptor (AChR) and muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), titin and lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4), edrophonium test, and computed tomography of the chest, and whether the patient had thymoma or not. Since the patient has developed diabetes, we should know whether the diabetes was triggered by the glucocorticoid treatment or not. Steroids are also known to be potentially harmful in KSS (6), so they should be avoided in KSS.

To summarize, KSS patients require comprehensive clinical and instrumental investigations, especially if they carry not only an mtDNA deletion but also an nDNA variant that additionally affects mitochondrial function, morphology, and reproduction.
