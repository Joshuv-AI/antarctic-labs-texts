Cystic fibrosis: Correction of a fatal disease
Authors: Jeffrey M. Friedman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The 2025 Lasker~DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award has been given to Michael Welsh, Jesús (Tito) González, and Paul Negulescu for their key roles in developing a novel treatment for cystic fibrosis (CF)—a three-drug combination that saves the lives of people with this lethal genetic disease [D. Keating et al., N. Engl. J. Med. 379, 1612–1620 (2018)]. The disease is caused by mutations that disrupt the function of a gene known as CF Transmembrane Conductance Regulator which encodes a chloride channel expressed in epithelial cells including the lung. Collectively the three recipients were responsible for the development of novel high-throughput drug screens that led to the development of the new drugs. Welsh is a pulmonologist who played a key role in understanding the physiology and pathophysiology of the disease. González is a physical organic chemist who developed a novel technology that enabled robust high-throughput screens for drugs that correct the channel defects. Negulescu led a group of extremely talented biologists, chemists, and physicians who built on these advances to develop novel three-drug combinations that have miraculous benefits for the majority of afflicted patients. This advance represents a true milestone in medicine and fulfills a dream of research scientists and families—the conversion of a fatal disease into a fully treatable one. It is also a stunning example of the power of medical research to save people’s lives.

There was an old wives tale in northern Europe that children with salty tasting sweat would not live past childhood. Our understanding of the cause began in 1938 when Dorothy Anderson, a pathologist at Columbia University encountered malnourished children who did not have celiac disease, another cause of malabsorption (1). Pathologic analyses revealed cystic lesions and scarring in the pancreas, leading to a failure to produce pancreatic enzymes resulting in malabsorption. Anderson also found that patients who survived the neonatal period often developed a progressive disease of the lungs and that it was inherited as an autosomal recessive gene. Diagnosis was difficult and required assaying duodenal fluid to measure the levels of pancreatic enzymes. In 1943, Anderson, together with Paul di Sant’Agnese introduced the use of newly developed sulfa and penicillin antibiotics which could quell the patients’ pulmonary infections, temporarily. Then during a heat wave in the summers of 1948 and 1949, di Sant’Agnese encountered several of his patients in the pediatric ward with severe dehydration. He noticed a chalky substance (salt) on a glass from which one of the children was drinking and di Sant’Agnese hypothesized that the cystic fibrosis (CF) children might be susceptible to heat stroke because of abnormalities in their sweat (1). He tested this by placing two CF and two normal children under a blanket in a hot room and collected sweat on a piece of gauze. The results were striking. The salt concentration in the sweat was increased with chloride levels 3 to 5 times higher than normal. The sweat test quickly became the basis for diagnosing CF but it also created a mystery. How does a single gene mutation alter the composition of bodily fluids and disrupt the function of multiple organs? The answer began to emerge three decades later as scientists began to study the electrical properties across epithelial membranes in CF patients.

In 1981 Richard Boucher measured the transepithelial electrical potential difference across the nasal and lower airway mucosa of CF patients and found a highly significant increase in the negative charge in the lumen (2). However, this study did not distinguish whether this was a result of abnormal sodium or chloride transit. In 1983, studies of sweat glands by Paul Quinton found a block in chloride reabsorption, thus accounting for the increased salt content in CF sweat (3). In 1984, Kenzo Sato reported that the induction of sweat production by isoproterenol was reduced in CF patients and suggested that the chloride conductance was regulated by cyclic adenosine monophosphate (4). And finally in 1985, studies of primary bronchial epithelium by Jonathan Widdicombe, Michael Welsh, and Walter Finkbeiner showed that the defective chloride conductance is localized to the apical or luminal surface of the epithelial cells (5). Based on these and other studies, a consensus emerged that CF is caused by reduced chloride flux across apical membranes.

In 1989, Tsui et al. electrified the scientific community when they announced that they had identified the CF gene by positional cloning (6). The 168 kD encoded protein was homologous to a large family of carrier proteins that bind adenosine triphosphate (ATP) known as ATP-binding cassette transporters. They were uncertain whether the normal protein was an ion channel or transported a factor that regulated an ion channel, so they named it CF Transmembrane Conductance Regulator (CFTR). The same group also showed that a deletion of a phenylalanine at amino acid 508 (known as Δ508) was present in at least one copy in 70 to 88% of Caucasians with CF with a lower percentage of Δ508 in other populations (1). Hundreds of other mutant alleles have been identified and it is hypothesized that the ∆F508 allele is present at high frequency because it confers a selective advantage, perhaps by decreasing the severity of diarrheal illnesses such as cholera (7).

Attention now turned to studies aimed at understanding the physiology and pathophysiology of CFTR and the (remote) possibility that its function could be restored. Numerous groups contributed to these efforts, but Michael Welsh was consistently at the forefront. Welsh grew up on a farm in Iowa often milking the cows before school. Inspired by John Kennedy’s exhortation to contribute to society, he entered medical school at the University of Iowa where he recalls being deeply troubled over the condition of a CF patient. Impressed by the faculty there, he aspired to become a physician-scientist and as a pulmonary fellow at University of California San Francisco, he became fascinated by how salt and water move across the epithelial sheets in airways. In 1981, he returned to the University of Iowa as a faculty member where he published several seminal studies on the normal function of CFTR and the pathophysiology of CF. Welsh’s group was among the first to show that the introduction of the normal channel could correct the chloride permeability defect in CF cells. In 1992 Welsh tested the hypothesis that CFTR is an ion channel by systematically mutating lysines in membrane spanning regions and found that this altered its ion selectivity (8). This demonstrated that CFTR is a chloride channel controlled by cAMP-dependent phosphorylation rather than being a regulator of an ion channel. CFTR is the only one of the many ABC transporters that is an ion channel. The loss of this ion channel leads to multiple pulmonary abnormalities including dehydration of airway mucus and decreased antimicrobial activity leading to impaired mucociliary clearance and decreased defenses against bacteria (9). This in turn leads to recurrent lung infections and a progressive decline in pulmonary function.

In 1990, Smith et al. at Genzyme showed that the Δ508 mutant protein was retained in the endoplasmic reticulum with an immature glycosylation pattern (10). Later studies from Lazdunski et al. reported that both the cell surface expression of ∆F508 and its channel function were decreased (11). Meanwhile, Drumm et al. studied the effect of mutations on CFTR function in Xenopus oocytes and found that ∆F508 mutation, as well as the G551D mutation (and others), were present on the cell surface, and that the induction of a chloride current by cAMP was markedly reduced (12). These results raised a puzzling inconsistency. While Smith and Lazdunski showed that little or no Δ508 made it to the cell surface in mammalian cells, Drumm and Collins showed relatively normal expression in Xenopus oocytes. Welsh resolved this discrepancy by showing that the Δ508 mutant channel is temperature sensitive. At the higher nonpermissive temperature such as in mammalian cells, the mutant protein is degraded and doesn’t make it to the cell surface. At the lower permissive temperature such as in Xenopus, the mutant protein folds normally and transits to the cell surface but opens much less frequently than wild-type CFTR (13). Thus, restoring the function of Δ508 would require that one develop drugs that both “correct” its abnormal folding and “potentiate” its abnormally decreased rate of opening. Drugs that address the latter were precedented but drugs addressing the former were not. But Welsh’s finding raised the possibility that small molecules might be able to replicate the effect of a low temperature to stabilize Δ508. His results also provided the basis for the pivotal drug screens that would follow.

In the 1990s, several groups began screening for “correctors” and “potentiators” to image anion transit in cells transfected with Δ508 using fluorescent sensors that are quenched when halides enter the cell, but none of the compounds were potent enough to develop further (1). According to the book Breath from Salt, in 1999 Alan Verkman who had developed an improved halide sensor, spoke to Bob Beall the head of the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation (CFF) and suggested that he engage companies willing to initiate drug screening efforts. Beall then spoke to one of his advisors Raymond Frizzell who referred him to his college roommate Roger Tsien, a professor at UCSD (1). Tsien together with Charles Zuker and Michael Geoffrey had recently started a new company capable of executing such a project, Aurora Biosciences. Tsien was a brilliant chemist who developed numerous chemical methods for studying biologic problems and he would later win a Nobel Prize. Unfortunately, Tsien passed away in 2016. Aurora was adapting methods developed by Tsien for use in high-throughput drug screens. After speaking to Beall, Tsien suggested that Paul Negulescu, who was Head of Cell Biology at Aurora and among the first hires, submit a grant to the CFF seeking funds to screen for drugs (1). With funding from the Gates Foundation, the grant was awarded to Aurora and it underwrote their search for correctors and potentiators in what later became the most successful example of Venture Philanthropy to date.

Paul Negulescu grew up in San Francisco with dreams of becoming either an archaeologist or architect and entered UC Berkeley as a History Major. As a college junior he attended a physiology course by Tsien on how the human body works and was so inspired that he decided to get a joint degree in History and Physiology. As a graduate student, Negulescu used cell imaging to link Ca++ influx to acid production by the stomach. After a fellowship studying Ca++ influx in immune cells, Tsien had asked him to join Aurora where he directed the CF program from its inception through the development of Trikafta, the first three drug combination, 20 y later.

One of the core technologies that Aurora licensed from Tsien’s laboratory was a fluorescent voltage sensor that could be used in cultured cells. A voltage sensor is useful in drug screens because it can detect the potential difference across a membrane generated by changes in ion flux, making it a broadly applicable readout for many cellular events. This was especially useful for CF because CFTR is an ion channel and manipulating ion concentrations and/or opening and closing the channel changes the potential difference. Tsien had conceived of an idea for a voltage sensor decades earlier as a graduate student but he couldn’t get it to work. The person who did was Jesús “Tito” González.

Tito González’s family has lived near the South Texas border for more than 225 y. Growing up in Brownsville, Texas, and later Connecticut, he was fascinated by Nature from insects to the Universe. As an undergraduate at Rice University, he was broadly interested in Chemistry, Physics, Biochemistry, and Material Science but decided to study chemistry reasoning that it was important to all these fields. He entered the graduate program in chemistry at Princeton and joined a laboratory studying the catalytic mechanism of P450 monooxygenase using synthetic porphyrins inserted into membranes. Fascinated by molecules that orient in membranes, González embarked on his own path synthesizing “spin probes” that allowed him to study the structure and dynamics of these reagents inside membranes. As he was nearing the conclusion of his thesis work, Tsien gave a seminar at Princeton and González, like Negulescu, was enraptured. After a series of animated discussions about ways to create a voltage sensor González joined the Tsien laboratory.

The voltage sensor they developed employs Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) (Fig. 1). In FRET, excitation of a fluorescent “donor” in proximity to a second fluorescent moiety (typically 1 to 10 nanometers) leads to an energy transfer with a spectral shift of the emission from that of the donor to that of the acceptor (14). Thus, if one excites the donor when it is far apart from the acceptor, the emission spectrum matches that of the donor. But if they are in proximity, excitation of the donor activates the acceptor leading to a change in the emission. The method is analogous to two tuning forks with similar frequencies which when in proximity can transfer energy from one to the other without direct contact.

Use of a voltage sensor for drug screening. Panel (A) The design of the voltage sensor is shown. The sensor is composed of two fluorescent components: a coumarin labeled phosphatidylethanolamine at the cell surface (blue circle with COU) and a lipophilic oxonol, a membrane soluble anion (red circle with Ox) that migrates across the membrane with changes in voltage and emits red light. When membrane potential is positive the oxonol moiety is localized to the inner surface of the membrane and excitation of the coumarin with violet light leads to a blue emission. In contrast, when membrane potential is negative, the oxonol localizes to the outer surface of the membrane. In this case, excitation of the coumarin with violet light leads to FRET to the oxonol with an increase in red emission and a decrease in blue emission. Thus one can quantitate voltage changes by calculating the ratio of the intensity of blue vs. red emissions. Panel (B) The voltage sensor was used to screen for drugs that restore the function of the ∆508 CFTR mutation. CFTR is a chloride channel and the mutant protein has defects in both cell trafficking and channel opening. Screening for drugs that restore channel function thus requires two steps (Top). In the first, drugs that correct the abnormal trafficking of ∆508 CFTR are provided to cells for 16 h leading to transit of CFTR to the cell surface. The second step tests for potentiators that increase channel opening and depolarize the cells. In initial screens for correctors genistein, a known potentiator, was used. Recombinant cells expressing ∆F508 CFTR were subjected to high-throughput drug screens in a low chloride extracellular solution with the addition of forskolin. In the presence of correctors and potentiators, chloride flows through CFTR down its concentration gradient leading to depolarization which is detected by voltage sensor. Changes in membrane potential are visualized as a change in fluorescence ratio as shown in the rainbow plot on the Bottom Left panel [Note, the rainbow color scheme is pseudocolored and is thus different from the color scheme in (A)]. In the Lower Middle panel, the red trace shows an increased cellular depolarization when the function of ∆508 CFTR is restored after treatment with a corrector and potentiator. González and Tsien used FRET to detect voltage changes by placing one fluorescent donor at the cell surface together with a fluorescent anion that rapidly redistributes in the membrane with changes in voltage. If the cell is depolarized, the fluorescent anion localizes to the inner side of the membrane and is too distant to be efficiently excited by the donor at the cell surface and there is a low FRET signal. However, if a cell is hyperpolarized, the fluorescent anion transits to the outer side of the membrane and is excited by the donor resulting in a diminution of the fluorescent signal from the donor and a simultaneous increase in the emission from the acceptor. This dual change allows one to calculate a ratio of the signals to measure the size of the voltage change.

Making this work required extensive testing and optimization of both the donor and the acceptor. For the donor, González employed chemical methods he learned as a graduate student to synthesize a coumarin labeled phosphatidyl ethanolamine. For the acceptor, he modified oxonol compounds that Tsien had first synthesized as a graduate student. Ultimately the method González and Tsien developed showed a 100% change in fluorescence per 100 mV change in voltage within milliseconds with sensitivity ~10-fold to 50-fold greater than existing sensors. González and Tsien authored two papers describing the method and the two were sole inventors on two patents licensed by Aurora. The next step was to adapt this system to perform high-throughput screens to identify drugs.

Tsien induced González to join Aurora shortly after Negulescu and he became Head of Ion Channels there. Together with a physicist Alec Harootunian, the two were tasked with incorporating the voltage sensor into high-throughput drug screens. By 1999 González’s optimized screening assays were in place (15) and by 2000 drug screens had begun (1). In 2001, Aurora was acquired by Vertex Pharmaceuticals and Negulescu, now Head of the Program, also assembled a team of chemists to accelerate the screening and transform chemical hits into drugs.

With continued funding from the CFF, Tom Knapp used the voltage sensor to screen chemical libraries selected by Peter Grootenhuis for hits that either potentiated or corrected the Δ508 mutant protein expressed in 3T3 cells provided by Welsh (16). These assays were based on Welsh’s finding that the mutant protein is temperature sensitive. Screens for potentiators were performed at the permissive lower temperature while screens for correctors assayed cells at the higher nonpermissive temperature in the presence of genistein, a weak potentiator previously identified by another group (Fig. 2) (17). The assays included forskolin which increases cAMP and is essential for opening the channel. By 2000, the team was screening thousands of molecules per day for possible correctors and potentiators and by 2003 chemical hits ultimately leading to the first two drugs for treating CF had been identified (1). These hits were then optimized by Sabine Hadida and a team of chemists who synthesized chemical series based on them. These new chemicals were then further validated by a growing team of biologists including Fred Van Goor who tested them in primary cultures of bronchial epithelium and other cells (16). One particularly enthralling moment was Van Goor’s demonstration that the drugs could restore the normal ciliary action of primary broncho-epithelial cultures derived from the lungs of CF patients (1). By 2005, ivacaftor a highly potent potentiator with drug-like properties had been identified.

Because the correctors were not as far along, the group decided to develop ivacaftor as a single agent potentiator to treat patients with G551D mutations; the G551D protein is processed normally but spends little time in the open state. The small number of patients with this mutation were identified through the efforts of Bonnie Ramsey who led the CF Therapeutics Development Network, a registry of patients with different mutations. The results of the ivacaftor studies were breathtaking. Improvements in pulmonary function were already evident by 2 wk and by the end of 48 wk, patients had gained weight, had a normal sweat chloride, and symptoms were greatly diminished. It was a lifesaving treatment for a subgroup of patients (19). But the challenge of treating the ~90% of patients with the Δ508 mutation remained.

Here Hadida’s contributions proved invaluable as she advanced medicinal chemistry efforts starting with two different classes of correctors identified in the screens. She realized that because she was targeting a membrane protein, she would need to develop classes of compounds previously thought to be unsuitable as drugs because they violated what are known as Lipinski’s rules (1). She optimized two different types of correctors ultimately developing a pair of molecules that in combination with ivacaftor (Trikafta)results in the stunning efficacy that has changed the lives of most CF patients (18, 20). The clinical development team at Vertex also played a pivotal role receiving approval of 4 different drugs in 7 y often in different combinations (1). This was also the first time the FDA allowed tests of efficacy in primary cells, necessary because CF animal models were not available (1). Later, Jue Chen a Professor at Rockefeller University solved the structure of Δ508 in the presence of the drugs and showed that the two correctors bind to the correctly folded state of the protein and stabilize it (21).

The treatment of CF is an astonishing example of the power of medical research to deliver lifesaving treatments to patients in need and highlights how federally funded research can provide a foundation for the development of new treatments by pharmaceutical companies. It is also the first example where the absent function of an abnormal protein was corrected thus providing the impetus for the development of other correctors to treat cancer and potentially other diseases.

In medieval Europe, there was a saying: “Woe to the child who tastes salty from a kiss on the brow, for he is cursed and soon will die” referring to the salty skin of babies with CF. In 2020, the median life expectancy for UK patients with CF was 36 but with novel three drug combinations, life expectancy is now predicted to be 82 y for patients who begin treatment by age 17 (20). It is because of the efforts of Welsh, González, and Negulescu and their colleagues at Vertex and in the academic community that this previously fatal, incurable disease is now well managed by safe three medicine combinations.
