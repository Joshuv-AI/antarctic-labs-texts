Experiences of nurses caring for state patients in North West Province of South Africa: a qualitative study
Authors: Dineo Mmolaeng, Leepile Sehularo, Ontlotlile Mpheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurses working with state patients face several challenges such as physical aggression and verbal threats while rendering care. State patients are individuals who have been charged with crimes but are deemed unable to face trial because of infirmity. This study explored and described the experiences of nurses caring for state patients in North West Province of South Africa.MethodsA qualitative exploratory-descriptive and contextual research design was adopted to gather the experiences of nurses caring for state patients. Thirteen nurses caring for state patients were purposively sampled from one selected public mental healthcare facility. Face-to-face in-depth semi-structured individual interviews were used to generate data. The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim, and the data were analysed separately by the researcher and co-coder using thematic analysis.FindingsFour themes emerged from data analyses: (1) psychological experiences of nurses caring for state patients, (2) consistently setting limits and maintaining boundaries, (3) receiving positive feedback from patients and families, and (4) suggestions for improving care and support.ConclusionsEffective care delivery, adequate training, emotional support, and resources should be provided in mental health care settings to ensure proper care to state patients. Mental health care institutions should implement proactive and holistic support systems for nurses to perform effectively. This study suggests that improving patient care depends on enhancing the working conditions and emotional resilience of nurses through empathetic leadership.Clinical trial numberClinical trial number not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03953-6.

Background

In many countries such as China, Canada and Finland have established legal frameworks and prescribed norms governing the evaluation and management of state patients within mental healthcare systems [1–3]. From a global context, a state patient generally refers to an individual who receives healthcare services funded, subsidised, or fully provided by the government through public health systems rather than private means [1]. For instance, China has implemented clear guidelines under the Criminal Procedure Law for assessing and managing state patients. However, despite these frameworks, nurses encounter numerous challenges in managing state patients due to demanding and often traumatic work conditions [1]. In the United States (USA), nurses regularly experience violent incidents involving psychiatric patients, resulting in significant emotional distress, physical harm, psychological exhaustion, and decreased job satisfaction [4]. Similar experiences are documented in Canada where nurses frequently face physical aggression and verbal threats, contributing to high stress levels, burnout, and subsequent challenges with staff retention [2, 5]. Comparable situations are salient in European contexts, particularly in countries like Finland where forensic nurses manage highly unpredictable patient behaviour, frequently encountering traumatic events such as assaults and intimidation [3, 6].

These negative experiences significantly affect the psychological well-being of nurses, reduce their ability to perform effectively at work, and compromise the quality of patient care. The cumulative impact of managing such hostile environments consistently results in emotional exhaustion, reduced morale, and increased burnout rates among nursing staff. These experiences call for clear and improved organisational support systems and safety measures [3]. These international experiences are closely related to those faced by South African nurses. Similar patterns of workplace stress and traumatic incidents have been reported by nurses managing state patients in South Africa (SA), including the North West Province (NWP). The challenges associated with caring for forensic psychiatric patients transcend national contexts and represent a global concern. Therefore, there is a critical need for coordinated interventions, adequate resource allocation, and improved institutional policies aimed at supporting nurses and fostering a safer, healthier, and more productive working environment, specifically in forensic healthcare settings such as those in the NWP of SA.

State patients are persons with intellectual or mental illness who have committed criminal offences [7]. In the context of SA, these individuals have committed criminal offenses but are deemed mentally unfit for incarceration such that they receive mental healthcare in designated facilities as stipulated by Section 42 of the Mental Health Care Act (MHCA) [8], of SA. On the other hand, the South African Criminal Procedure Act [SACPA] [9] Section 77 (6)(a)(i) and (ii) protects state patients from being jailed but rather that they receive care, treatment and rehabilitation services at mental health facilities. Recent studies have shown that approximately 58%-75% of state patients have a record of violent-related offence [10, 11], with an estimated 31%-42% of these reported to have been violent during their hospital stay [12, 13]. Nurses are at the forefront of providing healthcare to state patients [14]. These nurses play a central role in providing care, treatment, and rehabilitation services to this cohort known as state patients who are often under demanding and potentially hazardous conditions. These nurses face occupational risks such as physical aggression, verbal threats, and emotionally distressing situations which significantly impact their emotional health, job satisfaction, and work effectiveness. There is another problem in the global literature that there is a shortage of mental healthcare nurses who provide mental healthcare services to state patients [15, 16].

Nurses provide assessment, referral, and nursing intervention to state patients within their area of practice [17]. According to Urheim et al., [18], mental health nurses have distinct professional obligations and most times practice in a secure and controlled environment that has potentially violent state patients. Interestingly, nurses are the fulcrum of care in healthcare facilities with demanding and stressful job descriptions [19]. Moreover, nurses are exposed to various challenges related to traumatic events such as being hit with objects and spat upon [20]. Among other traumatic experiences of nurses include the threat of violence, verbal aggression, and physical assault resulting in injuries [21, 22]. In addition, Kriakous et al., [23] confirm that managing state patients is emotionally challenging and stressful to healthcare workers as they are often hostile and traumatising in workplace environments. These traumatic experiences have potentially negative effects on the health of these nurses [24]. Furthermore, burnout is common among nurses working with state patients; this exerts a significant impact on their job performance and quality of life [25]. In SA, state patients are referred to a mental healthcare facility by a competent court for forensic observation under Section 79 of the Criminal Procedure Act [26, 27]. State patients’ units have distinct challenges in mental healthcare facilities [19]. Nurses working in these mental healthcare facilities experience burnout and distress as healthcare providers to behaviorally difficult state patients [28, 29]. As such, nurses managing state patients in SA struggle to provide quality care because of the continuous threats they receive from state patients [29].

In Mafikeng, located in the NWP and the setting of this study, nurses face similar challenges, often exacerbated by staff shortages and inadequate institutional support. There are frequent incidents of verbal threats and physical aggression from state patients, leading to psychological distress, diminished morale, compromised professional identity, and ethical dilemmas in patient management. These experiences highlight the urgent need for improved support systems and targeted interventions to enhance the working conditions of nurses. That could improve resilience and patient care outcomes in forensic mental healthcare settings in the NWP. Interestingly, there is a lack of published research studies on the experiences of nurses caring for state patients, particularly in the NWP, SA. Therefore, exploring and describing the experiences of nurses caring for state patients in this region provides contextual knowledge crucial for improving the quality of care, treatment and rehabilitation services. Hence, this study explored and described the experiences of nurses caring for state patients in NWP, SA.

Methods

The methods followed to achieve the aim and objectives of this study are described below.

Research design

A qualitative exploratory-descriptive and contextual research design was adopted to achieve the aim of this study. This contextual research design focuses on exploring previously under-investigated topics which allows researchers to obtain first-hand information and deep insights directly from nurses caring for state patients [30]. The descriptive aspect of the design facilitated detailed accounts of the lived experiences of nurses without imposing preconceived frameworks, while the exploratory element enabled researchers to identify new insights and understandings of the nurses caring for state patients. The qualitative exploratory-descriptive and contextual design further ensures that the experiences are explored and described within the natural settings and provide context-specific knowledge.

Study setting

The study was conducted at a selected public mental health care institution in the NWP of SA. There is only one public mental health care institution which provides care, treatment and rehabilitation services to state patients in the NWP of SA. This means that data obtained from this study cannot be generalised to other mental health care institutions in SA or globally.

Participants

The participants in this study included 13 nurses providing care for state patients at one selected public mental health institution in NWP of SA. The participants have been placed in the state patient unit for the past 18 months. The nurses ranged in age from 27 to 58 years and included both male and female participants with varying levels of professional experience. The majority (11) were professional nurses, while two were auxiliary nurses, offering representation across different nursing roles. Their years of experience ranged between 2 and 20 years, allowing for input from both less to highly experienced practitioners. This diversity in age, gender, and professional designation contributed to a well-rounded perspective on the challenges and practices related to service delivery. The study adopted a non-probability purposive sampling technique to identify the 13 study participants. Purposive sampling technique is the deliberate selection of participants to optimise answering the research question [31]. Table 1 below presents demographic information of the study participants.

Data collection

Data was collected from thirteen (13) nurses caring for the state patients at the mental healthcare facility in Mafikeng, NWP of SA. Data were collected using individual face-to-face semi-structured in-depth interview consisting of two questions. English language was used to allow an interactive, personalised exchange of information and enabled participants to express their experiences. Before the main data collection, approval was received from the North-West University Health Research Ethics Committee (NWU-HREC), the North West Department of Health (NWDoH), including goodwill permission from the Hospital management. A pre-test of the interview guide was conducted with five participants who were not included in the main study. The findings confirmed that the questions were clear, reliable, and valid in measuring what they were intended to measure. As a result, all the questions used in the pre-test were retained and applied in the main study. The duration of each interview ranged from 25 to 44 min, ensuring adequate time to capture rich and in-depth responses from participants. A notepad and an audio recorder were used to ensure all information was adequately captured. Data saturation was reached after the 13th participant. Data saturation is reached when no new information emerges from the participants, and additional coding is no longer feasible as themes become recursive [30]. An interview guide consisting of two main questions was used: “How can you describe your experiences [in] caring for state patients in NWP?” and “What are your suggestions for supporting the nurses caring for State Patients in NWP?“. A detailed version of the interview guide is available in Supplementary File 1. These open-ended questions facilitated participants’ voicing of their personal experiences and proffering practical recommendations. The interview method allowed flexibility for probing into participants’ responses, thus enhancing the depth of data collected. To ensure clarity in the presentation phase and to uphold the anonymity of responses, pseudonyms were given to all participants to track their responses in the reporting phase. Data was stored in password-protected electronic files, while hard copies of consent forms and field notes were kept in a locked cabinet accessible only to the researchers.

Data analysis

Data was analysed by the researcher and co-coder through the six steps of thematic data analysis as described by [32–34]. All the 13 participants selected participated in the study, making a 100% response rate. During the analysis, the steps included collection of data and familiarisation with information, arranging data methodically, reviewing the coding for patterns and generating themes, reviewing the codes and themes, defining themes and lastly writing this study. Data from individual interviews were systematically organised into specific constructs, with themes and categories identified through careful comparison within each construct. The emergent themes and sub-themes were presented in a tabular format, facilitating easy comprehension and interpretation. This collaborative analytical process strengthened the accuracy and trustworthiness of the findings which enhanced the understanding of the detailed experiences of nurses in caring for state patients in NWP of SA.

Trustworthiness

The study applied trustworthiness measures such as credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was ensured by representing the views of participants through prolonged engagement and member checking. Transferability was facilitated by providing detailed descriptions of the research context and allowing knowledgeable others to determine the applicability of the findings to similar settings. Dependability and confirmability were achieved through consistent documentation of the research process, peer debriefing, and maintaining an audit trail.

Ethical considerations

This article was designed based on the Helsinki Declaration. The study obtained ethical approval from the North-West University Health Research Ethics Committee (NWU-HREC; Reference Number: NWU-00047-24-A1). The procedures in this study with the human participants were in line with the NWU ethical standards. The ethical precepts applied in this study include informed consent, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and anonymity. Participants were fully informed about the purpose and procedures of the study and were given the latitude to withdraw at any stage without consequence. Their identities were protected using pseudonyms, and all data collected was securely stored and used solely for research purposes.

Theme 1: Psychological experiences of nurses caring for state patients

The first theme, psychological experiences of nurses caring for state patients, had six sub-themes, which are elaborated below.

Sub-theme 1.1: Adjustment to and insight into manage initial fear

Participants believe that their initial fear was surmounted by a poor understanding of the state patients’ mental illness. Participants also stated that through daily interactions with patients, they gained an understanding of state patients’ behaviour, which allowed them to feel more settled. The following are the direct excerpts from the participants:

When I was an assistant nurse, I was scared of state patients, but I learned and understood that they are just like any other person, especially when they are on medication, whereby the medication stabilises them [Participant 4]Initially, when you hear the term “state patient” and it’s explained that they have committed a crime, you may panic or feel a bit afraid. However, as time goes by and you interact and talk with them daily through ward activities, you begin to realise they are just people. [Participant 12]

When I was an assistant nurse, I was scared of state patients, but I learned and understood that they are just like any other person, especially when they are on medication, whereby the medication stabilises them [Participant 4]

These reflections demonstrate that the initial fear of the participants was rooted in limited knowledge of state patients’ mental illness. As their exposure and daily interactions increased, fear gradually gave way to understanding and acceptance.

Sub-theme 1.2: Fear stemming from patients’ conduct

Some nurses explained that they experienced fear due to the aggressive behaviour of the state patients. The participants stated that the hostile environment led to inability to work freely, culminating in high absenteeism rate at work.Because patients can be frightening, you become anxious and feel restricted or uncomfortable working around them. As a result, you might decide to stay away or be absent, even though that’s not an effective solution, it’s still how some people cope. [Participant 9]Sometimes patients become very aggressive, shouting or even threatening staff, and it creates an atmosphere where you are always on edge. You feel unsafe, and it becomes difficult to focus on your work because you are constantly anticipating a negative incident. [Participant 6]

These quotations reflect how the aggressive conduct of some state patients generates fear among nurses, creating an environment that feels unsafe and restrictive. The lack of effective deinstitutionalisation, coupled with stigma and family rejection, left participants feeling that their hard work was futile and unrecognised. This loss of motivation reflects personal disappointment and a systemic failure in ensuring continuity of care beyond the institution.

Theme 2: Consistently setting of limits and maintaining boundaries

Setting limits and maintaining boundaries entails clearly defining acceptable behaviour, establishing firm guidelines, maintaining professional distance, and enforcing rules consistently to ensure safety, order, and mutual respect within the patient-care environment. The sub-themes that emerged in this category include using interventions to de-escalate aggressive behaviours, employing therapeutic approaches and advanced communication skills, and ensuring a safe and supportive therapeutic environment.

Sub-theme 2.1: Using interventions to de-escalate aggressive behaviours

Participants noted the importance of consistently setting boundaries among all nurses. They further mentioned that maintaining these boundaries tends to be easier for male nurses but is rather challenging for female nurses.As a male nurse, you don’t necessarily have to be intimidating, but you need to have a presence that demonstrates your authority or superiority. However, female nurses need to be tougher to effectively control patients in the ward. [Participant 5]But, with the state patients, they are not psychotic. They are just okay. The only thing is to learn to have boundaries. You should know, you affirm your boundaries every time. They will try their luck every time. just firm, and then you manage them very well. [Participant 13].

As a male nurse, you don’t necessarily have to be intimidating, but you need to have a presence that demonstrates your authority or superiority. However, female nurses need to be tougher to effectively control patients in the ward. [Participant 5]

The participants reveal that boundary-setting is viewed as a central strategy in managing and de-escalating aggression among state patients. While male nurses often felt that their presence alone established authority, female nurses described the need to assert themselves more firmly to maintain control in the ward. Across both perspectives, consistency in affirming boundaries was seen as essential, with participants stressing that patients continually test limits and therefore require clear, unwavering responses.

Theme 3: Receiving positive feedback from patients and families

Receiving positive feedback from patients and families encompasses expressions of gratitude, verbal appreciation, acknowledgement of nurses’ dedication and effort, strengthened nurse–patient relationships, increased motivation and morale, and affirmation of the positive impact nurses have on patients’ recovery and well-being. This theme emerged as the third in this study, with only one sub-theme which is experiencing other rewarding moments in caregiving.

Theme 4: Suggestions from nurses for improving care and support

This theme focused on the suggestions from participants to improve care and support for the state patients. Discussion

This study explored and described the experiences of nurses caring for state patients in NWP of SA. The study findings offer deep insight into the emotional complexities, challenges, and rewards embedded in the lived experiences of these mental healthcare professionals. Beyond the challenges, the study also sheds light on the resilience and professionalism of the nurses, as well as their perspectives on practical strategies for improving care and support. The discussions presented below align with the four central themes of the study which are corroborated with relevant literature to buttress the context in which these nurses work.

Theme 1: Psychological experiences of nurses caring for state patients

The emotional journey of nurses caring for state patients is shaped by intense psychological demands. Their initial encounters were described as fearful and overwhelming, driven largely by the unpredictable and sometimes aggressive behaviour of state patients. This emotional tension triggered feelings of anxiety, uncertainty, and stress. Over time, however, nurses demonstrated a remarkable agility to adjust. They developed coping mechanisms, gained insight, and adopted professional strategies that enabled them to manage the complexities of psychiatric care. This transition reflects both the emotional labour and the psychological resilience required in such high-pressure settings. Domingue et al. [5] and Thwala et al. [29] confirm this process of adaptation, noting that mental health professionals gradually build emotional regulation, resilience, and clinical judgment, allowing them to thrive in demanding environments [35]. Despite these coping mechanisms, secondary trauma remained a recurring experience. Nurses spoke of painful incidents involving patient suicides and failed reintegration attempts, which evoked strong feelings of helplessness, sorrow, and frustration. The emotional bonds they formed with the patients intensified these losses, reflecting the humanistic commitment that nurses bring to their work.

Emotional attachment is a natural outcome in long-term psychiatric care as acknowledged by [36]. However, they caution that unresolved emotional strain may lead to compassion fatigue. When nurses receive support, especially through reflective practice and peer counselling, they are better positioned to process trauma and continue offering high-quality care [37]. These findings locate the pressing need for institutional systems that recognise and respond to the emotional well-being of mental health professionals. A sense of constant vigilance also marked their daily experiences. The unpredictable behaviour of patients meant that nurses were always on alert, which was both physically and mentally exhausting. Despite this strain, they accepted it as part of their professional responsibility. In such emotionally intense environments, maintaining clear professional boundaries became crucial. Nurses expressed the importance of being empathetic while also safeguarding their emotional health. Kuosmanen et al., [3] posit that setting consistent boundaries is essential when caring for State Patients, as it helps ensure personal safety, preserves therapeutic structure, and upholds professional integrity.

Theme 2: Consistently setting limits and maintaining boundaries

Participants voiced the delicate balance required to manage boundaries effectively. The intensity of interactions in forensic psychiatric settings often creates blurred lines between emotional connection and professional detachment. Nurses acknowledged the need to remain emotionally present yet cautious, avoiding over-involvement that could affect both their well-being and the therapeutic process. In a setting where patients may struggle with emotional regulation and social norms, consistent boundary-setting was described as necessary for maintaining order and ensuring safety for both staff and patients. This aligns with the findings of Kuosmanen et al., [3] who argue that upholding professional boundaries not only protects healthcare workers but also reinforces the structure needed for psychiatric recovery. Despite these challenges, nurses remained committed to maintaining dignity and respect in their interactions. They reported employing therapeutic techniques, using de-escalation strategies, and striving to promote a sense of safety and structure within the facility. Still, their efforts were often hindered by systemic limitations such as inadequate staffing and poor infrastructure, making it difficult to create the kind of therapeutic environment they envisioned [38]. highlight the critical role of adequate resources in sustaining a safe, respectful, and supportive therapeutic environment. Without these essentials, both staff and patients face heightened risks of burnout, conflict, and diminished outcomes.

Theme 3: Receiving positive feedback from patients and families

Despite the psychological and structural challenges, nurses also shared meaningful and affirming experiences. Receiving appreciation from patients and their families provided emotional upliftment and reaffirmed the value of their work. Nurses expressed a deep sense of fulfilment when patients showed signs of progress, responded well to treatment, or were successfully reintegrated into the community. These positive moments helped reinforce their commitment to mental health care, reminding them that their efforts made a real difference [39]. note that nurses often derive professional pride from seeing the positive impact of their care [39]. further explain that patient appreciation can increase morale and strengthen the caregiver’s dedication. In this study, such experiences were described as validating and served as important psychological rewards in an otherwise demanding environment. Participants also discussed the importance of using effective therapeutic communication to build trust and foster patient cooperation. They emphasised that clear, respectful dialogue, active listening, and non-verbal sensitivity were essential for maintaining a calm atmosphere and addressing aggressive behaviours. However, nurses felt that achieving these goals consistently required systemic improvements that supported therapeutic engagement on all levels.

Theme 4: Suggestions from nurses for improving care and support

This section focused on solutions recommended by the nurses to improve care and professional support. One of the most consistent suggestions was the need for strong interprofessional collaboration. Nurses advocated for active involvement of psychologists, social workers, occupational therapists, and families in the rehabilitation of state patients. Their emphasis was on creating an integrated system of care that meets the numerous needs of state patients. This view is echoed in the works of [40] and [41] who argue that collaborative, patient-centred care is vital for effective rehabilitation [42]. also stress that such collaboration fosters better outcomes and long-term recovery. To further bridge the gap between institutional care and community reintegration, participants recommended psychoeducation and community outreach programmes that involve families in patient care activities [37]. support this view, affirming that family engagement and education can reduce stigma and support smoother reintegration.

Participants also raised concerns about staffing shortages, calling for more skilled professionals such as more male nurses to manage gender-specific challenges in male wards. Basic mental health training for all support personnel such as security staff, was suggested to ensure consistent, informed patient management. Similarly [43] and [44], advocate for adequate staffing and workforce training as contributing to quality care and reducing burnout.

Recommendations for caring for state patients

The findings of this study indicate that there is an urgent need to improve the working conditions and emotional well-being of nurses caring for state patients in NWP of SA. This invariably suggests that nurses should not be left to carry the burden of care alone. There should be regular mental health training, especially on how to manage aggression and communicate therapeutically to strengthen the confidence and skills of nurses. At the same time, there should be access to psychological support through counselling, debriefing sessions, or EAPs to help nurses to process the emotional strain they face daily. Online peer support groups facilitated through WhatsApp/Telegram and others could also be facilitated to provide a space where nurses can share experiences and learn from each other’s daily experiences. Findings from the study further highlight that staffing is another key area that needs attention. More nurses should be employed such as male nurses to balance gender dynamics in high-risk wards. Nurses also believe that categorising state patients based on their diagnosis and functionality can reduce conflict and allow for tailored care. This approach would create space for more focused rehabilitation activities such as cooking, gardening, or vocational training which can prepare patients for life outside the hospital and community integration. Community and family involvement is equally important and findings from the study confirmed that outreach programmes should be facilitated to educate families about mental illness and to encourage their support in the recovery journey of the state patients. Lastly, the study identified that strong and responsive leadership is essential and suggested that all nurses should be heard and always supported. Hospital management should regularly engage with staff, listen to their concerns, and create an environment where their suggestions are valued. Simple improvements like better communication, structured routines and the use of monitoring systems can make the work environment safer and more therapeutic for both patients and staff. When nurses are supported, they will be able to provide the compassionate quality care that state patients need to heal and be reintegrated into society.

Conclusion

This study focused on gaining insights into the experiences of nurses caring for state patients in the NWP of SA. Findings from the study confirmed that nurses in forensic psychiatric settings such as those in the NWP of SA are not only health professionals but also agents of rehabilitation, safety, and social reintegration. Their work exists at the intersection and interface of care and control, compassion and caution, which requires a delicate balance that is often undervalued within institutional structures. The findings from the study indicate that for meaningful care to occur, nurses must be supported through training and physical resources, and through psychological, managerial, and social systems. The emotional and mental toll experienced by nurses can undermine both patient outcomes and workforce sustainability if left unaddressed. As such, mental health care institutions should ensure that they transition from reactive models of support to proactive, holistic frameworks that promote the well-being and professional growth of nurses. Moreover, the role of families and communities cannot be overstated. Reintegration of state patients into society is not solely dependent on clinical outcomes but also on societal attitudes and acceptance. Thus, any comprehensive approach to forensic mental health should incorporate community outreach, education, and intersectoral collaboration. This study concludes that improving the quality of care for state patients is inseparable from improving the working conditions, emotional resilience, and professional agency of the nurses who care for them. A shift toward empathetic leadership, integrated support systems, and inclusive rehabilitation strategies could benefit patients and equally restore dignity and purpose to those who serve them on the front lines.

Limitations

The research focused exclusively on nurses and did not include the perspectives of other healthcare professionals such as psychologists, occupational therapists, or social workers, who also play critical roles in the care of state patients. The exclusion of other healthcare professionals limits the comprehensiveness of the findings, as the experiences and challenges of a multidisciplinary team may offer a more holistic view of institutional mental health care. The study was conducted only in the NWP of SA which may present contextual limitations. While the goal of qualitative research is not to generalise findings but to gain in-depth understanding, the specific socio-institutional characteristics of the province, such as infrastructure, staffing patterns, and regional policies, may differ significantly from those in other provinces in SA. Therefore, caution should be exercised when applying the findings to different geographical or healthcare settings. As a qualitative study, the results are context-bound and are not intended to be statistically generalisable to a broader population. Instead, they provide rich, narrative insights into the lived experiences of a particular group of nurses in a specific setting.
