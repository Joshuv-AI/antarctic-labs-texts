Actively targeted dendrimer-metal organic framework drug delivery nanoplatform for the treatment of neuroblastoma
Authors: Amr Kordy, Kholoud K. Arafa, Ahmed Abdellatif, Ibrahim M. El-Sherbiny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroblastoma remains a major therapeutic challenge due to inefficient drug accumulation within tumour cells and limited ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), causing the need for high systemic doses of chemotherapeutic agents, which causes severe off-target toxicity. In this study, we report the development and evaluation of a drug delivery system (DDS) based on a metal–organic framework (NH2-MIL-101(Fe)) core, surface-modified with hyperbranched poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimer and further functionalized with glutathione (GSH) to enhance BBB transport and intracellular delivery. The nanocarrier was loaded with doxorubicin (DOX) as a model anticancer drug and characterized for its physicochemical properties, drug release behavior, and biological performance. The DDS exhibited nanoscale size (150–200 nm), favorable surface charge (20 ± 0.3 mV), and a sustained drug release profile under tumour-mimicking conditions. In vitro cytotoxicity studies demonstrated significantly enhanced antiproliferative activity of the GSH-PAMAM-MOF (M3) system compared to the unmodified counterpart (M1) against SH-SY5Y and SK-N-AS neuroblastoma cell lines. Cellular uptake studies confirmed superior intracellular accumulation of DOX. Apoptosis assays and RT-PCR analysis further validated the induction of programmed cell death via upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes (p53 and Bax) and downregulation of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. Collectively, these results highlight the potential of the developed dendrimer-MOF hybrid nanoplatform as an efficient DDS for neuroblastoma therapy, offering improved efficacy and controlled release.

Introduction

Neuroblastoma treatment currently remains a challenging area of study due to the complex nature of this disease. Neuroblastoma is a type of tumour that develops from the neural crest affecting the sympathetic nervous system. The treatment of this disease usually requires protocols including intense chemotherapy, radiotherapy, stem cell transplantation and surgery.1 The main disadvantage of the current treatment protocols is the highly reduced patient quality of life caused by the adverse effects of these procedures. This is mainly caused by the inconveniently high side-effect profile associated with these procedures due to the low selectivity, leading to damage to normal as well as diseased cells.2 Therefore, the design of novel drug delivery carriers for the treatment of this disease is needed to ensure optimum treatment while maintaining patient convenience and quality of life.

Furthermore, the human brain shows unique characteristics and features that should be considered when developing a brain-targeted drug delivery system. The brain structure is covered with around 4 ounces of fluid called the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and is further safeguarded by the highly selective blood–brain barrier (BBB), which regulates the passage of substances from the bloodstream into the brain tissue. The BBB is considered the main protective structure of the brain, blocking most of the foreign small-molecule drugs as well as around all the large-molecule drugs from crossing into the brain. Although the brain is technically submerged within the CSF, drugs administered directly into the CSF using intrathecal injections do not ensure brain delivery of the drug. This is because the BBB and blood-CSF barrier are two distinct barriers anatomically and functionally. Drugs administered intrathecally diffuse through the leaky blood-CSF barrier into the blood at a rate equivalent to a slow IV-administered drug. Following that, drugs administered into the blood or CSF must cross the BBB to reach deep brain tissue.3

BBB consists of several components essential for its function. These include endothelial cells (ECs), tight junctions, pericytes, astrocytes, and the basal membrane. These components make up a physically tight structure essential for limiting the diffusion of foreign molecules and proteins.4,5 This structure includes endothelial cells that are tightly packed together, forming a strong barrier with minimal fenestrations, unlike endothelial cells found elsewhere in the body. Furthermore, tight-junction proteins seal the gaps found between endothelial cells preventing passive diffusion of ions and large molecules. Basal membrane adds a thin scaffold-like layer surrounding endothelial cells to provide structural support for enhanced barrier selectivity. Finally, pericytes and astrocytes are both types of cells enveloping the endothelial cells, and they function by communicating with each other as well as supporting the endothelial cells with various functions, ultimately maintaining the integrity of the BBB. It is also worth noting that all the components mentioned above are not static in the sense that they continuously adapt responding to various physiological changes in the brain.6,7

To overcome drug delivery challenges, new drug carriers are being developed and studied for facilitating drug delivery to the brain. Dendrimers have been extensively studied and have shown potential in enhancing drug delivery to brain tissues while maintaining desirable biocompatibility and safety profile. Polyamidoamine (PAMAM) is a type of dendrimer that has shown several favourable characteristics such as controlled size in the nano-range, ease of functionalisation, their high loading capacity, and good biocompatibility.8,9 PAMAM has already been studied as a drug carrier for anticancer applications and has shown the ability to enhance the efficacy of treatment while showing high biocompatibility.10–12 Ban et al. demonstrated that their developed PMPC-modified PAMAM dendrimers were able to achieve brain tumour targeted drug delivery while improving cellular uptake and biocompatibility, resulting in increased accumulation within cancerous tissues.10

Metal–organic framework-based drug delivery systems have also been recently studied due to their reported benefits. Their porous structures have been shown to provide high surface area for drug loading that is essential for biomedical drug delivery applications. In addition, iron-based MOFs have shown high biocompatibility and tolerability. In a study, it was shown that MIL-101 (Fe) based carrier caused improvement in cellular internalisation of loaded drugs while maintaining high biocompatibility in both in vitro as well as in vivo studies.13 In addition, in another study, MIL-101 (Fe) based nano-carrier was developed for application as an anticancer drug delivery system has shown pH-sensitivity, releasing significantly higher drug concentrations at tumour-mimicking pH conditions compared to normal cell pH.14 These studies demonstrated the potential benefits of incorporating this type of MOF-based DDS in cancer treatment due to its pH-sensitive releasing properties as well as its enhanced cell uptake.

Inefficient intracellular delivery of chemotherapeutic agents into neuronal tumour cells and the inability to cross the BBB are among the main limitations of neuroblastoma treatment. These limitations necessitate administrating higher doses of cytotoxic agents during treatment, leading to systemic off-target toxic effects, healthy tissue damage, and development of drug resistance, ultimately compromising treatment outcomes. Typically, effective DDSs should be able to deliver high concentrations of loaded drug molecules to their desired site of action effectively. Glutathione (GSH) has been recently studied as a targeting molecule for its ability to deliver drugs across the BBB exploiting specific blood-to-brain transporters and related efflux-regulating pathways on brain endothelial cells to target brain-specific diseases and promote carrier-mediated transcytosis across the BBB.15–17 Porkoláb et al. developed a DDS functionalized with alanine and glutathione highlighting the efficacy of GSH-mediated targeting for brain-directed DDSs.16 Another study by Fekete et al. quantified the specific adhesion of GSH-conjugated nanoparticles to brain endothelial cells, demonstrating mechanistic evidence of its role in enhancing nanoparticle uptake by the BBB.17 On the other hand, PAMAM dendrimers are emerging as effective surface modifiers in DDSs for enhancing cell uptake of anticancer drugs into tumour cells.10–12 The polycationic surface of PAMAM promotes strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged neuronal cell membranes, enhancing cell uptake and internalisation of loaded drug molecules. Dąbkowska et al. reported that PEGylated PAMAM dendrimers enabled efficient intracellular delivery and sustained release of BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) in differentiated SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells.11 In another study by Yesil-Celiktas et al., silica-PAMAM hybrid nanoparticles facilitated effective uptake into neuroblastoma cells, further highlighting PAMAM as an effective carrier for drug delivery in neuroblastoma treatment.12

This study aims to develop a novel drug delivery system composed of a MIL-101 (Fe) core coated with PAMAM dendrimer that is further conjugated with GSH for the effective delivery of anticancer drugs for the treatment of neuroblastoma. The adopted design is composed of the highly porous structure of the MIL-101(Fe) conjugated with hyperbranched structure of PAMAM should provide an accommodating platform for efficient drug loading. In addition, the integration of PAMAM-mediated enhancement of cellular uptake along with the GSH-mediated BBB transport properties present a rational strategy for enhancing brain accumulation as well as neuronal intracellular delivery of the loaded drug molecules. Furthermore, the study aims to characterise and examine the biological efficacy of the developed DDS using in vitro methods.

Materials and methods

Materials

2-Aminobenzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (H2ATA), 1,2-diaminoethane (EDA), N,N-diethylethanamine (TEA), dimethylformamide (DMF), (3,4,5-dimethyl-thiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), fetal calf serum (FCS), methanol (CH3OH), methyl acrylate monomer (MA), penicillin–streptomycin (Pen–Strep), reduced glutathione tripeptide (GSH) and Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium (RPMI 1640).

Methods

Synthesis of DDS

Synthesis of MOF (NH2-MIL-101(Fe))

The synthesis of NH2-MIL-101(Fe) was performed based on a method previously reported.18,19 For the inorganic metal source, FeCl3·6H2O was used, while H2ATA was the organic linker. They were both dissolved in DMF to make up a solution with molar ratio (2 : 1 : 156). This solution was placed in a Teflon-lined autoclave for 24 h at 120 °C before leaving the solution to cool. The NMOF powder was then washed to get rid of any excess unreacted products using DMF and methanol before drying at 60 °C for another 24 h.

Synthesis of polyamidoamine (PAMAM)

PAMAM polymer was synthesized using method previously reported.19,20 The method involves repeated steps of Michael addition and amidation reactions. The reactions are repeated until required generation is achieved. To prepare the dendrimer core, the first step involved adding MA and EDA to separate beakers containing CH3OH (15 mL). MA solution previously prepared was then added dropwise over EDA solution. The reaction was left stirring for 2 days. To control reaction temperature and to ensure inert conditions, the reaction was performed over an ice bath and under nitrogen gas. Finally, rotary evaporator was used to dry the obtained 0.5 G dendrimer solution at 50 °C for 20 min to ensure removal of excess methanol and unreacted species. Similarly, to prepare 1 G PAMAM dendrimer, 0.6 mL of EDA was added dropwise over a solution composed of the prepared 0.5 G PAMAM (1 g) in CH3OH (20 mL) using the previously mentioned reaction conditions applied in the previous step. Finally, to prepare 1.5 G PAMAM, MA (1.2 ml) was added using the previously mentioned conditions to the prepared 1 G PAMAM (1 g) (Fig. 1).

Graphical representation of the modified DDS composed of a MOF-based core, conjugated with PAMAM branching and functionalized using GSH.

Conjugation of NH2-NMOF with 1.5 G PAMAM and glutathione

PAMAM was chemically conjugated to NMOF by means of amidation reaction between the ester-terminated 1.5 G PAMAM (0.15 g) and NH2-MIL-101(Fe) (0.075 g) using the same previously mentioned conditions.19 Additionally, a cosolvent system was prepared to facilitate conjugation of GSH (0.46 g) to the PAMAM-coated NMOF (0.25 g) (composed of DMF (30 mL) and distilled water (15 mL)). This step was possible due to the presence of free unreacted ester groups which were utilized to form amide bonds with the amino group present in GSH. DOX was used to load the prepared DDS systems NMOF (M1), PAMAM-coated NMOF (M2) and GSH-ligated PAMAM-coated NMOF (M3) as the active anticancer agent. To induce alkalinization, TEA (5 µL) was added to a solution of DOX (0.015 g) dissolved in dH2O (10 mL) under constant stirring. Furthermore, the alkalinized drug solution was added to a suspension composed of the DDSs (0.060 g) suspended in dH2O (30 mL) and left stirring for 24 h inside a sealed container. Centrifugation (10 000 rpm for 20 min) was then used to obtain loaded formulation which was then dried at 50 °C (Fig. 2).

Scheme of the M3 DDS synthesis process showing the chemical conjugation steps involved in its preparation.

Characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

To observe morphological structure of the prepared NMOF, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed. NMOF nanosuspension was stained, dehydrated, and then fixed on a carbon coated copper grid for visualization under the microscope.

Particle size and zeta potential

Particle size (PS) and zeta potential (ZP) were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) with a Malvern zetasizer. Measurements were repeated three times for each sample and mean values were recorded.

TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to evaluate thermal stability and compositional characteristics of the DDSs MOF matrix using a temperature range of 10–700 °C under nitrogen atmosphere using a heating rate of 10 °C per minute.

In vitro drug release

In vitro drug release assessment was done on M1 in comparison with M3 to study the difference in their drug releasing properties. Both DDSs were dispersed separately in 1 mL of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at pH conditions mimicking the tumour microenvironment (pH 6.5). Additionally, M3 DDS was also tested at normal physiological mimicking environment (pH 7.4) to show the stimuli-responsive release properties of the modified DDS. For this purpose, DOX-loaded DDSs containing (1.0 mg DOX) were each placed in their respective sealed dialysis bags and consequently immersed in a 5 mL release medium of the same PBS previously mentioned. The setups were then placed inside a shaker incubator under dark conditions and 37 °C. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of PBS solution was pipetted out and replaced with an equivalent volume of fresh PBS. Finally, the released DOX amount was determined using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (λmax = 480 nm). (DOX)total: total amount of DOX-loaded within DDS.

In vitro biological assessment

Cell culture conditions

Human Neuroblastoma neuronal cells (SH-SY5Y, ATCC CRL-2266; SK-N-AS, ATCC CRL-2137™), were grown in T75 flasks containing RPMI 1640 with 10% FCS and 1% Pen–Strep. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 95% air and 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity assay

Diluted nanopreparation samples (0.1 mL) were added to SK-N-AS and SH-Sy5Y cells. Treatment samples were tested at different concentrations (150, 75, 40, 20, 10 µg mL−1) and DMSO was used as a control. Treatment effects were evaluated following 24 hours of incubation, and cell viability was evaluated using conventional MTT reduction assay. The supernatants were carefully aspirated, and dimethyl sulfoxide (0.12 mL) was added to each well to dissolve the precipitated dark blue formazan crystals. Finally, the absorbance, which is directly proportional to the number of live cells, was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (SPECTROstar Nano microplate reader, BMG Labtech) at 38 °C. Calculations of viability were performed using the following equation:Cell viability% = (At)/(Ac) × 100%where “At” is the blank corrected absorbance of treated cells, and “Ac” is the blank corrected absorbance of control cells. Data analysis was done by GraphPad prism version-8 software.

Fluorescence microscopy

SH-SY5Y cells were treated for 24 h with either M1 or M3 in 6-well culture plates (3 × 105 cells per well). The cells were then washed with PBS (pH = 7.4), fixed by adding (0.1 mL) of methanol for 10 min, followed by washing with (0.1 mL) of PBS. DAPI (nuclear stain) working solution (0.1 mL) was added to each well for 5 min, followed by washing with PBS. Images were captured using a fluorescent microscope.

Cell-cycle analysis

To perform cell cycle analysis, the most cytotoxic formulation (M3) was chosen. First, SH-SY5Y cells were divided into negative control and treated groups. The treated group was incubated with a concentration equivalent to the IC50 values for 72 h, while the control group was left untreated for the same duration of time. Afterwards, treated and non-treated cells were trypsinized and fixed with previously cooled ethanol (66% v/v). Afterwards, the cells were stained using PI and RNase before incubation in dark conditions at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, detection of DNA content of the samples was done using FL2 (λex/em = 535/617 nm) signal detector using ACEA Novocyte™ flow cytometer (ACEA Biosciences Inc, San Diego, CA, USA).

Apoptosis induction assay

The most cytotoxic formulation (M3) was chosen for apoptosis induction analysis using flow cytometry. First, cells were divided into negative control and treated groups. The treated group was incubated with a concentration equivalent to the IC50 value for 72 h, while the control group was left untreated for the same duration of time. Afterwards, the cells were trypsinised and washed with PBS (pH 7.4) before being centrifuged and suspended in 0.5 mL binding buffer solution. Finally, the cells were stained using Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) and analyzed with flow cytometer BD FACSCalibur.

Real-time PCR (RT-PCR)

Biorad iScript One-Step RT-PCR Kit was used to perform RT-PCR using SYBR Green kit following the manufacturer's instructions to evaluate and compare expression of pro-apoptotic genes (p53 and Bax) and anti-apoptotic gene (Bcl-2). Extraction of total RNA from lysates of treated cells was done using Qiagen RNA extraction kit. Furthermore, the extracted RNA was converted to cDNA for amplification by RT-PCR. Finally, Rotor-Gene software was used to analyze the obtained data. GAPDH was incorporated as a housekeeping gene for normalization purposes. The used primer sequences are listed in Table 1.21

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were represented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA test and all pairwise multiple comparison procedures (Holm-Sidak method), with overall significance level P = 0.05 were used for comparisons of parameters in study groups (Sigma plot 11 software).

Results and discussion

Synthesis of the DDS and release profile

Physicochemical characterization

Detailed structural and physicochemical characterization affirming the successful chemical conjugation and nanoparticle formation, including 1HNMR, FTIR, XRD, DLS, and ZP analyses, were performed, presented, and discussed in details in a previously published article by our team.19 As shown in Fig. 3, the incremental increase in particle size as per DLS data measurements from 150–375 nm suggests proper chemical conjugation of PAMAM to the core NMOF (M1), followed by successful GSH-functionalization to yield the targeted DDS (M3). Taking into consideration, the measured PDI for M1, M2 and M3 samples were found to be 0.37, 0.44, and 0.47 respectively. Concomitantly, the zeta potential affirmed the primary conjugation of the multi-cationic chains of PAMAM to M1, leading to drastic increase in ZP which was partially neutralized back post-GSH-conjugation. The increase in surface potential of M2 is attributed to the surface modification and the presence of internal tertiary amine groups within 1.5 G PAMAM structure that undergo partial protonation under physiological conditions, increasing surface charge of the DDS. Furthermore, results showed that modifications done to achieve M3 DDS increased EE% and LC% when compared to the starting M1 DDS. These results demonstrate the enhanced drug accommodating efficiency of the modified system, which can be explained by the increased drug homing capacity within the dendrimer branches when conjugated to the porous MOF core. When loaded with alkalinized DOX, M3 showed EE% and LC% of 67.52 ± 2.91 and 15.42 ± 0.57 respectively while the unmodified M1 DDS had an EE% and LC% of 50.86 ± 3.94 and 11.52 ± 0.49 respectively.

TEM imaging was used to show the morphological characteristics of the prepared DDS. The TEM images show octahedral shaped particles with particle size ranging from around 110–200 nm, which is typical of MIL-101 (Fe) structure, Fig. 4.

TGA analysis

TGA analysis was performed on the starting M1 as well as the final conjugated M3 formulations to ensure structural integrity and conjugation of the developed DDSs. Results showed that tested DDSs (M1, M3) quickly decomposed at temperatures higher than around 250 °C, Fig. 5.22 Additionally, at temperatures around 400 °C there is an additional 10% weight loss from the M3 sample compared to plain MOF (M1) sample which is likely caused due to decomposition of PAMAM branching and ligated GSH molecules.23 The obtained data goes in coherence with previous reports demonstrating PAMAM amide backbone degradation occurring at temperature range of 300–500 °C.24

In vitro drug release

Release study was performed using DDSs containing an equivalent amount of DOX (1 mg in each dialysis bag) as calculated from drug loading measurements to study the release behavior of the loaded DDSs. The release profile study performed at conditions mimicking normal physiological conditions (pH 7.4) showed minimal release over around 144 hours reaching around 14% from the drug-loaded modified M3 DDS. On the other hand, DOX-loaded DDSs (M1, M3) were used to study and compare the release profiles under conditions mimicking tumour microenvironments (pH 6.5). As shown in Fig. 6, results obtained showed a burst release of DOX during the first 8 h from both DDSs. However, the rate of burst release of the drug was different between the two counterparts. The pristine system (M1) released around 40% within the first 8 h time frame. This would be attributed to the drug particles potentially adsorbed onto the surface of the DDSs. Meanwhile, the branched nanocarrier (M3) released only around 20% during the first 8 h, followed by a second phase of sustained release reaching around 75% over a period of 140 h. This biphasic release behavior may be attributed to the multilayer architecture of the M3 system, where doxorubicin is entrapped within both the MOF core as well as the PAMAM dendrimer branching. Thus, the primary burst release seen in M3 might be attributed to the drug molecules residing within PAMAM branches. Additionally, the dendrimeric coating likely acts as a diffusion barrier, leading to prolonged drug release from the core. Further kinetic modeling was carried out for both M1 and M3 to understand the regulators of drug release (Table 2). Modeling results for both M1 and M3 indicated superior fitting to Weibull (R2 = 0.958 and 0.991, respectively). Comparing the shape parameter (β) for M1(0.972) and M3(0.996). Hence for both DDSs, the β-coefficient lies between 0.75–1, thus suggesting an anomalous drug release which indicates that a combination of non-Fickian diffusion and polymer-relaxation-controlled release.25,26 Such sustained release is particularly advantageous for neuroblastoma therapy, as it may reduce the need for frequent administration and therefore reduce systemic toxicity while maintaining drug release within tumour tissues.

The antiproliferative effects of DDSs on neuroblastoma cells

For the evaluation of cytotoxic activity of DDSs on neuroblastoma cells two cell-lines were tested (SK-N-AS and SH-Sy5Y). As shown in Table 3, the results showed a significantly lower IC50 value for M3 sample against both cell lines compared to M1 (2-fold and 1.6-fold against SK-N-AS and SH-SY5Y, respectively). The data obtained suggests that the polycationic dendrimer PAMAM improves cell uptake of the DDS within these cells therefore showed superior antiproliferative effect which goes in line with previous reports.27,28

Cell-cycle analysis

Cell-cycle analysis was performed to assess the effect of the DOX-loaded DDS on SH-SY5Y cells. IC50 concentration was used from the DOX-loaded DDS M3 on tested cells while control group was left untreated. Staining control and treated cell groups were done using PI to analyze the cellular DNA content and fluorescence intensity was measured to quantitate cell distribution. Results showed significantly (P < 0.01) a 10-fold increase in cellular arrest at the G2/M-phase in the treated group when compared to control group. These findings shown in Fig. 7 were consistent with the expected drug mechanism of action involving DNA intercalation and topoisomerase II inhibition.29–31 The marked increase in G2/M arrest compared to untreated control group confirms that the delivered drug retained its biological activity following encapsulation.

Cell-cycle analysis of (A) control untreated, (B) DOX-loaded M3 treated SH-SY5Y cells and (C) Quantitative bar chart representation of the results.

Apoptosis induction analysis

In addition, apoptotic analysis on M3 treated as well as untreated SH-SY5Y cells was evaluated to confirm and assess expected apoptotic mechanism of action of the formulation. Specific dyes with affinities to apoptotic (Annexin-V), and necrotic cells (PI) are used to classify cell populations to study the mechanism of M3. Results of this analysis showed significantly higher apoptotic cell death of M3-treated cells totaling (18.72%) when compared to control group (0.59) as shown in Fig. 8. This apoptotic response indicates that the enhanced cytotoxicity of M3 is primarily mediated through programmed cell death rather than necrosis. The ability of the DDS to induce apoptosis rather than non-specific toxicity is a desirable characteristic for anticancer therapy, minimising inflammatory responses and tissue damage. These results suggest that the prepared DDS shows high efficacy for applications of neuroblastoma treatment.

RT-PCR

RT-PCR was performed to elucidate the apoptotic effect of the loaded DDS and its effects on expression of pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic regulators, shown in Fig. 9. Results showed that treating SH-SY5Y cells with the loaded formulation led to a significant increase in apoptotic regulators by around 6.5- and 4.5-folds increase in expression of p53 and Bax genes respectively when compared with untreated control cells (P < 0.001). In addition, the results also confirmed a significant reduction of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 gene expression by around 65% compared to untreated control cells (P < 0.001). These results indicate the effectiveness of the developed formulation in promoting apoptotic cell death.

RT-PCR analysis comparison of M3 against control.

Cell uptake analysis

Cellular uptake analysis was performed to study the internalization behavior of the DOX-loaded DDS in SH-SY5Y cells, Fig. 10. As depicted by fluorescence images, the DOX-loaded DDSs (M1, M3) exhibited drug-emitted green fluorescence. After incubating SH-SY5Y cells with the DOX-loaded DDSs, the captured images demonstrated that M3 was effectively internalized into the targeted cells with higher intracellular fluorescence intensity in comparison with M1 counterparts. The increased intracellular accumulation of DOX-loaded M3 DDS correlates strongly with the lower IC50 value observed from the cytotoxicity study of M3-treated cells. These results suggest that PAMAM conjugation promoted cellular uptake of the DDS consistent with previously published studies.10–12 Intracellular uptake is a critical factor affecting efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents with similar mechanisms of action as that of DOX, whose anticancer efficacy depends on intracellular localization and DNA interactions.

Conclusion

Even though chemotherapy is usually the main treatment protocol for treating neuroblastoma, brain drug delivery remains a persistent challenge. This study aimed to synthesize a DDS combining the benefits of MOF and PAMAM carriers along with GSH to create a platform for the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents for the treatment of neuroblastoma. The developed DDS was characterized and tested using various in vitro methods. Results taken together showed that the developed DDS demonstrated controlled drug release, enhanced anticancer efficacy, as well as improved cellular uptake of the loaded DOX drug molecules. These findings highly support the potential of developing smart targeted innovative DDSs with capabilities that can significantly enhance the quality of available neuroblastoma and CNS related diseases treatment protocols and therefore improve the therapeutic outcomes and quality of life of the affected patients.
