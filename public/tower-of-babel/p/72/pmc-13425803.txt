A 3D RNA-protein proximity ligation protocol detecting the heterogeneity of LISRR-RPL26 interactions in patient-derived melanoma tumor explants
Authors: Alessandro Marino, Eleonora Leucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInvestigating lncRNA-protein interactors is crucial for understanding their contribution to tumor formation and progression. Here, we present a protocol to explore the heterogeneity of RNA-protein interactions between the lncRNA LISRR and RPL26 ex vivo in 3D clinically relevant patient-derived melanoma models. We describe steps for sample preparation, RNA probe hybridization, and a proximity ligation assay. We then detail procedures for detection and analysis. This protocol bypasses tissue embedding and sectioning, significantly accelerating data acquisition.

Before you begin

LncRNAs are non-coding transcripts longer than 500 nucleotides that regulate epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional processes through mechanisms including sponging and scaffolding other biomolecules, such as proteins. Despite their generally low sequence conservation across species, lncRNAs are frequently dysregulated and specifically expressed in cancer compared to normal tissues, making them promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets.1,2 However, while the functional relevance of lncRNA-protein interactions is increasingly recognized,3 understanding their spatial dynamics within complex tissue environments remains a major challenge. Importantly, tumors are highly heterogeneous and organized into distinct spatial niches in which cancer cells are exposed to different microenvironmental cues, including gradients of oxygen, nutrients, cytokines, and interactions with stromal and immune cells. These spatially defined signals can influence cancer cell behavior and enable context-dependent responses that can drive cellular plasticity, including dedifferentiation, phenotypic switching, and the emergence of therapy-resistant states.4,5 Consequently, visualizing where specific molecular interactions occur within the tumor microenvironment is essential for understanding how lncRNA-protein complexes contribute to adaptive cancer cell programs and treatment failure. Our laboratory recently described the lncRNA LISRR, which confers resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. Using several orthogonal biochemical and molecular assays, we demonstrated that the interaction between LISRR and the ribosomal protein RPL26 leads to the assembly of ER-associated ribosomes, promoting immune escape in a rare subpopulation of drug-tolerant melanoma cells.6 As a consequence, pharmacological inhibition of LISRR in humanized PDX models resistant to immune therapy increases overall survival by almost 40% and resensitizes them to immune checkpoint blockade. These findings highlight the importance of spatially resolved RNA-protein interactions for understanding mechanisms of immune evasion and therapeutic failure. Here, we developed a protocol to detect and quantify the interaction between the lncRNA LISRR and the protein RPL26 in a humanized PDX melanoma model (huMEL006). Typical in vivo approaches to discover and validate RNA-protein interactions include CLIP and Pulldowns.7,8 However, all these methods rely on bulk analyses and therefore lack single-molecule resolution and spatial information. As a result, these methods cannot resolve where specific interactions occur within intact tissues or heterogeneous tumor architectures. Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA) has emerged as a powerful technique to visualize corroborated molecular interactions at single-molecule resolution. When applied in 2D cell culture models, PLA allows detection of specific interactions but fails to recapitulate the spatial organization, cellular heterogeneity, and microenvironmental cues characteristic of in vivo tumors. As a consequence, critical information regarding the localization and context-dependent functions of lncRNA-protein complexes is lost. To address these limitations, we present a detailed protocol for PLA adapted to detect lncRNA-protein interactions in ex vivo patient-derived 3D tumor fragments (PDTFs).9 This method combines the specificity of biotinylated oligonucleotide probes targeting the lncRNA LISRR with primary antibodies against RPL26, a ribosomal protein associated with ER,10 and was adapted from an RNA-protein PLA protocol designed to detect a different interaction in 2D.11 This protocol is particularly valuable in cancer biology, where lncRNA-RNA-binding protein (RBP) complexes often exhibit spatially restricted and context-dependent functions associated with tumor heterogeneity, disease progression, and therapy resistance across multiple cancer types.12,13,14

This approach is, in principle, adaptable to study diverse RNA-protein interactions in other biological contexts, provided that an interaction has been demonstrated through an appropriate biochemical assay and a FISH assay has been optimized. By integrating PLA with advanced imaging techniques, RNA-protein interactions can be spatially mapped within 3D models, providing insights into regulatory networks and identifying potential biomarkers for patient stratification. Of note, this approach is fully compatible with phenotypic CRISPR- and drug-based screens and personalized medicine approaches.

Innovation

Here, we described a protocol to visualize a recently identified interaction between RPL26 and the lncRNA LISRR in tumor biopsies in 3D. Application of this protocol on patient-derived xenografted material allowed for the early detection of drug-tolerant cells, clearly demonstrating that our approach is a powerful tool for personalized applications and phenotypic drug screening. The method can be readily expanded to incorporate multiple PLA targets upon minimal adaptation and benchmarking, further increasing its versatility and impact.

The use of human biopsies for personalized drug screening has gained increasing recognition, particularly because it enables the evaluation of immunotherapies in the context of an autologous immune system.15 Currently, the analysis of these types of samples relies primarily on spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC), ELISA, and/or FACS. These approaches often require specialized instrumentation and can be resource-intensive. In addition, some of them necessitate tumor disaggregation and reassembly (e.g., scRNAseq, FACS), which can introduce biases. Importantly, these techniques have intrinsic limitations: mRNA levels do not always reflect protein expression, and protein abundance alone does not necessarily indicate productive molecular interactions or the activation of downstream signaling.

PLA overcomes several of these limitations by enabling the detection of functional molecular interactions. Indeed, PLA is easily accessible for clinical pathology laboratories,16 and PD-1/PD-L1 has been shown to act as theragnostic markers capable of accurately predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy.17 Commercial PLA kits do not support RNA–protein interaction analysis or 3D, instead relying on artificial 3D reconstructions from serial tissue sections. Additionally, blocks and sections need to be prepared. This methodology is not only relevant to RNA biologists willing to study RNA-protein interactions, but it is also broadly applicable to researchers interested in interrogating protein-protein interactions in biologically meaningful samples.

Preparation of primary antibodies

For PLA, two primary IgG antibodies with high specificity for the proteins of interest are required to detect the targeted protein-protein or RNA-protein interactions. Primary antibodies must be validated prior to the experiment by immunofluorescence, and the optimal concentration should be assessed upon titration. Either monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies can be used. To allow proper detection, the two primary antibodies must be raised in different species (usually mouse and rabbit), as they will be recognized by species-specific secondary antibodies. When the two target proteins are within ∼40 nm of one another, a detectable signal is generated. For our setting, we use a concentration of 0.4μg/ml (dilution 1:100) for anti-RPL26 and 2μg/ml for anti-biotin (dilution 1:500), according to manufacturer instructions.

Expected outcomes

Although tailored towards a specific RNA–protein interaction, our protocol can, in principle, be adapted with minor modifications to enable the spatial detection of RNA–protein interactions within patient-derived tumor fragments. Importantly, the interaction of interest must be validated using multiple complementary methods, as proximity ligation alone does not constitute definitive evidence of interaction. For an overview on this topic, please refer to Barra and Leucci.8

When performed successfully, users will be able to observe clear punctate fluorescent signals representative of the PLA event occurring between the RNA and the protein of interest within ∼40nm distance. In our experiments, the average signal-to-noise ratio was 4.6, with mean dot counts per cell ranging between 22 and 35. Importantly, this method preserves the native tissue architecture, allowing these signals to be mapped throughout the tumor fragment and showing their localization in distinct niches. Signal specificity is ensured by comparison to negative controls lacking RNA probes, which should display minimal background fluorescence. Alternative negative controls include the lack of the primary antibody, the use of an RNAse control and/or the use of scrambled probes. To optimize resource use, we recommend first establishing the technique in widely available 2D models using all the available controls to assess the specificity of the assay. Subsequently, when performing the protocol on precious (clinical) samples, the most informative negative control can be used. Here, we selected the no-probe control since the probes are labelled with biotin, and the noise we detect in tissues in our settings is mostly coming from endogenous biotin. This control is essential to set the threshold for the detection of the real fluorescent signal, as it was used for background subtraction.

The use of iohexol clearing provides optical transparency that facilitates high-resolution z-stack imaging, supporting quantitative analysis across the entire tissue volume. This generates reproducible quantitative datasets that can be directly compared across treatment conditions, sample types, or biological models. In the context of the lncRNA LISRR and the protein RPL26, the protocol is expected to reveal enriched interaction signals upon development of drug-tolerance, in agreement with the proposed role of LISRR-RPL26 complexes in sustaining an immunosuppressive microenvironment.6 More broadly, the assay is anticipated to uncover heterogeneous and context-dependent interaction patterns, providing insight into how RNA-protein complexes contribute to tumor plasticity, immune evasion, and treatment adaptation.

Limitations

Several factors may influence the efficiency and sensitivity of this protocol. First, the quality of the tumor fragment is critical. PDTFs that are extensively necrotic or poorly preserved may yield incomplete staining, regional signal loss, or uneven background. Moreover, RNA integrity is a major determinant of assay success. Samples with prior degradation due to wrong collection or poor storage handling may show reduced probe binding and consequently underestimate interaction. A second constraint concerns lncRNA abundance. Lowly expressed transcripts or interactions that occur at low stoichiometry may fall below the detection threshold. In these settings, increasing the number of probes per target or their concentration may enhance sensitivity, but could result in increased non-specific background. Probe sets must be carefully designed to minimize off-target hybridization and must be tested before using appropriate negative and positive controls. Similarly, antibody specificity is essential and needs to be validated before the experiment. Endogenous biotin background represents another technical limitation. Although the streptavidin/biotin blocking step mitigates this issue, background levels may still vary between tissues and must be accounted for by including probe-free negative controls.
