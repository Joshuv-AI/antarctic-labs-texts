Dynamic ultrasound assessment of knee displacement in healthy adolescent female soccer players: description and reliability
Authors: Jacob Jones, Katie Sloma, Jessica Garcia, Sophia Ulman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo describe a dynamic ultrasound modified prone Lachman test to assess total anterior/posterior knee displacement. To evaluate the intra- and inter-rater reliability of the ultrasound measurements obtained during the modified prone Lachman test.MethodsParticipants laid prone on an exam table as the trained researcher and assistant performed a Lachman test on both limbs. Keeping the posterior view of the medial femur, tibia, and overlying semimembranosus tendon in view, images were captured during the maneuver via a five-second video clip. No significant differences were found between raters or between sessions.ConclusionsWhile good intra- and inter-rater reliability was found for both posterior and anterior displacement measures, the notably lower intra-rater reliability observed for the novice examiner in total displacement highlights that examiner experience may influence measurement consistency. Using ultrasound while performing the prone Lachman test allows for an objective tibial displacement measurement to assess ACL competency and knee displacement in the presence of an intact ACL. Background

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is the ligament within the knee joint that limits the anterior displacement of the tibia in relation to the femur, thus providing stability to the knee joint. This ligament is often the source of injury in active youth and young adults [1] and can significantly disrupt activity levels and sporting goals due to the surgery and rehabilitation associated with this injury [2–4]. Prior studies related to the ACL have focused on improved treatment techniques, identifying risk factors, and injury prevention [5–8]. Despite these efforts, the incidence of ACL injuries continues to increase and is seen most often among adolescent and college aged athletes [9]. Prior research has also suggested that those with increased knee laxity, or displacement, are at risk of ACL injuries [10]. Knee displacement has been previously described as the total amount of anterior translation of the tibia in relation to the femur, and studies have suggested that for every 1.3 mm increase in side-to-side displacement, the odds of ACL injury on the laxer side increases four-fold [10].

In response to these findings, devices have been developed to objectively measure knee displacement to best determine who may be at increased risk of an ACL injury. These devices include arthrometers, electromagnetic devices, accelerometers, inertial sensors, and gyroscopes [11]. Arguably, the most popular device is an arthrometer known as the KT-1000 or KT-2000 device [12–15], which is an external device that attaches to the lower leg and measures anterior lower leg displacement as it applies an anterior force to the tibia. Even though some groups consider the KT devices as the gold standard to measure anterior/posterior tibial translation [16], or displacement, there are studies that question its reliability and validity. Specifically, these reports include that soft tissue mass, among other variables, could alter results [16]. Additionally, while the device may be valid in diagnosing an ACL tear, it is limited in its ability to accurately and objectively measure knee displacement with an intact ACL, such as when assessing for the presence of excessive knee displacement as a risk factor [17, 18].

Ultrasound is an imaging modality unique to other static imaging techniques because of its ability to assess musculoskeletal structures dynamically. The use of ultrasound for musculoskeletal evaluation continues to expand [19] and has been studied to evaluate the laxity of other joints [20–22]. Methods to ultrasonographically assess for ACL integrity have also been well studied [23] with the validated prone Lachman method being the most studied and accepted method [23, 24]. In this test, the patient is prone with the knee slightly flexed. A posterior view of the medial tibia plateau and medial femoral condyle with the semimembranosus in view is visualized. An anterior force is applied to the tibia causing anterior displacement of the tibia with the difference in translation providing direct visualization of the anterior displacement of the tibia [24].

While this method has been well-studied to assess for ACL competency or injury with a high sensitivity and specificity [23], it has not been used to assess for anterior/posterior knee displacement as a risk factor. The challenge with using this method to assess knee displacement is that the patient is prone allowing gravity to translate the tibia anteriorly until the ACL is taut. Therefore, increased anterior displacement will only be visualized with an anterior force if the ACL is injured. Modifying this method to remove the natural gravity-induced anterior displacement may be a reliable method to objectively assess knee displacement, thus signifying those who may be at increased risk for ACL injuries. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to describe this modified method to assess total anterior/posterior knee displacement and to evaluate the intra- and inter-rater reliability of capturing the ultrasound measurements using this method. We hypothesized that both intra- and inter-rater reliability would be high.

Methods

Participants

Healthy, female participants were recruited from the local community. All participants competed in competitive soccer and reported no recent injury (within the past three months) or orthopedic condition that would hinder physical activity. This study was approved by the University of Texas Southwestern Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. STU 082010-134), and written informed assent and consent were obtained from the participant and parent, respectively, prior to initiating study procedures.

Data collection

Ultrasound scans were captured by one operator (SU) who underwent a comprehensive training session led by an ultrasound certified and fellowship trained sports medicine physician with 6 years of musculoskeletal (MSK) ultrasound experience (JJ). The training session specifically focused on how to accurately obtain a view of the posterior knee joint and measure knee displacement with help from an additional staff member per the scan protocol outlined in this study. A GE P9 machine was used with a 3-12 MHz high frequency linear transducer for all images. Adequate contact was maintained between the probe and skin without applying excessive pressure to the posterior knee. Two scans were collected on both the right and left knees for each participant. All four scans were captured within a single testing session.

Scanning protocol

The participant would lay prone on an exam table. A firm foam roller would be placed at the level of the ankles and adjusted so the knees would be at 25 degrees of flexion as measured by a goniometer. The feet were in a neutral prone position without fixation suspended in the air. An orthogonal long axis view of the posterior medial femoral condyle, medial tibial plateau, and overlying semimembranosus tendon were obtained by the researcher (SU) with her dominant hand, as detailed in previous studies [24], and the researcher would use her non-dominant hand to stabilize the femur (Fig. 1). Then the trained assistant familiar with a Lachman test (JG) would grasp the tibia with both hands to translate the tibia posteriorly to the maximum posterior position and then anteriorly, such as in a Lachman test [25–27]. To reduce rotational variability, anterior/posterior forces were applied in a straight plane with the limb in a neutral position. The posterior view of the medial femur, tibia, and overlying semimembranosus tendon were maintained throughout the displacement maneuver to ensure that the same plane of imaging was maintained throughout [24]. If the landmarks were lost, the images were considered invalid. Images were captured during the maneuver via a five-second video clip at 35 frames per second.

Image measurement

All ultrasound images were saved, decoded, and measured by both an experienced and novice examiner. The experienced examiner was an ultrasound-certified and fellowship trained sports medicine physician (JJ) and the novice examiner was a physical therapist (KS). Training of the novice examiner included attendance of a musculoskeletal ultrasound course with education of ultrasound imaging techniques and hands on application with instruction of ultrasound techniques. Additionally, as part of training for this specific study, the novice examiner measured five videos prior to initiating the study measurements that were reviewed for accuracy by the experienced examiner.

Measurements were taken from the video clips on Sectra IDS7 PACS imaging software. Video clips were reviewed and advanced frame by frame to visualize the most posterior aspect of displacement that occurred during the prone Lachman maneuver. Horizontal lines were drawn tangential to the most posterior aspects of the distal medial femoral condyle and proximal medial tibia plateau which represents the most posterior relationship of the proximal tibia to the distal femur. Two separate measurements between these two parallel horizontal lines were obtained. These measurements represent the posterior displacement in millimeters (Fig. 2). In cases where the two measurements of the parallel, horizontal lines did not match, the horizontal lines and measurements were redrawn to improve accuracy of the measurements [24]. The video was advanced frame by frame until the most anterior tibial displacement in relation to the distal femur was visualized. Repeat measurement was obtained via the same measuring method with this measurement being the anterior displacement (Fig. 3). Both examiners measured the same maneuver segments of each video for both posterior and anterior displacement. The difference between the posterior and anterior measurements was computed as the total displacement in millimeters.

Data analysis

Left and right limbs were combined for analysis and mean and standard error values of the posterior, anterior, and total displacement measures were computed across all participant limbs for each scan. Reliability analyses were computed in SPSS (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Test-retest intra-examiner reliability for measuring knee displacement was assessed using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; 95% CI) of a two-way mixed-effects model and inter-examiner reliability was assessed with an ICC of a two-way random-effects model (95% CI). ICC values were interpreted as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), or excellent (> 0.90) [28]. The mean difference between scans for each measure was computed and paired t-tests were performed to identify statistically significant differences between scans. Lastly, independent samples t-tests were completed to compare differences between raters. Significance level (α) was set to 0.05.

Results

A sample of 38 limbs (16.0 ± 1.08 years, 165.7 ± 6.4 cm, 60 ± 6.8 kg) were considered for analysis. Given poor visualization and loss of semimembranosus landmark in a right limb scan for one participant, a total of 37 total limbs were included. For posterior and anterior measures, both examiners exhibited good intra-rater reliability scores (ICC: 0.87–0.88). However, for total displacement, the experienced examiner demonstrated an excellent intra-rater reliability (ICC: 0.90) and the novice examiner showed poor intra-rater reliability (ICC 0.48; Table 1). Good inter-rater reliability was demonstrated for posterior and anterior measures (ICC: 0.88–0.89) and moderate inter-reliability was found for the total displacement (ICC: 0.55; Table 1). No significant differences were found between raters (p = 0.268–0.913) or between sessions (p = 0.078–0.982).

Discussion

The primary purpose of this study was to describe a new method of assessing knee displacement using ultrasound and to test the intra- and inter-rater reliability of capturing ultrasound measurements using this method. The results demonstrated good intra- and inter-rater ICC scores for both the posterior and anterior displacement measures, indicating that this novel technique using dynamic ultrasound to assess knee displacement is a promising method. However, the notably lower intra-rater reliability observed for the novice examiner for the total displacement measure highlights that examiner experience may influence measurement consistency. The low intra-rater reliability of the novice examiner drove down the inter-rater reliability between the two examiners. Experienced examiners may more easily and reliably identify the appropriate landmark measurements which leads to more reliable measurements. Using ultrasound in combination with the Lachman test is valuable as the Lachman test is not only used to assess ACL competency [25, 26] but also used to assess knee displacement in the presence of an intact ACL [29–32]. Thus, the addition of ultrasound with this assessment can allow for an objective tibial displacement measurement.

Our method possesses many similarities with a few modifications to the method originally described by Chylarecki et al. (1996) [33] that was later formalized by Raja et al. (2022) [24]. While their described method was to assess ACL integrity, our method assesses knee displacement, defined as the total amount of anterior/posterior displacement between the femur and tibia. With the patient prone and the knee resting, gravity induces anterior displacement, so minimal to no displacement will occur in an intact ACL. A further anterior force will only produce displacement in an ACL deficient knee, as described by the Chylarecki and Raja methods. Our method of assessing displacement takes this into account by inducing a posteriorly translated position before assessing the terminal anterior translated position. This allows for a complete assessment of the knee displacement present, mirroring a prone Lachman [34, 35].

Using the prone Lachman technique is preferred over a standard supine technique because the supine Lachman presents some challenges when using ultrasound that would affect the accuracy and reliability of capturing ultrasound measurements. Specifically, the posterior placement of the transducer makes stabilizing the transducer more challenging. Additionally, based upon the size of the transducer, it may be difficult to position the transducer appropriately in the small space between the posterior knee and the table. These challenges could lead to undesired transducer translation resulting in a loss of landmarks and potentially a change in the image planes. Ultrasound can assess musculoskeletal structures in multiple planes and any plane in which the transducer is oriented. This can be beneficial given that structures can be evaluated in any given plane and imaging is not dependent upon pre-determined planes and image slice thicknesses which is a limitation of other imaging modalities. However, the downside of this is that any subtle movement changes the plane. Maintaining the appropriate landmarks, especially the semimembranosus, helps avoid undesired changes to the planes of the images. Therefore, it is highly important to maintain these structural landmarks to produce a reliable measurement as demonstrated based upon our results. Introducing such error would ultimately negatively affect the reliability and accuracy of the measurements. Lastly, other studies have suggested the prone Lachman technique eliminates guarding which is voluntary or involuntary muscle tensing that could negatively affect knee displacement assessment [36, 37].

With the rise of youth sport participation, there continues to be a growing incidence of ACL injuries [1]. While youth participating in sports is unlikely to slow down, an emphasis on injury prevention, especially ACL injuries, continues to be an area of focus [38–40]. Previous studies have already demonstrated that knee laxity is a risk factor for ACL injuries, however, the methods and devices to assess laxity, or displacement, may not be readily available. Additionally, some of these devices have their own limitations and results may be altered by other factors not directly related to tibial displacement [16]. The benefit of ultrasound is direct visualization of femur and tibia translation. For example, if examiners are able to perform the posterior and anterior displacement maneuver with ultrasound on a patient, the actual objective bony displacement can be visualized and measured. This would mitigate the limitation of arthrometers in assessing knee displacement.

While this study focused on measuring anterior-posterior tibial translation using ultrasound during a prone Lachman test, certain biomechanical factors were not assessed. A manual anterior-posterior force was applied until a firm endpoint was reached, but the exact force magnitude was not quantified. Attempts to reduce rotational and varus/valgus movement were employed by maintaining consistent landmarks but were not directly measured. Musculoskeletal ultrasound in its current B-mode (2D) application does not produce 3D femoral or tibial bone morphology. As such, our measurements reflect anterior-posterior joint displacement rather than full multidimensional instability. Future work should consider incorporating force quantification, rotational assessment, and 3D imaging to better characterize joint behavior beyond linear translation.

The use of point-of-care musculoskeletal ultrasound in clinical settings continues to expand as more providers are gaining experience and proficiency in its use [41]. The ultrasound curriculum for sports medicine fellows continues to expand, leading to incoming sports medicine providers with more ultrasound experience [19]. As a result, more sports medicine clinics have point-of-care ultrasound capabilities given the inherent portability, which allows for increased accessibility to assess knee displacement. Ultrasound machine costs can be from a few thousand dollars in simple handheld devices to over one-hundred thousand dollars in more sophisticated machines. As point-of-care ultrasound use continues to grow, it may eventually be favored among experienced examiners over other knee displacement measuring devices, especially given the singular capability of arthrometers. By determining the reliability of capturing ultrasound measurements of knee displacement during dynamic testing, any location that has an ultrasound machine can assess knee displacement. For these reasons, we suggest this modified prone Lachman technique with ultrasound is the preferred method.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations to note. First, while this study evaluated the reliability of obtaining ultrasound measurements from the dynamic ultrasound scan, the reproducibility of the test procedure itself was not investigated. Thus, results may differ between examiners based on specific procedure parameters not considered in the current study. For example, the current methodology does not standardize or quantify the force provided by the assistant performing the Lachman test. Future work should further investigate the reliability of the test procedure to ensure widespread adoption is feasible. Secondly, ultrasound obtains at 2D image of 3D structure, so although the methods attempt to limit rotational movement were employed, it could not be directly assessed. Additionally, while not a limitation of the study, one limitation to widespread adoption of this approach is that an assistant is required to capture the ultrasound measurements. This may incur personnel costs and potentially limit practical clinical application. Future work is needed to investigate whether clinics equipped with point-of-care ultrasound experienced any increased burden.

Further study on the use of ultrasound to assess knee displacement is needed. Normative data across sexes and age groups should be explored. We exclusively investigated female athletes who participated in a single sport to assess reliability. To make more clinical-based conclusions about the clinical role of knee displacement, more studies are needed. Future directions of this method should study how knee displacement may change in at-risk populations based upon different inherent and environmental factors.

Conclusions

Good intra- and inter-rater reliability was found for both posterior and anterior displacement measures. However, the notably lower intra-rater reliability observed for the novice examiner in total displacement highlights that examiner experience may influence measurement consistency. Using ultrasound while performing the prone Lachman test allows for an objective tibial displacement measurement to assess ACL competency and knee displacement in the presence of an intact ACL. Ultrasound provides direct visualization of femur and tibia translation, mitigating the limitation of arthrometers in assessing knee displacement.
