Functional Nano-to-Microstructures by Jet Printing and Direct Ink Writing
Authors: Junchen Luo, Ju An Park, Sanghoon Baek, Seongju Kim, Unyong Jeong, Sungjune Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Jet-based printing techniques and direct ink writing have emerged as complementary, convergent technologies serving as key platforms in additive manufacturing for functional nano- to microscale architectures. This review highlights how these approaches enable fine feature resolution and three-dimensional structures in advanced electronics and biointerfacing applications. The interplay of fluid mechanics, viscoelastic ink rheology, droplet–substrate interactions, and drying dynamics is examined as a critical determinant of printing fidelity. Application-focused case studies, from flexible thin-film transistors to bioprinted artificial tissues, demonstrate how precise structural control via printing translates to enhanced device performance and new functionality in electronic and biological systems. Finally, we discuss the challenges and future opportunities driving the evolution of these printing platforms toward autonomous, adaptive, and intelligent manufacturing systems.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, the convergence of materials science, precision manufacturing, and device engineering has propelled printing-based fabrication to the forefront of modern electronics, optoelectronics, and biointerfacing. − Unlike traditional subtractive microfabrication, which depends on predesigned masks and often generates substantial material waste, additive printing offers a digitally programmable, maskless, and resource-efficient pathway for constructing hierarchical architectures across scales, from submicrometer features to macroscale constructs. , This transformation has been driven by advances in functional inks, including metallic nanoparticles, conjugated semiconducting polymers, dielectric composites, and biomaterials, which enable seamless integration of electrical, optical, and sensing functionalities onto flexible, stretchable, and three-dimensional platforms. Beyond its role as a manufacturing tool, printing has become an enabling technology for rapid prototyping, structural customization, and on-demand production in both industrial and biomedical contexts.

Historically, printing technologies such as screen, offset, and gravure printing laid the groundwork for scalable patterning of colored inks on large-area substrates for graphic applications. However, these approaches were limited in their achievable compatibility with emerging electronic and biomedical materials. , The transition from these early, contact-based processes to high-resolution, noncontact modalities marked a turning point in additive manufacturing. Advances in droplet formation control, ink formulation and characterization, and process optimization have allowed printed features to be reduced from the hundreds-of-micrometers regime into the submicrometer domain, while simultaneously expanding the diversity of printable materials. This evolution has coincided with the rise of multifunctional devices, ranging from three-dimensional thin-film transistors to tissue-interfaced sensors, that require not only fine feature resolution but also complex three-dimensional architectures and heterogeneous material integration.

Within this rapidly evolving landscape, high-resolution inkjet printing (IJP) and direct ink writing (DIW) have emerged as complementary platforms that bridge nanoscale precision with manufacturing adaptability. , Drop-on-demand IJP enables digitally controlled droplet placement for maskless patterning, while DIW allows the continuous extrusion of viscoelastic inks to construct freeform three-dimensional architectures with high spatial fidelity. Together, these techniques accommodate an exceptionally wide range of ink rheology and material classes, making them ideally suited for next-generation technologies such as flexible displays, , soft robotics, bioelectronic interfaces, and integrated micro-optoelectronic systems. Breakthroughs in droplet–substrate interaction engineering, rheology-driven resolution enhancement, and in situ curing strategies have significantly expanded their capabilities, enabling complex, high-fidelity structures that were once beyond the reach of additive manufacturing. Notably, the integration of in-line monitoring, real-time process control, and machine-learning-guided optimization − has begun to transform these printing platforms into intelligent, adaptive manufacturing systems which makes them capable of autonomously adjusting to variations in ink, substrate, and environmental conditions.

Although several reviews have addressed aspects of printed electronics and additive manufacturing, ,,− there remains a need for a comprehensive, cross-disciplinary review that unites the physics of jetting and extrusion, the characterization of functional inks, and their emerging applications in electronic and biological sectors. This review provides an integrative perspective, focusing specifically on functional nano- to microscale architectures fabricated by jetting-based printing and DIW. We begin with a comparative analysis of printing technologies and their resolution limits, situating jet printing and DIW within the broader additive manufacturing landscape. We then examine ink formulation strategies, rheological design windows, and jetting dynamics that control feature fidelity, linking classical fluid mechanics with emerging machine-learning–assisted optimization of multicomponent inks and process parameters. Building on this framework, we highlight representative application case studies, from three-dimensional flexible and bioinspired transistor architectures to 3D-printed tissue and organ mimics, to illustrate how precise structural control at the voxel and device levels translates into enhanced electrical performance and improved biological integration. Finally, we suggest future research directions and related current challenges that will drive the evolution of printing-enabled manufacturing toward multifunctional, adaptive, and autonomous systems. Printing Technologies and Their Resolutions

Over the past several decades, printing technologies for electronic devices have undergone remarkable progress, largely propelled by the increasing need for scalable, cost-efficient, and adaptable fabrication techniques for modern flexible devices and bioprinting applications. Printed electronics has attracted substantial interest, spanning applications from standalone sensor units to complex integrated circuit systems. These printing methods are generally classified into contact and noncontact printing according to their reliance on physical templates during the patterning process. Contact-based techniques, such as screen printing and gravure printing, require direct interaction with a patterned mask, whereas noncontact methods, including IJP and DIW, facilitate pattern formation without the need for physical masks. In this section, we briefly review the operating principles and recent advancements of these representative printing technologies. We present a systematic overview, organized by increasing lateral resolution (line width) and vertical resolution (thickness) (Figure b). The width and thickness ranges shown in Figure b represent the typical operational windows of each printing technology used in fabricating functional microstructures. The minimum achievable feature size is primarily constrained by the intrinsic physical mechanisms of each process, such as fluid dynamics in inkjet printing, electrohydrodynamics in EHD printing, and meniscus stability in DIW printing. The upper limit of the feature width is determined by engineering practicality and process efficiency. For instance, employing these high-precision, low-throughput deposition techniques for large-area fabrication becomes increasingly inefficient compared to macro-scale, high-throughput manufacturing methods. Beyond lateral feature size, vertical resolution is a critical parameter that governs the functional performance of printed devices. Accurate control of vertical resolution is particularly important for multilayer architectures, such as conductors, dielectric layers, and semiconducting films, where deviations of only tens to hundreds of nanometers can significantly affect capacitance and charge transport pathways. Furthermore, high vertical resolution facilitates the fabrication of three-dimensional structures, graded materials, and mechanically robust microarchitectures, underscoring its importance for both advanced electronic devices and biofabrication applications.

Screen Printing

Screen printing is a mature and extensively adopted technique, valued for its high production throughput and cost-effectiveness. Here, throughput denotes the effective printing productivity, defined as the amount of material deposited or the printed area generated per unit time (e.g., g min–1 or m2 s–1). The typical screen printing setup consists of a patterned mask or stencil, a squeegee, and a designated substrate positioned on a platform (Figure a). During the printing process, ink is distributed across the stencil using a squeegee, while controlled pressure ensures that the ink is forced through the open regions of the stencil and deposited onto the substrate. While traditional flatbed screen printing offers limited throughput, roll-to-roll screen printing enables continuous, high-speed production, making it particularly advantageous for the scalable manufacturing of large-area flexible electronics. However, despite the enhanced efficiency of rotary systems, these setups remain relatively costly and demand frequent maintenance due to their susceptibility to material-induced clogging. These limitations can be partially mitigated by employing optimized ink formulations, surface-engineered screen stencils, and regular stencil or anilox cleaning protocols, which collectively reduce fouling and enhance operational stability.

The lateral resolution (feature width) achievable in screen printing is largely dictated by the quality and precision of the mask or stencil. Stencils are commonly fabricated by depositing patterned polymer layers onto a mesh screen using photochemical processes, while masks are often produced by laser cutting patterns into flexible polymer films. High-resolution stencils, generated via photolithography or laser drilling, can achieve line widths in the range of 10–30 μm. For example, Chung et al. developed a high-resolution hybrid fine metal mask using photolithography, enabling the fabrication of patterned electrodes with widths (10 μm) and heights (9 μm) via screen printing. To minimize uncontrolled spreading of low-viscosity inks through the screen mesh, screen printing inks are typically formulated with relatively high viscosities in the range of 0.5–5 Pa·s. While this helps maintain pattern fidelity, it also poses challenges for printing fine lines and limits the achievable vertical resolution, making it difficult to produce films thinner than 10 μm. Additional factors influencing the resolution include the gap between the stencil and substrate, substrate properties, printing speed, and the geometry and angle of the squeegee. Meseldžija et al. systematically examined the impact of various textile substrates on print quality, highlighting the critical role of interfacial interactions between substrate materials and ink.

Gravure Printing

Gravure printing is a high-speed, roll-to-roll printing technique that uses engraved cylinders to directly transfer functional inks onto flexible substrates through physical contact with the printing plate. The process involves three sequential stages (Figure b): (1) overfilling ink onto the engraved cylinder surface, (2) scraping excess ink with a doctor blade to confine it within the microcells, and (3) transferring ink to the substrate via pressurized contact during cylinder rotation. Print quality is governed by the capillary number (C a = ηU/γ), where η, U, and γ represent ink viscosity, printing speed, and surface tension, respectively. Suboptimal C a values impair performance; low C a causes insufficient ink retention due to excessive blade removal, while high C a hinders cell filling. Strategic optimization of these parameters is critical for consistent output.

While conventional gravure printing typically achieves 20–50 μm feature widths, recent advancements have pushed resolutions below 10 μm. Kang et al. demonstrated 7.5 μm conductive lines using electromechanically engraved cylinders paired with viscosity-optimized inks. Grau et al. further refined process parameters to realize fully gravure-printed organic thin-film transistors with 5 μm electrode spacing. Hybrid approaches, such as photolithography-fabricated silicon molds with engineered wettability contrast suggested by Zhang et al., achieved unprecedented 1.2 μm features without doctor blades. Challenges persist in high-speed operations, where ink residue accumulation accelerates cylinder wear and degrades pattern fidelity. Mitigation strategies include precise control of blade pressure/angle and the adoption of gravure offset printing with intermediate cylinders, which enhances long-term stability in high-volume production. These developments underscore the evolving role of gravure printing in scalable manufacturing of precision electronic components.

In addition, reverse-offset printing is recognized as one of the most promising roll-to-roll compatible printing techniques for achieving high-resolution patterning. This method can deliver lateral resolutions as fine as 1–10 μm. , In this process, the ink is initially coated onto a silicone blanket, selectively removed using an engraved glass cliché, and subsequently transferred onto the target substrate. Unlike gravure offset printing, reverse-offset printing defines the pattern after the blanket is inked. The partially dried ink on the blanket effectively suppresses spreading during transfer, thereby ensuring excellent feature fidelity. Owing to its high resolution, throughput, and scalability, reverse-offset printing is widely regarded as one of the most advanced R2R technologies for large-area, high-precision printed electronics.

Direct Ink Writing

Direct ink writing, an extrusion-based additive manufacturing technique, enables the fabrication of freeform electronic structures through controlled deposition of functional inks via a nozzle on a three-axis motion platform. This digitally controlled process, guided by computer-aided design (CAD) systems, allows precise material deposition along predefined toolpaths. A pneumatic pressure controller regulates ink extrusion, accommodating diverse ink formulations for printing nano/microscale materials. Printability hinges on the viscoelastic property of ink, specifically, its ability to form stable, continuous filaments through the nozzle. DIW is broadly categorized into two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) printing on the basis of pattern dimensionality.

In 2D printing, pneumatic pressure forces ink through the nozzle, forming a meniscus at the tip. Substrate contact initiates wetting, with subsequent stage or nozzle movement in the XY plane, ink is deposited via shear-driven flow. Print quality depends critically on meniscus–substrate interactions, modulated by pneumatic pressure and nozzle-substrate gap height (Figure c). Extrusion instability and nozzle clogging are critical obstacles to continuous printing. Here, extrusion instability refers to fluctuations in the volumetric flow rate at the nozzle, leading to variations in filament width, intermittent extrusion, or unintended droplet formation. Such instabilities primarily arise from variations in ink rheology, fluctuations in pneumatic pressure, and disturbances in the meniscus shape at the nozzle tip. Stabilization strategies, including pneumatic pressure control, ink rheology tuning (e.g., yield-stress adjustment), and active meniscus regulation, are anticipated to enhance extrusion uniformity. In 3D printing, an additional challenge is introduced, where improper ink viscosity can cause structural collapse after printing, ultimately affecting the shape fidelity of the printed features. Ideal inks exhibit shear-thinning behavior, described by the power-law viscosity model, η = Kγ· n–1, where η is viscosity, K the consistency index, γ· the shear rate, and n the power-law index. The Herschel–Bulkley model extends this framework by incorporating yield stress (τ y ) in the shear stress (τ), τ = τ y + Kγ· n , ensuring ink flows under shear stress while retaining shape after printing. Complementary stabilization strategies include rapid thermal curing, laser sintering, and photopolymerization.

Resolution of DIW is governed by nozzle diameter and process parameters (speed, extrusion rate), typically achieving 1–300 μm feature widths and 0.3–100 μm thicknesses. Smaller nozzles enable higher resolution but demand optimized ink rheology to prevent clogging. Lee et al. demonstrated meniscus-guided printing of liquid metal microgranular particles, producing 4 μm thick, 50 μm wide conductive electrodes without postprocessing. Park et al. achieved sub-2 μm line widths by using 5 μm nozzles at 0.1 mm/s printing speed, yielding 1 μm thick freestanding liquid metal patterns (Figures d,e). Despite its versatility, DIW faces challenges rooted in nozzle-substrate interactions. Poor wettability causes delamination or fidelity loss, while non-planar substrates induce pattern inconsistencies. Ongoing research has focused on interfacial engineering and dynamic gap control strategies to broaden the applicability of DIW in fabricating complex electronic architectures. ,

Digital Inkjet Printing

Digital inkjet printing (IJP) is a noncontact, maskless deposition technique that precisely delivers picoliter-scale droplets of functional inks onto substrates. As illustrated in Figure a, inkjet systems are broadly categorized into continuous inkjet (CIJ) and drop-on-demand (DOD) modes according to the droplet generation mechanism. In CIJ systems, droplets are continuously generated and selectively deflected toward the substrate using electrostatic fields, while nonaddressed droplets are recirculated. Although CIJ enables high printing speeds, its material compatibility and resolution are limited by low-viscosity requirements and complex fluid handling.

DOD systems generate droplets only when triggered by a digital signal. As shown schematically in Figure a, droplet formation is typically achieved through thermal or piezoelectric actuation. Thermal inkjet systems rely on rapid bubble formation induced by localized heating, whereas piezoelectric systems use mechanical deformation of a piezoelectric actuator to expel droplets. , Piezoelectric DOD printing is therefore more widely adopted for functional materials due to its broader ink compatibility and improved control over droplet volume and velocity.

Printable inks generally fall within a viscosity range of 1–25 mPa·s and surface tension of 25–45 mN/m to ensure stable droplet formation and suppress satellite generation. , The stability of droplet ejection and deposition depends on the interplay among viscosity, inertia, and surface tension, as discussed in section . Particle-laden inks require particle sizes significantly smaller than the nozzle diameter to avoid clogging and trajectory instability. ,

Following deposition, postprocessing steps such as photopolymerization, thermal curing, or sintering are often necessary to achieve structural integrity and functional continuity, particularly for nanoparticle-based conductors and photocurable resins. , Owing to these characteristics, digital IJP has become a versatile platform for printed electronics and emerging device fabrication, offering digital programmability, material efficiency, and compatibility with diverse substrates.

Aerosol Jet Printing

Aerosol jet printing (AJP) is a noncontact deposition technique capable of producing high-resolution conformal patterns on diverse substrates (Figure b), including those with complex geometries. The process initiates with ink atomization via ultrasonic or pneumatic methods, generating aerosol droplets of typically 1–5 μm in diameter. These droplets are transported by an inert carrier gas to the print head, where a coaxial sheath gas stream collimates the aerosol into a focused, high-velocity jet (10–100 mm/s). This aerodynamic focusing enables precise material deposition at nozzle-to-substrate working distances of 2–5 mm, a significant advantage over conventional inkjet or screen printing. The extended standoff distance permits freeform patterning on nonplanar and textured surfaces, with typical feature widths of 10–100 μm.

Recent advancements have demonstrated that integrating auxiliary energy fields into AJP can substantially enhance resolution. Ma et al. developed an acoustic-focused AJP system featuring a custom nozzle geometry that generates annular acoustic waves, sharpening the focal point of the aerosol stream. This innovation produced serpentine arrays with 6 ± 0.3 μm line widths and spacings. Jung et al. achieved nanoscale resolution by combining aerosol lithography with electrostatic steering, where a substrate-applied negative potential creates electrostatic lenses around mask apertures. This method enabled the direct printing of 3D nanostructures, including helices and nanopillars, with features as fine as 85 nm (Figure c). The versatility of AJP stems from its decoupled droplet generation and deposition stages, allowing independent optimization of ink formulation and flow dynamics. However, resolution remains contingent on precise control of gas flow ratios, droplet size distribution, and substrate charge states. These challenges can be mitigated through precise control of gas flow ratios, optimization of atomization parameters to narrow the droplet-size distribution, and substrate charge conditioning, all of which contribute to improved jet stability and printing resolution. Ongoing research focuses on hybrid approaches leveraging multiphysics interactions to bridge the gap between mesoscale AJP and nanoscale lithographic techniques.

Electrohydrodynamic Jet Printing

Electrohydrodynamic jet (EHD-jet) printing is a high-resolution additive manufacturing technique that leverages electric fields between the nozzle and substrate to deposit functional materials at micro/nanoscale resolutions (Figure d). The process initiates when an applied electric field induces surface charge accumulation at the ink–air interface of a conductive solution, generating electrostatic repulsion forces to form a conical meniscus (Taylor cone). When the electric stress surpasses the viscosity of ink and surface tension forces, a stable jet emanates from the cone apex, depositing material with minimal spreading due to the absence of lateral stresses during flight. This field-driven deposition enables feature widths significantly smaller than the nozzle diameter, distinguishing EHD jet from pressure-driven methods where droplet impact dynamics limit resolution.

Recent advancements demonstrate the capability of EHD jet for functional electronic devices. Xiao et al. optimized PEDOT:PSS electrode geometries using continuous EHD jet printing, achieving 5 μm line widths with 1250 S/cm conductivity. These electrodes enabled organic field-effect photodetectors with detectivities of 2.86 × 1014 Jones. Wang et al. advanced the resolution capability of perovskite patterning to produce 1 μm wide and 18 nm thick perovskite dots through the use of the ionic liquid methylammonium acetate (MAAc), achieving photodetector arrays with a pixel density of 5070 dpi. Remarkably, MAAc enables stable electrohydrodynamic (EHD) jet printing of perovskites in ambient air without the need for antisolvent treatment. The negligible vapor pressure of MAAc effectively retards solvent evaporation and delays perovskite crystallization, thereby facilitating controlled nucleation and promoting the formation of highly uniform perovskite dots. Nozzle miniaturization has yielded 50 nm features with 1 μm nozzles, though Li et al. demonstrated that resolution need not scale with nozzle size. Their coaxial e-jet system employed a 130 μm nozzle to print 40 nm lines by confining functional ink within a high-viscosity sheath, enabling nanoscale deposition without clogging (Figure e). While e-jet excels in resolution, challenges persist in maintaining jet stability and throughput. Ongoing research focuses on multimaterial printing and hybrid systems combining electric field precision with scalable deposition strategies for industrial adoption.

Properties of Inks for High-Definition Jet Printing

The performance and reliability of printed electronic devices fundamentally depend on the collaborative design between functional inks (including conductive, semiconductive and dielectric inks) and printable structures. The property of the printing ink has a significant influence on the electrical properties, printing performance and environmental stability of the final product. The common functional ink for electrical devices can be divided into conductive ink prepared by metallic nanomaterials (e.g., Ag or Cu nanoparticles/nanowires), carbon-based materials (e.g., CNTs, graphene), conductive polymer (e.g., PEDOT:PSS) and liquid metal materials (e.g., Ga, EGaIn), semiconductive ink including inorganic and organic semiconductive materials, and dielectric ink typically represented by various polymers or metal oxides. In addition, the viscoelastic property of the ink and understanding of the shape deformation of the ink droplet during printing determine the resolution of the printed structures. In this section, we introduce various functional inks from conductive to dielectric materials, and summarize the principles to formulate physical properties of printed inks.

Rheology in Inkjet Printing

For functional inks, the Oh alone is insufficient to fully describe their jetting behavior. The incorporation of functional materials imparts viscoelastic properties to the ink, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of its rheological characteristics for effective formulation. − Traditionally, materials are classified as either fluids or solids. However, some materials exhibit both fluid-like and solid-like behavior, indicating a viscoelastic nature. Functional inks require rheological analysis to characterize their mechanical response. In rheological modeling, the fluidic component of a material is represented by a dashpot, which accounts for viscous dissipation, while the solid-like component is represented by a spring, which accounts for elastic energy storage. The viscoelastic properties of such materials are time-dependent, meaning they behave as liquids or solids depending on time scale. When stress is applied, part of the energy is dissipated through the dashpot, while part is stored in the spring. Upon removal of stress, viscoelastic materials gradually recover from deformation, demonstrating a delayed elastic response. Understanding these properties is essential for optimizing the printability and performance of functional inks in inkjet applications. The viscoelastic properties can be determined by applying Small Amplitude Oscillatory Shear (SAOS) to the fluid. In this method a thin fluid sample is subjected to an oscillatory shear deformation such as the shear strain. The complex modulus characterizes how the viscoelastic fluids respond to the stress and strain under loading. The complex modulus is described by 4 G*=G′+iG″ The real part of the complex modulus exhibits the storage modulus (G′), and the imaginary part of the complex modulus indicates the loss modulus (G″).

The behavior of viscoelastic fluids can be characterized by Maxwell model, which consists of a spring and a dashpot connected in series. − The dynamic analysis of oscillatory test in the Maxwell model is given as 5 G′=G(ωτ)21+(ωτ)2 6 G″=G(ωτ)1+(ωτ)2 With τ = η/G, the relaxation time (τ) is constant for the Maxwell model. In analogous fashion, free surface flow of viscoelastic fluids can be characterized by the Deborah number (De). − 9 De=elastic time scalecapillary time scale=λμL3/γ Here, λ is the characteristic relaxation time of the fluid. Viscoelastic inks with De in the range of 0.1 < De < 1 are well-suited for IJP, as they achieve an optimal balance between elasticity and surface tension (Figure ). When De < 0.1, surface tension dominates, causing the ligament to break up prematurely and leading to the formation of undesirable satellite droplets. For the fluids with De between 0.1 and 0.5, breakup times were noticeably delayed. The recoiling filament detached from the nozzle plate and retracted into the main drop. For fluids with a De ranging from 0.5 to 0.9, the ligament extended, forming a long fine filament. Eventually, the filament’s tail ruptured form the nozzle and recoiled back into the main drop to minimize surface area, culminating in the formation of a single drop. For inks with higher De value exceeding 1.0, the ink filament elongates without breaking and retracts into the printhead due to strong elastic effects, preventing successful droplet formation. The De serves as a useful dimensionless parameter for characterizing the viscoelastic properties of inks in IJP. However, studies explicitly addressing the role of viscoelasticity in printability remain limited. While a few investigations have identified suitable De ranges for IJP, discrepancies exist due to variations in the relaxation time calculation methods. Despite these differences, a consistent observation is that inks with high De values exhibit non-jetting behavior, whereas those with low De values tend to generate satellite droplets.

Depending on the ink ejection mechanism, alternative jetting technologies have been developed, such as electrohydrodynamic (EHD) jet printing. In EHD jet printing, fluid is ejected by an applied electric field gradient. Unlike conventional IJP, EHD printing relies on the formation of a stable “Taylor cone jet,” where the electric stress deforms a droplet into a conical shape, from which a fine jet, much smaller than the nozzle diameter, is emitted. The stability and behavior of this electrified jet are governed by a complex interplay of rheological properties. Yu et al. investigated the critical role of viscoelasticity in Taylor cone jet formation. Both elasticity and viscosity of the ink significantly improve jet stability, thereby expanding the operational window in terms of voltage and flow rate. Elasticity was shown to broaden the stable voltage range by enhancing electric field tolerance, while viscosity extended the low-end flow rate range and further stabilized jetting at higher voltages.

Atomization Physics and Ink Rheology in Aerosol Jet Printing

Unlike inkjet printing, AJP operates on a continuous mist generation and aerodynamic focusing mechanism. Consequently, the ink rheology for AJP is dictated primarily by the physics of the atomization process. Depending on the different atomization method, AJP can be divided into Ultrasonic (U-AJP) and Pneumatic (P-AJP), each governed by distinct physical laws and rheological windows.

U-AJP utilizes a piezoelectric transducer to propagate high-frequency acoustic waves into the ink reservoir. The atomization mechanism is governed by the capillary wave theory. When the oscillation amplitude exceeds a critical threshold, the capillary waves become unstable and eject droplets from the wave crests. The resulting droplet diameter is therefore closely correlated with the capillary wavelength, leading to a strong dependence on ink surface tension and viscosity. The mean droplet diameter (D) is governed by the equation as follows: 10 D=0.34(8πσρf2)1/3 where σ is the surface tension, is ρ the ink density, and f is the ultrasonic frequency. In U-AJP, stable aerosol generation is typically limited to low-viscosity Newtonian or weakly shear-thinning inks (1 to 10 mPa·s), as excessive viscous dissipation suppresses capillary wave growth and reduces atomization efficiency. Consequently, U-AJP is particularly well suited for diluted nanoparticle inks, metal oxide inks, and polymer solutions.

In contrast, P-AJP relies on high-velocity carrier gas flow to atomize ink via shear induced breakup. In the pneumatic generator, the atomization behavior is primarily governed by the flow rate, as well as by the geometry of the pneumatic generator, which directly determines the local flow field in the aerosol formation region. The separation of droplets ligaments arises from the velocity difference between the gas and liquid streams after the stagnation pressure region, which generates sufficient inertial stress to overcome capillary resistance. As the velocity difference increases, the droplets become smaller. The governing physics of P-AJP atomization is commonly described by two fundamental dimensionless parameters the Reynolds number (Re) and the Weber number (We). The Reynolds number characterizes the ratio of inertial and viscous forces and is expressed as 11 Re=ρvdμ where ρ is the ink density, v is the viscosity, d is the characteristic ligament diameter, and μ is the ink viscosity. The Weber number relates inertial forces to surface tension and is described as 12 We=ρv2dσ where σ is the surface tension of the ink. Effective atomization pneumatic atomization requires sufficiently large inertial forces to overcome surface tension, while excessive viscosity suppresses droplet breakup. Compared to U-AJP, P-AJP accommodates a higher and broader viscosity window (1–1000 mPa·s), enabling the printing of more concentrated nanoparticle inks and moderately viscoelastic polymer formulations.

Overall, U-AJP and P-AJP possess different rheological requirements due to their distinct atomization mechanisms. U-AJP typically necessitates low-viscosity and weakly elastic inks to maintain stable capillary wave atomization, while P-AJP can process fluids with significantly higher viscosities but require control of gas flow and ink rheology to ensure jet stability. These differences underscore the critical role of ink rheology in defining the printable material and achievable resolution in aerosol jet printing. Ink Rheology in Direct Ink Writing

The ink rheological properties determine the printability, resolution, and fidelity of the printed features in DIW. Understanding the fluid behavior of ink is a prerequisite for determining their suitability in DIW. Ink viscosity can be calculated by the instantaneous ratio of the shear stress to the shear rate, which determines whether ink can be extruded through a nozzle and maintain the structural fidelity after printing. Non-Newtonian fluids exhibit variable viscosity with an increase of shear rate. As shown in Figure a, each printing method operates within a specific viscosity range. Based on the ink property, DIW printing can be broadly classified into two regimes: meniscus-guided DIW printing which utilizes lower-viscosity ink for 2D planar patterning, and extrusion-based DIW printing which employs high-viscosity inks suitable for constructing self-supporting 3D structures. Extrusion-based direct ink writing relies on pressure-driven extrusion of ink through a nozzle, making it particularly suitable for viscoelastic or yield-stress inks that can retain self-supporting structures after deposition. However, the mechanical shear stress at the nozzle tip and the reliance on pneumatic control systems may limit printing resolution and increase the risk of nozzle clogging. In contrast, meniscus-guided DIW utilizes a capillary bridge formed between the nozzle and the substrate to confine and deposit low-viscosity inks, thereby achieving submicrometer-scale patterning without the need for high extrusion pressures. This technique enables high-resolution and gentle ink deposition, which is advantageous for fabricating delicate or high-aspect-ratio architectures. Nevertheless, maintaining a stable meniscus requires precise control of the nozzle–substrate gap and ambient environmental conditions, such as humidity and temperature.

The ink used for traditional extrusion-based DIW printing typically requires shear-thinning behavior, a characteristic of non-Newtonian fluids in which viscosity decreases with increasing shear rate. The rheological behavior of shear-thinning ink can be evaluated by the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). The ink exhibits solid-like or gel-like behavior at low shear stress (G′ > G″), whereas it transitions to a viscous liquid at high shear stress (G′ < G″) (Figure b). This reversible transition enables the ink to flow inside the nozzles during extrusion and recover its solid-like structure immediately after printing. The shear stress applied by the nozzle must exceed the yield stress of the ink to enable ink flow from the syringe in DIW. Several mathematical models have been developed to quantify ink printability and predict shape fidelity in the DIW process. As we have mentioned above, the Herschel–Bulkley (HB) model is widely employed, as it incorporates stress into a generalized power-law viscosity framework, τ=τy+Kγ·n 13 where τ is the shear stress, K is the flow consistency index, γ· is the shear rate, and n is the flow index. The value of n and K can be determined by plotting shear stress in the form of τ–τ y as a function of the shear rates on a logarithmic scale, thereby helping to develop ideal ink for DIW. Recently, researchers have further refined mathematical models by incorporating practical printing conditions, including the behavior of thixotropic materials, the use of radial nozzles, and sagging effect caused by self-weight and capillary forces. −

On the other hand, meniscus-guided DIW printing enables deposition of solvent-based functional ink with low viscosity and Newtonian behavior, hence meniscus-guided DIW relies on capillary forces and surface tension for ink deposition and patterning. The ink is continuously delivered from the nozzle tip to the substrate through a liquid meniscus that forms between them, driven by evaporation-induced capillary flow, which replenishes the meniscus as solvent evaporates. , The fundamental role of the capillary force in governing meniscus behavior has been extensively studied. , Given the extensive mathematical formulations and model derivations involved, a detailed discussion of these theoretical aspects is beyond the scope of this review. The viscosity suitable for meniscus-guided DIW typically ranges from ∼0.01 to 1 Pa·s, striking a balance between sufficient fluidity for smooth flow and control for stable pattern formation without clogging. The rheological behavior of such ink is illustrated in Figure c, where G″ consistently exceeds G′ across the entire shear stress range. , This viscous-dominated (liquid-like) behavior is critical for ensuring smooth ink deposition, meniscus formation, and continuous flow throughout the printing process. Therefore, the rheological property of meniscus-guided DIW should focus primarily on tuning solvent volatility, surface tension, and wettability, rather than yield stress or shear-thinning properties. Although the meniscus-guided DIW printing has traditionally been used for 2D planar patterning, recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of fabricating 3D structures by controlling ink properties and printing conditions. ,, Meniscus-guided three-dimensional (3D) printing utilizes a femtoliter-scale Newtonian ink meniscus to fabricate free-standing micro- and nanostructures through solvent evaporation-induced solidification. In this process, an ink-filled nanopipette makes contact with the substrate surface, during which a minute volume of ink is dispensed by capillary wetting, forming a stable meniscus in the nanopipette–substrate gap. Rapid solvent evaporation at the meniscus interface leads to localized solidification at the contact region. Subsequent upward movement of the nanopipette continuously renews the meniscus at the growth front, enabling the layer-by-layer construction of 3D architectures. For example, Kim et al. demonstrated the fabrication of various free-standing graphene oxide (GO) nanostructures, including linear filaments, suspended bridges, interconnections, and even braided networks, using this meniscus-guided 3D printing approach. In their study, a GO aqueous ink was confined within a nanoscale meniscus formed upon nanopipette–substrate contact, and as the pipet was gradually withdrawn, rapid solvent evaporation promoted progressive solidification of the GO flakes, yielding continuous, self-supported nanostructures with controllable geometries.

Ink Drop Formation in Jet Printing

Printhead Operation

Among the various IJP technologies, piezoelectric inkjet (PIJ) printing is widely used in industrial applications due to its ability to control jetting behavior through precise waveform manipulation. As the name implies, a PIJ printhead uses a piezoelectric actuator to convert applied electrical energy into mechanical deformation of the ink chamber. PIJ printers have multiple drive modes determined by the position and operating mechanism of the piezoelectric actuator. Each of these modes has been invented and patented by different companies, with each mode offering distinct advantage. Jetting behavior is influenced by the super position of pressure pulse generated by the deformation of the actuator. The pressure wave generation mechanism is influenced by the shape of the drive waveform. − Assuming a simplified geometry of the inkjet device, with a long ink channel with a nozzle on the right side and a larger reservoir on the left side, the propagation of pressure waves is illustrated in Figure . An electrical voltage applied to the piezoelectric element causes the channel wall to expand, creating a negative pressure wave in the channels. This pressure wave propagates in both directions; the wave traveling to the left undergoes a phase inversion upon reflection at the open end of the reservoir, while the wave traveling to the nozzle retains its phase upon reflection. The positive pressure wave is then amplified by wall contraction during the next slope of the driving waveform and superimposed on the reflected waves. This combined wave accelerates the ink movement, resulting in drop formation when the ink has sufficient kinetic energy to overcome viscous dissipation. To maximize the amplitude of the pressure wave, the second slope of the driving waveform is applied as the reflected wave nears the piezoelectric actuator. If the second slope is applied too early or too late, the kinetic energy of the inks decreases. The voltage amplitude enhances the deformation of the chamber wall, thereby accelerating the ink ejection. Precise control of the voltage amplitude and pulse width is essential to achieve the optimal waveform design. To achieve reliable and stable single drop ejection with directionality, both the ink properties and the parameters of waveform should be optimized.

Optimization of Jetting Waveform

The waveform optimization is, therefore, an important procedure that must be performed to characterize the jetting behavior to determine a single drop ejection at the desired velocity. Numerous studies have been carried out on the design of a driving waveform in order to achieve reliable jetting, , control the droplet volume, , and reduce the residual vibration. , The establishment of a universally applicable waveform optimization guideline for all printheads is limited by the intrinsic diversity in the internal configurations and operating modes of piezo actuators among commercial printheads. A commonly used rule of thumb for optimizing a waveform is to systematically vary the dwell time within the trapezoidal waveform, while keeping the rise and fall time constant, and observe the formation of droplet. The other main advantage of tuning the waveform is that, unlike ink properties, it can be done in-process, as the jetting behavior can change during printing for various reasons.

Recently, machine learning approaches have emerged as a useful option for optimizing the inkjet printing process due to rapid application after training. Jetting prediction is a challenge due to the complicated relationship between the ink properties and driving waveform. Data-driven machine learning can find meaningful patterns in complex relationships. Therefore, artificial neural networks predict jetting velocity and jetting morphologies with high accuracy from Newtonian to viscoelastic fluids. ,, To build the prediction model of inkjet printing, the analysis of the fluids plays a crucial role in defining the input parameters of neural networks. In addition to neural networks, machine learning algorithms have been developed to predict droplet velocity and volume based on polymer concentration, applied voltage, and pulse width of the driving waveform. Deep recurrent neural networks have also been applied to video-based predictions of droplet formation, enabling the extraction of latent parameters such as density, viscosity, and surface tension. Additionally, novel approaches have been proposed to incorporate physical constraints into neural networks, addressing the limitations of purely data-driven models in extrapolative predictions. By integrating fundamental principles such as mass and energy conservation, these methods enhance the accuracy and reliability of machine learning models in IJP applications. While predicting jetting behavior is essential, a more comprehensive approach is needed to optimize the IJP process. To address this, a waveform recommendation framework has been developed, utilizing a closed-loop system and machine learning to predict droplet velocity and morphology. This framework optimizes waveform parameters by minimizing a predefined objective function, ensuring that a single droplet achieves the desired speed. However, this strategy does not yet account for the influence of viscoelastic ink properties, which remains an important aspect for further investigation. To further develop the waveform optimization, reinforcement learning has been recently highlighted. For reducing the droplet volume deviation, deep reinforcement learning based on stochastic state transition was used. The intelligent algorithm manipulated the waveform parameters for ensuring the droplet volume accuracy. This method achieved low deviation of the target volume for organic light emitting diode (OLED) manufacturing. For reliability of the jetting behavior, the deep Q-Network (DQN) is also a promising algorithm. A DQN agent was trained to find the optimal waveform for single drop ejection with target velocity. The trained agent adaptively controls the waveform to maintain the ideal jetting behavior with varying the temperature which induces the change of jetting behavior. Drop Impact and Spreading in Jet Printing

The ejected drops impact the substrate and spread until reaching the equilibrium. The impact of liquid drops on substrate creates various flow patterns depending on the properties of the liquid and the substrate. The velocity and the size of a drop also have a crucial influence on the resulting behaviors. The substrate also shows roughness, wettability, and heterogeneity. The inkjet printed drops undergo a sequence of five successive phases: kinematic, spreading, relaxation, wetting, and equilibrium. As the drop collides with the surface, the initial shape of the region of the drop which is out of contact remains largely unchanged in the initial (kinematic) stage. This phase lasts approximately until the contact diameter reaches the original drop diameter. The spreading phase then follows, in which the contact line expands radially, and nearly all the drop’s initial kinetic energy is consumed. The fluid properties and the impact conditions play a role in this phase. After spreading to a maximum extent, the drop may experience relaxation or oscillation, and possibly retraction of the contact line. A drop impacting on a hydrophobic surface may exhibit a receding contact line or even rebound if the initial kinetic energy is high enough; rebound is common from superhydrophobic surfaces. On the other hand, a drop on a hydrophilic surface may oscillate or show a momentary pause in its expansion and then undergo further spontaneous spreading due to capillary effects until its size reaches a final equilibrium state.

The extent to which drop wets the surface is usually described by its equilibrium contact angle (θeq), which is defined as the angle between the liquid–vapor interface as it meets the solid surface. For ideal substrate, the equilibrium contact angle is generally described as 14 cos⁡θeq=γSL−γSVγLV Hereby, γLV is liquid–vapor interfacial tension, γSL is sold–liquid interfacial tension and γSV is solid–vapor interfacial tension. However, actual substrates exhibit hysteresis due to factors such as surface roughness, contamination, and chemical heterogeneity. Consequently, the contact angle is represented by a range of stable equilibrium values rather than a single unique value. The upper limit of possible equilibrium contact angle is called the advancing contact angle θadv and the lower limit of it is called the receding contact angle θrec. The difference between θadv and θrec is called contact angle hysteresis which depends on the interaction between the liquid and substrate. − The inkjet-printed drop quickly attains the contact angle that lies between the receding and advancing contact angle.

To achieve a quasi-steady initial equilibrium, the spreading behavior of a sessile drop follows Tanner’s law, which describes the spreading dynamics as governed by the interplay between the capillary pressure gradient and viscous dissipation at the contact line. In the wetting regime, a reduction in capillary pressure induces an outward motion of the contact line. Under the capillary–viscous balance, the contact diameter (D) gradually increases with time (t) according to a power-law relationship (eq ), 15 D=ktn where the coefficient k represents the balance between surface tension and viscosity, while the power-law exponent n is typically ∼0.1. The ratio between the contact diameter of the droplet on the surface and its initial diameter, β = D/D 0, serves as a key parameter to characterize the spreading phase. During the wetting regime, the spreading dynamics of the droplet follow Tanner’s law, independent of the impact velocity.

Although most previous studies have focused on the impact and spreading dynamics of Newtonian fluids, the behavior of non-Newtonian fluids on solid surfaces has also become increasingly important in various applications. As shown in Figure , unlike Newtonian fluids, the impact dynamics of viscoelastic fluids can be effectively governed by their rheological properties in addition to viscosity and surface tension. Non-Newtonian fluid shows the difference oscillation motion compared with Newtonian fluid during the relaxation phase with the viscoelastic inks taking longer time in oscillation. In the equilibrium phase, the increment of polymer molecular weight in the solution decreases the final diameters on the substrate due to increase in low-shear viscosity.

Drop Coalescence and Line Formation

In inkjet printing, continuous lines are formed through the controlled coalescence of sequential droplets. As illustrated in Figure a, − the center-to-center drop spacing critically determines line morphology. When spacing is large, droplets remain isolated; at intermediate spacing, partial overlap produces scalloped edges; and at optimized spacing, droplets merge into uniform continuous lines. Excessively small spacing, however, results in liquid accumulation and bulging instabilities.

Evaporation and Film Formation in Jet Printing

The coffee-ring effect presents a significant challenge in achieving uniform surface profiles in inkjet-printed displays, where precise film foormation is critical for high-resolution and high-performance devices. IJP technology is widely used in the fabrication of display components, including OLEDs and quantum dot (QD) displays, where uniform deposition of functional materials is essential for consistent optical and electrical properties. The final film morphology in inkjet-printed displays is primarily governed by the evaporation dynamics of the printed drops. − When a droplet is deposited on a substrate, the evaporation rate at its edges is typically higher than at the center, leading to an outward capillary flow that transports suspended particles toward the periphery, resulting in a nonuniform deposit. This phenomenon is particularly problematic in display fabrication, as it can cause variations in film thickness, leading to inconsistencies in brightness and color reproduction.

The evaporation of submicroliter drop is governed by the diffusion of the vapor in the surrounding air (Figure ). Deegan et al. first reported that the evaporation flux on the drop surface is expressed by the analytic solution to the boundary value problem following as 17 J(r)∝(1−r̃)−λDeegan,⁣λDeegan=(π−2θc)(2π−2θc) where r̅ = r/R c and λ Deegan is a fitting parameter representing the nonuniformity of the evaporation. Equation shows that the behavior of the flux near the contact line is quantitatively influenced by the contact angle. For a drop with contact angle in the range of 0 ≤ θc ≤ π/2, nonuniform evaporation along the liquid–vapor interface induces a radial flow from the center of the drop toward the contact line. This flow compensates for the theoretically singular local evaporative flux, ensuring that the contact line remains pinned. The suspended particles in a sessile drop are transported by this radial flow, leaving a ring-like stains after the evaporation, a phenomenon known as coffee-ring stains.

To mitigate the coffee-ring effect and achieve uniform film formation, controlling the internal flow within evaporating droplets is crucial. One effective strategy is to reduce the outward capillary flow by inducing an opposing Marangoni flow, which is driven by surface tension gradients. This inward flow can be generated by adding surfactants or by heating the substrate, thereby redistributing particles more evenly within the droplet. − However, excessive surfactant addition can enhance the inward flow to the extent that it disrupts uniform particle transport, leading to nonuniform deposition. Therefore, a precise balance between evaporation-driven outward flow and Marangoni-induced inward flow is necessary to optimize film formation. In addition to surfactants, mixing solvents with different surface tensions is another effective method to manipulate internal droplet dynamics and suppress the coffee-ring effect. − By carefully controlling evaporation rates and internal flows, IJP can achieve high-quality thin films essential for next generation display technologies, ensuring uniform brightness, color stability, and enhanced device performance.

The type of evaporation mode significantly influences the final deposition patterns. The properties of the substrate can cause the droplet that is deposited onto it to achieve a range of stable equilibrium values of the contact angle due to the pinning or depinning of the contact line. This results in a variety of modes of evaporation. The coffee-ring pattern occurs in the constant contact radius (CCR) mode due to radial flow from the drop’s center toward the contact line. This flow accelerates as the contact angle decreases, causing most particles to move when the contact angle is small, making particle transport dominant in the final phase of evaporation. The constant contact angle (CCA) mode typically occurs on hydrophobic substrates, maintaining a high contact angle and inducing weak radial flow. This results in colloids being captured at the liquid–vapor interface, forming a dot pattern rather than a coffee-ring pattern. In the stick–slip (S&S) mode, the drop behaves like CCR mode during the stick phase, with particles moving toward the contact line. As the contact angle decreases to a critical value, enhanced shrinkage tension causes a slip, rapidly reducing the contact radius and increasing the contact angle until equilibrium is re-established. This evaporation process repeatedly cycles through these phases, with the alternating pinning-depinning motion producing a series of eccentric ring stains formed during the stick phase. In practice, various deposition patterns are observed depending on the evaporation mode and the interaction between liquid, particles, substrate, and vapor (Figure ).

In scenarios where Marangoni flow is absent, the deposition pattern can be influenced by manipulating particle–particle interactions. By adjusting the pH of the liquid, particle aggregation can be induced, thereby preventing particles from moving toward the contact line. , When the repulsive forces between particles are diminished by tuning the pH of the suspension, particle aggregates are more likely to settle on the substrate. Furthermore, a high viscosity of the liquid can also impede the transport of particles to the contact line, thereby affecting the final deposition pattern. The reduction of ambient pressure accelerates the evaporation rate, which leads to rapid shrinkage of the liquid–vapor interface. , This interface captures and drags into the center of the drop before moving the particles by the capillary flow. With the control of the pressure and concentration of the particles, the uniform and ordered patterns can be obtained in several seconds. Other authors reported the suppression of the coffee-ring stain by using different shapes of particles dispersed. A more homogeneous deposit rather than the accumulation of particles at edge was observed when using ellipsoids and spherical particles, respectively. In addition, Eral et al. investigated the suppression of the coffee-ring stain under alternate current (AC) electrowetting conditions applied to a drop containing polystyrene particles in the micrometer range. The electrostatic force inducing the contact line mobility and the internal flow generated by AC electrowetting prevent the accumulation of solutes along the contact line. The surface modification of particle dispersion can improve the uniformity of deposition patterns. High-molecular weight surface-active polymers prevent accumulation and pinning at the contact line.

Post-Deposition Sintering

Following solvent evaporation and film formation, nanoparticle-based functional inks, particularly metallic conductors, require a postdeposition sintering step to transform discrete particles into electrically percolated networks. In printed nano-to-microstructures, this process is governed by thermally activated surface and grain-boundary diffusion, ligand decomposition, and interparticle neck growth, which collectively determine the final conductivity, mechanical integrity, and dimensional stability of the patterned features. Conventional thermal sintering of silver nanoparticle inks typically requires temperatures in the range of 150–250 °C to promote particle coalescence and reduce interparticle contact resistance. However, such temperatures often exceed the thermal tolerance of polymer substrates used in flexible and stretchable electronics.

To overcome these thermal budget constraints, localized and rapid energy-delivery strategies have been developed. Photonic sintering using intense pulsed light (IPL) enables millisecond-scale heating of nanoparticle networks, allowing rapid neck formation while minimizing bulk substrate heating. This approach has been demonstrated for both silver and copper nanoparticle inks, achieving substantial conductivity enhancement on temperature-sensitive substrates. , Laser sintering provides spatially confined energy input with high positional precision, making it particularly suitable for high-resolution interconnects and multilayer structures. Electrical (Joule) sintering utilizes current-induced self-heating within percolated nanoparticle networks, enabling selective microstructural evolution without global thermal exposure. In addition, chemical strategies such as ligand displacement and room-temperature activation of nanoparticle surfaces have been reported to facilitate particle fusion and conductivity improvement without external high-temperature treatment.

Despite these advances, sintering remains intrinsically coupled to microstructural shrinkage, porosity redistribution, and stress development, which can distort high-resolution printed features. Therefore, precise control of energy input, diffusion kinetics, and particle–particle interactions is essential to preserve nano-to-microstructural fidelity while maximizing electrical performance in jet-printed and DIW-fabricated structures.

Functional Nano/Microstructures

The substrate plays a vital role in various printing strategies. The surface wettability and mechanical properties of substrate directly impact the mechanical performance and reliability of printed electronics. Interface engineering has been demonstrated to improve robust adhesion between substrate and patterns, mechanical stability under deformation and efficient electrical coupling. In order to obtain electronic devices with miniaturization and high integration, researchers tried to explore various printed materials structures, such as high-resolution dot and line printing, 2D patterning and 3D stacking. In this review, functional nano/microstructures refer to printed architectures with feature dimensions spanning the nano- to microscale that provide specific electrical, mechanical, optical, or biological functionalities. These structures, ranging from conductive microelectrodes and thin semiconductor layers to three-dimensional microscaffolds for biointerfaces, play a pivotal role in determining device performance in advanced electronic and biomedical systems.

Substrates and Their Treatment for Printing

Surface engineering plays a key role in determining the pattern fidelity, printing resolution, and reliability of printed electronics. The interfacial interaction between ink and substrate has a crucial impact on wetting behavior, feature size, film uniformity, and interlayer adhesion. Since most functional inks are colloidal dispersions or polymer solutions, their interaction with substrates is governed by surface energy compatibility, roughness, and chemical functional groups on the surface. The wettability of a substrate, typically characterized by the contact angle (θ) of a liquid droplet, directly governs ink spreading, adhesion, and pattern definition. Recent researches reported various surface modification techniques including physical strategies such as plasma treatment and UV/Ozone exposure, and also chemical approaches such as self-assembly monolayers (SAMs), silanization, fluorination, and in situ polymerization. For instance, oxygen-containing polar functional groups (−OH, −COOH) could be introduced on the substrate surface by high-energy ions and radicals in oxygen plasma modification (Figure a). These functional groups increased the surface free energy and enhanced hydrophilicity, thereby improving ink wettability on the substrate.

In general, low contact angles (<90°) promote ink spreading, which is advantageous for achieving uniform coverage in applications such as printed dielectrics and conductive electrodes. However, excessive wetting can result in uncontrolled spreading and merging of features, particularly problematic in high-resolution or dense pattern architectures. Conversely, high contact angles (>90°) typically indicate poor wetting behavior, which may lead to dewetting or incomplete coverage. Yet, in many printing scenarios for discrete features and high-resolution line printing, restricted ink spreading is essential for maintaining printed geometric fidelity. In such cases, surfaces with a contact angle slightly above 90°, typically in the range from 90° to 110°, can effectively enhance feature resolution. Therefore, it is a challenge to balance the wettability either for high-resolution printing or smooth printing. Prathapan et al. reported a surface modification method by using aligned cellulose nanocrystal coatings on hydrophobic polystyrene film and succeeded in enhancing the IJP resolution. In addition to the surface treatment, surface modification of the functional materials mixed in the ink was also demonstrated to strengthen the interaction between substrate and printing patterns. Zheng et al. developed a green liquid metal ink with liquid metal particles that were encapsulated with lignin. The hydroxyl groups of lignin strengthened the interface between the lignin-modified liquid metal particles and various substrates after printing (Figure b).

Recently, selective surface engineering has emerged as a useful strategy enabling high-resolution and complex patterning. This approach allows spatially controlled modulation of surface energy to deposit ink only onto predefined regions. Frabcis et al. demonstrated controlled wettability contrast on cells of doctor blader could produce precise and high-resolution patterns in gravure printing. Liu et al. utilized parallel vacuum ultraviolet technique to render selective region into high hydrophilicity on the hydrophobic substrate, providing precise spatial control over ink deposition. This localized surface modification could achieve printing feature width as small as 1 μm without need of physical mask (Figure d).

Zero-dimensional (0D) structures are usually defined as spatially discrete features with negligible lateral and longitudinal extensions, such as individual nanoparticles, isolated quantum dots, or LED array units, and have broad applications in high-resolution printed electronics. DIW is essentially a continuous extrusion-based printing technology, mainly used to make continuous lines or 3D structures. Therefore, it is basically unable to achieve digital droplet printing, which is the hallmark of technologies such as IJP, EHD, and aerosol jet printing. It is a challenge to print isolated 0D features by DIW technique. Approximate creation of 0D structures can be achieved through ink design or applying pulsed extrusion. Bae et al. fabricated a series of nanoscale color pixels (diameter of 620 nm) by printing different color-emitting nanophotonic inks in the direction perpendicular to substrate, showing a great promise in high-resolution display (Figure a). Although the resulting feature is a nominally zero-dimensional (0D) structure, an isolated pixel with negligible lateral extension, the deposition process inherently involves a directional flow of ink. In the study by Bae et al. (Figure a), the nozzle moved along the z-axis toward the substrate, enabling the deposition of a spatially confined ink droplet. Lateral spreading was effectively suppressed by finely tuning the ink rheology and implementing rapid extrusion modulation. Therefore, the term ‘perpendicular’ describes the direction of ink delivery (nozzle-to-substrate pathway) rather than the geometry of the final printed feature. The resulting structure remains 0D, while the perpendicular orientation simply characterizes the deposition approach used to create a localized, submicrometer pixel. This study demonstrated that submicrometer-scale DIW printing of zero-dimensional (0D) structures relies on the combined optimization of ink rheology and precise dynamic modulation of extrusion pressure and nozzle–substrate distance.

One-dimensional (1D) features commonly refer to continuous lines or wires that act as an interconnector, conductive electrode, strain sensor, or antenna element. In DIW, 1D patterns are formed by dispensing functional inks through micronozzles under pneumatic or mechanical pressure, thereby achieving continuous deposition of filaments with adjustable width and aspect ratio. Achieving high-resolution and high-fidelity 1D patterning using DIW relies on careful engineering of the ink rheologyoften requiring control of shear-thinning, viscoelastic behavior to enable smooth extrusion while retaining shape after deposition. Recent studies demonstrate that 1D DIW printing can allow a broad palette of ink materials including metal nanomaterials, carbon materials, and conductive polymers. Lateral resolution of the 1D lines could easily reach down to ∼30 μm. Some emerging studies have pushed the 1D resolution to sub micrometer via ultrafine nozzles and ink engineering. The construction of 1D structures with different engineered geometries have also been reported such as straight interconnects between functional units, serpentine wires for stretchable electrode, etc. These 1D designs can be monolithically integrated with other 2D or 3D features for the important part of multilayer flexible electronics. For example, Pak et al. developed a novel approach to print directly a strain sensor and an interconnect electrode onto textile substrates by direct ink writing (Figure b). The 1D interconnect printed electrode was used to bridge textile layers through controlled impregnation, confirming the stable conducive connection under strain and pressure.

DIW is not only a viable technique for the fabrication of 1D lines but also serves as an adaptable platform for printing 2D structures. The 2D structures typically refer to patterns or films that are extended in the plane and exhibited a limited thickness, commonly taking the form of planar electrode network, patterned active films and functional layers. For instance, Qian et al. demonstrated the CNT battery electrode with planar serpentine network structure exhibited good reversible stretchability and impressive electrical performance, showing only a 3% increase in resistance after 50 stretching cycles at 50% strain. (Figure c). Since 2D structures can be regarded as lateral extensions of 1D filaments, the factors influencing 1D printing, such as ink viscosity, substrate surface energy, and processing temperature, also play critical roles in determining the fidelity and uniformity of 2D pattern formation. Recent studies demonstrated DIW printed 2D structures severed as fundamental building block in a wide range of printed electronics, including stretchable circuits (Figure c), interdigital electrodes, and pressure sensing layers.

DIW technique is inherently well-suited for fabricating three-dimensional (3D) structures due to its layer-by-layer additive manufacture process. DIW allows the direct construction of vertically stacked or free-standing architecture without the need for sacrificial supports or complex transfer steps. The formation of 3D features in DIW relies on a balance between ink rheology and printing parameters. Specifically, the ink must exhibit shear-thinning properties and a sufficiently high yield stress to maintain its shape after extrusion. Rapid movement of the nozzle also facilitates the formation of 3D structures. In addition, rapid gelation or solvent evaporation further enhances the vertical buildability of the printed structure. Recent advances such as real-time curing (e.g., photothermal or UV assisted curing) can also significantly improve the shape stability of 3D structures. Similar to other 1D and 2D structures, the scale of 3D structure by DIW is also determined by printing nozzle size and ink properties. Recent studies show that DIW printing enables the direct 3D constructure of high-aspect-ratio pillars, bridges, coils, and stent-like structures which typically have lateral dimensions ranging from hundreds of micrometers to tens of millimeters. These 3D structures exploit their geometric anisotropy to achieve unique functions that cannot be realized in planar feature designs. Lee et al. developed a printable and conductive ink that demonstrated sufficient elasticity to preserve structural fidelity immediately after extrusion (Figure d). The printed features exhibited a minimum line width of 100 μm and maintained high stretchability up to 150%. The authors realized the direct printing of various 3D architecture, including elastic micropillars and arch-shaped soft interconnects, and demonstrated direct writing of 3D-structured skin electronics. Moreover, 3D-structured circuits expand the functional scope of printed architecture, paving the way for more complex and integrated device applications. Unique three-dimensional geometry significantly enhances the sensitivity of pressure-sensing layers by enabling greater deformation and stress localization under applied force, thereby making them highly suitable for tactile sensing systems. For instance, Cavallo et al. developed biocompatible PEDOT:PSS-functionalized CarboSil scaffolds via 3D printing, which exhibited tunable mechanical compliance and stable, linear piezoresistive response over a wide pressure range. Four-dimensional (4D) structure refers to the 3D printed features that undergo programmable transformations overtime in response to external stimuli such as heat, light, magnetic or electric field. Therefore, the DIW-based 4D systems represent a dynamic extension of 3D structures, in which geometry, material composition, and stimuli-responsiveness are coengineered to introduce temporal functionality and programmable shape reconfiguration into the printed architectures. Unlike static structures in lower dimensions, the formation of 4D structures via DIW relies on customized ink formulated with stimuli-responsive materials such as shape-memory polymers, liquid crystal elastomers or hydrogels. A representative example of shear-induced anisotropy in 4D printing was reported by Gladman et al., who demonstrated a bioinspired 4D printing strategy in which the orientation of cellulose fibrils within a hydrogel composite was precisely controlled during the DIW process (Figure e). In addition, introducing localized stress or strain during the printing process provides an alternative approach to achieving time-dependent shape transformation. Although the 4D structure is an extension of the 3D structure on a time scale, the design of the geometric structure will have a profound influence on the performance of the final device. Liaw et al. demonstrated DIW-printed hydrogels with geometry-dependent surface wrinkling patterns that emerge upon humidity exposure, highlighting the critical role of geometric surface design in programmable morphing behavior. Recent studies have highlighted the significant potential of 4D printed structures in diverse applications, including soft robotics, programmable actuators (Figure e), and smart grippers. Notably, the temporal dimension introduced by 4D printing extends beyond mechanical deformation, encompassing functional transformations such as tunable conductivity, dynamic optical modulation, and biological activity. Recent advances in 4D printing have significantly expanded the development of magnetically actuated systems, where deformation is precisely driven by externally applied magnetic fields. The design strategy typically involves dispersing magnetic particles or nanosheets within printable matrices such as PDMS, polyurethane, or shape memory polymers, thereby achieving an optimal balance between rheological properties for extrusion printability and sufficient magnetic responsiveness for reliable actuation. Ongoing research continues to address challenges in particle dispersion uniformity, material reprogrammability, and ink formulation versatility, enabling multimodal, reconfigurable, and microscale actuation capabilities for applications in smart devices and soft robotics. − Although DIW has been widely employed to fabricate structures from 0D to 4D, several challenges remain for micro/nanoscale printing, including extrusion instabilities, nozzle clogging, and postdeposition deformation or collapse of features when the ink exhibits insufficient yield stress. These limitations can be mitigated by tailoring ink rheology, implementing dynamically controlled pneumatic pressure, and integrating in situ curing strategies such as thermal, UV, or laser-assisted solidification.

Nano-to-Microstructures by Aerosol Jet Printing

Aerosol jet printing enables the fabrication of high-resolution micro- to nanoscale structures with precise geometric control and excellent conformity to complex or curved surfaces, whereas DIW is generally restricted to flat substrates due to its near-contact, extrusion-based printing mechanism. AJP enables high-resolution patterning by taking advantage of a high-velocity aerosol stream, collimated through a coaxial sheath gas, to deposit ink with exceptional precision. This aerodynamic focusing allows the formation of printed features with lateral resolutions typically between 10 and 100 μm. Recently, submicrometer scale could also be achieved by combing with other assistant methods.

Similar to the DIW technique, AJP is also limited in printing 0D structures, as its continuous aerosol stream is inherently designed for line-based deposition rather than drop-on-demand control. Nonetheless, recent advances have demonstrated effective strategies to enable 0D dot patterning. Mosa et al. introduced a pneumatic shuttering mechanism that allows rapid modulation of the aerosol stream, enabling precise dot printing with tunable diameters ranging from 15 to 100 μm (Figure a). Notably, the printed isolated dot structure had a height of over 2 μm, which corresponded to a limited 0D structure.

AJP offers distinct advantages for the fabrication of high-resolution 1D structures based on the aerodynamic focus through coaxial sheath gas. The biggest advantage of AJP is the creation of direct printing onto oblique or conformal surfaces without any additional tools. Recent studies demonstrated that the highest lateral resolution of AJP could reach up to less than 10 μm. In addition to utilizing conductive materials as printing inks, Toccla et al. also demonstrated the feasibility of micrometer-scale patterning using magnetic inks, achieving the preparation of magnetic printed 1D features with the width of only 20 μm onto both rigid and flexible substrate. Temperature control at the printhead has emerged as a critical parameter in improving the fidelity of 1D structures printed via AJP technique. As demonstrated by Guyll et al., in-line thermal manipulation of the printhead enables precise tuning of droplet evaporation dynamics, thereby decoupling resolution from deposition rate, which is beneficial to the improvement of resolution.

The continuous high-resolution capability of AJP enables the integrated 2D fabrication of diverse precision patterns, making it well-suited to produce complex electronic and functional layouts. Rich et al. demonstrated the use of AJP technology to fabricate 2D interdigital transducers using ink made of different conductive materials including silver nanowires, graphene, and PEDOT:PSS (Figure b), showing a great promise in the application of surface acoustic wave microfluidic devices. AJP technology has been widely used to print source and drain electrodes and semiconductive layer of thin-film transistors. Lu et al. demonstrated a fully printed fabrication of flexible 1D-2D thin film transistors with h-BN as a gate dielectric and AgNWs as electrodes by AJP technique.

Unlike conventional layer-by-layer 3D printing processes, aerosol jet printing (AJP) enables direct-write vertical and out-of-plane growth by exploiting the self-alignment and layer-stacking behavior of aerosolized droplets, thereby allowing the fabrication of high-aspect-ratio structures such as micropillars with diameters of approximately 60 μm and heights up to 1 mm. Recent advances have demonstrated the remarkable versatility of AJP in fabricating complex 3D architectures beyond conventional vertical or pillar structures. Jung et al. demonstrated the generation of helices with controlled geometries with feature width of only ∼340 nm by 3D AJP nanoprinting technique (Figure c). Moreover, Hu et al. introduced the fabrication of sophisticated ceramic microarchitectures such as freeform spirals, microlattices and micropyramids with minimum feature sizes of 20 μm and near-zero shrinkage (Figure d). More importantly, hierarchical porosity of printed 3D structure can be engineered over a broad range of length scale, from tens of nanometer to a millimeter. The porous 3D ZnO structures exhibited significantly enhanced photocatalytic performance compared to bulk ZnO. Despite its versatility, AJP also exhibits intrinsic limitations for micro/nanoscale fabrication, including diffraction-limited focusing of the aerosol stream, instability of droplet–droplet coalescence at submicrometer length scales, and diminished pattern fidelity during the growth of high-aspect-ratio features. These challenges may be alleviated by advanced nozzle miniaturization, real-time modulation of the aerosol flow, and the incorporation of coupled thermal or electrohydrodynamic assistance to stabilize droplet transport and enable reliable submicrometer feature control.

Nano-to-Microstructures by EHD Jet Printing

EHD jet printing offers a unique capability for fabricating nano-to microscale structures with exceptionally high spatial resolution, owing to its electric field-driven jetting mechanism that minimizes the lateral stress during deposition. This technique enables the controlled deposition of inks into filaments and patterns, making it particularly suitable for applications demanding ultrafine resolution and material efficiency. Generally, nonconductive substrates such as PET and PDMS films cannot directly serve as electrodes in electrohydrodynamic jet printing due to their lack of electrical conductivity. However, recent advancements have demonstrated that EHD jet printing can be successfully applied to insulating substrates through specialized electric field configurations. , For example, Wang et al. developed a hybrid stainless steel–quartz nozzle system and employed a phase-field model to achieve stable drop-on-demand (DoD) EHD jet printing on nonconductive substrates.

EHD jet printing has been considered as a powerful technology which can easily print 0D structures with high lateral resolution (<5 μm) and vertical resolution (<100 nm) onto flat and flexible structures. For example, Zhu et al. demonstrated the use of full-color perovskite-based inks for direct EHD printing, achieving high-resolution dot arrays as small as 5 μm (Figure a). More importantly, the printed dot has a minimum thickness of 40 nm with surface roughness as low as 4 nm, underscoring the high vertical EHD printing resolution. In addition, EHD printing can also achieve other 0D printing like quantum dots (QDs). Kim et al. demonstrated high resolution patterning of diverse quantum dots inks with precise spatial registration by EHD jet printing. The feature size and thickness of circular QDs were as small as 3.9 μm and 18 ± 10 nm, respectively.

EHD printing enables high-resolution line patterning through nozzles based on electrical field-driven jet mechanism. Some studies have reported that the printed QDs feature line patterns could be tuned down to 0.25 μm in width and 25 nm in thickness, by employing EHD nozzles with a small diameter. EHD-printed 1D structures exhibit high optical transparency, attributed to their ultrafine lateral widths and minimal vertical thicknesses, which collectively minimize light scattering and material coverage. Kim et al. developed individually addressable, transparent and stretching single silver nanofiber (NF) interconnections by direct EHD printing. These aligned Ag NFs exhibited ultrafine line width (∼600 nm), high optical transparency (∼97%) and reliable stretchability (∼45%), outperforming previously reported interconnects in both spatial resolution and transparency (Figure b).

EHD printing has demonstrated a promising potential for printing 2D structures in organic thin-film transistors (TFTs), particularly in the precise deposition of electrodes and semiconductor layers. Jung et al. successfully printed P3HT and PEDOT:PSS as the semiconductor and electrodes, respectively, using the cone-jet mode of EHD printing. In addition, the ion-gel electrolyte ink was deposited by the dripping mode due to high ionic concentration which exhibited unstable jetting behavior under cone-jet mode. This all-EHD jet printing allowed the facile fabrication of thin-film transistors. In contrast to the layer-by-layer deposition in DIW, realizing true 3D printing through EHD jet techniques remains a significant challenge, primarily due to the instabilities of fast electrified jets, which make precise vertical material buildup difficult to control. To overcome the limitations associated with high-speed jet instability in EHD printing, recent work has demonstrated a strategy based on electrostatic deflection of electrified jets, enabling precise control over jet trajectory during deposition (Figure c). This strategy enables the rapid printing of 3D cylindrical microstructures by electrostatically driven layer-by-layer assembly at frequencies up to 2000 layers per second. While EHD jet printing offers ultrahigh spatial resolution, it still faces several key constraints in micro/nanoscale fabrication, including jet instability at high operating frequencies, limited compatibility with highly insulating substrates, and difficulties in achieving controlled vertical build-up because of rapid electrohydrodynamic perturbations. These challenges can be alleviated by optimizing the applied field configuration, employing hybrid nozzle architectures, and implementing active electrostatic jet-deflection or real-time jet-stabilization strategies, which collectively enhance jet continuity and broaden substrate compatibility. Pattern Compensation Strategies

The fluidic and discrete characteristics of IJP inherently give rise to pattern fidelity issues such as scalloping, bulging, corner rounding, and ink accumulation, particularly at geometrically complex features like inner corners or narrow gaps. These morphological artifacts become increasingly detrimental to electrical performance and yield as circuit density increases, necessitating pattern compensation strategies to anticipate and correct such deformations.

A comprehensive framework for such strategies was demonstrated by Vila et al., who implemented and statistically validated eight geometric compensation schemes, including perimeter trenches, hammerhead terminals, and corner modifications, using a Dimatix DMP-3000 printer and silver nanoparticle ink on flexible PEN substrates (Figure a). Over 500,000 printed structures were analyzed using automated image-based distance mapping, and statistical tools including Box–Cox transformation, ANOVA, and Tukey HSD tests. Among the tested schemes, a one-drop perimeter trench (“ce”) consistently yielded the best fidelity, improving sharpness, minimizing bulging, and raising the functional yield of interdigitated electrodes from <10% to nearly 100% (Figure b,c).

Complementing this, Ramon et al. proposed a systematic methodology for extracting design rules and applying compensation strategies optimized for inkjet-printed organic thin-film transistors (OTFTS). Using a Dimatix DMP-2831 printer with 10 pL silver nanoparticle ink, they investigated how key parameters, drop spacing, jetting delay, substrate temperature, drop lattice geometry, and print direction, affect printed line morphology. They categorized typical pattern distortions such as scalloping, bulging, and coffee-ring effects, and identified process windows (e.g., 20 μm drop spacing at 40 °C in printing direction) yielding uniform, narrow lines. Their results highlighted that print orientation and lattice design substantially impact line width and edge fidelity. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the necessity of integrating inkjet-specific compensation strategies into the design phase. Such techniques bridge the gap between intended and printed geometries, enabling more aggressive layout scaling without yield loss. Moreover, their systematic and statistically grounded methodology lays a foundation for EDA-compatible correction engines, analogous to OPC and DRC in photolithography.

Design Rule Formalization and EDA Integration

While local pattern compensation addresses feature-level distortions, scaling inkjet-printed electronics to multilayer and high-density systems requires global design rule formalization and integration into electronic design automation (EDA) workflows. Unlike photolithographic processes, which benefit from vector-based layouts and well-established process design kits (PDKs), IJP relies on bitmap-defined patterns and faces challenges such as drop-to-drop variability, substrate-dependent wetting, and nonlinear interlayer misalignment.

To tackle these, Mashayekhi et al. developed an inkjet-specific PDK framework leveraging parametrized cells (PCells) to generate and characterize test structures systematically. Their methodology categorized design rules into three hierarchical levels: (1) single-layer rules (e.g., minimum printable line width and spacing) (Figure a), (2) two-layer rules for interlayer overlap, alignment, and spacing (Figure b), and (3) multilayer stacking constraints relevant to OTFT structures (Figure c). Using fully inkjet-printed test vehicles comprising up to four functional layers, they empirically determined process-specific limitsfor example, a minimum printable line width of 60 μm and a minimum interconnect spacing of 140 μm to prevent discontinuities and short circuits. Misalignments exceeding 15 μm in multilayer configurations were found to significantly degrade electrical performance. These design constraints were embedded in PCell-generated layout structures, instantiated in open-source editors such as Glade, and connected to a semiautomated characterization workflow. Electrical and optical testing of printed designs enabled automated extraction of rule-valid dimensions, with rule violations flagged through design rule check (DRC) logic (Figure d). Importantly, inkjet-specific artifacts such as scalloping and coffee-ring effects were incorporated directly into the PCell logic, allowing adaptive pattern generation based on empirical behavior. This approach bridges the gap between digital design tools and print-aware physical layouts, enabling scalable, rule-compliant design of multilayer inkjet-printed systems.

Complementing this vector-to-layout approach, Kwon et al. developed a bitmap-centric methodology that combines empirical process characterization with layout generation. They first investigated how drop spacing (DSx, DSy) and platen temperature (Tp) influence printed line morphology. Reducing spacing and raising Tp produced narrower, more uniform lines, suppressing scalloping and bulging, as confirmed through microscopy and quantitative plots (Figure e). To further improve spacing fidelity, they introduced a delayed-printing strategy that staggers droplet ejection in time, mitigating capillary coalescence and enabling minimum gaps down to ∼15 μm (Figure f). From these results, they formalized design rules in three domains: layout-to-bitmap (L2B) conversion, interconnect features, and TFT cell design. L2B rules addressed aliasing and pixel quantization, interconnect rules defined spacing and edge fidelity, and device-level rules specified contact geometries and alignment margins. These were summarized in a schematic illustrating rule domains for print-aware layouts (Figure g). The methodology was validated by fabricating a 10 × 10 OTFT array on flexible foil. Using the derived rules, layouts were bitmap-translated and printed with high yield. Measurements across 100 transistors showed consistent channel lengths and uniform ID–VGS behavior, confirming the effectiveness of rule-based layout design (Figure h). This framework complements vector-driven PCell approaches, together forming the basis for inkjet-specific PDKs. Embedding empirically derived constraints into both bitmap and vector workflows enables scalable, manufacturable design automationbridging the gap between prototyping and production in printed electronics.

Flexible Electronics

Flexible Devices by Direct Ink Writing

Flexible Circuit by DIW Technique

Flexible circuits are foundational components in the development of soft electronics, enabling conformal integration with nonplanar or deformable surfaces without compromising electrical performance. The excellent mechanical adaptability makes them essentialfor applications in flexible electronics such as flexible electrical platforms, electronic skin and soft robotics. The DIW technique enables direct deposition of 1D conductive features on the soft substrates, offering high design freedom for customized circuit patterning. Based on the dimensionality of the printed circuit patterns, flexible circuits can be categorized into 2D planar layouts and 3D architectures. Compared with conventional fabrication methods for flexible circuits, such as photolithography and transfer printing, direct ink writing offers distinct advantages when processing flexible and deformable substrates. DIW enables direct patterning of a wide range of functional inks (e.g., metals, carbon-based materials, conductive polymers, and their composites) without the need for masks or multistep processing, thereby facilitating rapid prototyping and high design adaptability. In contrast, conventional photolithography typically requires rigid, planar substrates and precise mask alignment, whereas transfer printing entails additional processing steps that can damage soft substrates or deteriorate interfacial adhesion. These advantages render DIW a particularly promising approach for the fabrication of both two- and three-dimensional flexible electronic circuits.

DIW printing offers a low-cost, straightforward, and efficient route for fabricating 2D planar flexible circuits on flexible substrates such as paper, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film, and polyimide (PI) film. The electrical durability of DIW-printed circuits is typically evaluated through cyclic deformation testing including bending, stretching, or twisting, where the relative resistance variation (ΔR/R0) is monitored as a function of deformation cycles. , In addition, some studies have demonstrated the mechanical compliance using finite element analysis to complement the experiments and provide insights into strain distribution and structural robustness. Abas et al. presented a robust flexible electronic circuit fabricated by printing conductive carbon paste on a PET substrate via microdispensing-based DIW approach (Figure a). The minimum line width could be as small as 198 μm by optimizing printing parameters. The printed foil circuit exhibited reliable electrical performance after cyclic full bending of 0° and 90°. Recently, printed flexible circuits on paper were also reported. Stretchable circuits can be fabricated by printing conductive inks onto elastomeric substrates (e.g., PDMS, Ecoflex, and thermoplastic elastomers), replacing conventional flexible substrates to accommodate large mechanical deformations such as stretching and twisting. Lopes et al. proposed a straightforward strategy for fabricating hybrid microchip-integrated stretchable circuits by directly printing Ag-EGaIn-SIS composite ink onto SIS substrates using an extrusion-based DIW process. Through a solvent-induced polymer-gel transition, microchips were seamlessly embedded and self-soldered onto the circuit, enabling reliable electrical performance under strains exceeding 500% (Figure b). In addition, the via-holes play an important role on the multilayer stretchable circuit, as they enable reliable vertical interconnection between multilayered conductive pathways while maintaining mechanical compliance under deformation. Kim et al. developed a fully integrated stretchable electronic system fabricated via DIW-printed layer-by-layer strategy, encompassing interconnects, insulating layers, and vertical via-holes. The engineered via structures exhibited robust electrical conductivity and maintained stable interlayer connections even under significant deformation.

3D-architectured flexible circuits offer significant advantages in terms of integration density, functionality, and application areas. Recent advances in 3D flexible electronics have demonstrated DIW as a promising additive manufacturing technique capable of fabricating 3D-architectured flexible circuits. Ling et al. reported a free-standing 3D metal structure with the tension of Field’s metal as the driving force by using 3D DIW technique, achieving high aspect ratio (up to 750:1), excellent conductivity (∼2 × 104 S/cm), and self-healing capability. The authors demonstrated a battery-free 3D-architectured temperature sensing flexible circuit. The formation of 3D stretchable circuits could be realized by engineering the rheology of viscoelastic conductive ink. Lee et al. reported an omnidirectional DIW strategy for intrinsically stretchable conductors, enabling the fabrication of freestanding 3D structures with minimum size below 100 μm and stretchability exceeding 150% (Figure c). This approach was successfully applied to 3D-structured on-skin electronic systems capable of real-time sensing and display, maintaining stable operation even under 40% tensile strain.

Flexible Sensors by DIW Technique

Flexible sensors are an important part of flexible electronics, showing a great potential promise in applications of wearable healthcare devices, haptic devices, and robotics skin. Compared to other nonprinting fabrication methods used in flexible sensors (such as photolithography, laser patterning, solvent coating, and template method), DIW technology presents significant advantages because of its low temperature processing, maskless patterning, and additive manufacturing. Moreover, DIW allows for direct deposition of various functional materials onto a wide range of flexible and stretchable substrates, greatly reducing the complexity of flexible sensor manufacturing. Recent studies demonstrated that DIW-printed flexible sensors could respond to various stimuli including strain, pressure, touch, proximity and temperature.

Flexible strain sensors represent one of the extensively studied sensing devices by DIW technique, primarily because DIW can precisely deposit conductive 1D patterns onto stretchable substrates, enabling the direct integration of sensing elements with mechanical compliance. Shi et al. demonstrated a flexible strain sensor by directly printing an aqueous nanocomposite ink composed of electrochemically derived graphene oxide (GO) and PDMS submicrobeads onto a microporous polymer template by DIW technique. The flexible sensor exhibited a nearly linear response to strain with a gauge factor of 20.0 ± 2.8 and was then used for real-time monitoring of body motions such as elbow bending, finger bending and ulnar pulse. Flexible pressure sensors could show the response to external mechanical pressure through the piezoresistive and piezocapacitive mechanism. DIW allows for the printing of hierarchical architectures with various microstructures such as microporous layers or micropillars to enhance the sensitivity of piezoresistive and piezocapacitive sensors. A notable demonstration of structural tunability enabled by DIW technique in pressure sensing was the fabrication of silicone meshes coated with CNTs/Ecoflex, which served as highly sensitive and ultrathin pressure sensing layer (Figure a). The hierarchical conductive mesh structure was engineered through control of filament diameter, interspacing and layer number via DIW, thereby enabling enhanced piezoresistive response. The contribution of 3D printed dielectric layer to the improvement of sensitivity of piezocapacitive sensors has also been demonstrated. Luo et al. developed a stretchable touch sensor with simple architecture by an all-printing method that integrates screen printing and DIW technology (Figure b). The device featured uniform resistivity and robust performance across large-area substrates, enabling real-time detection of touch positions based on different resistance signals even under bending or stretching.

In addition to the mechanical stimuli, DIW-printed flexible sensors capable of detecting nonmechanical stimuli have been developed. Owing to the capability to directly print planar 2D coil structures with precise control over geometry and spacing, DIW is considered as an effective approach to fabricate inductive sensors for proximity sensing application. Veerapandian et al. presented a stretchable inductive sensor by printing liquid metal microparticles/polymer composite ink on an elastic substrate via DIW. This inductive sensor could sense the approaching of metal objects (e.g., Cu) according to the eddy current effect (Figure c). Moreover, human skin can be distinguished from metallic objects based on the distinct inductive signal profiles, primarily attributed to differences in permittivity and capacitive coupling. In these years, DIW-printed flexible sensors have demonstrated responsiveness to temperature or humidity by incorporating functional materials that exhibited sensitivity to these stimuli. , With the technological advancement of single-function flexible sensors, increasing attention has been directed toward multimodal sensors which can mimic the multisensory perception of the human body. Because of the capability of predefining patterns across various dimensional scales, DIW exhibits significant potential for the design of integrated multimodal sensing devices. Oh et al. developed an all-in-one wearable glove system composed of stretchable sensors, heaters and vibratory feedback modules via entire DIW technique of liquid metal-eGaIn (Figure d). The multimodal gloves enabled users to perceive contact events and distinguish materials with different temperatures in virtual reality.

Inkjet-Printed Soft Electronics

Key Features of Inkjet-Printed Transistors

IJP has emerged as a transformative technology in the field of printed electronics, offering a cost-effective, scalable, and versatile approach to fabricating electronic devices. Unlike traditional manufacturing methods such as photolithography and vacuum deposition, IJP enables direct, maskless patterning of functional materials on a variety of substrates, reducing material waste and production costs. , This noncontact deposition method is particularly advantageous for flexible and organic electronics, where the ability to print precise patterns on lightweight, bendable, and even stretchable surfaces is critical. Furthermore, IJP allows for high-throughput, digital fabrication processes that are compatible with roll-to-roll (R2R) manufacturing, paving the way for large-area and high-resolution printed circuits, sensors, transistors, and energy devices. As the demand for sustainable and energy-efficient manufacturing grows, IJP is positioned as a key enabler of next-generation electronics, spanning applications from wearables and biomedical sensors to printed photovoltaics and advanced display technologies. This review highlights recent advancements in inkjet-printed electronics, focusing on the interplay between printing technology, material properties, and device performance, while addressing key challenges in achieving higher resolution, improved printing quality, and enhanced functionality.

IJP Process Development

IJP, based on DoD deposition, offers a unique additive route for patterning functional materials. However, the discrete and fluidic nature of this method introduces substantial challenges in pattern fidelity, such as droplet misplacement, coalescence, and wetting inconsistency, factors that severely compromise device yield and electrical performance. Unlike conventional CMOS lithography with robust and abstracted design rules, inkjet-printed electronics lack standardized layout constraints that can account for these process-specific variabilities.

The need for printing-aware design rules has been emphasized in recent studies. In particular, Sowade et al. performed a root cause analysis on over 900 all-inkjet-printed organic TFTs (on PEN substrates under ambient conditions) (Figure a), revealing a manufacturing yield of only ∼34%. , They identified the “first drop problem”, a jetting instability triggered by prolonged idle states of the printhead, as a dominant source of spatial nonuniformity. Early printed device regions (e.g., A1, B1 arrays) showed significant degradation in OSC layer thickness and I ON due to reduced droplet volume and trajectory deviation (Figure b). A key outcome of their analysis was the empirical derivation of primitive design rules, such as minimum printable spacing between source and drain electrodes. Thousands of test structures were printed to evaluate electrical isolation margins under stochastic jetting conditions. Furthermore, print directionality was found to strongly affect line morphology (Figure c): features printed along the jetting direction exhibited sharper edges and more uniform thickness than those printed across it, leading to fewer alignment-related defects in multilayer stacks. The study also highlighted systemic causes of device failures, including wetting-induced spreading, satellite droplet formation, and open circuits due to missing droplets or nozzle clogging (Figure d). Only ∼8% of defects stemmed from short/open circuits; most failures (over 60%) were attributed to electrical instabilities arising from trapped charges or morphological nonuniformities. For example, the use of hygroscopic dielectric materials like cPVP contributed to charge trapping and off-current variability, while OSC thickness fluctuations caused significant dispersion in I ON/I OFF ratios and field-effect modulation.

These findings show the necessity for a tight codesign approach between inkjet process physics and circuit layout constraints. Unlike in silicon VLSI design, where design rules are technology-independent abstractions, inkjet-based layouts must explicitly reflect the mechanical and fluidic behaviors of the printing system. This calls for the formalization of process-aware design rules, including critical dimensions, drop placement tolerances, and layer alignment strategies, and their eventual integration into electronic design automation (EDA) tools tailored for additive electronics. The subsequent subsections will discuss two foundational approaches to address this challenge: (1) pattern compensation techniques for real-time correction of printing distortions, and (2) systematic design rule formalization linked with EDA-based layout automation.

Inkjet-Printed Organic Transistors

IJP has emerged as a transformative fabrication technique for OTFTs and integrated circuits (ICs) due to its cost-effectiveness, precision, and compatibility with flexible substrates. Unlike conventional photolithographic methods requiring high-vacuum and multistep processing, IJP enables additive, maskless manufacturing, reducing production complexity and material waste. This advantage has positioned inkjet-printed OTFTs as a promising technology for wearable electronics, smart packaging, and IoT devices. ,

Despite its advantages, the realization of fully inkjet-printed organic transistors faces several critical technological challenges. First, ink formulation remains a major bottleneck, as not all functional materials used in transistor fabrication can be readily converted into inkjet-compatible inks. Second, the IJP process itself is inherently sensitive and prone to issues such as nozzle clogging, droplet misplacement, and ink spreading, all of which contribute to reduced device uniformity and yield. Third, the formation of high-quality printed thin films requires precise control over the ink drying dynamics, which are often complicated by phenomena such as the coffee-ring effect. In particular, dielectric layers, demanding high insulation, uniform thickness, and pinhole-free morphology, are especially challenging to print, often resulting in nonuniform films that degrade device performance. Fourth, solution-processable inks necessitate careful interlayer compatibility. Achieving uniform wetting and adhesion between layers requires fine-tuning of surface energies, while solvent orthogonality is essential to prevent damage to previously deposited layers.

Despite these challenges, ongoing research has led to significant advancements in inkjet-printed transistors by systematically addressing many of the underlying limitations. Printed organic TFTs are typically benchmarked using key device and circuit metrics, including capacitance, field-effect mobility, threshold voltage, subthreshold swing, on/off current ratio, operating voltage, voltage gain, operating frequency, and noise margin. Representative flexible organic TFTs commonly exhibit field-effect mobilities on the order of 1–10 cm2 V–1 s–1, on/off current ratios around 105–106, and low-voltage operation below a few volts, consistent with recent reports of high-performance printed devices (Table ). Comparison of Reported Inkjet-Printed Organic TFTs for Materials, Printed Channel Geometry, and TFT Performance

This section presents a comprehensive analysis of recent progress in inkjet-printed OTFTs, with a focus on process innovations and key technological breakthroughs. Specifically, it highlights developments in charge carrier mobility enhancement, high-resolution patterning techniques, and monolithic three-dimensional (3D) integration. Furthermore, the integration of these transistors into functional electronic circuits and sensing platforms is discussed, illustrating their potential for practical applications.

Development of Inkjet-Printed Organic Thin-Film Transistors

The development of inkjet-printed OTFTs has been enabled by advances in solution-processable semiconducting materials. Early demonstrations of polythiophene-based OFETs established conjugated polymers as viable channel materials, Subsequent efforts led to fully printed all-polymer OFETs fabricated entirely via solution-based techniques, initiating the field of flexible plastic electronics. An all-polymer field-effect transistor was reported where the device was fabricated by solution-processable printing techniques, opening the field of flexible plastic electronics. A breakthrough came with the work of Sirringhaus et al., who showed that molecular self-organization in conjugated polymers enabled two-dimensional charge transport, resulting in a significant enhancement of carrier mobility. This finding laid the foundation for achieving high-performance OTFTs via solution processing. Collectively, these early studies provided the materials and processing insights that would later be harnessed for IJP, transforming OFETs from laboratory prototypes into scalable components for flexible and printed electronics.

Partially Inkjet-Printed OTFTs

IJP emerged in the early 2000s as a promising fabrication technique for organic field-effect transistors (OFETs), offering additive, mask-less, and low-temperature processability suitable for large-area and flexible electronics. Initial efforts focused on hybrid approaches, where only selected components, typically the electrodes or semiconducting layer, were deposited via IJP. One of the pioneering demonstrations was by Sirringhaus et al., who reported high-resolution IJP of all-polymer transistor circuits using drop-on-demand (DoD) printing (Figure a). In this work, patterned wettability on the substrate was used to confine the aqueous PEDOT:PSS ink, enabling the definition of short-channel source/drain (S/D) electrodes (∼5 μm) with high precision. PEDOT:PSS, due to its water solubility, high conductivity, and compatibility with flexible substrates and low-temperature processing, has since been widely used as an inkjet-printed electrode material. , To improve conductivity and pattern resolution, metallic nanoparticle inks, especially silver and Ag–Cu alloys, have been employed. These inks offer low viscosity, good colloidal stability, and high conductivity after sintering. Gamerith et al. demonstrated the use of Ag–Cu nanoparticle inks and Ag-precursor-based inks for OFET electrodes, achieving process compatibility with polymeric dielectrics and stable device operation (Figure b). Further advancements introduced subfemtoliter IJP using micrometer-scale nozzles and electrostatic actuation, enabling precise deposition of Ag nanoparticle S/D electrodes with feature sizes as small as 1 μm. , These devices exhibited low contact resistance (∼5 kΩ·cm), minimal parasitic capacitance, and high performance even at low voltages (1.5–3 V), validating the feasibility of micrometer-scale organic circuits fabricated by inkjet.

In addition to electrodes, IJP has also been applied to organic semiconducting layers. A representative example is the deposition of small-molecule semiconductors such as 6,13-bis­(triisopropylsilylethynyl)­pentacene (TIPS-pentacene). Through careful optimization of substrate temperature and drop spacing, smoother film morphology and enhanced crystallinity were achieved at elevated temperatures (∼60 °C) (Figure c), leading to significant improvements in field-effect mobility (up to 0.24 cm2/V·s) and on/off current ratios (∼107). More recently, antisolvent crystallization strategies have enabled direct IJP of single-crystal semiconductor films. Minemawari et al. developed a dual-inkjet method where a semiconductor ink (e.g., C8-BTBT in 1,2-dichlorobenzene) and an antisolvent (e.g., DMF) were sequentially printed into hydrophilic confinement regions. Controlled mixing induced crystallization at the liquid–air interface, forming single-crystalline films with exceptional flatness and molecular order. OTFTs fabricated from these films showed record mobilities exceeding 30 cm2/V·s under ambient conditions, rivaling vacuum-deposited counterparts. Semiconductor-insulating polymer blends have also been investigated as a route to enhance film uniformity and device stability. Lim et al. used P3HT blended with polystyrene (PS) to form self-assembled nanowire networks within inkjet-printed single droplets, improving both mobility (∼0.02 cm2/V·s) and on/off ratios (∼106) with minimal semiconductor loading (Figure d). Cho et al. extended this strategy using TIPS-pentacene/APC (amorphous polycarbonate) blends. By tuning solvent composition (e.g., toluene/p-xylene), ink viscosity, and polymer ratio, they achieved well-aligned and vertically phase-separated TIPS crystals. The resulting devices exhibited average mobilities of 0.53 cm2/V·s and excellent mechanical and operational stability on flexible substrates. These studies collectively demonstrate that even in partially inkjet-printed OTFTs, precise control over materials, ink formulation, and printing parameters can yield devices with high performance and stability, highlighting the importance of hybrid process engineering as a bridge toward fully printed organic electronics.

Fully Inkjet-Printed OTFTs

Inkjet printing (IJP) technology has advanced to enable the fully printed fabrication of OTFTs, where all functional layers, including electrodes, semiconductors, and gate dielectrics, are deposited exclusively through inkjet processes. While coating-based dielectrics often achieve superior uniformity, inkjet-printed dielectrics remain advantageous for specific OTFT architectures. Inkjet printing allows localized dielectric deposition precisely at required regions, minimizing material waste and enabling heterogeneous dielectric engineering, for instance, employing distinct dielectric materials for n- and p-type devices within the same circuit. Moreover, its fully additive, maskless, and low-temperature characteristics render inkjet printing highly compatible with the scalable fabrication of flexible and large-area printed electronics. − Early studies focused on demonstrating the feasibility of such multilayer integration while addressing interlayer solvent compatibility and stack stability. A foundational example was reported by Kim et al., who printed silver nanoparticle electrodes, a sol–gel-derived hybrid dielectric, and TIPS-pentacene semiconductor on flexible PES substrates (Figure e). This study emphasized the vulnerability of printed dielectric layers to redissolution or damage during successive ink deposition, a key challenge for fully printed stacks. To mitigate this, Chung et al. employed a cross-linked poly­(4-vinylphenol) (PVP) dielectric, achieving improved chemical resistance and process reliability. Their devices demonstrated notable performance, including a field-effect mobility of 0.06 cm2/V·s and a subthreshold swing of 3.17 V/dec, indicating that printed layers, when appropriately engineered, can yield transistor characteristics comparable to vacuum-deposited counterparts. In parallel, registration precision was addressed by Tseng and Subramanian, who introduced a wetting-defined self-alignment strategy using PVP mesa structures to confine source/drain ink placement with submicrometer accuracy. This method drastically reduced parasitic overlap capacitance and enabled printed inverter circuits operating with submillisecond propagation delay. The functional integration of such devices was further demonstrated by Chung et al., who constructed a fully printed inverter using two p-type OTFTs, achieving a voltage gain greater than 7 at −40 V. Meanwhile, Mitra et al. extended this concept to paper substrates, promoting low-cost and biodegradable device platformsthereby broadening the scope of printed electronics to sustainable applications (Figure f).

Over the past decade, fully inkjet-printed OTFTs have advanced from basic layer stacking to high-performance device and circuit demonstrations through a series of technological refinements. Channel scaling was realized by Chung et al. and Chu et al. using short-channel geometries defined by drop control and patterned confinement (Figure g). For scalable manufacturing, Ramon et al. developed a simplified multistep process that stabilized film morphology and ensured device uniformity across larger substrates. Interface engineering emerged as a central enabler of performance enhancement. Ha et al. showed that incorporating a polymer binder layer facilitated wetting control and improved the crystallinity of printed semiconductors, while Chung et al. demonstrated a one-step organo-compatible interface layer that covalently linked the dielectric and electrode surfaces, yielding more ordered semiconductor growth and reduced contact resistance. These strategies enabled substantial gains in mobility, stability, and reproducibility without compromising the additive and maskless nature of inkjet processing. Recent demonstrations by Luczak et al. and Ziang et al. further validate the practical viability of this platform. Luczak et al. implemented fully printed voltage inverters as NOT gate components on flexible substrates, and Ziang et al. realized subthreshold OTFTs operating at nanowatt power levels, with high intrinsic gain suitable for energy-autonomous and ambient electronics (Figure h).

However, inkjet-printed OTFTs still face challenges in reproducibility, yield, and long-term operational stability, as printing defects, such as missing droplets, nozzle clogging, or layer misalignment, can result in open circuits or short failures. Ink formulation engineering has also been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in enhancing printing reproducibility. Nevertheless, recent studies indicate that these limitations are being progressively addressed through the establishment of robust design and process optimization strategies, as discussed in section . For example, Baek et al. achieved nearly 100% device yield and less than 10% device-to-device variation across a 10 × 10 printed TFT array, exhibiting stable operation on ultrathin polymer foil. , In addition, Kwon et al. reported inkjet-printed three-dimensional organic field-effect transistors (3D OFETs) that maintained stable electrical characteristics for over eight months under ambient storage conditions. A subsequent study further demonstrated year-long operational stability in vertically integrated dual-gate printed transistors, where device uniformity was preserved even after multiple printing and annealing cycles. Overall, although printed OTFTs still require improved defect management and process optimization, recent progress in yield and long-term durability marks a significant step toward practical large-area applications. These advancements highlight the potential of inkjet-printed OTFTs as a scalable, low-power, and sustainable platform for next-generation logic, sensing, and wearable electronic systems.

Channel Scaling Strategies

The miniaturization of channel length is a critical pathway to improving the performance of inkjet-printed OTFTs. Short-channel devices offer faster switching, reduced power consumption, and higher integration density, essential attributes for applications in flexible logic, radio frequency circuits and low-voltage electronics. However, digital IJP inherently limits pattern resolution due to droplet spreading, substrate wetting behavior, and nozzle size, typically constraining channel lengths to tens of micrometers. To overcome these limitations, two principal strategies have been pursued: (i) self-aligned ink engineering, which uses interfacial energy contrasts, chemical repellency, or capillary effects to precisely localize deposition; and (ii) ink confinement and spreading control, which focuses on tuning droplet behavior through ink rheology, solvent volatility, and printing parameters to restrict lateral flow and preserve narrow geometries.

Self-Aligned Ink Engineering

Self-alignment techniques enable precise control over electrode placement and interlayer registration without resorting to lithography or surface patterning. In a seminal work, Noh et al. introduced a self-aligned printing (SAP) technique in which the first printed electrode was treated with a hydrophobic self-assembled monolayer (SAM) (Figure a). This treatment repelled subsequent droplets, thereby confining the second printed electrode and yielding reproducible channel lengths as short as 100–400 nm. The approach minimized parasitic overlap capacitance and enabled MHz-range switching speeds. Building on this, Caironi et al. demonstrated high-yield fabrication of single-droplet electrodes using nanoscale drop spacing control, achieving over 94% device yield and nanoscale feature reproducibility (Figure b). This discrete droplet approach not only improved resolution but also reduced material waste. Additional strategies have leveraged capillarity and structural guidance. Hyun et al. developed self-aligned side-gate transistors by printing electrodes into imprinted microtrenches on flexible plastic substrates (Figure c). The capillary-driven ink flow enabled sub-5 μm gate-to-channel distances, well below the natural resolution limit of inkjet printers, and effectively minimized gate–source overlap, allowing for low-voltage operation.

Ink Confinement and Spreading Control

Complementary to surface-directed alignment, an equally powerful strategy involves tuning the physical behavior of ink droplets during deposition. Chu et al. demonstrated a direct-write approach wherein SU-8, a photopatternable polymer, was printed between electrodes. Upon exposure to solvent from the silver ink, the SU-8 formed a partially dissolvable ridge that acted as a physical barrier, confining the ink and producing source–drain gaps as narrow as 800 nm (Figure d). This method enabled short-channel devices with uniform geometry, without requiring any surface pretreatment or alignment marks. Another formulation-focused approach was presented by Doggart et al., who optimized ink viscosity and solvent systems to control dot shape, spreading, and coalescence. With careful tuning of droplet volume and spacing, they achieved line widths and channel lengths below 50 μm using only conventional inkjet tools, demonstrating how material and process engineering alone can push the resolution limits of printing. Similarly, Yokota et al. employed subfemtoliter-scale IJP to directly deposit 0.5 fl droplets, forming electrodes with ∼1 μm widths and channel lengths, all at sintering temperatures below 100 °C. These devices achieved a high transconductance of 0.76 S/m at only 4 V, and the gentle processing conditions made them compatible with temperature-sensitive organic materials and flexible substrates (Figure e). In a more accessible implementation, Chung et al. utilized a standard 1 pL cartridge and interdigitated electrode designs to fabricate fully printed OTFTs with sub-10 μm channel lengths (Figure f). Without any surface patterning or confinement structures, optimized droplet spacing and printhead calibration enabled the reliable formation of short-channel devices exhibiting average mobilities of ∼0.065 cm2/V·s and inverter gains >5 at −30 V. This work highlights the scalability and cost-effectiveness of purely printing-based methods for achieving sub-10 μm resolution.

Mobility Improvement

Enhancing charge carrier mobility in inkjet-printed OTFTs is essential for realizing high-performance and scalable flexible electronics. Printed OTFTs often suffer from structural imperfections and interfacial inhomogeneities that impede charge transport. Accordingly, mobility improvement strategies have focused on (i) morphology and crystallinity control and (ii) interface engineering.

Morphology and Crystallinity Control

Achieving high molecular ordering and phase uniformity within the semiconducting layer is central to mobility enhancement. Polymer blend engineering has emerged as a widely adopted approach, enabling controlled phase separation between semiconducting and insulating components to form favorable percolation pathways. As reviewed by Kwon et al., this strategy stabilizes inkjet-printed films while promoting nanoscale transport networks.

Lim et al. demonstrated that blending P3HT with polystyrene (PS) produces nanowire networks confined within an insulating matrix during single-droplet printing (Figure a), resulting in enhanced mobility and environmental stability. Vertical phase segregation has likewise been exploited to concentrate semiconducting domains near the dielectric interface. Cho et al. reported that TIPS-pentacene/APC blends enabled controlled vertical separation and improved interfacial ordering (Figure b), achieving mobilities up to 0.53 cm2/V·s. Extending this concept toward practical applications, Mizukami et al. fabricated AMOLED display drivers using DTBDT-C6/PS blend-printed OTFTs with mobility values reaching 1.2 cm2/V·s, sufficient for driving full-color flexible OLED panels.

In addition to blend strategies, antisolvent-assisted crystallization has enabled direct printing of highly ordered small-molecule films. Minemawari et al. employed a dual-inkjet approach to induce single-crystal growth of C8-BTBT at confined interfaces (Figure c), demonstrating record mobilities exceeding 30 cm2/V·s under ambient conditions.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that mobility enhancement in inkjet-printed OTFTs is primarily governed by droplet-mediated morphology evolution, phase segregation control, and interfacial ordering rather than solely intrinsic semiconductor properties.

Interface Engineering

In addition to improving the bulk properties of the semiconductor, reducing the energetic and physical mismatch at critical interfaces is essential for high-performance printed OTFTs. A frequent source of performance degradation arises from poor injection at the metal–semiconductor or dielectric–semiconductor interface. To address this, interfacial engineering techniques have introduced passivation or buffer layers to reduce charge injection barriers and mitigate contact resistance. Ha et al. demonstrated that introducing a polystyrene (PS)-brush interlayer between inkjet-printed silver source/drain electrodes and the organic semiconductor improved interfacial contact quality. This led to enhanced hole injection, lowering the contact resistance and boosting device mobility from <0.03 to ∼0.27 cm2/V·s. In a subsequent study, Ha et al. generalized this approach by embedding PS interlayers at multiple interface sites, including the dielectric–semiconductor and electrode–semiconductor junctions (Figure d). This one-step interface engineering method improved film morphology, mechanical adhesion, and electrical performance simultaneously. The resulting fully inkjet-printed and transparent OTFTs exhibited enhanced mobility and circuit yield, further underlining the importance of holistic interface optimization.

Three-Dimensional Integration

In the realm of inkjet-printed OTFTs, 3D integration has emerged not only as a strategy to increase transistor density but also as a means of improving electrostatic control, switching behavior, and architectural flexibility in printed circuits. Unlike conventional planar structures, 3D integration enables vertical stacking of functional layers, particularly gate electrodes and complementary transistors, offering more compact form factors and enhanced functionality. Recent developments in this field can be categorized into two synergistic directions: dual-gate configurations for enhanced electrostatics and logic behavior, and vertically stacked device architectures for footprint reduction and functional diversification. ,

One of the inherent limitations of conventional single-gate OTFTs is their partial depletion behavior, which results in suboptimal gate modulation, limited transconductance, and poor subthreshold performance. This drawback can be mitigated by integrating a second gate electrode either above or below the semiconducting layer. The resulting dual-gate OTFTs offer stronger gate-channel coupling, tunable threshold voltages, and better immunity to environmental fluctuations. Furthermore, in printed electronics, where interfacial quality and material inhomogeneity are challenging, dual-gate designs allow electrostatic compensation for variability, leading to improved device uniformity and operational robustness. A representative example is the work by Kwon et al., who reported a monolithically printed dual-gate complementary OTFT inverter. Compared to single-gate counterparts, the dual-gate design significantly improved static noise margin and voltage gain, while maintaining comparable dynamic response despite higher gate capacitance. This suggests that electrostatic improvement can be achieved without speed penalties. Singh et al. extended this concept by printing a dual-gate OTFT using silver electrodes and a DTBDT-C6/polystyrene active layer blend (Figure a). The top-gate bias was employed to shift the threshold voltage of the bottom-channel transistor, enabling finely controlled voltage transfer characteristics with symmetric gain and low-voltage operation (±2.5 V). This approach was further demonstrated in logic circuits, including NOR gates, in which independent biasing of each gate allowed logic reconfigurability and noise margin control.

Beyond individual transistor performance, stacking OTFTs vertically provides an elegant route to improve circuit density, minimize interconnect length, and implement multilayer logic in compact footprints. , In such configurations, multiple functional transistors are fabricated layer-by-layer with intermediate dielectric and routing layers, enabling “transistor-on-transistor” integration. A foundational study by Kwon et al. demonstrated vertically stacked complementary OTFTs where a p-type and an n-type transistor were printed on top of each other, sharing a gate electrode (Figure b). This vertically integrated complementary pair effectively doubled transistor density and simplified the design of logic gates such as NAND and NOR. In a subsequent work, the same group developed a full-adder logic circuit using vertically stacked NAND gates, all inkjet-printed with high resolution and alignment fidelity (Figure c). These circuits achieved 100% yield, excellent device-to-device uniformity, and year-long stability under ambient storage, validating the manufacturability of complex 3D organic logic. Taking this further, Kwon et al. introduced a fully monolithic 3D integration strategy, wherein a bottom n-type OTFT was overlaid with a top p-type OTFT, both featuring dual-gate configurations (Figure d). This arrangement enabled full-depletion mode operation with enhanced subthreshold swing, reduced leakage, and improved voltage gain. Importantly, the bottom-layer n-type device, being more sensitive to air and moisture, was physically encapsulated by the top-layer p-type transistor, improving environmental stability. This architecture also facilitated a programmable 3D logic array where logic functionality could be dynamically reconfigured via gate bias, illustrating the high design freedom enabled by vertical integration. Applications for Printed Flexible Electronics

As the printing resolution, material performance, and integration strategies have advanced, OTFTs are increasingly being adopted in circuit-level implementations. These include analog interfaces and digital logic for signal conditioning and control and computation, respectively, − wireless communication modules such as radio frequency identification (RFID) tags, − and a variety of physical and chemical sensors. This section outlines representative applications of inkjet-printed organic transistors, including analog and digital circuits, and transistor-based sensors reported in recent literature.

Analog Circuits

Analog circuits are indispensable components in systems requiring signal amplification, filtering, and preprocessing before digitization or wireless transmission. In inkjet-printed organic electronics, analog OTFT circuits are increasingly engineered for low-voltage, flexible environments where form factor, energy efficiency, and biosignal compatibility are paramount. A key application area for analog OTFTs is in front-end interfaces for sensors, where they function as voltage or current amplifiers to condition weak biological or chemical signals prior to further processing. For example, inkjet-printed common-source amplifier configurations have been monolithically integrated with electrochemical sensing elements, as demonstrated in potassium ion sensing and lactate detection systems (Figure a). These circuits, fabricated entirely on flexible substrates, effectively manage both bias control and signal gain in real-time chemical sensing contexts. In the domain of biopotential acquisition, Laurila et al. presented a fully printed, mechanically conformal charge amplifier system fabricated on a 1.5 μm parylene substrate. Designed for epidermal integration, the circuit operates at sub-200 pW power levels and directly amplifies signals such as ECG and pulse waves. Although it does not explicitly operate in the subthreshold regime, the design exemplifies how printed OTFTs can deliver biologically relevant gain under stringent mechanical and energy constraints. These sensor-integrated circuits reflect a broader evolution of analog OTFTs: from generic amplifier roles toward application-specific, wearable front-end interfaces capable of real-time physiological signal handling.

Beyond simple amplifiers, printed complementary OTFT technologies have been used to realize advanced analog blocks based on operational amplifier (OPA) topologies, including differential pairs, current mirrors, and multistage gain blocks. Matsui et al. demonstrated OPAs capable of amplification, integration, differentiation, and oscillation at a standard 5 V supply, consuming only ∼150 nW (Figure b). Likewise, Maiellaro et al. implemented folded-cascade architectures with complementary device stacks, achieving voltage gains exceeding 50 dB and improving gain–bandwidth trade-offs. These works highlight the potential of inkjet-printed OTFTs to implement complex analog signal processing functions for wearable or portable diagnostic systems.

A promising direction involves optimizing transistor characteristics for ultralow-power analog amplification in the subthreshold regime. Jiang et al. reported Schottky barrier OTFTs with steep subthreshold slopes, enabling intrinsic gain values above 1000 and transconductance efficiency approaching 38 S/A, all under subnanowatt operation (Figure c). Unlike conventional analog designs that rely on strong inversion, these circuits exploit exponential I–V behavior to maximize gain per unit power, setting a new benchmark for energy-efficient analog signal transduction in flexible electronics. The demonstrated use in electrophysiological signal detection suggests that printed organic transistors may soon rival subthreshold silicon CMOS in wearable bioelectronics.

Digital Circuits

Inkjet-printed OTFTs have become essential building blocks for digital logic in flexible electronic systems, particularly in applications requiring large-area integration, low-voltage operation, and mechanical compliance. Inkjet-printed logic circuits, including inverters, flip-flops, and NOR gates, have been demonstrated using complementary organic semiconductor technology. These circuits form the foundation for low-cost, flexible digital electronics and large-area computing applications.

The development of printed digital logic began with robust inverter architectures that established the feasibility of logic gate implementation using solution-processed organic semiconductors. One of the earliest milestones was achieved by Kawase et al., who demonstrated inkjet-fabricated depletion- and resistor-load inverters using PEDOT:PSS electrodes, PVP dielectric, and F8T2 semiconductors (Figure a). Through wettability confinement on photopatterned polyimide, sub-10 μm channel resolution was realized, achieving voltage gains of ∼2 and operational frequencies up to 250 Hz. The study further demonstrated a 4 × 4 active-matrix backplane for electrophoretic display driving, highlighting early system-level applications. To improve device electrostatics, Singh et al. introduced fully inkjet-printed dual-gate OTFTs using silver electrodes and a DTBDT-C6/polystyrene blend. The dual-gate configuration allowed threshold voltage modulation and enabled symmetric voltage transfer characteristics with noise margins exceeding 40% at ± 2.5 V. This electrostatic flexibility offered a pathway to robust and noise-tolerant inverter operation. Further pursuing minimal architectures, Luczak et al. developed a fully inkjet-printed unipolar inverter using a single p-type OTFT and a photodegraded P3HT-based resistor (Figure b). The circuit, fabricated with Ag electrodes, PVP dielectric, and DPPDTT semiconductor, achieved long-term ambient stability, maintaining functionality after over three years of storage, demonstrating the reliability of all-organic interfaces. Efforts to improve device density and performance are exemplified by Chung et al., who implemented high-resolution inkjet patterning using a 1 pL droplet cartridge, achieving average channel lengths of ∼9 μm. Their diode-load inverter, featuring W/L = 14,000/9, attained a voltage gain of 5.5 V/V and operated up to 1 kHz, with a propagation delay of 0.44 ms. This performance demonstrates how reducing parasitic capacitance is essential for extending the operating frequency of inkjet-printed logic circuits. Mechanical adaptability was emphasized by Fukuda et al., who printed OTFT inverters on 1 μm thick parylene-C substrates (Figure c). The devices showed field-effect mobilities of ∼1.0 cm2/V·s and operated under 50% compressive strain, reinforcing their applicability for ultrathin, skin-compatible electronics. In a significant advancement in switching speed, Tseng et al. combined inkjet deposition with micrometer-precision mechanical dragging (“print-and-drag”) to form 5 μm gate lines (Figure d). Using TIPS-CFP semiconductors and PVP dielectric, their hybrid-fabricated diode-load inverters achieved propagation delays of ∼15 μs, over 1 order of magnitude faster than conventional all-printed counterparts, pushing printed OTFT logic into the MHz-range regime.

Beyond inverters, the capability of inkjet-printed OTFTs has been extended to functional logic blocks and sequential digital elements. Ramon et al. demonstrated NAND gates and inverters fabricated entirely by IJP on photopaper substrates using silver nanoparticle ink, PVP dielectric, and TIPS-pentacene semiconductor. Their focus on process yield and pattern alignment tolerance enabled consistent logic function at VDD = −20 V. Building on the dual-gate concept, Singh et al. implemented inverters and NOR gates on PEN substrates using DTBDT-C6/PS semiconducting layers. By optimizing gate overlap and leveraging gate-induced mobility modulation, they achieved logic-level NOR behavior with clean voltage transfer curves, validating more complex logic operations on flexible substrates. Sequential logic was realized by Hayasaka et al., who reported complementary D-type flip-flops constructed from 18 fully inkjet-printed OTFTs (Figure e). The circuit combined top-gate n-type and bottom-gate p-type devices using TU-3/PαMS and diF-TES-ADT/PS semiconductors, respectively. Operating at VDD = 5 V, the flip-flop functioned as a negative-edge-triggered latch with 3-fold faster switching than NAND-based equivalents, owing to reduced series resistance and gate stack depth. Kwon et al. demonstrated a 3D monolithic integration strategy for inkjet-printed organic transistors to enable high-density digital logic on flexible substrates (Figure f). By vertically stacking three complementary dual-gate transistors (n–p–n), they implemented a NAND gate using only three devices, reducing transistor count compared to conventional CMOS logic. The 3D NAND exhibited ideal voltage transfer characteristics with balanced input–output behavior due to symmetric dual-gate control. The design was further extended to a programmable logic array capable of reconfiguring into basic logic functions such as AND, OR, XOR, and XNOR. A 12 × 8 gate array achieved ∼60 transistors/cm2, highlighting the scalability and compactness of the approach.

Sensors

Organic transistor-based sensors have emerged as powerful platforms for diverse sensing modalities, owing to their inherent amplification capability, material tunability, and low-temperature process compatibility. , Unlike conventional passive sensors that rely on external readout and amplification circuits, OTFTs allow for direct signal transduction and amplification within a single, compact device. Their intrinsic mechanical flexibility further enables seamless integration with nonplanar or deformable surfaces such as human skin, soft robotics, and biological tissues. Among fabrication techniques, IJP stands out as a digital, maskless, and additive method well-suited for scalable production of OTFT-based sensors. It eliminates photolithographic steps, reduces material waste, and supports design customization on biodegradable or flexible substrates. These features position inkjet-printed OTFT sensors as ideal candidates for next-generation wearable health monitoring, artificial skins, and interactive bioelectronic systems. In particular, IJP facilitates large-area active-matrix integration with high pixel density and addressability, enabling spatiotemporal mapping of tactile, chemical, or physiological signals.

Inkjet-printed OTFTs have shown substantial promise in pressure sensing due to their soft mechanical interfaces and facile stacking with deformable dielectrics. In an early demonstration, Noguchi et al. fabricated pressure-sensitive OTFTs on flexible substrates by combining inkjet-printed Ag electrodes with spin-coated polyimide and thermally evaporated pentacene (Figure a). The top dielectric layer was designed to deform under pressure, modulating capacitance and thus drain current, enabling tactile detection up to 20 kPa. Griffith et al. introduced a fully inkjet-printed device employing nanostructured blends of TIPS-pentacene and polystyrene as the semiconducting layer, interfaced with a quantum tunneling composite (QTC) film whose resistance changed under load (Figure b). This resistive–electrostatic hybrid mechanism yielded linear response over 0–50 kPa with fast dynamics, making it suitable for robotic skin applications. Cosseddu et al. further demonstrated an 8 × 8 OTFT active matrix with inkjet-printed TIPS-pentacene transistors layered beneath a PDMS pressure-sensing membrane. This array achieved millimeter-level spatial resolution for localized pressure mapping, showcasing its feasibility in skin-interfacing systems.

Owing to their functional surface accessibility and electrolyte gating compatibility, inkjet-printed OTFTs have been actively explored for biosensing applications. Martínez-Domingo et al. developed top-gate OTFT biosensors with inkjet-printed Ag electrodes and a Cytop dielectric, where the gate was functionalized with anti-IgG antibodies. Antigen binding induced threshold voltage shifts, enabling label-free detection down to 100 ng/mL. Medina-Sánchez et al. reported dual-gate OTFTs where antibody immobilization was achieved via EDC/NHS chemistry (Figure c). Dual-gate biasing allowed fine control over transconductance and off-state leakage, facilitating serum-phase detection of anti-IgG with nanomolar sensitivity. Chennit et al. introduced fully inkjet-printed coplanar electrolyte-gated OTFTs (EGOFETs), using DPP-DTT as the semiconductor and gold nanoparticle electrodes. These low-voltage (sub-1 V) transistors responded reproducibly to DNA hybridization, demonstrating potential for label-free biosensing in aqueous media. Baek et al. implemented dual-gate OTFT lactate sensors with enzyme-functionalized top gates (Figure d). Operating in the subthreshold regime (<0.5 V), their design yielded a transconductance-derived sensitivity of ∼20 μA/mM, validating their use in metabolite sensing under ultralow-power conditions.

Photoresponsive OTFT arrays fabricated via IJP have enabled large-area visual sensing with mechanical flexibility. Ruiz-Preciado, Baek et al. demonstrated a 10 × 10 active matrix comprising inkjet-printed organic photodiodes (P3HT:PCBM) and OTFTs (DPP-DTT) on 1.4 μm parylene-C (Figure e). Pixel-wise photocurrent was reliably read out under visible light, and operation remained stable under bending, supporting wearable vision system integration. Fang et al. further developed vertically stacked OTFT-based photodetectors (VOFETs) via IJP (Figure f). Their structure minimized pixel footprint while maintaining high optical sensitivity, achieving submillisecond response through efficient carrier accumulation under illumination.

The integration of OTFTs into active-matrix arrays has enabled dynamic, high-resolution mapping of tactile and physiological signals across large areas. These arrays leverage the addressability and amplification of OTFTs to realize real-time, pixel-level sensing in flexible and conformable formats. In a representative 2D system, Baek et al. demonstrated a 10 × 10 active matrix comprising inkjet-printed OTFTs and resistive pressure elements in a 1T1R configuration (Figure a). Each OTFT functioned as an electrical switch, enabling sequential access to the pressure signals from the corresponding pixels. The system achieved a spatial resolution of 2.5 mm and a temporal resolution below 5 ms, allowing for accurate visualization of pulse wave propagation across the radial artery. This work highlights the feasibility of high-speed physiological signal mapping using scalable printed electronics. Expanding into the third dimension, Jo et al. developed a 3D-integrated multimodal sensor array capable of simultaneously and independently detecting pressure and temperature at each pixel (Figure b). The architecture leveraged stacked device layers and orthogonal interconnects, including shared gate planes, to eliminate crosstalk and preserve signal integrity across modes. This configuration enabled the spatial decoupling of thermal and mechanical stimuli, allowing simultaneous 2D mapping of both parameters. The system was applied to robotic grippers, which demonstrated temperature-adaptive object handling, grasping and lifting a hot or cold cup while maintaining stable tactile control. This work exemplifies the power of IJP in enabling multifunctional, skin-like electronics through layer-by-layer 3D integration. Emerging Applications of Printed Nano- and Microstructures

Flexible Energy Harvesting Devices

Beyond conventional applications in flexible electronics and bioprinting, recent advances in printing technologies, particularly DIW, inkjet, and EHD jet printing methods, have extended their utility into emerging domains of energy devices including solar cells, generators, batteries, and supercapacitors. Since solar cells are integrated by multiple functional layers including photoactive layers, charge transport layers, buffer layers, and back electrode layers, printing technologies have emerged as a promising approach for large-scale and low-cost fabrication of these layers. For instance, Chen et al. demonstrated a fully screen-printed perovskite solar cell by using a viscosity-tunable perovskite ink based on a methylammonium acetate ionic liquid solvent (Figure a). Compared with other methods (e.g., spin coating, spray coating, and blade coating), this screen printing enabled precise control over large-area coating (up to 5 × 5 cm2) with nearly 100% ink utilization at a high printing speed of over 20 cm/s.

Based on different energy conversion mechanisms, the energy-harvesting power generators can be divided into piezoelectric, triboelectric, ferroelectric, and thermoelectric generators. Related research has focused on fabrication of various generators in a thin film, 3D porous, or bulk architecture (Figure b). For example, Lim et al. developed a flexible piezoelectric energy harvester based on a BaTiO3 hybrid film using an all-IJP method, achieving an output power of 5 μW and a power density of 0.42 μW·cm–2. Jeong et al. developed a microbrush-structured triboelectric generator with microfiber arrays by 3D DIW printing. It exhibited a high electrical output (up to 20 V) and good durability over 3000 cycles. Hu et al. reported a transparent molecular ferroelectric crystalline lattice based on imidazolium perchlorate (ImClO4) via continuous and rapid 3D printing technique, exhibiting dynamic tuning of mechanical metamaterials with an extended lifetime and improved sustainability. Moreover, a cost-effective, scalable strategy integrating solvothermal synthesis, screen printing, and sintering was developed to fabricate flexible inorganic thermoelectric films based on Bi2Te3 nanoplates and Te nanorods as nanobinders, enabling a large-scale manufacturing of flexible thermoelectric devices.

Batteries represent another promising application. Printing methods have been employed for the scalable fabrication of electrodes and solid-state electrolytes, particularly lithium-ion and lithium–metal batteries. , Recent study reported by Yang et al. demonstrated a roll-to-roll electrodeposition and transfer process for continuous prelithiation of lithium-ion battery (LIB) anodes, achieving high initial Coulombic efficiencies of 99.99% for graphite and 99.05% for silicon/carbon composite electrodes (Figure c). This method offered a high-performance and scalable prelithiations in LIBs, with strong potential for industrial adoption. Supercapacitors also benefit from printing-based fabrication of electrodes and electrolytes with engineering structures. Deka et al. demonstrated a woven carbon fiber and thermoset polyester resin based structural supercapacitor via 3D printing, exhibiting a high energy of 2.69 Wh kg–1, a power density of 43.20 W kg–1, and a Coulombic efficiency of 88.8% at the current density of 1000 mA g–1 (Figure c).

Flexible Catalysis

Integration of printing technologies into catalytic material design is fostering a paradigm shift in the development of photocatalysts and electrocatalysts. Unlike conventional manufacturing approaches, various printing strategies enable tailored architecture, compositional control, and spatially defined active sites, all of which critically affect catalytic activity, selectivity and stability. Recent advances in printing techniques have significantly expanded the material and structural diversity accessible for catalytic systems. −

Photocatalysis benefits from the design flexibility offered by 3D printing. Various photocatalysts, such as g-C3N4, TiO2, and doped metal oxides, can be incorporated into printable resins or inks, facilitating the fabrication of monolithic photocatalytic systems for air and water purification. Xu et al. demonstrated a 3D-printed air filter based on g-C3N4/PEGDA composite, which exhibited excellent photocatalytic NO removal efficiency and durability under visible light irradiation (Figure a). In addition, researchers have also successfully fabricated pure TiO2 nanostructures via high-precision 3D printing combined with a novel low-temperature chemical sintering approach, eliminating the need for organic binders. , The binder-free architecture enabled full exposure of active surfaces and maximized interfacial contact between particles, thereby enhancing charge separation efficiency and improving overall photocatalytic performance.

Inkjet Bioprinting

Inkjet Printing as a Synergistic Platform for Biofabrication

IJP was initially developed for graphic reproduction and was later adapted for precision deposition in microelectronics and materials science, including nanoparticle patterning, printed electronics, and thin-film fabrication. Its ability to dispense picoliter-scale droplets with micrometer-level resolution, programmable control, and minimal material waste has facilitated adoption across a range of industrial and research settings. The operational characteristics of IJP, including noncontact actuation, high placement accuracy, and digital controllability, are well aligned with emerging demands in biofabrication, particularly for tasks that require localized biomaterial delivery and spatially organized cell placement (Figure ).

Alignment between IJP functionalities and biofabrication criteria, supporting its application in spatially controlled cell and biomaterial patterning.

In tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, critical requirements include preserving cell viability, enabling multicellular copatterning, and supporting biologically derived materials. Drop-on-demand IJP, especially in piezoelectric and thermal configurations, fulfills these needs through low-shear and thermally controlled droplet generation. These features enable spatially defined deposition of cells and biomolecules under gentle conditions, making IJP suitable for constructing cell-laden architectures with microscale fidelity. Compared to manual cell seeding or bulk hydrogel casting, IJP offers precise material placement, reproducibility, and design flexibility. ,

The droplet-based actuation of inkjet systems supports the deposition of cells, proteins, and soluble factors with high spatial precision and minimal mechanical disruption. Multiple studies have demonstrated the feasibility of printing mammalian cells using both thermal and piezoelectric actuators, with maintained viability and function. For example, hepatocyte arrays, endothelial patterns, and neuronal cell cultures have been generated using IJP platforms. , Despite its advantages, inkjet systems are sensitive to nozzle clogging, especially when working with high-viscosity bioinks or cell suspensions. Proper ink formulation, nozzle maintenance, and filtering protocols are essential to mitigate these issues.

Inkjet bioprinting allows control over key droplet parameters such as size, velocity, and interdroplet spacing, which can be digitally adjusted to guide spatial deposition in two- and three-dimensional configurations. This capability is particularly useful in organizing early tissue constructs, where cell placement can influence local signaling environments and subsequent tissue formation. Unlike analog deposition methods, IJP enables automated and reproducible workflows that reduce operator variability and support high-throughput patterning.

Although its use is typically constrained to low-viscosity bioinks, IJP supports a wide range of printable materials including hydrogel precursors, extracellular matrix proteins, nucleic acids, and living cells. Postprinting cross-linking mechanisms such as photopolymerization (e.g., GelMA), , ionic gelation (e.g., alginate), − thermal gelation (e.g., collagen), − and enzymatic catalysis (e.g., fibrin) ,, are commonly used to stabilize printed structures. These strategies enable the fabrication of mechanically stable and biologically relevant microenvironments. Importantly, cells can be printed directly in a hydrated pregelation state, allowing spatial organization prior to scaffold solidification and supporting the emergence of biomimetic tissue architecture.

Several experimental demonstrations underscore the versatility of inkjet bioprinting. Endothelial cells have been printed to form vascular patterns, , hepatocytes have been organized into liver-like arrays, and neural progenitors have been spatially arranged to mimic neural tissue structures. These studies highlight the utility of inkjet systems in engineering biologically active constructs for tissue modeling, regenerative studies, and analytical platforms. Hybrid approaches have also incorporated inkjet modules for delivering cells or biomolecules onto scaffolds fabricated using extrusion or casting, leveraging the complementary strengths of each method.

Together, these sections provide a framework for understanding the technical foundations and translational potential of inkjet bioprinting in modern biofabrication.

Inkjet Bioprinting Process Development

The development of reliable inkjet bioprinting processes requires precise control over fluid dynamics, droplet formation, substrate interaction, and cell-handling conditions, all of which collectively influence printing accuracy, cell viability, and the structural fidelity of printed constructs. Unlike conventional inkjet systems that primarily prioritize resolution and throughput, bioprinting must balance physical precision with biological constraints such as shear sensitivity, nutrient diffusion, and cytocompatibility. This section outlines key technical factors that govern the performance of inkjet-based biofabrication, including the optimization of printability and droplet dynamics, strategies for maintaining biocompatibility, high-resolution patterning techniques, and postprinting stabilization and functional assessment (Figure a).

Optimization of Printability and Droplet Dynamics

Successful jetting in inkjet bioprinting requires bioinks with carefully controlled rheological properties, including viscosity, elasticity, and surface tension, to ensure stable droplet formation and accurate deposition (Figure b). , As discussed in the earlier sections on inkjet fundamentals, stable drop generation is only possible within a relatively narrow range of fluid properties and driving conditions. In bioprinting, this window is further constrained by the presence of cells and macromolecules, which turn the ink into a complex suspension with non-Newtonian and viscoelastic flow characteristics that strongly influence jetting behavior. , Inkjet nozzles used in bioprinting typically range from 20 μm to over 100 μm in diameter, with sub-100 μm orifices commonly used for high-resolution or single-cell applications. ,, These fluid parameters must be precisely tuned to ensure consistent droplet generation, stable ligament breakup, and reproducible deposition (Figure c).

Inks with excessively high viscosity can suppress acoustic or piezoelectric actuation, impeding droplet ejection. In contrast, low-viscosity or highly elastic inks may form elongated ligaments or satellite droplets due to delayed jet breakup, compromising pattern fidelity. These behaviors underscore the need for precise rheological tuning. A recent study demonstrated that ultrasonic treatment can effectively reduce the viscoelasticity of GelMA-based bioinks by shortening the polymer chain length through mechanical energy input, without requiring chemical modification or dilution (Figure d). This adjustment enables the ink to retain a high polymer concentration (up to 10% GelMA) while still achieving stable jetting under drop-on-demand conditions (Figure e). The treated inks exhibited reduced resistance to deformation and improved flow behavior, which minimized the formation of secondary droplets and improved deposition consistency. Importantly, this method preserved cell viability and maintained ink biofunctionality, providing a practical approach to tuning bioink properties while ensuring consistent performance throughout the bioprinting workflow.

Stable droplet formation in piezoelectric inkjet bioprinting requires not only appropriate ink formulation but also precise adjustment of actuation waveform parameters such as rise time, dwell time, fall time, firing frequency, and driving voltage. Experimental investigations have shown that specific combinations of these parameters are critical to avoid satellite droplet formation while ensuring stable primary drop ejection, mapped out via phase diagrams in terms of Weber and Z numbers. When actuation energy is too low, jetting may still produce single drops with high resolution but often leads to inconsistent ejection or dropped failures because of insufficient momentum (Figure c). At the other extreme, overly high actuation energy or improper waveform design can generate long ligaments that fragment into satellite droplets, compromising spatial accuracy and potentially exposing cells to elevated mechanical stress during impact, increasing risk of membrane rupture or viability loss. Bioprinting therefore requires tuning of waveform parameters to produce short, stable ligaments that cleanly break into single primary drops with minimal satellite formation. This compromise balances resolution, reliability, and cell viability within a regime of optimized droplet velocity and deformation control.

Substrate interaction significantly influences droplet trajectory, deposition accuracy, and postimpact cell viability (Figure b). The stand-off distance, defined as the vertical gap between the nozzle and the substrate, affects both the flight path and landing velocity of droplets. Excessive spacing increases trajectory deviation and impact force, elevating the risk of cellular deformation or lysis upon contact. Conversely, a stand-off distance that is too short may lead to nozzle fouling due to bioink backsplash or surface irregularities, compromising jetting stability. Interdroplet spacing is another critical parameter, as it determines local deposition density and influences whether droplets coalesce or remain discrete. If the spacing is too narrow, a newly deposited droplet can displace a previously settled one through surface tension-driven flow or mechanical momentum, resulting in misaligned deposition and distortion of the intended pattern. Such displacement may be especially detrimental in high-density cell printing, where microarchitectural fidelity and localized cell positioning are crucial. These spatial parameters must be carefully balanced in conjunction with substrate wettability, bioink viscosity, and drying kinetics to promote uniform spreading and prevent localized dehydration. Maintaining a hydrated interface during printing is especially crucial for preserving cell viability, as desiccation or osmotic imbalance at the droplet–substrate interface can lead to early loss of cell function. Optimizing these interactions ensures not only pattern fidelity but also supports long-term functionality of the printed tissue construct.

Strategies for Maintaining Biocompatibility

Biocompatibility in inkjet bioprinting depends on strict management of sterility, bioink formulation, and cell-handling conditions. All components in contact with the bioink, such as reservoirs, tubing, and nozzles, must undergo rigorous sterilization using ethanol rinses, UV irradiation, or surface decontamination. Ideally, printing is conducted within a biosafety cabinet. When this is impractical, enclosed systems and routine disinfection protocols can help reduce contamination risks. Effective sterility minimizes microbial growth and the presence of residual endotoxins, which can adversely affect cell viability, particularly in sensitive or primary cell types.

The formulation of bioinks is a critical determinant of cell viability both during printing and throughout subsequent culture. − Parameters such as polymer concentration, osmolarity, nutrient content, and cell density must be tuned not only to meet the physical requirements of jetting but also to preserve cellular homeostasis. The choice of medium should align with the metabolic needs of the target cell type. For example, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) is often used to suppress cell aggregation but lacks essential nutrients and buffering capacity, which may impair viability. In contrast, the inclusion of fetal bovine serum (FBS) improves survival but introduces biological variability and promotes spontaneous aggregation. To address these challenges, serum-free formulations are often used during printing to minimize cell aggregation and prevent nozzle clogging, while postprinting supplementation with amino acids or defined growth factors can help maintain long-term viability and support tissue development.

Cross-linking strategies in inkjet bioprinting must be carefully selected to maintain both structural stability and cell viability. Harsh chemical cross-linkers, such as glutaraldehyde, can improve mechanical strength but often generate cytotoxic byproducts or cause local pH shifts that adversely affect cells. Photopolymerization, a widely used approach in biofabrication, enables rapid gelation and high spatial control through the use of photoresponsive bioinks such as methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) or polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA). Common photoinitiators include Irgacure 2959, which is UV-activated but relatively cytotoxic, and lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP), which is visible light-activated and exhibits higher cytocompatibility. Nonetheless, even with visible light systems, the photoinitiator concentration and exposure dose must be optimized to prevent DNA damage and oxidative stress. In multilayered constructs, light attenuation can cause incomplete cross-linking in deeper regions, leading to structural heterogeneity. To address this, hybrid strategies that combine ionic gelation for initial shape retention with photopolymerization for final stabilization are increasingly employed. In addition to chemical safety, the mechanical properties resulting from the cross-linking process must be tailored to match the requirements of specific cell types. Constructs that are too stiff may restrict cell spreading, migration, or matrix remodeling, while overly soft gels can lead to loss of structural definition and reduced cell polarization. For example, mesenchymal stem cells require stiffer matrices to promote osteogenic differentiation, whereas neuronal or epithelial cells often favor more compliant environments. Therefore, tuning the cross-linking conditions to achieve an optimal balance between mechanical stability and biological permissiveness is essential for successful tissue maturation.

Cell sedimentation within the bioink reservoir during prolonged printing can lead to uneven cell distribution, resulting in spatial heterogeneity within the printed construct. Such heterogeneity can disrupt intended tissue architectures and influence cell–cell signaling, local differentiation patterns, and extracellular matrix deposition. To mitigate sedimentation, several strategies can be employed. These include increasing the ink’s viscosity using biocompatible polymers, incorporating density-matching agents such as Percoll or Ficoll, or utilizing gentle agitation systems like magnetic stirrers or rotational mixers. ,− Density-matching agents, such as Percoll or Ficoll, have been explored in other biomedical contexts to reduce cell sedimentation by minimizing buoyancy differentials. Although promising in principle, their integration into bioink formulations for inkjet bioprinting requires further investigation to assess compatibility, cytocompatibility, and washout efficiency. However, in rapidly gelling environments, residual retention may occur, requiring further optimization depending on the construct type. Care must also be taken to avoid excessive shear stress during agitation, as this can compromise membrane integrity or trigger stress responses. Moreover, the formulation must maintain printability while suspending cells uniformly, which requires balancing rheological properties with cellular demands. Effective control of sedimentation not only ensures homogeneous cell seeding but also contributes to reproducibility and functional reliability of the final tissue constructs.

In addition to immediate cell survival, comprehensive biocompatibility assessment should include indicators of metabolic activity, proliferation, differentiation potential, and inflammatory signaling. Standard viability and metabolic assays such as MTT, WST-1, and resazurin reduction allow quantification of mitochondrial activity and cellular metabolic status. To assess the potential for inflammatory activation, the secretion of cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ can be monitored using ELISA or multiplex bead-based assays, particularly when evaluating novel ink components or cross-linking chemistries. Depending on the intended tissue type, functional evaluation should extend to lineage-specific markers and matrix production. For example, osteogenic applications may require assessment of ALP activity or RUNX2 expression, while chondrogenic constructs benefit from monitoring SOX9, aggrecan, and collagen type II expression. In epithelial or endothelial tissues, tight junction formation (e.g., ZO-1, occludin) and barrier function may be relevant indicators. These functional readouts help determine whether printed constructs can support appropriate cellular phenotypes and tissue-specific functions. Biocompatibility also depends strongly on the cell source. Primary cells such as human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs), neural progenitor cells, or induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived lineages tend to be more sensitive to environmental stressors compared to immortalized lines like HEK293 or NIH 3T3. Parameters such as osmolarity, residual polymers or initiators, and cross-linker reactivity can influence cell adhesion, spreading, differentiation, and extracellular matrix deposition. Therefore, assay design and printing conditions should be tailored to the specific biological needs of the selected cell type.

High-Resolution Patterning and Surface Engineering

High-resolution patterning in inkjet bioprinting depends on the precise coordination of droplet placement, substrate properties, and the behavior of printed biological materials. In this context, inkjet systems use discrete, on-demand droplet ejection to define microscale spatial organization in both two-dimensional and three-dimensional constructs. The fidelity of these patterns is governed by jet stability, fluid–substrate interaction, and interdroplet dynamics, which must be tuned to preserve architectural accuracy while maintaining cellular function.

Substrate surfaces are frequently modified to facilitate selective cell attachment and pattern fidelity. Adhesion-promoting molecules such as poly-l-lysine (PLL), collagen, gelatin, or fibrinogen are commonly used to enhance initial cell anchorage. In contrast, antifouling materials including polyethylene glycol (PEG), Pluronic F-127, or agarose may be applied to define cell-exclusion zones and promote spatial confinement. Some systems employ microfabricated substrates patterned with alternating adhesive and nonadhesive domains to encourage self-organization and precise cell alignment. These surface engineering strategies help establish microenvironments that influence cell adhesion, polarization, and spatial organization within tissue constructs.

Heterotypic cell printing, which involves the sequential or simultaneous deposition of multiple cell types, introduces additional challenges. Variations in adhesion kinetics and sedimentation rates among different cell populations can disrupt intended patterns if not properly managed. To maintain spatial separation and interface stability, it is often necessary to optimize the printing order and allow sufficient settling time between deposition events. This strategy has proven valuable in applications involving tumor–stroma cocultures, epithelial–mesenchymal interfaces, and vascular networks.

Computer-aided design models are typically translated into droplet placement scripts that guide nozzle actuation with micrometer-level accuracy. In two-dimensional applications, spatially resolved droplet deposition defines cellular organization and functional patterning. For three-dimensional constructs, these 2D layers are printed in a layer-by-layer fashion to build volumetric architectures. One key advantage of inkjet bioprinting is its ability to modulate droplet frequency, ink composition, and spacing in real time. This enables the fabrication of spatially graded constructs with localized variations in cell density or molecular signaling. Unlike extrusion-based systems, which operate with continuous flow, inkjet platforms offer discrete control that is uniquely suited for modeling morphogen gradients, stem cell niches, and tissue heterogeneity.

When properly integrated, these high-resolution patterning strategies allow for the fabrication of biomimetic structures with fine spatial control. Such constructs provide an experimental basis for studying cellular behavior in defined microenvironments and contribute to the advancement of functional tissue models in regenerative medicine and systems biology.

Post-Printing Maturation and Functional Evaluation

Postprinting stabilization is a critical phase in inkjet bioprinting workflows, determining whether a printed construct can retain its geometry, support cell viability, and progress toward functional tissue formation. Immediately following deposition, most bioinks remain in a pregelled or semiliquid state. As outlined in section , cross-linking mechanisms such as photopolymerization, ionic gelation, or enzymatic catalysis are commonly employed to convert these into structurally stable architectures. In the context of postprinting maturation, the choice and timing of cross-linking must balance print fidelity and cytocompatibility, because both overly rigid and excessively soft matrices can impair cell behavior, differentiation, and matrix deposition depending on the target tissue type. Together, these parameters determine the structural stability of printed bioconstructs over extended culture periods.

Cross-linking procedures are often followed by washing steps to remove residual initiators, catalysts, or ions that may be cytotoxic over prolonged culture. It is essential to replace the initial printing medium with fresh culture media tailored to the specific cell type and application. In many cases, perfusable bioreactor systems or air–liquid interface cultures are introduced to enhance tissue maturation by promoting oxygen diffusion, nutrient exchange, and mechanical stimulation. These dynamic environments support the development of more physiologically relevant tissue constructs.

Biological evaluation must extend beyond assessments of immediate viability. While standard assays such as calcein-AM staining, ATP quantification, and LDH release provide useful indicators of cytocompatibility, functional validation is required to assess the long-term performance of printed tissues. Techniques such as immunofluorescence staining for lineage-specific markers (e.g., SOX9 for chondrogenesis or ALP for osteogenesis), along with gene and protein expression analysis using qPCR or Western blotting, can reveal differentiation outcomes and matrix remodeling.

Matrix deposition and microstructural integrity can be examined using histological staining methods such as Safranin O or Alcian blue, which highlight glycosaminoglycan and collagen accumulation. In some studies, ELISA or multiplex assays have been used to assess secreted cytokines such as VEGF, IL-6, or TGF-β, offering insight into paracrine signaling and immunomodulatory potential of bioprinted constructs. Tissue-specific functions may be assessed through contractility measurements for muscle tissues, electrophysiological recordings for neuronal networks, or mechanical testing such as compression modulus analysis for cartilage-like structures. These experimental end points enable a rigorous assessment of both structural integrity and biological functionality, establishing a comprehensive framework for evaluating the maturation of inkjet-printed tissues. This framework also provides a quantitative basis for assessing the long-term stability and reproducibility of inkjet-printed constructs over time.

As bioprinting strategies continue to evolve, refinement of postprinting protocols will be critical to achieving robust and functional tissue models. The integration of advanced culture systems, improved material formulations, and standardized evaluation methods will contribute to the successful translation of inkjet bioprinting technologies into preclinical and clinical settings.

Inkjet Bioprinting Applications

Inkjet Printing of Biomolecules

Inkjet bioprinting was first introduced into biomedical research through the patterning of noncellular biomolecules, including proteins, biopolymers, and nucleic acids, to create chemically defined surfaces for cell culture and molecular analysis. In early implementations, cell-adhesive or repellent molecules were inkjet-printed onto substrates, followed by manual cell seeding to generate patterned cultures. − Adhesive domains were commonly formed using materials such as collagen, gelatin, hyaluronic acid (HA), poly-d-lysine (PDL), poly-l-lysine (PLL), and poly­(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), while repellent areas employed poly­(ethylene glycol) (PEG), agarose, or alginate to restrict nonspecific cell adhesion (Figure a–d).

As the technology evolved, inkjet bioprinting enabled the high-resolution deposition of functional biomolecules including growth factors, morphogens, and nucleic acids, allowing spatial control over cell fate decisions. − Morphogens such as bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) and insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II) have been patterned using IJP to direct lineage-specific differentiation in spatially defined gradients. Neural patterning studies have demonstrated that inkjet-printed laminin-containing domains (e.g., laminin embedded in copolymers) can support neuronal adhesion and guidance, enabling patterned cell growth and network formation. In addition to protein patterning, DNA oligonucleotides have been directly synthesized and printed using inkjet platforms to generate high-density microarrays for gene expression profiling.

IJP enables the precise fabrication of microscale capsules and vesicles for applications in encapsulation, drug delivery, and engineered microenvironments. Alginate-based microcapsules have been produced by ejecting droplets into calcium chloride baths, with process parameters controlling size and sphericity. To enhance jetting stability and reproducibility, bipolar waveform modulation and composite precursor optimization have been introduced. , Reactive inkjet methods further allow porogen generation within hydrogels, enabling tunable pore formation in bulk matrices. Beyond hydrogels, piezoelectric IJP has been used to create polymer microparticles with defined geometries and controlled drug content (Figure e), while unilamellar lipid vesicles formed via inkjet serve as minimal cell models. These approaches demonstrate the versatility of inkjet systems in producing soft matter structures with spatial and functional control. The ability to digitally control droplet volume, spatial placement, and material combinations makes IJP particularly well suited for biofunctional surface engineering, spatial encoding, and multiplexed biochemical patterning in both two-dimensional and layered formats.

Inkjet Printing of Living Cells

Inkjet bioprinting has transformed the ability to recreate complex cellular environments by enabling the precise and programmable placement of living cells with micrometer-scale resolution. Among bioprinting techniques, inkjet-based methods stand out for their accessibility, noncontact nature, and adaptability to digital workflows. Unlike conventional cell patterning techniques such as photolithography, soft lithography, and microfluidic confinement, which require specialized equipment and considerable expertise in surface engineering, inkjet bioprinting offers a more flexible and scalable approach for both two-dimensional and three-dimensional cellular patterning.

To establish reliable cell deposition, initial efforts focused on optimizing the physical parameters that influence stable droplet ejection and minimizing cell damage during printing. Studies demonstrated that viable mammalian cells could be reproducibly printed using drop-on-demand systems while maintaining high postprinting viability. Further investigation into the droplet formation process revealed critical effects of viscosity, surface tension, and cell concentration on droplet stability. Thermal IJP was also evaluated using CHO cells, not only assessing viability but also demonstrating successful intracellular delivery of DNA plasmids and subsequent gene expression. To further enhance cell survivability and reduce clogging, modifications to ink formulation and system mechanics have been explored. For instance, incorporating surfactants and gentle agitation in the cell reservoir improved cell suspension uniformity and reduced sedimentation effects. An alternative pneumatic conveying mechanism was also developed, decoupling the driving force from nozzle ejection to prevent acoustic or thermal stress while maintaining high spatial fidelity.

Advancing toward cell-level resolution, multiple studies have aimed to control the number of cells per droplet to enable single-cell printing. A representative effort used a 30 μm diameter piezoelectric nozzle to achieve cell-level accuracy, systematically adjusting cell density to yield one cell per droplet on average. High-speed imaging confirmed the formation of stable droplets containing zero, one, or two cells based on the Poisson distribution, and printed cells exhibited over 90% viability (Figure a–d). Similarly, one study demonstrated single-cell ejection by positioning cells above the orifice and exploiting the velocity gradient near the nozzle to isolate individual cells during droplet formation. Another effort achieved deterministic single-cell deposition through designed seeding techniques and real-time position control. The strategy was further refined to increase single-cell success rate from 18% to over 60% by reducing cell concentration and adjusting ejection timing.

This approach underscores the potential of inkjet bioprinting as a digitally programmable platform for encoding spatial cellular information and engineering complex tissue architectures.

All-IJP of 3D Tissue Constructs

Inkjet bioprinting has gained increasing attention for its capacity to fabricate three-dimensional (3D) tissue constructs through the precise deposition of cell-laden hydrogels and bioactive molecules. As a drop-on-demand technology, it enables microscale patterning with minimal shear and thermal stress, which is particularly advantageous for preserving the viability and phenotype of sensitive cell types. These capabilities support the recapitulation of key structural and functional features of native tissue microenvironments. Recent research has focused on refining bioink formulations, optimizing droplet resolution and stacking fidelity, and developing fully inkjet-based platforms that support translational applications in tissue engineering, disease modeling, and pharmacological testing.

Early demonstrations of inkjet-based 3D structure fabrication explored the feasibility of constructing scaffold architectures without the incorporation of living cells. One representative study developed a zigzag tubular structure composed solely of hydrogel droplets by controlling deposition path and droplet stacking (Figure a,b). This system showed that stable and self-supporting geometries could be formed using inkjet-printed bioinks, highlighting the potential of the method for scaffold-free tissue engineering. Another approach utilized multinozzle inkjet systems to build bifurcated 3D channels via dropwise deposition of cell-free bioinks along digitally programmed trajectories (Figure c). The resulting constructs maintained their geometry after cross-linking, providing a foundation for vascular-like or branching tissue frameworks. A complementary strategy integrated a heating plate into a custom-developed 3D bioprinter to solidify gelatin-based bioinks during deposition, enabling the construction of supportive structures without external molding (Figure d–f). Additionally, high-fidelity constructs were fabricated using digitally designed image data to guide precise droplet deposition and curing. By adjusting parameters such as substrate temperature, droplet volume, and curing timing, this method achieved improved vertical resolution and layer alignment of printed features (Figure g). These studies established that inkjet systems can reproducibly create architecturally defined scaffolds through layer-by-layer accumulation of acellular materials.

Inkjet-based strategies have also been employed to fabricate fully three-dimensional constructs containing living cells. In contrast to support bath-based or extrusion-dominated approaches, all-inkjet bioprinting allows direct deposition of cell-laden droplets with spatial and compositional resolution at the microscale. This capability has been used to develop structural frameworks, vascular networks, and tissue-specific arrangements in a layer-by-layer fashion. One early demonstration employed sodium alginate-based bioinks loaded with fibroblasts to construct a zigzag tubular structure through repeated deposition and cross-linking on a customized 3D motion stage. The resulting scaffold-free tubes maintained their geometry postprinting and supported high cell viability for up to 7 days, indicating the feasibility of inkjet systems in freeform cell-laden structure fabrication. Another study used PEG-based hydrogels to print parallel microchannels embedded with mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), achieving stable stacking of multiple layers and guiding region-specific chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation. This strategy demonstrated that fully inkjet-printed constructs can support lineage-specific cues within a single integrated platform.

More complex multicellular architectures were also achieved by precise spatial modulation of droplet placement and ink formulation. In a representative example, differentially labeled cell types were patterned into voxel-like units to construct heterogeneous tissues with compartmentalized morphology. This approach enabled simultaneous control of cellular identity, position, and local hydrogel stiffness within each printed voxel, offering new pathways for heterotypic tissue modeling (Figure h,i). These examples collectively demonstrate that fully inkjet-based fabrication of cell-laden 3D constructs is achievable without external mechanical compression or physical templates, relying instead on precise control of droplet generation and deposition kinetics. The ability to integrate multiple cell types and programmable structural elements in a scaffold-free or hybrid fashion expands the functional scope of inkjet bioprinting in tissue engineering applications.

The ability of inkjet bioprinting to spatially coordinate multiple cell types and matrix components has enabled the fabrication of tissue-mimetic constructs that replicate organ-specific architecture and function. A representative effort by Kang et al. demonstrated the construction of a 3D alveolar-capillary barrier using an all-inkjet approach, employing sequential deposition of epithelial, fibroblast, and endothelial cells along with pulmonary surfactant components (Figure j). This trilayered model reproduced native alveolar geometry with a total thickness of approximately 10 μm and exhibited physiological barrier functions, including tight junction formation, selective permeability, and responsiveness to viral stimuli (Figure k). Upon exposure to influenza A virus, the model showed hallmark immune responses such as epithelial disintegration, elevated secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α, and compromised barrier integrity. Building on this platform, Lee et al. developed a fibrotic lung model by introducing TGF-β1 postprinting to induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition. The printed tissues displayed elevated fibronectin and collagen deposition, decreased transepithelial resistance, and transcriptional reprogramming consistent with fibrotic remodeling. This model was further applied to investigate pulmonary toxicity induced by PM2.5, where the same stratified construct was exposed to fine particulate matter under air–liquid interface culture. The exposure led to reactive oxygen species generation, junctional disruption, and upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β and MCP-1. These features enabled both antifibrotic drug evaluation, demonstrating dose-dependent rescue by pirfenidone and nintedanib, and toxicological assessment in a species-independent format, underscoring the versatility of the all-inkjet-printed alveolar tissue platform for disease modeling and environmental hazard screening.

These studies collectively demonstrate that inkjet bioprinting supports the fabrication of complex, physiologically responsive tissue constructs through digital spatial programming of cellular and biochemical components. The capacity to recapitulate native tissue interfaces and disease-relevant responses in a fully inkjet-printed format provides a versatile platform for both mechanistic studies and therapeutic screening.

Hybrid Inkjet Bioprinting Approaches

Hybrid bioprinting systems that combine IJP with extrusion, spray, or other fabrication modalities have emerged to overcome the inherent limitations of resolution, throughput, and mechanical stability associated with single-mode platforms. While IJP offers exceptional control over microscale deposition with minimal mechanical stress, its low volumetric throughput and limited stacking capacity can restrict its standalone utility in fabricating large or mechanically robust tissues. Hybrid strategies address these trade-offs by integrating complementary techniques that provide bulk support and structural integrity, while simultaneously preserving the high spatial precision and gentle handling advantages of inkjet-based deposition. By doing so, they enable the fabrication of complex, multilayered tissue constructs with enhanced architectural fidelity and biological functionality.

One representative example is the inkjet–spray hybrid printing strategy, designed to overcome the limitations of deposition rate and scale in inkjet-only systems. In this platform, IJP is used to deposit cells and biomolecules with high precision, while spray coating facilitates uniform stacking of the hydrogel matrix over broader areas (Figure a,b). This configuration shortens printing time while preserving fine lateral resolution in cell placement. Importantly, the spray-assisted matrix deposition promotes vertical integration of layered hydrogels without compromising cell viability or structural definition (Figure c). Yoon et al. demonstrated that this method could fabricate freeform, multilayered hydrogel architectures by combining the spatial control of inkjet deposition with the volumetric efficiency of spray printing.

In human skin model engineering, hybrid platforms have been employed to reconcile the conflicting requirements of bulk dermal stability and fine epidermal organization. Lee et al. integrated extrusion printing of fibroblast-laden collagen, a matrix prone to contraction and deformation during culture, with an inkjet module for keratinocyte delivery (Figure d). The low-shear inkjet process preserved the delicate architecture of the collagen layer while enabling uniform distribution of epidermal cells. The result was a functional skin equivalent that exhibited stratified epidermal structure and minimized contraction under air–liquid interface culture conditions.

Kim et al. further advanced this concept by developing a direct hybrid bioprinting process using pneumatic extrusion and IJP for the one-step fabrication of human skin constructs (Figure e). The extrusion module produced an extruded polycaprolactone (PCL) scaffold to reinforce the dermal matrix, while the inkjet system delivered keratinocytes with digitally controlled precision (Figure f). This dual-modality approach enabled biologically relevant layering and consistent construct fidelity, supporting stable epidermal differentiation and long-term culture viability over 14 days.

These studies exemplify the strategic role of IJP within hybrid workflows, providing noncontact, low-force, and digitally programmable cell deposition that complements the structural and mechanical capabilities of extrusion or spray-based systems. By enabling precise spatial control over cellular distribution atop robust matrices, hybrid inkjet bioprinting platforms offer a versatile strategy for constructing complex, multicellular tissues with both architectural precision and functional maturity.

Therapeutic and Preclinical Applications of Inkjet Bioprinting

Inkjet bioprinting has emerged as a valuable tool for therapeutic and preclinical applications by enabling the spatially controlled deposition of living cells and biomaterials. Beyond its utility in fabricating tissue models for in vitro screening, IJP has been leveraged to generate functional grafts, investigate disease heterogeneity, and support point-of-care interventions. This section highlights representative studies demonstrating how inkjet bioprinting can contribute to precision medicine and regenerative therapies.

To investigate clonal heterogeneity within solid tumors, Yoon et al. employed piezoelectric drop-on-demand IJP to isolate and pattern single bladder cancer cells derived from patient-derived tumor organoids, enabling subsequent culture and analysis of clonal organoid outgrowths (Figure a,b). This approach allowed quantitative assessment of intratumoral heterogeneity by tracking morphological variation, proliferative capacity, and cisplatin sensitivity among organoids derived from individual printed cells. Unlike bulk tumor models, the platform provided a high-resolution means to examine functional diversity within tumors, while preserving cell viability and minimizing mechanical stress during dispensing. The use of inkjet-based spatial indexing further enabled linkage between clone-specific phenotypes and molecular profiles. This study highlights IJP as a tool not only for precision deposition, but for generating standardized, scalable single-cell resolution assays applicable to preclinical drug screening and tumor biology.

A representative demonstration of inkjet bioprinting for preclinical application was presented by Matsusaki et al., who developed an automated platform for fabricating three-dimensional human tissue chips aimed at drug testing and toxicology screening. Using a modified thermal inkjet printer, the team precisely deposited droplets containing hepatocytes (HepG2), endothelial cells (HUVEC), and fibroblasts (NHDF) onto a gelatin-based hydrogel in a layer-by-layer fashion, forming spatially organized multilayered constructs within 2.5 h (Figure c,d). The resulting tissue chips maintained liver-specific functions, including stable albumin secretion and cytochrome P450 activity for at least 1 week, indicating their potential utility as reproducible, human-relevant platforms for in vitro drug metabolism and toxicity evaluation. This work exemplifies how inkjet bioprinting can support the rapid, scalable generation of functional tissue models tailored for therapeutic screening purposes.

The therapeutic potential of inkjet bioprinting for skin regeneration was demonstrated by Yanez et al., who evaluated the in vivo performance of bilayer skin grafts incorporating printed microvasculature. Using a commercial inkjet system, endothelial and fibroblast cells were printed into alginate-based hydrogel layers to construct vascularized skin grafts. The bilayer structure, mimicking dermis and epidermis, was fabricated by sequentially printing fibroblasts within a collagen–alginate matrix followed by endothelial cells to generate capillary-like channels (Figure e). Upon implantation in a full-thickness wound model in immunodeficient mice, the constructs facilitated rapid vascular integration, enhanced tissue remodeling, and supported re-epithelialization (Figure f,g). This study illustrates how inkjet-based cellular patterning can be leveraged to fabricate prevascularized tissue constructs with translational relevance, aligning with the goals of preclinical skin regeneration and demonstrating the feasibility of using inkjet bioprinting for functional therapeutic grafts.

As an example of direct clinical translation, Albanna et al. developed a mobile in situ bioprinting system integrated with a 3D laser scanner that captured wound geometry in real time and guided the deposition of autologous skin cells onto full-thickness excisional wounds (Figure h). This integrated system enabled precise reconstruction of the wound architecture by printing a bilayered skin construct composed of dermal fibroblasts and epidermal keratinocytes directly onto the injury site (Figure i). Treated wounds exhibited accelerated re-epithelialization and reduced contraction compared to untreated or matrix-only controls, highlighting the therapeutic potential of portable bioprinting systems for point-of-care wound management. By enabling personalized, on-demand cell deposition directly at the wound site, this study exemplifies how inkjet bioprinting can support rapid therapeutic intervention and functional tissue restoration in acute clinical scenarios.

Together, these studies underscore the growing relevance of inkjet bioprinting across the therapeutic development pipeline, from mechanistic studies and drug screening to regenerative tissue engineering and bedside implementation. The adaptability of inkjet platforms to dispense living cells with spatial precision has enabled their use in both high-resolution assays and clinically relevant constructs, demonstrating the breadth of their application in preclinical and therapeutic contexts.

Future Research Directions and Challenges for Manufacturing

IJP and DIW have evolved well beyond their initial role in simple patterning, now enabling the fabrication of highly functional micro- and nanostructures applicable across diverse domains, including flexible electronics, wearable sensors, tissue engineering, biomedical devices, energy storage, and catalysis. The convergence of advances in ink chemistry, precision fluid mechanics, machine learning-enabled process control, and integration with smart robotics is forecast to propel jet printing and DIW into mainstream device manufacturing. Realization of fully functional, cost-effective, and reliable nano-to-microstructured devices will require interdisciplinary efforts, including new theoretical frameworks, computational design tools, and cross-sector partnerships. Below are suggested emerging research directions and challenges remain to be addressed for industrialization of the printing technologies.

Emerging Research Directions

IJP and DIW have steadily advanced from simple patterning to enabling highly functional micro- and nanostructures relevant for diverse fields, including flexible electronics, flexible sensors, tissue engineering, biological applications, energy storage, and catalysis. Yet, several future research avenues deserve particular focus:

Formulation of Functional Inks and Processing Strategies for Flexible Electronics

Despite substantial advances in the development of electronic materials with exceptional intrinsic properties, their translation into high-performance printable inks has progressed slowly. Converting such materials into inks capable of reliably producing nano- and micropatterns requires not only systematic formulation strategies but also considerable domain expertise. Some materials are urgently needed for immediate applications. For instance, while p-type semiconductors such as DPP derivatives exhibit reasonable charge mobilities, there is still a lack of high-mobility n-type inks. Printable dielectric materials frequently suffer from excessive leakage currents or poor interfacial compatibility, underscoring the need for balanced complementary systems and mechanically robust dielectric formulations. At the processing level, IJP continues to face limitations in resolution, alignment accuracy, and reproducibility. Factors such as droplet spreading, inadequate substrate wetting, and layer misregistration hinder multilayer integration and large-scale device miniaturization. Emerging strategies, including surface energy patterning, self-aligned printing, and hybrid lithography–printing techniques, are likely to be pivotal for enhancing patterning precision and device yield. Furthermore, device stability and performance uniformity remain pressing concerns. Phenomena such as threshold voltage drift, bias stress effects, and device-to-device variability compromise the longevity and reliability of printed electronics. Addressing these issues will require advances in encapsulation technologies, interfacial engineering, and circuit architectures specifically designed to tolerate process-induced variations.

Fundamental Ink Chemistry and Material Science

To enable the transition from laboratory-scale demonstrations to large-scalable manufacturing of IJP and DIW for functional nano- to microscale structures, several foundational issues about ink chemistry and material science must be systematically addressed. First, ligand and precursor design plays an important role in particle-based inks. Ligands must simultaneously ensure long-term colloidal stability during storage and transport, while undergoing clean decomposition after deposition, thereby minimizing residual organic species that compromise electrical conductivity and carrier mobility. Second, rheological control must also be reconsidered from a chemical perspective, extending beyond macroscopic viscosity tuning. Molecular weight distribution, interparticle interactions, and transient network formation govern extensional viscosity and viscoelastic relaxation of inks, which in turn affect jet breakup, satellite droplet formation in IJP and filament stability in DIW. Precise molecular-level engineering of these parameters is required to expand the printable process window without sacrificing resolution or throughput. In addition, future research should also focus on surface chemistry and interfacial engineering, specifically how ink-substrate interactions evolve during the nonequilibrium drying process, which ultimately influences the adhesion strength, stress distribution, and mechanical durability of the printed features.

Fabrication of Multicomponent and Hybrid Structures

The development of printing strategies capable of combining different material classes, such as ceramics, metals, polymers, biomaterials, and composite inks, will be crucial for next-generation multifunctional devices. For example, a recent study demonstrated an anticounterfeiting device based on a hybrid ink composed of MXene and cellulose nanofiber materials, which exhibited multi-stimulus responsiveness and fast degradation. Achieving precise spatial control at the nanoscale to microscales, while maintaining material purity and interfacial integrity, is an ongoing challenge. Integration of stimuli-responsive or “smart” materials into printed architectures will open new possibilities in adaptive devices.

Ink Design for Achieving Both Sustainability and High Performance

Future work will focus not only on optimizing ink rheology and colloidal stability but also on formulating green, biocompatible, and recyclable inks. This includes the use of bioinspired solvents, nanocomposite additives for enhanced conductivity or mechanical strength, and self-healing or self-assembling ink systems. Sustainable sourcing and minimal environmental impact will become critical for both research and commercial adoption.

Hierarchical and 3D Architectures

There is expanding interest in utilizing jet printing/DIW to build hierarchical structures spanning multiple length scales, integrating nanoscale features with micrometer-scale supports. Multilayer, multimaterial 3D printing approaches will enable enhanced mechanical, thermal, and functional properties. , Efforts to print structures with controlled porosity, anisotropy, or gradient compositions will address the challenges in tissue engineering, energy devices, and photonic applications.

In Situ Monitoring and Process Control with Artificial Intelligence

Integrating real-time monitoring, using optical, electrical, and thermal sensors, directly into the printing workflow will greatly advance quality control and reproducibility. Closed-loop feedback systems utilizing machine learning can dynamically adjust printing parameters, enabling adaptive fabrication and defect minimization. Recent literature has demonstrated the computer vision combined with neural networks were successfully employed to predict the defects in 3D printing. In-situ characterization of curing, evaporation, and particle assembly is critical for structure–function relationship studies.

Automated Robotic Printing

The printed features can be poised for synergy with advanced robotics and automation, wherein robots can manipulate or deposit materials with high precision. Such robotic-assisted printing, potentially guided by artificial intelligence algorithms, may enable rapid prototyping, repair, and the direct manufacturing of complex, miniaturized devices even in constrained or remote environments. Some studies have reported the possibility of bioprinting based on the vision-guided robotic hand. ,

Key Challenges in Jet Printing and DIW for Device Manufacturing and Industrialization

While significant academic advances have been achieved, several key technological bottlenecks are remained in the jet printing and DIW for translating into practical or industrial-scale applications.

Reproducibility and Process Scalability

Achieving batch-to-batch reproducibility in both ink preparation and printed device performance is a formidable challenge. Scaling up from laboratory demonstrations to industrial production systems demands standardization of printing protocols, robust automation solutions, and quality-assurance best practices. High-throughput, large-area printing technologies, without loss of resolution, are needed to meet industrial demand.

Resolution, Fidelity, and Registration

As feature sizes approach the submicrometer scale, issues related to droplet/line placement accuracy, substrate wetting, and unintended ink spreading become pronounced. Advanced jetting and trajectory control, including new nozzle designs and precise stage movements, will be necessary to push resolution limits and minimize defects. Layer-to-layer registration and alignment, especially for multilayer and heterostructured devices, must be improved.

Integration with Conventional Manufacturing

To facilitate commercialization, jet printed and DIW structures must be compatible with existing manufacturing lines and device architectures. The manufacturing includes postprocessing steps such as sintering, curing, and surface treatments, as well as electrical/mechanical assembly. Hybrid approaches that combine additive printing with subtractive microfabrication (e.g., lithography, etching, photopolymerization) are often necessary for constructing complex device architectures that are difficult to realize using a single technique alone. For instance, integrating a near-infrared (NIR) laser-assisted curing module with DIW has been shown to enable scalable in situ photopolymerization and multiscale 3D printing, thereby overcoming key limitations of conventional DIW in terms of feature resolution and build rate.

Long-Term Stability and Reliability

Printed devices often face long-term stability issues, including ink phase separation, interfacial degradation, mechanical fatigue, and exposure-driven property changes. Rigorous aging studies under relevant operational conditions, thermal, chemical, mechanical, are essential to predict device lifetimes and inform ink and substrate design.

Cost, Environmental, and Regulatory Considerations

For industrial uptake, reduction of material cost, energy consumption, and waste generation must be prioritized. Regulatory compliance related to safety, biocompatibility, and recycling/reuse of components, especially for medical or environmental applications, should be integrally considered in research and development undertakings.

Standardization and Benchmarking

The absence of standardized protocols and benchmarking for print quality, device metrics, and testing methodology continues to impede comparative studies and industrial translation. The community should strive to establish best practices and openly shared data sets and testing resources.
