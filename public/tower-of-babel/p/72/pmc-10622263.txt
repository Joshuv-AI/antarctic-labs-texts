The Influence of Compensatory Pelvic Tilt on Patient‐reported Outcome Measurements in FAI Patients Who Received Arthroscopic Treatment
Authors: Mingke You, Weijia Wang, Kai Zhou, Jian Li, Gang Chen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePelvic compensation is common in femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) patients to reduce symptoms and increase range of motion. However, little attention was given to the postoperative clinical effect of pelvic compensation. Therefore, this study aims to compare the outcomes between pelvic compensation and normal pelvic position in femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) patients after hip arthroscopy.MethodsThe retrospective study was conducted from January 2019 to June 2022, recruited consecutive patients who were diagnosed with FAI and received subsequent arthroscopic treatment. All patients completed an X‐ray in the anterior–posterior standing position before and 4 weeks after surgery. Patients with pelvic compensation were compared with those who did not. Functional outcomes included hip disability and osteoarthritis outcome score (HOOS), modified Harris (mHHS) score, and lower‐extremity activity scale (LEAS). Secondary outcomes included the EuroQol Five Dimensions Questionnaire (EQ‐5D) and patient satisfaction. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to analyze interobserver and intraobserver reliability.ResultsNinety patients with a mean age of 39.40 years were included in the study. No significant compensation changes were noted within groups after the elimination of impingement. The functional scores showed no significant difference between groups (p(HOOS) = 0.352, p(mHHS) = 0.183, p(LEAS) = 0.865). The EQ‐5D revealed statistically better performance in usual activities in the compensatory group (p = 0.044). There are no significant between‐group differences in patients' satisfaction evaluations.ConclusionAs assessed by Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), patients with compensatory pelvic tilt demonstrated similar clinical outcomes without extra adverse events to patients with normal pelvic positioning in short‐term follow‐ups. Furthermore, compensatory pelvic tilt did not significantly enhance the range of motion or functional outcome at short‐term follow‐ups.Keywords: Femoroacetabular impingement, Hip arthroscopy, Pelvic tilt, Pubic symphysis to sacrococcygeal distance, Quality of life

Introduction

Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is described as a pathological process that leads to repetitive collisions caused by a morphological abnormality of the acetabulum and/or femur and vigorous hip range of motion (ROM), which causes pain and damages the structure of soft tissue within the hip joint. 1 , 2 The most common symptom of FAI is pain in the anterior or the anterolateral hip, which are referred to as the groin area, occasionally to the anterior thigh. 3 Limited ROM in hip flexion and/or internal rotation with difficulty in long‐time sitting and squatting 4 , 5 as well as mechanical symptoms, such as snapping, have also been reported. 5 Regarding these dysfunctions, researchers discovered that FAI patients exhibit a significant posterior pelvic tilt for compensation to reduce pain, increase hip ROM, and enrich patients' ability of daily living (ADL). 6 , 7

The diagnosis of FAI is based on physical examination and radiographic evaluation. 8 X‐ray is commonly used for radiographic evaluation, and an anterior–posterior (AP) view of the pelvis is suggested for impingement detection, which provides substantial information on the morphology of the hip joint. 9 Tannast et al. suggested using the pubic symphysis to sacrococcygeal distance (PSSC) as an indicator of pelvic tilting after comparison with six other indices and found that PSSC showed a moderately strong correlation coefficient in evaluation. 10 This index is also accepted and applied in other studies. 11

A recent study reported a decrease in pelvic tilt observed on the standing anterior–posterior view of X‐ray, which corresponded to a lower incidence and severity of pincer impingement. 12 Bagwell et al. also suggested that the decrease in posterior pelvic tilt contributed to impingement by the femoral head–neck junction with the acetabulum and may be diminished by hip extensor muscle activities. 13 Therefore, decreased pelvic tilt serves as a compensatory mechanism and may relieve impingement‐related symptoms in FAI patients. A previous study showed that compensated patients with pelvic posterior tilt had a significantly higher reduction in moderate pain after conservative treatment. 14 However, no research has investigated the influence of compensatory pelvic tilt on functional outcome in patients who received arthroscopic treatment.

The primary objective of this study was to compare the clinical outcomes and quality of life of significant pelvic compensation and normal pelvic position based on PSSC in patients with FAI who underwent hip arthroscopy. The second objective was to analyze patients' satisfaction and feedback, and discover potential subsequent therapeutic strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study included patients from February 2015 to June 2022 and was approved by the West China Hospital of Sichuan University Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (number 399 in 2022) on February 25, 2022. The study was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200060704) on June 8, 2022. All patients provided informed consent.

Eligibility of Studies

The inclusion criteria of this study were as follows: (i) patients were diagnosed with FAI by clinical (pain and/or limited flexion‐internal rotation and adduction) and radiographic (alpha angle >55° and center‐edge angle (CEA) > 40° in MRI) evaluation 15 ; (ii) patients had X‐rays obtained in the anterior–posterior standing view; and (iii) patients signed informed consent before participation.

The exclusion criteria of this study were as follows: (i) patients had a history of hip surgery or any history of hip disorders; (ii) patients were unwilling to complete radiographic examinations; and (iii) patients received conservative treatment instead of hip arthroscopy.

Radiographic Examination

The postoperative radiographic evaluation was performed by radiological technicians specializing in musculoskeletal imaging. Patients were asked to take X‐rays in the anterior–posterior view in a standing position, instructed to stand upright with their feet pointing straight ahead, weight distributed equally between both feet, and hands on their hips. Standardizing factors like beam angle, distance from the patients and machine settings were pre‐set, and patient positioning, exposure, and overall image quality were adjusted by technicians to minimize measuring errors.

Assessment of Pelvic Tilt

All postoperative radiographic outcomes were measured after the confirmation of the patients' information. Each measurement was performed by technicians specializing in musculoskeletal imaging who had more than 5 years of working experience. All technicians were trained and standardized the measurement criteria before the study. Measurements were performed before surgery and 4 weeks after surgery. The technicians were blinded to the groupings and patients' identifiable information. One observer was trained to perform the radiographic measurement, each measurement was taken twice. There was an interval of 1 months between two measurements. The average of two‐time results was calculated. The reliability was analyzed by intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Pelvic compensation was defined as changes and adaptations in morphology to achieve a new sagittal mechanical balance with an increase in the posterior tilt angle to influence hip function. 16 The PSSC indirectly reflects the pelvic tilt in the sagittal plane. The PSSC is calculated by creating a line at the upper border of the pubic symphysis and a parallel line at the sacrococcygeal junction and then measuring the vertical distance between these two lines (Figure 1). Based on previous research, the normal PSSC in the general population ranges from 31 to 52 mm in females (mean, 42 mm) and 2 to 31 mm in males (mean, 22 mm). 17

Functional Outcomes

Patients in both groups were required to complete evaluations. Patient‐reported outcome measurements (PROMs) were assessed using the hip disability and osteoarthritis outcome score (HOOS), modified Harris (mHHS) score, and lower‐extremity activity scale (LEAS). The HOOS scale consists of five subscales: pain, other symptoms, function in activities of daily living (ADL) and function in sport and recreation (Sport/Rec). Each subscale consists of questions scored from 0 to 4, and the total score is transformed to a 0 to 100 scale (0 indicates extreme problem and 100 indicates no problem). 18 The mHHS scale is used to assess pain and function with a total of 91 points (0 indicates extreme problem and 91 indicates no problem). 19 The LEAS scale is used as a self‐reported measurement of activity and consists of an 18‐level scale in which patients choose their best representative activity level (1 indicates entirely bedbound and 18 indicates a currently competitive athletic level). 20

Secondary outcomes included the EuroQol Five Dimensions Questionnaire (EQ‐5D) and patient satisfaction. The EQ‐5D is used to define health based on mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression. 21 Each term is determined by five levels, and the best health state is recorded as “11111.” Patient satisfaction was assessed based on self‐evaluated health condition, satisfaction with surgery, functional improvement and self‐satisfaction. Here, health condition, satisfaction with surgery and functional improvement were assessed using a 0 to 100 scale, with 0 representing totally dissatisfied and 100 representing completely satisfied. A self‐satisfaction score of 1 represented satisfied, whereas 0 represented dissatisfied.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 21.0 (SPSS 21.0). Baseline information was analyzed using descriptive statistics. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables, and percentages and frequencies were calculated for dichotomous variables. Mean group differences and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported and were generated using independent t tests. For data lacking a normal distribution, a Wilcoxon test was used for comparisons between groups. The ICC was used to analyze interobserver and intraobserver correlation. Graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4.1). A 2‐sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 97 symptomatic hips in 90 patients were included with a mean age of 39.40 ± 9.51 years, height of 165.43 ± 8.52 cm, weight of 63.77 ± 14.20 kg, and BMI of 22.83 ± 2.67 kg/m2. Among all FAI patients, 44.44% were affected on the left side, 45.56% were affected on the right side, and 7.78% were affected on both sides. Interobserver and intraobserver correlation for radiographic assessment

The ICC used to analyze inter‐observer and intraobserver correlation showed moderate to perfect agreement of the radiographic measurement (ICC = 0.858, p ≤ 0.0001).

Discussion

Main Findings

The clinical results of compensatory pelvic tilt patients with FAI who underwent surgery showed the same clinical outcomes, quality of life assessment and patient satisfaction after surgery as patients with a normally positioned pelvis. The compensatory group exhibited a significantly higher ability of usual activities and the potential for higher function performance postoperatively compared with the normal position group.

Grouping Feasibility

Our study used the PSSC distance measurement for the grouping process (normal males, range from 2 to 31 mm, mean of 22 mm; females, range from 31 to 52 mm, mean of 42 mm). This index has also been accepted and applied as an accurate measure by many studies. 10 , 11 , 16 , 22 Tannast et al. reported that PSSC showed a very strong correlation with pelvic tilt (r = 0.68, p < 0.001). 10 Pullen et al.'s research also reported a normal PSSC of 44.9 mm in females and 20.6 mm in males, 11 which was similar to the criteria applied in this study. Research comparing postoperative outcomes between FAI patients with or without pelvic tilt compensation is not available; therefore, the results of this study provide evidence on this topic.

Quality of Life Assessment

Patients in this study reported significant changes in their usual activities following surgery, as demonstrated by their EQ‐5D scores. This health‐related quality of life scale was also applied in recent studies to assess improvements. 23 , 24 , 25 Patients who reported being satisfied postoperatively showed significant changes in postoperative EQ‐5D usual activities and higher means in each EQ‐5D term and the EQ‐5D‐VAS assessment compared to unsatisfied patients. The results illustrated that patients' health conditions were directly related to their functional status and satisfaction with surgery, representing an evidence‐based suggestion for assessments of staged postoperative rehabilitation.

Satisfaction Analysis

In the present retrospective study, although no significant difference was discovered, the mean score in patients' satisfaction evaluation in the compensatory group was lower than that in the normal position group, which may be related to patients' higher expectations predicting greater improvement after hip arthroscopy. Jain et al. conducted a study to analyze the influence of patients' expectations on their self‐reported outcomes after total hip arthroplasty. The researchers concluded that the worse patients' preoperative conditions were, the greater their preoperative expectations and their improvements after surgery. 26 This finding indicated that the compensatory group performed better preoperatively, and the improvement in this group may be lower than that in the normal position group, which may lead to dissatisfaction in their self‐reported assessment.

Future Research Determination

The results in this study suggested that compensatory pelvic tilt changes may be beneficial postoperatively and may serve as indications for conservative treatment and preoperative training. Barbosa and his colleagues used manipulation and quadriceps eccentric and hamstring concentric contraction exercises to decrease pelvic tilt in patients with low‐back pain and found that an 8‐week therapy was effective for decreasing anterior pelvic tilt and reducing pain in daily activities. 27 Cottingham et al. recruited patients with an anteriorly tilted pelvis and performed soft‐tissue manipulation, which significantly decreased pain and the pelvic tilt angle of standing positions. 28 Brekke et al. applied a conservative treatment targeting pelvic tilt changes in FAI patients over 8 weeks and found a significant decrease in patients with preexercise moderate pain. 14 Regarding all conservative treatments, this study could provide evidence for preoperative training targets for increasing the posterior pelvic tilt angles to release symptoms, increase ROM, adjust motor activities and accelerate return to work and sports.

Strengths and Limitation

This study has several strengths. First, it provides guidance for surgical planning by demonstrating that pelvic compensation does not necessarily need stringent correction to a normal position. Second, by illuminating the influence of pelvic tilt on postoperative outcomes, the study informs postoperative care and rehabilitation strategies, thereby assisting clinicians in tailoring their approaches to meet the unique needs of each patient. Third, as no previous research has reported postoperative outcomes based on pelvic compensation versus normal positioning, this work serves as a basis for future studies in this field.

This study also has some limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective study, which may cause bias. Second, PSSC measuring errors were noted, which may influence the results. To minimize the effect, two authors performed the measurement and participated in discussion to reach a consensus on controversial data. Future research requires larger sample‐sized studies, and prospective cohort randomized controlled studies should be performed to analyze the postoperative outcomes.

Conclusions

Based on Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), it has been observed that the short‐term clinical outcomes for patients exhibiting compensatory pelvic tilt were comparable to those of patients with standard pelvic positioning, without any additional adverse events. Additionally, the compensatory pelvic tilt did not substantially improve the range of motion or functional outcomes during the short‐term follow‐up period. This reinforces the importance of the understanding of the role and effects of pelvic tilt on patient outcomes, providing basis for rehabilitation strategies and further long‐term studies.
