Utility score mapping from FACT-G to EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 in breast and colorectal cancer patients: a focus on beta mixture models
Authors: Thomas G Poder, Hosein Ameri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop algorithms mapping the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy—General Scale (FACT-G) onto the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-level (EQ-5D-5 L) and the Short-Form Six-Dimension version 2 (SF-6Dv2) for patients with breast or colorectal cancers.MethodsAn online survey was conducted to collect responses to FACT-G, EQ-5D-5L, and SF-6Dv2 from cancer patients in Quebec, Canada (N = 202). Linear models including ordinary least squares (OLS), Censored Least Absolute Deviations (CLAD), the robust MM-estimator model (MM), as well as mixture models including two-part model (TPM), and beta-based mixture (betamix) model were used. Mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), R2, Bayesian information criteria (BIC), and limits of agreement (LOA) calculated using the five cross-validation to assess the predictive ability of the models. Furthermore, the distribution of observed versus predicted values was assessed using Bland-Altman plot.ResultsBased on RMSE and MAE, mixture models better performed than linear models. The betamix model with truncation that included domains and squared terms was the best-performing algorithm for EQ-5D-5 L (MAE = 0.0518, RMSE = 0.0744, R2 = 46.40%, and LOA=-0.166 to 0.165) and SF-6Dv2 (MAE = 0.1375, RMSE = 0.1764, R2 = 35.32%, and LOA=-0.356 to 0.337). EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 utility scores for better health states and more severe health states were underestimated and overestimated, respectively.ConclusionThis study developed robust algorithms to estimate EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 utilities from FACT-G. Consistent with the recent literature, the betamix model outperformed all other econometric models considered in this study. This suggests that mixture models generally exhibit higher performance and are the best choice for mapping.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-025-02410-x.Keywords: EQ-5D-5L, SF-6Dv2, FACT-G, Mapping, Linear models, Mixture models

Introduction

Cost-utility analysis (CUA) is a prominent method for economic evaluation in healthcare, which evaluates the efficiency of healthcare interventions by balancing their costs against the health-related quality of life improvements they achieve. This method calculates the cost-effectiveness of treatments by measuring outcomes in terms of quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), which incorporate both the quantity and quality of life [1, 2]. QALYs are evaluated using utility weights that capture individual preferences for various health states, providing a comprehensive framework for comparing different treatments and informing resource allocation decisions in healthcare [3]. QALYs can be calculated using generic multi-attribute utility instruments (MAUIs) such as the EuroQol Five-Dimension (EQ-5D) [4] and the Short-Form Six-Dimension (SF-6D) [5, 6]. These instruments may not only capture all relevant aspects of certain diseases, such as unique symptoms, and but also not be sensitive enough to detect changes in health status associated with the disease or its treatment [7]. To address these limitations in capturing the full spectrum of cancer-related impacts, non-preference-based specific instruments like the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality of Life Questionnaire Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) and the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy—General Scale (FACT-G) are often preferred. Since these disease-specific instruments do not directly generate utility values necessary for QALYs, there is a critical need for mapping algorithms. The mapping to MAUIs, especially EQ-5D-5 L, is particularly effective, as it is one of the most widely used instruments for calculating QALYs, and several health technology assessment (HTA) agencies, such as NICE, recommend its use for consistency across different diseases. The mapping allows researchers to maintain this consistency, enabling the use of utility values that align with preferred methodologies [8]. Although utility scores the EORTC QLQ-C30 and the FACT-G can be derived from the QLU-C10D and FACT-8D, those are derived from only a portion of the QLQ-C30 and FACT-G items, respectively, which means they may not capture the full breadth of responses or dimensions covered by the complete instruments. Mapping specific questionnaires to MAUIs ensures a more comprehensive translation of the data, which can be crucial for certain cancer studies [9, 10].

Recent studies have developed mapping algorithms to translate scores from two cancer-specific measures (i.e., QLQ-C30 and FACT-G) onto the EQ-5D-3 L index [11]. However, there is a lack of mapping algorithms, especially for the FACT-G, onto the EQ-5D-5 L index. To our knowledge, only two studies have mapped the FACT-G onto the EQ-5D-5 L in patients with cancer [12, 13]. Additionally, no mapping algorithms have been developed to translate FACT-G scores onto the SF-6D version 2 (SF-6Dv2) [11]. The growing evidence demonstrated that the EQ-5D-5 L compared to EQ-5D-3 L has gained significant attention in research, as expanding the response options from three to five levels across each dimension has improved the instrument’s sensitivity to health changes and reduced the ceiling effect [14]. SF-6Dv2 is the updated version of the SF-6D, and its value set has been recently derived from general population in some countries such as Canada [15, 16] and Iran [17]. Compared to EQ-5D-5 L, the SF-6Dv2 includes additional dimensions and levels, particularly those related to cancer such as vitality, making it a potentially more comprehensive tool for assessing health related-quality of life (HRQoL) in cancer populations [18]. Therefore, mapping on SF-6Dv2 could significantly enhance our understanding of health outcomes in cancer populations and improve the accuracy of health economic evaluations in diverse healthcare settings.

In Canada, where two out of every five individuals are likely to develop cancer, the financial burden was substantial, amounting to CAD 26.2 billion [19]. Breast and colorectal cancers are the most frequently diagnosed types, making up 25% and 11% of new cases, respectively [20, 21]. These figures impose a considerable financial burden on healthcare systems, societies, and families, especially with advancements in treatment improving patient survival rates. Hence, there is a growing need to evaluate the CUA of interventions to ensure sustainable healthcare practices. To conduct CUA effectively, a mapping algorithm is essential for transforming scores onto utility values. This study aimed to map the FACT-G onto SF-6Dv2 and EQ-5D-5 L utility values using data obtained from breast or colorectal cancer in Quebec, Canada.

Method

Study design and patients

This study was conducted on patients diagnosed with breast or colorectal cancer at the University Hospital Center of Sherbrooke (CHUS) in Quebec, Canada, involving a total of 202 participants. Data were gathered over two periods: 2 days before and 8 days after a chemotherapy treatment. Initially, participants completed the survey with interviewer assistance. Then they were offered to complete the survey with an interviewer (at the hospital research center) or independently at home about 8 days after receiving the intervention. This approach allowed to monitor changes in HRQoL during early chemotherapy stages, which is one of the most common and costly treatments for cancer [22]. Patients aged 18 or older, who had completed at least one cycle of chemotherapy and were awaiting another cycle, and diagnosed with breast or colorectal cancer were included. Patients who were unable to complete the survey electronically, unable to provide consent, diagnosed with brain metastases, or experiencing delirium, psychosis, or severe depression were excluded. The questionnaire in French is available upon request to authors.

HRQoL preference-based instruments

EQ-5D-5 L is a HRQoL instrument that assesses five general dimensions of health: mobility (MO), self-care (SC), usual activities (UA), pain/discomfort (PD) and anxiety/depression (AD). Each dimension has five levels of severity, ranging from no problem to extreme problems. These five dimensions with their respective severity levels yielded a total of 3,125 possible health states. Each health state represents a specific combination of severity levels across the five dimensions. Health states measured by the EQ-5D-5 L can be converted into a single summary index score ranging from 0 (indicating a dead state) or even negative values (indicating a health state worse than the dead state) to 1 (indicating full health) using population-based value sets. This study used the Canadian population tariff (ranging from − 0.148 to 0.949) to calculate utility scores for all respondents [23]. In this scale, the value of 0.949 (representing the full health state) differs from 1 due to the use of a linear model in calculating health utility scores.

The SF-6Dv2 includes six dimensions: physical functioning (PF), role functioning (RF), social functioning (SF), pain (PA), mental health (MH), and vitality (VT). Each dimension has five levels, except for pain that has six levels, resulting in a total of 18,750 possible health states [18]. The SF-6Dv2 improved upon SF-6Dv1 by enhancing dimension levels and refining item wording while maintaining the same descriptive framework. These enhancements include a more detailed role functioning dimension, a transition in the pain dimension to emphasize severity over frequency, adjustments that harmonize the mental health description with SF-36 terminology, and the inclusion of negative wording in the vitality dimension to capture aspects of fatigue or reduced energy levels [5, 18]. The SF-6Dv2 value set was derived using a discrete choice experiment with time from a general population in Quebec, Canada [15]. This value set is used to calculate health utility scores, which range from − 0.683 to 1.

HRQoL non-preference-based instrument

The FACT-G is a widely used questionnaire designed to assess HRQoL in cancer patients. It comprises four primary domains: physical well-being (PWB), social/family well-being (SWB), emotional well-being (EWB), and functional well-being (FWB). The FACT-G questionnaire consists of 27 items, with responses ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much). Higher scores indicate better quality of life across these domains. Summary scores can be calculated for each of these four domains, alongside a single overall score for the instrument [24].

Data analysis

The analysis was reported following the MAPS reporting guideline, as outlined by Petrou et al. [25]. The corresponding checklist is included in Appendix 1 to ensure methodological rigor and transparency. The mapping algorithm can be estimated when the correlation between the source (FACT-G) and the target measure (EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2) is reflected to some extent. The Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate the association between the FACT-G domain scores and the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 index scores or item responses. The correlation was defined as weak for coefficients < 0.35, moderate for coefficients between ≥ 0.35 and ≤ 0.5, and strong for coefficients > 0.5 [26, 27].

Linear models are commonly used to develop mapping models [28]. However, they are often inappropriate and may lead to biased estimates due to the distributional characteristics of the EQ-5D. These characteristics include the right and left bounding of the EQ-5D distribution, a concentration of observations at full health, a gap between full health and the next feasible EQ-5D value, and multimodality of the distribution [29]. As a result, response mapping and mixture models have become increasingly popular in the development of mapping models [29]. Since there was a small sample size and insufficient observations at some of levels of the descriptive systems of the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2, we were not able to use response mapping. Therefore, mixture models in addition to linear statistical models were used. Among linear models, Ordinary least squares (OLS), Censored Least Absolute Deviations (CLAD), and the robust MM-estimator model (MM) were employed. OLS regression is commonly used as a standard model in developing mapping. Given the EQ-5D utility score is censored at lower or upper endpoints, OLS tend to produce systematic bias. The CLAD is a censored model designed to handle censored data without relying on robust distributional or heteroscedasticity assumptions [30]. The robust MM-estimator model (MM) was also used to effectively address heteroscedasticity and potential outliers in the data [31]. Mixture models, including the two-part model (TPM), Adjusted Limited Dependent Variable Mixture Model (ALDVMM), and the beta-based mixture (betamix) model, are introduced in the context of mapping due to their flexibility and capability to capture multimodality. Since the Canadian EQ-5D-5 L value set ranges between − 0.148 and 0.949, it is necessary to rescale the top value to 1 for the ALDVMM to be used effectively. However, rescaling MAUIs values introduces significant complexity. The ALDVMM does not handle simple linear transformations, such as rescaling, in a straightforward manner. As a result, rescaling inputs can alter the model’s output in unexpected ways, leading to potential misinterpretations. However, the betamix model internally handles the rescaling of MAUIs values, ensuring that predictions and standard errors are correctly interpreted. This internal handling simplifies the complexity from the user, reducing the risk of errors associated with manual rescaling [29]. The betamix model provides a flexible means to regress outcome distributions with truncation support. The model accommodates boundary values by adding a degenerate distribution with a probability mass at the boundaries, a feature known as the truncated-inflated beta model [32]. We used one component to estimate the model because models with more components tend to have convergence problems [32]. Furthermore, the two-part model (TPM) was also employed in this study. The first part of the model used logistic regression to estimate the probability that an individual is in full health, while the second part of the model focused on individuals with scores below full health [33].

For each model, four distinct specifications were developed. The initial specification regressed the EQ-5D and SF-6Dv2 index scores directly on the total score of the FACT-G. The subsequent specification regressed these index scores on the individual domains of the FACT-G. The third specification built upon the second by incorporating squared terms for the FACT-G domains. Finally, the fourth specification further refined the third by including interaction terms among the FACT-G domains. Including age and gender as covariates can enhance the generalizability of the optimal mapping function. However, we did not incorporate these variables due to the very small proportion of males in the sample (< 0.20%) and the fact that the participants were the same in the surveys (i.e., before and after chemotherapy). The specific models contained four aspects:

Model 1: FACT-G scale total score as the primary predictor of health utility score.

Model 2: scale scores for each dimension, including PWB, SWB, EWB, FWB.

Model 3: Model 2 + squared terms of each dimension.

Model 4: Model 3 + interaction term of each dimension.

Model validation and predictive ability

A five cross-validation technique was employed to validate our models [34]. The predictive ability of the models was assessed using the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE), with lower values indicating better performance. The MAE was used as the primary criterion for assessing model performance, as it is a more straightforward measure of average error compared to RMSE. Bayesian information criteria (BIC) was chosen to evaluate the goodness of fit of these specifications within each class of models, where a smaller BIC indicates a better model fit. R2, which measures the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variables, was used. The mean difference between the observed and predicted values was assessed using limits of agreement (LOA). Additionally, the Bland-Altman plot was employed to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed utility values derived from the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 instruments. These plots, designed to identify systematic bias between two measures, display the difference between predicted and observed values against their average [35]. All analyses were conducted using STATA software, version 16.0 (Texas, USA).

Results

The entire sample (N = 202) was included in the final analysis, which provided 374 observations. The patient sample had a mean age of 59.69 years with a standard deviation of 10.50 years, and 80.69% of the participants were women. More than half of the patients were married and lived in urban areas, with the majority having breast cancer. Figure 1 shows the distribution of FACT-G total and domain Scores. The mean score was 56.74 (SD = 7.88), ranging from 30 to 79.17.

The MAE ranged from 0.0518 to 0.0860, indicating a relatively small error across models, while the RMSE ranged from 0.0744 to 0.1126, reflecting the impact of squaring the differences in error calculation. The betamix models and certain specifications of the TPM showed significantly lower MAE compared to other models, suggesting that they provide more precise predictions with fewer deviations from the actual values. The R2 values for each specification were lower than 50%, indicating that these models explained less than half of the variance in the data. This suggests that while the models capture some of the underlying relationships between variables, a substantial portion of the variance remains unexplained. In general, the mixture models (TPM and betamix) performed better than linear models. All MAE values for the specifications of the mixture models were lower than 0.063, with only one exception at 0.0709, while those for the linear models were higher than 0.07. Similarly, all RMSE values for the specifications of the mixture models were lower than 0.088, except for one case (0.0955), while those for the linear models were higher than 0.102. The betamix specifications demonstrated the lowest MAE and RMSE values. Among them, the betamix with truncation that included domains and squared terms was the best-performing algorithm, with an MAE of 0.0518 and an RMSE of 0.0744. This model accounts for the gap between full health and the next feasible EQ-5D-5 L value, including a probability mass at full health. Convergence issues were encountered with a two-component model (Table 3). In the optimal model, improvement in FACT-G domains was associated with an increase in EQ-5D-5 L index score (Table 12 in Appendix 3). The excel calculator in Appendix 4 can be used to estimate EQ-5D-5 L values using the optimal betamix. The LOA ranged from − 0.166 to 0.165, indicating that the best model provided very small differences between predicted and observed utilities, with a mean difference of −0.001 (Fig. 2).

The Bland–Altman plot of the mean difference between observed and predicted EQ-5D-5 L values shows a mean difference of −0.001 (95% limits of agreement, −0.166 to 0.165). The Bland–Altman plot of the mean difference between observed and predicted SF-6Dv2 values shows a mean difference of −0.01 (95% limits of agreement, −0.356 to 0.337)

Similar to the EQ-5D-5 L models, the mixture models outperformed other models in most summary measures, with the most significant proportional improvements observed in MAE and RMSE. The betamix model with truncation that included domains and squared terms was the best-performing algorithm, with the lowest MAE (0.1375) and RMSE (0.1764). Furthermore, the best model demonstrated very small differences between predicted and observed utilities, with a mean difference of −0.01 and 95% LOA ranging from − 0.356 to 0.337 (Fig. 2). The best-performing algorithms estimated in this study can be easily implemented via Excel, as provided in Appendix 4. The predicted utility scores from two of the top models increased consistently with higher total FACT-G scores and across all subdomains, supporting the face validity of the mapping models. As expected, patients with better physical and functional well-being exhibited higher estimated utility values.

Discussion

The FACT-G is an important instrument widely used to assess the HRQoL of cancer patients. However, it is not a preference-based instrument and cannot be used to directly measure health utility values, which are crucial for economic evaluations. Recently, a subset of FACT-G (i.e., FACT-8D) was developed, and its value set was estimated in different country such as Canada, which allows to use it in estimating QALYs. However, the FACT-8D might not fully capture all dimensions of HRQoL measured in the FACT-G, limiting its applicability in certain contexts. Mapping FACT-G to MAUIs like EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 allows for a more comprehensive assessment of HRQoL by integrating additional dimensions that the FACT-8D alone might miss. This approach also increases the flexibility of utilizing FACT-G data across various healthcare settings, especially where EQ-5D-5 L or SF-6Dv2 are preferred or standard measures for economic evaluations. This ensures that the HRQoL information aligns with standard evaluation frameworks and can be integrated into CUA and other health economics research. Hence, using data from a population of breast or colorectal cancer patients in Canada, mapping functions were developed to convert FACT-G scores onto EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 scores. This development enables the application of FACT-G data in settings where EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 are commonly used, facilitating the comparison of HRQoL outcomes across studies and improving the accuracy and relevance of economic evaluations in cancer care. The mapping algorithms may be particularly useful in clinical trials and real-world evidence settings where preference-based utility measures were not collected but FACT-G was administered. Additionally, they can support health technology assessments, inform resource allocation decisions, and contribute to cost-utility analyses in oncology, especially when direct utility data are unavailable.

There was a low to moderate correlation between the EQ-5D-5 L or SF-6Dv2 utilities and the FACT-G domains. One potential reason for these results could be that the dimensions of the MAUIs do not adequately reflect those of the FACT-G. Since the SF-6Dv2 includes more dimensions than the EQ-5D-5 L, it was expected that the correlation between SF-6Dv2 dimensions and FACT-G would be stronger than the correlation between EQ-5D-5 L and FACT-G. However, this expected result was not observed in either this study or the other study conducted on Mapping IWQOLLite onto EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 [36]. Previous studies have shown that the overlap between original and targeted measures can influence the outcome of mapping [37].

This study adapted various linear and mixture models to develop and identify the mapping algorithm that best predicted EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 utility scores from FACT-G. The predictive ability of the models in this study showed that MAE ranged from 0.0518 to 0.0860 for EQ-5D-5 L and from 0.1375 to 0.1547 for SF-6Dv2. RMSE ranged from 0.0744 to 0.1126 for EQ-5D-5 L and from 0.1764 to 0.1914 for SF-6Dv2. These results are comparable to those generally reported in the mapping literature, where MAE ranged from 0.0011 to 0.19 and RMSE from 0.084 to 0.20 [38]. Specifically, the lower MAE and RMSE values for EQ-5D-5 L suggest that the model’s predictions were closer to the observed values compared to the SF-6Dv2, which had higher MAE and RMSE values. This suggests that the EQ-5D-5 L model may have better predictive accuracy or less variability in its predictions compared to the SF-6Dv2 model. This result is not supported by the mapping study that used the FACT-G with EQ-5D-3 L and SF-6Dv1. One possible explanation for this difference is the variation in target instruments used in the two studies. Additionally, the value sets estimated for the instruments in our study could contribute to these differences. The value set for EQ-5D-5 L (− 0.148 to 0.949) has a narrower range than that of SF-6Dv2 (− 0.683 to 1). This narrower range can lead to increased variability in the mapped data because it provides a shorter scale for capturing differences in HRQoL. As a result, models based on EQ-5D-5 L might show less variability in predictions compared to those using SF-6Dv2, which has a broader range and potentially more sensitivity to variations in HRQoL measurements. Our results from mapping FACT-G to EQ-5D-5 L using the betamix model demonstrated improved prediction accuracy compared to previously published models. Specifically, our model yielded a lower MAE (0.05) than a study using a two-part beta model in Japanese cancer patients (MAE = 0.09) [13], and also outperformed the generalized estimating equations (GEE) model applied to a study with non-small cell lung cancer cachexia patients (MAE = 0.0942) [12]. The comparison also showed that the betamix model is better to handle the bounded nature and skewed distribution of utility scores, which is particularly important when modeling EQ-5D-5 L values that often cluster near full health.

When comparing the results of linear and mixture models, our findings confirmed that linear regression might not be appropriate for mapping. The results indicate that models accommodating multimodality tend to achieve superior performance. These findings are consistent with what was reported in other studies using specific cancer instruments, such as the QLQ-C30 [39, 40]. The beta-mixture models produced the most accurate predictions of utility scores using FACT-G scores in both MAUIs in the present study. Our preferred betamix was a one-component model that included the gap between full health and the next feasible EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 values, with a probability mass at full health. Although adding more components to the betamix resulted in convergence problems in our study, evaluating the performance of models with a larger number of components in other datasets is an important area for future research. The superiority of the beta-mixture model is not only statistical but also conceptually grounded. Utility scores are inherently bounded and often exhibit ceiling effects resulting in multimodal and skewed distributions. The beta-mixture approach is well-suited to handle these properties [32]. Furthermore, the potential nonlinear relationships between the FACT-G domains and utility values were captured through the model specification. For example, improvements in physical well-being may not yield proportional increases in utility when emotional or social functioning remains poor. By including squared terms, the model captures such nonlinearities, including diminishing marginal returns. These interactions can produce clusters of certain health states that linear models fail to resolve, whereas the beta-mixture framework flexibly models this heterogeneity. However, the betamix model requires the dependent variable to be within the 0–1 interval and automatically rescales utility values accordingly. Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge that such transformations may subtly affect the interpretation of predictions near the limits of the scale. A recent systematic review found that the use of solely linear regression in mapping studies decreased from 49% during 1997–2011 to 13% in 2014–2016 [28]. Some studies have reported only minor differences in the predictive ability of linear models compared to more advanced models, such as response mapping and mixture models [41, 42]. However, these studies primarily focused on the observed mean values of MAUIs, without fully accounting for the underlying data-generating processes captured by these models. This oversight is significant because the data-generating process is essential for accurately simulating cost-effectiveness analyses. Response mapping and mixture models provide a more accurate understanding of how data is generated and how different variables interact, which is essential for more precise and reliable cost-effectiveness evaluations [43].

Our algorithm explained 46.4% of the variance in EQ-5D-5 L utility scores and 35.32% of the variance in SF-6Dv2 utility scores. This indicates that a significant portion of the variance remains unexplained, suggesting that our model may not capture all the factors influencing these utility scores. Consequently, there is a possibility that patients could be assigned incorrect utility values using this algorithm. The unexplained variance might be due to various factors not included in the model, such as individual patients’ characteristics. Moreover, this is not an uncommon characteristic in mapping studies related to cancers, which have revealed varying R2 values. The highest R2 was observed when responses to the QLQ-C30 was mapped to 15D (R2 = 0.909) [44] and a moderate R2 was noted when FACT-B was mapped onto the generic SF-6Dv2 (R2 = 0.6102) [45]. The lowest R2 was observed when the FACT-G was mapped to EQ-5D-3 L (R2 = 0.127) [46].

Our results indicated that, regardless of the econometric technique used, models incorporating domains and squared terms performed better than those based solely on the total score. Previous mapping studies have shown that incorporating additional terms, such as squared, interaction, and cubic terms, enhances the performance of mapping algorithms. These terms capture nonlinear relationships and interactions between variables, thereby more accurately reflecting the complexities between the instruments or scales being mapped [47, 48]. Huang et al. revealed that including squared and interaction terms for FACT-H&N (i.e., FACT for Head and Neck) domains improved model performance [48].

Although the majority of differences between observed and predicted values from the two best models fell within the 95% limits of agreement, this study found evidence of underestimation of EQ-5D-5 L utility scores in mild to moderately severe health states. However, due to the small sample size, firm conclusions cannot be drawn. This limitation highlights the need for future research to include patients in severe health states, those worse than dead (utility score < 0) and those at full health (utility score = 1), which were rarely represented in our sample.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, there were no observations at the most severe levels for some dimensions of the EQ-5D-5 L, such as MO, SC, and PA, and fewer than 5% of observations at the most severe levels for SF-6Dv2 dimensions. These gaps present challenges for accurately modeling severe health states and limit the applicability of indirect mapping methods, which depend on a balanced distribution across all levels of health severity. The absence of responses indicating “extreme” problems in several EQ-5D-5 L dimensions likely contributed to the typical pattern observed in mapping algorithms to underestimate utility scores in better health states and overestimate them in more severe ones. Without observations at the extremes of health severity, particularly for domains like pain that are highly relevant in terminal cancer stages, the models may fail to reflect how utility scores decline in a non-proportional way as health worsens. Although this limitation stems in part from the modest sample size and limited heterogeneity in reported EQ-5D-5 L states, it underscores the need for future research to deliberately include patients across a wider spectrum of disease severity to enhance model validity and utility. Second, although we employed five-fold cross-validation and random sample validation to reduce the risk of overfitting, we did not use an independent external dataset to evaluate the generalizability of our mapping algorithms. While internal validation techniques provide useful insights into model performance, external validation is widely regarded as the gold standard for establishing generalizability, particularly when the sample is geographically or clinically narrow. Our sample consisted exclusively of patients recruited from a single hospital center in Quebec, Canada, all of whom were post-chemotherapy breast or colorectal cancer patients. This limited geographic and clinical diversity may reduce the model’s applicability to broader cancer populations, such as those from other regions, treatment stages, or cancer types. Furthermore, as emphasized in the systematic review by Petrou et al. [25], models evaluated solely through internal validation may overestimate predictive performance. Future research should therefore aim to replicate and validate our mapping algorithms using multicenter datasets that encompass a wider range of cancer populations, including those at different stages of disease progression such as metastatic or terminal patients. Third, we did not incorporate age and gender as covariates in our mapping models, which could have potentially improved the generalizability of the mapping function. This decision was based on the substantial gender imbalance in our sample—80% of participants were women, while fewer than 20% were men—which limited our ability to include gender without introducing instability into the models. Additionally, the same individuals completed all surveys, and age differences across responses were minimal, reducing the relevance of age and gender as a modeling covariate. We acknowledge that the lack of demographic diversity, particularly with respect to gender, constrains the applicability of our mapping algorithms to more representative cancer populations. This is especially relevant in the context of colorectal cancer, where incidence rates are nearly equal between men and women. The underrepresentation of males in our study highlights a broader concern regarding the potential for bias in cost-utility analyses when key demographic groups are insufficiently represented. Future research should prioritize balanced recruitment and consider incorporating demographic covariates to improve the robustness, fairness, and external validity of utility mapping models used in QALY-based evaluations.

Conclusion

To enable the use of FACT-G in CUA, we created the first set of models to estimate EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 values from FACT-G data. These models were developed using data from adult patients with breast or colorectal cancer, providing a reliable method to convert FACT-G scores into utility values for economic evaluations. Our results confirmed the inadequacy of linear models for mapping and showed that betamix had superior performance. It enables comparisons of the cost-effectiveness of the cancer interventions and allows policymakers to make fair and efficient decisions on resource allocation. Further research is needed to assess the predictive ability of the estimated models in different datasets, particularly those that include variations in age, gender, and different types of cancer.
