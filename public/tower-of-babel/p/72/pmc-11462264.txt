Select Gut Microbiota Impede Rotavirus Vaccine Efficacy
Authors: Vu L Ngo, Yanling Wang, Yadong Wang, Zhenda Shi, Robert Britton, Jun Zou, Sasirekha Ramani, Baoming Jiang, Andrew T Gewirtz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThe protection provided by rotavirus (RV) vaccines is highly heterogeneous among individuals. We hypothesized that microbiota composition might influence RV vaccine efficacy.MethodsFirst, we examined the potential of segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) colonization to influence RV vaccine efficacy in mice. Next, we probed the influence of human microbiomes on RV vaccination via administering mice fecal microbial transplants (FMTs) from children with robust or minimal RV vaccine responsiveness. Post-FMT, mice were subjected to RV vaccination followed by RV challenge.ResultsSFB colonization induced a phenotype that was reminiscent of RV vaccine failure (ie, failure to generate RV antigens and, consequently, anti-RV antibodies following RV vaccination resulting in proneness to RV challenge after SFB levels diminished). FMTs from children to mice recapitulated donor vaccination phenotype. Specifically, mice receiving FMTs from high-responsive vaccinees copiously shed RV antigens and robustly generated anti-RV antibodies following RV vaccination. Concomitantly, such mice were impervious to RV challenge. In contrast, mice receiving FMTs from children who had not responded to RV vaccination exhibited only modest responses to RV vaccination and, concomitantly, remained prone to RV challenge. Microbiome analysis ruled out a role for SFB but suggested involvement of Clostridium perfringens. Oral administration of cultured C. perfringens to gnotobiotic mice partially recapitulated the RV vaccine non-responder phenotype. Analysis of published microbiome data found C. perfringens abundance in children modestly associated with RV vaccine failure.ConclusionMicrobiota composition influences RV vaccine efficacy with C. perfringens being one, perhaps of many, potential contributing taxa.Keywords: Microbiota, Segmented Filamentous Bacteria, Seroconversion

Summary

Rotavirus (RV) vaccines, which are live attenuated viruses, frequently fail to infect, and subsequently protect, their vaccinee against virulent RV. Transplant of infant microbiotas into germ-free mice shows that an infant’s microbiome can be a cause of such RV vaccine failure with Clostridium perfringens being one contributing bacterial species.

Rotavirus (RV) vaccines have markedly reduced the RV disease burden in high-income countries but have proven less effective in some low-income countries as assessed by both the RV-induced disease burden and levels of anti-RV antibodies in vaccinees.1,2 RV vaccines are orally administered live attenuated viruses and thus, like virulent RV, must infect their hosts to elicit protective immunity. Failure of RV vaccines to elicit anti-RV antibodies in individual vaccinees associates with the absence of fecal RV antigen shedding, thus strongly suggesting that some instances of RV vaccine failure reflect inability of RV vaccine viruses to infect their hosts.3 One might envisage that such instances of RV vaccine viruses failing to infect their host might reflect that such children do not need an RV vaccine. However, one can imagine instances in which resistance to RV is transient and/or is not sufficient to prevent infection by highly virulent circulating RV strains. In either scenario, such individuals would lack protection against pathogenic RV and thus potentially become examples of RV vaccine failure. One potential environmental factor that might impact infection of RV vaccine viruses is gut microbiota. Consequently, several recent studies have evaluated the impact of gut microbiota composition on oral rotavirus vaccine response with differing outcomes in infants.4,5

An example of a transient microbiota resident impeding RV infection is that of segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB).6 Specifically, mice naturally colonized with, or exogenously administered, SFB are resistant to RV infection, particularly at early time points following SFB exposure when its levels are highest. Herein, we examined the extent to which SFB influenced RV infection and immunity. We observed that the colonization of SFB in mice led to a phenotype resembling RV vaccine failure, thus corroborating the premise of our hypothesis. Investigation of the extent to which this finding might apply to humans led us to study fecal microbes in a group of Mexican children who received the RV vaccine Rotarix.7 SFB was not detected in fecal samples from these children, arguing against it playing a role in this cohort. However, conceptual relevance was evident in that transplant of fecal microbes from RV vaccine non-responders to germ-free mice recapitulated the RV vaccine failure phenotype. This phenotype could be partially mimicked by colonization of mice with Clostridium pefringrens (C. perfringens), which was present in one of the RV vaccine non-responders. These results support the concept that microbiota composition can influence RV vaccine responsiveness and that C. pefringrens is one example of a human microbiota constituents capable of promoting RV vaccine failure.

Results

Colonizing Mice With SFB Results in an RV Vaccine Failure Phenotype

Infection of adult mice with RV can be viewed as being analogous to RV vaccination in humans in that in both cases a live virus asymptomatically infects the intestine and leads to RV shedding and generation of anti-RV antibodies, which help clear the virus and provide lasting protection against future RV infection.8,9 One microbe capable of impeding RV infection in mice is SFB. SFB is frequently present at moderate levels in some mouse colonies6 and can achieve persistently high levels in mice with genetically encoded immune deficiencies. Such mice display stark lasting resistance to RV infection. Exposure of wild-type (WT) mice to SFB in young adulthood (ie, 4–8 weeks of age) results in transiently high SFB levels and, consequently, temporary resistance to RV infection.6,10 Accordingly, we envisaged that having relatively high levels of SFB at the time of RV vaccination might result in RV vaccine failure. To interrogate this hypothesis, mice were orally administered a fecal microbial transplant (FMT) that lacked or contained SFB. One week later, such mice were orally inoculated with 104 50% shedding dose (SD50) murine RV (vaccine dose) and, 5 weeks later, a 100-fold higher dose of this virus (RV challenge) (Figure 1A). Mice administered SFB-negative FMT exhibited a week-long course of fecal RV antigen shedding following the day (d) 0 RV inoculation (Figure 1B). Concomitantly, such mice displayed anti-RV antibodies in serum and feces on d21 and d28 (Figure 1C). Accordingly, these mice were fully protected against the RV challenge dose as evidenced by the lack of fecal antigens following the d34 RV inoculation. In contrast, mice receiving an SFB-positive FMT displayed few fecal RV antigens following the d0 inoculation and generated only very modest levels of anti-RV antibodies. This modest level of anti-RV antibodies was not sufficient to fully protect against RV infection, as evidenced by the detectable level of fecal RV antigens following the d34 high-dose RV challenge. Such fecal RV antigen shedding following the challenge but not vaccine RV inoculation may have reflected the higher RV levels in the RV challenge dose and/or that SFB levels had peaked within 2 weeks exposure and were low by day 34 (Figure 1D) and thus not sufficient to protect against RV challenge. Regardless, these results serve as proof of principle that select microbiota can impede infection of RV vaccine viruses, resulting in vulnerability to future RV challenge, a phenotype which is reminiscent of RV vaccine failure.

Transplant of Children’s Fecal Microbiomes to Germ-free Mice Recapitulates RV Vaccine Responsiveness

The extent to which SFB is present in humans is not entirely clear, although it has been detected in some cohorts/individuals,11,12 and one recent study suggests children carrying SFB are indeed at reduced risk of RV infection.13 Yet, irrespective of a specific role for SFB in RV vaccine failure, our more general hypothesis is that an individual’s microbiota composition influences infection and subsequently immunity elicited by RV vaccines. Thus, we sought to extend our studies to humans by broadly investigating the extent to which microbiotas of children exhibiting poor antibody responses to RV vaccination might harbor bacteria capable of impeding RV infection and anti-RV antibody generation. We leveraged previously collected fecal samples linked to previously determined measures of post-vaccination anti-RV antibody titers from a study of Mexican infants administered the Rotarix vaccine.7 Fecal samples from the infants exhibiting the highest and lowest post-vaccination serum anti-RV immunoglobulin A (IgA) titers were selected for transplant into groups of germ-free mice (Figure 2A). Mice were then administered vaccination and challenge doses of RV 7d and 34d post-FMT, respectively. Fecal RV antigens and anti-RV antibodies were monitored as above. Mice receiving FMT from the non-responding infant exhibited a phenotype reminiscent of RV vaccine failure. Specifically, relative to the mice receiving FMT from the high-responding infant, they exhibited low post-vaccine levels of fecal RV antigens and anti-RV antibodies, rendering them somewhat susceptible to RV infection from the d34 inoculation (Figure 2B). We repeated this experiment using fecal samples from infants with the second highest and lowest post-vaccination titers and observed a similar pattern of results (Figure 2C). Regardless of which fecal samples they received, no mice displayed detectable levels of SFB (Figure 2D). These results support the notion that non-SFB microbes harbored by select individuals contribute to infection and, consequently, the efficacy of RV vaccines.

C. perfringens, Present in an RV Vaccine Non-responder Impedes Antibody Response to RV Vaccination

We next sought to identify bacteria that had engrafted in the FMT recipients and thus potentially influenced outcomes of RV exposure. Hence, we analyzed fecal microbiomes of FMT recipients by 16S sequencing 7d post-transplant at the time of RV inoculation (we lacked sufficient sample to directly analyze donor samples). Principle coordinate analysis showed that a clear difference in overall compositions was evident between mice receiving the FMT from the top responders and non-responders (Figure 3A). The overall a-diversity (richness), although low compared with that seen in adults, was similar to previously analyzed infant cohorts5,14 and did not differ starkly between the groups (Figure 3B). One specific notable difference, evident from taxonomic bar charts, was the presence of C. perfringens, which comprised 10% to 20% of the 16S reads in the non-responder FMT recipients and was not detected in the responder recipients (Figure 3C). A more systematic approach, namely linear discriminant analysis effect size analysis, identified C. perfringens and several other taxa as being overrepresented in the non-responder FMT recipients (Figure 3D). Yet, except for C. perfringens, abundance of such taxa was very low (below 1% of total 16S reads). Hence, we viewed C. perfringens as the candidate effector of RV infection most worthy of further investigation.

We first administered a human clinical C. pefringrens isolate to conventional mice 1 week prior to RV inoculation (Figure 4A). C. pefringrens was present only transiently, and at low levels, in feces of recipient mice, suggesting it poorly colonized (Figure 4B). Moreover, such C. pefringrens administration resulted in only a modest delay in peak levels of fecal RV shedding (Figure 4C, D) and did not impact generation of serum or fecal anti-RV antibodies (Figure 4E). Considering this result in the context of our FMT results, in which C. pefringrens seemed to readily colonize the gut, we reasoned it may reflect the inability of C. perfringens to outcompete resident mouse microbiota. We sought to overcome this hurdle by using Altered Schaedler Flora (ASF) mice (Figure 5A), whose microbiota consist of only 8 bacterial species: Clostridium sp. (ASF356), Lactobacillus intestinalis (ASF360), Lactobacillus murinus (ASF361), Mucispirillum shaedleri (ASF457), Eubacterium plexicaudatum (ASF492), Pseudoflavonifractor sp. (ASF500), Clostridium sp. (ASF502), and Parabacteroides goldsteinii (ASF519).15 ASF mice display minimal colonization resistance but yet lack many of the overt mucosal immunologic abnormalities of germ-free mice. Indeed, C. pefringrens was readily detected in feces of ASF mice 7d following its administration, at which time the mice were inoculated with RV (Figure 5B). Such carriage of C. pefringrens by ASF mice resulted in delayed and reduced levels of RV antigen shedding (Figure 5C, D) and generation of anti-RV antibodies (Figure 5E), although the reduction was not sufficient to render mice prone to RV infection (ie, a second RV inoculation on day 34 did not result in detectable RV antigens in either group). Such ability to suppress levels of RV shedding and anti-RV antibodies following RV inoculation was not shared by a related clostridia isolate, namely C. sporogenes (Figure 6). These results support the notion that C. pefringrens, which is known to be selectively present in humans and can function as an opportunistic pathogen, may influence the extent to which RV vaccine viruses infect their hosts and elicit their intended immune responses.

C. perfringens Suppressed Model RV Vaccination in a Manner That Was Independent of RAR Signaling and Did Not Require Changes in Abundance of Other Bacteria

We sought to explore potential mechanisms by which C. pefringrens, but not C. sporogenes, impeded RV infection in ASF mice. We first considered that these bacteria might differ in their relative ability to colonize these mice and/or alter abundance of ASF species. However, these bacteria colonized ASF mice to a similar degree and neither significantly altered levels of ASF bacteria, thus arguing against this possibility (Figure 7). We next considered a possible role for retinoic acid receptor (RAR) signaling, which mediates SFB’s suppression of RV infection and, consequently, generation of anti-RV antibodies in that SFB’s impacts on these events are completely blocked by a pharmacologic inhibitor of RAR, namely BMS493.10 In contrast, this RAR inhibitor had no impact on C. pefringrens’ suppression of RV infection or antibody generation (Figure 8), arguing against C. pefringrens using RAR generation as a means of suppressing RV infection.

SFB is also reported to directly interact with RV in a way that reduces RV infectivity in mice.6 Using similar methodology, we investigated the possibility that C. pefringrens may also directly reduce ability of RV to infect the intestine. Specifically, RV was incubated with suspensions of feces from ASF mice that were or were not colonized with C. pefringrens. Fecal bacteria were then removed by filtration and RV containing supernatants used to inoculate ASF mice. This approach suggested that, like SFB, C. pefringrens had ability to interact with RV in a manner that modestly impeded its infectivity but did not result in a significant reduction in anti-RV antibodies (Figure 9A). Nonetheless, that such ability to directly modestly reduce RV infectivity was not shared by C. sporogenes (Figure 9B) leaves open the possibility that such interactions might contribute to the reduced RV infection we observed in C. pefringrens-colonized ASF mice.

C. perfringens Induces Interferonɣ Expression

We next considered the possibility that colonization of the intestine by C. perfringens might impede RV infection by elevating expression of host defense gene expression. To investigate this possibility, ASF mice were administered autologous FMT (ie, ASF bacteria), C. perfringens, or C. sporogenes (Figure 10A). Administration of C. perfringens resulted in mild weight loss, peaking at 6d (Figure 10B). One day later, which corresponds to the time mice would be inoculated with RV, mice were euthanized, and an array of host defense/pro-inflammatory gene assayed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Results were displayed as a heat map in which mean expression of each gene relative to Gapdh is shown for the 3 groups of mice (Figure 10C). We observed increases in several inflammatory genes with a particularly prominent elevation in interferon (IFN)-ɣ. Assay of serum via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) supported the general notion that C. perfringens was pro-inflammatory in that levels of the general inflammatory marker lipocalin-2 (Lcn-2) and INF-g were elevated by 7- and 8-fold, respectively (Figure 10D). To discern the source of the elevated INF-ɣ, we measured total and INF-g+ levels of candidate INF-g-producing cells by flow cytometry. We observed an increased abundance of INF-ɣ+ NK and CD8 T-cells following administration of C. perfringens but not C. sporogenes (Figure 10E). INF-g expression by CD4 T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells was not similarly impacted. Nor did we observe increases in chemokine attractants for neutrophils (cxcl1, cxcl2) and eosinophils (ccl11), suggesting these cells were not impacted by C. perfringens. The increased levels of INF-ɣ+ NK and CD8 T-cells was paralleled by increased perforin expression, arguing the increase in IFNɣ expression was functionally significant (Figure 10F). Whether such increased INF-ɣ expression contributes to suppression of RV infection requires further investigation, but observations that INF-ɣ can suppress RV infection in cell culture and pigs accords with this notion.16,17

Association of C. perfringens With RV Vaccine Responsiveness In Humans

Lastly, we probed the extent to which C. perfringens present in the intestine of RV vaccinees might associate with RV vaccine responsive by reanalyzing previously published data. We found 3 publications that reported fecal microbiome analysis relative to whether RV subjects seroconverted (ie, became positive for anti-RV antibodies post-vaccination). Such studies reported microbiome data as tables of operational taxonomic units (OTUs), which had been generated from 16S rRNA sequencing. In 2 of these studies, few samples displayed detectable levels of C. perfringens irrespective of vaccine responsiveness.14,18 In contrast, in a study of an RV vaccine cohort in Vellore, India, 7.7% of subjects (13 of 168) had a mean relative abundance of C. pefringrens of greater than 0.1% of all 16S reads.5 The seroconversion rate of these subjects was 23% (3 of 13) vs 53% (82 of 155) for the remainder of the cohort. This difference was statistically significant (P = .036) by the Fisher exact test, which is designed to test this type of hypothesis but would have certainly been deemed non-significant, and thus likely unnoticed, when using statistical analyses designed for untargeted discovery approaches used in this study. Viewing mean relative abundance of C. perfringens as a continuous variable (Figure 11) showed greater mean relative abundance of C. perfringens in non-seroconverters relative to seroconverters, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .11). This trend was not seen for other Clostridia species. These data support the notion that C. perfringens may contribute to some instances of RV vaccine failure.

Discussion

The advent of RV vaccines has ameliorated the disease scourge caused by this virus by both directly protecting vaccinees and via generating “herd immunity.”19 However, RV vaccines have proven less beneficial in some low-income regions.1,2 A variety of environmental factors, including breast-feeding, may contribute to RV vaccine inefficacy, but overall, determinants of the RV vaccine heterogeneity both within and between different populations remain poorly defined. That microbiota and oral vaccines share the gut environment, combined with the increasing appreciation that microbiota composition can broadly influence its host, led to the hypothesis that an individual’s gut microbiota composition might also be a determinant of RV vaccine efficacy. Studies seeking to identify associations between microbiome and RV vaccine-induced antibodies support this notion, although the strengths of such associations have been moderate, and taxa involved vary considerably between studies.20 In any case, it remains unclear whether these differences in microbiota simply predict responsiveness to the RV vaccine or actively influence it. Herein, we utilized a functional approach, namely human to mouse FMT, to examine the extent to which an individual’s microbiota might impact RV infection of and, subsequently, generation of adaptive immunity that would protect against future RV infection. Some caveats notwithstanding, our results support the view that microbiota can indeed drive RV vaccine failure, with SFB and C. pefringrens being examples of microbes that are occasionally present in humans and capable of contributing to this phenotype.

Some limitations of this study concern its human fecal samples, which were collected over 10 years prior to initiation of the current study.7 The original purpose of the study was to examine if breast feeding might contribute to RV vaccine failure. For this purpose, fecal samples were collected 3d post vaccine dose 1 and dose 2 to check for the presence of RV antigens as an indicator for RV vaccine virus infection in the host. In contrast, our purpose of examining impact of microbiota on infection of, and response to, the RV vaccine virus would be best served by collection of feces immediately before vaccine administration. That said, we think it is a reasonable assumption that any viable microbes present in these d3 to d7 post-vaccination samples would have likely been present on d0. Although one exception to this might be the RV vaccine virus itself, but this virus is incapable of infecting C57BL6 mice and, moreover would have been detected by our assays of RV antigens and antibodies. Another potential area of concern is that the samples were over 10 years old and appeared severely dehydrated, and thus, it is likely that only species capable of forming stable spores were transferred. A related limitation was that the lack of sample volume precluded sequence analysis to examine direct associations between RV vaccine responsiveness and microbiome, obfuscating the extent to which donor microbiomes faithfully engrafted in germ-free mice. Considering these limitations, together with the labor and expense of gnotobiotic work, we now seek freshly preserved samples to continue this line of investigation. Nonetheless, we submit the overall body of data reported herein with SFB, FMT, and C. perfringens as a compelling proof of principle that microbiota compositions can influence RV vaccine performance.

We do not argue that C. pefringrens itself is a major cause of RV vaccine failure. Indeed, even within the Vellore cohort, which had the highest frequency of C. pefringrens, 89% of RV vaccine non-seroconverters lacked detectable levels of this microbe. Thus, we view the association of C. perfringens with vaccine non-responsiveness in the Vellore cohort, together with our findings in mice, to reflect that C. perfringens may be one of a panel of microbes, including bacteria and viruses, that can impact infection of, and, consequently, immune responses elicited by RV vaccine viruses. In general, C. perfringens is considered a normal, albeit not universally present, resident of the intestine, and its appearance in FMT recipients may, in part, reflect its well-characterized ability to form hardy spores that would withstand harsh storage conditions and thus provide a variety of potential routes of exposure to infants. Furthermore, some non-exclusively breast-fed infants might experience exposure to higher levels of C. perfringens,21 which can occur from consumption of inadequately refrigerated food, can be envisaged to occur frequently in low-income countries wherein RV vaccine failure rates are high. Thus, collectively, we submit C. perfringens is a plausible contributor to RV vaccine failure. There is considerable heterogeneity between different C. perfringens isolates, especially regarding the toxins they carry and, consequently, their pathogenic potential,22 neither of which can be assessed from 16S data, and thus additional approaches might be needed to better address a potential role for C. perfringens in RV vaccine failure.

The mechanism(s) by which C. perfringens might impede infection of, and subsequently elicitation of immunity by, RV vaccine viruses is not yet clear. That this bacterium poorly colonized conventional mice and delayed but did not reduce RV shedding argues that colonization is critical. Yet, that C. sporogenes, which colonized the intestine to a similar degree, did not impact RV infection indicates that Clostridial colonization, by itself, is not sufficient to impact RV. We speculate that C. perfringens may impede RV infection via inducing host-defense gene expression that make epithelial cells resistant to RV infection. In support of this notion, we observed that colonization the ileum by C. perfringens resulted in production of IFNɣ, which protects epithelial cells against RV infection in vitro and in vivo.16,17 In this context, we speculate that the delayed RV infection in conventional mice administered C. perfringens may reflect a very small amount of IFNɣ that was elicited by the transient C. perfringens colonization. How and why C. perfringens, but not C. sporogenes, induced IFNɣ will require further study, especially determining the strain specificity and bacterial genes that drive this response.

In conclusion, our observations support the notion that microbiota composition is a determinant of RV vaccine efficacy, but further investigation is needed to identify contributions of specific microbes, potentially in specific cohorts, and, moreover, discern their mechanisms of action.

Materials and Methods

Mice

C57BL/6 conventional mice were acquired from Jackson Laboratory. Gnotobiotic mice colonized with ASF consortia were generated from C57BL/6 germ-free mice obtained from Taconic Bioscience Inc. All mice involved in the experiments were accommodated in an animal biosafety level 2 facility. The animal studies received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Georgia State University.

Microbiota Transplantation

The protocol governing collection of fecal samples was reviewed and approved by the Subcommittee on Research and Bioethics of the Ministry of Health of Mexico City.9 The fecal samples were diluted in a 20% glycerol phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (21-040-CV) and filtered through a 5-mm filter. Fecal suspensions were administered to recipient mice via oral gavage, using 200 μl per mouse. This use of the samples did not require review by the institutional review board of Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) because the CDC tested preexisting, anonymous specimens.

C. perfringens and C. sporogenes inoculation

C. perfringens and C. sporogenes were grown from human bacterial strain libraries at Baylor College of Medicine. Bacteria were grown in a custom-developed low-salt media comprised of the following ingredients (concentration in g/l): NaCl (0.4), K2HPO4 (0.04), KH2PO4 (0.04), MgSO4.7H2O (0.01), CaCl2.2H2O (0.01), Tryptone (1.0), Proteose peptone #3 (2.0), Yeast extract (2.0), Haemin (1.0), Tween 80 (2.0), Inulin (0.01), and Vit-K3 (0.2). Colonies placed into such media were grown in an anerobic chamber to late log phase, frozen as glycerol stocks (15%), and then administered to recipient mice via oral gavage, 200 μl per mouse.

Rotavirus Challenges

Aged- and sex-matched adult C57BL/6 and ASF mice were orally inoculated with 104 SD50 of murine rotavirus EC (mRV) strain in 200μl PBS. For re-challenge, mice were orally inoculated with 106 SD50 mRV. Feces were collected daily, and blood were collected on d14, d21, and d28 post first mRV challenge. ELISA measured RV fecal antigens and RV-specific antibodies as previously described.23

RV-antigen-specific ELISA

ELISA was used to detect RV antigens shedding in mouse feces, as previously described.4,11 Ninety-six-well plates (Costar, 3590) were coated with rabbit anti-rotavirus Group-A (BioRad, AHP1360) capture antibody overnight at room temperature and subsequently blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. Mouse fecal homogenates, prepared at a concentration of 100 mg/mL, were then incubated in the pre-blocked plates. Stock mRV was used both as a control and to construct the standard curve. The detection antibody, hyperimmune guinea pig anti-RRV, was diluted at 1:1000 in 1% BSA and added to the plate, followed by an incubation period at room temperature for 1 hour. After this incubation period, the HRP donkey anti-guinea pig antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat#706-035-148) was employed as the secondary antibody was added to plate at a 1:5000 dilution in 1% BSA and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. The signal was developed using TMB ELISA Substrate Solution (Invitrogen, cat#00420156), with the addition of TMB stop solution (KPL, cat# 50–85-04) after 5 minutes of TMB incubation. Optical density (OD) readings were measured at 450 nm.

Serum- and Fecal-specific RV Antibodies ELISA

The 96-well plates were coated overnight at room temperature with rabbit anti-rotavirus group-A (Biorad, AHP1360) capture antibody. Subsequently, RV was added, followed by incubation with serum or fecal supernatants. The amount of antibody was quantified after incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgA or IgG, diluted in a buffer containing 1% BSA and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. The signal was developed using TMB ELISA Substrate Solution (Invitrogen, cat#00420156), with the addition of TMB stop solution (KPL, cat# 50–85-04). OD readings were measured at 450 nm.

Serum Lcn2 and IFN-g ELISA

Lcn-2 and IFN-g in blood were measured using Duoset murine Lcn-2 ELISA kit (R&D Systems) and Mouse IFN gamma kit (ThermoScientific) according to manufacturer’s protocols, and OD was read at 450 nm.

Administration of RAR Inhibitor

The RAR inhibitor, BMS493, was suspended at a concentration of 400 μg/mL in corn oil and was orally gavaged to ASF mice in 200 ul every other day for 7 days before and after the RV inoculation.

RNA Isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse ileum tissues using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit with on-column DNase digestion, following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using the Superscript First Strand Synthesis Kit for RT-qPCR with random hexamer primers (Invitrogen). RT-qPCR was carried out with SYBR Green on a StepOnePlus PCR machine (Applied Biosystems), and gene expression was normalized to GAPDH. The primers used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Sequence-based Microbiome Analysis

Fecal DNA was extracted using DNeasy 96 PowerSoil Pro QIAcube HT Kit (Qiagen), and the region V3-V4 of 16S rRNA genes were amplified using the following primers: 341F 5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′; 805R 5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′. PCR products of each sample were purified using Ampure XP magnetic purification beads. An index PCR was performed to attach dual barcodes and Illumina sequencing adapters using Nextera XT Index kit (Illumina). Final PCR products was verified on 1.5% DNA agarose gel and quantified using Pico dsDNA assay (Invitrogen). An equal molar of each sample was combined and purified again using Ampure XP beads as the library. The library was diluted and spiked with 10% PhiX control (Illumina) and sequenced by Illumina iSeq 100 Sequencing System (2 × 150bp). Demultiplexed fastq files were generated on instrument. Sequence reads were quality filtered by DADA2 plugin in Qiime2. Taxonomy was assigned based on the Greengenes database. Raw sequencing data has been deposited at The European Nucleotide Archine (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/)with accession number: PRJEB78739.
