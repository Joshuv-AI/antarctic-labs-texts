Letter regarding “Grit is not associated with opioid consumption patterns in patients that undergo arthroscopic rotator cuff repair”
Authors: Tirayut Veerasatian, Schawanya K. Rattanapitoon, Nav La, Nathkapach K. Rattanapitoon
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

The recent study by Kleinsmith et al,2 provides valuable insight into the complex interaction between psychological traits and postoperative opioid use. Their finding that grit was not associated with opioid consumption within 2 weeks after surgery challenges the prevailing notion that perseverance-related traits universally influence recovery behaviors.

However, several important considerations arise. First, the authors limited their analysis to a short, 2-week period after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. While this window captures acute pain, it may not adequately reflect the adaptive behavioral processes that grit represents. Prior studies have shown that psychosocial traits such as resilience and optimism exert their greatest effects over longer recovery intervals, influencing functional restoration and satisfaction at 3-12 months rather than the immediate postoperative phase.4,5 A longitudinal design could therefore clarify whether grit affects delayed opioid tapering, adherence to rehabilitation, or transition to chronic pain syndromes.

Second, the study treated grit as an isolated trait. Yet, emerging evidence suggests that grit interacts with other psychological domains—particularly anxiety, depressive symptoms, and pain catastrophizing—that jointly modulate pain perception and medication use.1,6 Without controlling for these factors, the apparent null association between grit and opioid use may reflect overlapping constructs rather than a true lack of effect.

Lastly, the Grit-12 scale, while validated for long-term perseverance, may not fully capture situational coping or state resilience during acute recovery. Complementing it with measures such as the Brief Resilience Scale or preoperative mental health indices could enhance predictive precision.3,4 Integrating multidimensional psychometric profiling into perioperative assessment might allow surgeons to personalize opioid prescriptions and identify patients at risk for overuse.

In conclusion, Kleinsmith et al2 should be commended for highlighting an underexplored psychosocial domain in postoperative pain management. Future research should expand on their findings through longer-term follow-up, multivariate psychological modeling, and inclusion of functional recovery outcomes to delineate the true role of grit in orthopedic rehabilitation.
