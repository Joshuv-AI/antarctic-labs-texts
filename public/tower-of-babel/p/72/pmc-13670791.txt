Effects of stage-specific plant growth regulators application on lodging resistance and yield formation in maize under high planting density
Authors: Tong Xu, Xiaona Wang, Yuhan Zheng, Yanjie Lv, Yongjun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In response to the growing issue of maize lodging caused by typhoons shifting northward into Northeast China, the rational application of plant growth regulators (PGRs) offers a strategy to safeguard maize yields and mitigate economic losses. To elucidate how PGRs applied at different growing stages affect lodging resistance and grain yield, a two-year field experiment (2019–2020) was conducted at the Gongzhuling Field Experimental Station, Jilin Province. The hybrid “Fumin 985” was planted under two densities: D1, 60 000 plants ha⁻1 and D2, 90 000 plants ha⁻1, with three spraying treatments: PGRs at the 10-leaf stage (T10), PGRs at the 15-leaf stage (T15), and water as control (CK). A significant interaction was observed between plant density and application stage. At high density, spraying PGRs at the 10-leaf stage significantly reduced lodging by 37.2%. This effect was primarily attributed to the shortening of basal internodes, an increase in stem diameter, cellulose content and improvements in structural characteristics of roots. Following PGRs application at 15-leaf stage, the grain filling rate increased and the active grain-filling period (P) was prolonged by 2.3 d. Consequently, the thousand kernel weight (TKW) and grain yield improved by 2.3% and 5.8%, respectively. Structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis revealed that under high density, application timing directly improved root, stalk traits and yield components, while directly reducing lodging rate. In conclusion, under high planting density, early PGR application at the 10‑leaf stage significantly reduces lodging by 37.2% through enhancing stalk mechanical strength and root anchorage, whereas application at the 15‑leaf stage significantly increases grain yield by 5.8% by extending the active grain‑filling period and improving thousand‑kernel weight. These findings provide a clear density‑dependent strategy: early PGR application is recommended for typhoon‑prone, high‑risk lodging areas, while late application is preferable for fields where yield maximization is the primary objective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09531-5.

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) occupies a crucial position among global food crops, with its yield and quality significantly influencing food security and social stability [1]. With the continuous growth of the population and the increasing scarcity of arable land resources, close planting cultivation technology has emerged as a core strategy in maize production due to its significant advantage in enhancing yield per unit area [2]. In recent years, global warming has led to a significant northward shift in the activity paths of tropical cyclones, resulting in frequent typhoon impacts on the major maize-producing regions of Northeast China, where typhoons were previously rare [3]. Meteorological data show that from 2016 to 2021, the annual average number of typhoons making landfall in Northeast China increased by 1.8 times compared to the previous decade. Among them, strong typhoons such as “Bavi” and “Maysak” in 2020 moved northward consecutively, causing the area of maize lodging in Jilin, Heilongjiang, and other regions to exceed 2 million hectares, with an average lodging rate of 25% to 40%, and a grain mold rate as high as 12%, resulting in direct economic losses exceeding 5 billion [4, 5]. Lodging not only leads to yield losses, such as interrupted grain filling and imbalanced biomass allocation, but also significantly restricts the widespread application of mechanized grain harvesting technology [6]. Lodging of maize plants can lead to intertwined stems and ears touching the ground [7], thereby increasing the miss rate of combine harvesters, raising the grain breakage rate, and reducing work efficiency. Additionally, lodged stems entangling mechanical components can cause more malfunctions, significantly increasing production costs. Therefore, lodging resistance technology has become key to overcoming the bottleneck of mechanized harvesting, and improving maizeʼs lodging resistance has become a necessary condition for ensuring high and stable yields of maize.

PGRs are chemically synthesized or naturally derived substances capable of regulating plant growth and developmental processes [8]. In recent years, PGRs have demonstrated significant potential as an effective agronomic practice for regulating maize growth and enhancing lodging resistance [9]. In crop production systems, commonly utilized PGRs such as ethephon, chlormequat chloride, and paclobutrazol [10, 11] achieve the goals of plant height reduction, internode shortening, and stem diameter enhancement—ultimately improving lodging resistance by inhibiting gibberellin biosynthesis and retarding cell elongation in crop plants [12]. PGRs further enhance maize lodging resistance by promoting root system development, increasing root biomass and activity, and significantly altering root system architecture through the regulation of root apical meristem activity, cell elongation, and differentiation. When applied exogenously, auxin‑type regulators (e.g., naphthaleneacetic acid, indole‑3‑butyric acid) undergo polar transport to root tissues following foliar application, stimulating primary root elongation and lateral root primordia formation [13]; foliar application of low‑concentration IAA during maize seedling stages increases total root length and lateral root density [14]. Conversely, ABA and ethephon suppress primary root elongation but induce root hair proliferation [15], as demonstrated in soybean, where ethephon enhances root hair density and soil anchorage. Cytokinin‑type regulators delay root senescence and extend functional duration by modulating root cap cell division cycles [16]. Under abiotic stress, these compounds enhance stress tolerance by regulating root antioxidant systems and osmolyte biosynthesis; foliar SA application induces SOD and POD activities, mitigating salt stress‑induced membrane lipid peroxidation [17]. PGRs also influence grain yield and its components in maize [18]. By altering endogenous hormone levels [19], PGR application enhances photosynthetic capacity and nutrient uptake by root, promoting vigorous seedling establishment, which ultimately improves yield components such as kernels per ear and 100‑kernel weight, leading to higher grain yield [20]. Specific effects vary by regulator: foliar‑applied glycine betaine increases grain‑filling rate, boosts total floret numbers, reduces anthesis‑silking interval (ASI), and enhances kernel weight and number per ear [21]; foliar application of exogenous ABA effectively enhances grain‑filling performance by increasing the activity of key starch biosynthesis enzymes and modulating grain hormone levels, thereby increasing kernel weight and yield under high‑density planting conditions [22]. Concurrently, PGR application accelerates aerial root emergence in maize, modifying canopy and root architecture; this promotes soil nutrient acquisition, extends photosynthetic functionality, and increases photo‑assimilate accumulation—ultimately improving grain plumpness, kernels per ear, and 100‑kernel weight [23].

In summary, although PGR application has been widely adopted in maize production, most studies have focused on single-stage application, leaving the density-dependent optimal timing window remains poorly understood, and the coupling mechanisms among root architecture, stalk mechanics, and grain filling under different PGR timings remain poorly understood. To address these gaps, we conducted a two-year field experiment with two densities (60,000 and 90,000 plants ha⁻1) and three PGR timings (V10, V15, and CK) to test three hypotheses: (1) early PGR application (V10) enhances lodging resistance primarily through stalk reinforcement and root anchorage, with effects amplified under high-density stress; (2) late PGR application (V15) increases yield by optimizing source-sink dynamics—extending the grain-filling period and increasing kernel weight; and (3) PGR effects are density-dependent, being significant under high density but minimal or negative under low density. By testing these hypotheses, this study aims to provide a density-dependent, stage-specific PGR strategy for maize production in typhoon-prone regions of Northeast China.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

This experiment was conducted at the Gongzhuling Experiment Station of Jilin Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China, in 2019 and 2020 (43°31´N, 124°49´E). The altitude of the site is 210 m, and the climate is temperate continental monsoon, with an annual sunshine time of 2867 h, an annual accumulated temperature of 2885 °C, and an average frost-free period of 146 d. The local cropping system is annual. The soil is a typic Hapludoll, which is known locally as “black soil”. Soil physiochemical properties in the 0 to 20 cm tillage layer were as follows: organic matter: 16.2 g kg−1; alkali hydrolyzable nitrogen (N): 183.4 mg kg−1; available phosphorus (P): 12.1 mg kg−1; available potassium (K): 283.4 mg kg−1; pH: 7.6. Experimental design

A split-plot design was utilized in this experiment. The main-plot factor was planting density, with two levels: 60,000 and 90,000 plants ha⁻1. The sub-plot factor was the application timing of a plant growth regulator (PGR), consisting of three treatments: T10 (application at the 10-leaf stage), T15 (application at the 15-leaf stage), and a water-sprayed control (CK), each replicated three times. The PGR used was a commercial formulation named “Jindele” in China, with the main active ingredients of ethephon and cycocel (EC, w/w = 3:1). Based on the product specification, the application dose was 450 mL ha⁻1, and the concentration was 2 mL EC per 1 L H₂O at each application timing. The maize was planted in ridge culture with a row spacing of 65 cm. Each experimental sub-plot measured 60 m2 (6.0 m × 10 m). The maize hybrid Fumin 985 (FM985) was selected as the test material and sown at a row spacing of 65 cm. This hybrid was bred by Jilin Fumin Seed Co., Ltd. from the cross M801 × FM1101 and was nationally approved in 2019 (Approval No. Guoshenyu 20,190,111) for cultivation in the middle-maturing spring maize region of Northeast China. FM985 is characterized by a semi-compact plant type, with a plant height of 269 cm, ear height of 97 cm, and 19 leaves per plant. It exhibits high stalk rot resistance, moderate lodging resistance, and high density tolerance, making it an ideal material for studying lodging resistance mechanisms under dense planting conditions. The recommended planting density for this variety is 60,000–65,000 plants ha⁻1; therefore, D1 (60,000 plants ha⁻1) represents the optimal density, while D2 (90,000 plants ha⁻1) represents a high-density stress treatment. Fertilization followed local high-yield management practices. Phosphorus (P₂O₅, 108 kg ha⁻1) and potassium (K₂O, 126 kg ha⁻1) fertilizers were applied as a single basal dressing before sowing. Nitrogen fertilizer (total 252 kg N ha⁻1) was split into two applications: 50% as basal fertilizer before sowing and 50% as topdressing at the jointing stage, with the ratio of 1:1. Weeds, insects, and diseases were effectively controlled throughout both growing seasons to prevent yield loss. Sampling and measurements

Lodging rate

Four rows at the R6 (grain at physiological maturity stage) in the middle of each plot were selected to calculate the maize lodging rate, and then the number of lodged maize plants and total maize plants was recorded.

Yield and yield components

At the physiological maturity stage, the actual plant density for each treatment was investigated. Maize rows exhibiting a complete stand with no missing plants and uniform growth were selected for harvest. For each planting pattern, 10-m sections were separately harvested from the border rows and the inner rows. From the harvested ears, 30 ears were randomly selected to determine the kernel number per ear. All ears were then shelled, and the total grain weight was recorded. Subsequently, the thousand-kernel weight (TKW) and grain moisture content were measured, based on which the final grain yield was calculated.

Plant, ear, and center of gravity height

At 10 days after silking (10 DAS), plant height and ear height were measured on 10 consecutive plants, and the length from the ground to the top of the male spike and the first node with the ear was considered the height of the plant and ear, respectively. 10 maize plants were randomly selected from each plot and cut at ground level to determine the center of gravity height using a balance.

Grain filling characteristics

Before the VT (tasseling stage) stage in maize, uniformly growing plants were tagged per plot to record silking dates. From 10DAS until 60 DAS (physiological maturity), sampling was conducted at 10-day intervals. Three ears per plot were collected, and 100 kernels from the middle of the ear were dried at 75℃ to constant weight for 100-kernel weight determination. Using days after silking (t) as the independent variable and 100-kernel weight measured every 10 DAS as the dependent variable, grain filling was modeled using the logistic equation W = A/(1 + Be−ᶜᵗ). Internode mechanical strength and biochemical composition

Rind Penetration Strength (RPS)

At the VT and R3 (milking stage) stages, the rind penetration strength of the maize stalk's third internode was measured using a stalk strength tester (Zhejiang Top Instrument Co., Ltd., Model YYD-1). Measurements were taken at three points (upper, middle, and lower sections) of the internode and averaged. Unit: N mm⁻2.

Crushing Strength (CS)

At the VT and R3 stages, the crushing strength at the middle section of the maize stalkʼs fourth internode was measured using the stalk strength tester. Unit: N mm⁻2.

Bending Strength (BS)

At the VT and R3 stages, the bending strength at the middle section of the maize stalk's fifth internode was measured using the stalk strength tester. Unit: N.

Stalk biochemical composition determination

The cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin content of the 3rd, 4th, and 5th internodes were determined using a near-infrared analyzer (Beijing Bruker Technology Co., Ltd.).

Statistical analysis

Comparisons among treatments were performed using Duncan's multiple range test. Data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS (version 25.0) (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant. Calculations and linear regressions were performed using SigmaPlot 14.0 (Systat Software, Inc., Dundas, CA, USA). Mantel tests were performed using the R package 'ggcor' (R version 4.1.2). To analyze the effects of plant growth regulator application under different planting densities on maize root traits, stalk traits, lodging rate, yield components, and final grain yield, a structural equation model (SEM) was developed in Amos (Version 23.0). The best-fit model was determined using chi-square, P-values, goodness-of-fit index (GFI), and root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA). Maize lodging incidence in 2020 increased significantly by 22.1% (p < 0.05) compared with 2019. Among planting densities, high-density conditions resulted in significantly higher lodging rates. Regarding PGRs treatments: under high-density conditions in 2019, T10 exhibited the lowest lodging incidence, showing a 3.5% reduction compared to CK. Yield and yield components

Across both growing seasons (2019–2020), grain yield and its components were influenced by both planting density and PGRs. Increasing density enhanced grain yield and significantly raised ear number (p < 0.05), while simultaneously reducing kernels per ear and the TKW. At D2, grain yield increased by 5.0% compared to D1 across all treatments. Following PGRs application, the effects on yield differed markedly between application timings. At D1, neither T10 nor T15 significantly affected grain yield compared with CK. At D2, T10 significantly decreased kernels per ear by 5.6% and TKW by 6.4%, but increased harvested ears per hectare by 11.0% compared with CK; as a result, grain yield under T10 showed no significant change relative to CK. Grain-filling characteristics

Grain-filling process

Grain dry weight exhibited a slow–fast–slow S-shaped accumulation pattern during kernel development in both cultivars, and it decreased consistently as planting density increased. The rapid-filling phase spanned from 0 to 40 d after anthesis, after which grain growth entered a slow-filling phase. Differences in grain dry weight among densities were highly significant; under D1, the weight was 8.6% higher than under D2. At D1, T10 produced the lowest grain weight throughout grain filling, averaging 4.5% below the CK, whereas the T15 gave the highest weight, exceeding the CK by 4.0%. The grain-filling process of maize can be divided into three stages: the gradual increase period, the rapid increase period, and the slow increase period. The study found that when PGRs were applied at the 10-leaf stage, the duration of the rapid and slow increase periods was shortened compared with the CK, while the duration of the gradual increase period was extended. In contrast, application at the 15-leaf stage showed the opposite trend. In terms of grain weight increase, the weight gain in each filling stage (gradual, rapid, and slow increase periods) under T10 was lower than that of CK, while it was significantly higher after T15, with consistent trends observed under both planting densities. Regarding the grain-filling rate, under low planting density, T10 increased the average filling rate during the rapid and slow increase periods compared with CK, but reduced it during the gradual increase period. The effect of the T15 was opposite to that of T10. Under high planting density, T10 decreased the average filling rate in the gradual increase period, but increased it in the rapid and slow increase periods. Compared with CK, T15 showed higher average filling rates across the gradual, rapid, and slow increase periods (Table S1).

Plant morphological characteristics

Both planting density and the timing of PGRs application significantly influence maize plant height, ear height, center of gravity height, stem diameter, and ear position coefficient (p < 0.01). Under varying planting density conditions, the D2 resulted in increases of 3.2%, 10.8%, 10.8%, and 7.1% in plant height, ear height, center of gravity height, and ear height coefficient, respectively, when compared to D1. Discussion

Effect of PGRs application at different maize growth stages on lodging resistance

Lodging is one of the primary factors contributing to maize yield reduction, and the lodging resistance of stalks is positively correlated with stalk strength [24]. Stalk puncture strength, crushing strength, and bending resistance are indicators of stalk strength and stalk plumpness. These mechanical property metrics directly reflect stalk lodging resistance and are crucial for evaluating and assessing stalk quality. As planting density increases beyond a certain threshold, maize stalks may experience issues such as excessive elongation, an upward shift in the center of gravity, thinner and weaker stalks, reduced internode unit length, and decreased dry matter weight, ultimately resulting in higher lodging rates and diminished yields [25]. Furthermore, high planting density can lead to elongated and thinner basal internodes, accompanied by significant reductions in the stalk diameter coefficient, puncture strength, bending resistance, and crushing strength. This study found that increasing planting density increased plant height, ear height, and center of gravity height, along with basal internode length. However, the puncture strength, crushing strength, and bending resistance of the stalks significantly decreased, while the contents of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin were reduced, resulting in an increased lodging rate.

Chemical control technology has been shown to effectively enhance maize plant morphology and population structure, increase stalk thickness, and decrease the center of gravity and basal internode length [26]. In this study, the application of PGRs at the 10-leaf stage significantly reduced the length of internodes below the ear, leading to decreased plant height, ear height, and center of gravity height, while simultaneously improving stalk quality. Furthermore, the content of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin in the stalks increased, and the lodging rate significantly decreased. The robust fibrous network structure of plant cell walls provides mechanical support for the cells and the entire plant. As the primary component of the cell wall, cellulose plays a crucial role in maintaining the mechanical strength of the stalk. Research indicates that higher cellulose content in crop stalks correlates with greater lodging resistance [27]. Our results demonstrated that the enhanced lodging resistance under T10 was strongly associated with improved stalk mechanical properties and cell wall chemical composition. Specifically, compared with CK under high density (D2), T10 significantly increased rind penetration strength by 19.2% and bending strength by 24.6% at the R3 stage (Table 4). This mechanical reinforcement was underpinned by substantial modifications in cell wall components: T10 elevated lignin content by an average of 14.8% across the 3rd to 5th internodes, and increased cellulose and hemicellulose contents by 3.1% and 23.8%, respectively, relative to CK (Table 5). These quantitative linkages confirm that early chemical regulation (V10) promotes the deposition of structural carbohydrates, thereby thickening the stalk rind and enhancing its resistance to external bending forces, which ultimately reduced lodging by 37.2% under dense planting conditions (Fig. 2).

The ten-leaf stage is a critical period for the development of stalk strength in maize, during which the plant's growth is predominantly focused on vegetative development. At this stage, the cells in the stalk are actively dividing and elongating. The application of PGRs during this phase can significantly inhibit gibberellin levels in the plant, leading to a marked suppression of cell elongation in the basal internodes of the stalk, resulting in shorter and sturdier internodes. Furthermore, the inhibition of gibberellin facilitates the regulation of other hormones, such as auxin and ethylene, as well as various metabolic pathways [28]. This regulation promotes the synthesis and deposition of structural components, including cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, in the cell walls, thereby thickening the stalk walls and enhancing mechanical strength. In contrast, when PGRs are applied at the 15-leaf stage, while they do increase stem mass and cellulose content, this stage coincides with the most vigorous phase of both vegetative and reproductive growth in maize. Consequently, the application at this stage primarily hinders the development of the upper and middle internodes, resulting in reduced stem strength and lower concentrations of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin compared to plants treated at the ten-leaf stage. As a result, these plants had higher lodging rates than those treated at the 10‑leaf stage. Importantly, the T15 treatment also increased lodging resistance, but its effects on stalk puncture strength and lignin content were markedly weaker than those of T10 (Tables 4, 5), likely because the V15 stage coincides with the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth, when internode elongation has already progressed and cell wall thickening is less responsive to gibberellin inhibitors.

In 2020, the northeastern region of China experienced the impact of three strong typhoons, leading to a significantly higher lodging rate compared to 2019. This study found no correlation between lodging rate and yield. The analysis suggests that lodging occurred during the late grain‑filling stage, which had little effect on grain weight. Furthermore, manual harvesting was employed for plot yield measurement, resulting in fewer missed ears and consequently a smaller impact on yield. Previous studies indicate that mechanical harvesting after lodging can lead to harvest losses and significant ear loss, thereby reducing yield [29]. The SEM also showed that under high planting density, the application timing had a significant direct negative effect on the lodging rate, meaning that applying PGRs directly reduced lodging. Moreover, stalk traits exhibited a significant positive effect on the lodging rate, indicating that stronger stalks led to lower lodging.

Additionally, improving root system architecture serves as a crucial pathway for PGRs to enhance lodging resistance. High-density planting intensifies competition among plants for light, water, and nutrients, with root dry weight under D2 decreasing by 21.8% compared to D1, thereby restricting root development. Following the application of PGRs, T10 treatment significantly increased root projection area, root density, and root dry weight under D2. This suggests that early chemical control enhances the soil anchoring capacity of the root system by promoting the formation of lateral root primordia and the proliferation of root hairs, ultimately reducing the risk of “root lodging.” In contrast, the T15 treatment exhibited a weaker effect on root improvement, potentially due to the later application period and a decline in the activity of root meristems. The SEM indicates that the direct effect of root traits on lodging rate is slightly smaller than that of stalk traits. This demonstrates that under high planting density, the reduction in lodging rate by PGRs is primarily achieved by improving the physical strength and chemical composition of the stalks, while the direct lodging resistance effect from root optimization is relatively secondary.

Effects of PGRs at different maize growth stages on plant height and root system architecture

Optimizing plant architecture and root system configuration is a key mechanism by which chemical control enhances lodging resistance and yield [30]. Research indicates that PGRs significantly influence population structure and resource utilization efficiency by regulating plant morphology and root development; however, their effects vary depending on the timing of application and planting density.

The alteration of plant architecture by PGRs primarily results from their inhibitory effects on stem growth. Experimental data demonstrate that PGR application at the 10‑leaf stage under high density significantly reduces plant height, ear height, and center of gravity height while increasing stem diameter. This finding aligns with the mechanism by which PGRs inhibit gibberellin synthesis: the reduction of gibberellin directly suppresses the longitudinal elongation of internodal cells while promoting transverse cell division, resulting in shortened and thickened stems [31]. Furthermore, the increase in stem puncture strength and crushing strength suggests that the thickening of stems is accompanied by the accumulation of cell wall components, such as cellulose and lignin, thereby enhancing mechanical strength. In comparison, spraying at the 15-leaf stage exhibited a weaker regulatory effect on plant height; however, it optimized the spatial distribution of the ear by shortening the internodes at the ear position. This adjustment reduced shading within the population and indirectly enhanced the photosynthetic efficiency of the middle and lower leaf layers.

Additionally, improving the configuration of the root system, which serves as the key organ for plant anchorage and nutrient absorption, is another vital approach to enhancing lodging resistance. High-density planting resulted in restricted root development, with root dry weight decreasing by 21.8% compared to D1. A particularly noteworthy finding in our study is the density-dependent response of root traits to PGR application. Under low-density conditions (D1), neither T10 nor T15 induced significant changes in root area, root density, or root dry weight (Fig. 5). This contrasts sharply with the pronounced effects observed under high-density (D2), where T10 dramatically increased root projection area, root density, and particularly root dry weight.

This discrepancy can be attributed to the differential intensity of intra-specific competition between the two densities. Under D1, individual plants have ample access to light, water, and nutrients, maintaining high endogenous cytokinin and auxin levels that likely saturate root meristem activity, thereby diminishing the exogenous regulatory effects of ethephon. Conversely, under D2, intensified root competition and shoot shading trigger a stress-induced increase in root ethylene sensitivity. Under such competitive stress, foliar-applied ethephon synergistically stimulates the formation of lateral root primordia and crown root branching, significantly enhancing the soil anchorage capacity and root pulling resistance, which is particularly critical for preventing 'root lodging' under typhoon-associated waterlogged conditions. The weaker response under T15 further supports this notion, as by the V15 stage, the root system has largely reached its plateau of development, and meristematic activity has declined, limiting the window for morphological modification.

Following the application of PGRs, T10 significantly increased the root projection area, root density, and root dry weight, whereas the T15 only improved the projection area. This discrepancy may be attributed to the timing of spraying, which affects root meristem activity: at the 10-leaf stage, coinciding with the rapid expansion phase of the root system, PGRs (such as ethephon) promote the extension of the root system into deeper soil layers by inducing the formation of lateral root primordia and the proliferation of root hairs, thereby establishing a more stable 'anchoring' configuration. In contrast, when sprayed at the 15-leaf stage, the activity of root meristems has already diminished, limiting the stimulating effect of PGRs on the root system.

Effects of PGRs at different maize growth stages on grain filling and yield

Grain‑filling characteristics are strongly influenced by post‑flowering photosynthetic products and are closely associated with maize yield. The grain filling rate and the duration of the filling period jointly determine the accumulation of dry matter in grains, which represents a critical growth stage for yield formation and directly affects grain plumpness [32]. Research has shown that insufficient light during grain filling can decrease the grain‑filling rate in wheat, severely inhibiting photosynthesis and grain development, thereby reducing both yield and quality. Defoliation experiments have revealed that moderately reducing growth redundancy can enhance light energy use efficiency, extend the active filling period, and consequently improve yield [33]. Furthermore, studies on the application of PGRs and the judicious increase of nitrogen fertilizer have indicated that these measures can enhance the light and thermal utilization efficiency of the maize population, increase the endogenous hormone content in the kernels, improve the grain filling rate, and ultimately promote yield increases [34].

The findings of this study indicate that the application of PGRs at the 15-leaf stage enables grains to achieve the maximum filling rate in a relatively short period, while simultaneously delaying leaf senescence and maintaining photosynthetic function. This approach effectively extends the active grain-filling period, enhancing the average filling rate and increasing the growth amount at the maximum filling stage. Despite a slight reduction in Gmax in certain comparisons, T15 significantly extended the active grain-filling period (P) by 2.3 days under D2 compared with CK, and increased the weight at maximum filling rate Wmax by 7.6% (Table 2; Table S1). Moreover, Table S1 reveals that T15 enhanced the mean filling rate during the early (v1) and rapid (v2) stages, whereas T10 shortened the duration of the rapid filling stage (T2). This indicates that late-stage PGRs application (V15) optimizes the source-sink balance by maintaining higher photosynthetic activity of the upper leaves during the critical kernel-filling window, thereby prolonging the effective filling duration and achieving greater thousand-kernel weight, which offset the kernel number reduction caused by high density.

Consequently, this boosts the accumulation of dry matter in grains and the TKW, ultimately leading to an increase in yield. Conversely, the application of PGRs at the 10-leaf stage results in a reduction in the number of kernels per ear and a decrease in thousand-kernel weight. This seemingly contradictory result can be explained by the source‑sink balance mechanism in maize [35]. Specifically, the application of PGRs at the 10-leaf stage prematurely inhibits stem elongation. While this enhances lodging resistance, it severely restricts leaf expansion and the photosynthetic area, leading to an insufficient supply of assimilates during the spike differentiation period, which significantly reduces the number of grains per spike. At the 10-leaf stage, the tassel enters the spikelet differentiation phase. The application of PGRs during this period not only inhibits internode elongation but also hampers the development of the tassel's main axis and branches. Consequently, this leads to shortened rachises, more densely arranged spikelets, poor anther development, and reduced pollen viability, ultimately affecting the fertilization process [36]. In contrast, when PGRs are applied at the 15-leaf stage, the plant has progressed into the initial phase of reproductive growth. At this stage, the inhibitory effects of PGRs on vegetative growth are less pronounced, while the shortening of the upper internode length enhances canopy light penetration, promotes the translocation of photosynthetic products to the ear, and ultimately increases both the number of grains and the TKW [37].

The interaction between density and chemical control is another core factor regulating yield. Under low-density conditions, chemical control treatments have no significant effect on yield, and even T10 leads to a 4.5% reduction in grain weight. This indicates that when the resource competition among individual plants is weak, excessive morphological regulation by PGRs may disrupt the source-sink balance [36], which is detrimental to yield formation. In contrast, under high-density conditions, the yield-enhancing effects of PGRs are primarily achieved through two aspects: first, by reducing lodging losses, indirectly ensuring the number of effective panicles; and second, by optimizing the plant population structure. For example, the T15 treatment enhances plant stress resistance and improves the light energy utilization efficiency of the middle and lower leaves. By extending the photosynthetic period and delaying leaf senescence, it prolongs grain filling and ultimately increases yield.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the timing of PGRs application determines a density‑dependent trade‑off between lodging resistance and yield formation in maize. Under high‑density conditions, early application at the 10‑leaf stage (T10) reduces lodging by 37.2%, primarily through enhancing stalk mechanical strength—with rind penetration and bending strength increasing by 19.2% and 24.6%, respectively, and lignin content rising by 14.8%—while also improving root anchorage, as reflected by a 47.8% increase in root dry weight. In contrast, application at the 15‑leaf stage (T15) increases grain yield by 5.8%, attributed to extending the active grain‑filling period by 2.3 days and raising thousand‑kernel weight by 2.3%. Structural equation modeling further reveals that PGRs reduce lodging primarily via improving stalk traits rather than root traits under high density, highlighting the dominant role of stem reinforcement in lodging resistance.

However, these conclusions are constrained by several limitations. Our findings are derived from a single maize hybrid ('Fumin 985') and a specific PGR formulation (ethephon + chlormequat chloride), and the field experiments were conducted over only two years at one location; therefore, the universality of these strategies across diverse genotypes, PGR types, and agro‑ecological zones remains to be validated. Future research should prioritize (i) multi‑environment and multi‑genotype trials to verify the broad applicability of the V10/V15 strategy; (ii) omics‑based approaches to elucidate the molecular regulatory networks governing stage‑specific hormone signaling and cell wall biosynthesis. Addressing these gaps will transform our empirical observations into a robust and widely applicable framework for sustainable maize production under escalating lodging risks.
