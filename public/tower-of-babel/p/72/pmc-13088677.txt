Evaluation of the impact of malaria and erythrocyte G6PD deficiency on anaemia outcomes among children in Jos Northcentral Nigeria
Authors: Caroline Amuche Okoli, Deborah Isaiah Wamu, Favour Deffi, Philip Dakwar Leshak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnaemia is a major cause of morbidity and mortality globally. Erythrocyte Glucose 6 phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and malaria are leading causes of anaemia. The impact of malaria and erythrocyte G6PD deficiency on anaemia outcomes among children in Jos Northcentral Nigeria has not been ascertained, thus, this study aims to bridge this gap.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study conducted in 2023 among 100 children aged 1–5 years consecutively recruited for the study. 30% Giemsa-stained thick blood film microscopic examination was used to confirm for the presence or absence of malaria parasitaemia. Erythrocyte G6PD activity was determined colorimetrically using ICuBIO Ichaem-535 Chemistry Analyser. Haemoglobin and erythrocyte values were obtained using automated haematology analyzer (Accu cell DX360 3parts). Data were analysed using SPSS version 27. p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsFifty-five (55%) out of the 100 children that participated in the study have malaria parasitaemia and 24 (43.6%; 24/55) of these were G6PD deficient. Forty-nine (49%; 49/100) of the 100 children were G6PD deficient. Twenty-eight (28/100) were anaemic. Malaria and G6PD deficiency have significant impact (F = 7.327; p = 0.002) on anaemia outcomes in Nigeria children. Nine (37.5%) of G6PD deficient malaria positive children were anaemic.ConclusionIn conclusion, this study showed a significant impact of malaria and G6PD deficiency on anaemia outcomes. Consequently, policy on mandatory G6PD screening in children before antimalarial administration in endemic areas is recommended.Keywords: Malaria-induced anaemia, Paediatric G6PD-deficiency, Prevalence, Jos Northcentral Nigeria

Introduction

Anaemia is a major cause of morbidity and mortality globally [1]. Malaria and Glucose-6- phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency are leading causes of anaemia. Malaria is caused by Plasmodium species transmitted via the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito. It is a leading cause of severe anaemia in areas such as Nigeria where it is prevalent [1]. 31.9% of deaths in Nigeria in 2020 were due to malaria [2]. WHO reported 263milliliom cases and 600,000 malaria deaths worldwide in 2023 [3]. 95% of malaria death occurred in the WHO Africa region and 80% in children under 5 years of age [3]. Acute and severe malaria can cause anaemia due to oxidative lysis of erythrocytes [4]. This is worsened in G6PD deficient children with resultant more severe anaemia and mortality.

G6PD deficiency, an X-linked chromosomal disorder of the erythrocytes affects more than 400 million people worldwide, representing an overall 4.9% global prevalence [5]. It is the most common enzyme deficiency in the world [6] with annual prevalence of 20% in Nigeria [7]. In 2019, G6PD deficiency resulted in the death of 13,000 people across all age groups and 367 children under the age of five [8].

G6PD is an essential hexose monophosphate pathway enzyme. It is responsible for keeping adequate amount of the co-enzyme nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) within the cells. NADPH maintains the cell level of glutathione which protects the red blood cells membrane from possible oxidative damage caused by some foods, vigorous exercise, diabetes, myocardial infarction, infectious agent such as malaria and certain drugs (including antimalaria) [9, 10]. G6PD deficiency results in reduced glutathione and NADPH concentrations thereby, leading to oxidation of haemoglobin to methaemoglobin, membrane damage, haemolysis with consequent anaemia [11]. In G6PD deficient individuals, treatment of malaria with some oxidative drugs such as primaquine, dapsone etc., can lead to life-threatening acute intravascular haemolysis followed by anaemia, acute renal failure and other complications [12–14]. Anaemia can also, negatively affect child’s cognitive development and academic performance [15].

Thus, it is necessary to determine the rate of occurrence of anaemia and malaria-induced anaemia in erythrocyte G6PD deficient children as there is a dearth of information on this. This becomes much more important in the face of the notion that G6PD deficiency renders protection against malaria [16]. Knowledge gained from this study will not only add to the pool of knowledge on this subject matter, but can influence the choice of antimalarial administration in this part of the region and serve as a guide in designing malaria intervention approaches. It may also highlight the need to test individuals for G6PD deficiency before administration of oxidative drugs such as primaquine or dapsone and to include G6PD test as part of routine screening tests for neonates. Inclusion of G6PD test as part of routine screening tests for neonates will help in early detection. In time of crisis, prior knowledge of patient’s G6PD status will aid in accurate and prompt treatment thereby helping to reduce morbidity and mortality.

Methods

Study design

This research was a hospital based comparative cross-sectional study.

Study setting

The study was carried out in paediatric Clinic/Unit/wards of Jos University Teaching Hospital (JUTH), identified with letter “H” in Fig. 1 (https://www.google.com/maps/). JUTH is a tertiary hospital located 9° 55’ 42” N (latitude) and 8° 54’ 31” E (longitude), at Lamingo in Jos, Plateau state, Northcentral Nigeria [17].

Map of Jos showing the study site, Jos University Teaching Hospital represented by letter “H”.

Source: (https://www.google.com/maps/)

Study population

One hundred children aged 0 to 5 years who met the study inclusion criteria were consecutively recruited for the study from February to June 2023. Malaria parasite test, erythrocyte G6PD activity test, haemoglobin and erythrocyte concentrations determination were carried out in all the children. Erythrocyte G6PD activity reference range was 6.97–20.5 U/gHb (at 37 °C) for children using Randox Kit (England) [18]. Children with erythrocyte G6PD activity lower than 6.97 U/gHb were considered G6PD deficient [18].

The reference range for haemoglobin concentration for children aged ≤ 5 years is ≥ 11.0 g/dl [19]. Children with haemoglobin concentration lower than 11 g/dl were considered anaemic [19] in this study.

Sample size determination

To obtain data that is scientifically acceptable, the minimum sample size for the study was calculated using the formula by Thrustfield [20]. Based on the results of previous studies, the expected prevalence of G6PD deficiency in our study setting was 20% [7].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

One hundred children aged 0 to 5 years were included in the study based on the following criteria:

Children clinically presenting with or without the signs of anaemia. Children with symptoms of malaria or G6PD deficiency. Malaria was clinically diagnosed by the paediatrician as the presence of one or more symptoms of malaria: fever, chills, diarrhoea, bitter taste, anaemia, respiratory distress, jaundice etc. Children whose parents/legal guardians gave their consent. Children not meeting the above listed criteria, and/or had other co-infections or chronic disease, and/or already on antimalarial or had antimalaria two weeks prior to the study were excluded from the study. Structured questionnaire was used to collect data on clinical and demographic status of the children and parents’ socioeconomic status.

Sample collection

Using aseptic procedure 3 ml of whole blood was collected from the antecubital fossa into an EDTA (Ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid) anticoagulant bottle by the paediatrician and the medical laboratory scientist. The sample was well mixed and labeled appropriately. Analysis was carried out immediately in the Research Laboratory of Special Care Baby Unit of the Department of Paediatrics Jos University Teaching Hospital.

Laboratory malaria diagnosis

Thick blood films were made and stained using 30% Giemsa stain [21]. They were examined microscopically using x100 objective.

Parasite density was calculated as follows [22].

Erythrocyte G6PD activity assay

Glucose-6-phospate dehydrogenase activity estimation was carried out quantitatively with RANDOX Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase test kit base on manufacturer instruction using ICuBIO Ichaem-535 Chemistry Analyzer as described by Pfeffer et al. [18].

Principle

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G-6-PDH) oxidizes glucose-6-Phosphate (G-6-P-D) to 6-phosphogluconate (6-PG) and reducing NADP to NADPH: The rate of formation of NADPH is proportional to the G6PDH activity and is measured as increase in absorbance at 340 nm. Production of a second molar equivalent of NADPH by erythrocyte 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (6-PGDH) is prevented by use of maleimide, an inhibitor of 6-PGDH.

Haemoglobin and erythrocyte estimation

Haemoglobin and erythrocyte estimation were done using haematology analyzer (Accu cell DX360 3parts) as was described by Pfeffer et al. [18].

Principle of haemoglobin concentration determination

Blood is mixed with Drabkin solution which contain potassium ferricyanide and potassium cyanide, potassium ferricyanide react with the blood to form methaemoglobin which is converted to cyamethaemoglobin by cyanide. All other forms of haemoglobin except sulf haemoglobin are converted to cyanmethaemoglobin.

Calculation of G6PD activity

Key: 1000 = Factor to convert activity to 100 mL, Hb (g/dL) = haemoglobin concentration for each sample. Erythrocyte value in 109/L.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained were analyzed using statistical package for social science (SPSS) version 27. Z-test and ANOVA were used to analyze for the numerical variables. Chi-square was used for the categorical variables. Linear regression analysis was used to determine the impact of malaria and G6PD deficiency on anaemia. Results were presented in tables as frequency, mean and standard deviation. p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Age and gender distribution of the study participants by malaria status, parasite density and G6PD status

One hundred children aged 0 to 5 years participated in the study. Fifty –five (55/100) children had malaria parasitaemia. Forty (40%) out of 78 (78.0%) children aged 0–1 year had malaria; 28(28.0%) of these have high parasitaemia (parasite density > 200 × 103parasites/µl).

Forty-nine (49/100) of the children were G6PD deficient. Forty (40%) of the G6PD deficient children were aged less than one year. Twenty-four (24%) out of the 49 G6PD deficient children had malaria parasitemia.

Fifty- three (53/100) of the 100 study children were females. Thirty-four (34%) out of the 55 malaria positive children were females with 15(15%) having high parasitaemia.

27 out of the 49 (49%) G6PD deficient children were males out of which 13 (13.0%) had malaria parasitaemia (Table 1).

Mean hb concentration, G6PD activity and parasite density by age and gender

Haemoglobin level decreased progressively with increase in age except at > 1 to 2 years where there was a slight rise (10.8 ± 0.28 to 11.1 ± 0.52 g/dl). There was no significant difference in the haemoglobin level, G6PD activity and malaria parasite density by age.

Haemoglobin concentration was significantly (p < 0.05) lower in the females compared to the males (10.5 ± 0.24 versus 10.90 ± 0.18 g/dl). G6PD activity and parasite density were significantly (p < 0.05) higher in females (9.24 ± 0.70 U/gHb; 407 ± 43 × 103parasites/µl) compared to males (7.85 ± 0.72 U/gHb; 378 ± 45 × 103parasites/µl) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study was conducted to determine the impact of malaria and erythrocyte G6PD deficiency on anaemia. Malaria and G6PD deficiency are of public health concern. They are among the leading cause of anaemia [6]. The study reveals a high prevalence of anaemia, malaria infestation and G6PD deficiency at 28%, 55.0% and 49.0% respectively in this part of Nigeria. Malaria and G6PD deficiency have significant (F = 7.327; p = 0.002) impact on anaemia outcomes. Malaria induced anaemia was present in 9(9/24; 37.5%) of the G6PD deficient children.

The high prevalence of malaria obtained in this study is not a surprise as Nigeria is a malaria endemic country. Nigeria is at the top in WHO global malaria endemic ranking despite some measures being taken in the country to curtail it [3]. G6PD deficiency has been reported to be high in malaria endemic regions because of the failure of the parasite to thrive and survive in the low redox environment of erythrocyte G6PD deficient cells [16].

The prevalence of erythrocyte G6PD deficiency (49.0%) obtained from this study (Table 1) compares favourably with 40.7% reported by Jatau et al. [24] in a similar study conducted among newborns in Jos, North-central Nigeria. The high prevalence raises concern and need for caution when using antimalarial drugs such as primaquine or other oxidative compounds, as this can cause oxidative stress and acute haemolytic crisis in G6PD deficient children.

In addition, the high prevalence of G6PD deficiency among children with and those without malaria (24.0% and 25.0% respectively; p > 0.05) (Table 1) obtained from this study calls for attention and caution in the interpretation and application of the widely accepted notion, that G6PD deficiency confers immunity to malaria [25, 26]. Notwithstanding, large scale longitudinal studies conducted in different regions using molecular methods are necessary to assess the roles and interactions between malaria and G6PD deficiency polymorphisms.

Furthermore, understanding the influence of genes on malaria susceptibility in humans is very important. The relationship may influence the outcome of any intervention for malaria. Non- significant difference in the prevalence of malaria among G6PD deficient and G6PD normal children may suggest that this mutation might not influence susceptibility to malaria. Our finding agrees with the report of Bwayo et al. [27] in Uganda. Therefore, it could be important to include G6PD screening test among the battery of routine tests for children with or without malaria. This will help in early detection and prompt treatment of G6PD deficient children especially during acute haemolytic episode which needs urgent attention.

Additionally, from the study, age was found not to be a significant factor in the occurrence of G6PD deficiency. G6PD deficiency is not age dependent rather it is genetic [28].

Conversely, by gender, more of the males were G6PD deficient (55.1%) compared to the females (44.9%, p > 0.05). This is in consistent with several other studies [25, 29]. There is higher susceptibility of males to G6PD deficiency than females owing to the X-linked inheritance G6PD deficient alleles (single nucleotide proteins) and the hemizygous nature of the X-chromosome in males. This was further buttressed by the fact that G6PD activity was significantly (p < 0.05) higher in females compared to males (Table 2).

Malaria and G6PD deficiency are inducers of anaemia [6]. From our study, both have significant impact on anaemia outcomes (F = 7.327; p = 0.002). Malaria infestation triggers acute haemolytic crisis in G6PD deficient individuals and administration of oxidative antimalarial drugs such as primaquine or dapsone to G6PD deficient individuals with or without anaemia can lead to life-threatening complications with renal involvement and even death [14]. Therefore, it is important to determine the prevalence of malaria-induced anaemia in G6PD deficient children in this malaria endemic region. From our study, the prevalence of malaria-induced anaemia in G6PD deficient children was 37.5%. This high prevalence calls for attention to intensify mass enlightenment on malaria prevention and control measures, G6PD deficiency trait and disease, the risk factors and prevention. It also implies that administration of antimalarial drugs such as primaquine or dapsone should be preceded by G6PD test. It also suggests the importance of including G6PD test as part of screening tests for neonates for early detection. This could help in prompt and accurate treatment of such individuals especially during acute haemolytic crisis which usually needs urgent attention.

Conclusion

The study has shown that malaria and G6PD deficiency have significant impact on anaemia outcomes. Also, the prevalence of malaria-induced anaemia in G6PD deficient children in Jos, North central Nigeria was 37.5%. Our results suggest that there may be a need for inclusion of G6PD screening test as part of routine screening tests for neonates. Policy on mandatory G6PD screening before antimalarial administration in endemic areas is recommended. However, more robust studies in some other endemic areas is recommended for generalization of the finding.

Limitation of the study

This study was limited by low financial availability, for example we were not able to conduct a molecular analysis of malaria parasite and G6PD. This could have helped to differentiate the G6PD genotypes. Also, most of our study subjects were less than 1 year. These may limit the accuracy and generalization of our findings. Thus, more robust study on the subject matter is recommended.
