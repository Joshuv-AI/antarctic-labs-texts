Influence of wind barrier configuration on driving space over bridge decks: A PIV-based parametric study
Authors: Dandan Xia, Shaokun Shi, Zhixin Liu, Xiaobo Lin, Li Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWind-induced vibrations, such as vortex-induced vibrations, occur on long-span bridges because of their flexibility, especially for bridges with centrally slotted box girders, which may severely affect structural safety and driving comfort. In this research, the influence of wind barrier parameters on the wind field of the driving space on a centrally slotted bridge deck under crosswind was experimentally investigated. Particle image velocimetry (PIV) was employed in wind tunnel tests conducted in the low-speed test section to obtain the wind field on the bridge deck. The effects of wind barriers on the average wind speed, turbulent kinetic energy (TKE), and mean vorticity were analyzed under both with-vehicle and without-vehicle conditions. The effects of the height, porosity, and layout of the wind barriers on the driving space were also quantitatively presented by the equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients. The results show that vehicles on the bridge deck can induce the formation of vortices and high turbulence in the driving space. The parameters of the wind barriers affect both the distribution and magnitude of the average wind speed on the windward side. The corresponding results are important for the wind barrier design of long-span bridges to ensure driving safety on bridge decks.Keywords: Wind barriers, PIV tests, Centrally slotted bridge deck, CrosswindSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Physics

Introduction

With the trend toward more flexible long-span bridges, their sensitivity to wind loads has become increasingly prominent. The wind field on a bridge deck is a type of localized complex flow formed by the incoming wind flowing around the main girder and ancillary facilities, which may not only affect the wind-induced vibration of the bridge but also influence the driving safety of vehicles on the bridge. Research methods for determining the wind field on a bridge deck include mainly field measurements, wind tunnel tests, and numerical simulations. Most studies have focused on localized bridge deck wind fields, particularly the wind environment near bridge towers1–4, and have shown that bridge towers significantly influence the wind speeds on the deck, resulting in notable effects such as wind acceleration or shielding. Wu et al.3 quantified the interference of the bridge structure on the wind field on a bridge deck by establishing a conversion relationship between the incoming wind speed and the local wind speed on the bridge deck and proposed an attenuation law for the bridge deck wind speed along the longitudinal direction of the bridge. Wang et al.5 analyzed the effects of crosswind on vehicles on a bridge and showed that the lateral force coefficient and yaw moment coefficient of moving vehicles are lower than those of static vehicles. Additionally, Charuvisit et al.6 considered the influence of different vehicle types and bridge tower shapes on the variations in vehicle aerodynamic forces when passing a bridge tower. Kim et al.7 combined wind tunnel section model tests with long-term field-measured wind data at a bridge site to analyze the influence of vehicle type, lane position, and vehicle speed on vehicle stability under crosswind conditions and developed a procedure for assessing the crosswind risk for vehicles operating on a bridge deck.

In addition to the aforementioned research methods, PIV, a flow field visualization technique with advantages such as minimal interference with the flow field and high visualization capabilities8,9, has been applied to the study of wind field characteristics on bridge decks. To analyze flow fields around twin box girders, Zhang et al.10 discussed the computational results of square cylinder sections and twin-box girder sections under different sizes of control volumes and space resolutions. The evolution patterns and motion characteristics of periodic vortex structures in the flow around bridge deck sections were revealed through numerical simulations and PIV experiments11. Chen et al.12 conducted PIV experiments on twin box girders with varying gap ratios between the girders. The results show that the gap ratio can alter the vortex shedding positions, significantly increase turbulence in the gap region, and increase the fluctuating pressure on the leeward girder. In Yang et al.13 research, PIV experiments were performed to elucidate the aerodynamic stabilization mechanism of centrally slotted box girders, which revealed that the effectiveness of central slotting in box girders in improving aerodynamic stability depends on the slot width and girder shape. The influence of attachments such as wind barriers, guardrails, and crash barriers was also investigated14. Ma et al.15 examined the effect of grid plates on the suppression of vortex-induced vibrations in twin box girders using PIV and effectively suppressed the vortex-induced vibrations of twin box girder bridges by optimizing design parameters such as the porosity of the grid plates.

As a common attached structure on the bridge deck, wind barriers may cause vibration of the bridge deck, such as vortex-induced vibration16,17, due to the shedding of vortexes. However, because of their wind-resistance performance, wind barriers are also commonly applied on bridges in coastal regions to reduce the wind speed on the bridge deck, which can improve the driving stability for vehicles. Numerous studies have investigated the improvement of driving safety by wind barriers18–21 and the influence of wind barrier parameters on the bridge deck wind field and vehicle driving stability21–25. Moreover, researchers have applied PIV technology to study the detailed changes in the flow field on a bridge deck caused by wind barriers. For instance, Ávila-Sánchez et al.26 explored the influence of wind barriers and incoming wind attack angles on the mean wind speed and turbulence distribution in the driving area above a bridge deck through PIV wind tunnel tests. Kozmar et al.27 also applied PIV experiments to investigate the effect of wind barrier installation on bridge deck wind field characteristics and reported that it significantly reduced the wind speed in the near-wall region of the deck. Subsequent research further revealed that increased porosity increases the wind speed on the deck, especially in the downstream area of the windward side, while simultaneously reducing vorticity28. Under strong winds, vortices at the top of the wind barrier tend to approach the leeward side deck surface, increasing the height of the wind barrier can reduce the mean wind speed and move the vortices away from the wall29.

Existing studies have generally shown that structural elements such as wind barriers and bridge towers significantly influence the wind field on a bridge deck, particularly in altering the distribution of the wind speed. However, current research is mostly limited to the effects of individual factors, and studies on the evolution of the wind field under the coupled influence of multiple factors, such as bridge deck attachments and vehicles, are lacking. In this research, the wind field of the driving space on a bridge deck was considered. With the established sectional model of a centrally slotted box girder of a long-span flexible bridge, PIV wind tunnel tests were conducted to investigate the influence of different wind barrier heights, porosities, and vehicle models on the bridge deck wind environment. The mean velocity, turbulent kinetic energy, vorticity, and equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients for each lane were calculated. The influence of the wind barrier parameters and vehicles is presented, providing a reference for assessing the impact of the bridge deck wind field and evaluating vehicular traffic safety.

Experimental setup

Wind tunnel and PIV system

It is widely accepted that wind barriers may affect the wind field around a vehicle on a bridge deck. Yuan et al.30 have shown that the wind field within a region up to twice the vehicle height is affected by attachments, such as the wind barriers on the bridge deck, as shown in Fig. 1. To investigate the effects of the wind barrier parameters on the wind field around the bridge deck, PIV wind tunnel tests are conducted in the following sections.

Influenced driving space on the bridge deck31.

PIV measurement is one of the methods for obtaining velocity information of the flow field. In this research, an experiment was conducted in the low-speed section of the XMUT-WT Wind Tunnel Laboratory at Xiamen University of Technology. The low-speed test section measured 6 m in width, 3.6 m in height, and 25 m in length, with an adjustable wind speed ranging from 0.5 to 32 m/s. A schematic of the wind tunnel facility showing the PIV system is shown in Fig. 2. As indicated, the PIV system contains a pulsed laser generator, high-speed charge-coupled device (CCD) camera, smoke generator, signal synchronizer, and turbulent flow instrumentation (TFI) cobra probe anemometer for monitoring the incoming wind speed. The laser sheet emitted from the laser head is fan shaped; therefore, it is necessary to place the laser head at a certain distance from the bridge model to allow the width of the laser sheet to develop sufficiently. To minimize interference of the equipment with the flow field around the bridge deck, the laser head was positioned atop the model, and the laser bracket was designed as shown in Fig. 2. The bridge model was securely fixed to the support frame using screws at both ends of the four core beams. The CCD camera and the model were mounted on the same large support frame. The base of the frame consisted of two 80 mm × 160 mm aluminum profiles. Considering its self-weight and the requirement to remain stationary relative to the laboratory under wind flow, the model was assumed to be rigid and fully fixed within the wind field. The laser and cooling unit were placed in the wake region, and the laser was positioned on an elevated support to minimize its influence on the flow field around the bridge.

In the wind tunnel tests, the Reynolds number effect is among the key factors, which may lead to that small-scale vortices and boundary layer development could be underrepresented. It should be mentioned that previous studies also indicated that the effect of the Reynolds number on the wind field around bluff bodies such as railings or bridge decks is not obvious1,4,32,33. Although some studies have found that the aerodynamic performance of structures such as flexible bridges is affected by the Reynolds number, there is no consistent conclusion on the minimum value of the critical Reynolds number14. Hence, the effect of Reynolds number on the current experiment did not constitute a primary concern. In this wind tunnel test, to reduce the boundary effect and avoid the influence of the blocking effect (less than 5% in this study), the model scale ratio needs to be small enough to ensure a twin-box girder with sufficient span. Moreover, it is necessary to avoid vibrations of the laser beam, the CCD camera, and the models caused by high wind speed. Therefore, considering these two factors, the scale ratio for this wind tunnel test is selected as 1:200. The measured width of the flow field within the bridge deck traffic space was taken to be the same as the bridge deck width, and the height was set to 80 mm, corresponding to a prototype scale of 16 m. Given that the typical height of a freight truck is approximately 2.7 m, this selected height exceeds three times the truck height, thereby satisfying the requirement for flow field vertical coverage. The measurement section was located 80 mm downstream from the centerline of the bridge tower, as shown in Fig. 2. The sampling frequency is set at 10 Hz, with an image resolution of 125 pixels/mm. The interrogation area was set to 64 × 64 pixels, corresponding to an actual physical dimension of 8 mm × 8 mm.

Image processing and data acquisition

In the PIV wind tunnel tests, the laser forms a fan-shaped light sheet from the sheet light source, and the tracer particles in the flow field are illuminated by the laser. The moving motion of the particles is recorded. The spatial grid corresponding to the pixels on the camera chip, constructed by the computer based on calibration, is used to reconstruct the spatial vector velocity field by collecting multiple sets of images from different positions and perspectives. The data acquisition and processing procedure is shown in Fig. 8. The image sampling frequency was set at 10 Hz, meaning that 10 pairs of instantaneous particle images were acquired per second. For each test case, a total of 80 image pairs were captured to analyze the instantaneous velocity vector field, with the imaging area covering the key region of the driving space on the bridge deck. The displacement of the particle groups within these interrogation windows was analyzed to determine their velocity, thereby obtaining the velocity distribution across the entire field of view. Further postprocessing of these velocity data yielded distributions of key flow field characteristic parameters, such as the mean velocity, vorticity, and turbulent kinetic energy.

Turbulent kinetic energy

Turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) is an important parameter for describing the turbulence and energy of a fluid and encompasses the total kinetic energy of eddies and vortices. It is primarily used to analyze energy transport, dissipation, and conversion processes within fluid flow. In the flow field, turbulent energy can reflect the intensity and activity of the turbulent structure of the fluid. The magnitude of turbulent energy directly affects the intensity and chaotic nature of turbulence. A larger TKE represents more violent and chaotic turbulence, whereas the flow field is more stable for a smaller TKE. It should be mentioned that the exact definition of TKE requires all three orthogonal velocity fluctuations, whereas 2D PIV only captures in-plane components-thus yielding an underestimation of true TKE. In this research, we mainly focus on the driving space on the bridge deck, therefore, the influence of wind flow along the bridge deck are considered neglected. Also due to the limitation of the experimental setup, we adopted 2-D analysis for the tests, which can also been applied and verified in existing research34,35). Results and discussion

Influence of the porosity of the wind barriers

The porosity is a key parameter for the wind barriers on a bridge and may affect both the wind resistance performance and the economic cost of the barrier. In this research, to show the influence of the porosity of the wind barriers on the wind flow on the bridge deck, wind barrier porosities of 40% and 64% are selected to conduct the PIV wind tunnel test. The influence of porosity on the wind field under both with-vehicle and without-vehicle conditions is compared in Fig. 10 by presenting the average velocity field under different geometric parameters of the wind barriers at an incoming wind speed of 3 m/s. It should be mentioned that, in the tests, the wind barriers were included within the PIV field of view, due to the limitation of small size of wind barriers and the reason that this research mainly focus on the flow characteristics behind the wind barriers, in the plot of wind field on the bridge deck, wind barriers were not included. As indicated in Fig. 10, for the bridge deck with the passenger car, the average wind speed at the upstream side around the passenger car on the bridge deck for 64% porosity case is relatively lower than that the wind speed on the bridge deck for 40% porosity case.

Influence of the height of the wind barriers

To analyze the influence of the wind barrier height on the wind field on the bridge deck, PIV tests on wind barriers with heights of 3 m and 4.5 m are conducted. The average velocity field under different heights of the wind barriers is shown in Fig. 15. From Fig. 15, the difference in average wind speed with heights of 3 m and 4.5 m is not obvious. From the TKE and vorticity in Figs. 16 and 17, when there is a passenger car on the bridge deck, the distribution of the local vortex changes significantly compared with the cases without passenger car. Stronger vortex concentration zones form on the roof, the leeward side of the vehicle, and the tail of the barrier. With a higher wind barrier, larger TKE is distributed in the top region of the bridge deck, while the TKE away from the vehicle decreases. Moreover, a higher wind barrier helps reduce the average vorticity in the driving space which may enhance the driving stability of the vehicles on the bridge deck.

A comparison of the equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients in Lane 1 and Lane 3 for different wind barrier parameters is shown in Fig. 18. Generally, the equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients with vehicles are larger than those without vehicles, indicating that the vehicles can reduce the local wind speed on the bridge deck. With the increase of porosity, the coefficient decreases from 0.56 to 0.35 in Lane 1, but shows slight differences in Lane 2 with coefficients of 0.3 and 0.33 respectively. The blocking effect of vehicles causes an inconspicuous difference in the average wind speed behind the vehicles. For Lane 1 in the upstream, with a height of 3 m, the coefficient decreases from 0.56 to 0.35 with an increase in porosity from 40 to 64%. However, with a height of 4.5 m, the coefficient increases from 0.43 to 0.51 with an increase in porosity from 40 to 64%. They show different influence trends at different heights. For Lane 3 behind the vehicle, with a height of 3 m, the increase in porosity causes a slight change in the equivalent wind speed reduction coefficient from 0.3 to 0.33, However, the difference is larger, increasing from 0.21 to 0.33 when the wind barrier height is 4.5 m. As mentioned previously, the height of the vehicle is 3.7 m. When the height of the wind barrier increases to 4.5 m, the blocking effect of the vehicles on the wind speed is limited, which causes the difference in the wind speed reduction coefficient.

Comparison of equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients for different wind barrier parameters.

Figures 19 and 20 show the contour plot of equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients under different wind speeds with wind barrier heights and porosities at Lanes 1–6. Generally, the wind speeds are smaller behind the vehicle in Lanes 3 to 5. For the incoming wind speeds of 3 m/s and 6 m/s, the change pattern is similar especially for lanes behind the vehicle. However, the change of the coefficient is different in Lane 1 under the two wind speeds when considering vehicles on the bridge deck as shown in Figs. 19b and 20b. A lower wind barrier height of 3 m results in a higher coefficient, as in Fig. 19b, which is different from Fig. 20b, indicating that the presence of the vehicle may affect the flow structure around the vehicle. Generally, the equivalent wind speed reduction coefficient generally decreases with increasing wind barrier height and increases with increasing wind barrier porosity, and the coefficient value is usually smaller for lanes farther from the windward side. The presence of a vehicle significantly reduces the wind speed reduction coefficient in the occupied and adjacent lanes, and wind barriers with greater height and lower porosity result in the best wind protection performance, especially for lanes behind the vehicles.

Influence of the layout of the wind barriers

The wind barriers commonly seen in engineering applications are often installed perpendicular to the bridge deck. However, recently, active wind barriers have been proposed to dynamically adjust the wind direction. Such active wind barriers may require an inclination on the bridge deck. As exploration tests for active wind barriers, PIV tests were conducted for a triangular layout with two panels of wind barriers, as shown in Fig. 21. With an incoming flow of 3 m/s, PIV wind tunnel tests were conducted, and the corresponding results are shown in Figs. 22, 23, 24. Cases 2 and 10 represent wind barriers with 40% porosity: a circular-hole barrier with a perpendicular single-panel layout and a triangular barrier with a double-panel layout, respectively. The mean streamwise velocities for different cases are shown in Fig. 22. As indicated, compared with that of the wind barriers with a perpendicular layout, the height of the recirculation zone slightly increases for the triangular double-panel layout. From the comparison of Fig. 22b and d, the triangular double—panel layout reduces the wind speeds, especially in Lane 1 before the passenger car compared with the perpendicular single—panel layout.

The distributions of TKE in the bridge deck driving space under different experimental conditions are shown in Fig. 23. TKE is often applied to analyze the processes of energy transport, dissipation, and conversion in fluid flow. The magnitude of turbulent energy directly affects the intensity and chaotic nature of turbulence. As shown in Fig. 23, the values of TKE for the case with vehicles are greater than those without vehicles, indicating fluctuating wind flow in the driving space. Comparing the different types of wind barriers, as shown in Fig. 23b and d, the triangular double-panel layout of wind barriers induces greater TKE than the perpendicular single-panel layout, which may also have an adverse effect on the stability of vehicles. That may be caused by the complex interaction between the impinging flow and the dual—panel geometry, and it redirects airflow more effectively than single-panel barriers. However, for the wind field behind the vehicle, the TKE shows similar patterns for the two cases. Similar patterns can also be seen in Fig. 24, which shows the average vorticity under different geometric parameters of the wind barriers. The values of vorticity can reflect vortices in the flow field at different scales. Compared with the perpendicular single-panel layout, the triangular double-panel layout may cut the vortex into smaller scales, and vorticity with a larger magnitude may occur in the driving space. The movement of the vortex can induce instability in the wind flow and therefore affect the stability of vehicles, which is in agreement with the results in Fig. 23. Generally, with the same porosity and height, the TKE for triangular layout case is much higher. The triangular double-panel layout is mainly applied for active wind barriers, therefore, for the design of active wind barriers, the influence of turbulence on vehicles should be prioritized.

Conclusions

To analyze the parametric influence on the wind field over the driving space on a bridge deck, PIV wind-tunnel tests were conducted on a section model of a long-span bridge with different wind barrier parameters. On the basis of the data analysis of the average wind speed, TKE, vorticity, and equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients under different conditions, the following conclusions can be drawn:

In both the cases with and without vehicles, the equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients decrease sequentially along the lanes of the bridge. A comparison of the test results with and without vehicles reveals that the vehicle on the bridge deck has a significant influence on the wind field in the driving space. The wind speeds behind the vehicle decrease significantly, while high TKE and vorticity can be found in the driving space, which adversely affect vehicle driving safety.

In both the cases with and without vehicles, the equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients decrease sequentially along the lanes of the bridge. A comparison of the test results with and without vehicles reveals that the vehicle on the bridge deck has a significant influence on the wind field in the driving space. The wind speeds behind the vehicle decrease significantly, while high TKE and vorticity can be found in the driving space, which adversely affect vehicle driving safety.

The equivalent wind speed reduction coefficients indicate that with increasing wind barrier porosity, the wind speeds on the bridge deck decrease slightly behind the passenger cars. However, the wind speed on the leeward side increases with increasing porosity.

Tests were conducted for active wind barriers that require inclination on the bridge deck. The triangular layout was compared with the perpendicular layout of wind barriers under the same porosity and height. Comparing with the perpendicular single-panel layout, the TKE and vorticity for the triangular double-panel layout are obviously greater. Since the triangular double-panel layout type is often applied for active wind barriers, in the future design of active wind barriers, the influence of turbulence on vehicles should be prioritized.

Generally, in the design of wind barriers, both wind speed and turbulent characteristic should be considered. Such conclusions can provide reference data for the design of wind barriers to increase the driving safety of vehicles on bridge decks. Moreover, this research focuses mainly on the influence of parameters such as porosity and height; in the future, the design of active wind barriers and factors such as the inclination angle should also be considered.
