The effect of boron oxide(B₂O₃) and colemanite(2CaO·3B2O3·5 H2O) additives on the aerating, mechanical, post-fire, and microstructural behavior of perlite-based geopolymer lightweight concrete
Authors: Ali İhsan Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the aerating effect and contribution to mechanical performance of boron oxide (B₂O₃) and colemanite (2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O) in ground raw perlite (GRP)-based lightweight geopolymer concrete (GPLC) were investigated. Recent studies on lightweight geopolymer concrete have shown that, despite the advantage of low density, there are significant limitations in simultaneously achieving environmental sustainability, diverse production methods, adequate mechanical strength, and high-temperature performance. Instead of GRP, boron compounds were used at ratios of 5%, 10%, and 15%; a fixed amount of GGBFS and silica fume (SF) was added to improve bonding. Expanded perlite (EP) aggregate of 2–4 mm was used for lightness, and carbon fiber (CF) at ratios of 0%, 1%, and 2% was used for strength. Additionally, a hyperplasticizer was added at a rate of 1.93% of the total binder and activators to improve workability. The gel structure and phase formation were examined using FE-SEM, EDX, and F-TIR analyses; regression and Taguchi methods determined the optimized parameters. The findings showed that 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O formed a more homogeneous, but less ductile N-A-S-H network, while B₂O₃ provided a denser matrix and higher compressive strength with hybrid N-A-S-H/C-A-S-H gels. In high-temperature tests, both boron additions increased secondary phase formation and thermal stability. The best performance was achieved with a 10% boron content and a 2% CF mixture, offering a balance of low density, high strength, and high post-fire resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52787-y.

Introduction

Geopolymer lightweight concrete (GPLC) is a sustainable building material developed as an environmentally friendly alternative to ordinary Portland cement (OPC)-based concrete1–6. Lightweight concrete with a density below 1920 kg/m³ offers various structural and functional advantages, such as reducing the dead load of structures, facilitating transportation and placement, and improving thermal and acoustic insulation performance7,8. These concretes offer advantages not only because they have lower permeability compared to OPC, but also because they exhibit high temperature resistance9. GPLC has attracted significant attention from researchers in recent years because it reduces environmental impacts by utilizing industrial waste materials in the production process10–15. In these concretes, industrial by-products such as fly ash (FA) and granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) are commonly used for the formation of the binder phase, along with natural and artificial pozzolans such as perlite, vermiculite, basalt powder, marble powder, and zeolite16. Alkali-activated pozzolanic materials have attracted significant attention as promising alternatives to OPC due to their ability to improve welding efficiency and their potential to reduce CO₂ emissions17. The geopolymerization process is typically carried out using alkali activators, such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and sodium silicate (Na₂SiO₃), which enables production with lower carbon emissions and environmental impact compared to OPC-based production2,18,19.

In recent years, significant innovations have been made in GPLC systems to improve workability, mechanical strength, and long-term durability. In this context, the use of advanced materials such as boron oxide (B₂O₃)-based minerals, carbon fiber (CF), and chemical additives is becoming increasingly widespread. Additives such as boron compounds (e.g., Ca₂B₆O₁₁·5 H₂O), ground raw perlite (GRP), GGBFS, CFs, and superplasticizers can facilitate advanced geopolymerization reactions through synergistic interactions with alkali activators, including NaOH and Na₂SiO₃12,20–22. Perlite has an amorphous volcanic glass structure containing high levels of alumina (Al₂O₃) and silica (SiO₂). Due to this property, it is considered a highly reactive precursor material in alkali activation systems23,24. A comprehensive guide on the use of perlite as a building material further supports this selection25. In this context, GRP serves as both a primary source of aluminosilicate and contributes to the thermal and structural performance of concrete due to its lightness and low thermal conductivity. In particular, finely ground GRP promotes the formation of sodium aluminosilicate hydrate (N-A-S-H) gels, increasing the matrix’s binding properties and mechanical strength. A study by Acar et al. (2023) showed that when GRP is activated with NaOH and Na₂SiO₃, it achieves compressive strength values comparable to traditional FA-based systems2.

The integration of components containing B₂O₃ with geopolymer structures can provide significant improvements in both the physical and chemical properties of the binding matrix26. Turkey holds a significant portion of the world’s boron resources, with a reserve ratio of approximately 73%, making it a global leader in this field27. Eti Maden’s sale of 2.65 million tons of refined boron in 2022 not only broke the company’s own record but also increased its share of the global boron market to over 62%. Boron compounds, particularly in the form of Colemanite (2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O), are widely used in the ceramic industry for glaze and frit production, as well as in the cement industry28. The Emet mine in western Turkey contains approximately 40% of the world’s known 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O reserves. A boron variety rich in minerals, 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O, crystallizes in the monoclinic system. According to the Mohs hardness scale, its hardness is 4-4.5, and its specific gravity is 2.42 g/cm3. It is found in large, shiny, transparent crystals in ore cavities within clay29. The utilization of waste materials in geopolymer binder systems provides environmental benefits in line with the objectives of developing sustainable building materials. In a recent study conducted in this context, 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O, which is a boron waste, was used as a replacement material up to 30% in metakaolin-based geopolymer matrices, and composite production was carried out with the addition of 1% CF (12 mm and 24 mm in length). The results obtained showed that a 10% substitution of 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O had positive effects on compressive and flexural strength, but mechanical performance decreased at higher substitution rates. These findings reveal the potential for producing GPLC that is both economically and environmentally advantageous through the use of low-ratio 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O30. Additionally, in another study investigating the effect of different fiber types on the mechanical behavior of geopolymer composites, four different fiber types were evaluated, and it was determined that all fibers improved material performance. Specifically, it was reported that polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) fibers provided a 415.58% increase in flexural toughness compared to control samples31. These results demonstrate that fiber-reinforced geopolymer composites have significant potential for enhancing critical properties, such as mechanical strength and ductility.

GGBFS accelerates early strength development, especially compared to natural pozzolans with low reactivity32. This property balances the low binding capacity of perlite, providing an advantage in applications requiring early performance33–36. Perlite is a low-density volcanic glass containing high amounts of amorphous Al₂O₃ and SiO₂, and is considered a reactive aluminosilicate source in alkali activation systems37,38. Due to its lightness and thermal insulation capability, it is frequently preferred in GPLC production2,39. However, the low binding capacity of perlite and limited mechanical performance necessitate its use in combination with performance-enhancing components. At this point, GGBFS, known for its high calcium content, stands out as an ideal complement in perlite-based systems. Reducing the size of GGBFS particles increases strength due to the accelerated formation of strength-giving phases40.

Silica fume (SF) and FA are additives that enhance the mechanical properties of concrete, reduce its hydration rate, and lower its permeability and alkali-aggregate reactivity33,41. SF plays a significant role in perlite-based systems as both a microstructure densifier and a reactive pozzolanic admixture due to its high surface area and dense amorphous silica content. SF promotes the formation of additional binding gels such as calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and N-A-S-H by reacting with calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) or an alkaline environment within the cement or geopolymer matrix. In geopolymer systems, SF can promote the formation of C-S-H or C-(A)-S-H-like gels during the alkali activation process in the presence of calcium-containing components. Therefore, C-S-H formation is considered a limited and secondary reaction product rather than a dominant binding phase22,42,43. This situation provides a significant increase in the mechanical strength and durability properties of the matrix20.

Expanded perlite (EP) is produced by heating perlite, a naturally occurring volcanic glass, at high temperatures, causing the bound water in its structure to rapidly vaporize and expand. In the present study, EP is employed as the lightweight aggregate. In this context, referencing comprehensive reviews on vermiculite and expanded clay is relevant, as it situates EP within the broader group of widely used lightweight aggregates in concrete technology and allows for a more systematic comparison of their roles and performance characteristics44,45. Due to its low bulk density, EP is preferred in construction materials technology, particularly as a lightweight filler material46–48. The use of EP as a lightweight aggregate in GPLC production provides significant improvements in the physical, chemical, and thermal properties of concrete. This contribution enhances the overall performance of the material, supporting both structural efficiency and sustainability49,50. Nematollahi et al. reported that when light aggregates, such as EP, are used in SF-based geopolymers, they reduce the compressive strength51. In lightweight concrete systems, it has been demonstrated that high specific strength can be achieved simultaneously with low density through the optimization of the binder composition, the use of waste-based sustainable components, and fiber reinforcement52. In studies conducted, steel and CF have been preferred as reinforcement materials due to the high potential of geopolymer composites to increase strength properties1,53. CFs are used in the aviation, space, energy, and sports equipment industries. They are also used in construction to reinforce lightweight structures in earthquake-prone areas53–56.

GRP is an important binding material for GPLC. Çelikten et al. investigated the effect of replacing pottery glaze waste (PGW) with perlite and FA-based geopolymers on mechanical properties and high-temperature performance in their experimental study57. A study on ultra-lightweight, high-strength geopolymer composites has demonstrated that high mechanical performance can be achieved simultaneously with low density through the use of appropriate material combinations and microstructural control58. Geopolymer mortars produced with only perlite, only FA, and a composition of 75% FA and 25% perlite by weight were substituted with PGW at ratios of 10%, 20%, and 30%. The mortars were produced with a fixed molarity of 10 M NaOH content and a thermal cure of 8 h at 100 °C. The experiments revealed that substituting PGW material up to 20% by mass in PT and FA + GRP-containing samples had positive effects on the ultimate compressive strength. Based on this, studies can be conducted with other pozzolans, with GRP as the main binder. Furthermore, in addition to oven curing of geopolymers, conducting curing studies in the environment to increase strength and environmental and economic impacts would provide a significant contribution.

The existing literature is generally based on FA or metakaolin-based geopolymer systems. While Ali et al.30 investigated the substitution of 2CaO 3B₂O₃·5 H₂O waste at levels up to 30%, the present study utilizes a combination of up to 15% B2O3 and 2CaO 3B₂O₃·5 H₂O as a replacement for GRP. Expanded perlite-based geopolymer systems generally exhibit low mechanical performance and limited microstructural integrity due to their high porosity and the resulting increased water absorption capacity. These characteristics have adverse effects, particularly on compressive and flexural strengths. On the other hand, a review of the literature reveals that the use of boron compounds in geopolymer systems remains quite limited; specifically, the gas-forming or expansion-promoting effects of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O in lightweight geopolymer production have not been systematically addressed. In this context, elucidating the effects of these additives on both microstructure development and the balance between lightweight properties and strength emerges as a significant research need. This study also aims to fill the gap in the literature by examining the use of a GRP-based geopolymer matrix, which is an environmentally friendly and lightweight material. This approach represents a significant innovation in terms of sustainability and lightweight building material technologies. Furthermore, the comparative evaluation of the roles of high boron-containing B₂O₃-containing industrial wastes and the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O compound in geopolymerization processes constitutes the original aspect of the study in terms of understanding waste recovery and the potential of alternative raw materials. B₂O₃ and 2CaO · 3B₂O₃ · 5 H₂O can have significant effects on obtaining GPLC without the use of an additional foaming agent. CF usage is a crucial parameter in lightweight concrete production, and this study aimed to enhance mechanical properties with low CF reinforcement. This approach, particularly focused on optimizing the fiber ratio, offers an innovative solution in the field of building materials. In this study, within a systematic experimental design framework, a total of 18 different mixtures were prepared to investigate the effects of changes in the CF ratio and the amounts of B₂O₃ and 2CaO · 3B₂O₃ · 5 H₂O. Within the scope of the experimental plan, the first group of 9 samples was prepared with B₂O₃ addition, while the second group of 9 samples was prepared with 2CaO · 3B₂O₃ · 5 H₂O addition. The first three samples in each group were reference samples without CF, and CF was added at different ratios in the following six samples. A statistically analyzable dataset was obtained using the Taguchi method. In this regard, the study serves as an example of optimized mixture designs in the field of materials engineering. Finally, the utilization of industrial wastes such as FA, GGBFS, and SF, as well as natural resources such as B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O, which are abundant in Turkey, will provide significant environmental and economic impacts in terms of circular economy and sustainable construction materials production. In the experimental study, a Taguchi L18 (2¹×3²) orthogonal array consisting of 18 experimental conditions, including 1 two-level factor (2¹) and 2 three-level factors (3²), was used.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, GPLC samples were produced using a GRP-based binder. Within the scope of the experimental design, colemanite-Di-Calcium Hexaborate Pentahydrate, with the chemical formula 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O, was used as a substitute for B₂O₃ with high boron content. All mixtures were prepared with a constant binder/alkali activator = 1.13 ratio and constant alkali activator (Na2SiO3/NaOH = 2) content. In recent geopolymer studies, it has been observed that the Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio is generally preferred to be around 2.559. However, there are also studies where this ratio is set at 1.5, depending on the type of binder used60. In particular, it has been reported that a Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio of 2 provides higher compressive strength in lightweight mortar systems61,62. Furthermore, a recent geopolymer concrete study indicated that a 1.5 ratio offers higher load-bearing capacity compared to a 3.5 ratio63. In line with these findings, the Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio was set at 2 in the present study, taking into account both workability and setting behavior. The binder system was formed using GRP and EP, B₂O₃, 2CaO · 3B₂O₃ · 5 H₂O, GGBFS, and SF. The alkali activator solution was prepared by mixing Na₂SiO₃ with a density of 50 Böhme (Bé) and a 13 molar (M) NaOH solution. EP with a size range of 2–4 mm was preferred as the lightweight aggregate. CF reinforcement was determined at volumetric ratios of 0%, 1%, and 2% relative to the binding GRP weight fraction and was treated as an independent variable in the experimental design. Additionally, to improve the workability of the mixtures, a HP was added to the mix at a rate of 1.98% by weight of the total materials. In the mixtures, NaOH (807.03 kg) and Na₂SiO₃ (1614.14 kg) were kept constant. GRP was used as the binder at a rate of 1959.72 kg in the first mixture. B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additives were substituted for the main binder GRP at mass ratios of 0.5%, 10%, and 15%. The substitution levels for B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O compounds (0.5%, 10%, and 15%) were determined based on preliminary studies to evaluate the transition from low-level doping effects to high-concentration structural modifications, thereby capturing the critical performance thresholds of the lightweight geopolymer matrix. Additionally, 408.2 kg of GGBFS, 137.18 kg of SF, and 140.62 m³ of EP were added to each mixture. Experimental studies were conducted using the Taguchi experimental design method. This method, which allows for the systematic generation of all possible combinations within a two-factor, three-level structure, significantly enhances data comparability and contributes to obtaining a comprehensive dataset that enables the identification of potential trends. In this context, the experimental matrix shown in Table 1 was created using the Taguchi method with an L18 (2¹×3²) full factorial experimental design. Two-level factors are the additives B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O incorporated into the binder system, and three different levels have been defined for each factor: CF ratios of 0%, 1%, and 2%. Thanks to the L18 orthogonal array, the main effects of boron-based additives and their levels on performance outputs have been statistically evaluated independently and in a balanced manner with a limited number of experiments. Thus, while the number of experiments has been significantly reduced, the relative effect of each factor on system behavior has been reliably determined. Additionally, a clarifying statement has been added to the manuscript to specify that the CF additions were calculated based on the total weight of the binder components.

Experimental GPLC production of a mix design.

GPLC production was carried out based on the materials presented in Fig. 1 and the mixture ratios given in Table 1. GRP, used as the binder matrix component, is a natural source rich in Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ and has high efficiency in geopolymer production. B₂O₃ was evaluated in combination with GRP due to its effect of accelerating geopolymerization and its contribution to increasing thermal resistance. Similarly, 2CaO·3B2O3·5H2O, which provides a contribution that increases thermal stability with its calcium borate content, was substituted with GRP in a second set of samples. Na₂SiO₃ and NaOH were used as activators for the alkali activation system. In experimental studies, a commercial sodium silicate solution with a Ms=SiO₂/Na₂O ratio of 2.0 and a density of 50 °Bé was used12. This selection was made to optimize the strength and workability properties of the geopolymer binders. In the study by Konduru et al.64, the Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio was determined to be 2.5, and NaOH was used at concentrations of 8 M and 10 M to facilitate workability. In this study, however, the Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio was selected as 2. Indeed, recent studies have indicated that exceeding this ratio of 1.5 may lead to a significant increase in setting time in some cases12,65. Therefore, to maintain a balance between setting time and workability, the amounts of alkali activators were kept constant, and the NaOH solution was prepared at a concentration of 13 M in all samples. The incorporation of GGBFS was found to enhance early-age strength, which is primarily attributed to its ability to promote C-A-S-H gel formation within the geopolymer matrix. SF increases strength by filling microvoids. EP is a source of lightweight aggregate; it is effective in heat and sound insulation. CF bridges microscopic cracks, increasing mechanical strength and impact resistance. HP increases the workability of the mixture. The average particle size of the primary binder GRP used in the study is approximately 40 μm, and its specific gravity is 2.36. According to sieve analysis results, 37.40% of the material remains in the 500 μm fraction, indicating that GRP has a predominantly fine-grained distribution. Additionally, GRP is of a volcanic glass nature and contains a high proportion (90–97%) of crystallized minerals such as feldspar, apatite, and biotite. According to the sieve analysis results of expanded perlite, it was determined that the material is evenly distributed across different particle sizes, with the highest proportion—24.42%—found in the 2000 μm fraction66. The physical properties of the GGBFS supplied by Oyak Çimento indicate that it has high reactivity potential, with a Blaine fineness of over 2,750 cm²/g and an activity index of over 45% at 7 days and over 70% at 28 days67. The particle size of the B₂O₃ supplied by Eti Maden Firm is + 0.250 mm, and the maximum percentage of this fraction has been set at 35%. 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O contains 50.8% B₂O₃68. Commercially available SF is an amorphous powder with an ultra-fine, spherical particle size typically ranging from 0.1 to 0.2 microns (µm) (Supplementary Information).

B₂O₃ is a compound that can be found in an amorphous glassy form and in two different crystalline modifications. The amorphous form is a colorless, odorless, hard, glassy solid, typically obtained through the dehydration of boric acid. The most common and thermodynamically stable of the crystalline phases is hexagonal B₂O₃. B₂O₃ is known for its functions in ceramic and enamel glazes, such as regulating the thermal expansion coefficient, increasing the refractive index, and imparting chemical resistance69. The chemical properties of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, shown in Table 2, are important in terms of their potential to improve the mechanical strength, scratch resistance, and long-term durability against chemical effects of the matrix structure in GPC. 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O is a common ore of boron minerals, with a minimum B₂O₃ content of 29.5%. This mineral, which dissolves slowly in water but rapidly in acidic environments, is typically processed through a series of crushing and grinding stages after enrichment processes in concentrator plants to be ready for sale. Colemanite, in the form of Di-Calcium Hexaborate Pentahydrate (2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O), is widely used in the glass and ceramics industry due to its advantages, such as lowering the melting temperature, regulating the thermal expansion coefficient, and increasing chemical resistance70. B₂O₃ contributes to the formation of a glassy and dense matrix by incorporating B–O–Si and B–O–Al bonds into the geopolymer network structure. This leads to reduced porosity and increased strength. 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, in addition to B₂O₃’s contribution to the glassy structure, promotes the formation of C–A–S–H phases with its high CaO content. Thus, it can have a greater pressure-strengthening effect compared to the B₂O₃ contribution.

The chemical compositions of GRP, GGBFS, and SF in Table 3 provide critical data for understanding the geopolymer mortar design with B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additives. GRP has a structure rich in Al₂O₃ (13–14%) and SiO₂ (71–73%). This high Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ content forms the basis of the geopolymer binder matrix, providing a suitable substrate for the polycondensation process. However, its low calcium oxide (CaO) content (around 1%) limits its ability to impart rapid strength on its own. On the other hand, GGBFS is notable for its high CaO content (67.6%). This component contributes to the development of compressive strength, particularly by promoting the formation of C–S–H and C–A–S–H gel phases, in addition to facilitating rapid geopolymerization. Furthermore, the low but significant amounts of Al₂O₃ (7.5%) and SiO₂ (18.2%) support the formation of the reaction products. SF is a dense silica source with an amorphous structure and very high SiO₂ content (97%). This is extremely effective in terms of void filling in the binder system and strengthening the Si–O–T bonds. Additionally, the low LOI value (3.78%) has a positive impact on thermal stability. The GRP used in the experiment was finely ground GRP obtained from the Fixmax mine and factory in Çerkeş-Çankırı, Turkey. According to the sieve analysis of GRP, the pass percentages through sieves with diameters of 100 μm, 63 μm, 50 μm, 40 μm, 30 μm, and 25 mm are 100%, 95%, 85%, 55%, 8%, and 0%, respectively. The average diameter of the GRP particle size distribution is approximately 40 μm2. GGBFS was sourced from OYAK Cement’s İskenderun Demir Çelik facilities. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

XRD is a method used to determine the phase composition and crystal lattice structure of materials with a crystalline structure. X-rays sent to the sample are reflected from the crystal planes, creating diffraction patterns. The XRD analysis results for the B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O samples are obtained from the Eti Maden website and presented in Fig. 269,70. The B₂O₃ diffraction pattern shows broad, low-intensity peaks, indicating that the material is largely amorphous in structure. In particular, the broad peak that becomes prominent in the 2θ = 20–30° range indicates a glassy structure and confirms the amorphous character of B₂O₃. The absence of sharp peaks characteristic of a crystalline structure indicates that this compound contains structural irregularities, and due to its reactive nature, it can easily react in binding systems. In the XRD pattern of the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O sample, numerous sharp and high-intensity peaks were detected. This indicates that 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O has a distinct crystalline structure. The pattern obtained reveals the presence of characteristic phases belonging to 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, along with low-intensity calcite (CaCO₃) peaks. This finding indicates the presence of mineralogical impurities inherent in 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. The crystalline structure of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O enhances the material’s structural stability and contributes to its long-term durability.

In general, the amorphous character of B₂O₃ enhances the reactivity of geopolymer systems, facilitating their interaction with the binder matrix. At the same time, the crystalline structure of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O acts as a more stable additive mineral. The distinct structural properties of both materials have the potential to enhance the mechanical and chemical performance of GPCs through various mechanisms.

Mixture preparation, molding, swelling, and mechanical test methods

First, as shown in Fig. 3a, GRP, B₂O₃ or 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, GGBFS, SF, EP, and CF fibers were dry-mixed for approximately 1 min, as illustrated in Fig. 3a, to ensure a homogeneous distribution. Then, the alkali activator solution was gradually added to the mixture and mixed for a total of 3 min (2 min at low-medium speed and 1 min at high speed) in the mixer shown in Fig. 3(b). The resulting fresh mortar was placed into standard prism molds measuring 40 × 40 × 160 mm prior to the onset of setting, left to cure for 24 h under laboratory conditions, and then cured at 85 °C for 24 h. The flow properties of the fresh mixture were determined in accordance with ASTM C1437, as shown in Fig. 3c73. The results indicate that the GPLC mortar exhibits low flowability and limited workability. This is attributed to the high-water absorption capacity and porous structure of GRP, the primary binder, as well as the viscosity-increasing effect of the B₂O₃ additive on the system. The GGBFS additive, while increasing reactivity, can also cause an increase in consistency by raising the mixture’s viscosity during the early stages.

While the reference samples exhibited a smooth surface, significant gas formation and associated expansion were observed in the samples containing 5%, 10%, and 15% B₂O₃. This manifested as a distinct upward bulging of the sample surface. The expansion values obtained were systematically recorded by measuring them with a caliper, as shown in Fig. 3d. This observed behavior is attributed to the formation and growth of micro-gas bubbles within the mixture. The literature indicates that such gas formation may result from the release of gases such as water vapor or CO₂ due to reactions between NaOH, Na₂SiO₃, and B₂O₃74,75. In contrast, 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, a natural boron mineral, although it has a similar chemical composition, does not exhibit the same level of reactivity as B₂O₃ and therefore demonstrates a lower tendency for gas evolution and expansion.

The tests were conducted in accordance with the ASTM C34876 standard, which is widely used to determine the flexural strength of mortar specimens. Although ASTM C348 was originally developed for cement-based mortars, it is widely used to evaluate the flexural strength of geopolymer mortars due to similar specimen geometry and loading conditions77. Compressive strength tests were conducted in accordance with the ASTM C10968 standard. The loading rate applied to the specimens was selected as 2.4 kN/s. Fig.3e shows that the specimens were subjected to thermal treatment in a muffle furnace (200 °C, 400 °C, and 600 °C) to evaluate their performance under high-temperature effects; subsequently, surface temperature and heat distribution were monitored using a thermal camera. After heat treatment, the specimens were subjected to a compressive strength test again, and the effect of high temperatures on mechanical properties was analyzed.

Future outlook

The findings of this study indicate that boron-modified GRP-based geopolymer lightweight concretes possess significant potential in terms of both microstructural and mechanical performance. In future studies, it is recommended to investigate in detail the effects of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additives on long-term durability performance (drying-shrinkage, freeze-thaw, and resistance to chemical effects). Additionally, the reaction kinetics of boron compounds under different alkali activator types and molarities could be investigated to better understand the gas formation mechanism. The effect of fiber type and hybrid fiber combinations (e.g., fibers with different lengths and elastic moduli) on brittleness behavior, in particular, constitutes an important research area for future studies. Furthermore, the microstructural evolution of boron-containing systems at high temperatures and post-fire conditions should be supported by advanced characterization techniques. Finally, the evaluation of such sustainable geopolymer systems in terms of scale-up, field applications, and life cycle analysis (LCA) is of critical importance for their integration into practical engineering applications.

Results and discussion

In this section, to ensure the consistency of experimental data, all experimental tests (density, ventilation, and mechanical strength) were performed using three samples (n = 3) for each of the 18 mixtures. The arithmetic mean of the obtained results was taken and recorded as the final data. The error bars presented in the graphs represent the standard deviation of these three repeated measurements. Since the results obtained are very close, the error bars appear small.

Aeration effects

Perlite is an amorphous material based on volcanic glass, containing high levels of Al₂O₃ and SiO₂. It is an ideal source of Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ for geopolymer production. It dissolves in an alkaline environment, releasing Si⁴⁺ and Al³⁺ ions, which initiate geopolymerization78. It does not inflate on its own, but it creates a matrix that is open to gas formation2. B₂O₃ has been widely used in FA-based geopolymer applications, but its use in perlite-based geopolymers has not been observed. Therefore, the addition of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O will have a significant effect on GRP-based lightweight concrete. This is because boron compounds interact with the silicate structure derived from perlite. That is, when boric acid and boron oxide (Na₂B₄O₇·10 H₂O) combine with activators such as NaOH, the reaction B(OH)₃ + OH⁻ → B(OH)₄⁻ occurs. During this process, heat is released, and the free water in the structure evaporates, causing swelling. CO₂ may also be released from carbonate residues. 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O is less soluble and contains water in its crystal structure, but this water is more difficult to release79.

To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental results, all of the swelling measurements and aeration effect tests presented in Fig. 4 were conducted in triplicate (n = 3) for each mixture composition. The data points plotted in the figures represent the arithmetic mean of these independent measurements, while the error bars indicate the standard deviation (± SD). Statistical significance was evaluated to determine the impact of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additions on the aeration process. This confirmed that the observed variations were statistically consistent across the 18 different mixtures. The measurement unit is mm, indicating the height of the swelling. On the left side, B₂O₃ additives are shown at 0%, 1%, and 2% ratios, and on the right side, 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additives are shown at 0%, 1%, and 2% ratios with CF changes of 0%, 1%, and 2%. The B₂O₃ additive significantly increases the swelling effect. The 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additive, on the other hand, creates a much more limited swelling. The red dashed line represents a general decreasing trend curve (regression line). It emphasizes that the effect of aeration decreases depending on the type and content of the additive. The left side of the graph shows the increase in swelling amount between B₂O₃ additions (B05%, B10%, and B15%). At 5% B₂O₃, swelling is around 5–7 mm, at 10% B₂O₃, swelling is around 11–14 mm, and at 15% B₂O₃, this value reaches quite high levels, around 13–19 mm. This indicates that B₂O₃ causes gas production in an alkaline environment. While CF 0% (green) shows the highest swelling, the swelling effect remains at lower levels at CF 1% (blue) and CF 2% (brown). The highest swelling occurred at B15 – CF%0, reaching 19.43 mm. On the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O side (Right Region), the swelling increase between C0.5% - C10% - C15% is much more limited. The swelling effect was approximately 3 mm for 5% 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, approximately 4 mm for 10% 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, and approximately 6 mm for 15% 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. CF 0% (green) produced the highest swelling, while the swelling effect was lower at CF 1% (blue) and CF 2% (brown) ratios. This is likely due to CF forming a network structure that blocks gas passage within the mixture or accelerates the hardening of the matrix, thereby hindering gas escape. The gas evolution resulting from boron addition becomes more controlled as the CF ratio increases. The high expansion behavior observed with the addition of B₂O₃ is attributed to the high reactivity resulting from its amorphous structure. In contrast, the more ordered crystalline structure and low solubility of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O help control excessive expansion by limiting gas formation.

Flexural strength (MPa)

The twin graph in Fig. 6 shows the effect of CF addition at 0%, 1%, and 2% ratios on the flexural strength of GPLC samples with different GRP-based ratios of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. The horizontal axis shows the different mixing ratios for B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, while the vertical axis shows the flexural strength results. The percentages given above each mixture show how much the CF additive increases the flexural strength. For example, for B05, a 20% increase is seen when going from 0% to 1%, and a 35% increase when going to 2%. However, in some mixtures (e.g., C15%), the CF additive provided limited benefit (only a 15% increase for 2% CF), indicating that the effect of the additive varies depending on the mixture. The significant enhancement in flexural strength, particularly the 78% increase observed in the B10 CF1 specimen, can be attributed to the crack-bridging and fiber pull-out mechanisms. CF acts as a reinforcement bridge across the micro-cracks, preventing rapid crack propagation and facilitating a more controlled stress distribution within the matrix. The stabilization of compressive strength at ~ 6 MPa further suggests that the interaction between the geopolymer matrix and the fibers reached a synergistic optimum, where the energy required for debonding significantly enhances the overall mechanical toughness. In general, as the CF content increases (CF-%0, %1, %2), flexural strength increases in all mixtures. The most notable increase is observed in the B10% (GPLC_02_B10%) mixture, where a 78% increase is seen when transitioning from % 0% CF to % 1% CF. This indicates that the CF additive is particularly effective in this mixture. The highest flexural strength is observed in the B10% mixture with CF-2% additive, at approximately 6 MPa. When the CF additive is constant (e.g., CF-0%), flexural strength first increases and then decreases as the ratio of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O increases. This is due to the expansion resulting from the reaction between boron and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. It can be stated that the optimum flexural strength is obtained at a ratio of 10% B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. Çelik et al.16 produced a GRP-based GPLC by adding H₂O₂ and reinforcing with CF, achieving excellent expansion. However, expansion caused a decrease in flexural strength of up to 80%, and CF reinforcement was found to be insufficiently protective. In this study, produced with the addition of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, an increase and stability in flexural strength were observed with increased expansion and CF reinforcement support.

Compressive strength (MPa)

The graph in Fig. 8 shows the compressive strength results. The CF additive generally increased compressive strength, but the increases were more limited compared to flexural strength. For example, in the B05 mixture, the transition from 0% to 1% resulted in a 19% increase, while the transition to 2% resulted in only a 2% additional increase. In the B15 mixture, the CF-2% addition had almost no effect (1% increase). This indicates that CF’s effect on compressive strength is limited, and its main contribution is in properties related to tensile behavior, such as flexural strength. The results confirm previous similar studies on fiber-reinforced composites14,16. The highest compressive strength at all additive levels with B₂O₃ addition is observed in the B05, B10, and C05 mixtures (around 5.8–6 MPa). Strength decreased in the B15 and C20 mixtures. This indicates that high B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O ratios weaken the matrix structure, reducing compressive strength. The effect of CF addition is most pronounced at a 1% addition. A 2% addition generally provided very little additional benefit or, in some cases, caused a decrease. For B10, a 20% increase was observed from 0% to 1%, and only a 4% increase from 1% to 2%. For C15, a 3% increase was observed from 0% to 1%, and a 1% decrease from 1% to 2%. Compressive strength performance improvement is a result of the homogeneous fiber distribution observed in Fig. 7. Microcracks formed within the matrix under compressive load were bridged by CF (crack bridging), thereby limiting lateral expansion. Despite the swelling effect caused by B₂O₃ addition (Fig. 7), the strong adhesion of the fibers to the matrix kept matrix cracking under control and ensured that the material exhibited a more ductile fracture behavior. This demonstrates the critical advantage of using CF in lightweight structures in terms of maintaining mechanical integrity. Compressive strength peaked in the B05/B10 series with the use of CF1 and CF2. The failure mechanism shows that structural integrity was maintained thanks to the crack-bridging effect of the fibers, despite the B₂O₃ -induced swelling tendency of the matrix.

CF additive generally increases compressive strength by about 1%; however, at a 2% additive level, this increase either plateaus or decreases. Based on this data, the optimum CF additive ratio appears to be 1%. The most suitable mixtures are B05 and B10 ratios with 1% CF additive (approximately 6 MPa compressive strength). Excessive B₂O₃ /2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O content (e.g., B15 and C20) results in low strength despite the CF content. As the alkali concentration increases, the amount of unreacted perlite particles decreases, and the matrix appears more continuous, indicating increased gel formation78. In the GRP-based lightweight paste study conducted by Murat et al.80 with the addition of H₂O₂, it was stated that the apparent density decreased to 516 kg/cm³, but in contrast, the compressive strength decreased by 0.5 MPa. In this study, conducted with the addition of B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, the apparent density decreased to 748.2 kg/m³, but a more stable and protective condition was observed in the compressive strength.

At 200 °C (green line), the strengths are quite close to room temperature, with only a slight decrease (0.1–0.2 MPa). The general trend is that compressive strength decreases as temperature increases. The decline curve accelerates particularly after 400 °C. CF addition is most effective at 24 °C and 200 °C, with its effect being limited after 400 °C. Optimal mixtures: B05, B10, and C05 series with 1% CF addition, as they maintain the highest values at all temperatures.

Microstructural analysis

Field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) analysis

The FE-SEM images of perlite aggregate expanded with a B₂O₃-containing, GRP, SF, and GGBFS-based binder in Fig. 14, along with CF and the hyper-fluidizing agent GPLC, reveal the effect of microstructure on mechanical properties. Figure 14a) shows the formation of a dense and compact matrix, indicating that the amorphous structure of B₂O₃ increases the reactivity of the binder phase, thereby enhancing compressive strength. This finding corroborates the work of Çelik et al.26, who reported that the geopolymeric matrix formed an interfacial bond with the fibers. This is consistent with findings reporting that additives improve strength by increasing matrix density. The dense and amorphous structure of the geopolymer gel phase is clearly observable, and it can be seen that the CFs are partially embedded within this gel matrix and provide adhesion. This region is consistent with the typical N-A-S-H gel morphology. In contrast, Fig. 14b) shows a more porous and void-filled structure, but the fibers are seen to extend into the voids. These voids, irregularly distributed within the matrix, are due to gas formation in the porous structure and partially disrupt the gel continuity, creating a heterogeneous structure. In Fig. 14c), partially coated and, in places, weakly bonded phases are observed in the regions where the CF surface comes into contact with the geopolymer matrix. This indicates limited bonding at the fiber-matrix interface and the presence of local voids. The microcrack formations and discontinuities developing along the fiber shown in Fig. 14d are clearly visible. These cracks are attributed to the brittle nature of the matrix and thermal expansion effects, and they create weak zones that could adversely affect mechanical performance. In Fig. 14e), compact and well-bonded particles are associated with high compressive strength. Davidovits82 also reported that GGBFS addition is effective in this regard. Gel formations with a layered and irregular morphology represent the typical N-A-S-H gel structure; in addition, more compact regions indicate densified portions of the geopolymer matrix. In Fig. 14f), the homogeneous distribution of fibers appears to be a factor that increases flexural strength. The presence of CF plays a binding role in the microcracks within the structure. Normally, C-A-S-H gel does not form in geopolymer structures where raw perlite is used alone, because raw perlite mainly contains Al₂O₃ and SiO₂, but the CaO content is very low. Figure 14 g) clearly shows that both the N-A-S-H (amorphous, disordered gel) and C-A-S-H (more needle-like/dense crystalline structure) phases coexist within the same image. This confirms the formation of a hybrid gel associated with the presence of calcium. It demonstrates that the formation of C-A-S-H gel and N-A-S-H gel is due to the increased CaO content associated with the presence of GGBFS. It is observed that N-A-S-H fills the interparticle voids, creating a homogeneous and dense microstructure. This enhances compressive and flexural strength. Finally, Fig. 14h) shows that the hyperplastic effect reduces the void ratio, which positively affects the compressive strength. Moreover, N-A-S-H gel phase, dispersed within a more homogeneous and dense geopolymer matrix, is notable for its amorphous and clustered morphology and exhibits a relatively more compact microstructure.

The FE-SEM images in Fig. 15 show the microstructure of the geopolymer mortar containing 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. These images reveal some differences in terms of both the development of the binding gel phases and particle morphology compared to the previously studied B₂O₃-added samples. Figure 15a shows a very dense and compact microstructure. This dense texture appears to be due to the incorporation of Ca and B ions, released by the dissolution of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, into the matrix, supporting the formation of a tighter N-A-S-H-like gel. The inclusion of GGBFS and 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O promotes the formation of C-A-S-H gels as well as the N-A-S-H structure by adding additional reactive calcium to the system. This hybrid gelation process results in a significantly denser and more refined pore structure. The coexistence of these gels provides a stronger matrix and delivers higher early-age compressive strength and improved mechanical stability in 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O-modified samples compared to pure geopolymeric binders. A more porous and heterogeneous structure was observed in the B₂O₃-added systems; the porosity ratio appears to have decreased significantly with the addition of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. In Fig. 15b, fibrous structures are evident in the presence of CF. The fibrous morphology indicates that more ordered crystalline phases formed during the setting of the geopolymer matrix. Crystallization in systems containing B₂O₃ was more irregular and heterogeneous, while a more homogeneous distribution was observed with the addition of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. Layered and flat particle clusters are visible in images in Fig. 15c and d. These particles are partially undissolved raw material grains and are embedded in the matrix. While these particles appeared to be more strongly interlocked with the binder gel when 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O was added, voids were more prominent between the binder and the aggregates in systems added with B₂O₃. This indicates that 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O improved the binder-aggregate interface compatibility. Images in Fig. 15(e and f) show that the overall microstructure is denser, contains fewer microcracks, and has more developed interparticle bonding. While homogeneity increased with the addition of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O, interparticle discontinuities and porosity were higher in samples added with B₂O₃. In addition, the acicular crystals in Fig. 15(f) indicate the presence of secondary phases developed under the influence of B₂O₃; this can be interpreted as crystalline structures that may contribute to the strength of the binding matrix. While a more porous, heterogeneous, and irregular microstructure was observed in systems containing B₂O₃, a denser, homogeneous, and strongly bonded matrix was formed in systems containing 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. The Ca ions entering the system through the dissolution of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O promoted the formation of partial C-A-S-H gels in addition to the N-A-S-H gel, which resulted in compaction of the microstructure and increased mechanical strength. Therefore, the FE-SEM results reveal that the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additive developed a more stable and durable structure in the geopolymer mortar compared to the B₂O₃ additive.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis

In Fig. 16, EDX analyses obtained using an integrated FE-SEM device reveal in detail the presence and proportions of elements within the microstructure of geopolymer lightweight concrete containing B₂O₃, GRP, SF, GGBFS, EP aggregate, and CF. According to the EDX results, the highest proportions are oxygen (O) at 43.56% and silicon (Si) at 27.94%, indicating the presence of a dense Si–O–Al network structure within the binder matrix and the formation of a strong N-A-S-H gel phase. Furthermore, the sodium (Na) content determined as 13.55% indicates that alkali activator-derived ions are integrated into the geopolymer structure and contribute to strength development by partially filling the pore structure. In addition, 5.28% calcium (Ca) originates from the dissolution of GGBFS and partially from B₂O₃, which facilitates the formation of not only the N-A-S-H gel but also the hybrid C-A-S-H gel. The fibrous and needle-like crystal structures observed in SEM images indicate that the B₂O₃ addition supports secondary phase formation and, together with CF, strengthens the matrix by limiting microcrack propagation. Thus, while maintaining the lightness provided by the perlite aggregate, it is understood that both compressive and flexural strengths are improved through increased gel density and fiber reinforcement. In particular, the combined effect of the hybrid gel phase and fiber addition reduces the brittle fracture behavior commonly seen in GPLC, resulting in more ductile mechanical performance. These findings demonstrate that the B₂O₃ addition plays an important role in the production of high-performance lightweight concrete by regulating the pore structure in GRP-based geopolymer systems.

Fig. 16EDX analysis of B₂O₃.

EDX analysis of B₂O₃.

In the previous analysis, the B₂O₃-based admixture, with its high Na (13.55%) and Ca (5.28%) contents, supported the formation of hybrid N-A-S-H and C-A-S-H gel phases and provided a dense network structure. In contrast, the EDX results for the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O-based system in Fig. 17 show a more pronounced Si–O–Al polymerization with ratios of 42.6% O, 33.78% Si, and 7.05% Al. The Ca content in the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O structure was measured as 2.32%, which is lower than the Ca content in the system with added B₂O₃. This indicates that the N-A-S-H gel was more dominant in the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O samples, while the C-A-S-H gel was formed to a limited extent. Furthermore, the Na content was lower at 9.14%, indicating that pore filling was more limited compared to the B₂O₃-added system. FE-SEM images revealed that the matrix of the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O samples had a more homogeneous and dense structure, while fewer fibrous crystal formations were observed. This indicates that 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O develops a more stable but more brittle microstructure. Consequently, the B₂O₃-added system increases strength with more complex and hybrid gel phases, while the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O-added system presents a homogeneous N-A-S-H gel-based structure with more limited ductility due to its relatively low Ca content.

A careful comparison of the element ratios shown in Figs. 16 and 17 reveals that the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O system (Ca/Si ≈ 0.07, Na/Al ≈ 1.30, Si/Al ≈ 4.79) has a more balanced chemical composition. In particular, the fact that the Si/Al ratio is within the optimal range and the Na/Al ratio does not reach excessively alkaline levels supports the formation of a more compact and homogeneous N-A-S-H gel structure. In contrast, high (Na/Al ≈ 2.69) and excessive (Si/Al ≈ 5.54) ratios in the B₂O₃ system lead to irregularity in the gel structure and increased porosity, negatively affecting strength stability. Additionally, the lower Ca/Si ratio in the boracite system promotes the development of a more stable and long-lasting gel structure. This assessment is consistent with the composition–microstructure–mechanical property relationships reported in the recent study (Fath et al., 2026)83.

FTIR analysis of GPLC containing GRP in Fig. 18, SF, and GGBFS-based binders, EP aggregate, and CF reinforcement produced with B₂O₃ addition sheds light on the microstructural evolution of the material. The strong Si–O–T (T = Si, Al) vibrations observed in the 1014.7 cm⁻¹ band in the spectrum indicate that the geopolymerization reaction has occurred effectively and a three-dimensional network structure has formed. In addition, the band around 1397.8 cm⁻¹ proves that the B₂O₃ addition has integrated into the geopolymer structure by forming B–O–Si and B–O–Al bonds within the matrix. This contributes to the formation of an amorphous glassy matrix and a reduction in porosity. Furthermore, vibrations of water molecules observed in the 1615.1 cm⁻¹ region reveal the presence of some bound water within the system. This chemical structure is directly reflected in the mechanical properties. Densification of the glassy matrix, in particular, contributes to increased compressive strength, while the strong bond between the CF and the matrix significantly improves flexural strength. The lightness provided by the EP aggregate is offset by these improvements in mechanical performance, resulting in a sustainable and functional building material with low density but high strength.

FTIR analysis of GPLC with GRP, SF, and GGBFS-based binders produced with the addition of 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O indicates significant changes in the chemical bond structure of the material, as shown in Fig. 19. The strong Si–O–T (T = Si, Al) vibration observed in the spectrum at 1034.3 cm⁻¹ confirms the successful geopolymerization reaction and the formation of a three-dimensional network structure. However, thanks to the calcium content, the formation of C–A–S–H gel phases is additionally encouraged, further increasing compressive strength. The strong chemical interaction between CF and the matrix improves flexural strength, while the EP aggregate maintains a low density. Thus, the 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O additive contributes to the production of a lightweight yet high-strength GPC by both strengthening the network structure and significantly improving mechanical performance with the addition of gel phases compared to the B₂O₃ additive.

Conclusion

This study focused on the gassing effect of B₂O₃, and 2CaO·3B₂O₃5 H₂O, mechanical strength, statistical analysis, post-fire analysis, and microstructure analysis of GRP-based GPLC. GGBFS, FS, CF, and HP were also added to provide good strength and workability, as well as lightness. The Taguchi statistical model of the study was created by substituting B₂O₃ and 2CaO·3B₂O₃5 H as the main binder GPR at rates of 5%, 10%, and 15%, and additionally adding CF at rates of 0%, 1%, and 2%. Experimental results revealed that the B₂O₃ additive, in particular, interacted with alkali activators, leading to expansion and porous structure formation, while the 2CaO·3B₂O₃5 H₂O additive provided a more controlled microstructure. It was determined that CF reinforcement provided a significant contribution to flexural strength and that the 1% fiber addition, in particular, yielded optimum performance. The effect of CF on compressive strength was limited, but the low binding capacity of perlite was offset by the addition of GGBFS and SF, resulting in a compact structure. FE-SEM and EDX analyses showed that hybrid N-A-S-H and C-A-S-H gel phases formed in systems containing B₂O₃, while a more homogeneous but relatively brittle N-A-S-H matrix developed in systems containing 2CaO·3B₂O₃·5 H₂O. FTIR analyses confirmed the formation of a three-dimensional network structure with the intensity of the Si–O–T vibration bands. They also revealed that boron additives increased matrix density through the B–O–Si and B–O–Al bonds. Taguchi analysis showed that the B₂O₃ additive was more dominant in performance, while the CF and binder ratio played a critical role in strength optimization.
