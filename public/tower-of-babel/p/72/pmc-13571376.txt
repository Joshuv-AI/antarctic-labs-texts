Case Presentation

Patient 1 was a 79-year-old woman with severe mitral stenosis (mitral valve area 0.99 cm2) and persistent atrial fibrillation who was not receiving anticoagulation. She presented with progressive exertional dyspnea. Echocardiography revealed a large (39 × 25 × 30 mm), mobile left atrial mass initially suspected to be a myxoma. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (Video 1) demonstrated the mass bouncing dynamically within the left atrium with each cardiac cycle—the ping-pong ball sign—repeatedly approaching the mitral valve orifice without entrapment. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography confirmed a left atrial filling defect (Figure 1A). She underwent surgical resection, mitral valve replacement with a 25-mm bioprosthesis, tricuspid annuloplasty, patent foramen ovale closure, and left atrial reduction to address the dilated atrial substrate. Histopathology (Figures 1B and 1C) confirmed an organized thrombus with concentric laminated fibrin layers and a fibrous capsule. Postoperative echocardiography showed pulmonary artery systolic pressure decreasing from 46 to 35 mm Hg and left atrial diameter from 46 to 44 mm. The patient was discharged on warfarin.

Patient 2 was an 82-year-old woman with persistent atrial fibrillation and no anticoagulation. No mitral stenosis was identified. She presented with a left atrial mass on echocardiography, and her family declined surgical intervention. Transthoracic echocardiography (Video 2) demonstrated a nearly spherical (28 × 25 × 24 mm), free-floating left atrial thrombus and subsequently captured acute obstruction of the mitral valve orifice by the ball thrombus—the hole-in-one mechanism.1 On day 3 she developed sudden hemodynamic collapse (blood pressure 62/39 mm Hg, altered consciousness) and died from fatal cardiogenic shock.

Discussion

These cases raise the hypothesis that atrial fibrillation with inadequate anticoagulation, particularly when combined with mitral stenosis, may predispose to left atrial ball thrombus formation through blood stasis satisfying Virchow's triad.2 The characteristic ping-pong ball motion on echocardiography3—directly visualized in both cases—may be a useful clue to distinguish ball thrombus from myxoma. The hole-in-one mechanism, captured on echocardiography in patient 2 (Video 2), represents a potentially fatal complication that may occur rapidly without warning. The contrasting outcomes suggest that surgical intervention may alter the natural history of this condition, although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn from 2 cases.
