User experiences of the mobile stress autism mate (SAM) application: a qualitative study among autistic adults not receiving mental health care
Authors: Alvin van Asselt, Yvette Roke, Kirsten Hoeberichts, Sander Begeer, Anke Scheeren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutistic adults typically experience greater daily stress than their non-autistic counterparts, but professional mental health care is not always accessible or fitting. This qualitative study explored the user experiences of the Stress Autism Mate (SAM), a free co-created mobile application designed to reduce stress, among autistic adults not receiving mental health care.MethodsA total of 15 autistic users (8 satisfied, 7 dissatisfied; aged 27–70; 4 men, 10 women, 1 non-binary) were recruited through a prior SAM trial database and autism-related social media groups for semi-structured online interviews. Interview questions were informed by the User Experience Technology Acceptance Model (UX-TAM), whereas themes were developed inductively through thematic analysis.ResultsParticipants generally appreciated the SAM app’s focus on enhancing stress recognition and its non-coercive, intuitive, and minimalist interface. Nevertheless, they found the app’s stress tips insufficiently tailored to their context and the repeated login requirement frustrating. Satisfied users expressed a stronger need for stress-reducing support and greater trust in the app’s developers and its scientific foundations. Dissatisfied users perceived the app’s features as superficial and reported greater strain from attempting to adhere to its demands.ConclusionsDespite the diverse user experiences, the findings highlight various avenues to improve the SAM app and similar standalone mHealth tools for autistic adults. Future adaptations should carefully balance the provision of structure and support with the risk of inducing technostress.

Perceived stress, henceforth stress, refers to the negative feelings and thoughts of an individual when they believe they cannot adequately handle the demands of daily life (Lazarus, 1966). While mild to moderate stress can be adaptive in the short term—motivating individuals to take action to address stressors (Fink, 2016; Vikoler et al., 2024)—prolonged stress can be detrimental to health (Fava et al., 2019). Chronic stress has been linked to a wide range of adverse health outcomes, such as substance use, anxiety disorders, cancer, inflammation, and mortality (Epel et al., 2018). Moreover, these health consequences can be stress-inducing in themselves, creating a negative feedback loop (Ravi et al., 2021). Reducing daily stress has therefore become a key objective in mental health programmes (Galante et al., 2021; Jaremko & Meichenbaum, 2013). One increasingly explored approach is mobile health (mHealth): mobile applications (apps) designed to support health-related behaviour change (Istepanian et al., 2006). We evaluated the user experiences of a free, co-created mHealth app designed to reduce stress in autistic adults, 1 a population for whom stress reduction is particularly relevant (Van der Linden et al., 2022).

Adults with autism consistently report higher stress levels than their allistic (non-autistic) peers (Moseley et al., 2021; Van der Linden et al., 2022). Causes include, but are not limited to, mismatches between their unique communicative and sensory needs and their environments (Lydon et al., 2014; Mallory & Keehn, 2021), and exposure to minority stressors, such as stigmatisation and victimisation (Botha & Frost, 2018; Moseley et al., 2021). Moreover, alexithymia, which refers to difficulties in recognising, understanding, and describing emotional states (Sifneos, 1973), is experienced by about half of autistic individuals (Poquérusse et al., 2018). As a result, they may struggle to recognise their stressors and employ effective coping strategies (Poquérusse et al., 2018; Samson et al., 2015). Supporting this, a recent qualitative study found that autistic adults identified alexithymia as a key contributor to their mental health challenges (Beckwith & López, 2025). Addressing alexithymia may therefore be a promising approach to enhance their stress management.

Despite widespread calls for stress-reducing support, professional mental health care is often inaccessible or inadequately tailored to autistic adults (Crane et al., 2018; Grey et al., 2025). Adults with autism may experience a ‘service cliff’ upon reaching adulthood, with support options greatly declining after leaving high school (Brede et al., 2022; Laxman et al., 2019). Even when services are provided, a 2023 report by a Dutch organisation that monitors mental health care quality revealed that only one-third of autistic individuals felt these services adequately addressed their needs (Akwa, 2023). These findings align with research in other countries. For instance, Crane et al. (2018) found that only 25% of UK autistic young adults considered mental health care to be beneficial. Suggested barriers include high costs and limited availability of specialised providers (Malik-Soni et al., 2022; Płatos & Pisula, 2019), concerns of being stigmatised or feeling unheard by practitioners (Lewis, 2017; Malik-Soni et al., 2022; Marsden et al., 2024), and reliance on face-to-face contact, which may fail to accommodate autistic adults’ communicative and environmental needs (Lehmann & Leavey, 2016).

In response to these barriers, mHealth interventions are emerging as a promising alternative. A review by Koh et al. (2022) found that mHealth apps may improve accessibility in three ways: (1) providing timely support in user-defined environments; (2) lowering mental health care costs; and (3) combating stigma associated with seeking support. However, evidence on their effectiveness in autistic adults is limited. To illustrate, Kim et al. (2018) found that only 0.6% of 700 apps targeting people with autism on app stores had been empirically evaluated. In addition, mHealth interventions may face significant challenges in sustaining user engagement (Amagai et al., 2022). For example, a recent study on a mindfulness app for autistic adults reported a steep decline in usage during the six-week follow-up period, from 224 to 10 minutes per week (Li et al., 2025). These findings highlight a gap in knowledge regarding both the effectiveness of mHealth and strategies to sustain adherence.

One of the few mHealth apps for autistic adults that has been thoroughly evaluated is the Stress Autism Mate (SAM), a free, non-profit self-help tool co-created with autistic adults to reduce stress by addressing alexithymia. To our knowledge, the SAM app is the only publicly available, empirically evaluated stress management app specifically designed for autistic adults. The app is available in 34 countries and ten languages and is used by thousands worldwide. It prompts users to complete two to four short questionnaires per day about their activities and stress-related feelings over the past four hours. Afterwards, users are invited to reflect on the calculated stress scores and receive personalised stress-reducing tips. The questionnaires therefore function as a stress monitoring tool that helps users better understand when and why they are stressed, enabling them to respond more timely. In Dutch trials, including a large-scale RCT, using the app for four weeks has been shown to reduce stress levels with small to medium effect sizes (d = 0.29–0.65; Hoeberichts et al., 2022, 2024; Spaargaren et al., 2025). Replication studies are currently underway in other countries to ensure cross-cultural validation. Moreover, in a qualitative study (Hoeberichts et al., 2025), users reported that the obligation to use the app daily could be stressful. Nevertheless, most participants praised its effectiveness in enhancing stress recognition and promoting adaptive coping strategies, demonstrating that the app largely fulfilled its intended purpose.

However, three of the four studies (Hoeberichts et al., 2022, 2024, 2025) only included participants concurrently receiving professional mental health care. This population falls outside the main target users, and the services they received may have interfered with the outcomes. For instance, mental health support in the Netherlands often includes other effective stress interventions aimed at reducing alexithymia, such as the creation of a ‘stress signalling plan’ (GGZ Standaarden, 2017; Mournet et al., 2024). Furthermore, practitioners may have encouraged app use and provided guidance, both of which are known to increase mHealth adherence and effectiveness (Jakob et al., 2022). Supporting these influences, the RCT, which included participants not in care (N = 216), reported the smallest stress reductions across all evaluations of the app, with only 14.3% of participants showing reliable improvement (Spaargaren et al., 2025). Despite these potential influences, the exact reasons for reduced effectiveness in this group remain unclear.

In light of this knowledge gap, our present qualitative study explored user experiences of the SAM app in autistic adults not receiving mental health care. Qualitative research enables in-depth understanding of users’ perspectives, contexts, and challenges, thereby helping to identify mechanisms underlying engagement and effectiveness (Green & Thorogood, 2018) and complementing the preceding RCT. Our findings can inform adaptations of the SAM app as well as the development of similar mHealth apps for autistic adults. Given that adherence is a common barrier to mHealth effectiveness (Koh et al., 2022; Torous et al., 2018), we used the User Experience Technology Acceptance Model (UX-TAM) (Mlekus et al., 2020) to inform the interview guide and help ensure that key domains of user experience relevant to adherence were explored. According to this model, three primary user experiences drive the intention to use technology such as the SAM app: perceived usefulness (expecting that the app will have positive effects), perceived ease of use (believing that the app will take little to no effort), and hedonic quality (sensing the app will be stimulating and pleasant). A recent review concluded that the TAM is an effective framework for evaluating mHealth acceptance across both quantitative and qualitative studies (Adnan et al., 2025).

Materials and methods

Design

Our qualitative study utilised in-depth, semi-structured interviews to comprehensively evaluate the user experiences of the SAM app. We employed thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), a widely used method for identifying, analysing, and reporting patterns within qualitative data. The study adhered to O’Brien et al. (2014) standards for reporting qualitative research, a 21-item checklist designed to enhance study quality and transparency. We preregistered the study on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/gb4dy). Ethical approval was granted by METC Leiden Den Haag Delft (ref no. N24.101), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Our inclusion criteria for the present study were: a) age 18 years or older; b) having previously received a formal diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder from an authorised professional, based on DSM-5 or DSM-5-TR criteria; c) fluency in Dutch; d) having used the SAM app for at least one week, and e) willingness to participate in the interview. Exclusion criteria included: a) not owning a smartphone or tablet with a stable internet connection; b) prior involvement in the design of the SAM app or participation in the previous qualitative study; c) any professional or personal relationship with one of the researchers; and d) currently receiving basic or specialised mental health care 2 . Use of the SAM app was not verified; however, participants provided coherent and detailed accounts of their user experiences, suggesting adequate engagement.

We recruited participants in two ways: (1) via email from a database of individuals who had participated in the preceding RCT on the SAM app (Spaargaren et al., 2025), and (2) through autism-related groups on social media platforms (e.g. LinkedIn, Facebook, and WhatsApp).2 If respondents had not previously used the SAM app, we asked them to do so daily for a minimum of one week prior to the interview. In the digital application form, we asked participants to rate their overall user experience using an item derived from the Dutch version of the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8; De Brey, 1983): ‘Overall, how satisfied are you with the SAM app?’ (Very satisfied = 4; Quite satisfied = 3; Quite dissatisfied = 2; Very dissatisfied = 1). A preliminary analysis by the first author after 12 interviews indicated that data saturation had been reached, as no new codes were identified in the final two interviews. However, as the sample included more satisfied than dissatisfied users (8 vs. 4), we recruited three more dissatisfied participants to ensure a more balanced exploration of perspectives. Participant characteristics are provided in Table 1. Independent Welch’s t-tests and a chi-square test revealed no statistically significant differences between satisfied and dissatisfied in age, duration of app use, and education level, although the mean age in the satisfied group (M age = 46.9) was notably lower than in the dissatisfied group (M age = 57.7). However, as the RCT on the SAM app showed no difference in app use as a function of participant age (Spaargaren et al., 2025), and our analysis did not identify systematic age-related differences in how participants engaged with or used the app (e.g. in terms of digital literacy, general mHealth app experience, or app-related stressors), we did not pursue additional recruitment to equalise age distributions.

The SAM app was developed by Emerhese Flevoland, a specialised autism centre of GGz Centraal, in co-creation with autistic adults using Design Thinking approaches (Voorheis et al., 2022). The Netherlands Organisation for Applied Scientific Research (TNO) collaborated during the prototype phase. The app is freely available in the Google Play Store and Apple App Store. Procedure

Each respondent was interviewed by the first author via video call software for a maximum of 60 minutes. This researcher is autistic and had prior experience in conducting qualitative research, promoting effective communication with autistic participants (Crompton et al., 2020). At the beginning of each session, he introduced himself, explained the purpose of the study, and collected demographic information (age, gender, paid employment, co-occurring conditions, and duration of SAM use). Interviews then commenced with the open-ended question ‘What do you think of the SAM app?’, with follow-up questions tailored based on participants’ responses. To collect comprehensive data, we created an interview guide derived from the factors of the UX-TAM as a flexible framework (see Appendix). We explored both positive and negative experiences in relation to each UX-TAM factor and app feature. Following the interviews, we transcribed digital audio recordings in clean-verbatim form using Whisper Diarization (Ashraf, 2024) and manually checked them for accuracy.

Data analysis

We followed Braun and Clarke (2006) six-phase framework to perform thematic analysis. Braun and Clarke’s phases include 1) familiarising with the data, 2) coding the data, 3) identifying (sub)themes, 4) reviewing themes, 5) defining and naming themes, and 6) writing up the results. As preregistered, we initially planned a more deductive analytic approach informed by UX-TAM domains. However, participants’ accounts frequently spanned multiple domains and raised issues beyond the UX-TAM. We therefore adopted a more inductive approach, with codes and themes developed from the data to better reflect the breadth and nuance of participants’ experiences. We chose to retain a more semantic and detailed set of themes to best reflect the diversity and nuance of participants’ experiences and to prevent merging themes in ways that might have obscured practical implications for improving the SAM app and similar mHealth tools. Coding was conducted using ATLAS.ti (https://www.atlasti.com/) independently by the first author and an autistic medical worker with prior experience in coding qualitative data. We discussed discrepancies in coding of the first five transcripts and resolved them collaboratively. As no notable disagreements emerged in the sixth and seventh transcripts, the first author coded the remaining interviews alone. Inter-rater reliability, as calculated by the percentage of agreement in codes assigned to the extracts, averaged 71%. In the synthesis, we refrained from quantifying themes to prevent suggestions of statistical generalisability and to preserve the depth and richness of participants’ lived experiences (Giorgi, 2009).

Positionality statement

AvA is an autistic PhD candidate who contributes to the development of the SAM app as an expert by experience. He used the SAM app himself for three months and found it helped him to improve his stress recognition. YR is a psychiatrist who founded the SAM app and grew up in a neurodiverse family. KH, SB, and AS are autism researchers who contributed to this study as independent investigators. While they have previously collaborated in studies on the SAM app, they have not been involved in its development or implementation.

As several researchers had prior involvement in the SAM app, we acknowledge that this positionality may have influenced the research process. Our familiarity with autism and the app’s intended mechanisms likely supported nuanced interviewing and analysis of user experiences. However, we may also have unintentionally framed questions and interpretations in ways that reflected our assumptions, such as by privileging favourable accounts. Additionally, participants may have been less willing to express criticism to researchers affiliated with the app, or, conversely, may have been more critical, hoping this would lead to direct improvements. To reduce these potential influences, we purposively recruited dissatisfied users, explicitly invited both positive and negative experiences, and included verbatim quotations in the results to enhance transparency. We also conducted initial coding with an independent second coder and reviewed the final synthesis within the wider research team.

Results

Our final coding tree consisted of eight main codes, 10 subcodes (based on app features), and 16 sub-subcodes. The main themes were (1) Initial Interest, (2) Trust, (3) Depth of Features, (4) Fixed Questionnaire Intervals, (5) Relevance of Stress Tips, (6) Questionnaire Design, (7) Frequent Login Requirement, and (8) Interface. Summary of outcomes.

Initial interest

Participants’ perceptions of the SAM app prior to its use fell into three distinct categories. The first group expressed enthusiasm. They reported challenges in recognising, recalling, managing, and communicating their stress levels and hoped the app would help improve these skills.

I was really immediately like, okay, this is going to be useful, because this is really the thing I get stuck in. [...] If you then have a kind of overview of [stress levels of] the whole day, that can actually give a lot more clarity. [S5]

The second group initially held a more neutral stance and started using the app out of curiosity or after encouragement from a family member.

Well, what I thought, it was just kind of out of curiosity really [...] I do want to see if the app can support me, because I can feel depressed sometimes. [S2]

Finally, a group of dissatisfied users felt the app would be unnecessary for them. They either believed they had already learned to cope with their stress effectively or did not experience high stress levels. They participated in the studies on the app to support the researchers or to evaluate its potential benefits for others.

I have a very relaxed life. I am a bit of a granny and a housewife. […] But yes, that recovering [from stress] and putting myself back on track... I can imagine that someone who is quite young has less experience in that of course. Less for me now that I know myself well. [D1]

Trust

Another factor driving interest in the app was the sense of trust in both its developers and the app itself. Participants generally expressed this trust, providing five reasons: the app’s development by a mental health care provider, its co-creation with autistic peers, its non-profit nature, its scientific foundations, and data security measures.

At least it is not some big tech-like thing, but our [autistic] people are also behind it. [...] Well, that does give a bit more confidence in such an app. That gives you the idea that there are people working on it who get it. [S4]

I think there is quite a lot of insight on that. Which questions do affect [stress levels] and which questions affect it less. I think there has been conducted thorough research on that, so I think it is fine what comes out [of the questionnaire]. [S8]

Dissatisfied users, in turn, could express mistrust toward the app. This scepticism stemmed from negative past experiences with mental health care, doubts about its scientific foundations, and data privacy concerns.

It said scientifically supported. And then I wondered, but what exactly is scientifically supported? […] Where is the evidence that these tips do something? [D4]

One that also seriously bothers me, but probably I am just too distrusting, privacy. I checked it out on the website too, but also now again in the app. It simply says that you have insights into everything I write in my journal. I find that very wrong. I do not think that is acceptable. [D3] 3

Depth of features

After using the SAM app, the satisfied users praised its ability to help them reflect on their stress levels and discover causes and patterns through the insight pages. Additionally, they felt that the stress tips provided ‘a good number of suggestions’ [S6].

I felt that [the app] reminded me every now and then of how I felt. I was never doing that because I always put it all away. And now you are reminded every few hours anyway, of say, how did you actually feel? So I thought it was really good for that. [S3]

The app really made me know like okay, these are the moments when I am struggling and we need to do something about that. [S7]

Conversely, dissatisfied participants reported that the SAM app’s features were too superficial. Seven specific limitations were discussed. First, the four-hour stress measurement windows being too broad, allowing for too many activities and stress fluctuations to draw useful insights. This perceived limitation was exacerbated by the cap of five selectable activities per questionnaire. Second, only measuring stressors from the past four hours, overlooking earlier or anticipated events. Third, offering only four stress scores, with D7 suggesting a more nuanced scale from 0 to 10. Fourth, not differentiating between distinct types of stress, such as eustress (stress perceived as beneficial), overstimulation, and trauma-related stress. Fifth, only offering coping support without addressing underlying stressors. Sixth, stress tips being perceived as too generic. Seventh, a lack of integration options with other tools, such as biological stress measurement devices. Notably, this final issue was also raised by satisfied participants. To overcome some of these limitations, participants suggested implementing a ‘traffic light model’, which includes a personalised overview of feelings, behaviours, and coping strategies associated with each stress level.

I found [the app] quite superficial. I found it mostly relieving symptoms instead of causes and not very helpful. [...] You measure if you have experienced some sort of stress and then a tip follows, like go for a walk or listen to music. And that is it. [D4]

In essence, they deny the whole existence of trauma. In essence, they deny the whole existence of RSD [Rejection Sensitivity Dysphoria]. In essence, they deny the whole experience of communication problems, double empathy. Sure it is there in the background if you can think of that you felt stress because you just played sports. But it is not explored further. [D3]

Fixed questionnaire intervals

Another recurring topic that divided user satisfaction was the fixed four-hour questionnaire intervals. Dissatisfied participants expressed being too busy with their daily responsibilities, particularly work commitments, to follow this rigid schedule. Attempting to do so nonetheless often resulted in increased stress and frustration.

I like structure, but my days are not that structured. And you had to complete the SAM app [questionnaires] within an hour. So when I thought about it, it was just too early and then you cannot fill it out. And then I thought about it again and then I was one minute too late. And I did not like that. I really did not like that. [D2]

I have to work and I am not available at fixed times. So I missed the questionnaires very often. [...] That in itself causes stress, so to speak. [D5]

In contrast, satisfied participants reported fewer issues with the fixed intervals. One group initially found adhering to the rigid schedule stressful, but they learned to manage it over time. Others appreciated the structure it provided, as it helped them maintain a daily routine.

Before, I was very strict about it, like, I must [fill out the questionnaires] every time. And now I am like, it is for my own use, so if I do not fill it out once… If I felt bad, I would do it, so it is fine, I do not have to make such a problem about it anymore. [S3]

It maintains a certain structure. I find that pleasant. [...] I just like that, that you know what you are up to. [S4]

Nevertheless, both groups of participants suggested adaptations to the fixed questionnaire intervals. These included extending the one-hour windows, allowing users to customise the timing of all questionnaires, allowing users to complete missed questionnaires retrospectively, adding an option to complete extra on-demand questionnaires, introducing more but much shorter questionnaires (which D4 named a ‘bleep diary’), and removing the time constraints altogether.

Relevance of stress tips

Both satisfied and dissatisfied users perceived the stress tips offered after completing a questionnaire as insufficiently tailored to their personal circumstances. They provided five suggestions to improve this feature: asking users to enter the source of stress prior to receiving a tip, encouraging them to think of an action themselves first, tailoring tips to the time of day, categorising tips as ‘inside’ and ‘outside’ instead of ‘at home’ and ‘not at home’, and offering multiple tips instead of just one.

I had filled out that I had been puzzling behind the PC. And then as one of the tips, [the app said] go puzzle behind the computer. Yes, that is exactly what I was doing. So I found those tips more stress-inducing than stress-reducing. [D6]

The stress tips could be a bit better depending on the time of day. Look, if I am in bed in the evening, around 10 or 11 o'clock, then ‘go outside to cycle’ is not really a stress tip that is useful. [S8]

Questionnaire design

While one group of satisfied participants described the in-app stress questionnaire as ‘well-structured’ [S1] and ‘immediately very clear’ [S5], other satisfied and dissatisfied users could encounter three challenges in completing it. First, participants reported that their alexithymia could make them struggle to answer questions about their emotional states.

The questions on how you felt are also difficult for me, because those are exactly the things I am not aware of. So it is difficult to indicate directly whether I was anxious, because those are things I do not feel directly. [D6]

Second, participants could find it difficult to answer questions that required comparing their feelings to their ‘normal’ state, as they acknowledged this normal state could fluctuate over time.

If you are constantly in the red, what are you supposed to consider normal? [...] Sure, relative to a baseline, but in my case that baseline was already 21 months ago. [D7]

Third, participants could find the questionnaire too long and repetitive, which discouraged them from completing it and carefully reflecting on each question. Proposed solutions included an option to personalise the questionnaire by selecting specific questions, shuffling the question order, drawing questions from a larger pool, and providing a quicker stress score when initial answers indicate low stress.

I found the questionnaire, especially if you have other obligations, quite long. It is of course not that many questions in itself, but in my experience it still took me too long. [D5]

Frequent login requirement

Both satisfied and dissatisfied participants noted that the repeated requirement to log in was frustrating. Users are required to enter a five-digit PIN code each time they open the app and to log in using their email address and password once per week, in accordance with privacy regulations.

After a certain amount of time that you have not used the app for example, or sometimes you have used it all the time, it logs itself out and you have to log in again. Yeah, that is a minor irritation thingy. [S6]

At some point I stopped using [the app], because every time… What frustrated me most was that after so many times you get thrown out and had to log in again. [D5]

Interface

Finally, participants largely praised the app’s interface. They appreciated the calming design, intuitive navigation, predictability, absence of coercive or pressuring elements (such as rewards or guilt-inducing messages), personalisation options, and the ability to receive extra guidance through an info button on several pages. That said, some dissatisfied participants found the accessible language ‘childish’ [D3] and ‘patronising’ [D4]. Suggestions for further improvement included making the insight and stress tip pages more prominent, as these could be overlooked, and adding more colour personalisation options.

It is quiet. That is pleasant. No overwhelming stuff and it is uncluttered. It is clear, which is nice. [S1]

I immediately realised, oh yes, this is made for people with autism. Nice and calm, really clear, with lots of explanations, but in very small steps. [S5]

Discussion

Our qualitative study examined the user experiences of the SAM app among adults with autism not receiving mental health care. Both satisfied and dissatisfied users praised the app’s focus on enhancing stress recognition and appreciated its non-coercive, intuitive, and minimalist interface. However, they found the app’s stress management tips insufficiently tailored to their context and the repeated requirement to log in frustrating. Opinions about the design of the stress questionnaire were more mixed. Importantly, four key factors distinguished satisfied from dissatisfied users. Satisfied participants reported a stronger need for stress-reducing support and expressed greater trust in the app’s developers and its scientific foundations, while dissatisfied users were more likely to perceive the app’s features as superficial and experienced more strain from attempting to adhere to its demands.

Prior to using the app, most participants reported challenges with stress recognition and management and viewed the app as potentially helpful. According to the Health Belief Model (Rosenstock et al., 1988), individuals are more likely to engage in health-related activities when they perceive themselves vulnerable to health risks. Supporting this model’s relevance to mHealth, Paganin et al. (2023) found a significant positive association between perceived health risks, perceived usefulness, and the intention to use a work-related mobile stress app. Participants’ emphasis on the need for improved stress support thus underscores the app’s perceived usefulness, which, according to the UX-TAM, indicates strong potential for engagement.

Nevertheless, dissatisfied users ultimately considered the SAM app minimally useful, citing superficial content. As suggested by these participants, integrating more personalised or in-depth stress-reducing features, such as psycho-educational content or in-app exercises, could help sustain their engagement. At the same time, as Nuske and Mandell (2021) noted, the extensive support needs of some autistic adults may exceed what self-guided mHealth interventions can provide. For instance, the SAM app is designed for daily stress rather than the complex or trauma-related stressors described by some participants. Thus, while SAM may offer a meaningful alternative, it cannot fully substitute professional care. Other options must remain available, and future research should further examine for which autistic adults standalone mHealth tools are likely to be effective.

Participants’ views on the SAM app’s ease of use were more nuanced. The app was generally perceived as intuitive, but several functions, particularly the requirement to complete questionnaires at fixed intervals, could induce stress and frustration rather than alleviate it. These experiences align with technostress, which refers to emotional, cognitive, and physical strain caused by technological demands (Sanjeeva Kumar, 2024). In the preceding RCT on the SAM app, 42.9% of users in the intervention group reported technostress, and these individuals used the app significantly less (Spaargaren et al., 2025). Similarly, an RCT evaluating a mindfulness-based app for adults with autism found that 53% of users reported experiencing at least slight emotional discomfort while using the app (Li et al., 2025). In our study, although satisfied participants also reported technostress, dissatisfied users appeared to be less able to manage it. This difference may reflect user characteristics and lifestyle-related factors; for instance, we noted that dissatisfied users typically reported a greater drive to adhere to the app rigidly and often felt constrained by busy schedules.

Nearly all participants praised the SAM app’s hedonic quality, especially its minimalist, non-coercive design. This resonates with autistic people’s well-reported desire for structured, clear, and predictable digital environments (Rapp et al., 2018). Such preferences may be rooted in their common challenges with heightened sensory sensitivity (Ben-Sasson et al., 2019) and differences in cognitive processing (Frith, 2012). For example, Yaneva et al. (2019) found that browsing web pages took greater cognitive effort for autistic than for allistic adults. While prior literature primarily focuses on how to avoid technostress resulting from overstimulation in adults with autism (e.g. Li et al., 2024; Silveira et al., 2020), our participants’ positive descriptions of the SAM app (e.g. ‘nice and calm’ or ‘pleasant’) suggest that optimally stimulating designs may also play a role in stress reduction (‘techno-eustress’; Tarafdar et al., 2017) and support engagement. These characteristics warrant caution against incorporating features in mHealth apps commonly used to increase adherence, such as animations (Wu et al., 2020) and gamification (Liu et al., 2023), which may overstimulate and contribute to technostress. Consistent with this claim, a study on a gamified app designed to support autistic adults in increasing physical activity found that participants who increased their activity levels during the intervention phase also reported heightened anxiety (Lee et al., 2022), which the authors later attributed to excessive disruption of daily routines (Lee et al., 2024).

Trust also emerged as an important factor. Satisfied participants reported a sense of trust in both the SAM app and its developers, while some dissatisfied users expressed mistrust. Although trust is not explicitly included in the UX-TAM, it is frequently identified as a distinct and important contributor to technology adoption (Schuetz et al., 2024). For example, a meta-analysis of 221 studies by Kuen et al. (2023) reported a substantial positive association (r = .50) between trust and intention to use technology. As mirrored in our findings, these studies often distinguish between trust in the provider and trust in the technology itself, both of which predict intention to use equally strongly (Kuen et al., 2023). Notably, several expressions of distrust in the present study were based on misconceptions, such as the belief that the app’s designers could access users’ journals. Clearer communication about the app’s functionality may therefore increase trust and encourage use.

Taken together, the findings suggest an overarching tension: features designed to provide structure and support may also generate burden, frustration, or technostress, depending on users’ needs and contexts. Although such tensions are not unique to autistic adults (Sanjeeva Kumar, 2024), they may be particularly relevant in this population, as structure and predictability are often valued, whereas disruptions or added demands may more readily increase stress (Rapp et al., 2018). These tensions also appeared to operate interdependently. For example, participants desired more precise monitoring, yet also experienced stress from adhering to the questionnaire schedule. Likewise, those who expressed trust in the app were often satisfied despite reporting frustrations, suggesting that trust may have shaped how app limitations were interpreted. Similar patterns have been reported in technostress research, where strain is lower when effort is matched by perceived benefits (Pansini et al., 2023) and when demands are appraised as challenges rather than hindrances (Califf et al., 2020). Greater personalisation may help mitigate such tensions and accommodate diverse user needs (Berger et al., 2023). However, given participants’ appreciation of the app’s minimalism, such features should be carefully balanced against the risk of increasing complexity or burden.

A specific purpose of this research was to explore user experiences of autistic adults not receiving mental health care. Their accounts were largely consistent with those receiving mental health care, who also reported high perceived usefulness alongside technostress-related hindrances (Hoeberichts et al., 2025). In addition, dissatisfied participants in the current study often described being too busy to engage with the app, particularly due to work obligations. This barrier aligns with findings from a systematic review that identified time constraints as a common predictor of non-adherence (Beatty & Binnion, 2016). In contrast, other dissatisfied participants noted having a relaxed lifestyle (e.g. due to being retired) or already possessing effective coping skills, suggesting limited potential for further stress reduction. These characteristics—time constraints and reduced support needs—may be more common among autistic adults not in treatment and may partly explain the reduced effectiveness of the app observed in this group (Spaargaren et al., 2025).

Limitations

We acknowledge four limitations of this study. First, the qualitative design, small sample size, and the selectivity of our sample (generally high educational level, verbal, middle-aged, relatively many women) limit transferability (Collier & Mahoney, 1996; Sandelowski, 1995). Second, our recruitment of participants with prior experience with the SAM app may have biased the sample towards individuals with more explicit or polarised opinions. Similarly, recruitment through social media may have favoured autistic adults who are more active within the community. Third, all interviews were conducted by the same researcher. While this ensured consistency, it also increased the likelihood of interviewer bias. Fourth, a limitation of the UX-TAM is its focus on internal factors that drive app use (Mlekus et al., 2020). This focus may have led us to overlook external influences, such as social norms and stigma surrounding mHealth (Kim et al., 2022; Venkatesh et al., 2003).

Finally, while initial adoption of the app is a prerequisite for experiencing potential benefits, it is important to note that sustained mHealth use does not necessarily imply increased effectiveness (Eysenbach, 2005). People may not discontinue app use not only due to dissatisfaction but also because their goals have been achieved or because they perceive that they have extracted all its available benefits, a phenomenon referred to as e-attainment (Sanatkar et al., 2019). In light of the technostress reported by some participants, it is possible that for certain users a brief but intensive period of SAM app use may be sufficient to support stress recognition or reduction, whereas prolonged use may only contribute to technostress. Future research should therefore avoid overreliance on sustained app use or drop-out rates as proxies for effectiveness.

Conclusion

Although participants’ experiences with the SAM app were diverse, their strong interest in its underlying aim suggests substantial potential for standalone mHealth support tailored to autistic adults. Their insights point to various concrete directions for improving the app. Importantly, our findings indicate that future adaptations should carefully balance the provision of structure and support with the risk of inducing technostress. Once such changes are implemented, quantitative research should therefore reassess the app’s effectiveness. Exploring potential mediators (e.g. alexithymia, coping skills) and moderators (e.g. baseline stress, employment status) may further clarify mechanisms of action and identify subgroups for whom the app is more effective.
