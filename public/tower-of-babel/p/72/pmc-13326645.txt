asms: finding allele-specific methylation in human genomes without phasing
Authors: Emanuele Raineri, Miguel Ángel Esteve Marco, Eloi Casals, Anna Esteve Codina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllele-specific methylation (ASM) refers to the differential DNA methylation between two alleles at a specific locus. This phenomenon can be driven by genomic imprinting (where gene expression depends on the parent of origin) or by genetic variants (that may affect DNA–protein binding), both of which play crucial roles in gene regulation and contribute to normal biological variation and disease. ASM can be tissue- or cell-type specific, which adds an extra layer of complexity to its analysis. Detection methods typically rely on phasing using genetic variants, a process that is computationally intensive and can fail in regions with low heterozygosity. To overcome these limitations, we developed asms (Allele-Specific Methylation Scanner), a tool that identifies potential ASM loci from methylation data without requiring prior phasing by looking at heterogeneous methylation across reads. Although we tested it using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) data, the tool can be used with any platform using MM/ML tags to store methylation in BAM files. asms can examine efficiently thousands of loci (either from a list or through an automatic genomic scan) by segregating reads based on their methylation patterns. When information on variants is available, asms can verify whether distinct alleles correspond to different base modification profiles. asms is available at https://github.com/ecmra/asms.

Introduction

Allele-specific methylation (ASM) can vary across tissues and individuals and can offer insights for the interpretation of GWAS results and mechanisms of gene regulation ([1, 2]). This paper presents a pipeline to help find potential allele-specific methylation patterns, which does not need a previous phasing step. This is the main difference between asms and already existing software that is designed to detect ASM, for example hap-ASM [3] and Methphaser [4] (which uses methylation data to improve phasing), and Nanopore’s modkit. At the time of writing, we were aware of only one other method to study the heterogeneity of a set of genomic regions without prior phasing, introduced in [5] (which runs a likelihood ratio test on short reads). After submission we learned of another similar tool, wgbstools [6]. We ran a comparison with it and gave some results in the supplementary material, together with a comparison against modkit (Supplementary Figs S2 and S3.)

Here, we build upon our work [7], in which we have shown that an EM algorithm can group long reads depending on their methylation patterns. We present an optimized implementation so that it is practical to conduct a genome-wide study: asms has different sub-commands to help focus the analysis on specific regions of the genome and use prior variant information where available, specifically to decide whether a variant corresponds to methylation changes in cis.

Locus-level methylation heterogeneity can arise from allelic differences or the presence of a mixture of cell types within a sample. In the latter case, variation is independent of both parental origin and genomic sequence, which renders phasing-based methods ineffective. asms can still capture these events by clustering reads according to distinct cell types or states rather than alleles, although in this case it is not possible to interpret heterogeneity as a sign of allele specificity. Conversely, when heterogeneity is allele-driven, it may stem from parental imprinting or sequence-specific variants, such as SNPs.

Materials and methods

asms takes BAM files as input, assuming that they contain methylation information encoded in the MM/ML tags as explained in the SAM specifications [8]. It has \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $6$\end{document} subcommands: scan, cluster, filter, scan-vcf, cluster-by-snp, and pval.

These subcommands can be used to compose two kinds of pipelines, as shown in Fig. 1.

Two typical workflows with asms. The first step is selecting heterogeneous regions either by looking only at methylation alone or in combination with genetic variants. These regions can be studied by clustering the reads aligned to them and the output of the clustering can be filtered via a significance test.

On one hand, users can look at a set of regions in a BAM file to check whether these correspond to stretches of methylation heterogeneity, that is, whether the reads aligning there form 2 distinct groups in terms of their methylation patterns. This analysis uses the cluster command, which requires either the output of the scan command or a list of regions provided by the user. On the other hand, it is possible to inspect the genomic loci corresponding to a set of heterozygous SNPs and test whether reads coming from the alternative allele are methylated differently from reads corresponding to the reference allele.

We use a permutation test instead of AIC/BIC because it makes no distributional assumptions and works for non-asymptotic values of read depth.

While filter is designed to run on all the output of cluster, pval computes the P-value of a specific cluster by permutation test. The statistic used in the test is the same: median difference in absolute methylation between the two clusters.

Finally, there are two commands that can be used to look at the relation between methylation and heterozygous variants. scan-vcf checks a list of SNPs and selects the loci around heterozygous variants that have intermediate methylation. By default it looks at methylation in a window of plus or minus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $500$\end{document} bp from the variant. In our tests below we use a list of SNPs generated with Clair3 [9]. cluster-by-snp partitions the reads depending on whether they contain a given variant. Then tests (by permutation) whether the two groups of reads have different methylation in a window (plus or minus 200 bp) around the variant. The command outputs the methylation levels of the 2 clusters and the false discovery rate.

Results

In this section we evaluate the asms workflow and where possible we compare it with allele-specific methylation obtained by SNV calling and phasing. We use the publicly available Ashkenazi samples described in [10]. Reads are augmented with methylation measurements stored in MM/ML tags. For the analysis of somatic SNPs in cancer, we used the publicly available ONT data for COLO 829_2024.03, which consists of a tumor sample of melanoma fibroblasts and a control sample of normal B lymphoblasts: the data include somatic SNPs i.e. variants found in the cancer sample only (the matched control is used for contrast.)

First of all, we ran an experiment on chr15 to validate the clustering approach; we asked whether the read groups produced by asms agree with haplotype tags where these are available. For each region found with scan, we ran cluster and filter, then compared the resulting partition with the haplotag assignments using the Fisher test. Regions were assigned a PASS or FAIL status by filter depending on whether they met the criteria described earlier (Materials and methods). As shown in Fig. 2, PASS regions tend to have more significant P-values when their clustering is compared against haplotype labels, suggesting that the filter criteria are effective.

We then tested on the entire genome of HG002 (mean coverage 77X). Heterogeneously methylated regions do not straddle chromosomes; hence, a natural way to use asms is to run it in parallel across them. It is also possible to run the pipeline on a BAM file containing the whole genome. The BED file produced by scan will contain results pertaining to multiple chromosomes and the rest of the asms components work in the same way. In our test, running scan, followed by cluster and filter, took 3 h and has a maximum memory occupancy of 345 Mib on an Intel® Core™ Ultra 7 laptop. We looked at the relation between heterogeneous methylation and regulatory regions. We used the ENSEMBL annotation [11], which gives the coordinates of CTCF binding sites, enhancers, open chromatin regions, and promoters. We overlapped them with the regions that passed the asms filter and then we reshuffled randomly the coordinates of the filtered regions (preserving the size) 10 000 times so that we can estimate the empirical Z-score for the overlap, as in Table 1. We found a clear pattern of significant overlap (which might be in part driven by CpG density, which we did not control when randomizing).

Discussion

Allele-specific methylation is an important regulatory marker, which can be caused by (among other things) genetic variants in cis and parent of origin effects. The most direct way of finding ASM regions is to call variants from genomic data, phasing the aligned reads, and computing differential methylation across haplotypes. This strategy has some limitations in that it is computationally demanding and the lack of heterozygous variants can render some loci difficult to phase.

Our software helps in the search for allele-specific methylation stretches and the exploration of parts of the genome that might be interesting for further study because they are characterized by methylation heterogeneity across reads, which can include samples containing different types of cells or cells in different states.

We use two complementary approaches: on one hand, building upon concepts tested in [7], we cluster reads based only on methylation measurements. In the manuscript and supplementary materials, we show, as a sanity check, that this approach can locate known imprinted regions, and gives results concordant with haplotypes computed via phasing. On the other hand, we can also cluster reads based on whether they contain a heterozygous SNP and check if methylation changes across the clusters. Clustering on methylation alone might reveal patterns of heterogeneity that are not visible when using SNPs. For the same reason, it cannot ultimately prove the presence of ASM, rather only provide some useful evidence. Another potential cause of methylation heterogeneity is the presence of two cell types in more or less equal proportion in the DNA sample under analysis. Clustering based on heterozygous SNPs can overcome this limitation: it can be done on a pre-filtered list of variants and hence can be faster than phasing the whole genome. A compelling use case of our tool is to look at somatic variants in cancer. We ran a simple experiment on public ONT data to show that we can pinpoint somatic variants that correspond to heterogeneous methylation. Overall, using asms together with phasing-based pipelines can provide a more comprehensive understanding of methylation.

In all our examples we used Nanopore sequencing data. This is for three reasons: long reads help in cluster detection, Nanopore sequencing measures DNA methylation in the same step as the underlying sequence and the standard ONT software outputs the methylation data through the MM/ML tags contained in the same BAM files, which describe the alignments. None of this is a hard constraint, and any sequencing technology can be used in conjunction with asms as long as the results of the measurements are contained in a BAM file with MM/ML tags.

As noted, heterogeneous methylation can have many causes, allele-specific effects among them. What we present is a useful tool to focus the analysis on specific loci, which are promising by having distinctive changes between sequenced molecules.
