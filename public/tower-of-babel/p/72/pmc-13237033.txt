A principled basis for nonequilibrium network flows
Authors: Ying-Jen Yang, Ken A Dill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe great power of equilibrium statistical physics comes from its principled foundations: its First Law (conservation), Second Law (variational tendency principle), and its Legendre Transforms from observables (U, V, N) to their driving forces (T, p, μ). Here, we generalize this structure to nonequilibria in Caliber Force Theory, replacing state entropies with path entropies; and replacing (U, V, N) with dynamic observables (node probabilities, edge traffics, and cycle fluxes). Caliber Force Theory derives dynamical forces and a complete set of conjugate relations: (i) It yields generalized Maxwell-Onsager relations and fluctuation-response relations, applicable far from equilibrium; (ii) It constructs dynamical models from mixed force-observable constraints; and (iii) It reveals relationships—including a generalized Einstein relation, an “equal-traffic” rule for optimizing molecular motors, and a “third Kirchhoff’s law” of stochastic transport—and can resolve some dynamical paradoxes.Subject terms: Statistical physics, Thermodynamics, Biological physics, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Nonequilibrium (NEQ) statistical physics has not yet had the same level of solid and general grounding in foundational principles as equilibrium (EQ) statistical physics has had1. In EQ physics2,3, a phase space is represented by a complete set of observables such as (U, V, N) and is subject to conservation (First Law) and an entropic tendency (Second Law). Legendre Transforms then derive the conjugated force variables, such as (1/T, p/T, − μ/T), as well as Maxwell Relations and near-EQ fluctuation-response relations. These conjugates are forces insofar as they are derivatives of the underlying variational potential, the entropy. Examples of the practical manifestations of EQ physics are: (1) Computing forces is essential in modeling molecules; their net balance identifies stable states and dominant populations. (2) Maxwell Relations give conversions to compute experimentally realizable properties from theoretical model quantities such as S(U). In addition, early theories of NEQ took their cue from EQ, starting from (U, V, N) or (T, p, μ) and the First and Second Laws of EQ, but in so doing, such models were limited to near or local-EQ processes, e.g., requiring baths to be fast-equilibrating for well-defined T and μ4,5.

We develop a framework of principles for NEQ statistical physics, which we call Caliber Force Theory (CFT). In important ways, it resembles the framework of EQ: it entails a conservation law; a variational tendency principle; Legendre transforms that give forces; Maxwell-like relations among forces and observables; and fluctuation-response relations. However, for NEQ, we require two main differences relative to EQ theory. First, the Second-Law-like variational principle for NEQ is Maximum Caliber (Max Cal)6–9, maximizing an entropy over pathways, not Maximum Entropy, maximizing an entropy over states. Second, the complete set of observables is not the (U, V, N) quantities of EQ theory, but rather (π, τ, J), where πs are state probabilities at the nodes; τs are the total flux activity ("traffic”10,11) along the edges, and Js are net fluxes around the cycles12,13. An essential result of the present paper is the construction of a non-degenerate basis for these observables, forming a proper coordinate system that is required for their Legendre transforms.

We note that both NEQ and EQ physics have two main levels of usage: call them micro and macro. In micro, in EQ statistical mechanics, you build a probabilistic model: Given a microscopic state space, maximize the state entropy (as a functional of distributions) and infer the Boltzmann state distribution. In macro, in EQ thermodynamics, you are given a macroscopic function S(U, V, N), and you use mathematical transforms to compute relations between forces, observables and constraints, irrespective of the underlying model. The two situations both utilize Maximum Entropy (Max Ent), but in different ways: In micro, Max Ent gives distribution functions; in macro, it defines forces. NEQ physics has parallels to these two usages: Max Cal is used for model-making, while CFT, developed here, gives forces and mathematics for force-observable-constraint relations.

Results

Establishing the Two Laws

First, we require a complete set of variables that can characterize NEQ steady states (NESS) (see Method A for why steady state). In EQ thermodynamics, the First Law is a principle of complete parameterization for conserved quantities. In particular, energy is a conserved quantity of an underlying Hamiltonian dynamics. And energy conservation can occur through internal (U), mechanical (V), or chemical (N) forms; these define a complete set. In EQ physics, these macroscopic variables (U, V, N) are sufficient to specify the full EQ microstate probability distribution because microstates within an energy shell have equal probability (Liouville’s theorem).

Here instead, for NESS stochastic flows (e.g. for Markov jump processes on a network in Fig. 1a), what is conserved is the flowing agent itself, represented by the conservation of its state probabilities, πnode. These probabilities πnode describe the time fractions of the agent occupying each state (node) in the long term, but they do not describe the agent’s transition statistics. A complete set of observables for NESS is: x = (πnode, τedge, Jcycle) (Fig. 1b). The second type of observables needed is the total flux on each edge: we follow Maes et al. to call the sum of forward plus backward flows the traffic10,11. The traffic τedge and the probability distribution πnode are exclusive as an agent can have the same πnode but different τedge simply by having different transition timescales. The last type of observables needed is the net flows on the network, which must be cyclic because net inflow to a node must balance the outflow at NESS. The cycle flux Jcycle, too, is distinct because Jcycle describes the net flow whereas τedge is the total flow and πnode is not about flow. See Method B on why we take these observables to be fundamental.

There are some considerations in choosing the variables. First, note that generic Markov stochastic flows do not have the same Liouville symmetry as Hamiltonian systems at EQ. Therefore, we need the complete set of mesoscopic observables (πnode, τedge, Jcycle) in order to fully parameterize a generic Markov dynamics. Time scale separation is to Markov dynamics what Liouville symmetry is to Hamiltonian dynamics—it allows all higher-order fluxes to be parameterized by the first-order ones, only (τedge, Jcycle). It is also important that these coordinates be non-degenerate. The vector of node probabilities, πnode, omits one probability (from a chosen reference node, m) to handle normalization, while the net fluxes are described by a linearly-independent basis of fundamental cycle fluxes (see Method B), Jcycle, to handle steady-state flux conservation12,13. Next, we seek a tendency principle for NEQ. For EQuilibria, the tendencies are described by the adiabatic inaccessibility from Carathéodory, or Clausius’ inequality, or Boltzmann’s H theorem for irreversible relaxation, and/or the variational principle of Maximum Entropy (Max Ent) over microscopic states that “derives” the Boltzmann EQ distribution (Gibbs and Jaynes). Here, we seek notions of forces that govern NEQ tendencies. For EQ, such notions are derived from Max Ent; For NEQ, we thus assert instead the principle of Maximum Caliber (Max Cal)6–9, which says that maximizing the path entropy is to dynamics what maximizing the state entropy is to the static, EQ distribution5. At this point, it is worth touching base with the related research area of Stochastic Thermodynamics (ST), which has sought a variational definition of forces since Prigogine18–20. Lacking such a principle, ST adopted the cycle affinity12,13,21. While that correctly identifies the breaking of detailed balance (Kolmogorov’s criterion) and matches energetic driving in EQ-bath-driven systems (Local Detailed Balance), it remains ad hoc in the sense that it is not derived as the derivative of a variational potential. CFT supplies this missing variational basis. First, CFT shows that ST’s cycle affinity is exactly twice the value of the path entropy derivative (its cycle force). Second, CFT shows that its variational cycle force reduces to half the energetic form of cycle affinity, (Δμ − w)/(kBT), only in the limit of fast-equilibrating baths.

Below are four examples to illustrate applications of CFT: a machine driven to Far From Equilibrium; optimizing a machine under constraints; uncovering a stochastic transport law; and resolving a traffic paradox.

Optimizing molecular devices: a noise relationship

In this example, we show how CFT brings capabilities to design and optimize network flows that have not been available before. Consider a simple cycle, say the 2-step molecular motor in Fig. 3a, that takes in ATP in one step and performs an action in the other step. Traditionally, you would make a Markov State Model with rates kij, and you could vary those rates to maximize speed or power27,28 or minimize fluctuations29,30. But, now consider the challenge of design and optimization in the presence of constraints, or to study relationships more analytically among rate variables, even nonlinear ones. For example, how can you maximize the motor’s speed (i.e., its cycle flux, J) given a fixed energy source (e.g., a constant ATP/ADP ratio, a fixed cycle force, Fcycle) and given fixed traffic (forward plus back fluxes)?

A “third Kirchhoff’s law” for stochastic transport

In this example, we show how CFT brings insights about fluctuations on networks where the macroscopic flow behaviors are known. Kirchhoff’s two laws for electrical circuits describe the conservation of current at nodes and that the voltage sums to zero around loops. But these are macroscopic laws that consider only average forward flow rates and don’t address the underlying stochastic back-stepping of the mobile carrier agents that can occur at small flow rates or for small agent numbers. For transport that is stochastic, where fluctuations can be significant, CFT derives a third relationship.

Prior work that has introduced stochastic fluctuations into network flows34–36 is limited in two ways. First, it utilizes a relationship between net fluxes (J), state probabilities (π), and driving potentials analogous to a battery’s voltage. The potentials, can be related to CFT’s cycle force (Fcycle) but only under the assumption of Local Detailed Balance. Second, that description, which is based solely on (π,J,Fcycle), is fundamentally incomplete. In contrast, CFT gives a proper and complete set of coordinates, including edge traffic (τ) and edge force (Fedge).

To illustrate the value, we show here how the CFT framework derives a “third Kirchhoff’s Law” for stochastic transport, a rule that governs network flows where fluctuations are significant. Consider a system where particles can traverse between two points A and B via multiple parallel channels (Fig. 4a). In this example, we denote the forward rates from B to A through channel i as fi and the reverse as ri (not kij for brevity). When the two “battery-channel cycles” are driven out of equilibrium by equal cycle forces, CFT provides a simple and elegant rule. Here, the equal-cycle-force condition corresponds to an equal level of asymmetry in the channels with the forward rates and reverse satisfying f1/r1 = f2/r2. From this, we show that the ratio of net fluxes (J1/J2) through the two channels is identical to the ratio of their traffics (τ1/τ2), and both are determined by the exponential of their respective edge forces (Method I):

CFT forces can resolve dynamical paradoxes

In much of physics, it is better to work with forces than with displacements or velocities. This is because when different actions matter, their forces sum together to tell more complete stories. Force-based modeling can resolve cooperation and conflict among different actions. A key value of CFT is its principled definitions and completeness of sets of forces of different types of network flows. Neglecting any of the three components—e.g. focus narrowly on π, J, and Fcycle—can lead to apparent “paradoxes.”

In prior work, the cycle flux J is often paired with the cycle affinity (2Fcycle) because their product gives the entropy production rate12,13. One might expect these two components to be positively correlated. However, it is important to recognize that they are distinct, as CFT does, because such correlation is not guaranteed unless other degrees of freedom are fixed appropriately. In this section, we give an example of truck delivers on a simple network to illustrate how CFT resolves this puzzle of forces and flows by proper accounting of other degrees of freedom.

Consider two truck drivers, Alice and Bob, both delivering a product; see (Fig. 5a). The two drivers have delivery rates f and f′, but they also have error rates (backrates) ϵf and ϵf′, correspondingly. If Bob—who has the higher error rate than Alice (ϵ′>ϵ)—increases his delivery rate (f′), then the overall net flux of deliveries (Jcycle) from the team of two drivers increases, as expected. However, in contrast, the conjugate cycle force (Fcycle), which is often regarded as the sole driver of the flux, simultaneously decreases (Fig. 5b). This anti-correlation between a flux and its putative driving force cannot be explained if you only account for the force and flux of the cycle alone. The paradox is resolved within CFT by recognizing the crucial role of the edge force, Fedge. As Bob works faster (tuning the physical rate parameter f′), he is effectively tuning two forces simultaneously: the total edge force increases, while the cycle force decreases. A full decomposition of the response in the force coordinate shows that the positive contribution from the increasing edge force overwhelms the negative contribution from the decreasing cycle force (Method J):

This example shows the importance of completeness in a dynamical description, since a perturbation of a network can change multiple network forces.

Discussion

Here, we put CFT into a broader context.

CFT establishes completeness of observables and locality of forces. Past work relied on incomplete sets of flow observables and ad hoc definitions of forces, and lacked a Legendre-conjugate structure interrelating observables, forces and constraints. An example of incompleteness is taking just the cycle flux Jcycle and cycle force Fcycle (or Δμ in the local-EQ case of reservoir-driven flows). This matters in at least two ways that we have illustrated here. First, just like in EQ, different forces can come into conflict, not simply being proportional to their flows. If forces aren’t defined through a single variational principle, they don’t give a proper common currency for resolving conflicts. Second, network flows exhibit an intrinsic coupling between averages (net fluxes) and fluctuations (traffic), as we have shown in the Kirchhoff Third Law situation. This has important ramifications in biology, for evolution and design of molecular motors and enzymes, where fluctuations are crucial for establishing the measured average rates of their biological actions.

CFT is not limited to near-equilibria. Adjacent to CFT in the realm of forces and flows on networks is the field of Stochastic Thermodynamics (ST)12,13,37–40. Unlike ST, CFT is not limited to reservoirs at Local Detailed Balance (LDB), not lacking variational-principle-defined forces, and not limited to incomplete variable sets. The incompleteness of ST is reflected in two long-standing issues that CFT resolves. First, ST’s primary “potential,” the Entropy Production Rate, is not a general variational potential outside the linear-response regime (Method K)19,20. Second, it was not clear what are the right forces that are Legendre conjugated to net fluxes: Hill considered cycle affinities13; Schnakenberg called both the logarithm of edge flux ratio, ln(πikij/πjkji), and the cycle affinities forces12; others called edge affinities, ln(kij/kji), forces41,42. CFT solves these by unambiguously identifying the complete set of forces from a single variational principle and gives non-equilibrium force-response relations. Importantly, the two frameworks also differ in their foundations: ST is typically motivated by energetics, starting from fast-equilibrating baths to define entropy production. In contrast, CFT’s core formulation is statistical, based on Maximum Caliber, and does not require LDB, though LDB can of course be used in specific biochemical applications.

CFT is more than just the principle of Maximum Caliber. We noted above that our comparison, EQ physics, has two usages: micro, i.e. statistical physics, starting from a model and data, and inferring population distributions using Maximum Entropy; or macro, i.e. EQ thermodynamics, starting from a state description S(U, V, N) and deriving forces such as T and p and μ, and partial differential relationships, such as Maxwell’s Relations, among observables, forces and constraints. Here for NEQ, we have exactly the same type of parsing. In micro, we and others have explored and developed Max Cal as a principle of model inference5,43,44, from a limited set of observable constraints, ensuring axiomatic consistency and equivalency to Bayesian conditioning22,25,45,46: given a dynamical model, predict the flows through all the microscopic channels, for example8,47,48. Now in the present paper, we present the macro treatment for NEQ. CFT identifies the complete sets of dynamical variables for the full, force-flow phase space analysis of nonequilibria, deriving conjugate relations that give principled forces, Maxwell-Onsager-like symmetry relations and Legendre transforms for working with variables and constraints. These relations are useful in matters of network principles, design and optimization. CFT also reveals that traditional Max Cal inference for model updating—essentially predicting a transition from one NESS to another—implicitly fixes forces conjugate to unconstrained dimensions (see Supplemental Information Sec. II at 10.1038/s41467-026-71239-9 for more details.).

CFT’s principled approach offers physical interpretation for the mathematical tools of Large Deviation Theory (LDT)1,24,25,49. Where LDT uses abstract tilting parameters as auxiliary mathematical tools to probe the fluctuations of a single process, CFT’s First Law provides the proper coordinates to elevate the parameters into fundamental forces, F, that have clear physical meanings as affinities and parameterize the entire space of dynamics (tilting parameters in LDT did not have clear meanings as affinities). This reveals the Caliber as the true “free energy-like” potential for nonequilibria, with the scaled-cumulant generating function of each specific process generated from the universal Caliber (Method E). CFT thus transforms the analytical tools of LDT into a concrete and generative physical framework.

Some potential next steps. For other Markov or non-Markov processes, we present foreseeable steps and existing obstacles in Method L. For temporal coarse-graining, we note that traffic is a natural observable for identifying fast inter-converting states50,51. And for tackling spatially-extended systems or interacting populations, CFT could be integrated with hydrodynamic approaches49, cluster-expansion-like methods52, or field theories53,54, a direction we have begun to explore55. Beyond these theoretical extensions, CFT could be applied to biochemical or physical design. It offers a framework for enzyme kinetics fluctuation analysis (e.g. Michaelis-Menten substrate to product conversion rate limited by the fluctuation of molecule binding); it provides a force-based analysis for flow paradoxes in logistics (like Braess’s paradox); and it enables the inverse design of biochemical networks, solving for dynamics that meet specific evolutionary targets or physical constraints.

Summary: We have given a principled framework that, for steady state flows on networks, resembles what the two-laws structure provides for material equilibria. It requires switching the conceptual framework: (i) from state entropies to path entropies, and (ii) from complete observable sets such as (U, V, N) to complete observable sets of distributions and fluxes (π, τ, J). This now unlocks the same mathematical power as equilibrium physics has—i.e., of Legendre Transforms and Maxwell-like relations and fluctuation-response relations for general conjugate variable pairs, now for forces and flows. CFT enables parameterization via mixed force-observable coordinates and the complete phase-space analysis with conjugate potentials and fluctuation-response relations. We illustrated these capabilities with simple examples of far-from-EQ Einstein relation, NEQ machine optimization design, and stochastic flow control. A virtue of CFT—stemming from its foundation in entropy as envisioned by pioneers, like Szilárd and Jaynes and others56–59—is its ability to go beyond thermal processes, like heat and work and particle flows, to also model the transport of any mobile agent.

A. Justification for steady-state and long-term limit

Our framework is formulated for nonequilibrium steady states in the long-term limit. The necessity of such focus for a theory of processes has been discussed by H. Qian and one of us22. Here are the concepts. First, the steady-state analysis allows us to isolate a system’s dynamical rules (i.e., its transition rates) from the transient effects of its initial conditions. Transient behaviors can be analyzed afterward by its underlying steady-state characteristics—an ideal parallel to the bifurcation analysis in the mathematics of dynamical systems17. Time-inhomogeneous processes can also be analyzed by the “frozen” steady-state properties at each instance.

Second, the long-term limit (t → ∞) is necessary for characterizing a process. Unlike a static system, such as a collection of spatially distributed units, a process is described by a dynamical rule (the generator, e.g. the set of transition rates) that produces a trajectory of states over time, in principle indefinitely. To capture the properties of the process itself, its statistics must be analyzed over (arbitrarily long) paths, not just vectors of states with a fixed length. The necessity of this long-trajectory limit naturally leads to using the path entropy rate as the core thermodynamic-like potential. These two concepts are intrinsically linked: in the long-term limit, empirical observables converge to their steady-state averages, making the analysis of the path entropy rate the natural way to study a system’s inherent dynamics. Generalization is needed to handle “sweeping processes” without a steady state 60.

B. Constructing the observable basis

The observable coordinate x = (πnode, τedge, Jcycle) is constructed to be complete and non-degenerate. While constructing the node probabilities πnode and edge traffics τedge are straightforward, the basis for net fluxes, Jcycle, requires a specific construction to ensure a linearly-independent basis. This method is well-established12,13, so we simply illustrate the two main steps with an example.

First, a spanning tree is defined, which is a subgraph connecting all nodes without forming a cycle (Fig. 6b, left). The choice of spanning tree is not unique and can be engineered to best suit a system at hand.

We can check the completeness of the observable coordinate x with a quick dimension counts. Suppose transitions are invertible (if transition rate from i to j, kij > 0, then kji > 0), the dynamics then has a dimension equals to two times the number of (invertible) edges in the state space, 2Nedge. Node distribution has a dimension equals to the number of nodes minus one, Nnode − 1, and there are Nedge number of traffic terms on each edge. Finally, the number of fundamental cycles is known to be the first Betti number, Nedge − Nnode + 1. It is “+1” here because the state space, as commonly assumed, forms one connected graph (any state reachable from any other states).

We also note here that the observable coordinate x = (πnode,n, τedge,ij, Jcycle,c) is not unique: users can choose different set of observables as long as the set is complete (obeys the dynamical version of the First Law). As examples, one can choose different reference nodes m or different spanning trees for Jcycle. The predicted statistical properties of the physical observables won’t depend on the choices of the coordinates (by the mathematics of generating functions): The choice just affects which sets of them are chosen as coordinates and how they spanned the others, not their values. This flexibility of coordinates is in fact a powerful feature: it allows users to choose different sets to parameterize, to analyze, and to perturb the system.

Although the specific choice of coordinates x may vary by system, the (π, τ, J) decomposition presented here is fundamental inasmuch as it disentangles distinct physical aspects. First, it separates time-reversal symmetric dimensions (π, τ) from the antisymmetric ones (J). Second, it aligns naturally with dynamical conservation constraints: π handles normalization, τ remains independent of these constraints, and J satisfies Kirchhoff’s current law for stationarity. Finally, π provides the long-term, static view of state occupancy, whereas (τ, J) capture the dynamic flows.

Technical justification of the two NEQ laws

For nonequilibria, we seek to establish a complete conjugate structure based on Legendre duality, a hallmark of EQ thermodynamics. In EQ, this conjugate structure rests on the Second Law as a variational principle, where the Principle of Maximum Entropy (Max Ent) generates the Boltzmann-Gibbs exponential family. To generalize this to NEQ dynamics, we employ the Principle of Maximum Caliber (Max Cal)—the direct, pathway-based extension of Max Ent that gives exponential family for processes 6,8.

The First Law—Completeness and Symmetry: While Max Cal applies to any set of observable constraints, an arbitrary set does not guarantee a complete, thermodynamics-like description of the phase space (the space of processes). In EQ mechanical systems, coarse macroscopic observables like (U, V, N) suffice to derive a valid probabilistic model for all microstates via Max Ent. This is due to the underlying Liouville symmetry of Hamiltonian dynamics, which ensures that unconstrained microstates within an energy shell are equiprobable. Generic open systems, however, lack these Hamiltonian symmetries. Consequently, we require a First Law of Completeness to explicitly identify the full set of mesoscopic observables (π, τ, J) that completely parameterize the Markov dynamics. This shifts the foundational constraint from Macroscopic Energy Conservation (Hamiltonian) to Mesoscopic Probability Conservation (Markovian), replacing the role of Liouville symmetry with the concept of time-scale separation in Markovian dynamics.

The Second Law—From Inference to Representation: Once the average observables (π, τ, J) are fixed, the Markov transition rates are mathematically determined. In this limit, the role of the variational principle (Max Cal) shifts from statistical inference to physical representation. Standard Max Cal is often used to infer a “minimal model” from partial data. In our present framework, where data is assumed to be complete, Max Cal gives the dynamics as an exponential family. Using a variational principle to define structure has strong precedent in physics, paralleling the Principle of Least Action in classical mechanics. The Action principle is not merely a tool for finding a particle’s path (often fixed deterministically by initial conditions), but rather the requisite mathematical structure to define conjugate momenta (p=∂L/∂q°) and ensure the laws of motion satisfy fundamental symmetries. Similarly, Max Cal provides the mathematical engine that validates the Legendre transform for stochastic dynamics. It allows us to navigate the space of processes, defining the Caliber Forces as the rigorous conjugate variables required to derive the NEQ generalizations of fluctuation-response equalities and Maxwell relations.

Defining the forces: Within this Two-Law framework, the Lagrange multipliers (F)—used to compute the constrained maximization of path entropy—are elevated from ad-hoc parameters of an incomplete exponential family into fundamental entropic forces that serve as the coordinates for the entire phase space of dynamics. Their definition as derivatives of the path entropy potential, F=−∂spath/∂x, is principle-based, not arbitrary. It follows the foundational tradition of statistical physics, where conjugate variables are defined as derivatives of entropy with respect to their corresponding observables (e.g., 1/T = ∂S/∂U)3, which can be understood as “entropic forces.” This approach ensures the forces are the correct conjugate variables for later deriving the NEQ generalizations of fluctuation-response equalities and Maxwell relations. As we will also show in the main text and in the next Method section, the forces gain their physical meanings (as affinities) only after combining the Two Laws.

Summary: Finally, we remark on the generalization required and achieved here for nonequilibria. Traditional EQ physics describes the static distribution of time-reversible, mechanical systems governed by a Hamiltonian. Generic NEQ systems, however, require a dynamical framework that accommodates broken detailed balance and that does not rely on a Hamiltonian. While standard Max Cal generalizes Max Ent from statics to dynamics, it remains agnostic to these physical distinctions. By combining Max Cal with the fundamental observables from a First Law of Completeness, CFT bridges this gap: it explicitly distinguishes reversible from irreversible systems via specific variables—cycle fluxes and forces (connecting to Stochastic Thermodynamics)—and shifts the basis from Hamiltonian energy conservation to generic probability conservation.

L. Extensions to other Markov or non-Markov processes

In the present work, we focus on Markov jump processes (continuous-time Markov chain). A similar method for discrete-time Markov chain has been discussed by one of us for an unicircular state-state example22. It is readily extended to multicircules by following the present continuous-time framework.

As for diffusion processes in z∈Rn (Markov processes with continuous space and time), the landscape-flux decomposition of the process’s drift b(z)16,17 shows that the process can be parameterized by the π(z) as the steady-state density, the diffusion matrix D(z), and the divergence-free steady state probability flux J(z) (which is cyclic17,21). Conceptually, the parameterization (π, D, J) corresponds to the (π,Fedge,J) or (π, τedge, J) coordinate in Markov jump process. LDT has showed us how to solve the Max Cal posterior when the observable is composed of state observables and time-antisymmetric transition observables (see Eq. 34 of Chetrite and Touchette25). The formalism exponentially tilts the drift b—as well as the steady-state distribution and net cycle flux (π, J)—but keeps the diffusion matrix fixed D. Fixing D is needed for using the Cameron-Martin-Girsanov Theorem and Feynman-Kac formula to derive the path entropy25 (in order for the path probability ratio, i.e. the Radon-Nikodym derivative, to be well-defined). As far as we know, it is an open question for how to extend the Max Cal of diffusion to constrain on time-symmetric observables and thus to update D as well. The mathematical challenge is in defining path entropy (doing the path integral) rigorously for the continuous but non-differentiable paths 63–66.

Non-Markov processes

Forces and conjugated relationships become effective Markov descriptions. For a non-Markov processes defined on the same discrete network state space, the probability distribution and first-order fluxes x = (π, τ, J) can still be measured, and one can from it construct effective driving forces F(x) and effective transition rates kij(x). These are essentially the forces and transition rates in the effective Markov representations of the non-Markov process, and one can still define effective path entropy rate spath and effective Caliber rate c whose partial derivatives generate F and x. The conjugate relations between x and F still hold mathematically: we can still do Legendre transform to get alternative coordinates; we can still show monotonic relation between the effective forces and the observables; and we still have Maxwell-Onsager like symmetric cross responses. One thing this effective description no longer capture is that the effective Caliber rate no longer generates the correct statistical cumulants (other than the averages). The second derivatives of the effective Caliber rate c are no longer the correct asymptotic fluctuations. It gives the Markov fluctuations from the effective Markov representation, which in general will deviate from the actual non-Markov fluctuations. This mismatch can be used to check whether the process is Markovian, or can direct us to capture more details by including more states and transitions.

The effective Markov representation has a Max Cal derivation. In the main text and here, we start by assuming Markov for simplicity: we start with a space of Markov chain and compute the path entropy accordingly. Max Cal actually allows us to conceptually start from a bigger space of processes—including non-Markov processes—and will still predict a Markov model if the constrained averages are only about state and/or first-order transitions. This has been proven in discrete time with a uniform path probability prior67,68 or when the prior is a Markov chain22. Essentially, this tells us that the effective Markov models are the Max-Cal-predicted minimal model given the state and transition observables. Proving this in continuous time and/or in continuous state space (diffusion) requires taking the continuous space/time limit with the long-term limit. We do not know if this has been done in rigorous math.

For finite-order non-Markov processes, one can incorporate higher-order fluxes for the generalizations. If we know the (finite) order of the non-Markov process, the steps needed for the generalization of our framework are conceptually straightforward. We need the same two principles for non-Markov processes: (1) an extended First-Law-like principle to obtain a complete observable coordinate also spanning the higher-order fluxes, and (2) the path entropy of the non-Markov process and the associated Max Cal and LDT procedures.

In discrete time, the extension is straightforward as one can enlarge the state space to map finite-order non-Markov process to a Markov process69, known as delay embedding in time series analysis. For example, if a discrete-time process Yt is second-order Markov, meaning the next transition depends on both the current state Yt and the last state Yt−1, then one can map this to a first-order Markov by defining the augmented process Zt = (Yt, Yt−1). This is the same idea as converting the second-order Newton’s equation of position to the first-order differential equation with position and velocity. There are both invertible and non-invertible transitions in the augmented Markov process. For example, (1, 2) and (2, 1) can go back and forth, but (1, 2) and (3, 1) can not—it is not allowed to go from (3, 1) to (1, 2). Our framework can handle this: in constructing the complete observable coordinate, we simply discard the traffic variables for those non-invertible transitions—only including traffic variables for the invertible ones. Traffic variables are unnecessary and redundant for non-invertible transitions because traffic, as the sum of edge fluxes both way is equal to the net flux there. With the updated set of First-Law observable coordinates, one can use the same path entropy derivatives to derive conjugated forces for the augmented Markov process. Some of the rate expressions for forces will be different, but the observable-force conjugate relationships will hold.

For continuous time, the extension is nontrivial as the enlarged state space is continuous with much higher dimensionality. And for infinite-order non-Markov processes, new mathematics is likely needed to develop the Two-Law framework.
