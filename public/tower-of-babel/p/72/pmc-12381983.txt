Evaluating gene variations in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease patients using whole exome sequencing and phenotype to genotype analysis
Authors: Hande Aypek, Rumeysa Fatma Balaban, Nuseybe Huriyet, Ebrucan Bulut, Gulsah Cecener, Suat Akgur, Orhan Gorukmez, Ufuk Unal, Guven Ozkaya, Alparslan Ersoy, Aysegul Oruc, Cuma Bulent Gul, Abdulmecit Yildiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is the most prevalent hereditary kidney disorder. Between 85% and 90% of cases result from variations in the PKD1 and PKD2 genes. Over 30 genes have been associated with ADPKD, contributing to its heterogeneity. This study aimed to investigate novel variations in the PKD1, PKD2, and ADPKD-related genes through whole-exome sequencing (WES) and combine the genotype and phenotype data of the patients. WES was performed on peripheral blood samples from 44 ADPKD patients, and variations were evaluated and classified based on ACMG criteria. A heterozygous pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) PKD1 variant was identified in 33 patients (75%). Seven patients had a heterozygous P/LP PKD2 variant (15.9%). No heterozygous P/LP PKD1 or PKD2 variant was found in 4 patients (9.1%), and one of them (2.3%) had a heterozygous pathogenic PKHD1 variant. Thirteen novel PKD1 variations were identified, with nine associated with fast estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline. Potentially pathogenic Variants of Uncertain Significance in ALG9, CEP290, NPHP4, WDR19, and TTC21B were identified in three patients lacking PKD1/PKD2 variants. Survival analysis indicated that patients with PKD1 or PKD2 or without any PKD1/PKD2 mutations experienced a similar age of onset for end-stage kidney disease. The annual decline in eGFR was significantly higher in patients with a PKD1 mutation than in those with a PKD2 mutation, along with a higher Mayo Class. Genetic studies evaluating novel variants in the PKD1, PKD2, and ADPKD-related genes alongside patients’ clinical data are essential for a deeper understanding of ADPKD diagnosis, prognosis, and pathology.

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is the most common hereditary kidney disease. It is characterized by the progressive formation of kidney cysts, often with symptoms like kidney pain and bleeding, leading ultimately to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [1,2]. Patients diagnosed with ADPKD face a higher risk of developing liver cysts, intracranial aneurysms, and cardiovascular complications, particularly hypertension and left ventricular hypertrophy [3]. ADPKD affects more than 10 million people, and the prevalence of ADPKD is estimated at 1:1000 worldwide, with different incidence rates in various populations and ethnic groups [4,5]. Regarding the pathogenesis of the disease, tolvaptan is currently the only approved treatment option that modifies the prognosis for patients with ADPKD [6].

Intrafamilial variabilities, atypical disease presentation, and complex disease pathophysiology make it difficult to predict disease progression [7]. With a better understanding of total kidney volume and the types of genetic mutations associated with ADPKD, predictive models incorporating either or both of these factors have been developed to estimate disease prognosis [8]. The Predicting Renal Outcome in Polycystic Kidney Disease (PROPKD) and the Mayo Clinic Imaging Classification (MCIC) are the current models to predict ADPKD progression. Genetic screening of patients is essential for PROPKD, and it has been proposed that combining PROPKD and MCIC might provide a better yield for disease progression [9,10].

Although PKD1 and PKD2 mutations are the primary causes of ADPKD, the disease is heterogeneous. PKD1 and PKD2 encode polycystin-1 (PC1) and polycystin-2 (PC2), respectively. The levels of polycystin-1 negatively correlate with disease progression rate in a dose-dependent manner. As a result, PKD1 truncating mutations are associated with rapid disease progression, while PKD1 non-truncating mutations and PKD2 mutations lead to a slower progression [11]. PKD1 mutations are found in 75–80% of cases, while PKD2 mutations occur in 10-15%. Approximately 10% of patients do not show detectable mutations in PKD1 or PKD2. In addition to PKD1 and PKD2 mutations, over 30 genes have been linked to ADPKD in recent years. ALG8, ALG9, DNAJB11, GANAB, HNF1B, IFT140, PKHD1, PRKCSH, SEC61B, SEC63, and TSC2 are examples of genes linked to ADPKD [12–14].

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables high-throughput, parallel sequencing of DNA and is widely used in genetic kidney disease research, including ADPKD, to identify novel gene variants and disease-causing genes [15]. Given the genetic heterogeneity of ADPKD and the limitations of targeted sequencing, we performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) on a clinically diagnosed cohort. WES offers broader detection of rare and novel variants across ADPKD-related genes such as GANAB, DNAJB11, IFT140, and ALG9, while targeted sequencing detects only PKD1 and PKD2 variants [16–19]. Although WES has higher initial costs and challenges with PKD1 pseudogenes, its broader diagnostic yield and improved reliability, recent advances in sequencing technology and bioinformatics, make it well-suited for studying genetic heterogeneity in ADPKD [20–22]. Therefore, the study aimed to identify and evaluate novel variations in PKD1, PKD2, and other ADPKD-related genes using WES. Our analyses identified 13 novel variations in PKD1 in the cohort. In silico analyses were performed for variants classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS), and potential pathogenic variants in PKD1, PKD2, and other genes associated with ADPKD were assessed. The results of WES were correlated with the patient’s clinical phenotype to emphasize the significance of genetic analysis in diagnosing and predicting the prognosis of ADPKD.

Materials and methods

Study design: ADPKD patient cohort

A total of 44 patients diagnosed with ADPKD were enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria were based on established ADPKD diagnosis confirmed by family history and radiological criteria described by Prof. York Pei [23]. Demographic information, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), was recorded for each patient. Patients were also asked about the presence of renal and extrarenal complications.

All patients were regularly monitored at the outpatient clinic of the Division of Nephrology, Medical Faculty at Bursa Uludag University, and underwent biochemical and hematological assessments upon presentation. Disease progression was classified as fast or slow based on the annual decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (ΔeGFR/year), using a cutoff value of mL/min/1.73 m2/year [24]. A decline below 5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year was considered slow progression, while a decline above 5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year was considered fast progression. Hypertension status and family history of ADPKD were documented for each patient. The presence of liver cysts and intracranial aneurysms was also evaluated and recorded. Treatment modalities, including RRT and tolvaptan, were reported for all patients. Each patient underwent Mayo classification based on non-contrast computed tomography (CT) imaging. Those with over 6 months of follow-up were selected and monitored until death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study. The study protocol was approved by the Board of the Bursa Uludag University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (No. 2022-8/20). All patients provided written informed consent. The study was designed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Genetic analysis of patients

DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood of patients following the manufacturer’s instructions (E.Z.N.A. Plasmid DNA Maxi Kit, #D6922). WES was conducted for all patients using an NGS approach. Briefly, library preparation was carried out with the Illumina Nextera DNA Exome Kit (Illumina, USA, #20060059), adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines for fragmentation, end-repair, A-tailing, the incorporation of index/barcode sequences, amplification, enrichment of exonic regions, and purification steps. Sequencing was executed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The BWA mem program aligned the reads to the latest human genome sequence (GRCh37). The Genome Analysis Toolkit was utilized for variant calling, ensuring accurate identification of genetic variants. Additionally, the Variant Effect Predictor program was used for variant annotation.

The Sophia DDM™ 4.0 v5.10.46 Platform (Sophia Genetics SA, Lausanne, Switzerland) evaluated single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions (INDELs), and copy number variants (CNVs). A virtual ADPKD gene panel was developed to filter the WES data, concentrating on 38 ADPKD-related genes to avoid incidental findings (Supplementary Table 1). WES analysis identified various types of variants; nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants were classified as truncating, whereas missense and in-frame variants were considered non-truncating. Identified variants were classified as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variants of uncertain significance (VUS), benign (B), and likely benign (LB) by the criteria outlined by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [25]. B and LB characterized variations were excluded from the study. Variant interpretation was supported by the Polycystic Kidney Disease Variant Database (PKDB), maintained by the Mayo Clinic (https://pkdb.mayo.edu/variants, accessed July 2025) and ClinVar: National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), National Library of Medicine (NLM), National Institutes of Health (NIH) [26].

Variants characterized as VUS based on ACMG criteria but LB in the SOPHIA DDM platform were analyzed further with the following in silico analysis methods: Mutation Taster (http://www.mutationtaster.org), Sorting Tolerant From Intolerant (http://sift.jcvi.org), Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/index.shtml), and retained in the analysis if they were potentially significant [27–29]. Cases lacking clear mutations in the ADPKD virtual gene panel were reanalyzed using the WES data to identify P or LP variants. Confirmatory Sanger sequencing was performed for selected variants to validate the WES results and address the issue of PKD1 pseudogene interference (Supplementary Figure 1).

Statistical analysis: genotype–phenotype analysis

We compared and correlated the genotypes and phenotypes of the patients using various statistical tests. Variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while the median with interquartile range (IQR) was utilized for variables with skewed distributions. The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the normality of the variables. For survival analysis, the endpoint was defined when a patient met the following criteria: 1) a 40% decline in eGFR from the date of diagnosis until the last visit, and/or 2) eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2, or 3) initiation of RRT. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test. The subgroup analysis employed the Kruskal-Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables. The Bonferroni test was used for multiple comparisons. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All calculations were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29.0.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patients

A total of 44 patients [26 (59%) male, 18 (41%) female, mean age 47.70 ± 12.14 years] diagnosed with ADPKD were included in this study. The patients’ body mass index (BMI) was 26.04 ± 3.54 kg/m2, and 33 (75%) had a family history of ADPKD. Twenty-six (59.1%) patients had fast progression, while 16 (36.4%) of them had slow progression, and 2 (4.5%) had unknown progression. The progression was detected with ΔeGFR/year, and the cutoff value of ΔeGFR/year was 5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year [24]. Thirty-one (70%) patients had hypertension. Eight (18.2%) patients were on RRT, while another 8 (18.2%) received tolvaptan treatment. Liver cysts and brain aneurysms were observed in 25 and 5 patients, respectively (Table 1). Detailed clinical information for each patient is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Whole-exome sequencing analysis

The PKD1 or PKD2 variant was identified in 40 patients (90.9%), and no PKD1 or PKD2 variant was detected in four patients (9.1%), and one of these individuals (2.3%) has a heterozygous pathogenic PKHD1 variant (Supplementary Table 3). A heterozygous P/LP PKD1 variant was found in 33 patients (75%), and six (13.6%) of them have an additional VUS classified missense PKD1 variant being predicted as potentially pathologic in silico analysis. Seven patients presented with a heterozygous P/LP PKD2 variant (15.9%), and one of them had a VUS classified but potentially pathologic missense PKD1 variant (Figure 1). Seven missense PKD1 variants were classified as VUS according to ACMG criteria. However, they were predicted to be potential pathogenic variants based on in silico analysis and included in the study (Supplementary Table 4). One of these missense variants (SNP c.2081C > T) was identified in six patients, and it is the most common variant in the cohort. There are no entries in the Mayo Database or ClinVar for this variant.

Genotype and phenotype correlation analysis of the patients

The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and suitable nonparametric tests evaluated the relationship between genotype and phenotype in the ADPKD patient cohort. The Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that renal survival is similar among patients with PKD1, PKD2, and those without PKD1/PKD2 variants (Figure 3(A), p = 0.252). Additionally, the Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that renal survival was comparable in patients with truncating and non-truncating PKD1 variants (Figure 3(B), p = 0.597).

A subgroup analysis of ΔeGFR/year was performed, and median values for each parameter are shown in Table 3. Clinical factors such as CKD stage, Mayo class, and PKD1/PKD2 variations significantly affect the annual eGFR decline. Patients with CKD stage 4 and 5 experienced a notably greater annual GFR decline than those with CKD stage 1 (p = 0.059 and p = 0.011, respectively). In contrast, age, BMI, gender, and family history did not significantly influence the annual eGFR decline in our cohort.

Discussion

In this study, we used WES technology to identify novel PKD1/PKD2 and ADPKD-related gene variations to contribute genetic information and increase the impact of genetic analysis on ADPKD diagnosis and prognosis. In our cohort, 75% of the patients have a heterozygous P/LP PKD1 variant, while 15.9% have a heterozygous P/LP PKD2 variant. Around 9% of the patients have no relevant PKD1 and PKD2 mutations. The percentage distribution of the variants was similar in the previous studies [30–32].

Genetic analysis of the patients is crucial to evaluate the impact of gene variation on PC1 abundance and disease relationship [31,33,34]. The disease mechanism of ADPKD is not well characterized, and a dosage effect of PC1 abundance is one of the current theories [11]. Thirteen novel PKD1 variations, 10 truncating, and three non-truncating, were identified in the cohort. Nine of them were found in patients with fast progression, while four were related to the slow progression of the ADPKD. The identification of novel variants and assessment of their impact on disease progression remain critical, given the existing gaps in understanding the pathophysiology of ADPKD. Moreover, incorporating these novel variants into the prognostic models (e.g., PROPKD) score may enhance its clinical utility in predicting disease progression and guiding patient management, but it requires cautious validation through functional studies.

PKD1 is a large gene, and mutations in distinct regions can differentially affect the function of PC1. Mutations located in the 5′ region of the gene affect the extracellular N-terminal domains, impairing ligand binding and extracellular signaling. Variants in the central region may disrupt protein-protein interactions critical for mechanotransduction, while those in the 3′ region can impair the C-terminal domain responsible for membrane localization and interaction with PC2 [35–38]. Truncating variants typically result in loss of PC1 function, while the effect of non-truncating variants needs further functional characterization. Although the pathogenic effect may be independent of the exact location, certain missense variants in specific functional domains may be associated with variable disease severity and later onset of symptoms. The PKD1 missense variant c.11176T > C, located in exon 39, has been previously reported as likely pathogenic in the Mayo Database [39]. In our cohort, this variant was identified in one patient (Patient ID: 8355) who exhibits rapid disease progression and is currently receiving RRT. A novel PKD1 INDEL variant (c.5651_5671del), found in exon 15, lies within the central region of the gene and may impact protein–protein interactions involving PC1. The patient carrying this variant also shows rapid disease progression. Another novel PKD1 INDEL (c.12377_12388del) is located in exon 45, corresponding to the C-terminal region of PC1. The patient harboring this variant presents with slow disease progression, suggesting that this mutation may have a limited functional impact on PC1. Lastly, a novel PKD1 SNP (c.405G > C) in exon 4, situated in the N-terminal region, was identified in a patient with rapid disease progression. Mutations in the N-terminal region may interfere with ligand binding and extracellular signaling functions of PC1. Further functional assays are required to elucidate the effects of these variants on PC1 function and the underlying disease mechanisms. Notably, the PKD1 SNP c.2081C > T was identified in six patients and will be prioritized for functional analysis using in vitro protein expression tools. Interestingly, this variant was also reported in a Turkish ADPKD study [40]. In addition, there are studies that identified a diverse spectrum of PKD1 and PKD2 mutations in Turkish ADPKD patients, underlining the importance of genetic profiling for improved diagnosis and personalized management [32,41].

Comprehensive characterization of VUS variants is a critical step to improve the clinical utility and interpretation of genetic findings. There are technical challenges to evaluating the impact of non-truncating mutations on the protein structure. Functional analysis is necessary, but laborious and inconvenient when applied to all variants. On the other hand, in silico analysis tools are quite a handful and increase the understanding of the non-truncating variations [42]. We utilized these tools in our study to select potential pathogenicity variants. In addition to non-truncating variants in PKD1 and PKD2, other ADPKD-related gene variations were also evaluated (Supplementary Table 6). The impact not only of PKD1/PKD2 but also of other ADPKD-related gene variations on disease progression needs further investigation, but our data underlines the importance of comprehensive genetic analysis of patients for a better understanding of ADPKD heterogeneity and complex disease mechanisms.

In our cohort, four patients, three of whom had slow progression and one had fast progression, did not carry detectable pathogenic variants in PKD1 or PKD2 but had VUS variants in ADPKD-related genes. Patients who did not have detectable mutations in the PKD1 or PKD2 showed a slower progression of ADPKD compared to those with mutations in PKD1 or PKD2 [43,44]. One possible explanation may involve oligogenic inheritance or variants in other ADPKD-associated genes that might cause the ADPKD phenotype. Notably, one patient (ID 8349) carried a previously reported pathogenic PKHD1 variant, typically associated with autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD), raising the possibility of atypical or overlapping cystic disease phenotypes. The presence of VUS in genes such as NPHP4, CEP290, TTC21B, ALG9, and WDR19, all known to be involved in ciliary function, suggests a potential role for these genes in modifying disease severity or contributing to ADPKD-like presentations, particularly in patients with strong family histories and typical imaging findings [19,45]. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of expanding genetic screening beyond PKD1/PKD2 in unresolved cases, especially when there is a clear phenotype.

Genotype-phenotype correlation is a crucial aspect of enhancing our understanding of ADPKD and optimizing disease management. Interpreting genetic analysis results and connecting them with clinical data offers a reliable prediction of disease progression [45,46]. Our study’s correlation between genotype and phenotype indicated that patients with PKD1 variations experienced a greater annual eGFR decline than those with PKD2 variants, suggesting that PKD1 variants lead to faster disease progression than PKD2 variants. Survival analysis revealed no significant differences among patients with PKD1, PKD2, or without PKD1/PKD2 variants. While patients with PKD1 mutations showed a significantly faster decline in eGFR, this did not translate into a statistically significant difference in ESRD onset between the groups in the Kaplan-Meier analysis. This may be attributed to the fact that patients with fast eGFR decline may not have reached the ESRD endpoint within the follow-up period. Additionally, the small sample size may have reduced the statistical power of the survival analysis. Furthermore, patients with either PKD1 truncating or non-truncating variations showed similar renal survival rates and annual eGFR declines. PKD1 truncating variants hinder PC1 synthesis and are linked to severe disease progression and poor prognosis. In contrast, the effects of non-truncating variations cover a wider range, necessitating further analyses to fully understand their impacts, which are generally associated with milder disease [30]. On the other hand, reports indicate that non-truncating PKD1 variations may exhibit effects similar to truncating variants [47]. This could explain why patients with PKD1 truncating or non-truncating variations demonstrate comparable renal survival rates and annual eGFR decline in our cohort.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings and reduce statistical power to detect subtle genotype-phenotype correlations. Due to the limited number of ESRD (n = 11) and tolvaptan treatment (n = 8) cases, detailed time-to-event analyses stratified by genotype were not feasible. Second, although WES provides a comprehensive approach for variant detection, it has technical limitations, particularly in regions of high GC content, repetitive sequences, and genes with known PKD1 pseudogenes [48]. These factors may lead to reduced coverage or misalignment, potentially affecting variant calling accuracy. Although challenges remain because of the pseudogenes of PKD1, advances in sequencing technology and variant-calling algorithms have increased the accuracy and reliability of WES [49]. Finally, a key limitation of this study is the absence of functional assays, such as in vitro protein expression, to validate the pathogenicity of novel variants. The interpretation of novel PKD1 variants as in Table 2, missense VUS PKD1 variants as in Supplementary Table 4, was based primarily on clinical correlations, segregation data, and in silico predictive tools. While these approaches are useful for preliminary assessment, they are not sufficient to establish definitive pathogenicity and remain uncertain. Future studies will focus on functional validation, specifically for novel and VUS missense PKD1 variants, to more accurately assess the impact of these variants.

In conclusion, advancements in sequencing technology provide greater insight into the gene variations that cause ADPKD and their impact on the phenotype. Here, we report 13 novel PKD1 variants and assess the VUS variants in PKD1/PKD2 and ADPKD-related genes using in silico analysis tools to predict their pathogenicity. Additionally, we correlate the results of WES analysis with patients’ clinical data to understand the impact of these variants on disease progression. Each genetic analysis will enhance our understanding of ADPKD and offer valuable information for the clinical management of patients.
