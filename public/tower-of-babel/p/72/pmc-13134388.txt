Dnmt3a-dependent de novo DNA methylation enforces lineage commitment and preserves functionality of memory Th1 and Tfh cells
Authors: Bryant Perkins, Camille L Novis, Andrew Baessler, Linda M Sircy, Monyca M Thomas, Malia Harrison-Chau, Andrew W Richens, Bryce Fuchs, Nguyen X Nguyen, Kaitlyn Flint, Brittany S Winter, Katherine E Varley, J Scott Hale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYFollowing acute viral infection, naive CD4+ T cells differentiate into T follicular helper (Tfh) and T helper 1 (Th1) cells that generate long-lived memory. However, it is unclear how memory Tfh and Th1 cells maintain their lineage commitment. We demonstrate that Tfh and Th1 lineages acquire distinct Dnmt3a-dependent de novo DNA methylation programs that are preserved into memory. Dnmt3a deletion impairs memory Th1 and Tfh cell lineage commitment and functionality, resulting in aberrant Runx1 upregulation that represses germinal center Tfh cell differentiation. In contrast, transient pharmacological DNA methyltransferase inhibition during priming impairs repression of Tfh-associated genes while properly silencing Runx1, resulting in enhanced primary and secondary germinal center Tfh cell responses. Together, these findings demonstrate that Dnmt3a-mediated DNA methylation programming is required to enforce T helper lineage commitment and preserve lineage-specific functions during the recall response to infection and reveal strategies to improve long-lived adaptive immunity against infectious diseases.

INTRODUCTION

In response to acute viral infections, the immune system utilizes humoral- and cellular-mediated mechanisms to clear viruses and provide protection against reinfection. T helper 1 (Th1) cells migrate to sites of infection where they coordinate antiviral responses.1 T follicular helper (Tfh) cells are critical regulators of the humoral immune response that are required for formation of the germinal center (GC), where GC B cells undergo clonal selection, affinity maturation, and differentiation into memory B cells and plasma cells.2 GC Tfh cells are a subset of Tfh cells that are characterized by the highest expression of the follicle homing chemokine receptor CXCR5 and the Tfh-defining transcription factor Bcl6.2–6 GC Tfh cells localize within the GC, express Tfh helper molecules such as IL-21, and provide the most potent help to GC B cells.2 Many studies have demonstrated that the quantity and/or quality of GC Tfh or memory Tfh (mTfh) cells positively correlates with the magnitude of the GC response and/or production of neutralizing antibodies against viral infections.2,7–14 Thus, developing strategies to enhance Tfh cell responses may improve antibody-mediated protection against infectious disease.

Since the establishment of Tfh cells as an independent lineage defined by Bcl6,3–5 research has focused on defining the factors that regulate Tfh cell differentiation and function.15 Early Tfh cells express CXCR5 and traffic to the T:B border, where interactions with activated B cells, including ICOSL:ICOS signaling, promote Bcl6 expression.2,15–18 Several other factors, including the transcription factors Tcf1 (encoded by Tcf7) and Lef1, promote Bcl6 expression during viral infection.19–22 In contrast, IL-2-STAT5 signaling promotes expression of Blimp1 (encoded by Prdm1), a potent transcriptional antagonist of Bcl6.3,22–25 Several other factors restrain Tfh differentiation in this context, including Foxo1, Runx2, Runx3, and Id2.15,18,23,26,27 Importantly, overexpression (OE) of Bcl6 in virus-specific CD4+ T cells increases GC Tfh differentiation and improves GC B cell responses.3 In summary, these transcription factor networks determine Tfh differentiation and influence the quality of GC Tfh responses during acute viral infections.

Tfh and Th1 cells have the capacity to form long-lived mTfh and memory Th1 (mTh1) cells that preferentially recall their specialized effector programs upon viral reinfection.28–39 However, the mechanisms by which mTh1 and mTfh cells maintain lineage commitment remain unclear, especially given that several key regulators—including Bcl6 and Prdm1—are downregulated at memory.28 DNA methylation programming is one mechanism that can enforce long-term cellular identity.40 A growing body of research demonstrates that T helper differentiation is accompanied by changes in DNA methylation,28,39,41–50 though the contribution of gene silencing by de novo DNA methylation on the long-term commitment of mTfh and mTh1 cells is poorly understood.

In this study, we examined the role of the de novo DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) Dnmt3a in mTh1 and mTfh cell lineage commitment and recall responses. We report that Tfh and Th1 cells acquire distinct Dnmt3a-dependent methylation programs that are maintained in memory cells. Pharmacological DNMT inhibition during T cell priming impaired mTh1 cell lineage commitment, resulting in enhanced GC Tfh cell responses during secondary viral infections. Dnmt3a-deficient mTh1 cells also exhibit impaired lineage commitment resulting in increased Tfh recall potential during secondary responses; however, unlike the memory T cells resulting from transient DNMT inhibition, Dnmt3a-deficient memory T cells have impaired Bcl6 reexpression that results in poor secondary GC Tfh cell responses. We demonstrate that Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation at the Runx1 locus is required to repress Runx1 expression in secondary Tfh cells. Our data demonstrate that overexpression of Runx1 impairs Bcl6 reexpression, identifying Runx1 as a repressor of secondary GC Tfh cell responses. In contrast, transient pharmacological DNMT inhibition during T cell priming does not impair Dnmt3a-dependent silencing of Runx1, preserving secondary GC Tfh cell responses. In summary, this study demonstrates that Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation is a critical regulator of mTh1 and mTfh cell programming and identifies a targetable pathway to enhance the GC Tfh cell response to viral infections.

RESULTS

Lineage-specific de novo DNA methylation programming is acquired during primary differentiation and maintained in mTfh and mTh1 cells

Prior research has established that Th1 and Tfh cells that are generated in response to viral infection are transcriptionally distinct.15,28 To validate that Tfh and Th1 lineages acquire distinct transcriptional programs that are partly maintained into memory, we reanalyzed the gene expression microarray data that were collected by sorting effector and memory SMARTA cells into CXCR5− Ly6CHI and CXCR5+ Ly6CLO subsets.28 Example staining of CXCR5 and Ly6C for effector and mTh1 and mTfh cell identification is shown in Figure 1A. Effector and memory CXCR5− Ly6CHI cells had elevated expression of the canonical Th1 genes, including Tbx21, Ifng, Gzmb, Prdm1, and Runx3 (Figure 1B). In contrast, effector and memory CXCR5+ Ly6CLO cells had higher expression of canonical Tfh genes, including Bcl6, Cxcr5, Icos, Cd200, Il21, and Tcf7 (Figure 1B). These data validate that effector Th1 and Tfh cells acquire distinct transcriptional programs that are partially maintained in mTh1 and mTfh cells.

mTfh and mTh1 cells are lineage committed, meaning that they preferentially recall their effector programming upon secondary viral infection.28,37,39 To explore the role of de novo DNA methylation in this process, we performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) on effector and memory Th1 and Tfh cells. Briefly, naive SMARTA T cell receptor (TCR) transgenic CD4+ T cells (CD45.1+), which express a TCR that is specific for the lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) GP66–77 epitope, were transferred into C57BL/6J (B6) mice (CD45.2+) prior to LCMV infection.51 At effector (7 days post-infection [dpi]) and memory (60+ dpi) time points, spleen-derived SMARTA cells were sorted into CXCR5− Ly6CHI and CXCR5+ Ly6CLO subsets, and genomic DNA was processed for WGBS. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified for effector or memory Tfh and Th1 cells relative to naive SMARTA cells, and DMRs were associated with the most proximal genes. This analysis revealed changes in DNA methylation at over 25k regions per cell type relative to naive SMARTA cells (Figure 1C). De novo methylation accounted for approximately one-quarter of the DMRs (Figure 1C).

During LCMV infection, Tfh cells partially take on Th1 cell transcriptional qualities, including intermediate expression of Tbet and IFN-γ.28 To determine if Th1 and Tfh lineages acquire shared de novo DNA methylation programs, we identified de novo DMRs that are overlapping between Th1 and Tfh cells (Figure 1D). Shared de novo DMRs were found at genes associated with Th2/Th17 lineages (Gata3 and Rora), memory formation (Sell, Ccr7, and Bcl2), and epigenetic programming (Dnmt3a, Dnmt3b, and Tet2). These data demonstrate that Th1 and Tfh cells acquire a shared de novo DNA methylation program.

To identify the genes that are de novo methylated specifically in Th1 cells, we investigated the set of de novo DMRs in Th1 cells that did not overlap with de novo DMRs in Tfh cells. This list included de novo DMRs associated with the genes Tcf7, Lef1, and Bcl6 (Figure 1D). We performed a motif analysis on the set of de novo DMRs that were distinct to Th1 or Tfh cells (Figures 1E and 1F) and found ETS-, TCF/LEF-, and RUNX-like motifs to be highly enriched within Th1-specific de novo DMRs (Figure 1E). We next ranked de novo DMRs based on the mean difference in methylation between Tfh and Th1 cells (Figures 1G and 1H), further demonstrating that Th1 cells de novo methylated regions associated with several Tfh-promoting genes, including Bcl6, Tcf7, Lef1, P2rx7, and Il6ra (Figure 1G). We further investigated the location of select de novo DMRs at the loci encoding Tcf7 and Lef1. In both cases, de novo methylation occurred in Th1 cells at conserved intronic regions while remaining unmethylated in Tfh cells (Figures 1I and 1J). These data demonstrate that effector and memory Th1 cells acquire de novo methylation programming that is distinct from that of Tfh cells.

To identify the genes that are de novo methylated specifically in Tfh cells, we investigated the set of de novo DMRs in Tfh cells that were not shared with Th1 cells. This list included de novo DMRs associated with the genes Runx1, Runx2, Runx3, and Prdm1 (Figure 1D). Motif analysis on the set of de novo DMRs that were distinct to Tfh cells revealed enrichment of ETS-, RUNX-, and GATA-like motifs within these regions (Figure 1F). Analysis of the top 50 de novo DMRs that were most strongly methylated in wild-type (WT) Tfh cells relative to WT Th1 cells revealed that Tfh cells acquire de novo methylation at Prdm1, Ifngr1, and Selplg (Figure 1H), all of which were found to be more highly expressed in Th1 cells relative to Tfh cells (Figure 1B). We investigated the location of select de novo DMRs at the Prdm1 and Runx1 loci. A conserved intronic region of the Prdm1 locus was de novo methylated in Tfh cells and was demethylated in Th1 cells (Figure 1K). At the Runx1 locus, de novo methylation was strongest in Tfh cells relative to Th1 cells (Figure 1L). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Tfh cells acquire a distinct de novo DNA methylation program.

These analyses also revealed that the de novo methylation programs were shared between effector and memory cells of the same lineage (Figures 1G–1L), suggesting that lineage-specific de novo methylation was acquired during effector differentiation and maintained into memory. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that several DNMTs were targets of de novo methylation (Figures 1D, S1A, and S1B). Dnmt3a is the active de novo DNMT in T cells.42,43 To determine whether de novo methylation at the Dnmt3a locus correlated with reduced expression in memory and secondary (2°) effector SMARTA cells, we measured Dnmt3a by flow cytometry. We found that Dnmt3a expression was reduced at memory and memory recall time points relative to primary effector T cells (Figure S1C). This may suggest that Dnmt3a is epigenetically repressed during secondary infections. Collectively, these data demonstrate that de novo DNA methylation programming is acquired during the primary Th1 and Tfh cell differentiation and maintained in memory populations.

Early DNMT inhibition enhances GC Tfh differentiation in response to primary and secondary viral infections

We hypothesized that inhibition of de novo methylation would alter T helper differentiation and function. To test this hypothesis, we used decitabine (DAC), which is a pharmacological inhibitor of the DNMTs.52–54 We treated LCMV-infected mice with various concentrations of DAC to identify a dose that may impact CD4+ T cell differentiation while also limiting cytotoxic effects. We found that DAC doses of 0.75 mg/kg did not impact LCMV-specific CD8+ T cell or CD4+ cell responses, regulatory T cell numbers, or viral clearance (Figures S2A–S2D). To assess the effects of DNMT inhibition on primary CD4+ T cell responses during viral infection, we transferred naive SMARTA cells into B6 mice and infected with LCMV (Figure 2A). At 20 hours post-infection (hpi), mice were treated with DAC or PBS (Figure 2A). The SMARTA cell response was analyzed in the spleens of mice 7 days later. DAC treatment did not alter SMARTA cell expansion (Figure 2B), and Tfh/Th1 differentiation was unchanged relative to PBS-treated controls (Figures S2E and S2F–S2H). However, DAC treatment significantly increased the percentage and number of CXCR5+ Bcl6HI GC Tfh cells and the Bcl6 mean flourescence intensity (MFI) was increased in Tfh and Th1 cells (Figures 2C–2E). We concluded that DNMT inhibition during T cell priming enhanced Bcl6 expression and GC Tfh cell differentiation during LCMV infection.

To determine whether DAC treatment increased the GC B cell responses, we infected B6 mice (without SMARTA transfer) with LCMV. DAC treatment during T cell priming significantly increased GC Tfh and GC B cell numbers relative to PBS-treated controls in the spleens of mice at 7 dpi (Figure 2F). We also infected B6 mice with the mouse-adapted influenza strain PR8.55 In this context, DAC (0.375 mg/kg) increased NP311–325-specific GC Tfh and HA-specific GC B cell responses relative to PBS-treated controls (Figure 2G) and resulted in a modest increase (p = 0.1774) in HA-specific IgG (Figure S2I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that early DNMT inhibition enhances the GC Tfh and GC B cell responses to viral infections.

To determine the impact of early DNMT inhibition on memory T cell differentiation, we analyzed SMARTA cells in the spleens of LCMV-infected mice at 60+ dpi (Figure 2A). DAC treatment during T cell priming increased the numbers of memory SMARTA cells relative to PBS-treated controls (Figure 2H) and was attributed to an increase in the number of CXCR5+ Ly6CLO mTfh cells (Figures 2I and 2J). These data demonstrate that DNMT inhibition during the T cell priming enhances mTfh cell formation in response to viral infection.

To determine the effects of early DNMT inhibition on the memory recall response, we performed two different experiments. First, WT mice (without SMARTA transfer) were infected with LCMV and treated with DAC or PBS to generate GP66–77-specific memory T cells (Figure 2K). Forty days later, mice were infected intranasally with the recombinant strain of influenza PR8-GP61–80 (Figure 2K).55 We analyzed GP66–77-specific memory recall response in the spleen and mediastinal lymph nodes (mLNs) at 7 days post-secondary infection (Figure 2K). DAC treatment did not significantly impact the accumulation of GP66–77-specific 2° effector T cells in the mLNs or spleens relative to PBS-treated controls (Figures 2L and S2J), and accumulation of 2° Th1 cells was unaffected by DAC treatment (Figure S2K). However, early DAC treatment enhanced 2° GC Tfh cell responses relative to PBS-treated control in both tissues (Figures 2M, 2N, and S2L). This was associated with an increase in GC B cells in both tissues (Figures 2O and S2M) as well as an increase in the numbers of HA-specific GC B cells in the spleens (Figure 2P). To rule out that this enhanced GC Tfh cell response was due to enhanced primary flu responses in mice that had received DAC treatment 40 days prior, we retransferred PBS- or DAC-treated memory SMARTA cells into naive B6 mice. During secondary LCMV infection, memory SMARTA cells that had previously been treated with DAC had significantly enhanced 2° GC Tfh cell responses relative to PBS-treated controls (Figures S2N–S2P). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DNMT inhibition during T cell priming enhances GC Tfh differentiation during primary and secondary viral infections.

Early DNMT inhibition impairs the lineage commitment of mTh1 cells and increases GC Tfh recall potential

Several pieces of data suggest that DNMT inhibition may be interfering with mTh1 cell lineage commitment. We observed that Th1 cells undergo de novo methylation at the Tfh-promoting genes Tcf7 and Lef1 (Figures 1I and 1J). In addition, early DAC treatment enhances the secondary GC Tfh cell response (Figures 2K–2P and S2N–S2P). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that early DNMT inhibition may impair mTh1 cell lineage commitment, enabling these cells to contribute to secondary Tfh responses. To test this hypothesis, we sorted spleen-derived PBS- or DAC-treated memory SMARTA cells into mTh1 and mTfh subsets and transferred these cells into B6 mice to measure their recall potential (Figures 3A and 3B). At 7 days post-secondary LCMV infection, DAC treatment did not significantly impact T cell accumulation in the spleen relative to PBS-treated controls (Figure 3C). Recipients of PBS-treated mTh1 cells generated the most 2° TbetHI Th1 cells (Figures 3D and 3E) and expressed the highest amounts of granzyme B and IFN-γ (Figures 3F and 3G). Together, these observations validate that control mTh1 cells are lineage committed, preferentially producing 2° Th1 cells and generating few 2° Tfh cells relative to mTfh cells. DAC treatment during T cell priming impaired the lineage commitment of mTh1 cells, resulting in fewer 2° TbetHI Th1 cells (Figures 3D and 3E) and reduced expression of granzyme B and IFN-γ (Figures 3F and 3G). Instead, DAC-treated mTh1 cells produced 2° Th1 cells that aberrantly upregulated Tcf1 (Figure 3H) and had an increased production of 2° CXCR5+ Tfh and CXCR5+ Bcl6HI GC Tfh cells relative to PBS-treated mTh1 cells (Figures 3I–3L). Importantly, recipients of DAC-treated mTh1 cells had increased GC B cell responses (Figure 3M). These data demonstrate that DNMT inhibition during T cell priming impairs the lineage commitment of mTh1 cells and enhances their ability to contribute to the GC Tfh pool during the secondary response.

In contrast to mTh1 cells, the effects of early DAC treatment on the recall response of mTfh cells were minimal. PBS- and DAC-treated mTfh cells produced similar proportions of 2° Tfh and GC Tfh cells (Figures 3I–3K), and Bcl6 reexpression was not impacted by early DAC treatment (Figure 3L). DAC-treated mTfh cells did have an impairment in the production of 2° Th1 cells (Figures 3D and 3E). These data demonstrate that early DAC-treated mTfh cells have impaired Th1 cell recall response while preserving GC Tfh cell recall responses. Altogether, these data argue that DNMT inhibition during T cell priming enhances secondary GC Tfh cell responses by enhancing the potential of mTh1 cells to contribute to the secondary GC Tfh cell response.

Dnmt3a deficiency results in impaired secondary Th1 and Tfh cell responses

To investigate the role of Dnmt3a in primary and secondary Tfh and Th1 cell responses, we generated Dnmt3a conditional knockout (cKO) mice (Dnmt3aflox/flox × R26-CreERT2 × SMARTA) or CreERT2 control mice (R26-CreERT2 × SMARTA). Donor mice were treated with tamoxifen, WT and cKO naive SMARTA cells (CD45.1+) were transferred into B6 mice (CD45.2+), and mice were infected with LCMV. At 7 dpi, we validated that Dnmt3a deletion was successful (Figures S3A and S3B). SMARTA cell expansion was modestly and inconsistently increased in the absence of Dnmt3a (Figure S3C), although Tfh/Th1 differentiation was not impacted (Figures S3D and S3E). We also generated Dnmt3a knockout (KO) mice (Dnmt3aflox/flox × CD4Cre × SMARTA) or control mice (CD4Cre × SMARTA), and similar results were observed in this model (Figures S3F–S3K). We next assessed the effect of Dnmt3a deficiency on memory formation. WT and cKO SMARTA cells were undetectable at the memory stage in initial experiments using B6 recipients; this was resolved by transfer into CreERT2+ recipients (Figure S3L). Following transfer into Cre+ recipients, Dnmt3a cKO (CreERT2+) memory SMARTA cell numbers were similar in spleens relative to WT controls (Figure S3M), and differentiation into mTh1 or mTfh cell subsets was not impacted (Figure S3N). Similar results were observed in the CD4cre model (Figures S3O and S3P). Thus, Dnmt3a is not required for effector and memory T cell responses following acute viral infection.

To assess the effects of Dnmt3a deficiency on the memory recall response, we isolated WT and Dnmt3a KO (CD4Cre) memory SMARTA cells at 60+ dpi and retransferred equal numbers into naive recipients, which were then infected with LCMV. At 7 days post-secondary infection, we analyzed the SMARTA cell response in the spleens of infected mice. Memory SMARTA cell expansion was not impacted by Dnmt3a deficiency (Figure S3Q). However, there were clear defects in 2° Th1 and Tfh cells. Dnmt3a KO memory SMARTA cells produced fewer 2° TbetHI Th1 cells as well as IFN-γ-producing cells and had increased expression of Tcf1 (Figures S3R–S3T). Dnmt3a KO 2° Tfh cell responses were also impaired, as there was a trending reduction in the number of Bcl6HI GC Tfh cells, and Bcl6 reexpression was significantly reduced (Figures S3U–S3W). Mice that received KO memory SMARTA cells had a trending reduction (p = 0.07) in GC B cell responses as well (Figures S3X and S3Y). These data demonstrate that Dnmt3a is required for functional Th1 and Tfh cell recall responses during viral rechallenge.

Dnmt3a deficiency impairs mTh1 and mTfh cell lineage commitment and recall responses

We hypothesized that Dnmt3a deficiency would impair the lineage commitment of mTh1 and mTfh cells. To test this, we sorted spleen-derived WT and Dnmt3a cKO (CreERT2+) memory SMARTA cells into mTh1 and mTfh subsets and transferred these purified cells into naive B6 mice to measure their recall potential (Figures 4A and 4B). Dnmt3a deficiency impaired accumulation of secondary effector cells generated from mTh1 cells (Figure 4C), although this effect was not seen in repeat experiments. Relative to WT mTh1 cells, Dnmt3a cKO mTh1 cells produced significantly fewer 2° TbetHI Th1 cells and had a trending reduction in granzyme B expression and a significant reduction in IFN-γ expression (Figures 4D–4G). In addition, a subset of 2° Th1 cells from cKO mTh1 cells aberrantly expressed Tcf1 (Figure 4H) and cKO mTh1 cells produced more secondary CXCR5+ Tfh cells compared with WT mTh1 cells (Figures 4I and 4J). Despite this increased plasticity, however, cKO mTh1 cells did not have enhanced production of 2° CXCR5+ Bcl6HI GC Tfh cells (Figure 4K), and Bcl6 expression was reduced in 2° Tfh cells that were produced from cKO mTh1 cells relative to WT mTh1 cells (Figure 4L). Together, these data demonstrate that Dnmt3a-mediated programming is required to promote mTh1 cell lineage commitment and functional recall responses.

Dnmt3a deletion also negatively impacted mTfh cell recall responses. Relative to WT mTfh cells, cKO mTfh cells were impaired in their ability to reexpress Bcl6, and there was a trending reduction in the production of 2° GC Tfh cells (Figures 4I–4L). In addition, cKO mTfh cells were defective at producing 2° Th1 cells relative to WT mTfh cells (Figures 4D and 4E). These data demonstrate that Dnmt3a-deficient mTfh cells generate 2° Tfh cells that have impaired Bcl6 reexpression. In summary, these data demonstrate that Dnmt3a is required to enforce mTh1 and mTfh cell lineage commitment and to preserve effector Th1 and GC Tfh cell responses during secondary viral infections.

Dnmt3a is required for de novo methylation in Th1 cells and Tfh cells

Our data demonstrate that Dnmt3a enforces mTh1 and mTfh cell lineage commitment (Figure 4). In the absence of Dnmt3a, we found that secondary Th1 cells have reduced expression of Th1 effector molecules (Figures 4D–4G, S3R, and S3S) and increased expression of the Tfh-promoting factor Tcf1 (Figure 4H and S3T). This may suggest that mTh1 cells fail to silence genes that promote Tfh responses, including Tcf7 and Lef1, which are targets of de novo DNA methylation in Th1 cells (Figures 1I and 1J). Secondary Tfh cells that are Dnmt3a deficient have impaired Bcl6 reexpression (Figures 4L and S3W), suggesting that mTfh cells may fail to de novo methylate and silence repressors of Bcl6, such as Prdm1 (Figure 1K). Thus, we hypothesized that Dnmt3a is required for de novo DNA methylation that targets alternative T helper lineage genes in Th1 and Tfh cells. To test this hypothesis, we sorted WT or KO (CD4cre+) SMARTA cells into CXCR5− and CXCR5+ subsets from the spleens of LCMV-infected mice at 7 dpi. We isolated DNA for whole-genome enzymatic methylation sequencing (WGEM-seq). We identified DMRs between WT and KO Th1 or WT and KO Tfh cells. This analysis revealed 27,937 DMRs in KO Th1 cells relative to WT Th1 cells and 26,513 DMRs in KO Tfh cells relative to WT Tfh cells (Figure 5A). The overwhelming majority of DMRs were hypomethylated (Figure 5A), demonstrating that Dnmt3a deletion results in severe loss of DNA methylation in Th1 and Tfh cells.

To identify regions that were distinctly methylated by Dnmt3a in Th1 cells relative to Tfh cells, we identified overlapping (shared) DMRs between Th1 and Tfh cells. This analysis revealed that approximately 39% of the total Dnmt3a-dependent hypomethylated DMRs were distinct to Th1 cells (Figure 5B). We performed motif analysis on these regions and found that they were enriched for ETS, RUNX, and TCF/LEF-like motifs (Figure 5C). We next ranked hypomethylated DMRs by the mean difference in methylation between WT Th1 and WT Tfh cells (Figures 5D and 5E). Figure 5D shows the top 50 regions of the genome that were more methylated in Th1 cells relative to Tfh cells. This analysis highlights that Dnmt3a is required in Th1 cells to de novo methylate DMRs associated with several Tfh-promoting genes, including Tcf7 and Lef1 (Figure 5D). We analyzed the genomic location of representative hypomethylated DMRs at the Tcf7 and Lef1 loci. This analysis confirmed that these hypomethylated DMRs were de novo methylated in WT Th1 cells (Figures 5F and 5G). These data demonstrate that Dnmt3a is required to de novo methylate Tcf7, Lef1, and other Tfh-associated genes during Th1 differentiation.

We similarly found evidence of extensive Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation programming that was distinct to Tfh cells. By overlap analysis, we found that approximately 43% of the regions that were hypomethylated in KO Tfh cells were not shared with KO Th1 cells (Figure 5B). Motif analysis on these regions demonstrated that they were enriched for ETS, RUNX, and bHLH-like motifs (Figure 5C). We generated heatmaps ranking the top 50 hypomethylated DMRs that were more methylated in WT Tfh cells relative to WT Th1 cells (Figure 5E). This analysis highlights that Dnmt3a is required for de novo DNA methylation of several genes that repress Tfh cell responses, including Prdm1 and Runx1 (Figure 5E). The gene encoding Blimp1 (Prdm1) is strongly de novo methylated in Tfh cells in a region located within the fourth intron (Figure 5H). Of note, this region is demethylated in Th1 cells, which upregulate Prdm1 (Figure 1B). The Runx1 locus had the second-highest number of hypomethylated DMRs in KO Tfh cells (43 total DMRs), including DMRs near the proximal and distal promoters of Runx1. At a select intronic DMR nearest to the upstream transcription start site, we found that Runx1 was de novo methylated in both cell types and that this was strongest in Tfh cells (Figure 5I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Dnmt3a is required for the acquisition of Tfh-specific de novo methylation at the Prdm1 and Runx1 loci as well as other Th1-promoting genes.

Our analysis also revealed that Dnmt3a was required for de novo DNA methylation in the first intron of Bcl6 in Tfh cells and Th1 cells (Figure S4A). Upon further examination, we found that de novo DNA methylation at this region was strongest in Tfh cells relative to Th1 and naive CD4+ T cells and was positively correlated with Bcl6 expression (Figure S4B–S4E). These data may suggest that DNA methylation within the first intron promotes expression of Bcl6. In human B cells, intronic methylation is positively correlated with Bcl6 expression, and one study demonstrated that DNA methylation blocks CTCF-mediated gene repression in these cells.56 Using LiftOver, we found that our intronic DMRs at the Bcl6 locus overlapped with the regions interrogated in that study (Figure S4A). Next, using publicly available CTCF chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data (GEO: GSM3498276) from murine naive CD4+ T cells,57 we found evidence of two CTCF peaks overlapping with two hypomethylated DMRs (Figure S4A). Therefore, our analysis may suggest that this mechanism is conserved across cell types and species and that DNA methylation within the first intron of Bcl6 may positively regulate gene expression.

To determine whether Dnmt3a is required to repress expression of its target genes, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on WT and KO Th1 and Tfh cells at 7 days post-LCMV infection. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between WT and KO Th1 or Tfh cells. Th1 cells had 58 total DEGs (KO vs. WT Th1) and Tfh cells had 1,126 total DEGs (KO vs. WT Tfh). In Th1 cells, we found that 74% of the 39 upregulated genes were hypomethylated in KO Th1 cells (data not shown), including Lef1 (Figure 5J). To validate this finding, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using a dataset of genes that were downregulated in Lef1-overexpressing Th1 cells.19 For this analysis, we ranked DEGs by log2 fold change from highest (upregulated) to lowest (downregulated) in KO Th1 cells. This analysis demonstrated a significant negative enrichment of this gene set (Figure 5J), demonstrating that genes that were repressed by Lef1 overexpression were downregulated in KO Th1 cells. In Tfh cells, we found that 62% of 511 upregulated genes were hypomethylated in KO Tfh cells (data not shown), including Runx1 (Figure 5K). To validate this finding, we performed GSEA on transcriptional targets of Runx1 as identified by Reactome. For this analysis, we ranked DEGs by log2 fold change from highest (upregulated) to lowest (downregulated) in KO Tfh cells. This analysis demonstrated a significant and positive enrichment of genes that are regulated by Runx1 (Figure 5K), consistent with increased expression of Runx1 in KO Tfh cells. Collectively, these data demonstrate that Dnmt3a is required to de novo methylate and repress Lef1 in Th1 cells and Runx1 in Tfh cells.

Transient DNMT inhibition selectively blocks Dnmt3a-mediated methylation programming at Tfh-associated genes

Our analysis of the methylomes of Dnmt3a KO Th1 and Tfh cells demonstrates that Dnmt3a is required for silencing genes associated with alternative T helper lineages. We hypothesized that DAC treatment during T cell priming inhibits Dnmt3a activity in Th1 and Tfh cells. In agreement with this idea, both DAC treatment and Dnmt3a deletion impaired mTh1 cell lineage commitment (Figures 3 and 4). However, we observed some important differences between DAC treatment and Dnmt3a KO models. For example, Dnmt3a KO mTfh cells had impaired Bcl6 reexpression (Figure 4L), while DAC-treated mTfh cells re-expressed Bcl6 normally (Figure 3L), and DAC treatment enhanced 2° GC Tfh cell responses (Figures 2M and 2N). DAC inhibits methyltransferase activity by degrading DNMTs.52–54 To determine if early DAC treatment inhibits Dnmt3a activity, we measured Dnmt3a levels in early activated SMARTA cells. At 3 dpi, we found a small but significant decrease in the MFI of Dnmt3a within DAC-treated SMARTA cells, although it was not reduced to the levels seen in the Dnmt3a KO SMARTA cells (Figure 6A). These data suggest that DAC treatment reduced Dnmt3a expression within early SMARTA cells, although this effect appears to be transient and/or incomplete.

To determine if DAC treatment impaired Dnmt3a-mediated methylation programming, we performed WGEM-seq on sorted CXCR5− and CXCR5+ SMARTA cells from spleens of PBS- or DAC-treated mice. DMRs were identified between DAC- and PBS-treated cells of the same lineage. This analysis revealed 2,984 Tfh DMRs (DAC-treated Tfh vs. PBS-treated Tfh) and 3,127 Th1 DMRs (DAC-treated Th1 vs. PBS-treated Th1) that were DAC dependent (Figure 6B). Approximately half of the DMRs were hypermethylated (Figure 6B), suggesting that DAC treatment had direct and indirect effects on DNA methylation programming. We performed pathway analysis on the set of genes that were hypomethylated following early DAC treatment. This analysis revealed that DAC treatment resulted in hypomethylation of Lef1-regulated genes in Th1 and Tfh cells (Figure 6C), suggesting that early DAC treatment may enhance the activity of Lef1 and/or its homolog Tcf1 in these cells.

To determine if DAC treatment inhibited Dnmt3a-dependent methylation in Th1 cells, we identified overlapping DMRs between DAC-treated and Dnmt3a KO Th1 cells. Importantly, DAC treatment failed to inhibit Dnmt3a-mediated methylation at 22,801 regions in Th1 cells (Figure 6D), underscoring that early DAC treatment partially and/or transiently inhibited Dnmt3a-mediated programming in Tfh and Th1 cells. This analysis also identified 337 regions that were hypomethylated in both DAC-treated and Dnmt3a KO Th1 cells, including DMRs associated with several Tfh genes such as Tcf7, Lef1, Cxcr5, and Il6st (Figure 6D). We chose to examine the hypomethylated DMRs associated with Tcf7 and Lef1, as these genes were de novo methylated and repressed by Dnmt3a in Th1 cells (Figures 5F and 5G). DAC treatment resulted in partial inhibition of Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation at both Tcf7 and Lef1 (Figures 6E–6H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that early DAC treatment partially inhibited Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation of Tcf7 and Lef1 in Th1 cells.

To identify regions where DAC treatment impaired Dnmt3a-dependent methylation in Tfh cells, we performed an overlap analysis of regions that were hypomethylated in DAC-treated Tfh and Dnmt3a KO Tfh cells. This analysis revealed 421 regions that were hypomethylated in DAC-treated or Dnmt3a KO Tfh cells (Figure 6I). This included several hypomethylated DMRs that were associated with Tfh genes, including Cxcr5, Bcl6, and Il6st (Figure 6I). DAC treatment resulted in complete inhibition of Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation at regions associated with Bcl6 and Cxcr5 (Figures S5A–S5D). Thus, even in Tfh cells, early DAC treatment results in hypomethylation of Tfh-associated genes.

We hypothesized that DAC treatment during T cell priming may not inhibit de novo DNA methylation at genes that encode repressors of Tfh responses. To address this, we focused on the 24,481 regions that were hypomethylated in KO Tfh cells but not in DAC-treated Tfh cells (Figure 6I). In agreement with our hypothesis, we found that DAC treatment did not inhibit Dnmt3a-dependent methylation at DMRs associated with Prdm1 and Runx1 (Figures 6J–6M). DAC-treated Tfh cells also properly methylated the first intron of Bcl6, where DNA methylation is positively correlated with Bcl6 expression (Figures S5E and S5F). Broadly, DAC treatment failed to inhibit Dnmt3a-dependent methylation at any of the regions associated with Prdm1, Runx1, Runx2, Runx3, and Id2 (Figures 6N, 6O, and S5G–S5I). DAC treatment did, however, inhibit Dnmt3a-dependent methylation at 1 out of 26 total Dnmt3a-dependent DMRs associated with Foxo1 (Figure S5J). Collectively, these data argue that transient DNMT inhibition during T cell priming selectively inhibits Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation at Tfh-promoting genes but not at genes encoding repressors of Tfh differentiation.

At 7 days post-secondary infection with LCMV, Dnmt3a cKO mTfh cells had impaired production of 2° Bcl6HI GC Tfh cells and reduced Bcl6 reexpression relative to WT or DAC-treated mTfh cells (Figures 7A–7C). These analyses confirmed that complete Dnmt3a deficiency impairs Bcl6 reexpression and secondary GC Tfh cell differentiation.

Based on the different methylomes of DAC-treated Tfh cells vs. Dnmt3a KO Tfh cells, we hypothesized that Dnmt3a KO secondary Tfh cells may aberrantly upregulate one or several repressors of Bcl6, while in contrast, DAC-treated secondary Tfh cells will normally repress them due to acquired methylation for proper gene silencing of these factors. To determine whether repressors of Tfh differentiation were aberrantly upregulated during the secondary response to LCMV, we enriched bulk SMARTA memory cells that were WT (WT/PBS-treated), Dnmt3a cKO (cKO/PBS-treated), or DAC treated (WT/DAC-treated). We retransferred these cells into naive recipient mice that were then infected with LCMV. At 7 days post-secondary infection, we measured expression of Bcl6 and several negative regulators of Bcl6 in these cells. As anticipated, DAC-treated 2° GC Tfh cells expressed more Bcl6 relative to controls, while Dnmt3a cKO 2° GC Tfh cells had a reduction in Bcl6 relative to WT 2° GC Tfh cells (p = 0.0852) and a significant reduction in Bcl6 relative to DAC-treated 2° GC Tfh cells (Figure S6A). ICOS expression was also reduced on Dnmt3a cKO 2° GC Tfh cells relative to WT and DAC-treated groups (Figure S6B). Surprisingly, both Dnmt3a cKO and DAC-treated 2° Tfh cells had reduced expression of Blimp1, Foxo1, Runx3, and Id2 relative to control, although Runx2 expression was decreased only by DAC-treated 2° Tfh cells and not in Dnmt3a KO 2° Tfh cells (Figures S6C–S6G). Of the factors that we measured, only Runx1 was aberrantly upregulated in Dnmt3a cKO 2° Tfh cells (Figure 7D). Thus, Dnmt3a is required to silence Runx1 in 2° Tfh cells, and early DAC treatment does not inhibit this process.

Several studies suggest that Runx1 may repress Tfh responses50,58,59; however, this has not been directly tested. To test whether overexpression of Runx1 in memory CD4+ T cells is sufficient to impair Bcl6 reexpression and 2° GC Tfh differentiation, we transduced SMARTA memory cells to overexpress Runx1 and evaluated the response to LCMV reinfection (Figure 7E). Runx1 overexpression was validated by flow cytometry at 7 days post-secondary infection (Figure 7F). While Runx1 overexpression did not impact Th1/Tfh differentiation relative to empty vector (EV) controls (Figure 7G), Runx1 overexpression did result in significantly increased Tbet expression in both Th1 and Tfh cells (Figure 7H). Importantly, Runx1 overexpression also resulted in trending reductions in PD-1HI GC Tfh (Figure 7I) and a significant reduction in CXCR5, ICOS, and Bcl6 reexpression in 2° Tfh cells (Figures 7J–7L). This experiment demonstrates that aberrant Runx1 overexpression in memory CD4+ T cells is sufficient to impair Bcl6 reexpression during the recall response.

Based on the Runx1 overexpression experiments, we hypothesized that aberrant Runx1 upregulation in Dnmt3a cKO memory cells impairs their ability to reexpress Bcl6 and form secondary GC Tfh cells. To test this, Dnmt3a cKO SMARTA memory cells were transduced with a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) to knock down Runx1 or with a scrambled control sequence (Figure 7M). At 7 days post-secondary infection, we validated that Runx1 knockdown (KD) was successful (Figure 7N). Runx1 KD did not alter the frequency of secondary Tfh and Th1 cells during the memory recall response (Figure 7O), although Tbet was significantly reduced on Runx1 KD Th1 cells (Figure 7P). In addition, Runx1 KD did significantly increase in the proportion of KO 2° Tfh cells that were PD-1HI, ICOS+, and CD200+, consistent with an increase in 2° GC Tfh cell differentiation (Figures 7Q–7S). Furthermore, reduction in Runx1 in Dnmt3a cKO memory cells resulted in improved Bcl6 reexpression in 2° GC Tfh cells relative to scramble controls (Figure 7T). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Dnmt3a is required to silence Runx1, a repressor of Bcl6, to preserve the ability of memory CD4+ T cells to reexpress Bcl6 and generate secondary GC Tfh cell responses.

DISCUSSION

Acute viral infection generates Th1 and Tfh cells that give rise to distinct mTh1 and mTfh subsets that preferentially recall their specialized effector programs upon rechallenge.28–39 Prior to our study, it was unclear whether gene silencing by Dnmt3a-dependent de novo DNA methylation regulated the long-term lineage commitment and recall potential of mTh1 and mTfh cells. In this study, we used two models to partially or completely block Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation and demonstrate that the acquisition of de novo methylation programming is required to enforce mTh1 and mTfh cell lineage commitment and safeguard the reacquisition of lineage-specific effector programs during the memory recall response.

Dnmt3a-dependent de novo DNA methylation enforces mTh1 cell lineage commitment, preserving secondary effector Th1 cell functions and restraining the ability of mTh1 cells to contribute to secondary effector Tfh cell responses. Our results show that both partial and complete inhibition of Dnmt3a activity resulted in hypomethylation of the loci encoding Tcf7 and Lef1. We demonstrate that Dnmt3a is required to repress Lef1 in primary Th1 cells and Tcf7 in secondary Th1 cells. Dnmt3a may also repress Tcf1 and Lef1 activity by de novo methylating their binding sites across the genome. Several studies have demonstrated that deletion of Tcf7 and/or Lef1 impaired Tfh cell responses during viral infection.19–22 In addition, one study transduced SMARTA cells to overexpress Lef1, demonstrating that this was sufficient to drive a Tfh- and GC-Tfh-like transcriptional program in Th1 cells.19 Together, our findings support a model whereby Th1 cells require Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation to silence Tcf1- and Lef1-mediated transcriptional programming to enforce mTh1 cell lineage commitment and limit the contributions of mTh1 cells to secondary Tfh cell responses.

In mTfh cells, Dnmt3a promotes optimal Bcl6 reexpression and safeguards GC Tfh cell differentiation during secondary viral infection. Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation occurred at several genes encoding established repressors of Tfh differentiation and function, including Prdm1, Runx2, Runx3, Id2, and Foxo1.3,18,23,27,50 To our surprise, however, Dnmt3a was not required to repress reexpression of these factors in secondary Tfh cells. Instead, Dnmt3a was required to de novo methylate and repress Runx1 expression in secondary Tfh cells and to de novo methylate Runx1 binding sites across the genome. Using a combination of overexpression and KD experiments, we demonstrated that Runx1 is a repressor of Bcl6 and secondary GC Tfh cell responses. We also found that Dnmt3a is required for de novo methylation within the first intron of Bcl6 in Tfh cells. Previous research notes that this region is hypermethylated in Bcl6-expressing cells56,60 and is demethylated by the enzymes Tet2 and Tet3, which repress Bcl6 expression in T cells.39,48,50,61,62 In B cell lymphomas, DNA methylation within this region blocks CTCF-mediated repression of Bcl6.56 We also found evidence of CTCF binding within this region, suggesting that Dnmt3a-dependent methylation within the first intron of Bcl6 may promote Bcl6 expression in Tfh cells. Collectively, our data demonstrate that Dnmt3a-mediated de novo methylation is required to safeguard the capacity of mTfh cells to reexpress Bcl6 and generate secondary GC Tfh cell responses.

In contrast to the Dnmt3a KO model, early DNMT inhibition enhances primary and secondary GC Tfh cell responses. Our data suggest that early DNMT inhibition impaired the lineage commitment of mTh1 cells, thereby enhancing secondary GC Tfh cell differentiation. We propose that DAC treatment enhances the plasticity of mTh1 cells by inhibiting Dnmt3a-dependent silencing of Tcf7 and Lef1. Importantly, early DAC treatment preserves the ability of mTfh cells to produce secondary GC Tfh cells. In Tfh cells, transient DNMT inhibition did not block Dnmt3a-dependent de novo methylation at Runx1 and Prdm1. Collectively, our data indicate that early DAC treatment transiently and selectively inhibits Dnmt3a-dependent methylation at Tfh-promoting genes, resulting in enhanced GC Tfh responses during viral infections.

Rational vaccine design strategies to improve immunity to viral pathogens have recently focused on increasing the magnitude and duration of the GC response with the objective of enhancing selection of neutralizing antibodies and/or increasing long-lived plasma cell and memory B cell generation.2,14 Several studies demonstrate that GC Tfh, circulating Tfh, or mTfh cell numbers positively correlate with generation of neutralizing antibodies in HIV or influenza.2,7–14 In addition, increasing mTfh cell numbers accelerates re-formation and enhances the magnitude of the GC response following antigen reencounter.2,29,55 Therefore, modulating the abundance and/or functionality of Tfh cells may represent a strategy to enhance the GC response and improve vaccine efficacy.2 In this study, we demonstrated that early treatment with the methyltransferase inhibitor DAC during the primary immune response improves Tfh cell abundance and functionality in several ways. First, early DAC treatment increases primary GC Tfh cell differentiation in response to several viral pathogens, and in response to influenza infection, this correlates with increased HA-specific GC B cell formation. Second, early DAC treatment increases mTfh cell formation. Third, upon heterologous influenza infection, DAC treatment during the primary response increases GC Tfh cell recall responses that correlate with increased HA-specific GC B cell responses. Collectively, our data demonstrate that DNMT inhibition may represent an approach to modulate T helper cell differentiation and function and to enhance the GC response. Altogether, this study identifies key Tfh and Th1 epigenetic programs and strategies that could be used to modulate effector and memory T helper cell responses and enhance long-lived immunity to viral pathogens.

Limitations of the study

While we demonstrate that early DAC treatment impairs Dnmt3a-dependent de novo DNA methylation, our study cannot rule out effects that early DAC treatment may have on Dnmt1. Previous research has demonstrated that Dnmt1-deficient T cells have defects in survival.63 Therefore, it is difficult to assess the role that Dnmt1 may be playing in this process. Additionally, our study does not determine the direct effects of DAC treatment on other cell types, which may have indirect effects on CD4+ T cell responses during viral infections. Further studies are needed to further explore whether DAC treatment enhances the quality and longevity of GC B cell and antibody responses.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

The following mouse strains were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory: C57BL/6J (Strain # 000664), R26-CreERT2 (Strain #008463), CD4-cre (Strain #022071), and SMARTA (Strain #030450). Dnmt3aflox mice64 were bred to generate mice of the following genotypes: (1) Dnmt3aflox/flox x CreERT2 x SMARTA, (2) CreERT2 x SMARTA, (3) Dnmt3aflox/flox x CD4Cre x SMARTA, (4) CD4Cre x SMARTA, and (5) Dnmt3aflox/flox x SMARTA. For experiments, mice were between 8–12 weeks of age at the start of the experiment, and both sexes were used. Within individual experiments, mice were sex and age matched. Mice were housed in standard conditions in accordance with the University of Utah Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approved protocols.

METHOD DETAILS

LCMV Armstrong and influenza infections

For LCMV experiments, mice were infected by intraperitoneal injection with LCMV Armstrong (2×105 pfu). For select LCMV experiments that were analyzed at 3 days post infection, mice were infected by intravenous injection with LCMV Armstrong (2×106 pfu). For all influenza experiments, mice were anesthetized and intranasally infected with either 200 TCID50 of the H1N1 PR8 strain or 500 TCID50 of the recombinant PR8-GP61–80 strain.

Tissue processing

Single-cell suspensions of lymph nodes or spleens were prepared using 70-μm cell strainers. For spleen processing, red blood cells were lysed by incubation in Ammonium-Chloride-Potassium (ACK) Lysing Buffer (Life Technologies). Cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 5% FBS.

Tamoxifen treatment and Dnmt3a knockout validation

Mice containing the R26-CreERT2 transgene required tamoxifen treatment to conditionally delete Dnmt3a. Briefly, donor mice were intraperitoneally injected with four consecutive doses of tamoxifen (2mg/mouse), followed by a three-day rest period. Following tamoxifen treatment, genomic DNA was isolated and recombination at the Dnmt3a locus was validated by qPCR using the following primer pair: 5’-TGCAATGACCTCTCCATTGTCAAC-3’, 5’-GGTAGAACTCAAAGAAGAGGCGGC-3’.

Loss of Dnmt3a protein was validated by flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were stained for surface markers prior to fixation and permeabilization. Two step intracellular staining was performed with rabbit anti-Dnmt3a (D23G1, Cell Signaling Technologies) or an Isotype control at 1:100 dilution in perm/wash buffer, followed by secondary staining with chicken anti-rabbit IgG AF647 (Invitrogen) at 1:500 dilution in perm/wash buffer.

Decitabine treatment

5’-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (decitabine) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich and was dissolved in sterile PBS. For primary LCMV experiments, mice were intraperitoneally injected with decitabine (0.75 mg/kg) or PBS at 20 hours post primary LCMV infection. For primary influenza experiments, mice were intraperitoneally injected with decitabine (0.375 mg/kg) or PBS at 20 hours post primary infection.

Adoptive transfer of naive SMARTA T cells

Spleens were harvested and processed. For experiments utilizing Dnmt3a knockout cells, naïve SMARTA T cells (CD45.1+) were enriched by negative selection using magnetic beads (Catalog # 19852, STEMCELL Technologies) and then transferred into C57BL/6J (CD45.2+), CreERT2+ (CD45.2+), or CD4cre+ mice (CD45.2+). The use of CreERT2+ or CD4cre+ recipients was for experiments that were analyzed at memory or memory recall timepoints. All other experiments utilized C57BL/6J recipients. The number of cells transferred was either 2×104 for analysis at 7 days post infection, 2×105 for memory and memory recall experiments, or 2×106 for analysis at 3 days post infection.

Adoptive transfer of memory SMARTA T cells

Spleens from LCMV Armstrong infected mice were harvested and processed. Adoptive transfer of memory CD4+ T cells occurred between 45–85 days post infection. The specific times for each experiment are noted in the figure legends. For bulk memory transfer experiments, memory SMARTA T cells were enriched by negative selection using magnetic beads (Catalog # 19852, STEMCELL Technologies) prior to cell sorting. For experiments that required independent transfer of mTh1 and mTfh populations, splenocytes were first stained with anti-CD45.2-PE (Biolegend; clone # 104) for negative selection of memory SMARTA T cells using anti-PE MicroBeads (Catalog # 130-048-801, Miltenyi Biotec). Enriched memory SMARTA T cells were then stained and sorted into CXCR5- Ly6Chi mTh1, and CXCR5+ Ly6Clo mTfh populations at greater than 98% purity. For all experiments, 2–10×103 cells were transferred into C57BL/6J mice prior to reinfection with LCMV Armstrong.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

All surface staining was done in PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FACS buffer) for approximately 30 minutes on ice unless specified otherwise. Cells were then fixed and permeablized using the Foxp3 Permeabilization Fixation kit (eBioscience). Intracellular staining was performed at RT for 30 minutes in Perm/Wash buffer. Flow cytometry experiments were performed on either a FACSCanto or LSRFortessa X-20, and cell sorting experiments were performed on a FACSAria II (BD Biosciences).

For CXCR5 staining of live cells for analysis or cell sorting, a three-step CXCR5 staining protocol was used as described by Johnston et al. (2009)3. Briefly, cells were stained with anti-CXCR5 (BD Pharmingen, catalog # 551961) at 1:100 for 40 minutes at 4 degrees, followed by staining with biotin conjugated anti-Rat IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, catalog # 112-066-003) at 1:1000 for 30 minutes on ice, and finally with SA-APC (Invitrogen, catalog #S868) at 1:1000 for 30 minutes on ice. For the first two staining steps, antibody was diluted in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS, 2% normal mouse serum and 2% BSA. The SA-APC staining step was in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS along with all other surface markers. For CXCR5 staining of cells that will be fixed, CXCR5 was stained using anti-CXCR5-APC (Biolegend, clone #L138D7) alongside all other surface markers for 30 minutes on ice.

For tetramer staining, cells were stained in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum using the following tetramers: I-Ab:GP66−77, I-Ab:NP311−325, or I-Ab:CLIP87−101 (NIH Tetramer Core Facility). Cells were incubated for 1–2 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2 prior to surface and intracellular staining steps.

For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated with GP61–80 peptide for 5 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. Brefeldin A was halfway through the incubation period. Following stimulation, cells were surface stained prior to being fixed using the Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences). Intracellular staining was performed in Perm/Wash buffer for detection of cytokines.

For detection of HA-specific B cells, recombinant HA protein from PR8 virus strain (Catalog # IT-003–0010ΔTMp, Immune Technology Corp.) was biotinylated with 80-fold molar excess of NHS-PEG4-Biotin solution from the EZ-Link NHS-PEG4-Biotin kit (Catalog # A39259, ThermoFisher). Excess biotin was removed by buffer exchange of protein into sterile 1X PBS using Zeba Spin Desalting Columns, 7K MWCO (Catalog # 89882, ThermoFisher). Cells were stained on ice for 30min in FACS buffer with 1:100 dilutions of biotin-conjugated-HA and purified rat anti-mouse CD16/CD32 (BD Pharmingen; clone # 2.4G2), then stained on ice for 30min in FACS buffer with 1:1000 dilution of APC-conjugated streptavidin.

RNA sequencing and analysis

For RNA sequencing (Figure 5), WT and Dnmt3a cKO (CD4Cre+) SMARTA cells were sorted into CXCR5- and CXCR5+ subsets from the spleens of LCMV infected mice at seven days post infection. RNA was isolated using the Quick-RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research). RNA-seq libraries were prepared by the sequencing core at the University of Utah using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Kit (New England Biolabs). Prepared libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system.

Sequencing data were aligned to the mm10 reference genome using STAR in two-pass mode to output a BAM file sorted by coordinates. Mapped reads were assigned to annotated genes using featureCounts version 1.6.3, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 version 1.30.1 with a 5% false discovery rate. DEGs were identified between Dnmt3a KO and WT Th1 cells as well as Dnmt3a KO and WT Tfh cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the Bioconductor package fgsea. For this analysis, genes were ranked by log2 fold change from highest (upregulated in KO) to lowest (downregulated in KO).

Whole genome methylation sequencing and analysis

For whole genome enzymatic methylation (WGEM) sequencing (Figure 5), WT and Dnmt3a cKO (CD4Cre+) SMARTA cells were sorted into CXCR5- and CXCR5+ subsets from the spleens of LCMV infected mice at seven days post infection. For WGEM sequencing (Figure 6), SMARTA cells were sorted into CXCR5- and CXCR5+ subsets from spleens of LCMV infected mice that had been treated with either decitabine (0.75mg/kg) or PBS at seven days post infection. Sample purity was validated by flow cytometry analysis and was greater than 98%. Genomic DNA was isolated from using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen; catalog # 69504). DNA was fragmented using a S220 Focused Ultrasonicator (Covaris), and sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBnext Enzymatic Methyl Seq Kit (New England Biolabs; Catalog # C7120). DNA libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system.

For whole genome bisulfite sequencing (Figure 1), SMARTA cells were sorted into CXCR5- Ly6CHI and CXCR5+ Ly6CLO subsets from spleens of LCMV infected mice at 7 (effector) or 60+ (memory) days post infection. Naïve (CD44− CD62L+) SMARTA cells were sorted from the spleens of uninfected donor mice. Sample purity was validated by flow cytometry analysis and was greater than 98%. Genomic DNA was isolated using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen; Catalog # 69504). DNA libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep kit (New England Biolabs; E7645) and bisulfite treatment of the DNA was performed using the EZ DNA Methylation Gold Kit (Zymo Research; catalog # C5006). DNA libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system.

Sequencing data quality was assessed using FastQC v0.11.4. Adapters were trimmed from the sequencing reads using Trim Galore! v0.4.4 using options (trim_galore -o $OUTDIR –fastqc –paired $FORWARD_READS $REVERSE_READS). Alignment to the mm10 reference genome was performed using Bismark v0.19.0 with options (bismark –multicore 6 –bowtie2 -N 1 $MM10 −1 $FORWARD_READS −2 $REVERSE_READS). Deduplication was performed with deduplicate_bismark (deduplicate_bismark -p -bam $BISMARK_ALIGNED_BAM). Library quality was assessed based on the percentage of reads that aligned to the genome. Library quality was considered sufficient if greater than 50% of reads uniquely aligned to the genome. Enzymatic methyl conversion efficiency was assessed by evaluating the percent of methylation observed in the CHH genome context. Enzymatic methyl conversion was considered sufficient when this value was less than 3%. Genome coverage was assessed using the bedtools genomecov software v2.25.0. Library genome coverage was considered sufficient if 80% of the genome had a depth of at least 10 reads. For each library that met these quality metrics, methylation percentages at individual CpG positions in the reference genome were quantified using the Bismark Methylation Extractor v0.19.0 program with options (bismark_methylation_extractor-p–comprehensive–bedgraph $BISMARK_DEDUPLICATED_BAM). DMRs among the datasets were detected using BSmooth DMR finder. DMRs have at least 10 CpGs and an absolute mean difference of >0.1.

Additional analyses were performed following identification of differentially methylated regions (DMRs). Heatmaps of the top ranked DMRs were generated using the R package Pheatmap. The specific details on individual ranking metric can be found in the figure legends. Overlapping DMRs were identified using the R package GenomicRanges. Motif analyses were performed using the program HOMER (findMotifsGenome.pl). The following publicly available and processed ChIP sequencing datasets were downloaded from ChIP Atlas (chip-atlas.org): CTCF (GEO: GSM3498276). Human CpG island coordinates at the Bcl6 locus were collected from Lai et al. and were mapped onto the mouse genome (mm10) using LiftOver. Analysis of specific DMRs was performed using UCSC Genome Browser and included tracks for mammalian conservation and ENCODE identified cis candidate regulatory elements (cCREs). Grey boxes outlined regions that are differentially methylated. Select DMRs were further analyzed by heatmaps that were made in Prism (GraphPad) and show the percent methylation where the column represents individual CpG sites within the region.

Development of short hairpin RNA for knockdown of Runx1

We ordered the pMKO.1 GFP (Plasmid # 10676) plasmid from Addgene. Independent bacterial colonies were first selected by growth on LB Agar plates containing ampicillin and further grown in LB media overnight. Plasmids were purified using the Qiagen MiniPrep Kit. Purified pMKO.1 GFP plasmid was sequentially digested using AgeI-HF (New England Biolabs) and EcoRI (New England Biolabs) in the provided buffers, and cut plasmid was purified using the Qiagen Gel Extraction kit. DNA was further purified using the Monarch PCR and DNA cleanup kit (New England Biolabs). Candidate shRNA sequences against Runx1 were identified using the RNAi Consortium database. We ordered the following oligos from the University of Utah Core Facilities:

shRunx1 (TRCN0000229575) F 5’-CCGGCTCGCCCTCCTACCATCTATACTCGAGTATAGATGGTAGGAGGGCGAGTTTTTG

shRunx1 (TRCN0000229575) R 5’-AATTCAAAAACTCGCCCTCCTACCATCTATACTCGAGTATAGATGGTAGGAGGGCGAG

Scramble F 5’- CCGGCCATATGCACTTCAACATGTACTCGAGTACATGTTGAAGTGCATATGGTTTTTG

Scramble R 5’-AATTCAAAAACCATATGCACTTCAACATGTACTCGAGTACATGTTGAAGTGCATATGG

Annealed oligos were ligated into linearized pMKO.1 GFP plasmid using T4 ligase (Invitrogen), and ligation product was used for the transformation reaction using XL Blue Competent Cells. Plasmid was purified from selected colonies of transformed E. coli and the sequence was validated by Sanger sequencing.

Retroviral transduction of SMARTA T cells

For Runx1 overexpression experiments, the following MSCV plasmids were ordered from Addgene: pMSCV-Runx1-IRES-mCherry (Plasmid #80157) and pMSCV-IRES-mCherry empty vector controls (Plasmid #80139). For Runx1 knockdown experiments, shRNA targeting Runx1 were developed and inserted into pMKO.1 GFP constructs (see above section). For Runx1 shRNA knockdown or overexpression experiments involving memory CD4+ T cells, HEK293T cells were transduced with a mixture containing either the test or control plasmids (2ug total DNA), pCL-Eco (1ug, plasmid #12371), TransIT-293 Transfection Reagent (9uL, Mirus Bio), and OptiMEM (200uL, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfected HEK293T cells were then incubated for 48 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. One day prior to transduction, SMARTA memory cells were sorted from the spleens of mice. Purified SMARTA memory cells were activated in vitro for approximately 20 hours using mouse T cell Activator Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog # 11456D) in RPMI supplemented with 10%FBS, Pen/Strep, L-Glutamine, and beta-mercaptoethanol. On the day of transduction, retroviral supernatant was collected and mixed with polybrene (8ug/mL, Millipore Sigma) and recombinant human IL-2 (10ng/mL, R&D Systems). Activated SMARTA memory cells were then mixed with retroviral supernatant (100uL/1×106 cells) and spin-transduced for 2 hours at 200xG and 37°C. Transduced SMARTA cells were transferred into C57BL/6J mice, and mice were infected with LCMV Armstrong 1–3 days later.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

For most statistical analyses, unpaired t tests were performed using Prism GraphPad version 10. For all experiments, p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Each dot represents a single mouse, and the specific n value for each experiment is reported in the figure legends. For sequencing experiments, details on the programs used for statistical analyses are outlined in the Method Details section. All other specific details for the statistical analyses used can be found in each figure legend.
