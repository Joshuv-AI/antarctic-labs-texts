Mitochondrial codon usage bias and novel phylogenetic insights: implications for taxonomic reevaluation of seven Xylotini species (Diptera, Syrphidae, Eristalinae)
Authors: Yao Ji, Hu Li, Wenhui Yan, Chunfeng Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsFlies of the tribe Xylotini (Insecta, Diptera, Eristalinae) primarily inhabit dense forests or are frequently observed visiting flowers. Although many genera within this tribe are easily distinguishable and exhibit distinct characteristics, the scarcity of mitochondrial genome resources has limited phylogenetic research. This study addresses this gap by analyzing the mitochondrial genomes of seven newly sequenced species to investigate the tribe’s phylogeny and codon usage patterns.ResultsAll mitochondrial genomes exhibited typical Syrphid structure (37 genes, 15,663 − 16,341 bp) with conserved gene order and strong AT bias (78.56% overall, 93.21% at third codon positions). Codon usage analysis revealed differential evolutionary pressures: Brachypalpoides makiana and Chalcosyrphus curvaria (R² = 0.34–0.56) showed mutation-dominated bias, while other species displayed selection-driven patterns. ENC values (25.6-45.23) and RSCU analysis identified 14 preferred A/U-ending codons, notably UUA-Leu (RSCU = 3.20) and AGA-Arg (RSCU = 2.59). Phylogenetic reconstruction challenged the tribe’s monophyly, with Brachypalpoides and Xylota forming a well-supported clade (BS = 100%), while Milesia appeared polyphyletic. Additionally, C. curvaria showed closer affinity to M. ferruginosa than congenerics.ConclusionsThese findings provide novel insights into: (1) the dual influence of mutational pressure and natural selection on codon evolution, and (2) systematic relationships within this ecologically diverse dipteran group. The study establishes molecular foundations for re-evaluating Xylotini taxonomy and understanding mitochondrial genome evolution in hoverflies.

Introduction

Xylotini belongs to the order Diptera, family Syrphidae, and subfamily Eristalinae, which is widely distributed in the temperate regions of the Palaearctic and Nearctic zones, the tropical regions of Africa, and the northern part of the Neotropical zone. There are approximately 40 genera and 738 species known worldwide, making it a large group [1, 2]. As of now, a total of 94 Xylotini species have been recorded in China [1, 3, 4]. The seven species involved in this study are as follows: Brachypalpoides makiana, which is mainly distributed in the alpine coniferous forest zones of Zhejiang, Fujian, Sichuan, Guizhou, and Yunnan Provinces in China [1, 4]; Chalcosyrphus curvaria, occurring in Hebei, Xinjiang, Jilin and other provinces of China [5]; Korinchia sinensis, an endemic species to China primarily found in the southwest region (Sichuan and Guizhou Provinces) [4]; Milesia ferruginosa, with stable distribution records in Sichuan, Guizhou, and Yunnan Provinces of China; Milesia sinensis, widely distributed domestically in the broad-leaved forest habitats of Fujian, Jiangxi, Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, Sichuan and other provinces [1]; Syritta orientalis, mainly distributed in farmland and thicket habitats of Hubei, Hunan, Fujian, Sichuan, Yunnan, Shaanxi and other regions in China [6]; and Xylota cupripurpura, whose known distribution area is concentrated in the Qinling Mountains of Shaanxi Province and southern Gansu Province, with the type specimen collected from Taibai Mountain, Shaanxi Province [7]. Hull (1949) was the first to introduce the tribe Xylotini, and Thompson (1969) divided the Xylotini into multiple groups, treating the genus Xylota as an informal group in his classification system. In 1989, he again classified Xylota as a genus within the Xylotini.

Driven by mimicry, the Xylotini exhibit significant morphological diversity, including differences in facial shape, thoracic hair distribution, wing venation, abdominal shape, and surface protrusions [8, 9]. Although previous studies have explored the phylogeny of Xylotini species using morphological and molecular markers, there remain unresolved issues and controversies regarding the definition and phylogenetic position of Xylotini [10].

Recent molecular phylogenetic analyses have focused on single or multiple genes to explore the relationships within the Xylotini. However, the monophyly of Xylotini is still debated, with the classification of the genus Xylota being particularly contentious. For example, Ståhls (2003) showed through both morphological and molecular analyses that Xylota, Chalcosyrphus, Brachypalpoides, and Blera form a distinct cluster [11]. Mengual combined molecular and morphological data to classify Xylota, Chalcosyrphus, and Brachypalpoides as a single branch [12], while Young (2016) confirmed the monophyly of the Xylotini based on a broader range of subfamilies and tribes [13]. More recently, Pan et al. (2022) demonstrating that Xylota coquilletti should be considered the sister species of Ferdinadea cupera (Rhingiini) [14].

The mitochondrial genome (mtDNA) is the only extranuclear genetic information carrier in animals, ranging in size from 14 to 18 kb. Due to its stable gene composition, simple structure, small molecular weight, relatively conserved gene arrangement, maternal inheritance, and rare recombination, it is widely used as an important molecular marker in various fields such as mitochondrial genome evolution, phylogenetic relationship analysis, gene drift, biogeography, population genetic structure, and analysis of significant events in the origin and evolution of animals, facilitating the study of phylogenetic relationships, genetic evolution, geographical isolation, and taxonomic identification among animal groups [14].

Codon usage bias (RSCU) arises through long-term evolution and natural selection, reflecting how certain codons are favored over others in protein synthesis. In mitochondrial genomes (mtDNA), which are commonly used in phylogenetic studies, this bias is particularly pronounced in insects, including Diptera [15–17]. This bias can influence RNA processing, protein translation efficiency, and overall gene expression [18, 19].In Diptera, understanding codon usage bias is essential for interpreting mtDNA evolution and improving the resolution of phylogenetic trees. By examining codon usage, we can better understand the evolutionary pressures shaping mitochondrial genomes and optimize mtDNA-based phylogenetic analyses [20].

This study comparatively analyzed the 13 protein-coding genes of the mitochondrial genomes of 7 species from 6 genera of the Xylotini, and explored the usage patterns of their codon usage bias. It aimed to reveal the sequence characteristics and influencing factors of RSCU in the mitochondrial genomes of these 7 species, providing some basic biological information for the Xylotini and increasing the related gene information of this group. The study also aimed to reveal the evolutionary mechanism and evolutionary relationships of this species. It provided theoretical basis and reference for the phylogenetic relationships among closely related species of Xylotini insects, the evolution of gene sequences, species natural selection and classification, and supported further research and application. Based on the protein-coding genes of the mitochondrial genome, suitable tree-building methods were screened, such as maximum likelihood method (ML), and Bayesian inference method (BI) [21–23], constructed a phylogenetic tree of seven species of Xylotini and their related close relatives. By comparing different datasets and tree-building strategies, the reliability of the phylogenetic relationships was evaluated, the uncertainty of the phylogenetic tree topology was addressed, and the evolutionary branching order and the degree of kinship among Xylotini species were determined.

Genomic DNA was extracted using a genomic DNA extraction kit. The thoracic muscles and leg tissues of the Xylotini species were cut and placed in pre-cooled 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, and mechanical homogenization was performed with lysis buffer. After completion, they were classified and stored according to the experimental numbers. The DNA extraction process was strictly carried out in accordance with the instructions of the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing). The concentration of the extracted genomic DNA reached 20 ng/µl. At least 50 µl of each DNA sample was sent to Berry Genomics (Beijing) for sequencing. Whole-genome sequencing of 7 species was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform with a 150PE sequencing mode, generating approximately 12GB of data per sample.

Sequence assembly and annotation

In a Linux system server environment, the raw sequencing data was imported into the system, and then NOVOPlasty v4.3.1 [25] software was invoked for sequence assembly, guided by the COX1 gene sequence of the already published species of the tribe Xylotini in the NCBI database as the seed sequence. An automated assembly process was achieved by writing and executing a Perl script, which could automatically handle input parameters and invoke NOVOPlasty to complete the sequence assembly. After the assembly was completed, the obtained sequence file was imported into Geneious Prime R9 software [26], where editing was performed. The specific operations included precise trimming of both ends of the sequence to remove low-quality regions, and the final assembly of the circular sequence was completed through sequence alignment and overlapping region detection. The entire process combined automated processing and manual verification to ensure the acquisition of high-quality circular genome sequences. After obtaining the complete mitochondrial genome, tRNAscan-SE 1.21 [27] was used for tRNA gene annotation. In Geneious R9, the positions of each protein-coding gene were determined by combining open reading frame (ORF) analysis and homologous sequence alignment [28, 29].

In the mitochondrial genome of Xylotini, the 16 S rRNA gene shows a stable gene arrangement pattern, always located between the tRNA-Leu2 and tRNA-Val gene loci. The position of the 12 S rRNA was determined by homologous alignment of known Mydidae sequences based on the Clustal W algorithm. The control region is located between the 12 S rRNA and tRNA-Ile.

Analysis of nucleotide composition

Codon usage bias exhibits a strong correlation with the nucleotide composition of the genome, particularly with regard to the GC content at the third codon position (GC3), which has been widely recognized as a crucial indicator for assessing nucleotide compositional bias [30, 31]. In light of this established relationship, the present investigation employed MEGA11.0 software [32] to conduct a comprehensive analysis of overall nucleotide composition in the studied species, with particular emphasis on 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes. The analytical protocol encompassed quantitative determination of base composition at the first (GC1), second (GC2), and third (GC3) codon positions for each gene, in addition to the computation of species-level total GC content [33].

Parity plot analysis

The parity plot serves as a visualization tool for characterizing codon usage bias. This analytical approach employs the purine/pyrimidine ratio at the third codon position as coordinate axes, where the ordinate represents A3/(A3 + T3) and the abscissa denotes G3/(G3 + C3). Under ideal conditions of Chargaff’s parity rules (A = T, G = C), data points cluster at the plot’s geometric center (0.5, 0.5), indicating exclusive dominance of mutational pressure in shaping codon usage patterns [34]. Quantitative assessment of deviations from this central point, both in direction and magnitude, enables evaluation of nucleotide compositional bias in protein-coding genes and its underlying evolutionary determinants [35].

ENC-plot analysis

The effective codon number (ENC) is a key indicator to characterize the codon usage preference. In this study, 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs) in the mitochondrial genomes of 7 species of Xylotini were used as the research objects, and the ENC-plot analysis method was used [36, 37], using GC3 content as the abscissa and ENC observations (ENCobs) as the ordinate, systematically evaluated the characteristics of codon usage patterns [38, 39]. At the same time, the ENC expected value (ENCexp) and ENC ratio (ENCratio) are calculated based on the following formula:

ENCexp = 2 + GC3 + 29/[GC3² + (1 - GC3)]².

ENCratio = (ENCexp – ENCobs)/ENCexp.

According to the criteria proposed by Wright (1990), when the ENC value is less than 35, it indicates that the gene has significant codon use bias; On the contrary, the bias is weaker. In this study, | ENCratio| >0.05 [40, 41], when this condition is met, suggests that codon usage preference is mainly driven by natural selection pressure [42]. Through the above analysis methods, the influence of mutation pressure and natural selection on codon usage patterns can be effectively distinguished [43].

Neutrality plot analysis

The neutrality plot is a scatter diagram constructed with GC3 (GC content at the third codon position) as the abscissa and the mean GC content of the first and second codon positions (GC12) as the ordinate. A statistically significant correlation between GC12 and GC3 suggests that mutational pressure predominantly influences codon usage bias. Conversely, the absence of such correlation implies a more substantial role of natural selection in shaping codon preference.

Neutrality plot analysis serves as a critical methodological framework for investigating codon usage patterns. In this approach, a regression model is established using GC3 as the independent variable and GC12 as the dependent variable [35]. Linear regression analysis reveals that a significant positive correlation (P < 0.05) between GC12 and GC3 indicates the predominance of mutation-selection equilibrium in governing codon usage bias. In contrast, the lack of significant correlation (P >0.05) suggests that natural selection exerts dominant evolutionary pressure in codon preference formation [44, 45]. This analytical paradigm provides quantitative evidence for evaluating the relative contributions of different selective forces during mitochondrial genome evolution.

Correlation analysis

This study analyzed the correlation of mitochondrial genome codons using feature parameters and generates plots with chiplot [33]. The analysis variables included the GC content of each codon locus (GC1, GC2, GC3, GC12, GC) and codon use preference indicators (CAI, CBI, Fop, ENC) [46]. The correlation degree between the parameters was calculated by the Pearson correlation coefficient matrix, and the hierarchical clustering method was used to visualize the correlation pattern. This analysis method can systematically reveal the influencing factors and their interrelationships of mitochondrial genome codon use bias [47]. Additionally, CAI (Codon Adaptation Index) is used to measure the level of gene expression, with CAI values generally ranging from 0 to 1. The higher the CAI value, the stronger the CUB (Codon Usage Bias); conversely, the lower the CAI value, the weaker the CUB. It is generally believed that the higher the GC content of a codon, the stronger its CUB [37].

Relative synonymous codon usage analysis

Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) refers to the probability of a specific codon relative to the synonymous codons encoding the corresponding amino acid [48]. Its calculation method is the number of times a codon is used divided by the expected frequency, which is unaffected by amino acid frequency and gene length [49]. An RSCU value of 1 indicates no codon usage bias, RSCU >1 indicates higher usage frequency, and RSCU < 1 indicates lower usage frequency [50].

MEGA 11 was used to calculate the RSCU values for each codon of 13 PCGs in the mitochondrial genome [51]. Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) is a crucial indicator for measuring the preference degree of a specific codon relative to its synonymous codons [49]. The calculation method involves dividing the actual observed usage frequency of a codon by its theoretical expected frequency, thereby eliminating the influence of amino acid composition and gene length on the analysis results. Based on the distribution characteristics of RSCU values, when RSCU = 1, it indicates that the codon shows no usage bias; RSCU >1 reflects a usage frequency higher than the expected level; while RSCU < 1 suggests a usage frequency lower than the expected level.

Optimal codon analysis

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) was used as the evaluation index, and RSCU value greater than 1 was the high-frequency codon. In order to explore the relationship between codon use bias and gene expression level, we grouped gene sequences based on ENC values: the gene sequences with the highest ENC values (first 10%) and the lowest (bottom 10%) were selected to construct a codon use database for the low expression group and the high expression group, respectively [52]. The codon of ΔRSCU >0.08 was identified as a codon with high expression bias by calculating the difference of RSCU values between the two groups (ΔRSCU = RSCU high expression group-RSCU low expression group) [53]. Finally, the codon that satisfies both the high frequency usage (RSCU >1) and high expression bias (ΔRSCU >0.08) criteria was determined to be the optimal codon [54].

Phylogenetic analysis

A total of 56 mitogenetic genomes of the subfamily Eristalinae were used for phylogenetic analysis, including 7 newly sequenced species of Xylotini while outgroups are Dorylomorpha alaskensis (NC_081090) and Nephrocerus lapponicus (NC_081098). Four datasets were constructed based on 13 PCGs and 2 rRNA gene sequences: (1) 13 PCGs and 2 rRNA genes (12 S and 16 S rRNA), containing 13,496 nucleotides (PCGRNA); (2) the first and second codon positions of 13 PCGs with a total of 7,408 nucleotides (PCG12); (3) there were only 13 PCGs, containing 11,112 nucleotides (PCGs); (4) The PCG sequence (PCG12) of codon sites 1 and 2 is combined with the sequence of non-coding RNA genes, containing 9,488 nucleotides (PCG12RNA).

MAFFT v7.450 [55] was used for codon alignment of protein-coding genes, and rRNA genes were aligned using L-INS-i algorithm [55], and low-quality loci were filtered by trimAl v1.2 [56]. 13 PCG and 2 rRNA gene sequences were integrated into a single dataset using FASConCAT-g v1.04 [57].

Phylogenetic trees were constructed based on maximum likelihood, heterogeneous mixed model and Bayesian inference. PartitionFinder2 [58] was used to screen the optimal partitioning scheme and alternative model, and the greedy algorithm combined with AIC criterion was used to determine the optimal partitioning strategy. Maximum likelihood analysis was done in IQ-TREE v1.6.3 with 1000 bootstrap tests. Bayesian analysis ran 4 independent chains in MrBayesv3.2.6 [59] with a total of 100 million iterations and 1000 generations of sampling intervals. After convergence, 25% of the aging samples were removed, a consensus tree with posterior probability was constructed, and the phylogenetic tree was visualized and edited by the online tool chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/) [51].

Results and analysis

Analysis of nucleotide composition

Our study conducted systematic analysis of the mitochondrial genomes of seven newly sequenced Xylotini species (Brachypalpoides makiana, Chalcosyrphus curvaria, Korinchia sinensis, Milesia ferruginosa, Milesia sinensis, Syritta orientalis, and Xylota cupripurpura). The results showed that these species all possess a typical Syrphidae mitochondrial genome structure, containing 37 coding genes (13 protein-coding genes, 2 rRNA genes, and 22 tRNA genes) and a control region (Fig. 2). The total length of each species’ genome varied between 15,663 bp and 16,341 bp, with the variation mainly attributed to differences in the control region, while the protein-coding genes and RNA genes exhibited high conservation. The gene arrangement order of these newly sequenced genomes was identical to that of known Syrphidae species, with no evidence of gene rearrangement.Fig. 2The circular map of the complete mitochondrial genome of seven newly sequenced Xylotini species

The circular map of the complete mitochondrial genome of seven newly sequenced Xylotini species

By analyzing the nucleotide composition characteristics of the 13 protein-coding genes, we found a clear AT bias in the overall base composition (Fig. 3). Specifically, the T base content was the highest (43.86%), followed by A (34.70%), G (10.77%), and C (10.71%). In the analysis of the third codon position (A3, T3, C3, and G3), this AT bias was even more pronounced: A3 content was as high as 48.65%, T3 was 44.56%, while C3 and G3 content was only 3.56% and 3.23%, respectively. These data fully confirm the strong AT bias in codon usage of the hover fly mitochondrial genome.Fig. 3Nucleotide composition of 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes and nucleotide composition at the third codon position from seven newly sequenced Xylotini species

PR2 bias analysis

Codon usage bias analysis of insect mitochondrial genomes indicated that the evaluation of A/T and G/C bias directions provided important evidence for understanding the molecular mechanism of deviation from neutral evolution. In this study, the mitochondrial protein coding genes of 7 species of Xylotini (Brachypalpoides makiana, Chalcosyrphus curvaria, Korinia sinensis, Milesia ferruginosa, Milesia sinensis, Syritta orientalis and Xylota cupripurpura) were systematically analyzed by parity diagram analysis. The results show that the distribution of protein-coding genes in the four quadrants of each species is significantly unbalanced (Fig. 4), indicating that the codon usage bias of different species are affected by differential selection pressure.Fig. 4Parity plot analysis of 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes in seven newly sequenced Xylotini species. A Brachypalpoides makiana, (B) Chalcosyrphus curvaria, (C) Korinchia sinensis, (D) Milesia ferruginosa, (E) Milesia sinensis, (F) Syritta orientalis, and (G) Xylota cupripurpura

Parity plot analysis of 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes in seven newly sequenced Xylotini species. A Brachypalpoides makiana, (B) Chalcosyrphus curvaria, (C) Korinchia sinensis, (D) Milesia ferruginosa, (E) Milesia sinensis, (F) Syritta orientalis, and (G) Xylota cupripurpura

Specifically, ND1, ND4, ND4L and ND5 genes were mainly enriched in quadrant 1 and quadrant 4, and their third codon sites showed obvious G preference (G3 > C3). The remaining 9 protein-coding genes were mainly distributed in quadrant 2 and quadrant 3, showing opposite C preference (C3 > G3). AT bias (A3/(A3 + T3)) fluctuated around 0.5 for most protein-encoding genes, suggesting a slight T bias. This bias pattern may be related to species-specific gene expression regulation mechanism, genomic GC content characteristics and translation efficiency optimization.

PR2 analysis further revealed that all protein-encoding genes were distributed off-center, and most of them were far from the center. The long branch of Milesia sinensis (branch length: 0.1845) suggests that it may have undergone unique natural selection or geographical isolation—this phenomenon may indicate that the genus Milesia is not a monophyletic group and requires redefinition of its taxonomic scope. In terms of morphological characteristics, Milesia ferruginosa and Milesia sinensis share common traits: both have the r-m vein located behind the middle of the discal cell, and the apical crossvein intersects the r4+5 vein at a non-right angle, which is the basis for their classification into the same genus. However, the two species differ distinctly in morphology: Milesia ferruginosa has an abdomen mostly blackish-brown to reddish-brown, black coxae, and the anterior half of its wings brown; Milesia sinensis has a yellow abdomen with distinct black markings, yellow legs, slightly yellow wings, and yellow squamae and halteres.By integrating morphological and phylogenetic data, we found that these two species—currently placed in the same genus—are genetically distant [1]. Therefore, they may not be appropriately classified under the same genus.

Korinchia angustiabdomena and Korinchia sinensis form a strongly supported sister pair (100%), but there is a significant difference in branch length (0.0605 vs. 0.0454), which may reflect genetic differentiation driven by ecological adaptation or geographic isolation. Syritta orientalis, Syritta pipiens, and Syritta vitripennis form a monophyletic group (100% supported), of which Syritta vitripennis has a longer branch length (0.1517), which may be related to its special habitat adaptation. Tropidia scita is a sister group of the genus Syritta (100% supported), but its short evolutionary distance (0.0818) speculates that the two may share ancestral traits. Orthoprosopa grisea is located in an independent long branch (0.2862), which may represent an ancient lineage. Its differentiation time needs to be further explored in combination with fossil calibration.

This study reveals the complex systematic developmental relationship of Xylotini and related taxa, and some of the results conflict with traditional morphological classification (such as the relationship between Milesia sinensis and Sphegina). Future research needs to combine multi-gene data sets and morphological characteristics to clarify the classification disputes at the genus and species levels, and to explore the influence of ecological adaptation on branch length differences.

Discussion

Nucleotide composition analysis of 7 Xylotini species’ mitochondrial genomes revealed strong AT bias (78.56%, T: 43.86%, A: 34.70%), with more pronounced AT bias at third codon sites (93.21%), indicating mutation pressure effects. Codon usage showed GC1 >GC2 >GC3, A/U-ending codon bias, and mean ENC (30.65), similar to Rhingia [66] and Japanagallia [67]. GC12-GC3 correlation analysis showed 4 species’ codon bias driven by mutation pressure (e.g., Brachypalpoides makiana, R²=0.34), others by natural selection. RSCU analysis identified 14 high-frequency A/U-ending codons. Phylogenetic trees (4 datasets) indicated Xylotini is non-monophyletic, with unstable positions of some species (e.g., Orthoprosopa grisea), but high inter-family topology stability.

The analysis of codon usage preference revealed significant differences between each other. The codon usage of Milesia ferruginosa and Milesia sinensis is highly consistent, indicating that there is a strong evolutionary conservatism, and it may be closer in kinship or similar evolutionary pressure. Chalcosyrphus curvaria and Xylota cupripurpura are partially similar, while Syritta orientalis exhibits a unique codon usage pattern. These differences may reflect phylogenetic differentiation or functional adaptability. Unlike most Xylotini that inhabit dense forests, Syritta exhibits obvious flower-visiting habits and prefers open habitats [1]. It is speculated that different growth environments may lead to certain differences in the degree to which the seven species of Xylotini are affected by natural selection.

Codon preference is the continuous formation of species in the process of evolution to adapt to the environment. There are many reasons for the bias in the use of genomic codons, such as gene length, gene expression level, base composition of the genome, and transcription and translation. These factors affect the use of codons to varying degrees [68]. They usually do not exist alone, and it is often multiple factors that affect genomic codon preferences at the same time [69]. The analysis of codon preference showed that most of the codons of the seven species of Xylotini ended in adenine (A) or uracil (U), reflecting that these species are highly conserved in codon selection patterns and have small differences in genetic laws. The results of the study verify the hypothesis proposed in 1980 that species with similar relatives show conserved characteristics in codon usage patterns [70]. Although the seven species of Xylotini belong to the same tribe, interspecific differences in the frequency of codon use have been detected, and the use of codons in the Milesia ferruginosa and Milesia sinensis genomes is highly consistent. However, the phylogenetic tree of these seven Xylotini species was constructed from a genetic perspective, among which Milesia ferruginosa and Milesia sinensis are not clustered on the same evolutionary branch. It is speculated that this phenomenon may be caused by horizontal gene transfer (that is, homologous genes with conserved codon preferences are obtained from a common source), or by incomplete genealogical sorting (ancestral polymorphisms are still retained after speciation).

In this study, the mitochondrial genome codons of seven species of Xylotini were affected by base mutation pressure and natural selection at the same time, and were more affected by natural selection. In order to further clarify the phylogenetic relationship between Xylotini species, we plan to expand the sample size, especially to collect more species from different geographic areas and ecological niches, in order to improve the representativeness and resolution of the data. In addition, combining ecological and behavioral data will help to in-depth understanding of adaptive differences and ecological interactions between species, which can not only provide supplementary information for phylogenetic analysis, but also help reveal possible patterns of gene flow, species differentiation, and environmental selection between species. Through a multi-level and multi-perspective comprehensive analysis, we aim to overcome the limitations of existing methods and further clarify the evolutionary history and phylogenetic relationships among species within Xylotini.

Mutation and natural selection (dominant) jointly shaped codon bias, with interspecific differences possibly linked to ecology. Future studies need expanded sampling and multi-data integration.
