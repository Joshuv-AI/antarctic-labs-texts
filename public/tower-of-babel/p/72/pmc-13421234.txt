Cervical Cancer Screening With Human Papillomavirus Self-Collection Testing in an Opioid Treatment Program
Authors: Elizabeth M. Swisher, Mallory Davis, Mayumi Rubin-Saika, Enna Manhardt, Emiko Oshima, Renata R. Urban, Stephen L. Cherne, John Lin, Rachel L. Winer, Eric C. Huang, Thomas Hutch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study describes the implementation of human papillomavirus (HPV) self-collection testing during the annual wellness visit as well as HPV positivity rates and follow-up care among patients of an opioid treatment program.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a preventable cancer caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV) that disproportionately affects underserved populations both nationally and globally.1 HPV vaccination and screening to prevent and identify precancerous lesions and early-stage cancers have decreased the cervical cancer burden in the US.2,3 Yet these advances have not substantially improved survival for US patients with cervical cancer because there has been no change in the incidence of advanced stage disease, which contributes to the bulk of disease-related mortality, and because underscreened or unscreened individuals, who bear disproportionate cancer risk, have not benefited from these advances.2,4,5 Screening adherence decreased in the US from 86% in 2005 to 73% in 2021, and the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated screening disparities for marginalized populations.6,7,8

HPV-based testing has replaced the Papanicolaou test as the most effective screening test for cervical cancer.9,10,11 Unlike Papanicolaou tests that rely on cervical cytological examination, HPV tests can also be run on self-collected vaginal samples, with sensitivity and specificity comparable to those for clinician-collected cervical samples.12 In large randomized clinical trials embedded in Kaiser Permanente Washington, mailed HPV self-collection tests increased cervical cancer screening uptake.13,14 Multiple studies have also shown that HPV self-collection testing is effective in reaching patients who otherwise delay or opt out of cervical cancer screening,12,15 offering advantages of privacy and autonomy, engagement in one’s own health care, and avoiding the discomfort of a pelvic examination.16 Patients consistently report that HPV self-collection testing is easy and acceptable.15,17,18,19,20

Opioid treatment programs (OTPs) are ideal settings to provide cervical cancer screening to a marginalized population. OTPs serve individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD), for whom perceived stigma has often generated avoidance of health care interactions, leading to low receipt of primary preventive care.21,22 In addition to low uptake of cervical cancer screening, individuals with OUD may have other risk factors for persistent HPV infection and cervical cancer, such as tobacco use, nonvaccination for HPV, history of transactional sex, and history of sexual abuse.23,24 OTPs provide safe spaces and trusted medical relationships for their patients. Although staffing and resources vary, all OTPs have clinicians and are equipped to collect specimen (eg, the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration requires OTPs to conduct frequent urine toxicology tests). OTPs already administer screening and follow-up for multiple infectious diseases and may provide an optimal environment to offer cervical cancer screening using HPV self-collection testing to their underscreened and potentially high-risk population.

Screening using self-collected specimen for HPV testing is evidence-based and primed for widespread adoption. HPV self-collection testing was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 202425 and can affect cervical cancer screening by expanding access to previously unreached populations, increasing acceptability and administration in clinics without gynecological services. Within a prospective interventional cohort study, we implemented cervical cancer screening at an OTP in Washington state using HPV self-collection testing and monitored patients for screening eligibility, uptake, and outcomes. The goals of this study were to assess the feasibility and uptake of incorporating HPV self-collection testing for cervical cancer screening into routine care for patients with OUD and to track the rates of high-risk HPV and guideline concordant follow-up care in this population. To our knowledge, this is the first study of integrated HPV self-collection testing for cervical cancer screening within a federally certified OTP.

Methods

We conducted this study from June 24, 2024, through December 31, 2025, at We Care Daily Clinics (hereafter, the OTP), a federally certified OTP and addiction recovery clinic in Auburn, Washington, that operates a network of traditional clinics and mobile service locations across Washington’s King and Pierce counties. This OTP is an American Indian Health Facility serving both tribal and nontribal patients with OUD by providing a holistic approach to substance use, recovery, and health. The University of Washington Institutional Review Board deemed this cohort study exempt from ethics review and waived the informed consent requirement because study activities constituted minimal risk. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

The OTP patients have access to medical services, including medication management of substance use disorder (SUD), as well as testing and treatment of other conditions such as hepatitis A, hepatitis C, HIV, and other sexually transmitted infections. Each patient also has access to counseling services offered by a team of on-site SUD professionals and Certified Peer Support Specialists (CPSS). Furthermore, the OTP provides medication management, medical, and counseling services to 1600 patients at the Auburn clinic, while 4 mobile units serve more than 200 additional patients in Tacoma and Seattle. Mobile units are equipped with a medication dispensary, medical examination area, and restrooms.

We, the study team, assisted the OTP team in creating and implementing an HPV self-collection screening and follow-up program into their routine care. All patient interactions occurred through the OTP’s clinical staff as part of usual care. We did not have access to patient identifiers.

Feasibility was defined a priori as achieving 70% or greater screening uptake among eligible patients. Acceptability was operationalized as 70% or more of eligible patients agreeing to receive the self-collection kit when offered. These thresholds are consistent with prior HPV self-collection testing feasibility studies.14

Patients 25 years or older and who had a cervix were assessed for eligibility for cervical cancer screening by a medical assistant at the time of the annual wellness visit using standardized decision trees and patient education (Figure 1; eFigure in Supplement 1). Eligibility was restricted to patients 25 years or older, consistent with guidelines recommending HPV-based primary screening to begin at age 25 years (2020 American Cancer Society guidelines9). If deemed eligible, the patient was offered cervical cancer screening at that time. Patients were provided with the HPV self-collection testing kit and collection instructions, which included an illustration of swab insertion and collection technique (eFigure in Supplement 1). Patients completed the self-collection in facility restrooms, usually concordant with routine urine testing required for OTP medication management. Patients who were eligible for cervical cancer screening but were uncertain about proceeding to the next steps were encouraged to discuss this hesitation with their clinician during the visit. We developed written information on cervical cancer screening and prevention (eFigure in Supplement 1), and at the study start we downloaded general information on cervical cancer, genital HPV, and HPV vaccination from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)26 and used it for patient education (eFigure in Supplement 1). Use of publicly available CDC materials does not imply endorsement of this study by the CDC, US Department of Health and Human Services, or the US government. We downloaded these materials from the CDC website at no cost.

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the OTP’s electronic health record (EHR) by clinic staff for all patients assessed for eligibility. Data elements included age, self-reported race and ethnicity, tobacco use status, and cervical cancer screening history, as recorded in the EHR at the time of the annual wellness visit. Race and ethnicity (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White, multiracial, and other) were included in this analysis to identify correlations with screening uptake and HPV results. Primary outcomes were the rates of HPV self-collection testing uptake, HPV positivity, and successful linkage to follow-up care.

HPV self-testing kits were sent for analysis to the University of Washington HPV Molecular Pathology Laboratory in twice-weekly batches. The HPV Molecular Pathology Laboratory had validated the sensitivity of HPV self-collection testing (>90%) in 65 patients, with matched pairs of self-collected and clinician-collected swabs tested using the cobas 4800 HPV assay (Roche Diagnostics). The cobas tests for 14 high-risk HPV types; HPV-16 and HPV-18 are specifically identified, whereas the other 12 high-risk HPV types (31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68) are reported in a pool (hereafter, other HPV types). Following its validation, HPV self-collection testing became an in-house laboratory option for HPV testing on February 23, 2023, within the University of Washington Medicine. Starting September 25, 2025, the OTP switched its HPV testing analysis to Labcorp (Labcorp Holdings Inc), which also uses the cobas assay. The cobas HPV test was FDA-approved on May 14, 2024, for specimen self-collection within a health care setting.

HPV test results were electronically transmitted from the reference laboratory to the OTP and uploaded into the patient’s EHR. For participants with negative results, a personalized result letter was generated and physically provided to patients on their next clinic presentation. Given the high frequency of visits for medication dosing in this OTP setting, delivery typically occurred within 1 week and no later than 1 month after the result.

Participants with positive results were flagged in the EHR for a dedicated, in-person medical consultation to review findings, provide education, and discuss subsequent management. If a patient missed this consultation, they were rescheduled for the next available appointment, generally within 1 week. For patients who did not present to the clinic within 1 week of the result, clinicians attempted contact via telephone, text message, or email. Concurrently, an alert was placed in the EHR to prompt medical assistants to direct these patients to a clinician immediately after arrival for their next medication dosing.

Management of positive screening results adhered to the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology Risk-Based Management Consensus Guidelines.27 Participants with positive results for HPV-16 or HPV-18 were referred directly for colposcopy, while those with positive results for other HPV types were referred for cervical cytological examination to determine further management needs.

To address structural barriers to care and ensure linkage to services, the OTP developed a referral navigation system. Clinicians used EHR reminders to conduct medical record reviews at 1 and 4 weeks after referral to verify appointment scheduling and attendance. For patients requiring assistance, clinicians or CPSSs facilitated appointment scheduling directly. To overcome logistical and psychosocial barriers, the OTP provided patients with free transportation to and from referral partners and offered to patients requesting emotional support accompaniment by CPSS to off-site gynecologic appointments.

Statistical Analysis

Proportions with 95% CIs were calculated using the Wilson score method. Comparisons of demographic and clinical characteristics across groups were performed using χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate. All tests were 2-sided with a statistical significance threshold of P = .05. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 11.0.2 (GraphPad Software Inc).

Results

During the study period, approximately 880 patients visited the Auburn OTP daily. A total of 194 patients who met age and sex criteria were assessed for cervical cancer screening eligibility at their annual wellness visits. Patients had a median (range) age of 41 (25-65) years and included 29 American Indian or Alaska Native (14.9%), 3 Asian (1.5%), 20 Black (10.3%), 10 Hispanic (5.2%), 183 non-Hispanic (94.3%), 2 Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (1.0%), 132 White (68.0%), and 1 multiracial (0.5%) individuals, including 1 who selected other race and ethnicity (0.5%). Of 158 completed tests, there were 0 unsatisfactory results, 128 (81.0%; 95% CI, 74.1%-86.4%) had an HPV-negative result, 7 (4.4%; 95% CI, 2.2%-8.9%) had an HPV-16– or HPV-18–positive result, and 23 (14.6%; 95% CI, 9.9%-20.9%) had a positive result for other types of high-risk HPV. Among the 30 patients (19.0%) with HPV-positive test results, 2 (6.7%) were lost to follow-up and 12 (40.0%) have completed follow-up, which often required multiple coordination attempts by clinicians. The remaining 16 of 30 patients (53.3%) are actively engaged in care and in various stages of referral and scheduling, reflecting ongoing engagement rather than attrition. Among those who have completed follow-up, the median (range) time from receiving positive HPV result to completing diagnostic evaluation was 6.5 (2-26) weeks.

Reasons for delays were varied and included patient fear of follow-up, patient difficulty in committing to dates in the absence of immediate scheduling options for many weeks, cancellations, and no shows. We found greater success in arranging follow-up with clinics that agreed to see patients for intake and examination in a single visit, which was not universally available.

Discussion

Introduction of HPV self-collection testing into an OTP in Washington was a highly feasible method to provide cervical cancer screening, and the population in our study had a high unmet need for this service, with nearly 90% of patients either overdue for or unsure of their last screening. Thus, even the well-established patients in this OTP had an unmet need for cervical cancer screening. These findings are consistent with robust literature documenting that individuals with SUD are less likely to receive recommended preventive health care services and cancer screening, including for cervical cancer, which contributes to health care disparities.22,28,29,30,31,32

Our population also had a high uptake (90.8%) of HPV self-collection testing when offered. Stigma and interpersonal discrimination are frequently cited by individuals with SUD as barriers to preventive health care.33,34,35 The OTP strives to establish stable, supportive, long-term relationships with individuals with OUD, following models for addiction harm reduction.36,37,38 The trusting relationships built with clinicians and staff at the clinic are likely factors in the high uptake of HPV self-collection testing. Providing patient-centric cervical cancer screening in an OTP can add value to existing services for individuals with OUD and aligns with other studies demonstrating the importance of integrating primary and preventive care into OTPs.39,40

We found a higher than average prevalence of high-risk HPV (19.0%, including 4.4% with HPV-16 or HPV-18 types and 14.6% with other types of high-risk HPV [non–HPV-16 or HPV-18 types only]), which is nearly double the prevalence of both HPV-16 and HPV-18 and other high-risk HPV types observed in a recent large cervical cancer screening study in an integrated health care delivery system in Washington.14 Individuals with OUD often have risk factors for cervical cancer, including lack of prior screening, decreased uptake of HPV vaccination, and history of high-risk sexual contacts.22,23,24 Thus, the high prevalence of high-risk HPV is not surprising and reinforces both the need and potential benefit of introducing cervical cancer screening into routine medical care in OTPs.

Most studies of HPV self-collection testing have focused on clinics or health care systems that already provide routine cervical cancer screening using clinician-collected samples.14 In contrast to those studies, we had to create an entirely new workflow for HPV testing, result management, and follow-up. New patients with OUD often present with multiple medical and social issues, such as active withdrawal, making initial patient intake less opportune for routine HPV screening. However, those patients with established long-term relationships with the clinic come in annually for a wellness visit, which was when we implemented the HPV self-collection testing. Medical assistants were integral to identifying eligible individuals for screening using a simple decision tree (Figure 1). Testing was easily accomplished at the time of urine collection, which patients routinely provide for federally mandated monitoring. We had no inadequate samples, demonstrating the ease and effectiveness of the simple collection instructions we developed (eFigure in Supplement 1). The largest challenge has been in ensuring guideline-concordant follow-up of positive screening results. As mandated by Washington state, the OTP was already providing on-site screening for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, syphilis, and tuberculosis and had systems in place to provide patients with positive test results, referrals when needed, transportation if desired, and to track follow-up. We used those processes to provide referral and follow-up for patients with results positive for high-risk HPV. Nevertheless, multiple coordination attempts were often required by clinicians.

Patients with OUD tend to report high rates of sexual abuse history, making the avoidance of a pelvic examination at the time of cervical cancer screening particularly advantageous; specimen self-collection offers autonomy and privacy that may lower psychological barriers to screening participation. This history of trauma also underscores the importance of providing trauma-informed gynecologic follow-up care for patients with HPV-positive result who require colposcopy or cervical cytological examination.23,24 To facilitate follow-up care, we identified a list of clinicians who would provide trauma-informed gynecologic care in a variety of locations. One patient who completed colposcopy after several previous canceled visits had a positive experience and communicated a strong feeling of empowerment and self-pride to the clinician and to study staff. It was also important to provide patients with options for intake and exam completion in a single visit. To date, 2 patients (6.7%) have been lost to follow-up and 16 patients (53.3%) remain in active follow-up.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this single-site study was conducted at an OTP with a specific demographic profile, including a relatively high proportion of American Indian or Alaska Native individuals, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to OTPs with different patient populations or resource levels. Second, our recruitment strategy relied on patients undergoing annual wellness visits; this convenience sample may represent a subset of OTP patients who are more engaged in their health care than the patient population seeking care for OUD, potentially inflating cervical cancer screening uptake rates. Third, while screening uptake was high, our diagnostic follow-up is ongoing, with 40% of patients with positive test results having completed guideline-concordant follow-up, highlighting the persistent structural and psychosocial barriers to downstream care in this population. Fourth, 60.0% of patients with an HPV-positive result have not yet completed guideline-concordant diagnostic evaluation and remain in various stages of referral and scheduling, reflecting ongoing engagement rather than attrition. Fifth, the study involved a transition between 2 different HPV testing laboratories (from University of Washington HPV Molecular Pathology Laboratory to Labcorp) during the implementation period, although both used the same testing platform that is FDA-approved for high-risk HPV detection in a clinical setting.

Conclusions

This cohort study showed that incorporation of cervical cancer screening into an OTP using HPV self-collection testing was feasible and acceptable, with both high patient eligibility and uptake. Implementation of HPV self-collection testing minimally disrupted clinic flow when it was added to processes of the annual wellness visit. HPV self-collection testing can expand cervical cancer screening in settings that do not currently offer this service and reach previously unscreened patients, which should play a role in reduced health care disparities and cervical cancer mortality.
