Weight loss and a shunt on “off”: an odd case of overshunting in an idiopathic intracranial hypertension patient. Illustrative case
Authors: Thomas Hamre, Ashley Ricciardelli, Alex Flores, Ashwin Viswanathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), or pseudotumor cerebri, is characterized by elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) without an apparent etiology, potentially arising from impaired venous outflow, increased intra-abdominal pressure, and/or compromised CSF resorption.1 IIH predominantly occurs in obese women of reproductive age and presents with symptoms such as intractable headaches, pulsatile tinnitus, and vision threatening papilledema. Diagnosis relies on clinical presentation and neuroimaging, which often reveals characteristic features such as an empty sella, posterior globe flattening, optic nerve sheath distension, or transverse venous sinus stenosis.2

Management of IIH primarily aims to reduce ICP and thus preserve vision and alleviate headaches. Most patients respond well to first-line treatments such as weight loss and pharmacologiccal agents, including acetazolamide or topiramate.3–5 Some studies have demonstrated that a modest 3%–15% reduction in body weight can lead to disease remission with ICP normalization.6 When conservative measures fail, surgical management most commonly involves CSF diversion with ventriculoperitoneal (VP) or lumboperitoneal (LP) shunts.7 VP shunts are widely utilized in IIH management and have been demonstrated to yield significant short-term symptom improvement.2,8 However, long-term management of patients with shunts is characterized by a high incidence of shunt malfunction, with up to 20% of patients experiencing overdrainage.4,8,9 Programmable valves provide greater control over shunt flow postoperatively, yet concerns exist regarding the risk of overdrainage due to the siphoning effect in cases in which IIH patients with shunts experience significant weight loss that can reduce their CSF pressure.

Low-pressure headaches resulting from intracranial hypotension secondary to overshunting represent a significant clinical challenge, as they can be difficult to differentiate from high-pressure headaches resulting from inadequate CSF diversion.10 Both conditions can mimic primary headache disorders, frequently present as persistent daily headaches, and although low-pressure headaches traditionally worsen when upright and high-pressure headaches when supine, positional characteristics can vary in practice.11,12 Diagnosing overshunting and monitoring changes in ICP after valve adjustments often necessitate frequent CT imaging and clinic visits, resulting in considerable patient dissatisfaction. The significance of this clinical problem and the limited literature detailing effective diagnostic approaches and management strategies in this population underscore the need for optimized diagnostic/treatment pathways that reduce economic and health burdens while maintaining diagnostic efficacy.13,14

We present the case of a 48-year-old woman with IIH with a previously implanted programmable VP shunt with an antisiphon device (ASD) who subsequently achieved IIH remission through significant weight loss. The patient then developed low-pressure headaches, suggestive of overshunting. These symptoms persisted despite setting the Certas valve to the “virtual off” position and the intact ASD. MRI and CT myelogram studies ruled out an occult CSF leak. Given the patency of Certas valves in the “virtual off” position and without evidence of a CSF leak, overdrainage was attributed to the siphoning effect. Shunt ligation was performed, resulting in significant clinical improvement at follow-up.

Illustrative Case

A 48-year-old female initially presented to the neurosurgery clinic to establish care. Her medical history included multiple neurological conditions and surgeries, including a suboccipital craniectomy for Chiari type I malformation at age 24 years and a transsphenoidal resection of a pituitary adenoma for Cushing’s disease at age 28 years, both without complications. The patient’s history also included VP shunt placement for IIH, with three subsequent revisions over a 6-year period due to presumed shunt malfunctions. At this initial visit, the patient’s BMI was 40.03 kg/m2, which is class III obesity. The patient presented with persistent holocephalic pressure-like headaches without a clear positional component or photophobia. Associated symptoms included nausea, lightheadedness, pulsatile tinnitus, acute-onset bilateral blurred vision, and a pulling sensation in the abdomen. Fundoscopic examination showed no papilledema, and shunt malfunction was suspected, supported by CT imaging showing slight ventricular enlargement compared with a slit-like appearance 3 years prior. No infection was found.

It was decided to replace the existing Polaris SPV 400 shunt (Sophysa) with a Certas programmable valve and a Certas SiphonGuard ASD (Integra LifeSciences). Intraoperative manometry revealed both proximal and distal catheter dysfunction. The distal segment was revised with placement of a new peritoneal catheter. Proximal dysfunction was found to be caused by choroid plexus adhesion. Stylet manipulation and electrocautery restored catheter flow. The Certas valve was set to 4 postoperatively, leading to significant symptom improvement.

The patient presented for follow-up evaluation the next year with positional headaches that improved when supine, accompanied by nausea, blurry vision, and right-sided weakness. Head CT imaging revealed decompressed lateral ventricles without acute abnormalities. Given the postural headaches in the setting of a VP shunt and imaging findings indicative of low ICP, low-pressure headaches were deemed the most likely diagnosis. Consequently, the Certas valve was adjusted to setting 5, which provided substantial and sustained headache relief.

Subsequently, the patient unrelatedly began treatment with Mounjaro (tirzepatide; Eli Lilly and Company), a GLP-1 agonist for weight loss. She ultimately experienced a weight reduction of 25 kg in 1 year (with BMI decreasing to 33.09 kg/m2). A few months after beginning treatment, the patient again began experiencing severe postural headaches accompanied by nausea and vomiting that markedly improved while supine, indicating possible changes in CSF dynamics.

One year after adjusting the Certas valve to setting 5, the patient presented to the emergency department three times within 1 month with positional headaches that improved when supine, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and blurry vision. Serial CT imaging at each visit demonstrated globally decompressed, slit-like ventricles without interval change. Given the postural nature of the headaches in the context of a VP shunt, low-pressure headaches were felt to be the most likely diagnosis. Initially set at setting 5, the Certas valve was incrementally adjusted upward during each visit (to 6, then 7, and finally 8) due to the suspected overshunting. During the third visit, the valve was briefly set to the “virtual off” position (setting 8) but was reverted back to setting 7 after headache severity worsened, despite setting 8 providing temporary relief of the positional headache component. Despite the progressive valve adjustments, the patient’s symptoms remained refractory to the exhaustive valve adjustments/settings. Two months later, the patient returned to the emergency department with similar symptoms, and CT imaging again showed globally decompressed ventricles, further indicating persistent overshunting (Fig. 1).

As a result, the patient was admitted to the neurointensive care unit and underwent implantation of a Cerelink ICP sensor (Integra LifeSciences) by the neurosurgical team to a depth of 3 cm, enabling continuous ICP monitoring for a 5-day period. The patient was also instructed to maintain a headache diary throughout this period, documenting the timing, severity, and characteristics of headache exacerbations to facilitate the correlation of subjective symptoms with ICP measurements.

On postoperative day (POD) 1 following ICP sensor implantation, ICP recordings with the patient supine measured 7 mm Hg. In sitting and standing positions, ICP values of 0 and −1 mmHg were noted, respectively, correlating with the patient’s headaches in these positions. This evidence of postural intracranial hypotension informed an adjustment of the Certas valve from setting 7 to the “virtual off” position at setting 8. Despite maximized shunt opening pressure at 400 mm H2O (setting 8), the patient still experienced low-pressure headaches (recorded in her headache diary) and markedly low ICP throughout the remaining period with intracranial ICP sensing (consistently over 5 days in the hospital). A CT myelogram study performed on POD 2 revealed no occult CSF leak, and the ICP sensor was removed on POD 5 without complications. With the shunt valve still at setting 8, the patient continued to experience low-pressure headaches.

Based on the diagnostic findings including the correlation between acute headache episodes in the headache diary with low ICP monitor readings, positional changes in ICP consistent with overshunting, the absence of an occult CSF leak, and persistent symptoms for multiple months at setting 7 and during ICP monitoring at setting 8, it was decided to ligate or remove the VP shunt. After discussing both options, the patient indicated a preference for and elected to proceed with shunt ligation, which also allows for a less invasive means of reestablishing CSF diversion with an in-place shunt should the patient experience gain weight resulting in high ICP in the future. Although we did not interrogate the functionality of the SiphonGuard ASD or consider placing a new ASD, it was felt that, regardless of the status of the ASD, persistent low-pressure symptoms and overdrainage of CSF at increasingly high opening pressures warranted shunt ligation or removal. The patient was taken to the operating room, and radiographic images was obtained to identify the peritoneal catheter overlying the clavicle. An incision to the subclavicular region provided access to the catheter, and two silk ties were placed in series along the shunt tract to ensure complete occlusion. The patient was discharged home without complications on the same day. At the 1-month follow-up, the patient reported a sustained and complete resolution of headaches.

Literature Review

A comprehensive literature review was conducted using PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) methodology as described by Page et al. to identify relevant cases.15 Abstracts that described cases of weight loss unrelated to IIH or that lacked discussion of shunt independence were excluded. Of the 25 remaining abstracts that fulfilled the initial criteria, full-text review led to the exclusion of an additional 21 studies due to insufficient case-specific details. From the remaining 4 papers, we report 6 cases of shunt independence following IIH weight loss. Discussion

Observations

Multiple critical points for the managing neurosurgeon arise from this case. Foremost is the remarkable efficacy of weight loss in resolving IIH. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that achieving a relatively modest weight reduction of just 10%–15% of total body weight can induce remission of IIH and lead to complete resolution of its associated symptoms.6,16,17 However, despite the robust evidence supporting weight loss as an effective treatment for IIH, there is a relative lack of studies concerning unexpected complications in IIH patients with shunts who then experience significant weight loss.18,19 We compiled and present a summary of such cases in Table 1. Our case provides further insight and an example of successful diagnosis and management of this problem.

A comprehensive PRISMA review identified 6 patients, aged 17–28 years, with a history of IIH and preexisting VP or LP shunts (Fig. 2). All patients had undergone bariatric surgery with subsequent rapid weight loss. Four patients had weight change documented in kilograms, ranging from 27 to 50 kg. Two patients, with only BMI data available, experienced BMI reductions of 15 and 28.5 points, respectively. Following substantial weight loss, these patients exhibited significant changes in ICP dynamics, manifesting in two distinct patterns. In the first pattern, patients who previously had relatively well-controlled IIH developed low-pressure symptoms. This included headaches that were predominantly orthostatic, often holocephalic, with a dull pressure-like quality and sometimes accompanied by nuchal tenderness, audiovestibular disturbances, and nausea. In the second pattern, patients who had instead experienced persistently high ICP refractory to shunting achieved near-complete symptom resolution. Both clinical courses represent a significant ICP reduction due to weight loss.

Diagnostic evaluation to assess CSF pressure following weight loss included lumbar punctures and MRI in a single patient, while the remaining cohort was diagnosed with clinical evaluation only. Management strategies varied: 3 patients underwent shunt removal, 2 were candidates for removal but had not yet undergone the procedure at the time of publication, and 1 received shunt replacement, remaining shunt dependent. All patients reported significant symptomatic improvement, with all but one experiencing complete symptom resolution.

Weight loss in VP shunt patients is linked to persistent low-pressure headaches, as demonstrated by the PRISMA review, even after shunt deactivation or increased valve opening pressures seemingly incompatible with overdrainage. This inconsistency is attributed to a gravity-dependent siphoning effect inherent to VP shunts that can cause excessive CSF drainage.20 The siphoning effect often leads to postural low-pressure headaches when upright, slit-like ventricles, and significantly reduced CSF pressure. While supine, ventricular hydrostatic pressure slightly exceeds intraperitoneal pressure, promoting mild CSF drainage. Standing induces a slight ICP reduction and an increase in intraperitoneal pressure. However, standing also creates a substantial vertical gradient between ventricles and peritoneum, enhancing CSF drainage through a suction-like effect, potentially overpowering high valve opening pressures.21

The siphoning effect, arising from the significant vertical distance between the ventricles and peritoneum, is exacerbated by weight loss. Weight loss induces a decrease in intraperitoneal pressure, which can further intensify the suction-like pressure drawing CSF from the ventricles to the peritoneum when the patient is upright, thereby increasing CSF drainage to the peritoneum.22–24 ASDs have been developed to counteract this excessive drainage.

Although numerous classes of ASDs exist, design limitations hinder the development of an exceedingly reliable method for countering the siphoning effect. Diaphragm ASDs utilize the suction-like pressure created by the siphoning effect within the shunt lumen to pull a flow-occluding diaphragm closed, preventing CSF overdrainage.25 However, subcutaneous tissue compression and imprecise anatomical positioning can significantly alter the functionality of this ASD design. Gravitational ASDs use small balls that descend onto a valve seat to occlude CSF flow in vertical positions. However, their functionality depends on their orientation in 3D space and can thus be compromised by deviation from strict vertical alignment during neck flexion, oscillatory activities like jogging, or positioning away from the body’s midline.26–28 Flow reducing ASDs (like the SiphonGuard ASD added in series with our patient’s Codman Certas valve) utilize a primary channel that closes via ball occlusion at high CSF flow, redirecting CSF through a high-resistance secondary channel.27,29 While position independent, these devices remain technically patent at all times. Some studies have demonstrated that the flow rates of such devices, including the SiphonGuard, are significantly influenced by individual device characteristics and anatomical positioning.30,31

Thus, it becomes apparent that the Certas valve and SiphonGuard ASD system at setting 8 (as in our case), advertised as “virtual off,” is not truly off. Although setting 8 is advertised as having an opening pressure of 400 mm H2O, a claim substantiated by multiple studies, anterograde CSF flow can still occur due to the siphoning effect.32,33 Even ASDs cannot always prevent overshunting due to the complex and dynamic interaction of differential pressures within interconnected anatomical compartments, compounded by natural movements and fluctuations in body position over time and the possibility of ASD failure. And as in this case, the siphoning effect can induce sufficient CSF overdrainage to precipitate low-pressure headaches, despite the adjustment of the valve to the highest setting. While shunt ligation has been demonstrated to be an effective solution in many of the cases detailed in Table 1, we present a comprehensive solution to this problem that includes an effective diagnostic pathway and treatment, that is, confirmation of low-pressure headaches with invasive ICP monitoring and the maintenance of a detailed headache diary followed by shunt ligation. This case also highlights the significant implications of GLP-1 agonist utilization in neurosurgical practice. These medications can induce rapid weight loss that leads to unaccounted for changes in physiology, that is, intra-abdominal pressure changes, that require consideration in routine neurosurgical care and treatment planning.

Our findings are tempered by several limitations. The diagnostic pathway we have outlined, which involves invasive ICP monitoring and maintaining a headache diary, appears robust in assisting the diagnosis of low ICP secondary to overshunting. However, the limited trial period at setting 8 somewhat constrains the generalizability of our results, although the patient had experienced persistent low-pressure headaches for months at a high opening pressure at setting 7. Furthermore, although our patient expressed a strong preference for less invasive shunt ligation compared with shunt removal, shunt removal may confer a lower risk of infection, potentially outweighing its greater perceived invasiveness.

Lessons

In IIH patients with functioning shunts, decreases in intra-abdominal pressure following weight loss can result in persistent overdrainage and refractory low-pressure headaches. The Certas programmable valve at its highest setting remains technically patent despite a very high opening pressure, potentially still allowing uncontrolled overdrainage facilitated by the siphoning effect. Confirmation of low-pressure headaches with invasive ICP monitoring and a simultaneous headache diary followed by shunt ligation is presented as an effective diagnostic and treatment paradigm for this problem.
