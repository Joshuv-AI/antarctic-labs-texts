Evaluation of magnification- and fluorescence-guided techniques for composite restoration removal during endodontic retreatment among undergraduate and postgraduate dental students
Authors: Batuhan Kökci, Sabahat Serenay Satış, Serhan Akman, Ahmet Babalık, Liviu Steier, Sema Belli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study was planned to assess the impact of magnification and fluorescence-guidance on the performance of undergraduate and specialist program students during access cavity preparation before retreatment.Materials and methodsThe study was conducted with 4th, 5th year undergraduate and specialist program students studying in the Department of Endodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Selcuk University, Konya, Türkiye. Each participant received three root-filled and composite resin restored extracted human molar teeth, which were standardly prepared by the same clinician. The teeth were scanned with an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 5) before the restoration. The participants were asked to prepare access cavities by using the following: (1) naked eye (control group); (2) operating microscope; (3) fluorescence-guided magnification (Reveal). The time required for composite material removal and reaching canal orifices (procedure period) was recorded, and all specimens were re-scanned. The resulting standard tessellation language (STL) data were superimposed on the initial scan using a software (Geomagic Control X), and three-dimensional (3D) comparisons were made. Root mean square (RMS) values were then calculated and interpreted as an indicator of the amount of tooth tissue removed and the amount of composite resin remaining. Two-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the main and interaction effects of education level and visual guidance method on procedure period and RMS values. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.ResultsThe Reveal device showed the lowest RMS value among all the test groups (p < 0.001) while no difference was observed among the others. Reveal device also recorded as the fastest method for access cavity preparation during endodontic retreatment (p < 0.001). The operating microscope required the longest period regardless of the effect of experience level (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe Reveal device enabled effective composite material removal by facilitating the identification of previous composite resin, reduced the procedure period, and required no additional training for students with varying experience levels. Despite the advantage of high magnification, the operating microscope was found to be less useful at the beginning of education. While the control group had an advantage over the operating microscope in terms of speed, it was not as effective as the Reveal device for composite material removal.

Introduction

Endodontic treatment prevents tooth loss and ensures its long-term retention, eliminating microorganisms in root canals by addressing root-related problems [1]. Nonsurgical endodontic treatment procedures include access cavity preparation, cleaning and shaping, root canal obturation, and coronal restoration. Any errors at these successive steps [2] or coronal leakage because of a failed or low-quality restoration or cracks occurring in the tooth or restoration [3] can lead to further endodontic deterioration. Following failure of initial endodontic treatment, various compensatory approaches can be provided after evaluation of the tooth’s condition, such as: repeat nonsurgical root canal treatment (retreatment), a surgical approach, or extraction [4].

Removing the entire previous restoration is generally recommended to provide ideal access to the canals during nonsurgical endodontic retreatment [5]. Care should be taken during the removal of the previous restoration to avoid additional tooth structure loss. Particularly in teeth where the canal orifices are sealed with a tooth-colored restorative material, removal of the material can be extremely challenging for the operator [6]. Compared with amalgam restorations, the removal of tooth-colored restorations poses several complications, including the unnecessary weakening of tooth structure, excessive cavity enlargement, and extended treatment durations [7]. To address this challenge and ensure the conservative removal of tooth-colored materials, the current literature traditionally recommends the use of optimal illumination, visual and tactile inspection, and magnification systems [8, 9]. This conventional approach relies heavily on the operator’s clinical experience and tactile feedback to differentiate between the residual composite and natural dentin. However, novice operators and students typically lack this requisite experience.

Recently, fluorescence emission induced by artificial light of a specific wavelength has provided an alternative method for the objective detection of tooth-colored restoration margins [10, 11]. The Reveal device utilizes fluorescent light for the clinical detection of dental caries and provides simultaneous treatment. Having various applications such as early caries detection and treatment, oral biofilm detection, identification of dental cracks, periodontitis, and oral mucosal lesions [12], fluorescence guidance enables identification of the tooth-colored restoration margins [13, 14]. Working under fluorescence guidance can prevent unnecessary tooth structure loss during composite material removal in retreatment procedures [11]. Therefore, this device may be beneficial for inexperienced operators in retreatment scenarios where canal orifices are sealed with a tooth-colored composite material.

Although the European Society of Endodontology (ESE) requires dental graduates to possess adequate clinical competence in retreatment cases [15], students reportedly experience their lowest self-confidence during retreatment procedures [16]. Consequently, preparing dental students for diverse retreatment scenarios is of critical importance.

Root Mean Square (RMS) analysis based on three-dimensional (3D) surface superimposition is increasingly used as an objective method and reliable parameter to quantify morphological changes between two superimposed surface models [17]. A low RMS value reflects a high degree of agreement between the compared surfaces, whereas a high RMS value indicates greater deviation, resulting from either loss of substrate (negative deviation) or excess material (positive deviation) [18].

Although previous studies have evaluated the use of magnification [19] and fluorescence systems [6, 20] in various restorative and endodontic procedures, the specific effects on the accuracy of removing tooth-colored restorations from teeth requiring retreatment among practitioners with varying levels of clinical experience remain unclear. Moreover, the use of 3D superimposition and RMS analysis to objectively assess the efficacy of these visual aids during retreatment access is limited in the literature. Therefore, the aim of this in vitro study was to evaluate the effect of different visual guidance systems (naked eye, operating microscope, and fluorescence-guided systems) on the technical performance of undergraduate and specialty dental students during the removal of composite materials prior to endodontic retreatment. Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry at Selcuk University, Konya, Türkiye (Approval No: 2025/05), and funded by the Scientific Research Projects Coordination Center of Selcuk University (Project number: 25132018). The minimum sample size required for the study was calculated using an a priori power analysis performed with G*Power version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany), assuming an effect size of Cohen’s f = 0.40, a significance level of α = 0.05, and 80% power (1 − β = 0.80). To ensure a more conservative and robust sample size estimation and to strengthen the achieved statistical power at the end of the study, the calculation was performed based on a conventional fixed-effects ANOVA framework with 9 independent groups, corresponding to the 3 education level × 3 visual guidance method combinations. Accordingly, the required total sample size was calculated as 108, corresponding to 12 samples per combination group. This sample size also provided an achieved power of approximately 92% for detecting interaction effects of the same effect size. The schematic flowchart of the study is shown in Fig. 1.

Pilot study

The sensitivity of the TRIOS 5 (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) intraoral scanner in evaluating endodontic access cavities has not been previously studied. Five randomly selected teeth were scanned twice after access cavity preparation and superimposed using Geomagic Control X software (3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). The RMS values obtained from the 3D comparison of these models (22.1 ± 4.58 μm) were found to be close to or lower than the lower limit of the range reported in the literature [21, 22]. In an in vitro study, Almalki et al. utilized intraoral scanners for post space impressions and emphasized that digital impressions of post spaces with depths of 6–8 mm demonstrated favorable results compared to conventional impression methods, with deviations in RMS values remaining within a clinically acceptable range (< 100 μm) [22]. Similarly, Zhu et al. evaluated the scanning accuracy of intraoral scanners for post spaces of varying diameters and depths, determining the acceptable deviation in RMS values to be < 100 μm [21]. Furthermore, Jain et al. evaluated the precision of three different intraoral scanners for full-arch dental implant-supported prostheses and reported that the TRIOS 5 intraoral scanner exhibited the lowest deviation in terms of precision (37.8 ± 4.53 μm) [23]. A lower RMS value is considered a sign of better matching; thus, sufficient preliminary validation of the device’s sensitivity was provided.

Preparation of the samples

One hundred and eight caries-free mandibular molars with fully formed roots, extracted for periodontal or surgical reasons, were used in this study. During the tooth selection process, care was taken to ensure that all teeth were morphologically intact and free of any restorations. Additionally, teeth with severe anatomical variations or those presenting with four canals were excluded. After the tooth surfaces were cleaned, access cavities were prepared by the same researcher (B.K.) using a high-speed handpiece and diamond burs (Dimei Dental Material, Henan, China) under water cooling, following conventional access cavity protocols. All cavities were prepared using 2.5× magnification and standardized dental unit illumination (50,000 lx). Working lengths were determined using a #10 K-type hand file, and glide paths were established (PathFile, EndoArt, Istanbul, Türkiye). The mesial canals were prepared using a #25 reciprocating file (EndoArt Expert Gold, Istanbul, Türkiye), and the distal canals were prepared using a #40 reciprocating file (EndoArt Expert Gold, Istanbul, Türkiye). Irrigation was performed with 2 mL of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), and final irrigation was completed with 2 mL of saline. The root canals were dried with paper points (EndoArt, Istanbul, Türkiye) and filled with a resin-based sealer (Imicryl, Konya, Türkiye) and gutta-percha (EndoArt, Istanbul, Türkiye) using the cold lateral compaction technique. The root canal orifices were then cleaned with alcohol. Finally, the teeth were embedded in acrylic resin blocks up to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) (BG Dental, Ankara, Türkiye) and numbered consecutively from 1 to 108.

Scanning of the samples

First, the specimens were securely fixed in place and scanned using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 5) to obtain the initial scans (S1). The scanning procedure was performed in the following sequence: the outer surfaces (mesial, buccal, distal, and lingual), followed by the inner surfaces (pulpal side: mesial, buccal, distal, and lingual) (Fig. 2). The acquired datasets were then exported in Standard Tessellation Language (STL) format. STL is a standard file format widely used in computer-aided design and three-dimensional imaging systems, digitally representing the surface geometry of an object as a mesh of interconnected triangles without including color or texture information.

Restoration of the specimens

Prior to the restoration, shade selection was performed for each tooth using the button technique. The shades selected for the composite resins were A1, A2, and A3, matched individually to each tooth. During the restorative phase, a selective enamel etching technique was applied; the enamel surfaces were etched with 38% orthophosphoric acid for 10 s, subsequently rinsed with water for 20 s, and dried with an air syringe. A universal adhesive system (G-Premio BOND, GC, Tokyo, Japan) was then applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions and light-cured for 10 s. The root canal orifices and the floor of the pulp chamber were covered with a shade-matching flowable composite resin (G-ænial Universal Injectable, GC, Tokyo, Japan) and light-cured for 20 s. The coronal restorations were completed by placing a composite resin material (Charisma Classic, Kulzer, Hanau, Germany) in 2-mm increments, with each increment light-cured for 20 s. Following the restoration, any excess composite material was removed using an Arkansas stone, and surface polishing was achieved using polishing discs (Sof-Lex, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) applied sequentially from coarse to fine. Finally, all specimens were incubated in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h to ensure complete polymerization prior to the initiation of the composite material removal procedures.

Selection and training procedures for the participants

A total of 36 participants were included in this study. The study population was meticulously selected from the Selcuk University Faculty of Dentistry (Konya, Türkiye) and divided into three equal groups (n = 12 per group): fourth-year undergraduate students, fifth-year undergraduate students, and postgraduate endodontic specialty students. The undergraduate groups comprised students who had successfully completed their preclinical and clinical endodontic training. The postgraduate group was selected from specialty students who had completed at least 18 months of the Speciality Training Program in Endodontics. The undergraduate students were selected from individuals with no prior experience using any magnification systems. Conversely, all postgraduate specialty students had received standard operating microscope training as part of their specialty program and were active users of dental loupes. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of any procedures. To ensure ergonomic standardization and optical consistency throughout the study, the interpupillary distance and optimal working distance for each participant were individually measured and adjusted. Subsequently, all participants underwent a comprehensive calibration and training session. Standardized written guidelines were distributed to the participants, followed by a 40-minute oral presentation. This training detailed the standardized operating protocols for the use of the Reveal system and the operating microscope.

Re-access cavity preparation

Each participant was provided with three randomly selected human molar teeth that had previously been root-filled and restored with composite resin. Participants were instructed to remove all composite material without damaging the natural tooth structure and to expose the root canal orifices using three different visual methods, applying only one method per specimen. For these procedures, diamond fissure, diamond round, and long-shank (25 mm) black-banded diamond round burs (Dimei Dental Material, Henan, China) were utilized. To prevent a lack of standardization caused by bur wear, new burs were used for each procedure. To simulate real clinical scenarios, participants were not instructed to follow a specific bur sequence; instead, they were allowed to choose and sequence the provided burs as they deemed clinically appropriate. Furthermore, to eliminate potential learning and fatigue effects on the participants during consecutive procedures, all possible sequential combinations of the three different methods were predefined. The 12 participants within each educational level were randomly assigned to one of the six sequences using a simple randomization method (Table 1).

3D comparison of the samples

3D comparison is the process of comparatively analyzing the agreement between two different datasets derived from 3D point cloud data using computer software. The 3D analysis in this study was performed using Geomagic Control X software. The digital models obtained using the 3D scanner were preprocessed and digitally cropped before analysis using Autodesk Meshmixer (Version 3.5.474; San Francisco, CA, USA). The apical portion of the acrylic pedestal was cropped to minimize the pedestal area and thus improve the reliability of the comparison [24, 25]. Then, the STL files obtained from the S1 and S2 data were imported into the Geomagic Control X program. First, the S1 data was set as the reference. The two transferred STL files were then aligned using the initial alignment and the best-fit alignment methods. The aligned samples were superimposed and compared using the 3D comparison feature of the software. The sampling rate was set to 100% for both alignment and 3D comparisons. To eliminate the risk of measurement bias, all 3D superimposition and data analyses were performed by an independent examiner (A.B.) who was blinded to the experimental groups. RMS values for each comparison were calculated and recorded. Color maps were created to visualize the deviation areas between the initial cavities and the re-access cavities. The 3D comparison feature in this software calculates the distance differences between the models and visualizes these differences with a color map [23] (Fig. 5).

The RMS value provides a quantitative measure of the average difference between two 3D surface models and shows how well the two analyzed models fit. A low RMS value indicates high agreement between the samples. Conversely, a high RMS value signifies a discrepancy. However, RMS alone does not directly specify whether the cause of the deviation is dentin loss or residual composite material.

Assessment of measurement protocol reliability using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC)

To minimize potential bias arising from the measurement protocol or device-related factors, all RMS measurements were performed independently by two evaluators. In addition to the primary researcher, intraoral scans were independently performed by a research assistant with equivalent academic experience on a separate computer, in a sequential and blinded manner, and the corresponding measurement values were derived from these scans.

Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) analysis was applied to assess inter-evaluator reliability between the primary researcher and the research assistant. The calculated ICC for inter-evaluator consistency was 0.982 (95% CI: 0.962–0.991), indicating a statistically significant (F = 108.8; p < 0.001) and very high level of agreement between the evaluators, thereby confirming the reliability of the measurement protocol (29).

Statistical analysis

In the dataset, differences between education levels, differences between visual guidance methods used during the procedure, and the interaction effect between these two factors were analyzed. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests and further confirmed by evaluating skewness and kurtosis coefficients as well as Q–Q plots. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that RMS values were normally distributed (W = 0.080, p = 0.502), whereas the procedure time variable significantly deviated from normality (W = 0.838, p < 0.001); therefore, a natural logarithmic transformation [ln(x)] was applied, and all inferential analyses were performed using the transformed data, while the original scale values were retained in the tables/graphs to preserve clinical interpretability. Throughout the study, parametric tests were used for both variables. To examine differences between education levels and visual guidance methods used during the procedure, homogeneity of variances was first tested using Bartlett’s test, which indicated that the assumption of equal variances was satisfied.

Two-way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the main effects of education level and visual guidance method, as well as their interaction effect, on cavity procedure periods and RMS values. Before conducting the primary factorial ANOVA, participant-level variance was additionally examined using a mixed-effects model with participant/operator included as a random effect. The intraclass correlation coefficients derived from the random variance components were 0.087 for procedure period and 0.118 for RMS values, indicating limited participant/operator-level variance. Following statistically significant results, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons. Significant group differences were denoted using superscript letter notation.

Descriptive statistics were presented together with sample size as mean ± standard deviation, median ± interquartile range, and minimum–maximum values. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Significant results were additionally categorized as p < 0.01 and p < 0.001 where applicable to indicate the strength of statistical evidence. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Released 2016. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Discussion

This in vitro study evaluated the effectiveness of different visual guidance methods used during endodontic retreatment procedures performed by participants with varying levels of clinical experience. Participants included both undergraduate dental students and postgraduate endodontic specialty students. The findings demonstrated that the fluorescence-guided Reveal device provided the most effective composite material removal, resulting in the lowest RMS values across all study groups (p < 0.001). In contrast, there was no significant difference in RMS between procedures performed with an operating microscope and those performed with the naked eye (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the Reveal system yielded the shortest procedure period for exposing the root canal orifices, regardless of the participants’ training level (p < 0.001).

Based on these findings, the first null hypothesis was completely rejected, as the type of visual guidance method affected both the efficacy of composite material removal and the procedure period. The second null hypothesis was partially rejected; while the educational level of the participants did not significantly affect the removal efficacy, it did have a significant impact on the procedure period, specifically between fourth-year undergraduate students and postgraduate specialty students.

Retreatment is the first choice of treatment to preserve the tooth when periapical healing cannot be achieved following initial non-surgical root canal treatment [26, 27]. Retreatment procedures involve technical difficulties in the stages of removing existing restorations and re-accessing the root canals, especially in teeth restored with tooth-colored restorations [6]. Removing a large amount of natural tooth structure during this process can negatively affect the biomechanical integrity of the tooth and compromise the long-term prognosis [5]. Considering the lack of confidence and experience of dental students in retreatment cases [16], it is of critical importance to prevent iatrogenic errors [6]. Therefore, the ability to differentiate the previous restoration during re-access cavity preparation is an important issue [6]. Magnification systems expand the field of view in endodontic applications, allowing for better perception of anatomical details. The operating microscope offers significant advantages, especially for experienced clinicians, in evaluating root canal anatomy and managing complex procedures [28]. However, microscope use has a certain learning curve, as it requires indirect vision, hand-eye coordination, and ergonomic adaptation [29].

In this study, the fact that the operating microscope did not provide an advantage over the control group in terms of RMS values may be attributed to the participants’ insufficient familiarity with operating microscope use. The literature also reports that the advantages provided by the operating microscope mostly become apparent after long-term clinical experience [30]. However, it is important to note that an increase in the RMS value does not exclusively indicate excessive tooth structure loss; it also reflects the presence of residual composite material. The magnification and illumination provided by the operating microscope might have enabled the participants to work within smaller access cavities, which could have potentially resulted in more residual composite material and, consequently, a higher RMS deviation. In other words, the elevated RMS values in the microscope group may reflect incomplete composite removal rather than excessive removal of sound dentin. Ultimately, because the goal of the procedure was to remove the composite material as completely as possible without damaging the natural tooth structure—as residual composite can blend with the surrounding dentin, hindering the location of canal orifices and predisposing the tooth to coronal microleakage and reinfection [5, 6]—the operating microscope was less effective than the Reveal device in meeting this objective. Furthermore, the longer procedure period in the microscope group is consistent with previous studies suggesting that microscope use may slow down the procedure for a novice operator [30, 31]. This result demonstrates that magnification alone does not necessarily facilitate a faster procedure.

The literature contains conflicting results regarding the effectiveness of magnification use among undergraduates. While some studies report that magnification use improves access cavity quality [32, 33], other studies have shown no significant difference, especially in complex 3D preparations [34]. These contradictions suggest that the type of magnification system used and the operator’s experience have a decisive effect on the results. The findings of this study demonstrate that dental operating microscopy alone may not be sufficient to improve the efficacy and speed of composite material removal among inexperienced operators.

Fluorescence-based technologies, unlike magnification, allow for clearer visual differentiation between dentin and the restorative material [35]. Fluorescence is based on the principle that materials emit longer wavelength light when excited by short wavelength light [36, 37]. The differences in fluorescence responses between healthy tooth tissues and composite materials make these systems effective in selective restoration removal procedures [38]. The greater efficacy of the Reveal device in this study is consistent with previous studies [6, 38] demonstrating the effectiveness of fluorescence-guided identification techniques in restorative material removal procedures. The literature reports that fluorescence-guided systems result in less residual composite and less tooth tissue loss compared to conventional illumination [11]. However, most of these studies are based on shallow cavity models or laboratory studies performed with a single operator. The present study makes a significant contribution to the literature by evaluating participants of different educational levels in a simulated retreatment scenario.

Among the visual guidance methods used, Reveal yielded the lowest RMS values (Table 4). RMS values did not differ according to the education level (p > 0.05). No significant interaction was observed between the educational level and the visual guidance method (Table 5). Based on these findings, it can be concluded that the Reveal device exhibits consistent performance across different educational levels. Because the device offers hands-free operation and visual feedback under direct vision, it is easily adaptable for the operator. The procedure period analyses of the present study also support this interpretation. The shorter procedure period with the Reveal device can be attributed to the faster visual differentiation of the composite material from the dentin. Finally, the decrease in the procedure period observed with increased operator experience demonstrates an inverse relationship between clinical experience and the procedure period [39].

The 3D surface comparison method and RMS analysis were used in this study for the evaluation of re-access cavities. Both the removed tooth structure and the residual composite were indirectly evaluated by superimposing the initial and final images. 3D analysis methods are increasingly used in dental research for objective, repeatable, and precise comparisons [11, 40]. The colorimetric deviation maps provided by 3D comparisons present a visual opportunity to identify areas of unnecessarily removed tooth structure and residual composite. Students thus have the opportunity to review and analyze the procedural errors they made during the operations. Furthermore, this method is fast and relatively inexpensive. However, it does not offer volumetric analysis capabilities. For this reason, precise volumetric analyses must be included in future studies for a more detailed evaluation.

Although the RMS value is a quantitative metric representing the mean 3D morphological deviation between two surfaces, its use as a surrogate endpoint constitutes a significant limitation. Because RMS provides an absolute value, it cannot differentiate between over-preparation (excessive tooth structure loss) and under-preparation (residual composite). Consequently, definitive claims regarding ‘conservative preparation’ or the precise preservation of sound tooth structure cannot be established based solely on RMS values; rather, the results reflect the overall efficacy of composite material removal.

Other limitations of the present study include its in vitro design, which cannot fully reflect complex intraoral clinical challenges such as saliva contamination, limited visibility, and patient-related factors. Furthermore, the methodology evaluated only a single type of resin composite. Since different restorative materials exhibit varying optical and fluorescent properties, the visual guidance performance of the Reveal system may be material-dependent. Therefore, future studies should incorporate various restorative materials to validate these findings. Additionally, from a clinical perspective, retreatment frequently involves the critical challenge of locating missed canals, which requires the targeted removal of specific dentin and enamel overlying the original anatomy. This important clinical aspect was not simulated or analyzed in the current experimental model.

Despite these limitations, the findings of this study demonstrate that the Reveal device is an effective method for supporting the removal of tooth-colored restorative materials during retreatment. By providing a different visual perspective compared to traditional methods, this technology enhances the efficacy of composite material removal during re-access procedures. Particularly in scenarios where canal orifices cannot be readily located, fluorescence guidance can successfully identify the composite material and thus prevent unintended loss of sound tooth structure. Nevertheless, further studies in dynamic clinical environments are required to confirm the generalizability of these findings.

Clinical and educational significance

The integration of fluorescence-guided systems into dental education curricula holds promise for supporting clinical performance. Reveal demonstrates a positive and consistent impact across all educational levels. This suggests that adaptation to the device is relatively easier. Furthermore, providing students with hands-on experience using this method during their undergraduate education is highly valuable; it equips them with new skills and may help them select the most appropriate treatment approaches in the future to access root canal orifices in teeth where the orifices have been obturated with tooth-colored composite material.

Furthermore, the 3D analysis utilized in this study presented the errors in the form of a color map, providing both students and evaluators with the opportunity to visually assess the re-access cavities.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, the fluorescence-guided Reveal device enabled faster and more accurate removal of composite restorative material to re-establish access to the root canal system during endodontic retreatment than the operating microscope or the naked eye, regardless of the operator’s educational level. Furthermore, three-dimensional superimposition with RMS analysis proved to be a valuable objective method for quantifying the morphological changes associated with retreatment access cavity preparation.
