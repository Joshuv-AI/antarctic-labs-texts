In ovo administration of ethanolic extracts of sweet orange peel, ginger and their combinations: effects on post-hatch growth performance, apparent nutrient digestibility and health status of broiler chickens
Authors: Adebukola T Orbugh, Damilola U Kareem, Emmanuel A Adeyeye, Obafemi F Akinjute, Oyegunle E Oke, Adebukunola O Lala, Olajide M Sogunle, Abimbola O Oso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn ovo administration of bioactive nutrients can mitigate poor hatchability, suboptimal growth, and weak innate immunity in broiler chicks. This study evaluated the effect of in ovo administration of ethanolic sweet orange peel extract (ESOPE), ginger extract (EGE) and their equal combinations (BLD) on egg hatchability, post-hatch growth performance, apparent nutrient digestibility and health status of broiler chickens. A total of 900 fertile Cobb 500 broiler eggs (58 ± 2 g) were randomly allocated to five treatments consisting of 180 eggs with six replicates of 30 eggs each. The treatments were: un-injected eggs (UIE), eggs injected at day 6 with 0.02 mL distilled water (DW), ESOPE, EGE and an equal blend of ESOPE and EGE (BLD). Egg incubation was maintained at 37.8 °C and a relative humidity of 60%. Parameters including incubation variables, Tona chick quality scores, post hatch growth performance, nutrient digestibility, haematological and serum indices, gut morphometry and caecal microflora were measured and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance in a completely randomized design. Means were separated using Tukey’s test at p < 0.05. Hatchability was significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the ESOPE group compared to the DW group. In ovo injection of eggs with ESOPE also improved (p < 0.05) chick weight and hatch yield compared to UIE and DW groups. At the finisher phase, Hatchlings from ESOPE had improved (p < 0.05) weight gain and feed conversion ratio compared with the UIE group. Dry matter digestibility improved in all extract groups when compared with DW group. The BLD group recorded increased (p < 0.05) duodenal and jejunal villi height compared to the UIE group and achieved complete suppression of caecal Staphylococcus aureus compared to other treatment. In conclusion, in ovo injection with these extracts, especially ESOPE at 0.02 mL per egg improved hatchability and growth performance of broiler chickens. Future studies should employ a dose-response design (0.02, 0.04, and 0.06 mL) to identify the precise threshold for optimal embryonic development.Keywords: Egg incubation, In ovo injection, Egg hatchability, Nutrient utilization, Post-hatch performance

Introduction

Modern broiler production achieves rapid growth and high feed efficiency, historically supported by the routine use of sub-therapeutic antibiotic growth promoters (Rafiq et al., 2022; Tona et al., 2022). However, indiscriminate use of these compounds has led to the emergence of multidrug-resistant microorganisms and environmental contamination (Mehdi et al., 2018; Manyi-Loh et al., 2018; Low et al., 2021). Falsified and substandard drugs exist in every region of the world, however, this challenge is particularly severe in Sub-Saharan Africa and south east Asia, where weak regulatory oversight and the widespread circulation of poor quality medicines contributes substantially to the growing burden of antimicrobial resistance (Van Boeckel et al., 2015; Holloway et al., 2017; Olagunju et al., 2025; Wada et al., 2022). These deficiencies compromise antibiotic efficacy, reduce treatment reliability, and further accelerate the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance in poultry production systems (Doris and Felicia, 2023). Consequently, there is an urgent global mandate to identify safe, non-antibiotic functional additives to safeguard therapeutic efficacy and maintain livestock productivity (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2022; Aminullah et al., 2025).

Among the alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters, phytobiotics, encompassing plant-derived bioactive constituents including essential oils, plant extracts, flavonoids and phenolic compounds, have been reported to exert antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, while simultaneously enhancing growth performance, intestinal architecture, nutrient digestibility and immune competence in broiler chickens (Kpomasse et al., 2023; Onagbesan et al., 2023; Oke et al., 2024; Oni et al., 2024; Urban et al., 2025; Aminullah et al., 2025). Specifically, sweet orange (Citrus sinensis) peels contain high concentrations of flavonoids with proven antioxidant capacity (Ogo et al., 2024; Majekodunmi et al., 2025). Similarly, ginger (Zingiber officinale) rhizomes possess bioactive gingerols and shogaols that enhance gut morphology and nutrient utilization (El-Kersh et al., 2021; Đurić Jarić et al., 2025). The selection of sweet orange peel extract (ESOPE) and ginger extract (EGE) for this study is based on their complementary phytochemical profiles and hypothesized synergistic effects on embryonic development. ESOPE is rich in d-limonene, a monoterpene that acts as a systemic antioxidant and has been shown to improve hatchability and villus dimensions by mitigating oxidative stress during the late-term incubation transition (Gore et al., 2024; Peşmen, 2025). In contrast, EGE contains bioactive gingerols and shogaols, which possess potent antimicrobial properties and enhance digestive enzyme secretion (Dalsasso et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). By combining these extracts, this study aims to explore a dual-action mechanism, providing a protective antioxidant shield for the embryo through ESOPE, while simultaneously priming the gastrointestinal environment for superior post-hatch nutrient utilization through EGE (Bozbay and Göneci, 2023; Residiwati et al., 2024). While the benefits of these extracts are well-documented, research has predominantly focused on post-hatch dietary supplementation (Oke et al., 2017; Oke, 2018; Lu et al., 2023).

Chick embryo derives all its nutrient requirements during incubation from the shell, albumen, and yolk. Therefore, egg composition is important for embryonic growth (Onbaşılar et al., 2017). In ovo administration represents a paradigm shift, allowing for the direct delivery of bioactives to the developing embryo. This technique facilitates early gastrointestinal maturation and immune priming, potentially reducing the need for extensive post-hatch interventions (Ajayi et al., 2022; Akosile et al., 2023a; Ajayi et al., 2024). Feeding phytobiotics in ovo can potentially improve chickens performance and antioxidant status, while decreasing the use of the traditional antibiotics in poultry (Akosile et al., 2023a). Kuka et al. (2023) found that in ovo plant extracts shield the embryo from lipid peroxidation, facilitating a more successful hatch. Additionally, Shehata et al. (2021b) reported that in ovo phytobiotics establish a favorable microbial landscape, decreasing vulnerability to enteric infections post-hatch.

Most in ovo studies with essential oils or oil extracts (EOs) are injected from day 9–18 (Aberbour et al., 2023; Meijer et al., 2024; Khaskheli et al., 2025), not as early as day 6. Research indicates that substances administered prior to day 18 of incubation are primarily utilized for organogenesis and embryonic growth. In contrast, injections performed during the later stages of incubation serve to provide the essential energy reserves required for the hatching process (Das et al., 2021). Very early timing (earlier than day 9–12) is underexplored. Day 6 of incubation was selected for in ovo administration in this study to target the early phase of embryonic organogenesis, gut and immune development in accordance with the developmental observations of Ohta and Kidd (2001), which confirms embryonic viability at this stage. This timing is supported by established literature demonstrating that early-stage interventions (Days 1–8) can significantly modulate post-hatch growth and gene expression (Kocamis et al., 1998; Subramaniyan et al., 2019). A conservative injection volume of 0.02 mL was selected to mitigate risks associated with embryotoxicity and albumen obstruction (Subramaniyan et al., 2019; Aberbour et al., 2023). This dosage aligns with the findings of Sulaiman and Al-Gharawi (2025), confirming that 0.02 mL facilitates optimal distribution of phytogenic extracts while preserving embryonic viability and maintaining internal physiological homeostasis throughout the incubation period.

Despite the known potential of citrus and ginger bioactives, their combined effect when administered during early embryonic development has not been explored. The rationale for this research is based on the premise that early-life intervention with potent phytogenic extracts can program the embryo for superior physiological resilience. Therefore, we hypothesized that the in ovo delivery of ethanolic extracts of sweet orange peel, ginger, and their combinations would improve hatchability and provide a biological foundation for enhanced post-hatch growth, nutrient utilization, and overall health status in broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

Test ingredients and the site of the experiment

Ginger rhizomes were sourced from the Eleweran market in Abeokuta, while sweet orange peel rinds were sourced from a single sweet orange seller around the Abeokuta metropolis, Ogun State.

Extraction of the oil extracts from sweet orange peel and ginger was carried out in the Animal Nutrition laboratory, Department of Animal Nutrition, Federal University of Agriculture, Abeokuta (FUNAAB). In ovo injection and incubation of eggs were carried out in the Hatchery unit of the College of Animal Science and Livestock Production (COLANIM), while feeding trials were carried out at the Poultry Unit of the Directorate of University Farms, FUNAAB, Nigeria.

Extraction of ethanolic extract from ginger rhizomes and sweet orange peels

Fresh ginger rhizomes were thoroughly washed with clean water, uniformly sliced to approximately 10 mm in thickness, and sun-dried until brittle, reducing the moisture content from an initial value of 82.7% to below 10%. The dried ginger rhizomes (1 kg) were milled using a Nima electric dry spice blender (Foshan Jizya Electric Appliance Co., Ltd. Guangdong, China) and sieved through a 1 mm mesh to obtain a fine powder. Thirty grams (30 g) of ginger powder were weighed into a thimble per batch and subjected to Soxhlet extraction using ethanol as the solvent. For each batch, 300 mL of absolute ethanol (Fisher Scientific, Bishop Meadow Road, Loughborough, LE11 5RG, UK) was added to a round-bottom flask, and extraction was carried out via reflux action for 75 min until an extract was obtained from the entire 1 kg of dried ginger powder. The solvent was subsequently removed from the combined filtrate by gentle distillation using a water bath maintained at ≤ 60°C. The concentrated ginger extract (110 mL) was transferred to a dark glass bottle to prevent photolytic degradation and stored in the refrigerator until use.

Similarly, fresh sweet orange (Citrus sinensis) peel were cleaned to remove dirt and other foreign materials. The peels were shade-dried until the moisture content decreased from 67.2% to below 10%. One kilogram of the dried peels was milled using the same blender used for ginger and sieved through a 1 mm mesh to obtain a fine powder. Extraction from powdered sweet orange peel was carried out using the same Soxhlet extraction procedure and conditions, as for ginger oil. After solvent removal, 194 mL of sweet orange peel extract was obtained and stored in dark bottles in a refrigerator until further use.

Hatchable eggs and experimental design

A total of 900 hatchable eggs, each weighing 58 ± 2 g were procured from a reputable commercial Cobb 500 broiler breeder hatchery for the study. The eggs were fumigated with potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) and liquid formalin (40% formaldehyde), individually weighed right before incubation (day 0) and assigned to five treatment groups of 180 eggs. Each treatment comprised six replicates of 30 eggs, which were placed in the setting trays of a single-stage incubator (Avida G4; Chick Master, USA) based on weight equalization to ensure uniform distribution and were properly labeled before incubation. The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design. Egg turning was performed automatically every 1 h, while incubation conditions were maintained at 37.8 °C and a relative humidity of 60%.

The five treatments were:

Preparation of extract-in-water emulsion for in ovo injection

Extract-in-water emulsions for in ovo injection were prepared using lecithin as the emulsifying agent. The formulation comprised 5% (v/v) extracted oil, 92.5% (v/v) distilled water, and 2.5% (w/v) lecithin. Lecithin was weighed at 0.5 g per 1 mL of the extract and initially dispersed in a portion of warm distilled water (40 – 50°C) to enhance solubilization. The dispersion was stirred continuously until a uniform and homogeneous solution was obtained.

The extracts were then gradually added to the lecithin solution under constant stirring. Homogenization was performed using a Nima high-speed blender (Foshan Jizya Electric Appliance Co., Ltd, Guangdong, China) for 10 minutes to ensure effective emulsification of the extracts. Subsequently, the remaining volume of distilled water was slowly incorporated while stirring vigorously to obtain a stable extract-in-water emulsion suitable for in ovo administration. The prepared emulsion was allowed to cool to room temperature prior to use and was freshly prepared to maintain emulsion stability and sterility during injection.

Egg injection procedure

In ovo injection was carried out on day 6 of incubation in accordance with the method of Ohta and Kidd (2001). The injection site (broad end of the egg) was disinfected with 70% ethanol prior to injection. A 1 mm hole was made using a sterile plastic hand egg puncher (Yiwu City Qiyuan Trading Co., Ltd). Diluted solutions of ginger and sweet orange peel extracts were administered into the albumen at a dose of 0.02 mL per egg to a depth of 3mm using a 1 mL sterile syringe fitted with a 13 mm needle with a physical stopper set at 3 mm. Following the injection, the drilled sites were sealed with sterile paraffin wax, and the eggs were immediately returned to the incubator for continued incubation. On day 18 of incubation, eggs were candled to confirm viability using an ultraviolet hand lamp and briefly removed from the incubator under aseptic conditions. Total fertile eggs were selected from the non-viable ones. All fertile eggs were arranged in individual labeled boxes in preparation for hatching to properly monitor each egg at hatch.

Post–hatch management of broiler chicks

On the 21st day of incubation (at hatch), the total number of hatchlings per replicate was recorded. Hatchlings from each replicate were weighed individually, tagged and reared according to their respective treatments (6 pens per treatment) in disinfected deep litter pens (3m x 2m), with wood shavings used for the floor covering at a 5cm depth. For the first three weeks of the brooding period, pen dimensions were reduced to 1 m × 2 m using a wooden partition to optimize environmental conditions. Following this stage, the partitions were removed for the remainder of the growth cycle. Throughout the trial, management and brooding practices adhered to the Cobb-Vantress guidelines (2021) for the Cobb 500 broiler strain. A step-down lighting program was implemented, providing an initial illuminance of 50 lux for 23 hours during the first three days of the rearing period to facilitate early brooding. Light intensity adjusted to 20 lux from day 4 to 7, then maintained at 10 lux for the remainder of the 42-day rearing period. The environmental conditions within the broiler rearing facility were monitored throughout the study. Weekly mean Temperature-Humidity Index (THI) values were calculated according to the formula described by Mader et al. (2006).

To align with age-dependent physiological requirements, the temperature was lowered by 2–3°C per week, reaching a final temperature of 23°C at week six. Relative humidity of 55-65% was maintained. The broiler chicks were fed a starter diet from day 1 to day 21 and a finisher diet from day 22 to day 42. Both diets were formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of broiler chickens as specified by the National Research Council (NRC, 1994), as presented in Table 1. The normal Cobb-500 vaccination programme and protocol as described by Cobb procedure management guide (2025) was strictly adhered to without the use of antibiotics. Growth performance

The daily quantity of feed consumed from each pen was measured while feed consumption per bird was calculated by subtracting the leftover feed from the feed offered divided by the total number of birds in each replicate. Each chick in each replicate was weighed once a week to determine weight gain, and the initial live weight was deducted from the final live weight. Similarly, the number of dead birds (mortality) were recorded daily and reported as a percentage, and the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as the feed intake divided by the weight gain.

Apparent nutrient digestibility

On d 21 and 42 of the study, a metabolic trial was conducted in a digestibility pen fitted with metabolic cages. Two birds were randomly selected from each replicate and housed in metabolic cages (measuring 0.4 m L by 0.3 m W by 0.45 m H) and fitted with independent feeding, drinking, and a sliding tray located below the mesh floor for excreta-collection. The birds were housed individually in metabolism cages and received the same experimental diets as their respective treatment groups. Feed offered and refusals were weighed daily to determine feed intake accurately. Throughout the trial, environmental conditions were maintained at a relative humidity of 50–65%. The temperature was set to 25–27°C during the starter phase and 23°C during the finisher phase. Following the procedure described by Bourdillon et al. (1990), birds were allowed a 3-d adaptation period to stabilize metabolism and acclimate to the experimental diets. During this period, birds were provided ad libitum access to feed and water. After adaptation, total excreta were collected twice daily for 4 d, pooled by replicate, and oven-dried at 60°C for subsequent analysis. Proximate composition of the diets and dried excreta was determined according to AOAC International (2000) methods for dry matter (Method 934.01), crude fiber (Method 978.01), ether extract (Method 920.39), ash (Method 942.05), and crude protein (N × 6.25; Method 990.03). Hematological indices and serum chemistry

On the 21st and 42nd day of the bird’s age, blood samples (6 ml each) were collected from the wing vein of six birds per treatment (1 bird per replicate). 3 ml was put into bottles containing anti-coagulant for the determination of hematological indices, while the other 3 ml was collected into plain bottles, centrifuged (5282 rpm) to harvest sera for serum chemistry analysis. Hemoglobin (Hb) was determined following the cyanmethemoglobin technique as described by Cannan (1958). The Packed Cell Volume (PCV), Red Blood Cell (RBC) count, and White Blood Cell (WBC) count were measured using Wintrobe haematocrit tubes, based on the procedure outlined by Schalm et al. (1975).

Total serum protein, albumin, and globulin were assessed following the procedure outlined by Varley et al. (1980). Serum uric acid levels were measured using the method described by Wootton (1964), while serum creatinine was analyzed based on the technique developed by Bonsnes and Taussky (1945). Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT), Alkaline phosphatase and Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST) were determined using standard protocols with Randox diagnostic kits (Randox Laboratories, Antrim, UK). Total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) were measured using commercially available diagnostic kits (Randox, GPO-PAP method; Randox Laboratories, Antrim, Northern Ireland).

Gut morphology

On day 42 of the study, six birds whose weights were representative of the average weight from each replicate were randomly selected and were humanely euthanized by a trained technician via cervical dislocation, following the protocol described by Jacobs et al. (2019). The abdominal cavity was opened, and the entire gastrointestinal tract was carefully removed. Tissue samples were collected from the mid-portions of the duodenum, jejunum and ileum. The collected samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, dehydrated using a graded series of alcohol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Thin tissue sections were mounted on glass slides, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined under a light microscope (Olympus CX21) at various magnifications. Photomicrographs were captured using an Amscope® 319CU CMOS digital camera and analyzed with Micrometrics imaging software. For each animal, one histological section was examined and five well-oriented intact villi per section were randomly selected and measured. In accordance with Onbaşılar et al. (2025), VH was recorded from the apex to the crypt junction, and CD was measured as the invagination distance between villi. Morphometric variables, including villus height, villus width, crypt depth, and mucosal thickness, were measured using Motic Images Plus 2.0 ML software (Motic China Group Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China).

Cecal microflora

The contents of the ceca from the birds selected for intestinal sampling were carefully collected into sterilized containers and stored at –20°C until further analysis. For bacterial enumeration, the frozen cecal samples were thawed and prepared for processing following the procedures outlined by Mountzouris et al. (2007). Bacteriological examination was performed to quantify total bacterial populations and to isolate specific bacterial strains using established microbiological methods. Total bacterial count was determined using the Miles and Misra technique as described by Hedges (2003). After incubation, bacterial colonies were counted, and the total bacterial load was calculated accordingly.

For isolation and culture, colonies grown on Nutrient Agar were transferred to fresh Nutrient Agar and MacConkey Agar plates to support the growth of both general and selective bacterial species. These plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Distinct colonies were then sub-cultured repeatedly to obtain pure bacterial isolates for further study. Colonial characteristics of the isolates were assessed according to the criteria described by Cowan and Steel (1993), with observations made on colony size, shape, color, texture, elevation, and margins to aid preliminary identification.

Statistical analysis

Data were tested for normality using Shapiro-Wilk test prior to ANOVA. Observations identified as outliers were removed only upon confirmation of experimental implausibility. Statistical significance was declared at p ≤ 0.05. Data were then subjected to one-way analysis of variance in a completely randomized design and analyzed using the SPSS ver. 30 statistical package (IBM Corp, 2024). Egg incubation variables and chick quality score

Effect of in ovo injection of ethanolic sweet orange peel extract (ESOPE) and ethanolic ginger extract (EGE) and their combinations (BLD) on egg incubation variables and chick quality scores is shown in Table 2. Hatchability, chick weight, hatch yield and leg score were influenced (p < 0.05) following in ovo injection of ESOPE, EGE and BLD. Hatch yield was increased (p = 0.003) by 17.54% in the ESOPE group (0.67) compared to the UIE (0.57) group. A significantly lower hatchability was observed in the UIE group (66.00%) compared to the ESOPE cohort (90.00%), representing a 26.67% reduction in hatching success for the UIE group (p = 0.023). Chick weight at hatch was 19.51% higher in the ESOPE group (39.32 g) compared to UIE (32.90 g; p < 0.001). All extract-treated groups (ESOPE, EGE, and BLD) achieved perfect Leg Scores (16.00), representing 26.58% increase over the DW group (12.64).

Post-hatch growth performance

Post-hatch growth performance of broiler chicks obtained from in ovo injection of ESOPE, EGE and BLD is shown in Table 3. Most growth variables measured during the starter phase were affected (p < 0.05) by ESOPE, EGE and BLD, except percentage mortality. Discussion

The chemical composition of the extracts used in this study served as the primary driver for the observed embryonic improvements. The qualitative and quantitative analysis of sweet orange peel oil by Tao et al. (2009) and Ferrer et al. (2022) revealed a phytochemical profile dominated by monoterpene hydrocarbons, with limonene as the major constituent. Specifically, Tao et al. (2009) identified that limonene accounts for 77.49% of the total oil in Bingtang sweet orange peels. Similarly, ginger oil is characterized by a high content of zingiberene, often constituting approximately 18–20% of the total oil, making it the most abundant sesquiterpene (Singh et al., 2008). Zingiberene largely contributes to the characteristic aroma of ginger and has been associated with antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities in biological studies (Mahboubi, 2019).

The improved hatchability and superior chick weight gain in the ESOPE group in this present study could be linked to the presence of limonene, the primary component of orange peel oil. During the internal pipping phase, embryos undergo intense oxidative stress. We hypothesize that in ovo delivery of ESOPE provided a pre-emptive antioxidant shield, as plant extracts have been shown to up-regulate the expression of antioxidant enzymes, thereby protecting the embryo and facilitating a more successful hatch (Kuka et al., 2023; Peşmen, 2025). Furthermore, Kocamis et al. (1998) reported that the in ovo injection of a growth hormone on the 3rd day of incubation increased the efficiency of muscle growth and development of the muscle during the first weeks of life. Whereas, the results from a study by Subramaniyan et al. (2019) revealed that survival and hatching rates, as well as body weight, were increased on the 14th day of incubation compared to the 8th and 18th days of incubation at lower concentrations of L-Arg. Recent research by Gore et al. (2024) indicates that limonene can enhance lipid metabolism, potentially providing the embryo with greater energy availability during the "explosive" growth phase of the final three days of incubation.

A critical observation in the present study was the comparatively lower hatchability recorded in the UIE and DW control groups relative to the extract-treated groups. The period between days 1 and 7 of incubation represents a highly sensitive phase of embryogenesis characterized by rapid organogenesis, vascularization, and tissue differentiation (Subramaniyan et al., 2019; Das et al., 2021). Consequently, embryos at this stage may be particularly vulnerable to environmental or procedural stress associated with in ovo manipulation. The relatively improved hatchability observed in extract-treated groups may partly suggest a protective role of the bioactive compounds present in sweet orange peel and ginger extracts, both of which contain phytochemicals with reported antimicrobial and antioxidant activities. Although microbial load and oxidative status were not directly assessed in the present study, these bioactive constituents may have contributed to improved embryonic stability during incubation. The comparatively lower hatchability observed in the distilled water group may indicate that the injected aqueous medium provided no additional protective or nutritive benefit to the developing embryo (Saeed et al. 2019). Previous studies have similarly reported reduced hatchability in water-injected controls compared with phytogenic-treated eggs. For example, Aberbour et al. (2023) observed lower hatchability in eggs injected with distilled water compared with rosemary oil-treated eggs, while Hajati et al. (2014) reported improved hatchability following in ovo administration of grape seed extract relative to intact and sham-injected controls. Variations in hatchability among studies may further reflect differences in injection timing, embryonic developmental stage, injection site, extract composition, egg handling procedures, and incubation conditions.

The assessment of chick quality using the Tona et al. (2003) scoring system revealed that extracts provided a protective buffer during the strenuous hatching stage compared to the DW group. In the Tona system, lower leg scores are triggered by signs of stress such as hock redness or joint swelling. The superior scores in the extract groups suggest that bioactive compounds like gingerols and shogaols in ginger extract, which possess potent anti-inflammatory properties (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022), reduced neonatal tissue inflammation. Additionally, the observed improvement in leg score among birds in the ESOPE-treated group indicates enhanced chick quality and skeletal integrity at hatch. While the current study did not measure bone mineralization markers directly, this observation aligns with previous findings where limonene was reported to promote osteoblast differentiation through the modulation of signaling pathways like p38MAPK (Soundharrajan et al., 2018). It is hypothesized that the antioxidant properties of limonene may provide a favorable microenvironment for early osteoblastogenesis during embryonic development (McCallum and Fox, 2025), though further molecular studies are required to confirm this specific mechanism in broilers as citrus fruits have been reported to be rich in d-limonene (Dosoky and Setzer, 2018). This priming of the musculoskeletal system ensures that the birds begin the starter phase with the mobility required to maximize their metabolic potential.

Post-hatch growth performance of broiler chicks obtained from in ovo injection revealed improved final live weight (FLW) of chicks from eggs injected with 0.02ml ESOPE pre-hatch (starter phase). The improvement in FLW can be attributed to the bioactive compounds present in ESOPE, particularly limonene and linalool. D-limonene is considered the most abundant compound in citrus peels and the most active (Ferrer et al., 2022). Limonene is known to have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which may confer neuroprotective effects (Cutillas et al., 2018; Eddin et al., 2021). Limonene is considered a potent antioxidant that fights free-radical damage and inflammation, which can lead to various diseases (Hirota et al., 2010). This result is consistent with the findings of Sulaiman and Tayeb (2020), who reported that in ovo injection of rosemary oil and black seed oil enhanced body weight in Ross broiler strains on days 7 and 21. The higher final live weight (FLW) in the ESOPE group highlights the importance of embryonic "priming."

Improved FCR in oil-treated groups indicates that birds were able to convert nutrients into muscle more effectively than the UIE group. This is often attributed to the limonene in ESOPE and gingerols in EGE, which stimulate the secretion of endogenous digestive enzymes (amylase and protease) early in the chick's life (Akosile et al., 2023a; Peşmen, 2025). The fact that both oil groups and the DW group recorded higher WG than the UIE group is interesting. It suggests that the act of injection itself might trigger a mild biological response, but only the plant extract groups possessed the bioactive "tools" to optimize that growth into better FCR. This result is consistent with the findings of Sahu et al. (2019), who reported that supplementing broiler diets with essential oils improved body weights. Their study demonstrated that the inclusion of these essential oils in ovo positively influenced broiler growth performance. The primary reason the DW group outperformed the UIE group at the starter phase is likely due to improved embryonic hydration as described by Tako et al. (2004).

The enhanced growth performance observed in the DW and extract-treated groups (ESOPE, EGE, and BLD) relative to UIE group in this study suggests that in ovo stimulation provides a long-term developmental advantage that persists into the finisher stage. This early structural development ensures that the bird enters the finisher phase with a more robust digestive architecture, allowing for more efficient nutrient absorption during the period of maximum growth. The superior performance of the ESOPE and other extract groups indicates a synergistic effect between early hydration and the bioactive properties of phytochemicals. Essential oils or plant extracts act as potent stimulants for endogenous digestive secretions, significantly increasing the activity of enzymes such as amylase, trypsin, and chymotrypsin (Jang et al., 2007). Furthermore, this physiological boost is complemented by a favorable modulation of the intestinal micro flora, which suppresses pathogenic loads while promoting beneficial microbial populations (Mountzouris et al., 2011). Such improvements in the gut environment and nutrient digestibility collectively enhance feed efficiency and weight gain, even under varying environmental pressures (Bozkurt et al., 2012). The persistence of higher FLW in the ESOPE group into the finisher phase shows that the benefits of in ovo feeding are not just temporary "boosts" but permanent physiological advantages. In the finisher phase, EGE-injected birds exhibited the highest feed intake, likely due to the orexigenic compounds in ginger, which increase gastric motility (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). However, the ESOPE group achieved the most efficient FCR. Interestingly, this was not driven by the longest villi, as the BLD group led in morphometry, but likely by a combination of reduced gut cell turnover (indicated by moderate crypt depths) and enhanced enzymatic activity (Jang et al., 2007). This suggests that in ovo ESOPE delivery prioritizes functional nutrient utilization and metabolic efficiency over purely structural intestinal expansion.

The improved Crude Fiber (CF) and Ash digestibility at the starter phase in the extract treated groups indicates that phytogenic compounds actively shaped the early development of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT). Interestingly, the combination of the two extracts reduced CF digestibility compared with other groups. This could suggest a competitive interaction between the bioactive compounds (e.g., limonene from ESOPE and gingerols from EGE) that may slightly alter the gut microbial population or transit time in a way that was less favorable for fiber breakdown during the sensitive starter phase. The high ash digestibility in the ESOPE and EGE groups indicates that these extracts enhanced the absorption of essential minerals. This correlates with data on "best leg scores" in this study, as improved mineral metabolism (Calcium/Phosphorus) is vital for skeletal strength (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022). Similarly, research conducted by Karangiya et al. (2016) demonstrated that in ovo addition of garlic extract positively affected the digestibility of ash and crude fiber in broiler chickens. These findings indicated that natural compounds in the extracts injected into the eggs can significantly enhance hatchlings' digestive efficiency.

By the finisher phase, the "DW" group showed the lowest digestibility across almost all variables (Dry Matter, CP, CF, and Ash). This confirms that without the bioactive support of ESOPE or EGE, the physical stress of in ovo injection may have a lasting negative impact on the gut’s absorptive capacity. The ESOPE group leading in ash digestibility even at the finisher phase suggests a permanent physiological adaptation. Plants extracts and essential oils have been shown to act as selective antimicrobials. They reduce the load of pathogenic bacteria (which compete for nutrients) and favor lactic-acid-producing bacteria. These beneficial bacteria locally produce organic acids that can improve mineral solubility at the intestinal wall (El-Saadony et al., 2023).

The elevation of PCV and Hb in the UIE and ESOPE groups at the starter phase, followed by a sustained higher PCV in all the extract-treated groups at the finisher phase suggests that these oils might have promoted erythropoiesis or protected erythrocytes against oxidative damage in the chickens. Higher PCV and RBC counts generally indicate a robust erythropoietic system, ensuring efficient oxygen delivery to tissues (Oladokun et al., 2023). This supports the high metabolic rate required for the rapid growth and higher final live weight (FLW) observed during both growth phases. The lower RBC count in the distilled water group at the finisher phase suggests that the stress of the injection, without the antioxidant support of the extracts, may lead to slight erythrocyte fragility or suppressed red cell production over time. Majekodunmi et al. (2022) reported that the inclusion of sweet orange peel powder in broiler drinking water improved hematological indices, including PCV and Hb, particularly under heat stress conditions. These antioxidative constituents in sweet orange peel and ginger oil may contribute to the maintenance and production of healthy RBCs as recorded in the oil-treated groups (Shehata et al., 2021b). The elevated WBC counts in the ESOPE and BLD groups at the finisher phase suggest an enhanced state of immune alertness because the elevation of Eosinophils in EGE and BLD-treated birds and Basophils in the BLD group indicates a specific modulation of the inflammatory response. Phytogenics are known to act as "immune-modulators," ensuring the bird can mount a swift response to environmental pathogens without the energy-wasting effects of chronic inflammation (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022).

Increased total protein concentrations observed in starter broilers obtained from eggs injected with ESOPE and EGE groups suggest that in ovo administration of ESOPE or EGE individually stimulates protein metabolism or enhances immune function in their hatchlings. The observed increase in total protein might be linked to the antioxidant constituents of sweet orange peel, such as hesperidin and vitamin C, which are known to promote protein synthesis and bolster immune responses (Miles and Calder, 2021; Kowalczyk, 2024). In a related study, Djeuta et al. (2021) investigated the effects of in ovo injection of Manihot esculenta extract on Sasso broiler chickens, reporting no significant differences in serum total protein and triglyceride levels across treatment groups. They suggested that these nutrients might have been utilized for energy synthesis during the birds' growth phase. Similarly, Oladokun et al. (2023) found no significant changes in serum total protein levels in their evaluation of the thermoregulatory effects of in ovo-delivered bioactive substances (probiotics, folic acid, and essential oils) in broilers. The reduced ALP in the ESOPE group may be due to the potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of ESOPE that shielded the liver from oxidative injury. However, all other serum enzyme values remained within the reference range established by Meluzzi et al. (1992).

Early in life, chicks rely heavily on the lipid-rich yolk sac. The elevation of these lipids in the BLD group suggests that the synergistic effect of ESOPE and EGE may accelerate the mobilization of yolk lipids into the bloodstream to fuel rapid early growth. Notably, BLD group also had the highest HDL. Higher HDL at this stage is a positive indicator of efficient reverse cholesterol transport, ensuring that even as lipid mobilization increases, it is being managed effectively by the liver (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022). Previous reports have shown that supplementation with ginger and orange peel, individually, can elevate HDL concentrations in broilers (Abdulameer, 2019; Njus et al., 2020; Al-Khalaifah et al., 2022).

During the finisher phase, the oil-treated groups maintained lower cholesterol and LDL levels. Compounds like limonene (in ESOPE) and gingerols (in EGE) can inhibit the activity of HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis in the liver (Shewita and Taha, 2018; Gore et al., 2024). These findings are consistent with those of Georganas et al. (2023), who reported that the dietary inclusion of dried sweet orange peels significantly reduced plasma cholesterol, LDL, and triglyceride levels in broilers without compromising growth performance. The lipid-lowering effect was attributed to bioactive components such as flavonoids and pectin, known to modulate hepatic cholesterol metabolism. The higher HDL levels in the DW, ESOPE and BLD groups compared to the control and ginger groups suggest a long-term improvement in the birds' lipid profile. This "heart-healthy" shift in broilers is often associated with better meat quality, as lower serum cholesterol can sometimes correlate with lower abdominal fat deposition (Oladokun et al., 2023). The increase in HDL concentrations in birds receiving ESOPE, BLD, and distilled water treatments supports the findings of Carvalho et al. (2022), who reported that citrus-based supplementation consistently lowered total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides, while increasing HDL levels. These effects are largely attributed to the antioxidant potential of citrus-derived flavonoids, which influence key targets in lipid metabolism. While the distilled DW group has high HDL, it also showed the highest LDL and Total Cholesterol levels. This profile suggests that the increase in HDL might be a compensatory mechanism to manage the concurrently high LDL levels. Unlike the oil-treated groups, where citrus-derived bioactives, specifically flavonoids and limonoids- exert a "braking" effect by actively inhibiting hepatic cholesterol synthesis (e.g., via the down-regulation of HMG-CoA reductase), the DW group lacks such biochemical regulation. Consequently, the elevated HDL in these birds likely reflects the chickens’ attempt to maintain lipid homeostasis through enhanced reverse cholesterol transport, in contrast to the more efficient, bio-regulated lipid profiles observed in the ESOPE and EGE treatment groups (Carvalho et al., 2022; Gore et al., 2024).

Broiler chickens from BLD group displayed greater villus height and mucosal thickness in the duodenum and jejunum, indicating a potential synergistic effect of the combined phytogenic additives on intestinal architecture. This observation is consistent with findings by Oso et al. (2019), who reported that dietary inclusion of a phytogenic blend significantly enhanced duodenal villus height in broilers compared with birds fed a standard basal diet, thereby suggesting improved intestinal morphology and absorptive capacity. Similarly, Fafiolu et al. (2017) documented increased villus height in broilers supplemented with citrus peel extracts, supporting the role of plant-derived compounds in promoting gut health. Akosile et al. (2023a, b) demonstrated that in ovo administration of clove and cinnamon positively influenced intestinal organ development in broilers reared under hot, humid conditions. Heidary et al. (2020) similarly found that in ovo delivery of nano-curcumin significantly improved jejunal villus height by day 10, enhancing gut maturation and nutrient uptake potential. Additionally, Apalowo et al. (2024) observed that ginger root extract positively influenced mucosal structure, supporting current findings regarding the role of ginger oil in intestinal enhancement. The elevated crypt depth noted in the distilled water group may reflect enhanced cellular turnover, potentially as a compensatory mechanism for suboptimal absorptive function.

The lowest crypt depth in the UIE group may reflect reduced epithelial cell proliferation, potentially impairing the regenerative capacity of the intestinal lining and compromising nutrient absorption. This aligns with the findings of Prakatur et al. (2019), who emphasized that deeper crypts indicate elevated mitotic activity, which is essential for maintaining a healthy absorptive epithelium. Geyra et al. (2001) also reported that early access to nutrients post-hatch increased crypt depth and epithelial proliferation in chicks, thereby promoting efficient nutrient absorption and gut development. These findings are in agreement with Shehata et al. (2021a), who found that in ovo supplementation with phytogenic compounds accelerated gastrointestinal tract maturation in broiler chickens.

The composition of the cecal microflora plays a critical role in maintaining health, immune function, and optimal growth performance in broiler chickens. Recently, in ovo administration of bioactive compounds, such as essential oils and plant extracts has gathered attention as a proactive means of modulating gut microbial populations during embryonic development, potentially decreasing vulnerability to enteric infections post-hatch (Shehata et al., 2021b; Mangan et al., 2024). In the present study, injecting ESOPE, EGE, or their combination in ovo led to marked alterations in the cecal microbial landscape of broiler chickens. A notable decline in total bacterial count was observed in the group receiving the combined treatment, suggesting a synergistic antimicrobial effect. This observation aligns with findings by Niknafs et al. (2024), who reported that in ovo delivery of oregano essential oil modulated gut microbiota and stimulated xenobiotic detoxification pathways in chicks.

Among specific pathogens, Escherichia coli populations were high in the ESOPE group, while the EGE-treated group exhibited complete elimination of this bacterium. This reinforces the antibacterial potential of ginger extract, which is attributed to bioactive compounds such as gingerol and shogaol (El-Saadony et al., 2023). Wang et al. (2020) demonstrated that ginger essential oil disrupts bacterial cell membranes, increases membrane permeability, and compromises cellular function, ultimately leading to bacterial death. Beristain et al. (2019) further noted that the hydrophobic components of ginger oil may integrate into the lipid bilayer and mitochondria, disrupting energy metabolism and protein synthesis.

Conversely, elevated levels of Staphylococcus aureus were recorded in the EGE group, whereas the BLD group showed complete suppression of this pathogen. This enhanced efficacy of the combined treatment is consistent with Angane et al. (2024), who investigated plant-derived essential oils and extracts and found that although individual components such as peppermint, thyme, and lavender exhibited antimicrobial activity at high concentrations, their combinations exerted broader and more potent effects against foodborne pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella enterica, and Bacillus cereus. In this study, Salmonella enterica was identified only in the EGE group but was completely absent in the UIE, ESOPE, and BLD groups. This could be attributed to the antimicrobial activity of d-limonene, a dominant constituent in citrus oils, as supported by multiple studies (O'Bryan et al., 2008; Bora et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). For instance, O'Bryan et al. (2008) demonstrated that d-limonene-rich orange oil inhibits various Salmonella strains by disrupting bacterial membranes, while Shin et al. (2021) confirmed strong antibacterial activity of orange peel essential oils predominantly composed of limonene, against Salmonella spp. Additionally, Li et al. (2022) suggested that the antimicrobial activity of citrus oils likely results from a synergy of multiple bioactive constituents, including cis-citral, α-pinene, and trans-citral, rather than limonene alone. Klebsiella pneumoniae was absent in the EGE group but present in all other treatments. This supports the view that EGE may exert bactericidal effects against K. pneumoniae, possibly through the actions of gingerol and zingiberene, which have been reported to impair membrane integrity and inhibit bacterial efflux mechanisms (Wang et al., 2020).

The complete inhibition Pseudomonas aeruginosa observed in the UIE, ESOPE, and BLD groups can be attributed to the antimicrobial activity of d-limonene in citrus peel oil, which is known to disrupt microbial cell membranes and promote oxidative stress in pathogens (Fisher and Phillips, 2008; Shin et al., 2021). Proteus vulgaris was exclusively identified in the ESOPE group, suggesting a potential limitation of ESOPE's antimicrobial spectrum or variability in bacterial strain susceptibility. The absence of this organism in the EGE and BLD groups suggests that EGE, either alone or in combination, may confer additional inhibitory effects. The present study demonstrates that the in ovo combination of ESOPE and EGE enhances antimicrobial defense in the cecum of broiler chickens, likely through broad-spectrum activity mediated by involving multiple bioactive compounds with complementary mechanisms of action. Li et al. (2022) also reported that pronounced antimicrobial and antioxidant properties result from the combined action of several bioactive components, highlighting the importance of synergy among essential oil constituents.

In conclusion, this study shows that in ovo administration of ESOPE), EGE and their blend (BLD) improved hatchability, growth performance, nutrient utilization, gut morphology, cecal microflora, and selected physiological indices in broiler chickens. Among the treatments, ESOPE consistently produced the greatest benefits, including higher hatchability, better chick quality, and superior feed and nutrient efficiency, likely through its antioxidant and antimicrobial effects.

Based on the dose evaluated, 0.02 mL ESOPE per egg appeared to provide the most favorable overall response; however, this recommendation should be interpreted cautiously because intermediate and higher inclusion levels were not systematically tested. While these findings highlight the potential of ethanolic extracts of sweet orange peel and ginger as natural phytogenic interventions, additional studies involving different dosage levels, injection timing, hatchability optimization, and large-scale commercial validation are required before practical field application can be recommended.
