Longitudinal Changes in Coronary Artery Calcium Distribution and Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk
Authors: Shyon Parsa, Colby R Ayers, Ayeeshik Kole, Amit Khera, Parag H Joshi, Fernando U Kay, Anand Rohatgi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Agatston score quantifies coronary artery calcium (CAC) burden but does not account for its spatial distribution. The CAC diffusivity index (DI) may refine cardiovascular risk stratification in individuals with Agatston CAC>0. This study assessed whether CAC distribution improves cardiovascular event prediction beyond the Agatston score in a young cohort.MethodsAmong DHS2 (Dallas Heart Study 2) participants with CAC>0 and no prior cardiovascular disease (35% of the cohort), we studied 1013 participants with CAC DI. CAC DI was calculated as 1−(CAC in most affected vessel/total CAC) and categorized as concentrated (<25th percentile), standard (25th–75th percentile), and diffuse (>75th percentile). Cox proportional hazards models and C‐statistics assessed coronary heart disease (CHD), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and mortality, adjusting for Agatston CAC, demographics, and traditional risk factors.ResultsMean±SD age was 55±9.4 years (49% women, and 47% Black), with a median follow‐up of 9±2.5 years (57 CHD and 92 atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease events). Compared with a concentrated CAC pattern, standard and diffuse patterns were associated with incident CHD (hazard ratio, 5.97 [95% CI, 1.24–28.78] and 5.48 [95% CI, 1.04–29.04], respectively; P<0.05) but not atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in fully adjusted models. Positive and negative changes in CAC DI were associated with increased CHD and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk in models adjusted for pooled cohort equation variables, although effects were attenuated in fully adjusted models. Adding continuous CAC DI did not significantly improve model performance.ConclusionsHigher CAC DI at baseline significantly contributes to adjusted models for incident CHD in a young population‐based cohort, but temporal changes provided little additional benefit.Keywords: atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, coronary artery calcium, Dallas Heart Study, diffusivity indexSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Imaging, Computerized Tomography (CT), Diagnostic Testing

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) burden has been established as a surrogate for total coronary plaque. 1 , 2 The Agatston score provides a quantitative characterization of CAC using cardiac‐gated computed tomography (CT) and has been correlated with the risk of incident myocardial infarction and overall atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The Agatston score, calculated as the summation across the coronary tree of the product of the total calcified lesion area by maximum lesion density in Hounsfield units, has been the long‐established metric for characterization of CAC. 8 More recent work has suggested that the Agatston score may be an oversimplification of CAC characterization. 9 For example, increased CAC density, a known effect of statin therapy, has been shown to “stabilize” the lesion and therefore decrease the likelihood of coronary heart disease (CHD) events. 10 , 11 In addition, although the Agatston score assumes uniform effect independent of lesion location and distribution, an increased number of coronary arteries with calcifications (versus single‐vessel disease) may inform increased incremental risk. 12

Given such potential improvements on the Agatston score, there remains a gap in understanding whether additional metrics of CAC characterization can further improve risk prediction. Specifically, parameters such as the number of vessels with calcifications, vessel‐specific distribution of CAC, and the number of distinct calcified lesions within the coronary tree have been proposed as potential scoring methods capable of providing additional risk stratification data. 9 , 13 , 14 Among these parameters, the diffusivity index (DI) is capable of capturing the extent and distribution of calcifications and has shown promise in quantifying the spread of CAC throughout the coronary vasculature. 13 DI offers a unique perspective compared with traditional metrics by addressing plaque dispersion rather than solely focusing on cumulative burden.

In addition, there remains a paucity of data examining the longitudinal changes in CAC distribution patterns and their implications for ASCVD risk. Prior studies have demonstrated limited utility in serial CAC scores given general predictably increase in CAC burden with age. 15 , 16 Previous work has outlined the tendency for incident CAC to be detected first in a single vessel, and subsequently disperse into multiple vessels throughout time. 17 However, less is understood about how the longitudinal change in the spatial distribution of calcifications informs future adverse events independent of overall CAC progression.

This study seeks to evaluate the risk predictive ability of CAC DI and its longitudinal change (ΔDI) for CHD and ASCVD events. Using a multiethnic population cohort study, we analyzed a cohort of patients undergoing serial CAC assessments to determine whether these novel metrics offer incremental predictive value over traditional measures and explored their potential to refine risk stratification and eventually clinical decision‐making.

METHODS

Study Design and Population

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. The detailed description and rationale behind the DHS (Dallas Heart Study) have been described previously. 17 Briefly, the DHS is a multiethnic, probability‐based, population cohort study conducted in community‐dwelling adults living in Dallas County, Texas, with a designed oversampling of African Americans. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, and informed consent was waived. DHS phase 1 was conducted between 2000 and 2002 in which during the span of 3 visits, participants underwent a detailed health survey, blood and urine collections, and a clinical examination along with imaging studies. DHS phase 2 is a longitudinal follow‐up study of phase 1 participants who had repeat survey, laboratory, and imaging studies performed between 2007 and 2009. 18 The study included 2836 candidate participants with a CAC score>0 at DHS phase 2. After excluding 1823 participants with a CAC=0 and other exclusion criteria, including prior ASCVD/cardiovascular disease (Figure 1), the primary analysis cohort was 1013 participants. The longitudinal component of our analysis included 552 participants who had a CAC CT scan in both DHS1 and DHS2 and excluded those with an outcome event occurring between DHS phase 1 and DHS phase 2.

CT Protocol

Electron‐beam CT on an Imatron 150 XP scanner was used for CAC measurements in DHS phase 1. Electron‐beam CT duplicate measurements were conducted 1 to 2 minutes apart. Multidetector CT on a Toshiba Aquilon 64‐slice multidetector CT scanner was used in DHS phase 2. Participants were scanned twice within 1 to 2 minutes. For both modalities and phases, the CAC scores were characterized using the Agatston method, and any discrepancy between duplicate scores was averaged. CAC scores were adjusted using a participant‐specific standard calcium phantom. This adjustment allowed for the comparison of scores obtained between the electron‐beam CT and multidetector CT, and concordance between the 2 modalities has been previously described. 19 , 20

Definition of Study CAC Parameters

For each CAC scan, the number of vessels with CAC>0 was calculated on a quantitative scale from 1 to 4 vessels. For participants who had ≥2‐vessel involvement, a raw, continuous DI was calculated using a previously described equation: DI=1−(per‐vessel Agatston from vessel with most CAC/total Agatston score). 13 DI ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates all CAC is confined to a single vessel, whereas values approaching 1 represent a more homogeneous and diffuse distribution of CAC throughout the coronary tree. Each participant's DI was then stratified into percentiles and assigned into the DI categories “diffuse” (≥75th percentile), “concentrated” (≤25th percentile), or “standard” (25th–75th percentile), with each corresponding to thresholds established by existing literature. 13

ΔCAC DI (ΔDI) is a continuous variable derived through the equation: ΔDI=DHS2 DI−DHS1 DI. Participants with both DHS1 and DHS2 DI values were categorized into negative (ΔDI<0), 0 (ΔDI=0), or positive (ΔDI>0) groupings for additional model analysis. Agatston categories were stratified into low (1–100), intermediate (101–300), and high (>300) groupings based on preestablished CAC thresholds. 21 , 22

Study Covariates

Variable definitions, including traditional clinical risk factors, demographics, and medical history, have been defined previously. 23 Race and ethnicity, gender, and smoking history were self‐reported. Body surface area, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol were assessed using previously described standardized methods. 17 , 24 Statin and antihypertensive medication use were described using established definitions for each medication class. Type 2 diabetes was defined as fasting glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin. 25

Event Ascertainment and Definitions

Event adjudication for the DHS data set has been described in detail previously (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7440773/). 26 CHD was defined as a compositive of myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention, or coronary artery bypass grafting. Cardiovascular disease was defined as a compositive of CHD, stroke, transient ischemic attack, hospitalization for congestive heart failure, and hospitalization for atrial fibrillation. All‐cause mortality, as described previously, relied on a combination of an annual formal survey to participants in addition to the national death index and Dallas Fort Worth hospital council data initiative database. All cardiovascular events were adjudicated by 2 physician members of the clinical end points committee. Events were analyzed following the DHS2 visit for both the single DI analysis and ΔDI analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented stratified according to the categorical DI. Continuous data are noted as mean (SD), and categorical data are given as total number positive (percentage). Intergroup differences are calculated using the appropriate statistical test; χ2 test, 1‐way ANOVA, or other nonparametric testing was implemented.

Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Given the nonnormality of the data, the Spearman test was used to analyze correlations between CAC DI and Agatston CAC. The analysis was similarly conducted for ΔDI and Agatston CAC.

Time‐to‐first CHD, cardiovascular disease, or all‐cause mortality event was modeled using Cox proportional hazards regression. Absolute event rates of the outcomes were presented per 1000 person years. Event rates were displayed graphically in a Kaplan–Meier survival plot across concentrated versus standard versus diffuse CAC DI for those with a baseline Agatston >0.

For each outcome, model 1 contained a logarithmic CAC DI or ΔDI variable. Model 2 adjusted for the pooled cohort equation (PCE) covariates (age, sex, smoking, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and antihypertensive medication use). Model 3 sequentially adjusted for the Agatston score. Finally, model 4 was fully adjusted for PCE covariates, Agatston CAC, and the number of vessels with coronary calcification. The models were then repeated for a categorical assessment of CAC DI and ΔDI. Risk prediction was assessed through calculation of the C‐statistic and net reclassification index.

All statistical analysis was conducted using STATA 13 (StataCorp statistical software; Stata Corp, College Station, TX). A 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Among the 2836 candidate participants in the DHS, 1013 (age, 55 years [SD, 9 years], 49% women, and 47% African American) met the inclusion criteria. The percentage of participants with a high Agatston category was 18.1%, intermediate Agatston was 16.3%, and low Agatston was 65.5% (Table 1). The percentage of participants categorized as having a diffuse DI was 25.1%, standard DI was 28.7%, and concentrated DI was 46.2% (Table 2). Participants with more diffuse distribution of CAC were more likely to be older and men. There were differences among diffusivity categories in those with a history of hypercholesteremia (P<0.001), statin use (P<0.001), diabetes (P<0.001), and smoking (P<0.001).

Table 3 outlines the results of the Cox proportional hazards models for both CHD and ASCVD with the addition of categorical CAC DI to models adjusted for various covariates. When adjusted for the numerical Agatston CAC score (log [CAC+1]) alone (model 1), standard CAC DI is associated with a hazard ratio (HR) of 8.24 (95% CI, 1.99–38.8; P<0.05) for CHD events and 2.47 (95% CI, 1.21–5.03; P<0.05) for ASCVD events relative to concentrated CAC DI (Table 3). Table 4 shows the results of the Δ diffusivity analysis. When adding the ΔDI to the PCE (model 1), positive ΔDI was associated with a 15 to 16‐fold increase in CHD risk when compared with stable DI (P<0.05). A negative ΔDI was associated with a 10 to 11‐fold increase in CHD risk when compared with stable DI (P<0.05). A similar positive risk was evident for ASCVD events. With regard to risk prediction (Tables 5 and 6), the addition of CAC DI and number of vessels to the PCE and total traditional Agatston CAC was assessed for improvement in discrimination. Adding CAC DI did not improve the C‐statistic beyond PCE and CAC score. The number of vessels also did not improve the C‐statistic beyond PCE and CAC score.

DISCUSSION

Our study was conducted within a large, diverse epidemiologic cohort with serial CAC testing. We aimed to expand on the role of both the CAC distribution and change in CAC distribution over time in CHD and ASCVD risk prediction. We have shown that a higher CAC DI as a measure of CAC distribution is associated with increased risk of both CHD and ASCVD beyond PCE and with CHD beyond PCE, CAC score, and number of vessels with CAC. However, given the high discrimination with PCE and CAC score alone, DI did not improve discrimination. Finally, we reported trends in CAC diffusivity over time and provide the theoretical basis for future work examining its role in clinical risk prediction.

The basis for the utility of CAC diffusivity as a risk predictor stems from limitations present in the Agatston score. Although a well‐established metric for CAC quantification, the Agatston score fails to account for factors such as disease focality, distribution, and plaque characteristics. 9 , 21 , 27 Indeed, such metrics have been shown to improve on cardiovascular event beyond the Agatston score itself. Studies have found that left‐main coronary artery calcification, as an example, confers a higher incremental risk compared with other coronary vasculature. 28 , 29 , 30 Increased CAC density, often associated with statin therapy use in patients, is associated with stabilization of plaque and reduced CHD events despite the resulting increase in Agatston score. 27 , 31 Such work highlights the necessity for continued refinements of CAC reporting as early cardiovascular risk prediction becomes increasingly important. 32

The importance of identifying those at risk of CHD and ASCVD events from an early age has been amplified in recent work. 33 , 34 Often used risk calculators, such as the PCE, are not validated for use in those aged <40 years, and therefore there remains a need for a screening modality capable of stratifying the population. 35 Early CAC scanning may provide prognostic information in this cohort, as the presence of CAC at a young age is capable of informing development of eventual large CAC burden and CHD events with notable accuracy. 36 , 37 Early intervention has clear long‐term effects in mitigating risk, as the treatment of dyslipidemia over time has proven effective in lowering ASCVD event rate. 38 Therefore, there is interest in assessing the potential for CAC distribution to provide increased prognostic information in young individuals.

Previous work examining the role of CAC DI in cardiovascular disease risk prediction has had promising results. 13 , 14 , 29 , 39 , 40 Initial work found both area under the curve and net reclassification index improvement when adding the number of vessels to CAC for major adverse cardiovascular events; however, work failed to find additional improvement on the addition of the CAC DI score. 13 The CAC‐DRS (Coronary Artery Calcium Data and Reporting System) validation study found a similar outperformance of vessel number when compared with CAC DI. 39 Our study found that neither categorical CAC DI nor the number of vessels provided incremental improvement in model area under the curve for CHD outcomes. This finding is likely because of a combination of CAC DI characterization, the study cohort examined, and event rates. Previous work used per‐vessel percentiles to stratify CAC DI into concentrated, standard, and diffuse categories. Our work instead simplified the categorization by creating the percentiles on all CAC DI scores for those ≥2 vessels. Our simplified metric encompasses both risk predictors, and although it failed to meet statistical significance, the numerical model performance was higher than the number of vessels alone. In addition, our study cohort is notably younger and more diverse than those analyzed previously, such as the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) data set in which the initial diffusivity score was derived. The prognostic information provided by coronary calcium varies by age and, therefore, measures of calcium diffusivity may differ as well. 41 , 42 Finally, although the numerical increases in area under the curve present in our analysis suggest that our sample size and event rates may be underpowered to determine if the increases present are statistically significant, further analysis to elucidate a potential relationship is warranted. The Agatston score has been validated and implemented spanning multiple decades, and the findings of this study were unable to show a significant improvement over the total CAC scoring. However, there is emerging evidence that the use of advanced imaging methods, including radiomics and artificial intelligence, could find potentially hidden features beyond the traditional Agatston method. 43 Future research should investigate their potential for improved cardiovascular risk prediction.

Multiple studies have examined the utility of repeat CAC testing and the propensity of a repeat score to improve ASCVD risk prediction. 15 , 44 , 45 Although previous work found improved risk prediction and potential utility in a repeat scan, improvements tended to be modest, especially in those with an Agatston score>100. 46 Our study is the first to examine the change in CAC DI over time and the potential for this characterization of CAC dispersion to provide the theoretical support for a repeat CAC scan. In the present study, we found that changes in CAC diffusivity over time were associated with higher event rates compared with stable CAC distribution in models adjusted for traditional risk factors alone. Notably, this association was not direction specific as both increases and decreases in diffusivity were associated with higher risk than stability, suggesting that ΔDI may reflect underlying instability or heterogeneity in plaque evolution rather than simply progression toward more diffuse disease. However, these associations were attenuated and no longer significant after accounting for total CAC burden and number of vessels, indicating that ΔDI does not add independent prognostic information beyond established CAC metrics in this cohort.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, the relatively younger population in the DHS cohort may reduce statistical power to detect associations between imaging features and clinical outcomes, particularly over shorter follow‐up periods. Second, the use of different CAC scanning technologies in DHS1 and DHS2 (electron‐beam CT versus multidetector CT) may introduce variability in plaque detection and quantification. Third, although the DI provides a more refined analysis of coronary calcium, it still relies on segmentation derived from the traditional Agatston method, which uses a 130–Hounsfield unit threshold. As a result, it may miss early or subtle calcific changes that fall below this cutoff. Future work incorporating artificial intelligence and radiomics may allow for detection of microcalcification and other imaging biomarkers that are not captured by conventional scoring approaches, potentially enhancing the granularity of risk assessment.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study underscores the potential of CAC DI and ΔCAC DI as complementary metrics to the Agatston score for cardiovascular risk assessment. Although CAC DI was associated with increased CHD and ASCVD risk beyond traditional metrics, it did not significantly enhance discrimination in fully adjusted models. These findings underscore the strength and durability of the Agatston score as a risk prediction tool and illustrate the difficulty in substantially improving on its prognostic performance using additional measures of calcium distribution. The prognostic value of changes in CAC DI highlights the importance of interpreting calcification patterns, although larger studies with a longitudinal component are needed to both validate and build on these findings. Targeting CAC distribution, rather than solely CAC burden, may provide a novel strategy for improving cardiovascular risk stratification and guiding preventive interventions.
