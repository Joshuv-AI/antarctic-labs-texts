Prevalence and risk factors for antenatal anxiety in the first trimester among Chinese pregnant women
Authors: Shuyin Xu, Yilin Peng, Mengqi Xu, Junzhe Cheng, Jintao Xiong, Xuemei Qin, Zhuo Peng, Weiguo Mao, Meiqun Xiao, Mi Wang, Li Zhang, Yumeng Ju, Jin Liu, Yan Yu, Bangshan Liu, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTBackgroundAntenatal anxiety (AA) is a common mental disorder during pregnancy and adversely affects the well-being of both pregnant women and their offspring. The prevalence of AA is exceptionally high in the first trimester, yet there is a lack of studies focusing exclusively on AA in the first trimester.AimsThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence and risk factors of AA among Chinese pregnant women during the first trimester.MethodsWe retrospectively retrieved and analysed data on the mental health screening of perinatal women at Shenzhen Baoan Women’s and Children’s Hospital in China from 1 January 2020 to 31 January 2024. A total of 42 013 pregnant women with less than 14 weeks of gestation were assessed using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). A GAD-7 score ≥10 indicates AA. Univariable analyses and multivariable logistic regression were employed to identify risk factors for AA.ResultsAmong the participants, 1066 (2.54%) experienced AA in the first trimester. Factors associated with a higher risk of AA included being under 25 years old, temporary residence, below senior high school education, low or moderate economic status, primipara, unplanned pregnancy, smoking, alcohol use, lack of exercise, low or moderate living conditions, low or moderate marital satisfaction and reluctance to discuss troubles with others.ConclusionsAA manifests as a multifaceted phenomenon influenced by various sociodemographic, obstetrical, lifestyle and psychosocial factors. Preventing AA requires collaboration among hospitals, communities and families.Keywords: Anxiety

Introduction

Pregnancy represents a vulnerable period when women are prone to emotional distress due to physical, hormonal, psychological and social changes.1 Antenatal anxiety (AA) is a common psychological state characterised by excessive and persistent worry, fear and restlessness that pregnant women experience during pregnancy; this affects pregnant women more than the general population.2 The estimated prevalence of AA varies by country, sample characteristics, measurement tools and timing. Dennis et al3 reported a pooled prevalence of clinically diagnosed anxiety disorder of 15.20% antenatally, while Fawcett et al4 reported a pooled prevalence of 20.70% across the perinatal period. AA disproportionally affects women in low- and middle-income countries, with a recent meta-analysis reporting that the pooled prevalence of self-reported anxiety symptoms was 29.20% antenatally.5 Epidemiological data indicate that anxiety is prevalent in pregnancy and associated with an elevated risk of hypertensive disorders,6 underscoring its significance as a mental health concern during pregnancy.

AA has adverse short-term and long-term effects on mothers, infants and society.7 Pregnant women experiencing anxiety may display physical symptoms (eg, heart palpitations and shortness of breath) and psychological symptoms (eg, nervousness and uncontrollable worrying).8,10 AA negatively affects gestation and child health outcomes, as evidenced by its correlation with increased risks of fetal distress, preterm birth, low birth weight, and compromised emotional, neurological and cognitive development in children.11,16 In addition, AA causes significant economic costs to society due to the direct medical expenses for treatment and the indirect costs of lost productivity and caregiving burden.17 Thus, it is crucial to identify the risk factors of AA to help target vulnerable populations and guide effective prevention and intervention programmes aimed at reducing its occurrence.

Previous studies have identified various risk factors associated with AA, including age, rural residence, unemployment, adverse pregnancy reactions, unplanned pregnancy, low economic status, partner violence, low marital satisfaction and lack of social support.18,23 However, most previous studies did not distinguish various stages of pregnancy, and limited research exists on risk factors for AA in the first trimester. The first trimester of pregnancy is a vulnerable period for pregnant women to develop anxiety and depression symptoms.24 25 The prevalence of AA in the first trimester is relatively high throughout the gestational period.24 26 AA in the first trimester predicts anxiety throughout pregnancy26 and predicts preterm birth and postpartum depression.27 28 The significant impact of AA in the first trimester on the physical and psychological well-being of pregnant women and their offspring emphasises the importance of early screening and intervention in the first trimester.

Therefore, we conducted the current study to examine the prevalence and risk factors of AA in the first trimester based on a large-scale screening of perinatal women in a hospital in Shenzhen, China. Specifically, we collected sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric factors, lifestyles and family support of pregnant women to explore their potential effects on AA in the first trimester. Our findings would provide comprehensive insight into AA and facilitate the early identification of high-risk populations, thus enabling customised prevention and interventions to enhance health outcomes for mothers and children.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data on the mental health screening of perinatal women at Shenzhen Baoan Women’s and Children’s Hospital in China from 1 January 2020 to 31 January 2024 were retrospectively retrieved and analysed. Pregnant women underwent psychological screening and assessment during each prenatal check-up.

In the present study, pregnant women under 14 weeks of gestation were included if their medical records were established within the hospital system. Exclusion criteria were severe physical disorders (eg, diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, cancer, tuberculosis, hepatitis, thyroid disease and other major illnesses) and a history of any mental disorders. All participants scanned a quick response code to complete an online questionnaire, which included an informed consent form and clinical assessments. All participants agreed to the use of their deidentified data for scientific research. Among 46 326 individuals in early pregnancy, 4313 were excluded due to age under 18 (n=32), physical disease (n=3794) and mental disorders (n=487), leading to 42 013 individuals included in the final analysis (effective rate: 90.69%) (figure 1).

Measurements

Risk factor assessment

Participants completed a self-designed questionnaire that covered basic sociodemographic information, obstetric factors, lifestyle and family support. Sociodemographic data included age, residence, occupation, educational level, economic status and history of physical and mental disorders. Obstetric factors investigated included gravidity, parity, planned pregnancy status, methods of conception and completion of pre-pregnancy screening. The lifestyle factors assessed encompassed smoking and alcohol use of pregnant women and their partners, as well as the frequency of exercise among pregnant women. Family support factors comprised coresidence status, living conditions, marital satisfaction and the willingness to share one’s troubles.

Anxiety assessment

The Chinese version of the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) was employed to assess participants’ anxiety symptoms.29 Each item is rated on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe anxiety symptoms. A GAD-7 score of ≥5 indicates mild or higher levels of anxiety, while a score of ≥10 indicates moderate to severe anxiety. AA was defined as a total GAD-7 score of ≥10 in the present study.30 In this study, GAD-7 was administered once during early pregnancy (<14 weeks) as part of routine mental health screening. The scale exhibited good reliability in the current study with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS V.25.0. Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to present the sample characteristics and prevalence of AA in the first trimester. The associations between categorical variables and AA were examined using the Pearson χ2 test and simple logistic regression. To assess the adjusted effect of sample characteristics on AA, multivariable logistic regression was employed to identify independent risk factors of AA. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 (two- tailed).

Results

Univariable analysis of the association between sample characteristics and AA

Among the 42 013 participants, 2.54% (1066) individuals experienced AA (GAD-7 score ≥10) in the first trimester. The mean (standard deviation) gestational age of pregnant women participating in the study was 8.64 (1.73) weeks. For lifestyle factors, both participants and their partners’ smoking and alcohol use were associated with a higher risk of AA. Individuals who never engaged in exercise exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of AA compared with those who exercised daily. No significant difference was observed in the prevalence of AA between participants who exercised at varying frequencies and those who exercised daily (table 2).

Regarding family support, pregnant women who lived alone, had low or moderate living conditions, experienced low or moderate marital satisfaction and seldom discussed their troubles—the latter shared only when prompted or confided exclusively to close confidants—exhibited a higher prevalence of AA compared with their counterparts (table 2).

Multivariable logistic regression of risk factors for AA

Further multivariate logistic regression analysis identified the following independent risk factors for AA: under 25 years of age, temporary residency, below senior high school education, low or moderate economic status, primipara, unplanned pregnancy, smoking, alcohol use, lack of exercise, low or moderate living conditions, low or moderate marital satisfaction and never discussing troubles. However, pre-pregnancy screening, partner smoking and drinking and living alone were no longer associated with AA after adjusting for covariates (figure 2, online supplemental table S1).

Discussion

Main findings

To our knowledge, this was the first large population study to focus on AA in the first trimester and comprehensively explored its risk factors from multiple dimensions. Our analysis revealed a prevalence of 2.54% of AA among pregnant women in the first trimester. We identified various risk factors associated with AA, encompassing sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric factors, lifestyles and family support. Our findings are crucial for identifying high-risk populations during early pregnancy and can thereby support prompt prevention and intervention for AA.

Our data were collected during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. Several studies conducted during this period have found that the pandemic led to an increase in the occurrence of anxiety among pregnant women, with incidence rates ranging from 3.87% to 27.95%.20,24 However, the prevalence of AA during early pregnancy in our study was lower than the range reported. Apart from the differences in the areas studied, survey methods used and assessment criteria for measuring anxiety symptoms, the reasons for the discrepancy may also include the following factors: first, pregnant women with a history of mental disorders were excluded from the present study as they can be easily identified in clinical settings; second, the varied assessment tools and cut-off points used in the present and previous studies could also lead to different AA prevalence. Moreover, pregnant women in Shenzhen, a highly developed city with a better economy, may have better access to health education and services from hospitals or communities compared with other regions in China, leading to lower AA prevalence. Our findings suggest that high-quality medical services may help reduce the occurrence of AA.

Consistent with previous literature, our study showed that sociodemographic risk factors such as younger age, lower education and lower economic status were associated with a higher risk of AA in early pregnancy.20 22 In addition, we found that temporary residents were also at a higher risk for AA, which was rarely reported in previous studies. A study in Shenzhen surveyed 680 primiparous women among the rural-to-urban migrant population and found that these women had a significantly poorer mental health status than non-migrants.31 In our study, over 60% of the participants were temporary residents, accurately reflecting the demographic structure of Shenzhen, a highly urbanised city with a large migrant population. Significantly, after residence status was statistically adjusted for in the multivariable logistic regression model, temporary residency remained an independent risk factor for AA. Therefore, although the high proportion of temporary residents highlighted a vulnerable subgroup, it does not compromise the internal validity of our findings. Special attention should continue to be directed to this vulnerable population when implementing prenatal interventions. In several studies, unemployment has been identified as a risk factor for AA.20 32 However, in our sample, no significant difference was found between the employed and unemployed groups. This is likely because the unemployment rate in our sample was only 0.46%, indicating that most women remained employed during early pregnancy.

Our study showed that obstetric risk factors, including primiparity and unplanned pregnancy, were associated with a higher risk of AA, which was congruent with previous studies.22 33 In contrast, we did not find that gravidity had an impact on anxiety. For primiparous women, common sources of anxiety include pregnancy complications such as vomiting and pre-eclampsia and worries about the fetus’s health.21 Popularising information about pregnancy and childbirth among primiparous women may effectively alleviate their stress.34 Meanwhile, we found that pre-pregnancy screening can also effectively reduce the anxiety of pregnant women. Furthermore, we discovered that unplanned pregnancy primarily occurred in young individuals (age <25) with low educational levels, consistent with previous research.35 The results call for the community-led promotion of sex education, with a particular focus on young people with low literacy levels.

In addition, our study identified unhealthy lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol use and lack of exercise as risk factors for AA. Perinatal smoking and drinking pose a severe public health issue due to their adverse effects on fetal development and pregnancy outcomes.36 37 Women with higher levels of anxiety and depression were also less likely to quit smoking and drinking during pregnancy.38 39 One study showed that among smokers, women who smoked before pregnancy had higher rates of anxiety symptoms during the first trimester of pregnancy compared with women who did not smoke, regardless of smoking cessation.40 Therefore, we advise pregnant women to avoid smoking due to the higher prevalence of anxiety symptoms in continuous smokers during pregnancy, especially in the first trimester, compared with non-smokers and quitters.41 As for drinking, a study found that continued alcohol consumption during early pregnancy was associated with lower levels of conscientiousness, and AA can partly explain the association.42 Exercise has been well established as having a protective effect against AA. In contrast, lack of exercise is a risk factor for AA.32 43 Notably, our study showed no significant difference in the prevalence of AA between participants who exercised daily and those who exercised less frequently. This implies that the presence or absence of exercise, rather than its frequency, is associated with AA. For women in the first trimester, we recommend light exercises such as walking and yoga.44

Regarding family support factors, our study demonstrated that reluctance to discuss troubles and marital dissatisfaction were risk factors for AA. Both factors reflect the relationship between pregnant women and their partners. In our study, we found that 89.83% of pregnant women actively shared their problems, and the prevalence of AA in this group was significantly lower than in the passive-sharing and non-sharing groups. Pregnant women who do not share their troubles may experience lower social support, leading to increased risks of depression, anxiety and self-harm.19 45 Several studies have identified insufficient support and care from partners as a risk factor for AA.20 46 A previous study showed that benign marital relationship quality was associated with increased partner involvement during pregnancy.47 These findings suggest that targeted interventions should be implemented to enhance partner involvement and promote the mental health of pregnant women from the beginning of pregnancy.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design may preclude any causal inference between AA and risk factors. Future longitudinal study designs are needed to establish causal relationships. Second, the participants were recruited from a single medical centre in a city with high economic development in China, which may limit their representativeness and generalisability. In the future, conducting multicentre studies, especially in underdeveloped areas, can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the prevalence and risk factors of AA in China. Third, AA was assessed through a self-reported screening scale rather than standard diagnostic interviews, which may lead to potential measurement bias. In the future, we aim to train obstetric nurses and other medical staff to assist pregnant and postpartum women in completing assessments, either on-site or online, to minimise bias. Fourth, due to the time constraints for maternity check-ups, the questionnaires were limited in length and depth. Information on specific details of certain risk factors, such as tobacco and alcohol consumption patterns and other risk-related factors, was not collected. This may impact the interpretation of results and hinder a deeper analysis of these risk factors. Future studies should aim to gather more detailed information to provide a comprehensive overview of the risk factors associated with AA. Last but not least, although GAD-7 was administered only once, a validated clinical cut-off (≥10) was used to minimise misclassification bias, and the large sample size supports the reliability of prevalence estimates.

Implications

The study investigated the prevalence and risk factors of AA in the first trimester among pregnant women in Shenzhen, China. The results emphasise the need to assess anxiety symptoms and risk factors to identify women at risk of AA and provide appropriate support. Specifically, pregnant women with characteristics such as young age, primiparity and unplanned pregnancy should receive prioritised attention in clinical settings. Particular focus should also be directed at vulnerable populations, such as temporary residents and individuals with low socioeconomic status. Research indicates that risk factors for AA are interconnected rather than independent of one another. Low educational levels, a risk factor for AA, are associated with low income, unhealthy lifestyles and unplanned pregnancies. Therefore, enhancing people’s education can significantly decrease the prevalence of AA. Among various risk factors, changing poor lifestyle habits is the simplest and most effective. Therefore, we recommend that pregnant women maintain healthy lifestyle habits, avoid smoking or drinking alcohol and engage in regular exercise. Additionally, fostering a healthy marital quality emerges as a protective factor against AA, emphasising the pivotal role partners can play in AA prevention. To bolster support systems and enhance the well-being of perinatal women and their offspring, collaborative efforts among hospitals, communities and families are essential.
