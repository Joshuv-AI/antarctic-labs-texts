Nanoindentation study of human fingernail for determining its structural elasticity
Authors: Hironori Tohmyoh, Masaru Abukawa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman nails play an important role in transmitting force to the fingertips, and their mechanical properties are important indices. The nail has a three‐layered structure consisting of top dorsal, middle intermediate, and under ventral plates, and its internal structure is believed to affect its mechanical properties. However, this has not been investigated in previous studies.MethodsThe Young's moduli of the top, middle, and under plates were measured using nanoindentation, and a theoretical model was developed to estimate the structural elasticity for the bending deformation of human nails, which is an index describing the deformability of the nail without depending on its external dimensions. The structural elasticity of human nails was compared with that of human hair collected from the same person. The effect of the softening treatment on the nails was also evaluated.ResultsThe Young's moduli of the top, middle, and under plates measured using nanoindentation were 2.9, 3.1, and 2.8 GPa, respectively. The structural elasticity of the nail was estimated to be 2.9 GPa, approximately 75% that of hair. Moreover, softening treatment with a urea cream reduced the structural elasticity of the nail to 70%.ConclusionThis paper proposed a method for estimating the structural elasticity of a human nail with a three‐layered structure. This index is a mechanical property with “Pa” as a unit, and is useful for comparing deformability with the Young's modulus of other homogeneous materials or for investigating the effect of various treatments quantitatively.

INTRODUCTION

The human nail is a skin appendage mainly composed of hard keratin. In addition to protecting the fingertips, the fingernail plays an important role in transmitting force to the fingertips when grasping objects 1 , 2 , 3 and to the toenails when walking. Nails become harder and more easily broken with age. 4 , 5 For ingrown nails, mechanical correction of the nail shape has been performed. 6 For these cases, softening of the nail is required. Therefore, a quantitative evaluation of the mechanical properties of the nail is useful for its diagnosis and treatment.

Human nails have a three‐layered structure: top dorsal, middle intermediate, and under ventral plates. 7 , 8 , 9 Fibres are oriented vertically on the top and under plates, and horizontally on the middle plate. Similarly, human hair, which is also composed mainly of hard keratin, has a three‐layered structure from the outside: cuticle, cortex, and medulla, 10 and its mechanical properties are known to depend on its internal structure. 11 The structural elasticity of human hair, which describes the deformability of the hair without depending on the external dimensions, has been studied. 12 The structural elasticity of human hair for bending deformation was measured experimentally; however, it does not match that of tensile deformation. 13 The experimental results suggest that the Young's modulus, which represents the deformability of homogeneous materials, cannot express the deformability of human hair with a three‐layered structure.

As the nail has a three‐layered structure, its mechanical properties are assumed to depend on its internal structure. However, reports on the mechanical properties of human nails are limited compared with those of human hair, for example, they only involve tensile testing 14 and nanoindentation, 15 , 16 , 17 and no discussion is available on the deformability of the nail considering its internal structure. This is because nails bend when clipped, 18 making mechanical testing difficult. A testing machine has been developed to measure the mechanical properties of nails, such as elasticity and viscoelasticity, in addition to their geometrical features 19 ; however, the measured properties are qualitative indices without units. Therefore, such indices are difficult to compare with conventional mechanical properties, such as the Young's modulus. Here, a question arises. Which is easier to bend, a human nail or human hair, both of which have different external dimensions and internal structures?

This paper proposes a method for estimating the structural elasticity of a human nail for bending deformation. Accordingly, a theoretical model was constructed to estimate the structural elasticity of a nail sample by considering its internal structure, and the Young's modulus of each plate of the nail sample was measured via nanoindentation. First, nanoindentation was performed on the plane of the nail parallel to the free edge (cross section) and the plane perpendicular to the free edges (longitudinal section) to verify whether there was anisotropy in the Young's modulus of each plate in both planes. Subsequently, nanoindentation was performed on hair samples collected from the same person, and the structural elasticity of the hair was estimated using a theoretical model to determine whether the nail or hair bent more easily. Furthermore, changes in the structural elasticity of the nail samples owing to softening treatment with a urea cream were evaluated quantitatively.

THEORY OF STRUCTURAL ELASTICITY FOR BENDING DEFORMATION OF HUMAN NAIL

A microscopic image of the cross section of a human fingernail is shown in Figure 1A. The human nail has a three‐layered structure consisting of top dorsal, middle intermediate, and under ventral plates. This was modeled as a three‐layered rectangular cross section, as shown in Figure 1B, and a straight beam with such a cross section was considered, to develop a theoretical model to estimate the structural elasticity for bending deformation (SEB) of human nails.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fingernail samples

Nail samples for this study were collected from the fingernails of an Asian male in his 20s. Ten samples were collected and embedded in a methacrylate medium (Technovit 4000) for nanoindentation. In this study, five samples (N1–N5) were embedded such that the cross section became the test plane, and five other samples (N6–N10) were embedded such that the longitudinal section was the test plane. These samples were prepared to investigate the anisotropy of the Young's modulus in different planes of the nail sample. The embedded samples were cut with a precision cutter, polished with abrasive paper, and finally finished with 0.25‐mm diamond slurry, as shown in Figure 1A.

Nanoindentation

An atomic force microscope (FlexAFM, Nanosurf) and a cantilever with a cone‐shaped probe tip (Tap190A1‐G, BudgetSensors) were used for the nanoindentation. Prior to nanoindentation, an AFM image of a 10.4 μm square within each plate was acquired. Nanoindentation was then performed at 10 points on the line in the image to obtain the force curve, which is the relationship between the load, P I, and the displacement, δ. The distance between the indentation points ranged from 0.5 to 1.0 μm. The force curve while pulling the probe up was recorded to determine the Young's modulus of each layer.

The Hertz model was adapted in this study, and the relationship between P I and δ for this model is given by (5) PI=E∗R12δ32, where R is the radius of curvature of the probe tip, and E * is the reduced modulus. If the Young's modulus of the probe is sufficiently higher than that of the sample, E * is given by (6) 1E∗=31−ν24E, where ν is the Poisson's ratio, and E is the Young's modulus of the sample. From Equations (5) and (6), E can be determined as follows: (7) E=31−ν2PI4R12δ32.

In this study, the ν value of the nail sample was assumed to be 0.30. 21

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Young's modulus

Figure 2A shows the AFM image of a nail sample (N1) obtained from the middle plate. Nanoindentation was performed at the positions on the line in the figure (indentation numbers 11−20). The relationship between P I and δ at indentation #3 for sample N1 is shown in Figure 2B. In this relationship, the data over δ = 0 nm were fitted into Equation (5) to determine the Young's modulus at the point. The relationship between P I and δ is given by P I = 6.087 × 105 δ 3/2, and the Young's modulus was determined to be 3.03 GPa, where the value of R was 18.80 nm. Figure 2C shows the Young's moduli against the indentation numbers. In the sample case (N1), the values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 were determined to be 2.77, 3.03, and 2.69 GPa, respectively.

The values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 for samples N1 to N5 are summarized in Table 1, and their average values were 2.94 ± 0.22, 3.13 ± 0.26, and 2.79 ± 0.16 GPa, respectively. The Young's modulus of the middle plate was slightly higher than those of the top and under plates. This may be due to the higher percentage of hard keratin in the middle plate than in the other plates. 22 The values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 for samples N6 to N10 are also summarized in Table 1, and their average values were 2.66 ± 0.29, 2.84 ± 0.36, and 2.55 ± 0.08 GPa, respectively. The value of E 2 was slightly higher than those of E 1 and E 3, similar to the results of the samples N1 to N5. The values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 for samples N6 to N10 were comparable to those of N1 to N5, and anisotropy in the Young's modulus of the examined nail was not observed in the different planes, that is, the cross section and the longitudinal section.

Let us estimate the SEB values of the nail samples using the determined values of E 1, E 2, and E 3. It has been reported that the ratio of the thicknesses of the top, middle, and under plates is 3:5:2. 9 The values of κ and λ were assumed from the literature to be 0.3 and 0.8, respectively. The SEB determined from the average values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 for samples N1 to N5 was 2.91 ± 0.19 GPa, similar to the value of 2.64 ± 0.20 GPa for samples N6 to N10. Note that the movement of the neutral axis determined by Equation (2) was almost zero; η = −0.002 for samples N1 to N5 and −0.001 for samples N6 to N10. The average values of E 1 to E 3 for samples N1 to N5 and N6 to N10 are 2.82 ± 0.16 and 2.71 ± 0.11 GPa, respectively, and the difference between these values and the values of SEB was approximately 3%. Thus, it is acceptable to express the deformability of the examined nail as a homogeneous material in terms of the Young's modulus. In other words, the structural elasticity for bending deformation of the examined nail sample matched that of the tensile deformation. However, this was not the case for the hair samples. 13

Comparison with human hair

Human hair, like nails, is composed mainly of hard keratin and has a three‐layered structure with a top cuticle, middle cortex, and inner medulla. Here, the Young's moduli of the medulla, cortex, and cuticle layers were measured via nanoindentation, and the SEB of the hair samples was determined and compared with that of the nail samples. It has been reported previously by the experiment that human hair is twice as difficult to bend as fingernail. 23

Human hair has an elliptical cross section, 13 with a long axis at 2a and a short axis at 2b. Let the long and short axes of the middle cortex be denoted by α 2a and α 2b, respectively, using the dimensionless parameter α, and those of the inner medulla be denoted by β 2a and β 2b, respectively, using the dimensionless parameter β. The SEB of human hair is given by 12 (8) SEB=E4β4+E5α4−β4+E61−α4, where E 4, E 5, and E 6 are the Young's moduli of the medulla, cortex, and cuticle, respectively. The SEB of the human hair is related to the fourth power of the dimensionless parameters α and β of each layer. It has been reported that the value of SEB determined using Equation (8) with E 4, E 5, and E 6 measured via nanoindentation is consistent with that measured directly via the bending test. 24

The hair samples were collected from the tip of the hair of the same person, and no visible damage to the outer cuticle layer was confirmed via microscopic observation. Five samples were collected for the nanoindentation tests. Figure 3A shows the AFM image of a hair sample (H4) obtained from the cuticle layer. Nanoindentation was performed at various positions on the line in the figure (indentation numbers 1−10). The relationship between P I and δ for indentation #4 of sample H4 is shown in Figure 3B. The relationship between P I and δ is given by P I = 9.069 × 105 δ 3/2. The Young's modulus was determined to be 4.40 GPa, where the value of R was 19.79 nm, and ν was assumed to be 0.38. 25 , 26 Figure 3C shows the Young's moduli against the indentation numbers. In the sample case (H4), E 4, E 5, and E 6 were determined to be 2.76, 3.46, and 4.39 GPa, respectively.

The values of E 4, E 5, and E 6 for samples H1 to H5 are summarised in Table 2, and their average values were 2.65 ± 0.46, 3.61 ± 0.33, and 4.42 ± 0.05 GPa, respectively. The Young's modulus was higher in the cuticle, cortex, and medulla layers, consistent with previous reports. 24 Medulla is an amorphous structure formed by fibrous structures derived from cortex cells, and is believed to have a lower Young's modulus than the cortex. 27

Effect of softening treatment on the structural elasticity of nail

Various types of treatments are used to soften brittle nails caused by aging and for the mechanical correction of ingrown nails. In this study, the degree to which the structural elasticity of the nail was reduced by softening treatment with a urea cream was quantitatively evaluated.

The softener used in this experiment was a cream containing 20% urea (Keratinamin Kowa, Kowa). As with the procedure described in Section 3.1, three samples (N11–N13) were prepared such that the cross section was the test plane, and the surfaces of the samples were polished. Subsequently, a hole with a diameter of approximately 1 mm, in which the urea cream was later embedded, was drilled in the embedded medium on the top plate side using a pin vice. Similar to the procedure described in Section 3.2, nanoindentation was performed at 10 points on each plate before the softening treatment. The holes in the samples were then filled with the urea cream. After 24 h, the cream was removed from the hole, and the sample was subjected to nanoindentation. The Young's modulus of each plate was obtained via nanoindentation at 10 points that differed from the points at which nanoindentation was performed before the treatment in each layer.

The values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 for samples N11–N13 before and after the softening treatment are summarized in Table 3. The average values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 for samples N11–N13 after the softening treatment were reduced to 65%, 63%, and 76%, respectively, compared to those before the treatment. The rate of decrease in the Young's moduli of the top and middle plates was greater than that of the under plate. This may have been due to the penetration of the cream from the top plate to the under plate. The SEB values of samples N11–N13 determined using Equation (4) with the values of E 1, E 2, and E 3 shown in Table 3 are summarized in the same table, and the SEB values before and after the softening treatment are compared in Figure 5. The SEB of the nail samples after the softening treatment was 68% of that before the treatment. The proposed method, which is a combination of nanoindentation and a theoretical model for the SEB of nails, is useful for quantitatively detecting changes in the deformability of the nail sample.

CONCLUSION

This study aimed to determine the structural elasticity for the bending deformation of a human nail, which describes the deformability of the nail without depending on the external dimensions. Accordingly, the Young's moduli of the top dorsal, middle intermediate, and under ventral plates were measured via nanoindentation and were determined to be 2.9, 3.1, and 2.8 GPa, respectively. No anisotropy of the Young's modulus of each layer was confirmed, indicating that there was no difference in the Young's modulus of each plate between the plane parallel to the free edge of the nail and that perpendicular to the free edge. A theoretical model was developed to estimate the structural elasticity for bending deformation of the nail sample from the Young's modulus of each layer, which was determined to be 2.9 GPa. This value was approximately 75% of the structural elasticity for the bending deformation of human hair samples collected from the same person. The neutral axis for bending the nail sample passes through the centroid of the sample. Furthermore, it was evaluated quantitatively that softening treatment with a urea cream reduced the structural elasticity of the nail to 70%.
