Gender moderation in the association between academic stress, sleep quality, and adolescent mental health
Authors: Wenyan Zhang, Jingmiao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescent mental health has become a growing concern in China, with academic stress identified as a prominent risk factor. This cross-sectional study examined the associations among academic stress, sleep quality, and mental health in county-level areas of Liaoning Province, with particular attention to the mediating role of sleep quality and the stage-specific moderating effect of gender. A total of 2150 junior and senior middle school students completed standardized questionnaires measuring academic stress, sleep quality, and mental health. The findings revealed that academic stress was significantly associated with poorer mental health across both educational stages. However, the mediating role of sleep quality differed by stage. Sleep quality significantly mediated the association between academic stress and mental health among senior middle school students, but not among junior middle school students. Gender demonstrated stage-specific moderating patterns. Among senior middle school students, gender moderated the association between academic stress and sleep quality, with girls showing stronger stress-related sleep disruptions. Among junior middle school students, gender moderated the association between sleep quality and mental health, with girls being more vulnerable to the psychological effects of poor sleep.Keywords: Academic stress, Sleep quality, Mental health, GenderSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Adolescent mental health has emerged as a pressing public health concern in China and worldwide. Recent surveys highlight the alarming prevalence of mental distress among adolescents, including depression and anxiety. In China, where academic achievement is highly emphasized, the issue is particularly acute. A meta-analysis reported that approximately 24.3% of Chinese adolescents experience depression, a rate higher than many global estimates1. Furthermore, national data suggest that around 30 million children and adolescents under the age of 17 struggle with emotional or behavioral problems2. These challenges are amplified in county-level regions, which in China refer to administrative areas below prefecture-level cities and typically include rural and semi-urban communities3. In these areas, limited educational and mental health resources, larger class sizes, and reduced parental supervision due to labor migration collectively heighten students’ stress exposure and emotional vulnerability4. Therefore, examining stress-related mechanisms in such contexts is of particular significance.This research focuses on country-level middle school students (including junior middle school students and senior middle school) in China to explore adolecents’ mental health issues.

Academic stress and mental health

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that academic stress is associated with adolescents’ mental difficulties (such as psychological difficulties), especially in East Asian educational contexts that prioritize academic performance5,6. Numerous studies have documented associations between high academic stress and increased depressive and anxiety symptoms1. According to the stress–coping model, stress arises when perceived demands exceed available coping resources, thereby triggering maladaptive responses such as rumination and emotional exhaustion. In China’s exam-driven environment, high-stakes examinations (e.g., the high school and college entrance examinations), prolonged study hours, and widespread tutoring are associated with heightened stress and reduced psychological well-being6,7. Even in county-level schools, where resources may be more limited, students still encounter strong academic pressure due to competition for access to high-quality middle education8,9. These contextual pressures suggest that academic stress is closely related to adolescents’ mental health. Beyond its direct mental effects, academic stress is also associated with sleep-related difficulties.

Sleep and its role in adolescent mental health

From a theoretical perspective, sleep represents a central self-regulatory and recovery mechanism through which academic stress may influence adolescents’ mental health10. Prolonged academic demands heighten cognitive and emotional arousal, such as worry and rumination, which interfere with the initiation and maintenance of sleep11. Chronic exposure to stress may further disrupt physiological regulation processes and increase allostatic load, thereby associated with sleep quality and restorative functioning12. Given the critical role of sleep in emotional regulation and psychological recovery during adolescence, impairment in sleep provides a theoretically grounded pathway through which academic stress can be translated into psychological difficulties10. In practical terms, this regulatory function of sleep is reflected in adolescents’ daily sleep duration and quality10. Adequate sleep (8–10 h per night) is essential during adolescence for brain maturation and emotional regulation13, yet many Chinese adolescents report insufficient sleep14. For example, high school students often sleep fewer than seven hours per night1,15. Academic stress contributes to sleep disruption by reducing sleep opportunity and increasing cognitive–emotional arousal16. Empirical findings indicate that academic stress is associated with shorter sleep duration and poorer sleep quality1,17. Developmental data similarly show that as adolescents grow older, academic expectations intensify while sleep quality declines, with stress from academic demands accounting for significant variance in sleep outcomes18. Taken together, these findings suggest that sleep quality may function as a mediating pathway linking academic stress to adolescent mental health. Building on this perspective, Cheng et al.14 reported that among high school students, academic stress is associated with sleep, which subsequently reduced well-being feeling, highlighting a vicious cycle in which stress-induced sleep disruption exacerbates emotional problems. Although previous research has demonstrated the mediating role of sleep quality, whether this mediating mechanism is also applicable among county-level middle school students remains uncertain.

Gender and developmental differences in stress and sleep

In addition, junior (Grades 7–9) and senior (Grades 10–12) middle school students are at different developmental stages. According to Developmental Task Theory, individuals experience substantial emotional and cognitive changes across growth stages from childhood to adulthood19. Junior and senior middle school students differ in pubertal maturity20, emotional reactivity21, and exposure to exam-driven pressure, suggesting distinct stress-related vulnerabilities across stages22. Gender differences may further complicate the relationship between academic stress, sleep quality, and mental health. Evidence suggests that girls are more likely than boys to internalize stress, leading to heightened risks of sleep disturbances and emotional problems, whereas boys may under-report psychological symptoms or externalize stress in behavioral ways23,24. Recent studies in China have reinforced this pattern, showing that female adolescents tend to experience shorter sleep duration, higher rates of insomnia, and stronger associations between poor sleep and depressive symptoms compared to males. Given the limited attention previously paid to these differences in county-level contexts, it is necessary to examine whether educational stage and gender systematically shape the mechanisms linking academic stress, sleep quality, and mental health among Chinese adolescents.

Methodology

Participants and procedure

This study adopted a cross-sectional survey of middle school students in Liaoning province, China. A total of 2441 adolescents (aged 12–18) were recruited from county-level junior and senior middle schools across northern, central, and southern Liaoning using stratified cluster sampling. Six public schools participated (junior middle and senior middle), from which intact classes were randomly selected in Grades 7–12 (Grade 7–9 is junior middle school and Grade 10–12 is senior middle school). All students in the selected classes were invited, with participation voluntary. Ethical approval was obtained from the University Institutional Review Board, and permission was granted by school principals. Surveys were administered in classrooms by trained research assistants in May 2025. To minimize bias, teachers were absent, confidentiality was stressed, and no identifying information was collected. The questionnaire required approximately 30–40 min to complete. After excluding invalid or incomplete questionnaires, the final sample comprised 2150 students (88.08% valid response rate), 51.2% female and 48.8% male. About half (51%) were junior middle students and 49% senior middle, with ages averaging 15.0 years. No significant demographic differences were observed across the three regions, supporting the appropriateness of pooled analyses (Table 1).

Measures

Academic stress questionnaire for middle school students

Adolescents’ academic stress was assessed using the Academic Stress Questionnaire for Middle School Students developed by Xu et al.25. The instrument consists of 21 items, across four dimensions: parental pressure (6 items) “eg. My parents often exhort me, with the hope that I will become a person of distinction”, teacher pressure (5 items) “eg. When I fail to answer the instructor’s questions, I am subjected to his/her criticism”, social pressure (4 items) “eg. I frequently apprise my parents of the occurrences that transpire at school”, and self-imposed pressure (6 items) “eg. I perceive further education as my sole viable path forward”. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all true”) to 5 (“completely true”), with higher scores indicating greater perceived academic stress26. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.91.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

Sleep quality was measured using the Chinese revised version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) by Liu et al.27. The PSQI contains 19 self-rated items and 5 observer-rated items “eg. During the past month, when have you usually gone to bed at night?”, though only 18 self-rated items are included in scoring. These items yield seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Consistent with common practice in adolescent sleep research, this study used the global PSQI score because it provides a reliable and psychometrically supported indicator of overall sleep quality and generally shows stronger internal consistency28–30. Higher scores are associatied with poorer sleep quality. The Cronbach’s α for the PSQI was 0.90. The results from Pittsburgh Sleep Quality index can reflect the issues of sleep quality.

Mental Health Scale for Middle school Students (MSSMHS)

Mental health was evaluated using the Mental Health Scale for Middle School Students (MSSMHS) developed by Wang31. The scale includes 60 items covering 10 dimensions: obsessive–compulsive symptoms “eg. It is requisite to meticulously review and verify one’s assignments repeatedly”, paranoia “eg. I invariably maintain views that diverge from the common consensus”, hostility “eg. I feel an impulse to engage in acts of physical destruction”, interpersonal sensitivity “eg. To expect perfection from others”, depression “eg. I often experience ideations of suicide”, anxiety “eg. I carry a perpetual sense of preoccupation”, academic stress “eg. I hate taking tests”, maladjustment “eg. I find the environment of the class displeasing”, emotional imbalance “eg. I alternate between being affectionate and aloof with my parents”, and psychological disequilibrium “eg. My peer achieved higher grades than me, yet their competence does not exceed my own.” (6 items per dimension). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), with higher scores indicating poorer mental health32. In this study, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.98). The results from mental health scale can mirror the issues of mental health issues.

Procedure

Prior to data collection, informed consent was obtained from schools, teachers, and parents/guardians. A detailed description of the stratified cluster sampling procedure and full research protocol, including informed consent procedures, survey administration training, and post-survey debriefing, is provided in Appendices A and B to ensure transparency and replicability. Students completed the survey after reading the guidance, with the process lasting approximately 15–20 min.

To assess potential common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. The first factor accounted for 40.37% of the variance, below the 50% threshold33, suggesting no serious common method bias. Descriptive statistics for academic stress, sleep quality, and mental health are reported in Table 3. On average, both junior (46.26) and senior (47.21) high school students reported comparable levels of academic stress, while sleep quality problems were more pronounced among junior middle students (M = 3.89, SD = 3.24) compared to their senior counterparts (M = 3.36, SD = 3.04). In contrast, mental health problems were slightly higher among senior middle students (M = 1.54, SD = 0.67) than junior middle students (M = 1.42, SD = 0.63). These results suggest that although stress levels are similar across developmental stages, junior middle students experience greater sleep disturbances, while senior middle students report poorer mental health.

Mediation analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0. Mediation effects were examined with the Bootstrap method, employing 5000 resamples and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. The PROCESS macro (Model 4) was applied separately for junior and senior middle school students to test the mediating role of sleep quality. As shown in Table 4, academic stress significantly associated with sleep quality problems (junior middle: β = 0.11, p < 0.001; senior middle: β = 0.14, p < 0.001), which in turn significantly associated with poorer mental health (junior middle: β = 0.10, p < 0.001; senior middle: β = 0.09, p < 0.001). Mediation was considered significant when the confidence interval excluded zero, and the results show that indirect effects were indeed significant in both groups (junior middle: effect = 0.01, 95% CI [0.01, 0.02]; senior middle: effect = 0.01, 95% CI [0.01, 0.02]). These findings indicate that sleep quality partially mediated the relationship between academic stress and mental health among adolescents across developmental stages (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Academic stress negatively associated with mental health

Academic stress was negatively associated with mental health in both junior and senior middle school students. This is consistent with a large literature showing that highly demanding and socially evaluative school contexts foster internalizing psychological issue via heightened physiological arousal, negative affect, and ruminative appraisal cycles34. In exam-salient environments, performance-contingent self-worth and fear of failure can maintain dysphoric mood and worry even outside the classroom, consolidating a direct stress-to-symptoms pathway7. In addition, perfectionistic concerns from parents and teachers and threat-based interpretations of routine assessment further exacerbate vigilance and self-criticism, which are reliable cognitive risks for depression and anxiety in adolescent35,36.

Academic stress negatively associated with sleep quality

The present findings demonstrate that academic stress is significantly associated with sleep quality among both junior and senior middle school students. This result is consistent with previous studies reporting that adolescents facing heavy academic demands are more likely to experience insufficient or disturbed sleep15. In the Chinese educational context, where students commonly extend study time late into the night to cope with homework and exam preparation, academic pressure directly reduces sleep opportunity and disrupts regular sleep routines14. Moreover, stress may increase pre-sleep worry and cognitive arousal, further contributing to delayed sleep onset and poorer sleep continuity16. Together, these findings underscore the dual behavioral and psychological pathways through which academic stress compromises adolescents’ sleep. Given that sleep quality is a critical determinant of emotional regulation and cognitive functioning, interventions targeting workload reduction and stress management may help protect both sleep and mental health in this population.

Gender moderation of the stress–sleep link: a stage-specific pattern (H2a/H2b)

Consistent with a stage-specific moderation account, gender did not moderate the stress–sleep association in junior middle but did moderate the association in senior middle, with stronger stress-linked sleep quality among girls. In China, for junior middle school students, this lack of moderation may reflect relatively homogeneous school schedules and stronger parental oversight, which limit gender disparities in sleep-hygiene behaviors37, helping standardize sleep hygiene across boys and girls. Indeed, studies show that consistent parental regulation of bedtimes and involvement in evening routines improves adolescents’ sleep quality and reduces disparities38.

As students transition into senior middle school, academic demands increase, which makes gender differences in stress and sleep more prominent. In this context, girls appear to be more vulnerable to stress-induced sleep disruptions. This heightened susceptibility may be attributed to both biological and psycho-social factors: adolescent females experience greater hormonal fluctuations, which can intensify stress reactivity39, and they are more likely to engage in rumination and emotion-focused coping strategies that disrupt sleep40. These factors help explain why girls consistently report higher rates of insomnia and sleep difficulties than boys. In line with these explanations, the current study’s moderated mediation analysis found that gender significantly moderated the "academic stress → sleep quality" relationship for senior middle school students (RQ2b). Specifically, girls exhibited a stronger association between academic stress and sleep issues than boys. This finding underscores the greater vulnerability of high school girls to sleep disruptions under academic pressure, further supporting the notion that both biological and psycho-social factors contribute to their heightened susceptibility. No significant moderating effect was found for junior middle school students (RQ2a), indicating that the relationship between academic stress and sleep quality was not influenced by gender in this group.

Sleep quality positively associated with mental health

Poorer sleep is associated with poorer mental health among adolescents across both junior and senior middle school. Experimental and longitudinal research supports a mechanistic account: insufficient or poor-quality sleep disrupts prefrontal regulation of limbic systems, amplifies emotional reactivity, and reduces stress tolerance—thereby increasing vulnerability to depressive and anxiety symptoms. Neuroimaging studies show that sleep deprivation weakens inhibitory control by the prefrontal cortex over emotional brain regions, leading to exaggerated negative responses to psychological stimuli. Furthermore, sleep disturbances are associated with daily psychological functioning in youth more than adults. Adolescents experiencing sleep loss report greater psychological dysregulation, aligning with an increased sensitivity to social rejection and emotional triggers41. Taken together, these findings underscore that sleep quality is a critical determinant of adolescent mental health.

Sleep quality as a mediating mechanism between academic stress and mental health

The findings indicate that sleep quality operates as a mediating mechanism linking academic stress to adolescents’ mental health (H4 is supported). Elevated academic stress increases pre-sleep cognitive load and physiological arousal, delays sleep onset, shortens and fragments sleep, and disrupts circadian regularity42. In turn, impaired sleep undermines emotion regulation and stress tolerance, heightening negative affect and worry the following day, through this pathway, academic demands are translated into psychological symptoms43.This mediating pathway is corroborated by several empirical studies. Among Chinese high school students, sleep quality mediated the effect of physical stress on subjective well-being, highlighting sleep’s intermediary role in the stress–well-being connection14, Additionally, Yang et al.44 conducted a large three-wave longitudinal study among Chinese adolescents and found that insomnia significantly mediated the effect of life stress on subsequent anxiety and depressive symptoms, indicating that sleep quality plays a crucial role in transforming stress into internalizing psychological symptoms (such as anxiety, depression).

Gender moderation of the sleep–mental health link: a stage-specific pattern

Consistent with a stage-specific moderation framework, gender significantly moderated the association between sleep quality and mental health in junior middle, but no such moderation emerged in senior middle. This indicates that the protective or detrimental effects of sleep quality on psychological well-being vary by gender primarily during early adolescence. In junior middle, girls showed greater susceptibility to the psychological costs of poor sleep than boys, consistent with evidence that adolescent girls display higher emotional reactivity and greater vulnerability to sleep-related disturbances40,45. Pubertal hormonal fluctuations further heighten girls’ stress sensitivity during early adolescence39. At the neuro-developmental level, this period is marked by an imbalance between a reactive limbic system and an immature prefrontal cortex, which weakens emotional regulation46,47. This imbalance is often more pronounced in girls due to earlier pubertal onset and stronger hormonal variability48. From a developmental-task perspective, early adolescence involves core socioemotional challenges such as establishing emotional autonomy, stabilizing mood regulation, and adapting to rapid pubertal changes49. These developmental demands heighten adolescents’ dependence on biological regulatory systems such as sleep for maintaining emotional stability, making sleep disruption particularly consequential during this period. Poor sleep exacerbates these regulatory limitations by further associating with prefrontal control over emotion50. When external stressors are relatively moderate, sleep quality becomes a particularly salient regulator of emotional functioning because sleep plays a central role in prefrontal–limbic integration during adolescence13,51-57. This mechanism may be especially pronounced in girls, who show stronger pubertal-related emotional reactivity and greater sensitivity to sleep-related changes in regulatory functioning39,48.

Practical implications

The findings suggest that interventions should not solely focus on reducing academic stress but also on strengthening sleep hygiene and emotional regulation skills. For junior middle school students, programs aimed at improving sleep habits and supporting emotional coping, particularly for girls, may help buffer mental health risks. For senior middle school students, stress-management strategies targeting examination pressure may be especially important for preventing sleep-related deterioration, particularly among female adolescents.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference regarding temporal sequencing among academic stress, sleep quality, and mental health. Longitudinal designs are needed to confirm the proposed mediation pathway and to examine dynamic changes across developmental transitions. Additionally, reliance on self-report measures may introduce shared method variance and subjective bias, although Harman’s test suggested that common method bias was not severe. Future research could incorporate objective sleep measures or multi-informant mental health assessments. Furthermore, although the sample was drawn from multiple regions within Liaoning Province, findings may not generalize to urban populations or other cultural contexts. Replication in diverse educational settings is warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that sleep quality serves as an important intermediary mechanism linking academic stress to adolescent mental health, while gender moderation varies across developmental stages. These findings highlight the need for developmentally informed and gender-sensitive approaches in promoting adolescent psychological well-being within academically demanding environments.

Appendix 1: Detailed sampling procedure

A stratified cluster sampling procedure was adopted to obtain a representative sample of county-level adolescents in Liaoning Province. First, three geographic regions (southern, central, and northern Liaoning) were treated as strata to capture regional variation in county-level educational conditions. Within each stratum, two junior middle schools and two senior middle schools were randomly selected from the list of all public schools provided by local education authorities. The sampling cluster was intact classes. In each selected school, two classes per grade level were randomly drawn, and all students within these classes were invited to participate. This approach ensured that (a) students were nested within naturally occurring classroom clusters, and (b) each regional and school-level stratum was proportionally represented in the final sample. A total of 6 schools were included, yielding a sample of 2106 valid respondents.

Appendix 2: Detailed research procedure informed consent
