Optimizing degassing for particle-reinforced Al composite: Advanced ultrasonic-assisted argon treatment and numerical simulation insights
Authors: Jian Chen, Xinyuan Jin, Jiwei Geng, Xianhui Luo, Yugang Li, Peikang Xia, Xianfeng Li, Fangming Wang, Dong Chen, Mingliang Wang, Haowei Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen removal from particle-reinforced Al composite is critical for preventing porosities near particle clusters and improving comprehensive material properties. This study investigated the effects of different argon flow schemes and power ultrasound on bubble dispersion in water. Based on similarity theory, the optimal argon flow scheme for ultrasonic-assisted argon (UAA) treatment was identified and applied to TiB2/2024 Al composite to explore the degassing mechanism. Three argon flow schemes for UAA treatment: EV-EC (External ventilation, equal channel), IV-EC (Internal ventilation, equal channel), and IV-RC (Internal ventilation, reduced channel) were evaluated through numerical simulation and water simulation experiments. The results demonstrated that IV-RC scheme exhibits superior bubble dispersion characteristics and a broader bubbling range. Experimental results in Al composite confirmed that UAA treatment with IV-RC scheme achieved the highest degassing efficiency, with a relative density improvement of up to 98.01 % and the lowest hydrogen content (0.0235 ppm). Synchrotron radiation X-ray computed tomography results indicated that UAA treatment effectively reduces the pore size and volume, transforming interconnected, complex pores into dispersed, nearly spherical ones. Ultrasonic cavitation aids this process by breaking small argon bubbles into numerous tiny bubbles that penetrate deeply into the melt, reducing the wettability angle between particles and the matrix and thereby improving hydrogen removal efficiency.Keywords: Ultrasonic-assisted argon treatment, Similarity theory, Al composite, Argon flow, Bubbling regime, Hydrogen

Introduction

Particulate-reinforced metal matrix composites (PRMMCs) have emerged as promising materials for the aerospace and automotive industries due to their lightweight, exceptional strength, and superior stiffness [1], [2], [3]. Despite their advantages, traditional casting methods used for fabrication of aluminum (Al)-based PRMMCs face significant challenges. One major issue is the tendency for particles to agglomerate in Al melt [4], [5]. Another challenge is the hydrogen trapped in the Al melt and within particle agglomerates during solidification presents [6], [7]. High hydrogen content in castings often results in defects such as porosity [8], leading to increased internal stresses and cold cracking [9].

Porosity in alloys can generally be attributed to several factors: (1) shrinkage during solidification in the mushy zone, exacerbated by insufficient interdendritic feeding [10]; (2) the formation of hydrogen gas bubbles due to a sudden drop in hydrogen solubility during solidification [6]; (3) entrapped air within particle clusters, which impedes metal flow [11]; and (4) mechanical stirring introduces oxides, gases, and other impurities into the melt [12]. Porosity causes weak points in alloys, particularly under strong temperature gradients during the solidification of large melts or die-casting of thin-walled components, leading to microcrack formation and propagation [13]. High porosity rates can also significantly impact mechanical properties and fatigue performance [14], [15]. Consequently, effective degassing treatments are essential in metals and alloys casting processes [16].

The most commonly used degassing technique today is argon rotary degassing. This process involves immersing a porous graphite rotor into the melt and directing argon gas through the rotor, which rotates to shear and disperse the argon bubbles while capturing dissolved hydrogen [17], [18]. However, this method has drawbacks, including a high rate of scum formation and potential contamination of the melt, which can damage the graphite rotor.

Ultrasonic degassing stands as an early application of acoustic cavitation, emerged in the 1960s and 1980s [19]. Industrial-scale trials in foundries have shown it to be an effective and clean technology for degassing Al melts, although it requires specialized equipment [20]. Recently, its environmental benefits and versatility have led to increased interest. Ultrasonic degassing operates by generating cavitation bubbles in the melt through power ultrasound. These cavitation bubbles, driven by vibration and diffusion, grow and rise to the surface, carrying hydrogen atoms with them and thereby removing the gas from the melt [21].

The ultrasonic degassing process occurs in three stages [22]: (1) Cavitation bubbles vibrate and diffuse in an alternating ultrasound field, utilizing the hydrogen partial pressure difference to become a pump for extracting hydrogen from the melt; (2) Dispersed bubbles coalesce due to Bjerkness and Bernoulli forces; (3) Larger bubbles rise to the surface and release hydrogen gas into the atmosphere buoyantly. As highlighted by Eskin [23], ultrasonic waves induce melt flow, including acoustic and secondary flows, which aids in bubble dispersion and enhances degassing efficiency. Ultrasonic treatment applied in PRMMCs casting can not only remove hydrogen, but also disperse the strengthen particles and refine grain size [24].

However, achieving low hydrogen levels solely with ultrasonic degassing can be challenging. Recently, ultrasonic-assisted argon (UAA) treatment has been developed to reduce hydrogen in metal melt [25], [26]. This method breaks down large argon bubbles into smaller ones, ensuring uniform dispersion and effective degassing while minimizing metal loss [27]. Despite its advantages, the mechanisms behind rapid degassing through cavitation and bubble interactions remain complex and not fully understood [28]. Our study builds upon these advancements by examining the underlying mechanisms through detailed numerical simulations and experimental validations, offering new insights into the interaction between ultrasound and argon flow schemes.

Due to the high temperature and opacity of metal melts, as well as the limitations of current characterization methods, it is very difficult to directly observe the bubble behavior in metals and alloys melts in situ and in real time. Various numerical methods have been applied to simulate bubble dynamics, e.g. the boundary element (BEM) method [29], [30], volume-of-fluid (VOF) method [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], level set (LS) method [36], front-tracking (FT) method [37] and Lattice Boltzmann (LB) method [38]. Among these, the VOF method is well-suited to simulate gas–liquid interfaces with large deformations due to its mass conservation properties, although it is less accurate than FT or LS methods. Among various numerical methods for simulating bubble dynamics, the combined level set and volume-of-fluid CLSVOF method stands out due to its ability to accurately model gas–liquid interfaces with large deformations [39], [40]. This method combines the advantages of both the VOF and level set approaches, providing a robust framework for our simulations. Compared to the BEM and VOF method, the CLSVOF approach offers enhanced accuracy in capturing complex bubble dynamics while addressing the limitations observed in other methods.

To address these challenges, we developed the simulation and water experimental validation that allows for directly visualizing the bubbling regime under different argon flow schemes. Based on similarity theory with incompressible Navier-Stokes (NS) equations, we utilized the CLSVOF method (detailed in Section 3) to simulate the bubbling regime. Model validation was achieved by comparing computational results with high-speed photographs in water experiments. The influence of ultrasound on the bubbling regime of different flow paths was also studied. Meanwhile, The UAA treatments for different argon flow schemes were applied to the TiB2/2024 Al composite to implore the related degassing mechanisms. Additionally, synchrotron radiation X-ray computed tomography (SR-CT) was employed to investigate pore distribution and its relationship with second phases as well as particle clusters. The paper concluded with an analysis of the mechanism behind ultrasonic-assisted argon treatment for hydrogen removal.

Experimental methodology

The experiments described in this work can be divided into two main parts: (1) For the UAA treatment in the water, the bubbling regimes for three UAA schemes were observed and visualized via a high-speed camera; (2) Previous research [41], [42] has demonstrated that water closely resembles the bubble characteristics and collapse behavior of Al melt, making it the most suitable transparent medium for simulating Al melt. Therefore, the three UAA schemes were applied to the TiB2/2024 Al composite to investigate the related degassing mechanisms.

Water simulation experiment

Fig. 1 shows the schematic of the experimental apparatus for water simulation experiments, including a transparent tank, an argon gas cylinder, an ultrasonic system, a light source, and a high-speed camera. In detail, the ultrasonic system consisted of a 20 kHz ultrasonic generator, a water-cooled piezoelectric lead zirconate titanate (PZT) crystal converter, and a cylindrical boron nitride sonotrode with a diameter of 50 mm. The water was contained in a rectangular transparent tank with dimensions of 120 mm × 200 mm, filled to a height of ∼ 80 mm. Sonotrode was submerged vertically 30 mm underneath the water surface via the slide bar and the ultrasonic output power was adjusted to 300 W.

Experiment in melt and characterization methods

Following the water simulation experiment, UAA treatment was applied to TiB2/2024 Al composite (chemical composition: Cu 4.1 wt%, Mg 1.5 wt%, Mn 0.57 wt%, TiB2 5 wt%, Al Bal.). Details of the preparation and analysis of the TiB2/Al composite could be found in our previous research [43], [44].

The composite was melted in a graphite crucible using a direct electrical resistance furnace at 800 ℃. After melting, the slag was removed, and the melt was vacuum degassed at 750 ℃ for 10 min. High-purity argon gas (≥99.999 %) was maintained at the flow rate of 1 L/min through the organic glass rotameter. The UAA treatment for the composite melt was applied in three schemes to control the argon gas flow paths. In each flow path, the experiment was conducted for the durations of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min. Finally, the melt was cast into an iron crucible under vacuum for macrostructure and relative density measurement, and into a copper mold for solid-state hydrogen measurement and microstructure.

Synchrotron radiation X-ray computed tomography (SR-CT) experiments were conducted at beamline BL13HB of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). A needle-shaped specimen, measuring 0.7 mm in diameter and 10 mm in length, was sectioned from copper mold ingots by electro discharge machining. The specimen was mounted upright with its vertical axes perpendicular to the monochromatic X-ray beam. A focused beam of 28 keV X-rays passed through the specimens. Cameras equipped with charge-coupled devices (CCDs) recorded the transmitted beam with a spatial resolution of 0.325 µm, positioned 50 cm behind the specimen. Image contrast was achieved through absorbing contrast (sensitive to density differences) and phase contrast (sensitive to internal structure changes) [45], [46]. As the specimens rotated about the vertical axis, CCDs captured frames every 2 s. A total of 2048 images were collected to reconstruct the 3D structure of the specimens. PITRE software was used to slice a total of 2048 collected images, which were then reconstructed into a three-dimensional (3D) structure using Dragonfly software. This process produced a detailed 3D morphology of pores and particle clusters inside the composite.

The movement of the contact line was numerically managed by enforcing a static contact angle (θ0). This was achieved through the boundary condition: ∂ϕ/∂z=-cosθ0, which dictated the contact line behavior. It was assumed that the contact angle remains (θ = θ0), regardless of the contact line’s velocity and direction of movement. If θ < θ0, the contact line (bubble base) would extend outward until the static contact angle (θ = θ0) was reached. Based on high-speed camera images (Fig. 3b), the contact angle of the wetted (argon-water-boron nitride) wall was set to 135°.

Results and discussion

Numerical simulation and experimental validation in water situation

Numerical simulation

Numerical simulations were conducted to investigate how various argon flow schemes (EV-EC, IV-EC, and IV-RC) affect the behavior of bubbles at a constant argon flow rate. The simulations result at several characteristic time of EV-EC, IV-EC, and IV-RC were depicted in Fig. 4.

Simulated bubbling regimes for argon flow path of (a) EV-EC; (b)IV-EC, and (c)IV-RC.

In the case of EV-EC (Fig. 4a), bubbles were observed to rise individually from the gas inlet and then emerge from the water surface until they reached the bottom of the sonotrode. This sequential and discrete bubble ascent was clearly captured in the simulations.

The IV-EC configuration exhibited the formation of a large and continuous bubble beneath the sonotrode, as shown in Fig. 4b. This bubble expanded outward until it reached the edge of the sonotrode and then emerged at the water surface.

In the IV-RC scheme, a significant gas–liquid mixing zone was observed in Fig. 4c. Bubbles merged and interacted extensively, creating a turbulent and dynamic interface. The simulations reflected this complex behavior, showing a pronounced mixing zone that enhanced bubble interactions in the water.

Experimental validation

To verify the correctness of the simulation results, we compared the bubbling regime taken using a high-speed camera with the simulation results. At the same time, we also studied the bubble behavior under ultrasound, further investigating the interaction mechanism between ultrasound and argon bubbles.

Water experiment results for the EV-EC scheme, both with the non-ultrasonic treatment (NU) and ultrasonic treatment (UT), were shown in Fig. 5. At 30 ms, bubbles with a diameter of approximately 1 cm formed at the gas inlet (Fig. 5a), similar to the simulations in Fig. 4a. Bubbles exhibited a necking area at their base, and their shape aligned well with the simulation results, confirming the accuracy of the model in predicting bubble shapes. After detaching from the inlet, the bubbles rose towards the bottom of the sonotrode, forming a convex shape with a height of about 0.7 cm. This behavior was highly consistent with the simulation results at 50 ms in Fig. 4a. While the full simulation sequence of bubble evolution up to detachment has been explored in detail elsewhere [37], it will not be elaborated upon here. The bubbles continued to rise vertically (Fig. 5b), reaching the bottom edge of the sonotrode and then escaping to the water surface at 92 ms (Fig. 5c). The time required for the bubbles to detach from the inlet and make contact with the sonotrode was approximately 60 ms, which was in agreement with the simulation results. This consistency between the experimental observations and the simulated predictions underscored the accuracy of the model in forecasting bubbling regime.

Water experiments of EV-EC at different time: (a-c) NU; (d-f) UT.

In both NU and UT conditions, the bubbles emerged sequentially from the inlet and rose to the end surface of sonotrode. However, there were notable differences in their behavior: bubbles in the NU condition had smooth surfaces, while those in the UT condition displayed constant vibration and unevenness (Fig. 5d). Additionally, bubbles in the UT condition were deflected from the center of the sonotrode (Fig. 5e) and the rise rate was slower (Fig. 5f). The uneven surface of bubbles in the UT condition resulted from the ultrasonic pressure field in the liquid. The bubble surfaces expanded in areas of rarefaction and contracted in regions of positive pressure, causing continuous vibration. Moreover, the downward ultrasonic streaming from the center of the sonotrode caused the bubbles to deviate from the central axis, resulting in a slower rise velocity.

In the water experiments for IV-EC in NU condition, as depicted in Figs. 6(a-c) the central bubble grew into a larger bubble with a height of approximately 0.35 cm before reaching the edge of the sonotrode and escaping through the outlet (Fig. 6c). This behavior was consistent with the simulated results (Fig. 4b). The experimental observation showed that the bubble took approximately 350 ms to emerge from the inlet and escape from the sonotrode edge. It was shorter than the duration predicted by the simulation (Fig. 4b). This discrepancy might be attributed to disturbances or the sonotrode not being perfectly horizontal, which could have caused the bubble to escape earlier from a specific point on the end surface of sonotrode.

Water experiments of IV-EC at different time: (a-c) NU; (d-f) UT.

When the ultrasonic device was on, the bubbles become less spherical and exhibited intense surface vibrations, as illustrated in Fig. 6d. Ultrasonic streaming drove the bubbles to migrate away from the center of the sonotrode (Fig. 6e). The downward acoustic streaming beneath the sonotrode not only disrupted the ascending bubbles but also delays their ascent to the surface, which could enhance the degassing process in the Al melt. Additionally, cavities formed by the merging of cavitation bubbles were present at the bottom of the sonotrode. These cavities also contributed to the degassing process in the melt. As the bubble approaches the edge, it makes contact and escapes through this point, forming sharp tips, as depicted in Fig. 6f.

In contrast to EV-EC and IV-EC, IV-RC exhibited more vigorous bubble motion and generated numerous tiny bubbles beneath the sonotrode, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The creation of these tiny argon bubbles complicated the precise detection of their emergence from the inlet and escape. The argon flow in IV-RC scheme split when the gas was sprayed downwards, generating a multitude of small bubbles around the main bubble and forming a conical bubble structure (CBS), as shown in Fig. 7b. Then, the bubble zone in IV-RC scheme developed into a deep bubbling CBS regime (Fig. 7c) with many tiny argon bubbles at the tip. This observation was consistent with the simulation results (Fig. 4c), which showed a high density of dynamic interface in the gas–liquid mixing zone.

Water experiments of IV-RC at different time: (a-c) NU; (d-f) UT.

The observation indicated that under ultrasonic vibration, bubbles continually broke apart, producing more tiny dispersed bubbles and leading to a more chaotic bubbling regime (Fig. 7e). Consequently, the ultrasonic field further dispersed the bubbles within the liquid, causing them to oscillate and float freely (Fig. 7f). Power ultrasound induced ultrasonic cavitation broke up small argon bubbles into numerous tiny ones. The simultaneous generation of microjets and shock waves increased the depth and range of bubble activity. Additionally, the downward ultrasonic streaming enlarged the bubbling regime and extended the residence time of argon bubbles in the liquid, both of which contribute to improved hydrogen gas removal efficiency.

Thickness of gas–liquid mixing zone

From the above comparison, the simulation results aligned well with the bubble behaviors obtained from experimental high-speed photography using high-speed cameras. The variations in bubble behavior among the three argon flow paths (EV-EC, IV-EC, and IV-RC) significantly affected the degassing efficiency of metal melts. The effectiveness of argon degassing depended on several factors: the retention time of argon gas in the melt, the size of the bubbles, and the degree of bubble dispersion. Longer retention time allowed argon bubbles to absorb more hydrogen due to lower hydrogen partial pressure. Smaller bubbles absorbed hydrogen more efficiently due to their increased surface area. Meanwhile, greater bubble dispersion enhanceed the overall degassing effect by expanding the interaction range within the melt. These factors were reflected in the finite element simulation results, which showed bubble dwell time and the extent of the gas–liquid mixing zone. A larger gas–liquid mixing zone generally led to better degassing efficiency.

To better understand these effects, we analyzed the phase field distribution along the symmetry axis at the final moment of Fig. 4, as shown in Fig. 8a. The phase distributions for EV-EC and IV-EC (Figs. 8(b-c)) revealed similar thicknesses of the gas–liquid mixing zone of approximately 0.3 cm. Notably, the gas–liquid mixing zone in IV-RC was considerably larger (Fig. 8d), as evidenced by water simulation results. This indicated a significant presence of tiny bubbles underneath the sonotrode. This extensive mixing zone contributed to the improved degassing efficiency. Under the guidance of numerical simulation, designing ultrasonic welding electrodes with multiple optimal channel diameters could further improve the degassing effectiveness of UAA treatment in casting.

Degassing Efficiency in composite melt

Relative density and hydrogen content

To evaluate the degassing effectiveness of the three argon flow schemes, we subjected TiB2/2024 Al composite subject to UAA treatment for durations ranging from 0 to 10 min and compared these with a control group that received ultrasound treatment without argon gas (UT-NA). Fig. 9a shows the relative density of the ingots for each treatment: UT-NA, EV-EC, IV-EC, and IV-RC. Evidently, the relative density increased with treatment time for all schemes. Among these groups, the UT-NA sample shows the lowest relative density growth. Both EV-EC and IV-EC samples show similar improvements, with IV-RC sample achieving a slightly higher relative density of 96.99 % due to its faster initial degassing effect. In contrast, IV-RC demonstrates the highest effectiveness, achieving a maximum relative density of 98.01 %.

(a) Relative density and (b) hydrogen content of ingots as a function of degassing time for UT, EV-EC, IV-EC, and IV-RC samples.

Fig. 9b illustrates the hydrogen content measured in solid-state for each treatment for durations ranging from 0 to 10 min. Prior to UAA treatment, all samples underwent 10 min of vacuum degassing, resulting in a starting hydrogen content of 0.142 ppm. The ultrasonic treatment further reduced hydrogen content, with the effectiveness varying across the schemes. The decrease in hydrogen content was minimal for UT-NA sample, while IV-RC sample proves the most effective, reducing hydrogen levels to a minimum of 0.0235 ppm. which was significant reduction in hydrogen levels compared to traditional ultrasonic methods. The hydrogen removal efficiencies in EV-EC and IV-EC samples were similar, with IV-RC sample showing slightly superior performance. After 10 min of degassing, the hydrogen removal efficiencies for the UT-NA, EV-EC, IV-EC, and IV-RC schemes are 29.2 %, 64.7 %, 69.9 %, and 83.5 %, respectively. This aligned with the simulation results, where IV-EC sample was slightly more effective than EV-EC, likely due to the longer retention time of argon gas in the liquid despite similar gas–liquid mixing zone thicknesses. It is worth noting that samples for relative density measurement and macrostructure observation were solidified under vacuum solidification, whereas samples for solid-state hydrogen measurement were solidified in a specially customized copper mold under normal pressure. As a result, the relative density may still lower than 98 % even when the hydrogen content is very low.

To clearly and intuitively show the degassing results, the macroscopic microstructure of the vertical bottom surface of ingots were examined after UAA treatment in IV-RC scheme over a period of 0 to 10 min using vacuum solidification, as shown in Fig. 10. Initially, the untreated sample exhibited several large pores, approximately 1 cm in size (Fig. 10a). After applying UAA treatment through IV-RC scheme, these pores progressively diminished in size Figs. 10(a-d). At 6 min of UAA treatment of IV-RC scheme, only a few small pores were visible at the edges of the ingot (illustrated in Figs. 10(d-f)). By 8 min, the pores were extensively reduced (Fig. 10e). After 10 min, the ingot appears free of visible pores (Fig. 10f). It was confirmed that UAA treatment employed IV-RC scheme significantly improves degassing efficiency, achieving a high level of pore reduction in Al-based composite materials that are challenging to degas.

Pores distribution

Based on the above results, it is clear that IV-RC was the most effective gas flow scheme for UAA treatment among the three experimental schemes. Fig. 11 shows the SR-CT analysis on samples treated for 0 and 10 min in case of IV-RC. Initially, pores were predominantly associated with second-phase and particle clusters (Fig. 11a), forming complex networks through gaps between these clusters (Fig. 11b). The pores were interconnected and some of them exceeded 1 μm in size (Fig. 11c).

SR–CT and SEM micrographs of porosity in IV-RC and control samples under chill cast condition: (a–c) control sample: (a) Relationship between pores and clusters, (b) Pores shape and connectivity, (c) Spatial distribution of pores; (d–f) IV-RC sample treated for 10 min: (d) Relationship between pores and clusters, (e) Pores shape and connection relationship, (f) Spatial distribution of pores; (g) Volume fraction of pores with average size divided by 1 μm; (h) SEM image of pores in control sample; (i) SEM image of pores in IV-RC sample treated for 10 min.

After 10 min UAA treatment, pores were more uniformly dispersed throughout the matrix (Fig. 11d). Upon magnification, the pores appeared nearly spherical and lacked interconnections (Figs. 11(d-e)). The volume fraction of pores was decreased significantly from 0.922 % to 0.129 % (Fig. 11 g), with a notable decrease in pores larger than 1 μm. While larger pores were not entirely eliminated, their content was greatly reduced. This was further evidenced by SEM images: the control sample displayed large pores associated with particle clusters (Fig. 11 h), whereas the IV-RC sample showed only a few small pores (Fig. 11i).

Compared with the traditional ultrasound [47], [48] or argon degassing methods [49] alone, the UAA treatment with IV-RC regimen significantly reduced hydrogen levels, up to 0.0235 ppm. Therefore, it is necessary to further analyze the mechanism by which UAA treatment significantly improves refinement efficiency.

Bubble dynamics simulation

From Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, it was evident that in EV-EC and IV-RC schemes, the ultrasound field primarily caused surface vibrations of the bubbles without generating new small bubbles. In contrast, the IV-RC process effectively produced numerous tiny bubbles due to ultrasonic cavitation. In order to investigate the responses of argon bubbles of different sizes on ultrasonic cavitation, we developed argon bubble dynamics simulation to verify the effect of ultrasound on argon bubbles of different sizes.

Efforts have been made to model bubble dynamics and elucidate the phenomenon of acoustic cavitation [50]. Due to the complexity of cavitation bubble wall motion, solving the governing equations is challenging, necessitating various simplifications [51]. Researchers have developed a significant model to calculate the pressure during the collapse of a spherical cavity, which neglects fluid compressibility [52]. Plesset subsequently extended this model to the Rayleigh-Plesset framework, incorporating modifications for fluid compressibility, viscosity, and surface tension [53].

The Rayleigh-Plesset model was used to incorporate various parameters and damping factors that influence the motion of the bubble wall. It was assumed that the bubble remains spherical and that the gas within was modeled as an ideal gas. Based on this framework, numerical simulations were conducted to investigate the behavior of the bubble under experimental conditions, with a particular emphasis on the impact of acoustic pressure on bubble wall motion across different radii.

The model is illustrated in Fig. 12a, considering an argon bubble immersed in a liquid subjected to a uniform ultrasound field. As shown in Fig. 12b, during the rarefaction phase, the bubble rapidly expands as the resultant force at the bubble wall directs outward from the melt. Conversely, during the compression phase, the bubble experiences significant shrinkage due to the opposing direction of the resultant force. Importantly, the bubble's radial dynamics are analyzed under the assumption that it is situated far from any obstacles or boundaries that could disrupt the spherical symmetry of the flow.

From Fig. 12c, it is evident that the response of argon bubbles of varying sizes in water differs under ultrasonic field. As the size of the argon bubble increases, the response intensity of acoustic cavitation effect gradually decreases. When the radius of the argon bubble is 2 μm, the maximum bubble amplitude can reach 59.8. During the compression half-cycle of the ultrasound, the bubble experiences significant compression, resulting in a substantial compression ratio that leads to strong transient cavitation. This process can cause a bubble to collapse into numerous tiny bubbles [54]. Under the extreme pressure and temperature generated by the collapse of the cavitation bubble, these tiny bubbles generated by the collapse can be propelled at high velocity [55] to other locations in the water, consistent with the phenomena observed in Figs. 7(d-f).

When the radius of the argon bubbles increases to 20 μm, the maximum bubble amplitude rapidly decreases to 7.1, and the intensity of cavitation event is significantly weakened. Further increasing the radius to 200 μm results in stable non-linear and linear cavitation prevailing over transisent behavior. In this scenario, the bubble cannot expand rapidly or compress effectively under the influence of ultrasound pressure, and instead, it only oscillates as the ultrasound propagates in the liquid.

Due to variations in ultrasound pressure at different locations during the propagation of ultrasonic waves (assumed to be a sine wave, as shown in Fig. 12b), the ultrasound pressure fluctuates periodically. Consequently, the uneven ultrasound pressure across the surface of the larger bubble causes it to appear irregular and constantly oscillating, as illustrated in Figs. 5(d-e) and 6(d-e). Specifically, when the bubble surface is located in rarefaction half-cycle of ultrasound, it protrudes outward, whereas in compression half-cycle of ultrasound, it becomes concave. Thus, as ultrasonic waves propagate, the surface exhibits an uneven and continually oscillating behavior.

Observing the response of argon bubbles of different sizes in Al melt, as shown in Fig. 12d, reveals a similar trend to that in water; however, the compression ratio of argon bubbles of the same size in Al melt is lower than in water, and the transient cavitation effect is less pronounced. When the radius of the argon bubbles increases from 2 μm to 20 μm, the maximum amplitude decreases from 20.8 to 5.9, with the attenuation being less significant than in water. Notably, at a radius of 20 μm, the compression ratios in both water and Al melt are comparable. This observation suggests that the threshold for transitioning from transient to steady-state cavitation is lower in water than in Al melt.

Furthermore, during the oscillation phase prior to rapid growth of argon bubble, the oscillation of argon bubbles in water are relatively subtle, whereas the oscillation of bubbles in Al melt is much more pronounced. This disparity is attributed to the micro Reynolds number of Al melt being two orders of magnitude higher than that of water [42], resulting in a more noticeable oscillation of bubble in the Al melt. When the compression ratio falls below 5, the cavitation effect becomes relatively weak. Consequently, it can be concluded that ultrasonic cavitation is effective primarily in breaking smaller bubbles in both water and Al melt. For bubbles larger than 40 μm, the cavitation effect is significantly diminished, and for millimeter-sized bubbles, only steady-state cavitation occurs.

Degssing mechanism analysis

This difference arises because ultrasound can only break small bubbles, causing them to collapse and form a multitude of tiny bubbles, as illustrated in Fig. 13a. For larger bubbles, ultrasonic pressure mainly causes surface vibrations and results in aggregation or expansion before they emerge, with minimal impact on their overall behavior. Conversely, small bubbles are sufficiently affected by ultrasonic pressure to collapse into numerous tiny bubbles [54]. These tiny bubbles significantly increase the specific surface area and enhance the efficiency of argon bubbles in extracting hydrogen from the melt due to the hydrogen partial pressure difference. Additionally, the micro-jet and shock waves [55] generated by bubble collapse enlarge the bubbling regime and increase bubble velocity, further boosting degassing efficiency in IV-RC.

Emergence mode with different initial radius under ultrasonic treatment: (a) Acoustic cavitation effect; (b) Acoustic streaming effect.

The downward acoustic streaming (Fig. 13b) beneath the sonotrode affects the behavior of bubble movement. For, large bubbles, the streaming primarily reduces their rising speed, causing them to ascend away from the center of the sonotrode (Fig. 5e) and slightly increasing their residence time. In contrast, for tiny bubbles, the streaming alters their motion direction, creating vortices that disperse the bubbles throughout the liquid. The enhanced dispersion increases the retention time of argon bubbles in the melt, thereby further improving degassing efficiency.

Al-based PRMMCs present a greater challenge for degassing compared to Al alloys. This challenge stems from the presence of TiB2 particles in the melt, which are often featured as growth steps and grooves on their surfaces. As shown in Fig. 14a, TiB2 particles are hexagonal prisms with multiple growth steps, with numerous nanoparticles adsorbed on the surface due to van der Waals forces. During the ultrasonic treatment process, the micro jets and shock waves generated by acoustic cavitation can significantly reduce the wetting angle between particles and matrix [56], [57], thereby promoting the escape of hydrogen trapped inside the corners of these growth steps (Fig. 14b) and interparticle gap (Fig. 14c). This reduction induced by ultrasonic cavitation can reduce the storage of hydrogen around particles and enhance the wettability between particles and matrix [58], [59].

Conclusion

This work systematically and comprehensively investigated the effects of ultrasonic-assisted argon (UAA) treatment on bubble behavior in water and degassing efficiency in TiB2/2024 Al composite. The research employed numerical simulations using the CLSVOF method and experimental validation to explore the influence of various argon flow schemes on the bubbling regime. The results have practical implications for optimizing UAA treatment in the casting of metals and alloys. The following conclusions can be drawn:

(1)The simulation results well reflect the bubbling regime for different argon flow paths in the UAA process. Both experimental and finite element simulations have demonstrated that ultrasonic argon treatment with a reducing channel (IV-RC) is the most effective approach. The simulations and water experiments revealed that IV-RC achieved superior bubble dispersion characteristics and a broader bubbling range.

(2)Extending the UAA treatment time improves hydrogen removal efficiency. After 10 min of IV-RC, the hydrogen content is decreased from 0.142 ppm to 0.0235 ppm, while the relative density of the composite increased from 75.78 % to 98.01 % under vacuum solidification.
