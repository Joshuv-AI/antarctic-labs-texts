Dynamic changes of volatile compounds during coffee-grounds beer fermentation with different yeast strains: An integrated HS-SPME-GC/MS, electronic tongue and electronic nose analysis
Authors: Jingan Yang, Jinyu Zhao, Jiashun Jiang, Dapeng Lu, Ye Lv, Lijing Liu, Hong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a major coffee processing byproduct, spent coffee grounds (SCG) contain abundant bioactive compounds despite being typically discarded. This study analyzed flavor changes in coffee-grounds beer fermented with four yeast strains (Lalvin R2™ (R),Lalvin EC1118™ (E)，Lalvin ICVD254R(D)，Uvaferm43R(U)) through an integrated multi-omics approach. Electronic tongue and nose analyses revealed distinct bitter, umami, and sulfurous notes, with Strain E showed the most balanced sensory profile. HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis detected 460 volatile compounds, showing increasing trends during fermentation. Nine key aroma-active compounds (ROAV>1) were characterized, including ethyl butyrate, guaiacol, and 2,3-diethyl-5-methylpyrazine. Notably, guaiacol contribution decreased markedly in late fermentation with strains U and D, may indicate potential time-strain synergistic effects. Metabolic trajectory analysis revealed distinct pathways among the yeast strains: Strain R favored phenolic-ester-heterocyclic compounds, Strain E predominantly produced ester‑sulfur-heterocyclics, Strain U formed aromatic aldehydes via phenylalanine metabolism, and Strain D showed acid-ester‑sulfur conversion.Yeast selection and fermentation duration significantly influenced physicochemical properties. These findings provide new insights for valorizing coffee processing byproducts.Keywords: Coffee-grounds beer, Physicochemical properties, Sensory profiles, Volatile compounds, Diverse yeast strains

Introduction

Beer ranks as the third most consumed beverage globally, following only water and tea, and leads the alcoholic beverage market (Marques et al., 2022). The craft beer sector has expanded rapidly, driven by demand for distinctive flavors (Arya et al., 2021). Coffee production generates substantial waste, primarily SCG, which contains valuable nutrients like hemicellulose (39.10 %), lignin (23.90 %), protein (17.44 %), cellulose (12.40 %), and lipids (2.29 %) (dos Santos et al., 2021). It is noteworthy that chlorogenic acid (3.2–8.7 mg/g) and caffeine (0.8–2.4 mg/g) present in SCG may contribute to beer flavor formation (Bouhzam et al., 2023). Recent studies propose SCG's use in sustainable biorefineries, employing processes like extraction, hydrolysis, and pyrolysis to produce biofuels such as biogas, biodiesel, and bioethanol (Rajesh Banu et al., 2020). Applications in composting and soil amendment have been demonstrated as successful. The sustainable utilization of SCG supports environmentally friendly production while enhancing economic value (Cruz et al., 2013; Cruz et al., 2014). SCG also provides functional components such as dietary fiber and polyphenols. Sustainable utilization of SCG supports eco-friendly production while enhancing economic value.

Recent studies highlight SCG's potential in food applications. Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2023) demonstrated its use in novel alcoholic beverages using Lachancea thermotolerans and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum. Similarly, Milinčić et al. (Milinčić et al., 2024) incorporated grape seed flour into beer, improving phenolic content and sensory properties. Jiang et al. (Jiang, Yang, et al., 2025) applied SCG in craft beer fermentation, analyzing metabolic changes via HS-SPME-GC/MS to optimize product quality. These findings establish a theoretical framework for quality improvement of SCG-infused craft beers. Collectively, these studies confirm the technical feasibility of utilizing coffee byproducts in brewing applications. More importantly, they reveal SCG's remarkable capacity to impart distinctive sensory characteristics to final products. This research opens new possibilities for value-added utilization of waste materials and product innovation in the craft beer industry.

The sensory profile of beer is fundamentally shaped by four key elements: malt/adjunct selection, hop varieties, water quality, and yeast metabolism. Crucially, yeast metabolism not only converts wort sugars into ethanol and CO₂ but also synthesizes flavor-active compounds including higher alcohols, esters, and volatile phenols, creating complex flavor profiles with fruity, floral, and spicy notes (Gonzalez & Morales, 2017). For instance, the Ehrlich pathway generates higher alcohols (e.g., isoamyl alcohol), while ester biosynthesis (e.g., ethyl acetate) is mediated by alcohol acetyltransferase (ATF1). These pathways exhibit strain-specific variations that directly influence the flavor intensity and complexity of the final product (Kumar Verma et al., 2021). Notably, phenolic compounds (e.g., guaiacol) originate from phenylalanine metabolism, whereas sulfur-containing volatiles (e.g., dimethyl trisulfide) are associated with methionine degradation. Such metabolic divergence underscores the critical role of strain selection in aroma modulation. However, the brewing industry's predominant reliance on genetically limited yeast strains (Borneman et al., 2016) substantially constrains flavor innovation through metabolic engineering approaches. Notably, significant metabolic variations exist among yeast strains, directly determining the intensity, complexity, and uniqueness of final flavor profiles. However, few studies track volatile compound changes during Coffee-Grounds Beer fermentation, especially how different yeast strains affect flavor development. While Jiang et al. (Jiang, Yang, et al., 2025) explored coffee grounds' brewing applications, their work focused primarily on static flavor characterization and single-strain fermentations, lacking temporal tracking of volatile compounds throughout the fermentation process.

Moreover, the brewing industry's reliance on genetically similar yeast strains with limited diversity has become a bottleneck for flavor innovation (Langdon et al., 2019). This constraint is particularly pronounced in coffee-grounds beer, as the interactions between coffee grounds' unique bioactive matrix (including polyphenols and dietary fibers) and yeast metabolism remain incompletely understood. Consequently, exploring more diverse yeast strains becomes imperative to fully exploit coffee grounds' potential as functional brewing adjuncts—different strains may metabolize coffee components in distinct ways, potentially yielding novel and desirable flavor profiles.

Building upon prior research, this study investigated how yeast metabolic variations regulate the dynamic changes of flavor compounds during Coffee-Grounds Beer fermentation. Based on the hypothesis that “strain-specific metabolic pathway modifications may differentially transform SCG bioactive components and generate distinct flavor profiles,” we systematically examined compound release patterns and flavor formation mechanisms in Coffee-Grounds Beer fermented with different yeast strains, employing electronic sensory analysis coupled with HS-SPME-GC–MS technology. By analyzing physicochemical data, this approach improves understanding of flavor formation, overcoming limitations in traditional brewing research. The predictive models enable precise brewing of SCG-based beers by optimizing yeast selection.

Materials and methods

Preparation of samples

SCG used in this study were provided by Tongsantai Agricultural Development Co., Ltd. (Tengchong, Yunnan Province, China). The preparation protocol was as follows: Coffee beans of the Catimor variety were medium-roasted at 190–200 °C, ground to a particle size of 0.4–0.6 mm, and then extracted using an espresso machine to obtain coffee grounds. The resulting SCG was subsequently dried in a convection oven at 60 °C for 48 h. Wheat malt, Australian malt, hops, and four yeast strains were procured from Diboshi Homebrewing Equipment Co., Ltd. (Yantai, Shandong Province, China). This study investigated four commercial yeast strains for craft beer fermentation: Saccharomyces cerevisiae 117 Lalvin R2™ (designated Strain R, with enhanced ester production), Lalvin EC1118™ (Strain E, showing high attenuation), Lalvin ICV D254R (Strain D, exhibiting high-gravity wort tolerance), and Torulaspora delbrueckii CBS 1146 Uvaferm 43R (Strain U, generating complex aromas). Though selected for winemaking, lab tests (wort assimilation, fermentation performance, and volatile analysis) showed these yeasts were better suited for specialty beer. These non-conventional yeasts produced more fruity esters and complex phenolics than traditional brewing yeasts. All strains were sourced from DiBo Fermentation Technology Co., Ltd. (Yantai, Shandong, China)(The four yeast strains were genetically authenticated through ITS sequence analysis, with complete identification data provided in Supplementary Material 2.). This research establishes a new paradigm for applying wine yeasts in beer brewing, expanding microbial resources for innovative craft beer development. The wort was fermented in a temperature-controlled vessel at 20 °C for 15 days. Samples were collected at 5-day intervals (days 0, 5, 10, and 15) and labeled according to the yeast strain and fermentation time: R0, R5, R10, R15 for Strain R; E0, E5, E10, E15 for Strain E; U0, U5, U10, U15 for Strain U; and D0, D5, D10, D15 for Strain D. The experimental design consisted of 16 sample groups, each with three biological replicates, resulting in a total of 48 samples.

The process flow is illustrated in Fig. 1. The detailed procedure was as follows: Australian malt and wheat malt were precisely blended at a ratio of 5:3 (w/w), thoroughly homogenized, and milled for 3 min using a laboratory-scale grinder (Model: 1000 A, Yongkang Hongtaiyang Electromechanical Co., Ltd., Jinhua, Zhejiang, China). Coffee grounds (40 mesh) were added at 20 % of the total malt weight, along with 0.4 % amylase (relative to malt weight), and a mash-to-water ratio of 1:4 was maintained. The saccharification process was initiated at 50 °C for 20 min, followed by a temperature increase to 52 °C (40 min), 65 °C (70 min), and 72 °C (10 min), before finally raising to 78 °C. Throughout heating, continuous stirring was applied at an average rate of 1 °C/min to ensure uniform temperature distribution. After saccharification, the mash was directly transferred to a filtration tank for wort separation. The filtered wort was boiled for 60 min, with hops added in three stages (total addition: 0.15 % of wort volume): 30 % at 5 min, 40 % at 30 min, and the remaining 30 % at 55 min. Heating was terminated at 60 min. The wort was then transferred to a whirlpool tank for 20 min sedimentation to remove hot trub, followed by cooling to approximately 20 °C via a plate heat exchanger before entering the fermentation vessel. Activated yeast was inoculated at 1 ‰ (w/w) of the boiled wort volume, and fermentation was conducted at 20 °C. Diacetyl concentration served as the key maturity indicator; fermentation was considered complete when levels stabilized below 0.1 mg/L after 15 days.

Physical and chemical determination

The physicochemical properties of coffee-grounds beer, including alcohol content, original wort concentration, real degree of fermentation, total acidity, pH, bitterness units, and diacetyl concentration, were determined in strict compliance with the Chinese National Standard (GB/T 4928–2008). Alcohol content was measured by distilling 100 mL of sample with 50 mL water, followed by density bottle analysis of the distillate. Original wort concentration and real degree of fermentation were calculated from the specific gravity of dealcoholized residue (adjusted to 100 g at 20 °C) and measured alcohol content. Total acidity was quantified via titration with 0.1 M NaOH to a phenolphthalein endpoint (pink coloration) after boiling 10 mL sample with 100 mL water. International Bitterness Units (IBU) were determined spectrophotometrically at 275 nm following isooctane extraction of acidified beer (10 °C) with 1-octanol as antifoam agent. Diacetyl analysis involved derivatization with o-phenylenediamine (10 g/L) and spectrophotometric measurement at 335 nm. Turbidity was directly measured using a PJZ-1 turbidimeter (Hangzhou Qiwei Instrument Co.), while pH was determined with a calibrated pH meter. All analyses were performed with three biological replicates (n = 3), and each measurement was conducted in triplicate. The relative standard deviation (RSD) for technical replicates was maintained below 5 %. Biological variability was evaluated using three independent fermentation batches per yeast strain. Inter-batch consistency was ensured through standardization of raw materials, equipment, and fermentation conditions across all batches. Certified reference materials and routine calibration checks were employed to verify instrument precision. To minimize operational variability, key parameters were strictly controlled, including distillation temperature (± 0.5 °C), centrifugation duration (10 min at 4000 ×g), and colorimetric conditions (20 ± 1 °C).

Determination of caffeine

Caffeine content was determined in accordance with the Chinese National Food Safety Standard GB 5009.139–2014. The caffeine standard solution was prepared following the standard procedure detailed in Supplementary Material 1. Sample pretreatment consisted of ultrasonic extraction at 40 °C for 5 min, followed by accurate weighing of 5 g of sample, dilution to 5 mL with water, addition of 0.5 g magnesium oxide (MgO), mixing, and filtration of the supernatant through a microporous membrane. HPLC was employed for qualitative and quantitative analysis of both standard working solutions and processed samples. Each sample solution was injected in triplicate (technical replicates). Spike recovery tests demonstrated intra- and inter-batch coefficients of variation (CVs) below 3 % and 5 %, respectively. Three biologically independent samples (n = 3) were analyzed.

Electronic tongue and electronic nose measurement

The analysis was conducted following the method of Zhu et al. (Zhu et al., 2020) with minor modifications. The German AIRSENSE PEN3 electronic nose was employed to analyze the aroma profile of T15 coffee grounds craft beer. Data were collected over 120 s, with triplicate samples (A1, A2, A3) for analysis. Basic taste attributes were evaluated using an INSENT SA402B electronic tongue, with a reference solution of tartaric acid and potassium chloride. Key steps included electrode cleaning, equilibration, and a 30 s detection phase. Parallel samples were labeled A1–A3 (see Supplementary Material 1 for detailed sensor protocols).

Sensory evaluation

The sensory analysis was conducted at the Sensory Analysis Laboratory of Yunnan Agricultural University in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki ethical guidelines. Prior to evaluation, all participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed about the study. Participation was voluntary with unrestricted withdrawal rights. Sixteen trained panelists (8 males, 8 females) with alcoholic beverage testing experience evaluated beer samples fermented with four yeast strains for 15 days. Samples were served in identical glasses labeled with random 3-digit codes. Evaluations focused on foam stability, color, aroma, and taste (scoring criteria in Table S1). Panelists rinsed with water between samples. Tests were performed under standardized lighting at 25 °C.

HS-SPME-GC/MS detection conditions

Analytical instruments: Gas chromatography mass spectrometry (Model: PAL RTC 120–8890-5977B, Agilent company, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (Jiang, Ouyang, et al., 2025). HS-SPME conditions: The oven temperature was set at 60 °C, with an adsorption time of 20 min and a desorption time of 2 min. For gas chromatography, a VF-WAXms column was used, with helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, and a programmed temperature ramp from 40 °C to 230 °C was applied. Mass spectrometry conditions included an EI ion source and a scanning range of m/z 50–500. System stability was verified with QC samples (see Supplementary Material 1). The QC sample was made by mixing all the test samples and processed in the same way as the formal sample. During the instrument detection process, three QC samples were dispersed and inserted. In data analysis, the repeatability of the QC samples can reflect the stability of the instrument throughout the analysis process. It can also be used to discover variables with large variations in the analysis system, ensuring the reliability of the results.

Data processing and annotation

All analyses were performed in triplicate, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), employing one-way ANOVA followed by multiple-range tests, where a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure with q < 0.05 as the significance threshold. For key volatile compounds, effect sizes were calculated to quantify the magnitude of inter-strain differences. Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares (PLS), were performed using SIMCA-P+ 14.0 software. The validation of these models included 7-fold cross-validation with 200 permutations to compute RMSE, Q2, and 95 % confidence intervals for partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) components. Differential metabolites were identified by screening the KEGG Pathway Database and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, https://hmdb.ca/), both accessed on April 18, 2025. The biological pathways associated with these differential metabolites were determined through metabolic pathway annotation in the KEGG database. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Python package SciPy (version 1.0.0), with the most relevant biological pathways identified via Fisher's exact test. Data visualization, including heatmaps and other graphical representations, was generated using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2.

Results and analysis

Terminal flavor profile analysis of coffee-grounds beer using Sensomics and intelligent analytical technologies

Sensory profiles were evaluated using radar charts for four key attributes of coffee-grounds beer quality: foam stability, color, aroma, and taste (Fig. 2A). Overall, the E yeast-fermented coffee-grounds beer demonstrated the most balanced sensory profile across all dimensions, followed by the D yeast-fermented sample, while R and U yeast-fermented beers exhibited less balanced sensory characteristics. GC–MS analysis of beers fermented with different yeasts for 15 days (Fig. 2B) revealed similar relative abundances of sensory flavor compounds, with sweetness being predominant, followed by fruity, green, and fatty notes. Electronic nose (e-nose) and electronic tongue (e-tongue) technologies proved valuable for rapid and accurate detection of volatile compounds and basic tastes (Poeta et al., 2023). The electronic nose radar plot (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table S2) revealed distinct response patterns among the different yeast strains, with particularly strong signals detected for W5S (nitrogen oxides), W1S (methane), W1W (sulfides), W2S (ethanol), and W2W (aromatics and organic sulfides) sensors. Notably, R\U-type yeast beers demonstrated the highest response intensity to W5S sensor (93.8710; 85.8260), while Strain E and Strain D showed predominant responses to W1W (88.1802, 64.9879, respectively). These characteristic response profiles clearly indicate the presence of substantial concentrations of nitrogen oxides (W5S), methane (W1S), sulfides (W1W), ethanol (W2S), and aromatic/organosulfur compounds (W2W) in all fermented samples, reflecting the complex volatile metabolite composition generated during the coffee-grounds beer fermentation process. The observed sensor-specific response patterns provide valuable insights into strain-dependent metabolic differences in volatile compound production. E-tongue analysis (Fig. 2D) detected pronounced bitterness, aftertaste-B, umami, richness, saltiness, and slight aftertaste-A in all beers, with three samples (excluding E) showing strong sourness. These flavor characteristics were primarily attributed to specific chemical components such as caffeine, amino acids, and minerals (Maicas, 2020).

Dynamics of volatile flavor substances during fermentation of coffee-grounds beers with different yeasts

Comprehensive characterization of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) by HS-SPME-GC/MS

The volatile aroma compounds in coffee-grounds beers fermented with different yeasts were analyzed using comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC × GC–MS) coupled with headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME). As shown in Fig. 3, the method effectively discriminated between coffee-grounds beer fermented with different yeasts, demonstrating its efficacy and reliability. Fig. 3A-D indicate that the most abundant compounds in coffee-beer fermentation with the four yeasts were esters (21.34 % to 22.96 %), heterocyclic organic compounds (22.31 % to 23.16 %), ketones (10.02 % to 10.58 %), and alcohols (9.26 % to 9.50 %), collectively accounting for over 50 % of the total volatile compounds in coffee beer, with similar trends observed across samples. As illustrated in Fig. 3A-D and Table S3, a total of 460 volatile flavor compounds were detected in the four yeast-fermented coffee-grounds beer, including 109 esters, 77 heterocyclic compounds, 56 ketones, 50 alcohols, 30 aldehydes, 25 hydrocarbons, 20 acids, 15 terpenes, 8 ethers, 6 phenols, 6 amides, 5 amines, 5 organic sulfur compounds, 1 nitrogen-containing organic compound, 1 halogenated hydrocarbon, and 46 other miscellaneous compounds. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 3E-H, the coffee-beer fermented with Strain R, Strain E, Strain U, and Strain D contained 404, 417, 400, and 387 shared volatile compounds, respectively, during fermentation. The total number of volatile compounds exhibited an increasing trend in all four yeast-fermented coffee-grounds beer, with D-yeast demonstrating the most significant increase. Notably, R-yeast produced 30 newly formed VOC with significantly increased concentrations compared to the initial fermentation stage (0 days). Among these, 12 compounds were also detected in other yeast groups (E, U, D) but at lower levels, while the remaining 18 were uniquely elevated in R-yeast. The fold changes for the shared compounds ranged from 1.5 to 2.8, and for the unique ones, from 2.1 to 4.3 (Supplementary Table S3 and S4). Meanwhile, yeast strains E, U, and D produced 22, 29, and 30 newly formed compounds, respectively.

It should be noted that the analytical results represent the combined volatile compounds derived from both malt fermentation and coffee grounds. The absence of a control group brewed without coffee grounds makes it difficult to definitively assign the origin of each individual compound. However, by comparing our data with the established volatile profiles of traditional beer produced using similar yeast strains, we can infer that certain compounds, such as guaiacol (imparting smoky notes) and 2-isobutyl-3-methoxypyrazine (imparting bell pepper notes), are recognized as key aroma substances in coffee but are not typically dominant in conventional malt beers (Chen et al., 2025; He et al., 2025). This indicates that the coffee grounds significantly contributed to the overall aromatic character.

Esters: These are characteristic flavor compounds derived from yeast metabolism during beer fermentation. With low sensory thresholds and distinct fruity aroma profiles, they play a crucial role in shaping the overall flavor of beer (Saison et al., 2008; Verbelen et al., 2008). Esters are formed from higher alcohols produced by yeast during fermentation via the Ehrlich pathway (catabolism) or amino acid metabolism (anabolism) (Hazelwood et al., 2008). In this study, key ester compounds including ethyl acetate and isoamyl acetate were detected across all four yeast strains (R, E, U, D) during fermentation, with their concentrations exhibiting significant variations. Notably, isoamyl acetate - synthesized through the catalytic action of yeast alcohol acetyltransferase (ATF1) - was characterized by its distinct banana and pear-like aromatic profile (Stribny et al., 2016). The relatively high concentrations of ethyl acetate, ethyl hexanoate, and ethyl octanoate demonstrate that the ester synthase activity of different yeast strains plays a pivotal role during fermentation, directly influencing the flavor profile of the beer (Roberts et al., 2023).

Ketones: These characteristic flavor compounds, generated through fatty acid β-oxidation and amino acid degradation during beer fermentation, exhibit distinct flavor profiles and low sensory thresholds, making significant contributions to beer flavor formation. In this study, key ketones including 2,3-butanedione and 3-hydroxy-2-butanone were detected across all four yeast strains (R, E, U, D), with their concentrations demonstrating dynamic changes throughout fermentation. Notably, 2,3-butanedione – produced via pyruvate metabolism - imparts characteristic creamy and buttery aroma notes (Szudera-Kończal et al., 2023). The elevated concentrations of long-chain methylketones—particularly 2-nonanone, 2-undecanone, and 2-tridecanone—demonstrate marked activity of the fatty acid β-oxidation pathway in yeast during beer fermentation (Yan et al., 2020), highlighting its crucial role in flavor compound biosynthesis. The study further revealed that ketone concentration dynamics during late fermentation (days 10–15) were strongly correlated with yeast reductase activity. This phenomenon was particularly pronounced in E-yeast, potentially associated with late-stage flavor modulation and beer maturation processes.

Alcohols: These characteristic flavor compounds are derived from carbohydrate metabolism and amino acid transformation during beer fermentation. Exhibiting high flavor activity and distinctive organoleptic properties, they make fundamental contributions to the overall flavor profile of beer (Willaert & Nedovic, 2006). The study identified key higher alcohols—particularly isoamyl alcohol and phenylethanol—across all four yeast strains (R, E, U, D), with their concentrations exhibiting distinct strain-specific variations. Isoamyl alcohol, synthesized via the Ehrlich pathway through leucine catabolism, demonstrated characteristic alcoholic and solvent-like aromatic notes (Pires et al., 2014). The elevated concentrations of short-chain alcohols—particularly ethanol, n-propanol, and isobutanol—demonstrate heightened activity of both glycolytic pathways and amino acid metabolism in yeast during beer fermentation, underscoring their determinant role in foundational flavor compound formation (Lin et al., 2024; Mendes et al., 2017). Furthermore, the accumulation of specific polyols (e.g., 2,3-butanediol) significantly enhanced beer mouthfeel roundness, with these compounds predominantly originating from yeast reductive metabolism during late-stage fermentation.

Given the significant influence of yeast strain on the volatile flavor profile, PLS-DA was employed to further elucidate these differences and identify the key compounds responsible for distinguishing the beers fermented with the four yeast strains.

Differential analysis of volatile flavor compounds in coffee-grounds beer fermented with different yeast strains

PCA, a dimensionality reduction technique that preserves maximal multivariate information (Boateng et al., 2021), was applied to assess global metabolic variation and sample heterogeneity across experimental groups (Fig. 4A). The results confirm excellent bioanalytical performance and data quality, with observed inter-group separation driven by genuine biological variation rather than analytical artifacts. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, all sample coordinates were distributed within the 95 % confidence interval. Principal components PC1, PC explained 66.7 % and 5.76 of variance respectively, achieving a cumulative contribution rate of 72.46 %. This demonstrates that these three principal components effectively captured the predominant characteristic features of coffee-grounds beer samples. QC samples formed tight clusters near the coordinate origin, indicating stable instrument performance and consistent experimental conditions during data acquisition, thereby ensuring data accuracy and reliability. The three-dimensional PCA score plot revealed significant distributional differences among the 18 sample groups (R0-R15, E0-E15, U0-U15, D0-D15, and QC) within the multivariate space. These findings provide visual validation for subsequent differential analysis and mechanistic investigations, while demonstrating the superior multidimensional resolution of PCA in deciphering complex omics datasets (Zhao & Wang, 2022). Furthermore, we investigated inter-group variations among the sixteen sample sets by constructing a PLS-DA model. Supervised PLS-DA further elucidated similarities and differences between groups, with the score plot demonstrating clear separation of all 16 sample groups (by yeast strain and fermentation time), exhibiting distribution trends consistent with PCA results. In PLS-DA modeling, R2Y and Q2 were employed to evaluate the model's explanatory and predictive capabilities respectively (Yan et al., 2021). A 200-permutation test was conducted to validate model robustness. As shown in Fig. 4C, the Q2 regression line intersecting the Y-axis below 0.05 confirms the model's reliability without overfitting (Tu et al., 2025), demonstrating strong predictive power and enhanced confidence in the PLS-DA model's validity. This statistically validated the metabolic differences among the 16 sample groups.

In multivariate analysis, compounds with Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores >1 and FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 are generally considered significant discriminators of intergroup differences. We initially characterized global metabolic variations through PCA and PLS-DA, followed by identification of differential metabolites using three criteria from OPLS-DA analysis: (i) VIP values, (ii) fold-change (FC) from univariate analysis, and (iii) p-values, with results visualized in VIP plots. Metabolites exhibiting FC ≥ 2 were deemed statistically significant. Applying thresholds of VIP ≥ 1.3 and P < 0.05 in OPLS-DA, we identified 432, 425, 421, and 427 flavor-active metabolites in coffee-grounds beers fermented by Strain R, Strain E, Strain U and Strain D, respectively (Fig. 4D, S2). As illustrated in Fig. 4D, distinct compound classes demonstrated differential patterns: phenolic and terpenoid compounds frequently exhibited elevated VIP scores with marked up/down-regulation, whereas common fatty acids and alcohols showed less pronounced VIP profiles despite concentration variations, suggesting limited discriminatory power. These strain-specific metabolic fingerprints likely are consistent with fundamental differences in yeast physiological performance.

The contribution of volatile compounds to tea aroma characteristics is intrinsically linked to both their concentration levels and corresponding odor perception thresholds (Yin et al., 2022). Compounds with relative odor activity values (ROAV) >1 are established as aroma-active constituents (Chen et al., 2023). This study detected nine volatile compounds with ROAV >1, including: ethyl butyrate, guaiacol, ethyl isobutyrate, dimethyl disulfide, 2,3-diethyl-5-methylpyrazine, 2-isobutyl-3-methoxypyrazine, damascenone, dimethyl trisulfide, and 3-ethylphenol. These compounds collectively form the fundamental aroma profile of coffee-grounds beer. As shown in Table 1, these nine compounds with ROAV >1 were identified as key aroma substances common to coffee-grounds beer fermented by all four yeast strains. The elevated concentrations of these aromatic compounds play a decisive role in shaping the distinctive aromatic characteristics of different coffee-grounds beers, thus being regarded as critical flavor determinants in yeast-specific fermentation processes. Damascenone showed the highest ROAV value of 100, indicating its predominant contribution to the coffee-grounds beer flavor profile, with aroma descriptors including apple, tobacco, rose, smoky, honey, and sweet apple-rose notes (Zhang et al., 2025). This was followed by 2-isobutyl-3-methoxypyrazine, which contributed peppery, bell pepper, green pepper, green, galbanum, earthy, pea-like, and spicy characteristics (Cronin & Stanton, 1976). In coffee-grounds beer fermented with Strain U and Strain D, guaiacol contributed smoky and spicy aromas (Huo et al., 2025). At appropriate concentrations, it enhances the complexity of the flavor profile, whereas excessive levels may lead to phenolic off-flavors. Notably, during the later stages of fermentation (beyond 10 days), guaiacol does not act as a key flavor contributor in these beers. In contrast, in beverages fermented with E and R yeast strains, this compound consistently remains a major contributor to the overall flavor throughout the entire fermentation process.

Correlation analysis of volatile flavors during fermentation of coffee-grounds beer with different yeasts

Metabolomic analysis of flavor compounds during Coffee-Grounds Beer fermentation revealed intricate temporal dynamics and distinct flavor variations among yeast strains, providing novel insights into flavor formation mechanisms. The metabolite clustering patterns (Fig. 5A, B) demonstrated characteristic temporal evolution, with samples from four yeast strains (R, E, U, D) forming distinct clusters according to fermentation stages (initial 0-day, mid-phase 5/10-day, and final 15-day). This cross-strain temporal clustering indicated that despite initial metabolic variations between yeast strains, the core fermentation process of Coffee-Grounds Beer follows a conserved biochemical pathway that transcends strain-specific differences. Significant separation of all coffee-grounds beer samples across the 15-day fermentation period confirmed substantial metabolic transformations during fermentation. While sharing similar overall trends, each yeast strain exhibited unique metabolic signatures, highlighting strain-dependent flavor modulation during Coffee-Grounds Beer production.

Heatmap analysis of metabolites during coffee-grounds beer fermentation by different yeast strains (A); Temporal metabolic profiling during coffee-grounds beer fermentation by different yeast strains (B); Correlation network analysis of volatile metabolites during coffee-grounds beer fermentation by different yeast strains (C); KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of metabolic signatures during coffee-grounds beer fermentation by different yeast strains (D); Schematic illustration of metabolic pathways for key VOCs by yeast strains (E).

Correlation analysis (Fig. 5C) revealed that most compounds exhibited statistically significant positive correlations, forming several distinct functional clusters. These clusters included volatile esters (such as isoamyl acetate and ethyl octanoate), alcohols (including ethanol and 1-penten-3-ol), and short-chain fatty acid-alcohol complexes (exemplified by ethyl decanoate and caproic acid). These clustering patterns imply that these compounds may share common biosynthetic routes or be subject to coordinated regulatory mechanisms. In contrast, we observed localized negative correlations, particularly between specific fatty acids (capric acid) and aldehydes (nonanal), as well as between certain heterocyclic compounds (2-ethyl-3-methylpyrazine) and ketones (hexyl butyrate). These negative correlations suggest the presence of metabolic branching points, competitive inhibition relationships, or functional antagonism among metabolic pathways. These patterns likely represent specific adaptive responses of yeast metabolism to nutrients derived from spent coffee grounds. While the heatmap analysis provides statistical support for these compound interaction networks, future studies integrating multi-omics methodologies, including detailed metabolic pathway analysis and gene expression validation, would help elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Such integrated studies could ultimately identify promising targets for metabolic engineering interventions and quality enhancement strategies in spent coffee-grounds beer production.

Metabolomic profiling of coffee-grounds beer fermentation identified 460 differentially abundant metabolites across four distinct fermentation stages. Mapping against the KEGG database revealed 53 functionally relevant metabolic pathways. Enrichment analysis of the top 20 metabolites (ranked by adjusted p-value) indicated that ester-phenol synergy serves as a core metabolic module, a finding supported by integrated circular heatmap and chord diagram analyses. Fig. 5D displays the central metabolic pathways (M), primarily involving glycolysis and pyruvate metabolism (specific pathway IDs are provided in Supplementary Table 4). Additionally, certain pathways were significantly enriched in alcohol-related disease pathways, while other significant pathways included protein digestion and absorption, cholinergic synapse, and carbohydrate digestion and absorption, consistent with previous reports (Jiang, Yang, et al., 2025). This multivariate statistical analysis delineates the temporal dynamics of metabolic network alterations, pathway specificity, and statistical significance, offering mechanistic insights into metabolism–pathology interactions through enhanced visual analytics and multi-dimensional data integration. As illustrated in Fig. 5E, which outlines key metabolic routes for flavor compound formation, the final flavor profile of coffee-grounds beer is not determined by a single metabolic pathway but results from the complex interaction between the inherent metabolic properties of the yeast strains and the unique chemical matrix of the coffee grounds. Strategic selection of yeast strains allows targeted modulation of metabolic flux, enabling the customization of beer profiles with emphasized characteristics such as fruity intensity, full-bodied richness, or prominent coffee-like notes.

Dynamic analysis of physicochemical properties during coffee-grounds beer fermentation by distinct yeast strains

The pH and total acidity (mL/100 ml) were monitored during the fermentation of coffee-grounds beer using four yeast strains (Stra ins R, E, U, and D) over 0, 5, 10, and 15 days (Fig. 6A, B). All groups exhibited progressive pH decline (P < 0.05) and acidity increase (P < 0.05), confirming enhanced microbial metabolic activity and acid production capacity, consistent with Jiang (Jiang, Yang, et al., 2025). D-yeast demonstrated the most pronounced metabolic shifts, achieving significantly lower terminal pH (4.18 ± 0.03) and higher acidity (52.93 ± 1.11 ml/100 ml) than other strains (P < 0.001), indicating superior acidogenesis and pH regulation. In contrast, R-yeast showed attenuated fermentation efficiency with slower pH reduction (final pH 4.41 ± 0.04) and limited acid accumulation (44.63 ± 0.93 ml/100 ml). Minimal intra-group variation was observed for Strain E and Strain U, indicating high process stability. In contrast, Strain D exhibited greater fluctuations in acidity during the later stages, which may reflect heightened metabolic activity or greater sensitivity to environmental conditions.

Dynamic changes in physicochemical parameters during coffee-grounds craft beer fermentation: (A) pH, (B) total acidity, (C) diacetyl content, (D) bitterness units, (E) caffeine content, (F) turbidity, (G) alcohol content, (H) original gravity, and (I) real degree of fermentation. *p ≤ 0.05; ***p ≤ 0.001.

The dynamics of diacetyl content are shown in Fig. 6C, during coffee-grounds beer fermentation by four yeast strains. R-, E-, and D-yeast fermentations showed significant diacetyl reduction over time, while U-yeast reached peak diacetyl levels at 5 days. This divergence stems from distinct metabolic phases during sampling: U-yeast was in the early fermentation stage at 5 days, prioritizing α-acetolactate synthesis for valine biosynthesis (Takpho et al., 2018), whereas R-, E-, and D-yeast fermentations had entered the maturation phase, where yeast metabolism shifted toward equilibrium (Iosue et al., 2023). During maturation, thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP)-dependent pyruvate regulation suppressed pyruvate decarboxylase (PDC) activity, while secondary metabolic pathways activated mitochondrial NADH-dependent diacetyl reductase. This enzymatic conversion reduced diacetyl to acetoin and 2,3-butanediol - compounds with flavor thresholds 10- and 100-fold higher than diacetyl respectively. Consequently, this metabolic transformation likely reduced diacetyl's negative sensory impact, ultimately enhancing the overall flavor profile of coffee-grounds beer (Wang et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2019).

The evolution of bitterness units (BU) is intrinsically linked to fermentation progress, primarily governed by hop-derived isoalpha acids and inherent bitter compounds from coffee grounds (Boateng et al., 2021). During the initial fermentation phase, isoalpha acids generated in the boiling stage (primary hop bitterness source) remain relatively stable, resulting in elevated BU values. However, progressive fermentation induces precipitation of bitter compounds with yeast and proteins, accompanied by hydrolytic degradation and structural modifications. Fig. 6D systematically analyzes BU dynamics in coffee-grounds beer fermented by four yeast strains (R, E, U, D) across 0, 5, 10, and 15-day intervals using column charts. All treatments exhibited significant BU reduction during 0–10 days (p < 0.05), consistent with the biodegradation characteristics of hop bitter compounds. Specifically, R-group demonstrated the highest initial BU (29.73) at day 0, significantly exceeding U-group (24.22 BU) (p < 0.05). While BU levels consistently declined during fermentation, a non-significant increase emerged by day 15 across all groups. This pattern may reflect the combined effects of biotransformation (enzymatic modifications) and physical sedimentation of bitter compounds during fermentation.

Changes in caffeine content throughout fermentation are presented in Fig. 6E, during fermentation (0, 5, 10, 15 days) of coffee-grounds beer produced by four yeast strains (R, E, U, D) using column charts. The data reveal that R- and U-yeast reached minimum caffeine levels at day 10, followed by a slight increase at day 15, while E- and D-yeast exhibited a more complex pattern of initial decrease, subsequent increase, and final reduction - all within controlled ranges. This phenomenon could potentially be linked to enhanced cellulase activity post-day 10, which degrades coffee grounds' cellulose matrix to release physically encapsulated caffeine. Additionally, the dissociation of lignin-carbohydrate complexes (LCCs) exposes bound caffeine molecules, increasing its dissolution rate in later stages (Pereira et al., 2022). Fig. 6F illustrates turbidity evolution during fermentation (0, 5, 10, 15 days) across all four yeast strains. All fermentations showed significant turbidity reduction over time (p < 0.05), except for U-yeast between days 10–15 (p > 0.05). While initial fermentation (≤24 h) typically exhibits turbidity spikes, this study's sampling from day 5 onward revealed that turbidity decline depends on yeast flocculation/metabolism and coffee grounds' inherent adsorption capacity, creating strain-specific clarification patterns. CO₂ disturbance and β-glucan secretion might contribute to fluctuations, while final stabilization is governed by pH-induced isoelectric precipitation and residual colloidal complexes (polyphenol-proteins). The final clarity appears to result from the interplay of yeast flocculation competence, coffee grounds' defatting pretreatment/particle size, and process parameters - requiring balanced optimization of all three factors.

Fig. 6G-I show alcohol content (% vol), original gravity (°P), and fermentation degree (%) for four yeast strains (R, E, U, D) over 15 days. Alcohol evolution showed strain-specific patterns: R/E/D strains showed late-stage declines (Fig. 6G) attributable to yeast autolysis releasing intracellular metabolites (amino acids, sugars) that undergo minor re-metabolism(Duc et al., 2017), while U-yeast demonstrated progressive alcohol accumulation through both rapid primary sugar fermentation and sustained substrate availability from the acidic hydrolysis of coffee ground hemicellulose (Liu et al., 2021; Maicas, 2020). Original gravity stabilized near initial values despite interim fluctuations (Fig. 6H), confirming complete sugar utilization. All strains achieved significant fermentation efficiency improvements (p < 0.05, Fig. 6I), with U-yeast exhibiting superior performance (78 % vs R-yeast's 72 %), evidenced by the synchronized trends of rising alcohol, declining residual sugars, and increasing fermentation degree - collectively mapping yeast metabolic dynamics.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the dynamic changes of volatile compounds and flavor formation mechanisms during coffee-grounds beer fermentation using four yeast strains (Strains R, E, U, and D). The results both confirmed previous findings and revealed unique metabolic characteristics. Notably, the selected wine yeast strains exhibited metabolic pathway divergences distinct from those of traditional brewer's yeast (e.g., Weiss yeast) reported by Jiang et al. (Jiang, Yang, et al., 2025). The selected wine yeast strains exhibited distinct metabolic pathway divergences. The R strain displayed a phenolic-ester-heterocyclic metabolic profile, while the E strain primarily followed an ester‑sulfur-heterocyclic pathway. The U strain enhanced phenylalanine metabolism, forming an aromatic aldehyde-benzyl ester‑sulfur compound-terpenenetwork, whereas the D strain showed a typical acid-ester‑sulfur compound conversion pattern. These differences suggest that conventional beer yeasts may have limitations in unlocking the flavor potential of coffee grounds. Although the overall increase in volatile compounds (primarily esters and alcohols) aligned with prior studies, our multi-strain design systematically demonstrated strain-dependent activation of metabolic pathways. Notably, Strain U achieved 78 % fermentation efficiency, positively correlating with the consumption rate of hemicellulose-derived sugars (xylose/arabinose). This observation supports Cun et al.'s (Cunha et al., 2019) theory on coffee-ground valorization. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further suggested the potential involvement of the xylose isomerase gene, though knockout experiments are needed to confirm its necessity. While our pathway analysis revealed intriguing links between glycolysis, phenylalanine metabolism, and flavor profiles, the strain-specific metabolic flux differences require validation through enzyme activity assays. Future studies could integrate transcriptomic analysis of key enzymes (e.g., ester synthase ATF1, pyruvate decarboxylase PDC) with targeted metabolic flux analysis to establish definitive mechanistic connections.

Concerning flavor regulation mechanisms, the present findings corroborate and extend existing knowledge. Consistent with (Bennis et al., 2024), who stated that yeast metabolism governs the production of key flavor compounds (e.g., isoamyl alcohol, ethyl acetate), but our study reveals that strain-specific ester synthase activity can lead to divergent flavor profiles from identical substrates. For instance, while all four strains produced isoamyl acetate (associated with banana/pear aroma, as reported by (Fukuda et al., 1998), concentrations varied 3- to 8-fold, indicating that beyond gene presence, expression regulation plays a critical role. Additionally, SCG phenolics (e.g., chlorogenic acid) may indirectly modulate flavor by influencing yeast metabolism—a phenomenon not observed in grape-seed study, highlighting how raw material properties shape metabolic networks (Zhao et al., 2024).

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Primarily, the absence of a control group brewed without coffee grounds is a constraint that hampers our ability to accurately delineate the aroma profile and quantify the respective contributions of the coffee grounds versus the malt base - a methodological constraint similarly observed in the studies by Jiang et al. (Jiang, Yang, et al., 2025). Secondly, the exclusive use of medium-roasted Catimor coffee grounds inherently limits the generalizability of our findings, given the well-documented variations in cellulose/phenolic compositions across different coffee varieties and roasting degrees. Most critically, our metabolic pathway attributions remain inferential in nature, as the experimental design did not incorporate direct measurements of enzymatic activities or gene expression patterns underlying the observed volatile compound dynamics. This knowledge gap limits our current understanding of the potential relationship between late-stage flavor fluctuations and yeast autolysis, especially concerning the dynamic equilibrium between cell wall hydrolase activity and volatile compound release. These limitations reflect common challenges in the field that warrant attention in future investigations through more comprehensive experimental designs.

Future research should address three key dimensions: (1) Methodologically, establishing quantitative models linking coffee-ground composition (e.g., cellulose/phenolic content) to yeast metabolic pathways; (2) Practically, incorporating sensory evaluation to correlate the 460 detected volatiles with human perception; and (3) Mechanistically, applying transcriptomics to decipher U strain's efficient hemicellulose utilization. Such advancements would elevate the valorization of agricultural byproducts from empirical observations to theoretically predictable processes.

Conclusions

This study analyzed flavor profiles in coffee ground beer fermented with four yeast strains using electronic sensing and GC–MS. Multimodal analysis demonstrated that all coffee ground beers exhibited similar basic taste characteristics (bitterness, umami, richness, and saltiness with subtle Type A aftertaste) after 15-day fermentation, while notably, three strains (excluding strain E) displayed significantly enhanced sourness, potentially related to strain-specific metabolic traits. HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis identified 460 volatile compounds, with esters, heterocyclic compounds, ketones, and alcohols constituting the predominant aromatic components. Multivariate statistical analysis combined with ROAV>1 revealed nine key differential volatile compounds – ethyl butyrate, guaiacol, dimethyl trisulfide, ethyl isobutyrate, dimethyl disulfide, damascenone, 2-isobutyl-3-methoxypyrazine, 2,3-diethyl-5-methylpyrazine, and 3-ethylphenol - that critically determined the flavor profile. Yeast strains differentially modulated ester and heterocyclic compound production via glycolysis and pyruvate metabolism. These changes altered physicochemical properties and improved sensory quality. Pathway enrichment revealed indirect links to protein digestion/absorption pathways, suggesting new flavor formation mechanisms. Notably, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed an indirect association between yeast metabolic activity and pathways linked to alcoholic liver disease and protein digestion and absorption. While this computational finding does not imply direct biological effects in humans, it may offer novel mechanistic insights for understanding strain-specific flavor modulation. Future studies should validate whether these pathway overlaps reflect conserved metabolic bottlenecks or stress responses in yeast. Future research should prioritize: establishing quantitative models correlating microbial dynamics with flavor formation; in-depth exploration of yeast apoptosis characteristics on flavor metabolomics.
