Interventional Partial Cavopulmonary Connection: Initial Clinical Experience With Acute Procedural up to Midterm Results
Authors: Katarzyna Gendera, Peter Ewert, Stanimir Georgiev, Andreas Eicken, Daniel Tanase, Kristina Borgmann, Paul Philipp Heinisch, Nicole Nagdyman, Julie Cleuziou, Dunja Renner, Pinar Bambul Heck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCreation of a partial cavopulmonary anasthomosis in patients with isolated right‐sided heart failure or patients with cyanosis and intracardial right‐to‐left shunt and reduced blood flow in the pulmonary circulation may provide a significant improvement in exercise capacity and also facilitate the performance of activities of daily living in patients with a deteriorated clinical condition. However, surgical partial cavopulmonary connection creation may be a high‐risk procedure in this patient group. An interventional partial cavopulmonary connection may provide a less invasive treatment modality for these patients. The aim of the study was a retrospective evaluation of patients who underwent the creation of an interventional partial cavopulmonary connection.MethodsEach patient who was qualified for treatment underwent a preinterventional computed tomography scan with 3‐dimensional reconstruction for improved procedure planning. As a preparation preceding the interventional partial cavopulmonary connection, a prestent (bare metal stent) was implanted into the superior vena cava during cardiac catheterization in most cases. The perforation was performed with the use of needle punctures as a straightforward method in all cases. Thereafter, a covered stent was implanted, connecting the superior vena cava and the right pulmonary artery.ResultsBetween July 2019 and July 2024, 14 patients (male n=6, 43%) underwent an interventional partial cavopulmonary connection. The indication for treatment was deteriorated clinical condition (n=12, 86%) or significant cyanosis (n=2, 14%). The procedure was performed successfully in all cases. The median follow‐up time was 19.8 months (minimum 2.7; maximum 65.6).ConclusionsOur study shows that transcatheter creation of partial cavopulmonary connection provides a promising treatment modality for selected adult patients with right‐sided heart failure and may lead to clinical improvement in this patient group.

Since the first surgical partial cavopulmonary anastomosis (PCPC) was reported by Glenn in 1958, 1 a large number of patients worldwide have been treated with a modification of this treatment modality, bidirectional Glenn anastomosis, as a step for total cavopulmonary palliation in patients with univentricular circulation.

Nevertheless, an analog procedure, involving the connection of the superior vena cava (SVC) to the pulmonary artery (PA), though applied in different patient groups, may yield varying clinical outcomes and offer a promising treatment modality for other patient populations with congenital heart diseases. In patients with biventricular circulation and isolated right‐sided heart failure, for example, patients with Ebstein anomaly, or other severe tricuspid valve diseases, creating a PCPC reduces volume overload for the right side of the heart, establishing a 1.5 circulation. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Another group of patients who may also benefit from a PCPC are patients with univentricular circulation, like tricuspid atresia with a conduit connection between the right atrium (RA) and the right ventricle (Björk–Fontan modification). 6 In both cohorts, this management may lead to an improvement in the patient's functional status.

In patients with significant cyanosis due to intracardiac right‐to‐left shunt and reduced blood flow through the pulmonary circulation, a PCPC may provide an improvement in arterial blood oxygen saturation. It comprises an alternative to the aortopulmonary shunt in a selected patient population. 7

Although surgical creation of a bidirectional cavopulmonary connection in small children proved to be safe and feasible, in adult patients with a poor clinical condition it might be associated with a higher risk for complications, increased morbidity and even mortality. For that reason, a less invasive treatment modality is needed in this patient group. A computed tomography‐based transcatheter implantation of covered stents functioning like extravascular conduits has shown to be a promising, less invasive approach for patients with different congenital heart anomalies. 8 In this study, we present our initial clinical experience with an interventional creation of a PCPC (iPCPC) in 14 adult patients.

The aim of the study was a retrospective evaluation of patients who underwent the creation of an iPCPC.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Indications for Treatment

The indication for treatment was a reduced clinical status, assessed either subjectively (New York Heart Association functional classification, clinical symptoms) or objectively by a decreased peak oxygen uptake during cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), or significant cyanosis. Before the procedure, each patient was evaluated by the heart team and deemed a bad candidate for open‐heart surgery due to their poor clinical condition. Additionally, 12 patients (86%) showed impaired right ventricular function or systolic dysfunction of the systemic ventricle in those with univentricular circulation. In patient no. 7, severe cyanosis, and in patient no. 9, a history of previous surgeries along with significant rhythm disturbances, constituted contraindications for surgical intervention. At the time of the procedure, none of the patients presented with acute cardiogenic shock.

Each patient who was qualified for treatment underwent a pre‐interventional computed tomography scan with 3‐dimensional reconstruction to exclude unexpected anatomical obstacles and to facilitate optimal procedural planning (Figure 1). In every case preceding the procedure the patient was informed about possible complications and signed an informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 version). The study protocol was approved by the local institutional ethics committee.

Procedure

As a preparation preceding the iPCPC, a prestent (bare metal stent) was implanted into the SVC during cardiac catheterization. This aimed to avoid a secondary dilatation of the SVC under higher pressures after creating the iPCPC in the follow‐up by restriction of the SVC diameter to the diameter of the implanted prestent. All procedures were conducted in deep conscious sedation, without mechanical ventilation. In all patients, the interventions were performed by experienced senior congenital interventionalists with the use of a biplane fluoroscopy platform. The right jugular vein was used as a vascular access in all but 1 case, in whom the procedure was performed from the left jugular vein for a left‐sided iPCPC (patient no. 14). Additionally, femoral venous access was obtained in all cases. After gaining vascular access, 5000 IU heparin was given if not contraindicated. In each patient, after the initial simultaneous angiography in the SVC and right PA, the optimal angulation for the needle pathway was defined, and a snare was placed in the right PA (from the femoral access) for a better landing zone orientation. The perforation was performed with the use of needle punctures (22G Chiba needle Optimed, GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany or 18 G Somatex Needle Sheath, Medical Technologies GmbH, Berlin, Germany) as a straightforward method in all cases. After exiting the SVC, crossing the extravascular distance, the needle sheath was introduced in the proximal part of the right PA (RPA). In the initial patient group, the dilation of the extravascular segments and vessel walls (SVC and RPA) was performed using small balloons. In recent cases, the Sentrant sheath (Medtronic, Galway, Ireland) was used, and there was no need for balloon dilatation before the sheath introduction (Figure 2). Thereafter, a covered stent was implanted, connecting the SVC and the RPA. After stent implantation, the final angiography was performed in all cases to exclude residual shunt and any vascular complications. Patients were discharged home with a recommendation for treatment with novel oral anticoagulants, provided there were no contraindications. In some cases, however, ongoing anticoagulant therapy was continued. A detailed procedural description of the first 2 cases was already published by Tanase et al. 9

Follow‐Up

During follow‐up, clinical evaluation included assessment of functional status according to the New York Heart Association classification, transthoracic echocardiography, measurement of NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels, and, in selected cases, CPET and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.

RESULTS

Between July 2019 and July 2024 14 patients (male n=6, 43%) underwent an iPCPC. Patient characteristics are given in Table 1. The indication for treatment was reduced clinical state (n=12, 86%) or significant cyanosis (n=2, 14%). The median interval between prestenting and the iPCPC procedure was 3.07 months (minimum 1.53; maximum 8.90). The procedure was performed successfully in all cases. In some patients, the anatomical variety required some additional technical modifications. For example, in patient no. 14, the persistence of a left SVC led to a connection to the left PA, creating a left‐sided PCPC. However, the principles of the approach remain similar to the aforementioned description. Hemodynamic characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 2. In 6 patients (43%), a covered CP stent (NuMED, Inc., Hopkinton, NY, USA) was implanted, in 5 (36%) a covered Optimus stent (AndraTec in Koblenz, Germany), in 2 (14%) a Bentley stent (Inomed GmbH, Hechingen, Germany), and in 1 patient (7%) a Formula stent (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA). In 5 patients (36%) a second stent was implanted as an extension of the first to direct all the blood volume to the pulmonary artery and cover the residual shunt to the right atrium. In 4 patients (29%) the right upper lobe pulmonary artery was covered by the stent, and in 2 patients (14%) it was possible to recanalize it. No serious complications occurred. In 1 patient (No. 7) an incomplete sealing of the SVC wall by the covered stent and a residual shunt between SVC and RA caused blood backflow from PA into the SVC and subsequently into the RA causing significant cyanosis. Occlusion of the implanted stent using a ventricular septal defect occluder was performed to stop blood flow from the RPA into the SVC; however, the effect was insufficient. Two days later, the patient underwent successful reintervention involving removal of the occluder and extension of the initial stent with an additional covered stent, resulting in completion of the iPCPC (Figure 3). After the procedure, 7 patients (50%) received novel oral anticoagulants, 4 patients (29%) vitamin K antagonists, 1 patient acetylsalicylic acid as monotherapy (7%), and 1 acetylsalicylic acid combined with clopidogrel (7%). In 1 patient (7%) no anticoagulation was prescribed. Eleven patients (79%) received prestenting some months before the procedure. With this approach development of residual shunt between the dilated SVC and the right atrium (along the stent), causing cyanosis, had been avoided in the follow‐up.

Follow‐Up

Detailed follow‐up characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 3. The median follow‐up time was 19.8 months (minimum 2.7; maximum 65.6). During this time, New York Heart Association functional class improved in 9 patients, remained unchanged in 4 patients, and was unavailable for 1 patient who died 2 months after the procedure due to pulmonary embolism. This patient had permanent atrial fibrillation and had declined anticoagulation therapy. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging data were available for 5 patients. Among the 4 patients with Ebstein anomaly, a slight decrease in the right ventricular end‐diastolic index and an increase in cardiac index were observed following the procedure. Three of these patients reported improved exercise capacity, and 1 patient with good baseline functional status remained clinically stable. In 1 patient (patient no. 7), who underwent iPCPC due to significant cyanosis, the cardiac index was lower at follow‐up; however, the patient exhibited marked clinical improvement, and arterial oxygen saturation increased.

No significant changes were observed on echocardiography between baseline and follow‐up. In 12 patients (86%), the SVC demonstrated adequate flow without any signs of stenosis. In the remaining 2 patients, imaging documentation was insufficient; however, no clinical evidence of SVC stenosis or occlusion was present.

NT‐proBNP values were available for 11 patients (79%) at follow‐up. Levels decreased in 10 patients, and 1 patient (no. 6) exhibited a slight increase. Notably, this patient also reported no improvement in exercise capacity during follow‐up. Among patients in whom cyanosis was the primary indication for treatment (no. 2 and no. 7), peripheral oxygen saturation increased from 75% to 83% and from 70% to 83%, respectively. CPET data were available for 5 patients (36%) at follow‐up. Decreased peak oxygen uptake increased in 4 of these patients, and in 1 patient (no. 11), it remained unchanged. This patient also did not report any improvement in exercise capacity. During the follow‐up, 3 patients died, 1 due to pulmonary embolism and 2 due to unrelated causes (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Partial cavopulmonary anastomosis represents an effective treatment modality in patients with right‐sided heart failure providing volume relief for the overloaded right‐sided heart structures in a selected patient group for example in patients with tricuspid valve diseases. At the same time, it ensures an increase in blood flow through the pulmonary circulation in patients with significant cyanosis (cardiac right‐to‐left shunt) due to reduced blood volume passing through the lung capillary bed. In both cohorts, the creation of the PCPC may significantly improve exercise capacity and also facilitate the performance of activities of daily living in patients with a deteriorated clinical condition.

However, many patients who might benefit from this treatment option have an increased risk for surgery, because they are often in a poor clinical state and open‐heart surgery under general anesthesia with mechanical ventilation may significantly worsen the outcome in this high‐risk patient group. Percutaneous iPCPC may represent a promising treatment alternative for these patients. Not only is this a far less invasive approach compared with the surgical treatment but it can be performed in spontaneously breathing patients without the need for mechanical positive pressure ventilation at any time and with significantly shorter intensive care stays, if any at all, which constitutes a strong argument in the decision‐making process of these patients.

The initial iPCPC animal experiments, conducted more than a decade ago and described by Schmitt et al., showed promising results in creating an anastomosis between the SVC and the RPA using radiofrequency perforation and subsequent implantation of a covered stent. 10 In contrast to that, in all patients from our study group, the puncture was performed with a puncture needle providing excellent steering facilities of the needle under biplane guidance. As emphasized by Tanase et al., using the radiofrequency perforation system may result in system dislodgement during simultaneous forward push and energy application, and finally lead to the undesirable course of the wire. 9

Our experience showed that in some patients, the RPA branch to the right upper lung lobe is localized in the neighborhood of the puncture site. Depending on the anatomy, it may be helpful to secure access to this vessel with a guide wire before stent implantation for a potential possibility of reopening the vessel in case of jailing with the implanted covered stent. In our study group, the right upper lobe PA was covered by the stent in 4 patients and successfully reopened in 2 cases.

An important issue is the proper selection of patients for this approach. At our center, the patients were scheduled for the procedure if the mean pressure in the PA was ≤15 mm Hg or if the pressure in the SVC was significantly higher in comparison to the mean pressure in the PA. A “significantly elevated” SVC pressure was defined as a pressure exceeding the PA pressure by >3 mmHg. However, this threshold is not universally applicable and must be interpreted in the context of individual anatomical and physiological characteristics. Patients with lower mean PA pressures may be more likely to derive clinical benefit from the intervention, whereas in others, the procedure may result in limited therapeutic effect or an increased risk of complications.

Only symptomatic patients with reduced exercise capacity, cyanosis, or a significantly enlarged RA with increased risk for open‐heart surgery were scheduled for this procedure so far.

The aforementioned animal studies demonstrated the development of cyanosis in animals approximately 4 weeks after performing iPCPC. 10 The cause of that was an increasing dilatation of the SVC due to an increase in pressure after creation of the anastomosis (SVC connection to the RPA with a higher pressure than RA pressure), leading to a dilatation of the SVC and a blood flow passage around the stent into the RA. In light of this experience, 11 out of 14 patients from our study group received a prestenting with a bare metal stent before the iPCPC to prevent postprocedural dilatation of the SVC. In 2 patients with total cavopulmonary connection and extracardiac conduit, and in 1 patient in whom there was no intracardiac shunt present (Björk–Fontan modification), prestenting was not performed. This step enabled the restriction of further SVC diameter expansion to the diameter of the ingrown metal stent implanted some weeks before the procedure. Thanks to this approach, in none of the patients cyanosis caused by the SVC‐RA shunt has been registered during follow‐up. We suggest performing prestenting in all patients who undergo the iPCPC procedure.

The available clinical data about this treatment modality are still very limited. Ratnayaka et al. described the first‐in‐human successful creation of a percutaneous PCPC in a 35‐year‐old woman with univentricular circulation, significant cyanosis, and a higher risk of surgical approach, whose clinical state improved during the 6 months of follow‐up after the intervention and no complications occurred. 11

Tanase et al. published our initial experience with 2 patients who were treated with iPCPC. 9 Since then, another 12 patients have been treated with this approach in our center.

In our study group, 9 out of 13 patients reported clinical improvement according to the New York Heart Association classification, as well as enhanced ability to perform activities of daily living during follow‐up after the intervention. In some patients, CPET and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging data were available, demonstrating good correlation with the respective clinical findings.

The proper recruitment of patients for this intervention is a very important but still uninvestigated issue. Further investigations, larger study groups, and extended follow‐up periods are required to determine which patients derive the greatest benefit from this procedure.

Our promising results raise the question: Could transcatheter creation of PCPC find application in noncongenital groups of patients with low pulmonary arterial pressure? In theory, patients with acquired right heart failure, for example, after myocardial infarction and severe tricuspid valve regurgitation or patients after myocarditis and poor right ventricular function, may benefit from creating iPCPC using the same method as patients with congenital heart diseases. 12 However, available data on this topic are very limited. 13

Whether this promising treatment modality will find clinical application in infants remains an open question, as there are currently no available data on the performance of this procedure in small children.

Limitations

Our study is a retrospective study. Further investigations, larger study groups and longer follow‐ups are needed to identify the best candidates for this procedure, the best time to perform it and to determine the most universal technical approach. During follow‐up, routine cardiac magnetic resonance imaging or CPET was not performed and was available only in a subset of patients. In most cases, clinical improvement was assessed based solely on clinical presentation and NT‐proBNP levels.

Conclusions

Our experience shows that transcatheter creation of PCPC provides a promising treatment modality for selected adult patients with right‐sided heart failure and may lead to clinical improvement in this patient group.
