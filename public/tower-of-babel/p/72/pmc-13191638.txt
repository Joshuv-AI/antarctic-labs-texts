Diet, the protective bridge connecting nutrition and cardiovascular health: A review
Authors: Tianming Feng, Yixiao Wei, Zihan Wang, Jia Sun, Ruigong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular Disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death globally, resulting in approximately 17.9 million deaths each year, which accounts for 32% of total deaths worldwide. Its pathogenesis involves the intricate interactions of genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, and lifestyle behaviors, among which smoking, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia are well-recognized core risk factors. In the field of nutritional research related to CVD, there has been a notable shift from focusing on individual nutrients to evaluating overall dietary patterns and their interactive effects with other health determinants. Based on this research paradigm shift, this review synthesizes the latest evidence regarding the roles and mechanisms of diet-related factors in CVD prevention and management, designing personalized dietary interventions by clarifying the cardiovascular protective mechanisms of these dietary factors, thereby supporting efforts to alleviate the global burden of CVD.Keywords: Cardiovascular diseases, Dietary patterns, Nutrients

Introduction

As the leading cause of death globally, CVD claims approximately 17.9 million lives each year, accounting for 32% of all global deaths (Vardell, 2020) Its pathogenesis is inherently complex, driven by the long-term interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and lifestyle practices (Sattar et al., 2020). Although age-standardized CVD mortality has declined in recent decades, the escalating burden of metabolic comorbidities, such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, and obesity, tied to contemporary lifestyles continues to elevate individual CVD risk, thereby remaining a pressing challenge to global cardiovascular health (Ali et al., 2024).

Diet is widely recognized as one of the most modifiable and foundational determinants of cardiovascular health. Over recent years, nutritional research has undergone a paradigm shift: from investigating isolated nutrients toward evaluating holistic dietary patterns, food synergies, and their integrated effects on metabolism, inflammation, and gut microbiota. Dietary components exert cardiovascular effects through multiple interconnected pathways, including regulation of lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, vascular endothelial function, gut microbial homeostasis, and epigenetic modification. For instance: long-chain n-3 PUFAs upregulate fatty acid oxidation genes via the PPAR signaling pathway while inhibiting the production of inflammatory mediators (Cherian, 2007); dietary fiber is fermented by colonic microbiota to produce SCFAs, which exert anti-inflammatory effects and improve endothelial function (Chen et al., 2017); polyphenolic compounds alleviate vascular oxidative damage by activating antioxidant pathways such as Nrf2 and SIRT1 (Mirmiran et al., 2022). Furthermore, active components from food-medicine homologous substances also demonstrate cardiovascular protective potential by regulating specific molecular targets. Moreover, with the improvement of material living standards, the concept of food-medicine homology also aligns with current precision nutrition needs. Furthermore, dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet, DASH, and healthy plant-based diet have shown remarkable effectiveness in reducing CVD risk, as they target traditional risk factors like blood pressure, blood lipids, and blood glucose, and also exert comprehensive protective effects through multiple mechanisms, including improving inflammatory status, enhancing endothelial function, and regulating gut microbiota. Current nutritional research is moving toward precision and personalization. Significant heterogeneity exists in different populations' responses to the same dietary intervention, due to variations in genetic background, metabolic status, microbial composition, and sociocultural environment (Roumi et al., 2024). Thus, developing evidence-based personalized nutritional recommendations has become a key direction for future CVD prevention and treatment. Against this backdrop, this review aims to systematically summarize the synergistic roles of nutrients and dietary patterns in the development and progression of CVD, deeply explore the cardiovascular effects and molecular mechanisms of major nutrients and food bioactive components, evaluate the protective efficacy of specific foods and dietary patterns, and simultaneously investigate the potential application value of traditional food-medicine homologous substances in cardiovascular health. The ultimate goal is to provide a theoretical basis and practical reference for advancing targeted personalized dietary intervention strategies.

Cardiovascular protection via macronutrients: Regulation with quality over quantity

Dietary macronutrients form the foundation for maintaining normal physiological functions in the human body, and their intake type, dosage, and metabolic characteristics are closely associated with the occurrence and progression of cardiovascular diseases. As core macronutrients, fat and carbohydrates play crucial roles in processes such as energy supply and substance metabolism, while exerting significantly distinct effects on the cardiovascular system depending on their specific forms.

Trans fatty acids are widely used in the food processing industry such as margarine and shortening, and their hazards to cardiovascular health have been confirmed by multiple studies. Animal experimental results have shown that long-term intake of a diet high in trans fatty acids leads to increased blood pressure and heart rate in rabbits, confirming its adverse effects on cardiovascular health and the body's stress state (Ismail et al., 2024). Epidemiological studies indicate that a daily intake of 4–5 g of TFA increases coronary artery disease risk by 30%, with a more adverse impact on the HDL/LDL ratio than saturated fatty acids (Astrup et al., 2008). Accumulating evidence suggests that trans fatty acids may cause dyslipidemia, aggravate liver disorders, and modulate the expression of related genes via epigenetic pathways (Hegazy et al., 2025). However, these findings are mainly based on animal and in vitro studies, requiring additional clinical research for confirmation.

Current dietary and nutritional guidelines recommend reducing saturated fatty acid intake and replacing it with unsaturated fatty acids, primarily based on the evidence that saturated fatty acids elevate low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. In addition, excessive saturated fatty acid intake may increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases by impairing inflammatory responses, coagulation function, heart rate and other multiple indicators (Maki et al., 2021; Worm et al., 2022).Notably, some studies have suggested that not all saturated fatty acids exert adverse effects on cardiometabolic health. Certain short-chain and medium-chain saturated fatty acids may confer potential health benefits and contribute to the prevention of metabolic diseases (Arellano et al., 2023; Bloise et al., 2022). Furthermore, a study focusing on antimicrobial agents has demonstrated the potential antibacterial effects of long-chain saturated fatty acids (Perna & Hewlings, 2022). Overall, current evidence is associated with substantial limitations. Conclusions supporting the restriction of saturated fatty acids are largely derived from observational epidemiological data, which cannot fully eliminate confounding factors such as dietary patterns and lifestyle, leading to relatively general findings. In contrast, evidence supporting the beneficial effects of short-chain and medium-chain saturated fatty acids is mostly based on animal experiments and small-scale clinical studies, resulting in insufficient levels of evidence. Research on the antibacterial properties of long-chain saturated fatty acids remains at the basic exploratory stage and is still far from practical application.

Based on the current research status of trans fatty acids and saturated fatty acids mentioned above, the regulatory effect of unsaturated fatty acids on cardiovascular diseases is of greater research value. Unsaturated fatty acids can be further divided into monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), which have different regulatory effects on cardiovascular diseases due to differences in their chemical structures. Among them, MUFA can participate in the regulation of the atherosclerotic process by regulating hepatic cholesterol synthesis, reducing LDL-C levels, and exerting anti-inflammatory effects, with most current studies focusing on oleic acid (OA) (Huth et al., 2015; Pedersen et al., 2025). Pharmacological studies have shown that OA can effectively improve renal function, alleviate inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, and exert a good cardiovascular protective effect by regulating cell apoptosis, MAPK(Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase) and PPAR-γ signaling pathways in mice with acute kidney injury (AKI) (Zhang et al., 2022). In addition, epidemiological studies have indicated that palmitoleic acid can prevent hypertension by inhibiting NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses (Tang et al., 2021); animal experiments have also confirmed that long-chain monounsaturated fatty acids can improve vascular endothelial function by regulating the structure of intestinal microflora, thereby providing protection for cardiovascular health (Tsutsumi et al., 2021).

PUFA can be further divided into n-6 and n-3 series. Numerous studies have shown that n-6 PUFA, represented by linoleic acid, can exert mild but significant beneficial effects on relevant physiological indicators in populations at high risk of cardiovascular disease, yet have not met the criteria for clinical application (Esmaeilnejad et al., 2024). Therefore, current relevant research has mostly focused on n-3 PUFA centered on ALA, DHA, and EPA. Multiple meta-analyses have clearly demonstrated that dietary ALA intake is associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular mortality (Chen et al., 2025a; Naghshi et al., 2021a). ALA not only activates PPAR-γ to participate in the regulation of lipid metabolism and inflammation (Cambiaggi et al., 2023), but can also be converted into other n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids in small amounts in vivo, exerting protective effects against CVD through intrinsic mechanisms such as anticoagulation, lipid regulation, blood pressure reduction, and gene expression modulation (Naghshi et al., 2021a; Petersen et al., 2024). The cardiovascular protective effects of EPA and DHA are the most well-established. They can significantly reduce plasma triglycerides, inhibit platelet aggregation, stabilize atherosclerotic plaques, and improve cardiac rhythm stability and vascular endothelial function (Djuricic & Calder, 2021; Huang et al., 2023; Sidorkiewicz, 2024). Clinical guidelines recommend that high-risk individuals take 2 g of EPA/DHA twice daily to effectively reduce the risk of cardiovascular death (Rodriguez et al., 2022); a daily dose of 1.8 g is sufficient for the early prevention of cardiometabolic diseases. Cellular experiments have confirmed that DHA can activate GPR120 on the surface of macrophages and adipocytes, thereby directly upregulating the insulin signaling molecule AKT and promoting glucose consumption in adipocytes (Yang et al., 2024a). Notably, the cardiovascular protective effects of unsaturated fatty acids largely rely on their substitution for saturated and trans fatty acids, rather than simply increasing intake. Excessive intake of any unsaturated fatty acid can lead to obesity due to excess energy intake, which in turn increases cardiovascular risk (Borges et al., 2022; Djuricic & Calder, 2025).

Dietary carbohydrates and cardiovascular health

Carbohydrates serve as the primary energy source for the human body. Modern nutritional guidelines recommend that carbohydrates should contribute 45%–65% of total energy intake, and research focus has shifted from intake quantity to quality (Sievenpiper, 2020). The quality of carbohydrates can be assessed by food sources, glycemic index/load (GI/GL), dietary fiber content and added sugar levels. Higher quality correlates with a lower risk of chronic diseases, with dietary fiber being a key protective component (Korat et al., 2025).

Refined carbohydrates, deficient in dietary fiber, induce a rapid rise in blood glucose, impair healthy aging, trigger insulin resistance, and increase hepatic triglyceride synthesis. In individuals with metabolic syndrome, a TyG index >9.13 corresponds to a cardiovascular mortality hazard ratio of 4.21 (95% CI: 1.82–9.78). High refined carbohydrate intake is also linked to dyslipidemia and elevated CRP(C-reactive protein), raising diabetes and CVD risk (Yang et al., 2024b). In contrast, whole grains, rich in soluble fiber, are fermented by gut microbiota to produce SCFAs, which activate GPR43(G protein-coupled receptor 43) receptors to exert anti-inflammatory effects, improve lipid profiles, and lower blood pressure. Each 6.8 g/1000 kcal increase in total fiber reduces systolic blood pressure by 1.69 mmHg (Aljuraiban et al., 2015). Meta-analyses indicate that high fiber intake lowers CVD mortality by 26% (HR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.71–0.77), with whole-grain fiber reducing all-cause mortality (Ramezani et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024b). Oat β-glucan is FDA-approved for coronary risk reduction, supported by 49 clinical trials (Mathews et al., 2020).

Low-carbohydrate diets facilitate weight loss, primarily by reducing energy intake rather than increasing energy expenditure. High-carbohydrate diets may exacerbate glucose intolerance and dyslipidemia, thereby elevating the risk of coronary heart disease (Liu & Manson, 2001). However, no definitive association exists between carbohydrate quantity and CVD risk when quality is maintained (Korat et al., 2025). Studies have shown that a healthy low-carbohydrate dietary pattern is associated with lower all-cause mortality in patients with cardiovascular disease and lower cardiovascular mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (Mirrafiei et al., 2023). In contrast, a diet high in refined carbohydrates increases cardiovascular burden, which highlights that the quality of carbohydrates is more important than the quantity.

Minerals and vitamins: Dose-dependent regulation, microbiota interaction, and health effects

Vitamins and minerals, as indispensable micronutrients, play a pivotal role in cardiovascular health through their integral involvement in energy metabolism, immune function modulation, and the prevention of chronic diseases (Tardy et al., 2020). As most are obtained solely from the diet or supplements-which vary in health impact-their effects are dose-dependent and involve complex interactions with gut microbiota. Crucially, the body requires these compounds within precise ranges: appropriate levels maintain metabolic homeostasis, while imbalances significantly increase cardiovascular risk, highlighting their bidirectional regulatory nature. Minerals and cardiovascular health

Potassium and sodium are key mineral elements that regulate blood pressure homeostasis. Multiple cohort studies have firmly established that higher serum potassium levels are significantly associated with reduced arterial stiffness (Chatterjee et al., 2025), whereas the sodium-to‑potassium ratio is significantly positively correlated with the risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality (Gan et al., 2024). Potassium intake is inversely associated with blood pressure, particularly under conditions of low sodium intake, where potassium supplementation effectively reduces the sodium-to‑potassium ratio and further exerts an antihypertensive effect (Fan et al., 2024; Mosallanezhad et al., 2023). Although most current hypertension prevention and treatment guidelines recommend limiting salt intake to target ranges, they have not established specific dietary intake or supplementation targets for potassium. It is therefore evident that core strategies for lowering blood pressure and improving its control through dietary modification should not focus solely on reducing sodium intake, but also emphasize increasing dietary potassium intake to optimize sodium‑potassium metabolic balance (Chia et al., 2025).

Magnesium is a core antioxidant predictive of comorbidity between cardiovascular disease and cancer (Qi et al., 2025). Its depleted state, quantified by the magnesium depletion score (MDS), is significantly associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease and constitutes an important risk factor for increased all-cause mortality and cardiovascular-specific mortality in patients with cardiovascular disease (Fan et al., 2021). Meanwhile, magnesium serves as a key target for precise nutritional intervention in cardiovascular disease. Intervention strategies involving increased magnesium intake in populations with high MDS can improve the accuracy of chronic disease risk prediction, aligning with the paradigm of precision nutrition. Furthermore, the combination of MDS with serum magnesium and urinary magnesium measurements may further optimize the precision of cardiovascular disease risk prediction and intervention (Fan et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2023).

Calcium ions are key minerals in the cardiovascular system, and their concentration regulates physiological processes including myocardial contraction and cell membrane potential. Hypocalcemia is independently associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes and high mortality in acute coronary syndrome and after myocardial infarction (Schmitz et al., 2021). Adequate dietary calcium has also been shown to improve the regulation of blood pressure, blood lipids, and blood glucose, helping to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease (Narayanam et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021a). Notably, elevated serum calcium levels promote vascular calcification and increase the risk of coronary plaque formation and cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension and myocardial infarction (Reid et al., 2016). Calcium supplements tend to cause a rapid increase in serum calcium and are more likely to raise the risk of coronary heart disease and other conditions compared with dietary calcium (Anderson et al., 2016); some studies have even linked them to adverse cardiovascular events in elderly women (Lewis et al., 2015). Current conclusions regarding the effects of calcium and calcium supplements on cardiovascular health remain inconsistent. Moreover, most studies on calcium were conducted 15–20 years ago, with limited new supporting evidence. However, it is clear that appropriate dietary calcium intake is more conducive to cardiovascular protection, whereas the use of calcium supplements requires caution.

Vitamins and cardiovascular health

Most vitamins participate in the regulation of cardiovascular health through antioxidant effects, metabolic modulation, vascular endothelial protection, and other pathways. Deficiency or excess of certain vitamins may affect the occurrence and progression of cardiovascular diseases.

Vitamin D deficiency is associated with multiple cardiovascular risk factors. Existing studies indicate that vitamin D deficiency promotes oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, endothelial dysfunction, hypertension, cardiac hypertrophy, and myocardial fibrosis (de la Guía-Galipienso et al., 2021; Herrmann et al., 2025; Renke et al., 2023). Notably, the effects of vitamin D are closely linked to the gut microbiota (Wyatt et al., 2024). Vitamin D can modulate dendritic cell function and promote the development of IL-10-producing(Interleukin-10) immunoregulatory Treg cells, and this immunomodulatory function can inhibit inflammation-induced apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells (Wellington et al., 2021). Microbes such as Lactobacillus can convert vitamin D into its active form, and probiotic supplementation improves calcium absorption efficiency (Zeb et al., 2025). Meanwhile, vitamin D can reciprocally regulate microbial composition. A randomized controlled trial showed that vitamin D supplementation increases the abundance of anti-inflammatory bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, reduces the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, and thereby decreases gut-derived TMAO(Trimethylamine N-oxide) (Wang et al., 2020; Zeb et al., 2025). However, excessive vitamin D exacerbates calcium‑phosphorus deposition, and the risk of its excess needs to be adjusted based on dietary calcium levels (Gallant et al., 2016). Animal experiments have suggested that combined supplementation of vitamin D and ω-3 fatty acids may impair the intestinal wall structure and increase fecal triglyceride levels in healthy rats (Abdulrazzaq et al., 2024a). In conclusion, although vitamin D exerts certain protective effects on the cardiovascular system, existing clinical trial evidence does not support cardiovascular benefits from vitamin D supplementation due to the complexity of gut microbiota and individual physical conditions.

Dietary intake of vitamins E and K significantly influences cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes in a dose-dependent manner. High vitamin E intake (≥9.86 mg/d) was associated with a 43% reduction in CVD risk and lower all-cause mortality (Zeng et al., 2025a), supported by RCTs showing lipid improvement and microbiota modulation-though excess supplementation may be harmful. Vitamin K2 supplementation notably improved glucose metabolism in T2DM patients, despite limited genetic evidence for a causal role in T1D (de la Barrera et al., 2024). B vitamin status is co-determined by diet and gut microbiota, which interact bidirectionally (Yang et al., 2024c). Deficiencies in B12 and folate are linked to specific microbial taxa and elevated cardiovascular mortality in T2DM (Hou et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2022), highlighting the importance of maintaining vitamin homeostasis through balanced dietary and microbial factors.

Vitamins and minerals do not function in isolation but rather within a complex interactive network, further modulated by bidirectional crosstalk with the gut microbiota. For instance, vitamins D and K act synergistically in cardiovascular and bone health: vitamin D stimulates the synthesis of vitamin K-dependent proteins, while vitamin K activates them via carboxylation, collectively improving bone mineral density and reducing vascular calcification (Zhang et al., 2023). Antioxidant nutrients such as vitamins C and E, together with β-carotene, interact to mitigate membrane oxidative stress, with vitamin C also enhancing non-heme iron absorption (Li et al., 2023a; van Ballegooijen et al., 2017). However, some combinations may yield neutral or adverse effects; for example, co-supplementation of vitamin E with ω-3 fatty acids has shown limited cardiovascular benefit in older adults and may potentially disrupt intestinal integrity (Abdulrazzaq et al., 2024b; Gaengler et al., 2024). Synergistic interactions and mediation by the gut microbiota

The gut microbiota plays a central role in mediating vitamin and mineral bioavailability and activity. Certain bacteria, including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, contribute to the activation of vitamin D and synthesis of B vitamins, thereby supporting calcium absorption (Hou et al., 2025a; Zeb et al., 2025). In turn, micronutrients help shape microbial composition: vitamin D supplementation increases the abundance of beneficial Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, lowers the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, and reduces TMAO production (Wang et al., 2020). Similarly, vitamin E enriches SCFA-producing bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae and Faecalibacterium, modulating bile acid metabolism (Chen et al., 2025b), while multivitamin/mineral supplements alter microbial community structure and elevate SCFA levels (McKirdy et al., 2025). Vitamins C and E and β-carotene also help maintain gut barrier function, reducing endotoxemia (Li et al., 2023a). Conversely, microbial dysbiosis, such as reduced Akkermansia abundance, has been linked to deficiencies in vitamins B12 and D (Hou et al., 2025b), underscoring the interdependence between micronutrient status and gut ecosystem balance.

Cohort studies and RCTs consistently demonstrate the superior safety and health benefits of dietary-derived vitamins and minerals compared to supplements. In terms of dosage, nutrients in natural foods generally align with physiological needs and seldom exceed safe thresholds, whereas supplements carry risks of overdose-for example, high-dose calcium increases myocardial infarction risk (Anderson & Klemmer, 2013), and excess selenium disrupts apoptosis (Zachariah, 2017). Regarding microbiota compatibility, dietary nutrients act synergistically with food matrices to support microbial health: whole grains rich in B vitamins simultaneously deliver these nutrients and promote growth of beneficial bacteria like Bifidobacterium. Supplements, while capable of short-term microbial modulation, lack this synergistic support, and their long-term impacts remain unclear. From health-outcome perspectives, dietary vitamin E intake correlates with reduced CVD risk, whereas supplemental vitamin E may promote peroxidation and increase risk (Chen et al., 2025b; Zeng et al., 2025a). Similarly, vitamin D supplementation benefits hypertensive patients predominantly in subgroups without diabetes/CVD, and only when dietary calcium intake is sufficient (Ye et al., 2024). In summary, the efficacy of vitamins and minerals depends on a balanced interaction of dosage, microbiota, and dietary context: appropriate intake forms the basis, gut microbiota acts as a key mediator, and whole-food sources represent the optimal means to achieve this balance. Future interventions should account for individual microbiota variation and aim to optimize micronutrient metabolism through personalized diets or targeted probiotic strategies.

Cardiovascular protection by specific foods

The cardiovascular effects of nutrients are mediated through their presence in whole foods, where protective outcomes arise from the synergistic interactions among diverse bioactive components. The influence of different food groups on cardiovascular health varies according to their compositional profile, processing methods, and consumption patterns. This section reviews current evidence on the roles of dairy, plant-based, animal-based, and specialty foods in the prevention of CVD.

Dairy consumption

Recent evidence has led to a reassessment of the relationship between dairy consumption and cardiovascular outcomes, shifting focus from total fat content to fat quality and product type. Cohort studies suggest that certain dairy-derived fatty acids may confer cardioprotective benefits. For instance, a Swedish cohort study observed that higher serum levels of pentadecanoic acid (15: 0) and heptadecanoic acid (17:0), established biomarkers of dairy fat intake, were linearly associated with a reduced risk of CVD (Trieu et al., 2021). An international multi-cohort analysis further indicated inverse associations between cheese and low-fat dairy consumption and CVD incidence, with cheese intake specifically correlated with a lower risk of stroke (Zhuang et al., 2025). Calcium, a key micronutrient in dairy, exhibits bidirectional effects: while supplemental calcium alone shows limited efficacy in fracture prevention, it may contribute to blood pressure reduction in younger populations and, when combined with vitamin D, does not increase coronary heart disease mortality (Torfadottir & Uusi-Rasi, 2023). These findings suggest that the cardiovascular impact of dairy is shaped by the complex food matrix and fatty acid composition, rather than fat content.

Plant-based/animal-based foods

Plant-based foods are consistently associated with a lower risk of CVD, largely due to their high content of dietary fiber, polyphenols, unsaturated fats, and essential micronutrients. In a study of overweight individuals in Malaysia, legume consumption (≥3 servings/day) was associated with a 44% reduction in CVD risk, likely mediated by improvements in lipid profiles (Jaafar et al., 2023; Thorisdottir et al., 2023). Nuts such as walnuts have been shown to support endothelial function (Jarai & Koller, 2024), while pistachios contribute to lipid metabolism modulation and beneficial alterations in gut microbiota (Mateos et al., 2022). Whole grains, particularly oats, are linked to reduced all-cause mortality, partly attributable to soluble fiber delaying triglyceride absorption and synthesis (Wehrli et al., 2021). A large Chinese cohort study reported that high intake of fruits and vegetables (>600 g/day) was associated with a 15% lower CVD risk, with fruits exhibiting particularly strong protective effects (Wang et al., 2022a). These results align with findings from the Golestan Cohort Study, which demonstrated an inverse relationship between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality, partially explained by anti-inflammatory SCFAs produced through microbial fermentation (Park et al., 2022).

The cardiovascular effects of animal-based foods differ substantially based on the food source, fatty acid profile, and processing degree. Fish and seafood, which are rich in long-chain omega-3 PUFAs such as EPA and DHA, are among the most consistently cardioprotective animal-derived foods. A South Korean cohort study found that high seafood consumption was associated with a 28% reduction in CVD risk in women, with enhanced benefits observed in individuals with adequate sleep quality (Kim et al., 2024; Park et al., 2022). Mechanistically, omega-3 PUFAs contribute to lipid homeostasis by reducing methylation of the ABCA1 gene promoter, thereby promoting cholesterol efflux from macrophages (Fujii et al., 2021). A dose-response meta-analysis further indicated that each 1 g/day increase in dietary ALA intake was associated with a 5% reduction in CVD mortality (Naghshi et al., 2021b).

In contrast, processed meats, characterized by high sodium, preservatives, and saturated fat content, are consistently associated with elevated CVD mortality and stroke risk (Meinila & Virtanen, 2024). The cardiovascular impact of red meat is influenced by animal rearing practices; grass-fed beef contains higher levels of n-3 PUFAs and CLA, along with lower saturated fat, compared to grain-fed beef, suggesting a more favorable risk profile (Nogoy et al., 2022). Evidence regarding poultry consumption is more differentiated: intake of skinless, unprocessed poultry may be associated with a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome, whereas consumption of poultry with skin has been linked to weight gain and unfavorable changes in lipid metabolism (Damigou et al., 2022). The balance between dietary omega-6 and omega-3 PUFAs also plays a critical role; an elevated omega-6/omega-3 ratio is independently associated with increased CVD mortality, while prescription-grade omega-3 supplementation reduces major adverse cardiovascular events by up to 12% in high-risk populations.

Specialty foods and dietary component

Certain specialty foods and dietary components demonstrate distinct cardiovascular effects due to their unique bioactive compositions. Regular avocado consumption (≥2 servings/week) is associated with a 16% lower CVD risk, with substitution analyses indicating greater benefit when avocados replace margarine or processed meats (Pacheco et al., 2022). Sacha Inchi (Plukenetia volubilis), which is rich in ALA and antioxidants, shows potential for improving dyslipidemia and hypertension, likely via Nrf2-mediated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways (Abd Rahman et al., 2023).

Sugar-sweetened beverages represent a modifiable dietary risk factor; each additional 250 mL consumed daily is associated with a 10% increase in CVD risk, primarily mediated through adverse effects on glucose and lipid metabolism (Sun et al., 2023). The relationship between egg consumption and CVD remains complex. While moderate intake may be neutral in healthy individuals, high consumption (>50 g/day) has been linked to elevated CVD risk in some populations, an effect largely attributable to obesity and hypertension, particularly observed in Asian cohorts (Yakti et al., 2024).

Drug therapy

The cardiovascular effects of foods are influenced by a combination of nutrient composition, bioactive properties, dietary context, and individual health characteristics. Future research should aim to clarify dose-response relationships, food matrix interactions, and population-specific factors to support evidence-based dietary guidance. At the public health level, a dietary pattern emphasizing plant-based foods, fish, and moderate dairy intake, while limiting processed meats and sugar-sweetened beverages, represents a well-established strategy for reducing the global burden of CVD.

While such dietary interventions remain CVD prevention, the management of established CVD has evolved into a multimodal strategy that integrates pharmacotherapy with sustained lifestyle optimization. Modern clinical approaches target key pathophysiological drivers, including dyslipidemia, thrombogenesis, hypertension, and maladaptive myocardial remodeling, through evidence-based drug classes, which are typically combined with long-term dietary and behavioral modifications to achieve optimal outcomes.

First-line therapies focus on lipid management, with statins HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors remaining the cornerstone. These agents reduce low-density lipoprotein cholesterol LDL-C by 20–55% and lower major adverse cardiovascular events MACE by 25–35% in secondary prevention populations (Chen et al., 2017). For thrombotic risk, antiplatelet agents such as aspirin and P2Y₁₂ inhibitors e.g. clopidogrel, ticagrelor inhibit platelet aggregation, critical in acute coronary syndromes and post-stent care. Antihypertensive regimens encompassing angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors ACEIs, angiotensin II receptor blockers ARBs, β-blockers, and calcium channel blockers collectively reduce blood pressure and CVD mortality, with choice guided by comorbidities e.g. ACEIs/ARBs for diabetes or heart failure. Emerging options expand this toolkit proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 PCSK9 inhibitors lower LDL-C by an additional 50–60% in statin-resistant or very-high-risk patients (Zeng et al., 2025b), while sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 SGLT2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 GLP-1 receptor agonists originally developed for type 2 diabetes have demonstrated independent cardiovascular and renal benefits, reducing heart failure hospitalizations and atherosclerotic events even in non-diabetic cohorts (Palmer et al., 2021).

The efficacy of dietary interventions and drugs is best understood as synergistic rather than mutually exclusive. Notably, large-scale randomized controlled trials RCTs have shown that adherence to the Mediterranean diet reduces MACE by ∼30% in primary prevention populations an effect comparable to moderate-intensity statin therapy (Buccheri et al., 2025). Yet the magnitude of benefit differs by context while a heart-healthy diet typically lowers LDL-C by 10–15%, statins achieve reductions of 30–50% or more, making them indispensable for high-risk patients with refractory dyslipidemia. Dietary strategies offer unique advantages, however they target multiple pathways simultaneously e.g. anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and endothelial-protective effects, carry minimal adverse effects, and address upstream metabolic dysfunction factors often overlooked by single-mechanism drugs. Clinicians must also navigate diet-drug interactions high fiber intake, for instance, may impair digoxin absorption, while grapefruit juice inhibits CYP3A4-mediated metabolism of certain statins, requiring patient education (Roşian Ş et al., 2025).

The interplay between diet and CVD is deeply shaped by food accessibility. In high-income settings including the U.S., U.K., Australia, and much of Europe the proliferation of inexpensive, ultra-processed foods has sustained high CVD prevalence despite advances in pharmacotherapy. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, dietary risks contribute to 20–25% of global CVD-related disability-adjusted life years DALYs, driven by excess sodium, inadequate whole grains, and low fruit intake (Lancet, 2019). Similar trends emerge in rapidly urbanizing middle-income nations in China and India, rising consumption of processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and refined carbohydrates over the past two decades has paralleled a 40% increase in CVD incidence (Sun et al., 2020). These shifts underscore the urgency of population-level policies front-of-pack labeling, sugar taxes, and trans-fat restrictions implemented in countries like Mexico and Denmark, which have already yielded measurable declines in CVD mortality (Jarzebski et al., 2021).

Optimal CVD management today integrates evidence-based dietary patterns with targeted pharmacotherapy, tailored to individual risk profiles, comorbidities, and cultural contexts. While medications provide potent, rapid risk reduction, dietary interventions offer foundational benefits that enhance drug efficacy and address root causes of metabolic dysfunction. Future efforts must prioritize improving global food environments particularly in regions undergoing nutrition transitions to curb the growing CVD burden linked to processed food consumption. By aligning clinical care with public health action, we can bridge the gap between prevention and treatment, ultimately reducing CVD morbidity and mortality worldwide.

Food synergistic effect of dietary patterns and regulation of cardiovascular metabolism

Main healthy dietary patterns and their characteristics

The cardiovascular benefits of dietary patterns stem from their synergistic nature, enabling multi-target interventions against pathways like dyslipidemia, inflammation, and gut dysbiosis. Consequently, dietary patterns demonstrate more robust and consistent protective effects in clinical and observational studies than any single nutrient or food. This evidence supports a shift toward evaluating overall dietary quality, reflecting the complex etiology of CVD. Roles of Dietary Components in Cardiovascular Disease Protection and Related Mechanisms.

Plant-based diets gain core advantages from the synergy of high-quality plant components, and are divided into healthy (hPDI) and unhealthy (uPDI) subtypes. Characterized by high intake of non-starchy vegetables, whole grains, and fish, hPDI can reduce the overall CVD risk by 8%, the myocardial infarction risk by 18% (Wang, 2025), and the CVD mortality rate of adults with sarcopenia by 70% (Liu et al., 2025). The mechanisms include soluble fiber binding intestinal cholesterol, polyphenols activating the Nrf2 pathway to alleviate oxidative stress, and phytosterols inhibiting cholesterol absorption (Glenn et al., 2021a; Qiao et al., 2025). In contrast, uPDI, rich in refined carbohydrates and processed plant foods, counteracts these protective effects. Gender-specific differences are also observed: in the Rotterdam Study, each 1-SD increase in hPDI reduces CVD risk by 8% in men, while hPDI shows a U-shaped correlation with stroke risk in women, indicating the need for personalized nutrition plans (Wang et al., 2024).

The Mediterranean diet (AMED), emphasizing plant-based foods, olive oil, and moderate fish intake, demonstrates consistent cardioprotection. Evidence links higher adherence to a 50% reduction in CVD risk, improved lipid profiles, and significant anti-inflammatory effects (Ghosh-Jerath et al., 2025; Mata-Fernandez et al., 2021; Panbehkar-Jouybari et al., 2021). A Greek microsimulation suggested that increasing protective foods-fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes-has greater impact than reducing meat and full-fat dairy, predicting 851 fewer first CVD events per 100,000 people with only a 10% increase in dietary adherence (Kouvari et al., 2022). Nonetheless, real-world adherence is often suboptimal, even among high-risk patients, and the lack of standardized scoring systems impedes its formal integration into therapeutic guidelines (Ghosh-Jerath et al., 2025).

The DASH diet, designed to lower blood pressure, significantly reduces the risk of hypercholesterolemia-related events and unfavorable lipid ratios, primarily through vascular effects of high potassium and magnesium intake (Panbehkar-Jouybari et al., 2021). However, its efficacy may be condition-specific; in diabetic patients, DASH offers less CVD protection than a modified Mediterranean diet (Liu et al., 2023). Other patterns also contribute insights: a hybrid Japanese-Western breakfast is associated with lower CVD mortality, while skipping breakfast increases coronary risk, likely due to metabolic dysregulation (Tang et al., 2023). Notably, the ketogenic diet is beneficial for obesity-related CVD, but its high-fat nature may exacerbate dyslipidemia in diabetic patients, requiring cautious application (Zhang et al., 2021). Overall, key principles for atherosclerosis prevention include replacing animal fats with olive oil and limiting processed meats, offering practical implementation guidance (Riccardi et al., 2022).

Common cardiovascular protective pathways of healthy dietary patterns

Despite differences in composition, major healthy dietary patterns exert cardiovascular protective effects through four key pathways. At the level of lipid metabolism, monounsaturated fatty acids in the Mediterranean diet reduce the proportion of LDL; phytosterols in plant-based diets inhibit intestinal cholesterol absorption; and the high dietary fiber content of the DASH diet delays triglyceride synthesis (Panbehkar-Jouybari et al., 2021; Riccardi et al., 2022). In terms of anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, polyphenols in plant-based diets suppress the NF-κB pathway, while polyphenols in olive oil activate the SIRT1 pathway, and the two work synergistically to alleviate chronic inflammation and oxidative stress (Choi et al., 2021). Regarding vascular endothelial function, dietary nitrates are converted by gut microbiota into nitric oxide (NO), promoting vasodilation. Simultaneously, omega-3 PUFAs from fish intake inhibit platelet aggregation, collectively supporting endothelial integrity and reducing thrombosis risk (Tsoupras et al., 2022). In terms of gut microbiota modulation, dietary fiber from whole grains is fermented into SCFAs, which regulate immune response via GPR43; the Mediterranean diet also enriches beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia, enhances gut barrier function, and establishes a protective microbiota-metabolism-cardiovascular axis (Clark et al., 2022).

To effectively translate these evidence-based insights into practice, three core priorities must be emphasized: precision, cultural adaptation, and multisectoral support. Stratified dietary recommendations fall into three key categories: the Mediterranean diet for high-risk populations, which offers proven benefits for CVD prevention; the DASH diet for hypertensive individuals, given its focus on balancing potassium and sodium; and healthy plant-based patterns for younger adults, designed to mitigate midlife CVD risk early on. To enhance the feasibility of these recommendations, culturally adapted adjustments are critical—such as substituting tea seed oil for olive oil in Asian diets and incorporating local functional foods like turmeric or green tea. Notably, specific groups like vegetarians also require targeted guidance to prevent micronutrient deficiencies, as their dietary restrictions may otherwise undermine the benefits of these recommended patterns. Crucially, sustained adherence to these diets depends on multidimensional support strategies, which encompasses culinary education to build practical cooking skills, policy interventions to reduce access to processed foods, and targeted efforts to address socioeconomic barriers that hinder healthy eating (Jilani et al., 2021; Krenek et al., 2025).

Bioactive compounds from medicinal foods in CVD management

While modern dietary patterns emphasize the broad protective benefits of food diversity, traditional medicine-food homologous substances serve as concentrated sources of bioactive compounds that complement modern nutrition through personalized, multi-target mechanisms. These natural compounds, particularly phenolic acids and flavonoids, have demonstrated significant cardiometabolic benefits. Fig. 5 illustrates how these Chinese herbal medicines exert their effects in CVD. In hyperlipidemia, cinnamic acid modulates key glycolipid metabolic enzymes (Safaeian et al., 2025), while ellagic acid and its metabolite urolithin ameliorate lipid dysregulation and inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Li et al., 2023b). For vascular protection, terpenophenols such as carvacrol reduce inflammatory and apoptotic damage (Ghani et al., 2023; Khazdair et al., 2024), and anthocyanins support gut microbiota homeostasis to delay vascular endothelial senescence (Dong et al., 2022). Apple-derived polar lipids, rich in monounsaturated fatty acids, suppress platelet aggregation induced by platelet-activating factor and ADP, offering antithrombotic benefits (Tsoupras et al., 2021a); similar activity is observed in phosphatidylcholine from apple processing by-products (Tsoupras et al., 2021b).

Mechansims of Traditional Chinese Medicine Components in Modulating Cardiovascular Functions and Diseases.

Traditional Chinese medicine formulations and plant extracts act through synergistic multi-pathway mechanisms. Compounds such as salvianolic acid from Salvia miltiorrhiza suppress pro-inflammatory signaling via the α7nAchR-NF-κB/STAT3 axis, reducing IL-6 and TNF-α release (Zou et al., 2023). Formulations like Yiyiren Fuzi San modulate mitochondrial-endoplasmic reticulum interactions to improve calcium homeostasis and oxidative stress (Ding et al., 2024). In metabolic cardiovascular disorders, paeoniflorin regulates glycolipid metabolism and inhibits atherosclerosis (Li et al., 2023c), and Ilex pubescens targets HSP90AA1 and ESRα to alleviate myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (Zheng et al., 2022). Icariin contributes to cardiac repair in heart failure and myocardial infarction by attenuating apoptosis and inflammation (Zeng et al., 2022). Clinical studies show that Terminalia chebula powder produces lipid- and blood pressure-lowering effects comparable to amlodipine and atorvastatin (Saleem et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2022). Furthermore, salvianolic acid B in Danhong Injection curbs endothelial autophagy via the miR-19a/SIRT1 pathway (Guo et al., 2021), illustrating the multi-target potential of botanicals in cardiovascular protection.

Studies on dietary interventions and specific food ingredients provide practical evidence. Specifically, in virtual vegan cooking interventions, the group with high EVOO intake showed a significant increase in skin carotenoid levels, which was associated with improved dietary quality (Krenek et al., 2025). A magnesium-rich diet is associated with reduced risks of CVD and coronary heart disease (Copp et al., 2025). What's more, Brassicaceae microgreens improve hemodynamic parameters in hypertensive rats by regulating lipid metabolism (Ramirez et al., 2024). Moreover, PHB, an extract from Undaria pinnatifida, upregulates eNOS expression and alleviates nanoplastics-induced endothelial senescence (Sim et al., 2024). Intake of soy products is associated with lower all-cause and CVD mortality, and intake of ≥4 times per week can reduce the risk of death from acute myocardial infarction (Lu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021b). High dietary spermidine from vegetables, grains, etc., is associated with reduced CVD mortality risk. Additionally, green tea consumption reduces the risks of CVD and coronary heart disease in overweight/obese patients with type 2 diabetes, and this effect is related to the duration of consumption (Liu et al., 2024).

Research on supplemental and functional ingredients continues to broaden their potential applications in cardiovascular health. For instance, vitamin K supplementation has demonstrated inhibitory effects on vascular calcification (Kampmann et al., 2023), while sulforaphane activates Nrf2 via the ERK/GSK-3β/Fyn pathway to protect against angiotensin II-induced aortic injury (Wang et al., 2022b). Similarly, the natural compound taraxasterol has been shown to attenuate cardiac aging and fibrosis (Li et al., 2022). The flavonoid acacetin exerts cardioprotection through anti-inflammatory and anti-obesity mechanisms, and terpenoids and flavonoids in propolis contribute to the amelioration of atherosclerosis and hypertension (Ruskovska et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2022). Yerba mate polyphenols modulate immune cell gene expression, suggesting a potential role in cardiovascular protection (Ruskovska et al., 2023).

However, the translation of medicine-food homologous substances into practical applications faces several challenges. Some studies report no significant association between flavonoid intake and mortality (Castaneda et al., 2024), and HFCS consumption significantly elevates CVD risk among Black young adults (Dechristopher & Tucker, 2024). Both excessively high and low dietary acid loads are associated with increased CVD mortality (Hejazi et al., 2022), and the protective effect of lycopene remains controversial (Przybylska & Tokarczyk, 2022). Future research should prioritize elucidating dose-response relationships, population-specific effects, and clinical translatability to advance precision-based dietary interventions for cardiovascular disease prevention and management.

Conclusion and future outlook

This review synthesizes evidence on the multi-level regulatory effects of dietary factors, from isolated nutrients to whole dietary patterns, on cardiovascular disease. Key findings indicate that whole-food sources of nutrients offer superior safety and efficacy compared to isolated supplements, and that macronutrient quality exerts a greater influence on CVD risk than quantity alone. Healthy dietary patterns, including the Mediterranean, DASH, and well-designed plant-based diets, consistently reduce CVD incidence through integrated effects on lipid metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, and gut microbiota. Bioactive components such as polyphenols, ω-3 PUFAs, and specific vitamins and minerals contribute to cardiovascular protection through structure-specific mechanisms.

In addition to dietary factors, regular physical activity represents a critical lifestyle determinant that synergizes with healthy eating patterns to optimize cardiovascular outcomes. Accumulating evidence indicates that combined diet and exercise interventions yield greater improvements in insulin sensitivity, endothelial function, and inflammatory profiles than either modality alone. Mechanistically, exercise enhances skeletal muscle glucose uptake, reduces visceral adiposity, and upregulates antioxidant defense systems, thereby amplifying the cardiometabolic benefits of a heart-healthy diet. Future lifestyle interventions should therefore integrate both nutritional counseling and physical activity promotion as complementary, non-negotiable components of CVD prevention.

Several critical evidence gaps remain. Future research should clarify the interactions between dietary patterns and genetic, epigenetic, and gut microbial factors, and quantify the heterogeneity in response across populations. Dose-response relationships, bioavailability, and long-term safety of key bioactive compounds require further validation in robust clinical trials. The influence of socioeconomic and cultural factors on dietary adherence also warrants systematic investigation.

Current pharmacological approaches for CVD management, including statins, antiplatelet agents, ACEIs/ARBs, β-blockers, PCSK9 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors, are discussed in detail in Section 3.4, where we also compare the complementary roles of diet and pharmacotherapy and analyze the impact of processed food environments on global CVD burden. The interplay between diet and medication underscores the need for integrated care models that address both lifestyle and pharmacological aspects.

Moving forward, the field should prioritize the development of integrated risk assessment models that incorporate multi-omics, biomarker, and environmental data to enable dynamic, personalized nutrition guidance. Well-designed randomized controlled trials and long-term cohort studies are needed to establish optimal dietary patterns and bioactive compound thresholds for diverse populations. Implementation efforts should leverage digital health tools and community-based support to enhance feasibility, while policy actions should promote healthier food environments. Through interdisciplinary collaboration, CVD prevention can evolve from generalized recommendations toward precision strategies, ultimately reducing the global burden of cardiovascular disease.
