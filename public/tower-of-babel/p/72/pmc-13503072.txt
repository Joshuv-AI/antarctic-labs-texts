Physician modification of a thoracoabdominal branch endoprosthesis into a shortened unibody branched device
Authors: Mitri K. Khoury, Shiv Patel, Richard T. Rogers, Tiziano Tallarita, Avigayil Ribner, Curtis Erickson, Alex Westerband, Hasan Aldailami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A short distance between the lowest renal artery and the flow divider after previous endovascular aortic repair presents a significant technical challenge, often necessitating open conversion. The GORE Thoracoabdominal Branch Endoprosthesis (W. L. Gore & Associates) offers an off-the-shelf endovascular solution for these patients; however, its fixed length results in extensive supraceliac coverage, increasing the risk of spinal cord ischemia. In addition, in smaller paravisceral aortas branch crowding may compromise visceral stent patency. We present a case of physician modification of the Thoracoabdominal Branch Endoprosthesis device into a shortened unibody bifurcated configuration by removing and reconstructing the distal aspect of the device. This technique may expand endovascular options for patients with limited infrarenal working length to the flow divider of a previous endovascular aortic repair while mitigating risks associated with standard device configurations.

A short distance between the lowest renal artery and flow divider following previous endovascular aortic repair (EVAR) presents a significant technical challenge.1 Historically, these patients were often unsuitable for endovascular reintervention due to limited infrarenal working length to the flow divider which often requires open conversion. Several alternative strategies have emerged to obviate the need for open conversion which include fenestrated and/or branched EVAR (F/BEVAR), inverted iliac limb,2 and unibody configurations3; however, each approach carries inherent technical limitations.

BEVAR using a conventional bifurcated component offers a practical solution in this setting. The GORE Thoracoabdominal Branch Endoprosthesis (TAMBE; W. L. Gore & Associates) is an off-the-shelf device that enables treatment of patients with limited distance between the renal arteries and the flow divider. However, its fixed length results in substantial supraceliac aortic coverage, increasing the risk of spinal cord ischemia.

We present a case of a patient with previous EVAR and a type IA endoleak in the setting of limited infrarenal working length to the flow divider, treated with a physician-modified TAMBE device. This modification creates a shortened unibody bifurcated configuration designed to reduce supraceliac coverage and minimize mechanical stress within the paravisceral aorta. The patient provided written informed consent for the report of his case details and imaging studies.

Case report and procedure details

An 80-year-old man presented with an enlarging juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm following EVAR with an Endologix Alto device (Endologix LLC) performed 5 years earlier. The aneurysm measured 5.2 cm at the index procedure and was treated due to rapid growth. Surveillance computed tomography (CT) demonstrated sac expansion to 6.1 cm with a suspected type IA endoleak secondary to proximal neck degeneration (Fig 1). Centerline analysis revealed 37 mm from the lowest renal artery to the flow divider, limiting the feasibility of unibody fenestrated EVAR or fenestrated EVAR with an inverted iliac limb (Fig 2). In addition, the suprarenal fixation of the Endologix Alto device went to the level of the celiac artery (Fig 2). The suprarenal fixation of the Endologix Alto device is extremely rigid and landing inside of this prevents apposition of the graft on to the aortic wall. Therefore, at least 20 mm of supraceliac coverage is preferred to prevent a type 1A endoleak. Thus, BEVAR using a GORE TAMBE was selected. However, the 160-mm device length would have required deployment approximately 1 cm above the flow divider, resulting in an estimated 80 mm of supraceliac coverage and risking coverage of critical intercostal arteries identified on CT scan (Fig 2). In addition, the paravisceral aorta measured 24 mm in diameter, raising concern for branch crowding and increased radial force. Device modification

The TAMBE device contains three longitudinal deployment wires that must remain intact throughout modification to preserve deployment functionality. A gold band located 35 mm proximal to the distal end of the graft was retained to serve as the base of the reconstructed iliac gates. (Fig 3, A, Video 1, online only). The distal sleeve was removed by dividing the posterior deployment line with an 11-blade, followed by deployment of the distal segment using a hemostat (Fig 3, B and C, Video 1, online only). The anterior fixation knot securing the outer sleeve was then divided, allowing the sleeve to be peeled back and removed (Fig 3, D and E, Video 1, online only). There is also a wire that tacks the distal aspect of the TAMBE device. The distal fixation wire was released by incising the surrounding graft material, freeing the distal component from the delivery system (Fig 3, F and G, Video 1, online only). The graft fabric was circumferentially incised, the distal stents are transected, and the distal part of the graft is removed (Fig 3, H-J, Video 1, online only). Additional stent rows were excised several centimeters proximal to the gold band, and the remaining fabric was trimmed flush to this marker (Fig 3, K, Video 1, online only). A large clip was placed centrally to create two approximately 10-mm iliac gates, one containing the delivery system and the other free (Fig 3, L, Video 1, online only). The fabric was secured around the clip using 4-0 GORE-TEX sutures (Fig 3, L, Video 1, online only). Radiographic markers were added to the lateral gate to facilitate intraoperative cannulation (Fig 3, L, Video 1, online only). A through-and-through wire was then placed across the newly created iliac gates (Fig 3, M, Video 1, online only), and the portals were marked to maintain orientation following resheathing (Fig 3, N, Video 1, online only). Implantation

Percutaneous access of the bilateral common femoral arteries was obtained, and a left brachial artery cutdown was performed. A 22F DrySeal sheath (W. L. Gore & Associates) was introduced through the left common femoral artery, and a 14F sheath via the left brachial artery. Through-and-through wire access was established from the brachial to femoral approach.

The TAMBE device portals were prewired with 0.014-inch wires and advanced into position over the through-and-through wire. Stage 1 deployment was performed, followed by cannulation of the celiac artery. The device was positioned as close to the celiac origin as possible to minimize aortic coverage. Stage 2 deployment was completed, and the celiac artery was stented. The superior mesenteric artery and bilateral renal arteries were subsequently cannulated and stented. Stage 3 deployment was performed before stenting the final visceral branch.

Following visceral revascularization, attention was turned to reconstruction of the bifurcation. Owing to removal of the distal metal framework, the iliac gate fabric was prone to collapse, complicating cannulation. Using the prepositioned through-and-through wire, a 6-mm balloon was advanced into the contralateral gate via the ipsilateral sheath to expand the lumen (Fig 4, A). The gate was then successfully cannulated, and a 14-mm balloon was used to confirm intraluminal positioning (Fig 4, B).Fig 4A, The contralateral gate is opened using a 6 mm balloon advanced over the through-and-through wire placed during modification. Discussion

The management of patients with a short distance between the lowest renal artery and flow divider in the setting of previous EVAR remains technically challenging. Historically, these patients were often relegated to open repair due to anatomic constraints that limited the feasibility of standard endovascular options. However, with the evolution of fenestrated and branched technologies, multiple endovascular strategies have emerged, each with inherent trade-offs.

In this report, we describe a novel physician modification of the GORE TAMBE to create a shortened unibody bifurcated configuration. This approach was specifically designed to address two major limitations of conventional TAMBE use in this anatomy: excessive supraceliac coverage and increased device crowding within the paravisceral segment.4 Standard TAMBE deployment in this patient would have resulted in at least 80 mm of supraceliac aortic coverage, exceeding the minimum required length and placing the patient at elevated risk for spinal cord ischemia. By shortening the device and eliminating the need for an additional bifurcated component, we were able to minimize aortic coverage while preserving critical intercostal arteries.

Another important consideration is the hemodynamic and mechanical impact of device configuration in smaller paravisceral aortas. In this case, the visceral segment diameter was approximately 24 mm, raising concerns for branch competition and chronic outward force exerted by overlapping stent graft components. The modified unibody design reduces the number of overlapping layers and eliminates the need for an internal bifurcated piece, thereby decreasing the radial force from the main body onto the branching and improving long-term visceral stent patency. In addition, by creating a unibody device, this eliminates the need to balloon overlapping segments in an already crowded paravisceral segment. Although this theoretical advantage is compelling, long-term data will be required to confirm durability.

Alternative approaches—including fenestrated EVAR with inverted iliac limbs, unibody FEVAR, or BEVAR with a conventional bifurcated component—have all been described for similar anatomy.2,3 However, these techniques may be limited by inadequate working length, technical complexity, or risk of component separation. The present technique offers a hybrid solution that leverages the off-the-shelf availability of TAMBE while adapting it to patient-specific anatomic constraints. Importantly, the use of physician-modified endografts allows for procedural customization but requires meticulous attention to device integrity, particularly preservation of deployment mechanisms and structural stability.

The authors found this technique advantageous when managing a type IA endoleak following implantation of an Endologix Alto device. The Alto endograft presents unique technical challenges due to its short, conical main body configuration.5 This anatomy can make transfemoral navigation within the pararenal segment particularly difficult, favoring an upper-extremity approach for renal branch cannulation. In addition, definitive treatment generally requires relining of the graft to minimize the risk of subsequent type III endoleak formation.

Despite the technical success demonstrated in this case, several limitations warrant discussion. First, this is a single-case experience, and generalizability remains uncertain. Second, physician modification of commercially available devices is off-label and may carry regulatory and medicolegal considerations. Third, the long-term durability of the modified iliac gates—constructed without a supporting metal framework—remains unknown, particularly with respect to gate stability, limb patency, and risk of fabric-related complications. Studies are required to determine the integrity of these iliac gates that lack a metal framework and determine if there is a substantial type 3 endoleak risk. Consideration can be given to sewing a wire, coil, or even the struts from the resected graft to sew onto the iliac gates to provide structural integrity if fabric alone is not sufficient.

In addition, although the technique reduces supraceliac coverage, it introduces additional procedural complexity, including the need for precise device modification and careful cannulation of fabric-based iliac gates. These steps may increase operative time and require a high level of operator expertise. As such, this approach may be best suited for experienced centers with familiarity in advanced endovascular and physician-modified endograft techniques. Lastly, TAMBE can be cost-prohibitive in certain centers and accidental damage of the device during the modification process can result in financially disadvantageous situations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, physician modification of the TAMBE device into a shortened unibody bifurcated configuration represents a feasible and innovative solution for patients with limited infrarenal working length after previous EVAR. This strategy may reduce supraceliac coverage and mitigate issues related to device crowding in the paravisceral segment. Further experience and longitudinal follow-up are needed to evaluate the safety, durability, and broader applicability of this technique.
