A Rare Case of a Solid Variant Aneurysmal Bone Cyst of the Medial Sphenoid Bone: Clinical Features, Diagnostic Points, and Treatment
Authors: Shinji YAMASHITA, Fumitaka MATSUMOTO, Hironobu OKUYAMA, Natsuki OGASAWARA, Mitsuru TAMURA, Tomoki KAWANO, Kiyotaka YOKOGAMI, Takumi KIWAKI, Tsuyoshi FUKUSHIMA, Yuichiro SATO, Takumi TOMONAGA, Yoshiko OKITA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A 5-year-old boy presented to our hospital with ptosis and an abnormal ocular position. Magnetic resonance imaging showed a well-defined mass measuring 20 mm in diameter in the medial sphenoid bone extending to the orbit and compressing the external ocular muscle. The patient underwent total surgical excision and was subsequently diagnosed with a solid variant of aneurysmal bone cyst via molecular integrated diagnosis. Solid variant of aneurysmal bone cyst is an extremely rare subtype of aneurysmal bone cyst, accounting for 0.2% of all primary bone tumors. It is characterized by the absence of a solid cystic component, which is difficult to diagnose via conventional hematoxylin and eosin staining. Molecular analyses revealed that this subtype is also characterized by the rearrangement of USP6 and the absence of the H3F3A mutation. This report discusses the clinical features of this extremely rare neoplastic lesion, the importance of an integrated diagnosis, and treatment options.

Introduction

Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is a benign, expansile, osteolytic lesion that is characterized by thin-walled, blood-filled cystic spaces. It accounts for 1%-2% of all primary bone tumors. ABCs can develop in almost any bone but most commonly in the metaphysis of long tubular bones, the vertebrae, and the pelvic bones.1-3) The solid variant of ABC (SVABC) is a rare subtype, accounting for 3.4%-7.5% of all ABCs.4) It is characterized by a variable solid material without a significant cystic component, as typically observed in classical ABCs.5-7) Owing to its distinct histological features, SVABC often lacks the typical radiographic characteristics of ABCs, thereby resulting in a broad differential diagnosis. Thus, molecular analyses are often required for an accurate diagnosis, with USP6 rearrangements serving as a hallmark finding.5)

We herein present a rare case of SVABC occurring in the medial sphenoid bone with a favorable prognosis owing to the total removal of the tumor. Furthermore, we discuss the clinical features, therapeutic approach, and essential point of diagnosis for SVABCs.

Case Report

A 5-year-old boy presented with ptosis and abnormal eye position after a minor injury to the right orbit, which typically does not cause symptoms. He was assessed by an ophthalmologist and was found to have right exotropia and ptosis. Thus, he was referred to a neurosurgeon for further evaluation. Physical examination showed that he was alert and cooperative and had no focal neurological deficits. A head computed tomography (CT) scan showed a well-defined mass lesion measuring approximately 25 mm in diameter with a rimlike high-absorption area at the tumor margin and thinning of the medial sphenoid bone around the tumor (Fig. 1A and B). CT angiography revealed no aneurysms (Fig. 1C). Head magnetic resonance imaging showed a solitary mass lesion in the right half of the sphenoid bone with an intraorbital involvement, which was isointense on the T1-weighted images, heterogeneously hyperintense on the T2-weighted images, and homogeneously enhanced on the T1 Gadolinium enhanced images (Fig. 1D-F). The arterial spin labeling image showed increased perfusion only at the edge of the lesion (Fig. 1G). Whole-body bone scintigraphy showed hyperaccumulation only in the skull base lesion (Fig. 1H and I). Cerebral angiography was not performed due to concerns about the potential adverse effects of radiation exposure in a 5-year-old boy. Based on these findings, we preoperatively considered Langerhans cell histiocytosis, plasmacytoma, giant cell tumor, solitary fibrous tumor, and ABC and removed the tumorlike lesion.

Right frontotemporal craniotomy with zygotomy was performed to approach the tumor. The tumor was an extra-axial mass with a grayish color and was easily hemorrhagic (Fig. 2A). No invasion of the dura mater and periorbital membranes was observed, and the brain parenchyma and extraocular muscles were intact. The blood flow to the tumor was reduced by processing the feeder from the middle meningeal arteries. Then, internal decompression was employed to separate the tumor from the surrounding tissue. At the bottom of the tumor, the sphenoidal bones were invaded and cranial nerve V2 was involved, but it was carefully preserved (Fig. 2B). Finally, the tumor was totally removed. The amount of blood lost during the surgery was 220 mL, and 280 mL of irradiated red cell concentrate, and 240 mL of fresh frozen plasma were transfused.

Histological examination via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that the spindle-shaped cells proliferated, forming fascicles and a storiform structure. Osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells were intermingled. The giant cells exhibited a non-uniform distribution. Hemorrhage was associated in part. High mitotic activity or apparent atypical mitosis could not be identified. Furthermore, the cystic structure was inconspicuous (Fig. 3A and B). Immunohistochemically, the multinucleated cells were positive for CD68 (PG-M1), whereas the spindle cells were negative for CD68 (PG-M1) (Fig. 3C). The multinucleated and spindle cells were negative for H3.3 G34W immunostaining (Fig. 3D). Dual-color split-apart ubiquitin-specific peptidase 6 (USP6) fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay was conducted using probes targeting the centromeric (green) and telomeric (red) regions of the USP6 locus. The FISH analysis revealed the presence of a USP6 rearrangement (Fig. 3E). Taken together, the histopathological diagnosis was confirmed to be SVABC. No additional postoperative therapy was performed, and the ptosis and abnormal eye position had improved after 6 months. There has been no evidence of the lesion recurrence for 2 years.

Discussion

ABC is a benign expansile osteolytic lesion consisting of thin-walled, blood-filled cystic spaces, with an incidence peaking in the first 2 decades of life. It is a rare neoplasm accounting for approximately 1%-2% of all bone tumors and commonly develops in the metaphysis of long tubular bones, the vertebrae, and the pelvis outside of the head/neck region.1-3) Cranial ABC is particularly uncommon, accounting for only 2%-6% of all ABCs.8) Meanwhile, SVABC, characterized by a variable solid material without a significant cystic component, is an uncommon entity accounting for 3.4%-7.5% of all ABCs.4) Only 7 SVABC cases involving the skull, including the present one, have been reported in the literature.6,7,9-11) Among the 7 cases, only 4 involved evaluation of the USP6 rearrangement, of which 3 (75%) were positive. Interestingly, all 7 cases (100%) were men; 5 (71%) of them were aged below 20 years, and 4 (57%) had SVABC occurring near the orbit and exhibiting ocular symptoms (Table 1). Contrarily, classical ABC involving the skull has been reported to be more common among women, with those aged below 20 years accounting for 80% of the cases.12,13) Regarding the location, the calvaria is the most frequently affected site in the skull. The bones most commonly involved in the skull are the temporal bone (22%), occipital bone (20%), frontal bone (14%), parietal bone (11%), orbit (11%), sinus (5%), sphenoidal bone and ethmoid bone (3%).14) These findings indicate that the potential characteristic features of SVABC involving the skull are male predilection and occurrence near the orbit.

Unlike classic ABC, SVABC is difficult to diagnose not only because of its rarity but also due to its unusual radiological and histopathological characteristics. On imaging, classical ABC typically appears as a “blow-out” lesion on plain films and exhibits an expansile soft tissue mass with eggshell-like bone margins and fluid-fluid levels.1) Contrarily, SVABC usually presents as an expansile and osteolytic solid lesion that is iso- to hypo-intense relative to the muscle and exhibits homogeneous contrast enhancement.4,15) These imaging features make SVABCs challenging to distinguish from other conditions, with a broad differential diagnosis including Ewing sarcoma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, osteosarcoma, and giant cell tumor of the bone (GCTB).

Histologically, classical ABCs are characterized by cavernous, hemorrhagic cystic spaces surrounded or traversed by fibrous septa containing spindle cells, scattered osteoclast-like giant cells, and a reactive woven bone.16) However, the absence of blood-filled cystic spaces and the predominantly solid architecture in SVABCs lead to the consideration of several conditions, such as GCTB, brown tumor, and osteoblastoma, in the differential diagnosis.5)

In the present case, molecular and immunohistochemical analyses helped achieve a definitive diagnosis. H3.3 G34W immunostaining and USP6 rearrangement evaluation via FISH have proven particularly valuable. Amary et al.17) reported that the H3F3A G34W mutation is present in 90.6% of GCTB cases, making it a reliable marker for differentiating GCTB from SVABC. In addition, recent studies have demonstrated that USP6 rearrangements are detected in approximately 65%-70% of classical ABCs and their solid variants, making this molecular test a widely used diagnostic tool.1,5,18) As these molecular features are not completely present in all SVABC cases, an integrated diagnosis based on findings from H&E staining, H3.3 G34W mutation analysis, and USP6 rearrangement evaluation is essential for distinguishing SVABC from other pathologies. Although the prognosis for ABC and SVABC is generally favorable, treatment is necessary to slow down disease progression and alleviate symptoms. In particular, ABCs in the sphenoid bone of children have been reported to cause progressive visual deterioration, proptosis, and ocular motility disorders due to compression of the globe, optic nerve, extraocular muscles, and associated nerves. Delayed diagnosis can lead to irreversible functional impairment, as the lesion may rapidly enlarge over weeks to months, causing structural bone destruction and neuro-ophthalmologic complications. Surgical removal and potential orbital bone reconstruction are often considered treatment options.19) Curettage, with or without bone grafting, has become the standard of care in ABC management; however, the recurrence rate for this approach has been reported to reach as high as 59%.20,21) Meanwhile, en bloc resection has the lowest reported recurrence rate of as low as 10%.3) Despite its effectiveness, this method is not commonly performed due to the increased risk of functional disabilities, including pathological fractures.22) To address the limitations of conventional approaches, several alternative therapeutic modalities have been used to decrease the recurrence rates. These modalities include intraoperative sclerotherapy, percutaneous doxycycline injection, arterial embolization, intracavitary corticosteroid administration, and the use of Receptor Activator of NF-kB Ligand inhibitors, all demonstrating efficacy in specific clinical settings.23-29) Meanwhile, radiotherapy was not found to be effective and is associated with reports of malignant transformation, including sarcoma development, at the site of previously irradiated ABCs.30,31)

In the present case, the tumor was totally removed, and the patient had a favorable prognosis without additional treatment. For cranial tumors involving the sphenoid ridge, the risk of bone instability or pathological fractures associated with total resection need not be considered, making gross total resection an advantageous approach. Previous cases of tumors in this location have also shown good prognoses after gross total resection (Table 1).6,7,9-11) In contrast, in the present case, there was a significant amount of intraoperative bleeding, necessitating a blood transfusion. Zhu et al.19) discussed the importance of completely removing all ABCs in the spheno-orbital region in children to prevent a recurrence, and pre-preparing blood in case of intraoperative bleeding. Saez et al.6) also reported the safe removal of SVABC in the temporal bone of a 6-year-old child following preoperative embolization of the feeding artery. Similarly, preoperative embolization could have been performed in our case and was considered an effective approach to minimize intraoperative bleeding.

Conclusions

We herein report a rare case of SVABC occurring in the medial sphenoid bone. Diagnosing SVABC, a rare ABC subtype that is exceptionally uncommon as a skull tumor, proved challenging not only in preoperative imaging but also in postoperative pathological evaluation. Male predilection and occurrence near the orbit are potential characteristic features of SVABC involving the skull. For this condition, total removal of the lesion with careful attention to bleeding and integrated diagnosis are recommended.
