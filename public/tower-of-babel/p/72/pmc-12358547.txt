Deep learning and wavelet packet transform for fault diagnosis in double circuit transmission lines
Authors: Ziad M. Ali, Ehab M. Esmail
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fault diagnosis in double-circuit transmission lines (DCTLs) involves fault detection, section identification, and accurate location, critical components in ensuring robust protection schemes. This paper proposes an advanced directional protection framework that integrates wavelet packet transform (WPT) with deep learning (DL) models, utilizing double-ended measurements of three-phase currents and voltages. The system is modeled using a distributed parameter line representation that includes shunt capacitance. The WPT technique extracts approximation coefficients from current and voltage signals, which serve as inputs to deep learning models, particularly using the mother wavelet packet db10 for optimal decomposition. The proposed method comprises two main stages: (i) detection and identification of the faulted section and direction, and (ii) estimation of the fault location from the relaying point. The approach is evaluated using multiple deep learning architectures, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and implemented in MATLAB. Simulation results across diverse fault scenarios, varying in location, resistance, and inception angle, demonstrate high accuracy and robustness. Compared with other hybrid approaches integrating WPT with artificial neural networks (ANNs) and adaptive-network-based fuzzy inference systems (ANFIS), the proposed method achieves superior precision, with an average error of only 0.03%. Notably, the technique offers primary protection for most line sections and backup coverage for adjacent areas.

Introduction

Background and motivation

Faults in transmission lines (TLs) pose significant challenges to the stability and reliability of modern power systems. Ensuring rapid fault isolation is essential to maintain continuous power delivery and prevent cascading outages. Double-circuit transmission lines (DCTLs) present additional complexity due to mutual coupling and bi-directional power flow, requiring accurate fault detection (FD), section identification, and fault location (FL) to maintain protection integrity. Advanced protective relaying strategies rely on comprehensive measurements and fast, intelligent decision-making. Every delay or inaccuracy in fault detection may compromise network reliability. This has motivated the development of intelligent systems capable of addressing the limitations of traditional impedance-based and rule-based protection methods. Increasingly, research has turned toward digital signal processing (DSP), machine learning (ML), and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques to enhance the performance of protective relays, particularly under varying operating and fault conditions.

Contributions and novelty

This paper presents a new method that combines wavelet packet transform with deep learning for double-ended fault detection, fault section identification, and fault location estimation in double-circuit transmission lines. The proposed technique analyzes voltage and current signals from both ends of the transmission line to detect faults, identify the affected sections, and accurately estimate the fault location. It addresses variations in fault parameters such as direction (forward and reverse faults), position (close-in and remote faults), resistance, location, and type of fault. A comparative evaluation with existing approaches demonstrates the advantages of the proposed method. Its innovative design and practical features highlight improvements in functionality, cost-efficiency, flexibility, independence from thresholds, response speed, and accuracy. Discrete wavelet packet

Wavelet Packet Transform (WPT) is a sophisticated signal processing technology that extends traditional wavelet conversion by allowing more flexible decomposition of signals into distinct frequency bands. Unlike the discrete wavelet transform, which divides signals only into approximation and detail coefficients, WPT decomposes both low-frequency (approximation) and high-frequency (detail) components. It utilizes a series of low-pass and high-pass filters to split the signal across multiple levels, with each level providing a set of coefficients that represent signal characteristics in various frequency bands. This enables comprehensive analysis of transient events, such as faults in transmission lines. The coefficients obtained from WPT are used as input features for neural network models. Due to its ability to capture transient behavior and local signal variations, WPT is particularly effective for fault diagnosis. It supports signal analysis with varying levels of accuracy, which is essential for identifying faults occurring over different time scales. Additionally, filtering development helps eliminate noise and improves the quality of features used to train ML models. Wavelet decomposition is generalized by the WPT approach, which provides more possibilities for signal analysis. WPT eliminates high-frequency and low-frequency signal components using low- and high-pass filters. Splitting the filtered signals is the next step in processing, performed as often as needed. The original signal processed by successive pairings of high-pass filters (HPFs) and low-pass filters (LPFs) is represented by f(t) in Fig. 3, which shows a signal decomposition employing WPT.

Models of neural networks

In recent years, there has been an increasing dependence on advanced AI technologies to address complex tasks across various fields. The ability to automatically extract significant features from raw data, without widespread manual intervention, enhances the accuracy and scalability of fault diagnosis. The proven effectiveness of these AI approaches in modular studies corroborates their superiority over conventional methods, supporting their pivotal role in the development of modern fault diagnosis techniques. Three ML models (DL, CNNs, and RNNs) were employed for the fault diagnosis system, each carefully selected based on its unique strengths in handling different aspects of data. The selected ML models were trained and tested offline to ensure robust performance across various operational conditions. To achieve a highly generalized algorithm, a dataset was prepared, encompassing comprehensive variations in fault occurrences. Specifically, the dataset included 5,000 distinct fault scenarios, covering a range of fault distances from 0.1 to 1.0% in increments of 0.1%, and from 0.1 to 1% in increments of 0.1% for fault resistance scenarios and arcing faults. Additionally, the same fault cases were considered for changing the fault inception angle from 0 to 180°, along with corresponding changes to the fault distance and resistance. Furthermore, changing the boundary condition location was included.

Deep learning network architecture

The architecture of the proposed DNN for the fault diagnosis system is shown in Fig. 7. The input layer consists of 10 neurons accepting X1, X2, Y1, PA4, and Y2 formats, as derived in the fault features section. A complex-split mechanism is used to process these inputs effectively. The 1 st hidden layer is a fully connected dense layer with 8 neurons, using the hyperbolic tangent (tanh) activation function. This layer begins the feature extraction process by introducing non-linearity and transforming the input into a more abstract representation.

Fig. 7MLP model diagram: DL concept illustration.

MLP model diagram: DL concept illustration.

The 2nd hidden layer, another fully connected layer, expanded to 16 neurons and uses tanh activation. With its deeper structure, it captures more complex patterns, enhancing the model’s ability to generalize. The 3rd hidden layer is a fully connected layer with 16 neurons, utilizing the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation. ReLU helps mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, improves training efficiency, and promotes sparsity for better fault discrimination. The output layer is the final fully connected dense layer of the neural network, consisting of 4 neurons. It is responsible for producing the network’s output based on the learned representations from the previous layers. The three-output layer uses the sigmoid activation function, which maps the output to a probability range between 0 and 1, and the one-output layer uses ReLU activation. The training and testing of the DL model were utilized for developing a robust predictive model. 80% of the data set (4000 cases) was used for training, while the remaining 20% (1000 cases) was reserved for validation. The data partitioning was done randomly to ensure the model’s performance could be accurately assessed across a diverse range of data points, minimizing biases and ensuring reliability in real-world applications.

The DL model was implemented using the MATLAB Deep Learning Toolbox, which provides a comprehensive environment for designing, training, and validating deep learning models. This toolbox is well-suited for handling large datasets and complex model architectures. Training of the DL model was performed using the SGDM optimization algorithm, known for its efficiency in handling large datasets and complex models within the MATLAB Deep Learning Toolbox. The training process was structured to span a maximum of 100 epochs, with convergence typically achieved within just 20 epochs, totaling 7888 iterations. The initial learning rate was set at 0.01, with a learning rate reduction mechanism applied after each epoch. This adaptive learning rate strategy involves reducing the learning rate sequentially, which refines the adjustments made by the optimizer as training progresses. This method of dynamically adjusting the learning rate is critical for optimizing the training process and enhancing the model’s performance in fault detection tasks. A batch size of 64 was used, which is optimal for balancing the computational load and effective handling of the dataset size. The categorical cross-entropy loss function was employed, appropriate for the multi-class classification framework of our study.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)

CNNs are a class of deep learning models particularly effective for processing grid-like data, such as images or time-series signals. They consist of multiple layers, including convolutional layers that automatically learn spatial hierarchies of features. Although they can also be used to solve regression issues, CNNs, also known as ConvNets, are a specific ANN frequently employed for image processing and classification applications. When it comes to image, speech, or audio signal inputs, CNNs perform better. CNN comprises a fully connected feedforward neural network, a pooling part, and a convolutional (Conv) part. The central component, the convolutional section, has three important hyperparameters – the number of filters that determines the output’s depth, the filter or kernel’s stride that represents the distance it travels over the input matrix, and zero padding when the filters do not fit the input matrix. The sigmoid and ReLU activation is used to add non-linearity following each convolution operation. The pooling part then decreases the volume size. As depicted in Fig. 8, ConvNets’ primary component design is shown, where numbers are used as an example to demonstrate the convolution and pooling processes.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs)

RNNs are designed for sequential data, allowing for the processing of data where temporal contexts is important. They maintain a memory of previous inputs, making them suitable for time-series data. An RNN model’s architecture is depicted in Fig. 9. The output variable in the RNN is called yt., while the input variable at time step t is called xt. The hidden state st, which is determined using the current input xt and the prior hidden state st−1 from the previous time step, is the key component. The current input xt and the preceding hidden state st-1 determine the hidden state. The current hidden state, st, determines the output, yt.

Algorithm outline

Concerning the selected system shown in Fig. 2, the proposed fault diagnosis is dependent on WPT. The scenarios of fault detection, section, the direction of fault, and fault location can be generalized. At each circuit in each bus, sending and receiving end phase voltages and phase currents are measured and extracted using WPT. These scenarios are identified as follows:

First, after the fault occurrence, the fault detection is declared based on high values for WPT approximation coefficients that are estimated at each end for each circuit in the four buses. These high values are declared by holding their values in normal operation and comparing them in each two-power cycle, which obtains the high flag that declares the fault is detected by inserting its flag into the neural network model. Second, estimate the indices X1 and X2 at each end of each circuit in the four buses and start to evaluate the faulty sections by estimating S1, S2, and S3 by using the standard deviation (std) of all indices. Evaluating the faulty section starts by estimating the output Y1, which is inserted into neural network models as training data. The final output is obtained in the FDSI unit as presented in Fig. 10a. Third, based on the sending and receiving end data that was measured, the aerial mode currents and voltages in each end for each circuit in the four buses are estimated, consequently. The extracted WPT approximation coefficients for its mode are calculated. The corresponding transient power indexes PA4 are estimated to be over two power cycles and then checked the polarity. As shown in Fig. 10b, its polarity is inserted into neural network models to give the fault direction for each section in the FDS unit. Fourth, the fault location algorithm needs sending and receiving end data for each circuit in the four buses to estimate the local fault location m, as shown in Fig. 10c. Consequently, the local fault location is inserted into neural network models to give the final fault location output Y2=[D] in the FL unit.

Comparative study

As previously mentioned, transmission line protection techniques can be broadly classified into four categories: conventional signal processing, wavelet transform methods, AI/Fuzzy/Expert systems, and hybrid approaches that combine wavelet transform with AI/Fuzzy/Expert systems. Conventional signal processing methods are generally inadequate for ultra-high-speed protection due to delayed detection times, inaccuracies in impedance calculations (often caused by current and potential transformer errors), and malfunctions resulting from fault resistance and transformer saturation. AI/Fuzzy/Expert systems rely heavily on expert knowledge and historical system behavior, which may limit adaptability. Among wavelet-based techniques in the literature, notable differences exist in algorithm complexity, computational burden, and response time. Many approaches employ multi-level wavelet decomposition, which requires intensive filtering and introduces structural complexity. However, combining wavelet transformation methods with AI/Fuzzy/Expert systems has been shown to yield faster response times and higher accuracy compared to conventional standalone techniques.

Comparison with AI-based methods

The suggested strategy is compared with the WANN approach reported in81 and the ANFIS discussed in93. According to the test results, the suggested DLNN-based approach provides FL estimations that are noticeably more accurate than those of the two methods. The following subsections further assess and explain the effectiveness of the proposed backup protection technique in the event of faults in nearby forward and reverse line sections.

Impact of changing fault resistance and high impedance fault

High impedance faults (HIFs) and other fault types in Sect. 2 were tested with various fault resistances to assess the effects of changing fault resistance on the performance of the suggested DLNN-based technique. Tables 4 and 5 show the DLNN-based FSI and FL results, which considered various fault resistances at locations varying from 0.1 to 99.9 km from the relaying point. As seen in Table 4, the outputs for S2 are high (close to 1) because the fault is in Sect. 2, whereas the outputs for the other sections are low (close to 0). Table 5 shows the estimated fault distance and percentage error in FL for ANFIS, WANN, and the suggested DLNN approach for various fault types with varying resistances. The proposed approach significantly improves the reliability of the power transmission system. By enabling rapid fault detection and accurate localization, it reduces outage durations and ensures uninterrupted service to end-users. This is crucial for maintaining system integrity and preventing cascading failures across the network. The combination of WPT and DLNN represents a substantial advancement in fault diagnosis, as it effectively leverages signal analysis and pattern recognition capabilities. This integration sets a foundation for future research into hybrid fault detection methods across various power system components. The approach demonstrates strong adaptability under diverse fault conditions, including variations in fault types, locations, and resistances, which broadens its practical applicability. Such flexibility is essential for modern power systems, especially with the increasing complexity introduced by renewable energy sources and distributed generation. As systems evolve toward smart grids, the need for advanced diagnostic techniques becomes more pressing. The contributions of this work align with smart grid goals by supporting improved monitoring, control, and automation. Enhanced fault management can reduce operational costs for utilities, potentially leading to lower energy prices and a reduction in environmental impact during fault conditions.

Conclusions

Summary of the paper

This paper presented a double-ended directional FS and FL scheme based on WPT and DLNN, which demonstrated high effectiveness under diverse fault scenarios in DCTLs. A key strength of the proposed method is its ability to extend the first protection zone coverage up to 99% of the line length. Its performance remained stable regardless of fault type (LG, LL, LLG, LLL), distance, inception angle, or resistance. The results confirm that the proposed system accurately identifies fault sections and estimates fault locations with an average error of just 0.03%. By utilizing double-ended data, it provides both primary protection for the full line and backup protection for adjacent sections in forward and reverse directions. Comparative analysis with existing methods, including ANFIS and WANN (wavelet + ANN), confirmed the superiority of the proposed FSI–FL framework in terms of accuracy and robustness.

Despite these advantages, the study also highlights areas requiring further investigation in DCTL fault diagnosis. The FL estimation was based on a fault locator that does not depend on the fault type or line specifications and assumes synchronized measurements from both ends. Lumped parameter equivalent circuits were used to estimate fault locations, and communication systems, specifically fiber optic links, were assumed to replace traditional power line carrier communication, improving system reliability under abnormal conditions. The proposed method can be integrated into existing monitoring and protection systems using voltage and current transformer data or relay measurements. This allows for enhanced fault detection without requiring major infrastructure upgrades. The method enables both primary and backup protection, thus contributing to improved system resilience and preventing cascading failures. Implementation requires the use of transducers capable of acquiring three-phase voltage and current signals at both line ends. These signals are then processed using WPT and DLNN models trained on historical fault data and continuously updated with real-time inputs to maintain performance. User-friendly operator interfaces, along with real-time alert systems and fault visualization, can further enhance decision-making and response time. The method’s performance, however, is dependent on the quality of input data. Noisy or inaccurate signals may lead to erroneous results. Therefore, data filtering and preprocessing are essential. Moreover, the computational demands of DLNN could exceed the capabilities of some existing systems, particularly for real-time deployment. Integrating the approach into legacy systems may require staged implementation, starting with pilot testing. Utilities may also need additional training or collaboration with academic partners to ensure successful adoption. Compliance with industry standards and regulatory frameworks is essential, and early engagement with regulatory bodies can help facilitate the deployment process.

Deficiencies and prospects

While effective, the integration of WPT and DLNN introduces several challenges. The method requires significant computational resources, which may be unsuitable for low-power or real-time systems. Its accuracy depends heavily on the quality of voltage and current input data, and the presence of noise or measurement errors can degrade reliability. In fast-changing fault conditions or in scenarios not represented in the training dataset, the model may fail to generalize effectively. Additionally, environmental influences such as temperature and humidity variations may affect performance but are not explicitly accounted for.

The approach’s reliance on previously observed fault patterns means it may not respond well to completely novel or unforeseen events. Although it avoids using fixed threshold values, this flexibility may introduce ambiguity in interpreting borderline cases. Furthermore, DLNN models are prone to overfitting if not properly trained with diverse datasets, potentially reducing their reliability in unseen scenarios.

Future directions

Future research should focus on developing real-time data acquisition and processing systems to enhance the responsiveness and reliability of fault detection. Investigating alternative machine learning models, such as ensemble methods, support vector machines, or hybrid approaches, could improve diagnostic accuracy under diverse conditions. Improving data preprocessing techniques, including advanced filtering and anomaly detection, will be essential to ensure high-quality input signals. Adaptive learning systems capable of updating their models with new operational data are needed to maintain performance as system conditions evolve. Additionally, simulating rare or extreme fault scenarios can help assess system resilience and guide the development of more robust protection schemes. Creating intuitive user interfaces for fault visualization and integrating real-time alert mechanisms will support faster decision-making by operators. Gradual integration through pilot implementations is recommended to ensure compatibility with existing infrastructure, especially in legacy systems. Building technical capacity within utility teams through training and academic collaboration, along with early coordination with regulatory bodies, will further support the successful deployment of advanced fault diagnosis technologies.
