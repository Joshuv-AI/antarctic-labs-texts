Comparative analysis of microRNAs in bovine colostrum and neonatal calf blood
Authors: Do T Hue, Kiro Petrovski, Yan Ren, John L Williams, Tong Chen, Wai Y Low, Thien D Van, Cynthia D K Bottema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDMicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short non‐coding RNAs that participate in a wide range of biological processes. MiRNAs are present in high concentrations in colostrum, and these may be potentially transferred to newborn calves being fed colostrum. The miRNA profile and levels in colostrum and in calf blood before and after colostrum ingestion were analysed in this study.RESULTSTwo groups of calves, A and B, received colostrum from the same Group A dam twice per day, whereas a third group, C calves, received only 2 L of ‘pooled day 0–4’ colostrum. MiRNAs from the colostrum and calf blood (before and 24 h after feeding colostrum) were sequenced, analysed, and results validated by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR). The number of miRNAs in the calf blood was approximately four‐fold higher than in the colostrum. A total of 303 miRNAs were identified in colostrum, of which 296 were known and seven were novel. Whereas 1198 miRNAs (1004 known and 194 novel) were identified in calf blood. Of the miRNAs detected in the calf blood at day 0, 94% were also found at day 1. Of these, 22 miRNAs had significantly different levels between the two time points. However, only miR‐1260b was validated to have higher levels in calf blood at day 1 compared to day 0 by RT‐qPCR.CONCLUSIONSAs there were minimal differences in the neonatal calf blood miRNA profiles and levels before and after the calves received colostrum, if miRNAs were absorbed into the blood of neonatal calves from the colostrum, that absorption is unlikely to be extensive or ongoing. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Calves are born agammaglobulinemic and have low levels of protective immunoglobulins in their bloodstream.1, 2 Consequently, neonate calves rely upon the transfer of passive immunity from colostrum to survive until their own immune system has fully developed. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) is the principal immunoglobulin found in bovine colostrum, and the absorption of IgG from the colostrum by newborn calves provides the passive immunity for the neonate.1, 3, 4 After receiving colostrum, high levels of the absorbed IgG are found in the neonate calf blood serum at days 1 and 2.2, 5, 6 These high IgG levels in the neonate calf blood decrease but are still evident at day 7 postpartum.2, 5

Insufficient consumption of high‐quality colostrum soon after birth results in inadequate passive immunity transfer.1, 7 IgG concentration has been used as a gold standard to classify the quality of the colostrum in most studies. 1 Colostrum is generally considered of high quality if IgG > 50 g/L.1, 8, 9

In addition to immunoglobulins, colostrum also contains many other components, including growth factors, hormones, leukocytes, cells, nutrients and microRNAs (miRNAs).1, 4, 10, 11, 12 As with IgG, miRNAs are present at very high levels in colostrum suggesting that they may play an important role.13, 14 However, whether these miRNAs are transferred to the calf and contribute to its health or development is not known.

MiRNAs are short non‐coding RNAs that are approximately 22 nucleotides in length.14, 15, 16, 17 First reported by Lee et al. in the worm Caenorhabditis elegans, 18 miRNAs are found both intra‐ and extra‐cellularly, including in the bodily fluids of humans and animals.11, 19, 20 MiRNAs play an important role in regulating eukaryotic gene expression by binding to the 3′‐untranslated regions (3’‐UTR) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), which results in mRNA cleavage, translational repression, or mRNA de‐adenylation.16, 21, 22 In animals, miRNAs have been predicted to regulate the expression of up to 30% of the protein coding genes and many miRNAs have been identified.14, 23 These miRNAs have been shown to be involved in numerous biological processes, including stem cell differentiation, immune responses, cholesterol and insulin metabolism, neurogenesis, haematopoiesis, cardiac and skeletal muscle growth, oocyte maturation and early‐stage embryonic development,22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 but the processes regulated by most miRNAs are still unknown.

It has been postulated that miRNAs in colostrum may regulate the immune system, mammary function and neonate development.21, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 As the miRNAs in colostrum and milk are stored in exosomes,21, 29, 33 they are very stable even under harsh conditions, including extreme temperatures, freeze–thaw cycles, low pH and RNase treatment.14, 34 Therefore, exosomal miRNAs may avoid degradation in the gastrointestinal tract.11, 35, 36 Colostrum derived miRNAs could potentially enter the systemic circulation of newborn mammals and be transferred to other cells to exert immune and developmental functions.29, 36

If miRNAs in the colostrum are important for neonatal calf development or immunity, then like IgG, the miRNAs would be absorbed from colostrum into the neonate calf blood, resulting in high levels of colostrum derived miRNAs in the calf blood for at least the first day after colostrum consumption. Therefore, in this study, we examined the miRNA profiles and levels between day 0 colostrum versus pooled day 0–4 colostrum, which differ in IgG concentration, to assess if there were also differences in the miRNAs. Then, the miRNAs were measured in the blood of newborn calves before and after feeding day 0 colostrum and pooled day 0–4 colostrum to determine if miRNAs in colostrum are absorbed by neonate calves. In addition, feeding the calves colostrum from its dam was compared with colostrum fed from a foster dam to determine if there were differences in compatibility that may affect absorption of miRNA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design and sampling

These animal studies were approved by the University of Adelaide Animal Ethics Committee (approval number S‐2017‐060), and the details of the experimental design are described in previously published papers.2, 30 Briefly, Group A calves (n = 12) were fed colostrum from their own dam, and each of the Group B calves (n = 12) were paired with Group A calf and fed the same colostrum from each milking of their respective Group A foster dam for 3 days. The dam colostrum was collected from Group A cows within 2 h of calving (dam day 0 colostrum) using a portable milking machine for the first milking. From the second milking onwards, the dams were milked in the dairy using a rotary milking parlour system. The Group C calves (n = 11) were fed a single bottle of mixed colostrum and transition milk (2 L) collected from multiple cows from day 0 to day 4 postpartum (called ‘pooled day 0–4’ colostrum). Thereafter, the Group C calves were fed bulk tank milk collected from many cows more than 5 days postpartum. All matched pairs of calves (Ai and Bi, and i = 1–12) were born within 12 h and they received the same colostrum from cow Ai. This feeding programme enabled the possible transfer of colostrum components from the calf's own dam versus a foster dam versus pooled colostrum/transition milk from many cows to be compared. The feeding programme used for the group C calves replicated the normal practice on commercial dairy farms in South Australia. For all calves, the first feed was within 4 h after birth and the second feed was 12 h after birth. Calves were then bottle fed 5% of their birth weight of colostrum twice a day to ensure adequate IgG absorption as suggested by Jaster. 37 The quality of colostrum from Group A dams day 0 and the pooled day 0–4 colostrum was analysed for IgG concentration and total soluble solids (Brix%) as described in Hue et al. 2 (Supporting Information, Table S1). The average IgG concentration and total soluble solids in the dam day 0 colostrum were 181.4 g L−1 and 25.4 Brix%, respectively, and in the pooled day 0–4 colostrum were 90.5 g L−1 and 19.4 Brix%. 2

Colostrum samples

The dam day 0 colostrum and pooled day 0–4 colostrum samples were collected into sterile 50 mL‐tubes immediately after each milking, frozen at −20 °C, and then transported to the University of Adelaide, Roseworthy Campus, Australia, for storage at −80 °C until analysis.

Calf blood samples

Calf blood samples were collected within 4 h after birth and before feeding colostrum (day 0) and 24 h after birth (day 1). Blood was drawn from the jugular vein into 6 mL BD vacutainers with EDTA. Next, 1 mL of whole blood from the EDTA vacutainers was mixed with 3 mL Trizol LS reagent (catalogue numbers 10 296 010 and 10 296 028; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), incubated at room temperature for 20 min, and then frozen at −20 °C. The samples in Trizol LS were transported to the University of Adelaide, Roseworthy Campus and stored at −80°C until analysed for miRNA.

MicroRNA profile using Illumina next‐generation sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 500 μL colostrum and calf blood (four random colostrum samples from each colostrum source, and the four corresponding calf blood samples from each calf group) using miRNeasy Mini Kits (catalogue number 217004; Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions for miRNA sequencing (Supporting Information, File S1). Total RNA was extracted from whole colostrum and whole blood rather than from exosomes.

After extraction, the total RNA concentration was quantified with a NanoDrop ND‐1000 (BIOLAB, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germantown, MD, USA) and the optical density (OD260/280) ratio was used to determine the RNA purity. The integrity of the total RNA was assessed on a TapeStation 2200 using an RNA ScreenTape assay (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany). The miRNA quality and integrity were verified using an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, version 1.2) with Small RNA Bioanalyzer chips (Small RNA Analysis 6–150 nucleotides). The samples that had a total RNA concentration of 1 μg in a maximum of 10 μL volume (1 ng per sample) and good quality were used for the small RNA library preparation.

MiRNA sequencing libraries were prepared using NEXTflex Small RNA‐Seq kits v3 (Bioo Scientic Corporation, Austin, TX, USA) for Illumina‐compatible next‐generation sequencing following the manufacturer's recommendations. The NEXTflex™ Small RNA‐Seq Kit v3 was optimized and validated using total RNA (1 ng per sample). Library construction was performed using 3′ and 5′ adapter‐ligated RNA, which was reverse transcribed to generate complementary DNA (cDNA), and then the cDNA was amplified with 14 polymerase chain reaction (PCR) cycles for both the blood and colostrum samples. Following PCR, the products were analysed by tris‐borate EDTA‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (TBE‐PAGE) and Agilent Bioanalyzer HS DNA assay for small RNA. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NextSeq 500 System by the Australian Cancer Research Foundation (ACRF) Cancer Genomics Facility, Centre for Cancer Biology, SA Pathology and University of South Australia. The average read length was 75 bp and the target number of reads was 22 million per sample.

MicroRNA sequence identification

The miRNAs were identified from the raw reads provided as FASTQ files. Briefly, this included checking raw data quality using the FastQC report, then filtering and trimming sequences to eliminate reads of low quality, removing the adapter sequences, and trimming the poly‐N sequences. The reads of mean quality scores ≤ 25 were removed 38 using Prinseq (version 0.20.4). Cutadapt (version 1.9.1) was used to remove the 3′ adapter and the sequences downstream, including the barcode. The trimmed reads with the length of 17–28 nucleotides were selected using Prinseq (version 0.20.4). BWA software (version 0.7.17) was used to align the trimmed reads to the cattle reference genome (a combination of the UOA_Angus_1 (GCA_003369685.2)) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCA_003369685.2/) and the X chromosome from UOA_Brahman_1 (GCF_003369695.1) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCF_003369695.1/). 39 This reference genome was chosen because it has a more complete representation of all chromosomes than the other available cattle genome sequences. After alignment with the cattle genome, SAMtools (version 1.10) was used to sort the aligned BAM files.

To identify the miRNAs, sequences were aligned to miRNAs in two databases, miRBase (mammalian miRNA database, Release 22, http://www.mirbase.org/) and RumimiR (ruminant miRNA database, Release January 2020, http://rumimir.sigenae.org/). First, the sequences were aligned against the miRNA precursors and mature conserved mammalian miRNAs in the miRBase (Release 22) using the Perl script of miRDeep2.pl. 40 The reads that aligned with miRNAs in miRBase were classified as known miRNAs. Reads that did not align with the miRNAs in miRBase were designated as potential novel miRNAs. To avoid the limitation of using miRBase alone, the potentially novel miRNAs were aligned to the miRNAs in the RumimiR database. Those that did not align to the known miRNAs in RumimiR were classified as novel. Based on the recommendations in the manual of miRDeep2, only the known and novel sequences with randfold P‐values < 0.05 were considered true miRNA and used in subsequent analyses. To avoid biasing the analyses with rare or spurious reads, only miRNAs with a minimum of five counts in at least four blood samples or two colostrum samples were retained. The remaining sequences were then aligned to the ARS‐UCD1.2 (GCF_002263795.1) reference genome for non‐coding RNA. RNA sequences that had a query coverage > 90 and a bitscore > 30 were classified as other types of non‐coding RNAs (lncRNA, misc_RNA, Mt_rRNA, Mt_tRNA, rRNA, scaRNA, snoRNA, snRNA, sRNA) and removed.

Differential expression of miRNA analysis

The differential expression of the mature known bovine miRNA, identified from the RumimiR database alignment, was analysed with a custom R script using linear models (limma package) 41 and edgeR (version 3.11) packages.42, 43 The trimmed mean of M (TMM) method was used for normalization of the miRNA expression of the known bovine miRNA as implemented in the R Bioconductor package edgeR as proposed by Robinson et al. 42 This method gives the proportion of counts for a specific target across all samples. If a miRNA was present in the same proportion across all samples, it was deemed as not being differentially expressed. 44 The linear models (limma package) were used to compare the differential expression of miRNAs for the different colostrum samples (dam day 0 colostrum versus pooled day 0–4 colostrum), and calf blood at different time points (day 0 versus day 1). The P‐values were adjusted for multiple tests and miRNA expression differences were considered significant if the adjusted P‐value (or Q‐values) was < 0.05 and the |log2fold change| > 1.

Based on miRNA sequencing results, only three miRNAs (let‐7a‐3p, miR‐12042 and miR‐1260b) (Table 1) were significantly higher at day 1 compared to day 0 in the calf blood. These miRNAs were quantified using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) to validate sequencing results using TaqMan microRNA assays.

Total RNA, including miRNAs, was extracted from 250 μL of colostrum (n = 12 dam day 0 colostrum, and n = 6 pooled day 0–4 colostrum) or 250 μL of calf blood sample (n = 12 for each calf group) using Trizol LS reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol, as described in Hue et al. 30 Briefly, C. elegan miRNA, 1 uL (5 picomole/μL) Cel‐miR‐39‐3p (mirVana), was added to 250 μL of sample (colostrum or calf blood) as an internal control before RNA extraction. The concentration and quality of total RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop ND‐1000 (NanoDrop Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific). All samples that had a ratio of A260/280 in the range from 1.5 to 2.0 were used for RT‐qPCR. Total RNA was transcribed into cDNA using a TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystem, Foster City, CA, USA) with the specific miRNA primers supplied by the Thermo Fisher Scientific company, and all steps followed the manufacturer's protocol (Supporting Information, File S2). To ensure that all the miRNAs were quantified, the miRNAs used for RT‐qPCR were extracted from whole colostrum and whole blood and not just from the exosomes.

The cDNA was diluted at a ratio of 1:10 with RNase‐free water and used as the template for the qPCR. The qPCR was performed using a Motion Reloads Eppendorf system to load the PCR reagents into a 384‐well plate, which was placed into a thermal cycler (QuantStudio TM 6 Flex Real‐time PCR system, Life Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Marsiling Industrial Estate Road 3, Singapore). Briefly, a PCR master mix was prepared for each reaction by mixing 7.5 μL of TaqMan Universal Master Mix II, no UNG 2× and 0.38 μL of TM primer 20× for each reaction, and then cDNA template was added to a final volume of 15 μL. The PCR was run for 40 cycles as follows: hold (95 °C for 10 min), cycling (95 °C for 15 s, then 60 °C for 60 s). Cel‐miR‐39‐3p standard conversion for RT‐qPCR and normalization steps are described in Supporting Information File S2.

The concentrations of the three miRNAs from RT‐qPCR were compared between day 0 and day 1 calf blood using R (R version 4.4.3) after testing for normal distributions using the Shapiro–Wilk test in the ‘nortest’ package. Furthermore, t‐tests were performed to compare differences between each miRNA in calf blood at day 0 and day 1 when the data were normally distributed; whereas Kruskal–Wallis tests were used when the data were not normally distributed. The miR‐1260b level was compared between the three calf groups (Groups A, B and C) at each time‐point using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and differences were tested using Tukey's honestly significant difference (Tukey HSD) test.

RESULTS

MicroRNA identification in calf blood and colostrum samples

Following filtering to remove unique and low count sequences, 1296 putative miRNA sequences were identified. After removing the other non‐coding RNAs, a BLAST search of miRBase identified 558 known and 640 potentially novel miRNA sequences. The 640 potentially novel sequences were then mapped against the RumimiR database, and 446 miRNAs of the 640 were classified as known. Thus, after the alignment to both databases, there were 1004 known miRNAs and 194 novel miRNAs giving a total of 1198 miRNAs found in the blood and colostrum samples. Most of the miRNA sequences were 20 to 23 nucleotides long, with 22 nucleotides being most common (42% in total of miRNAs) (Supporting Information, Fig. S1).

There were four times more miRNAs found in the blood samples than in the colostrum samples (1198 versus 303 miRNAs, respectively) (Table 2). All miRNAs found in colostrum were also found in the blood. All of the 194 novel miRNAs were found in blood, but only seven were found in colostrum samples (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

The length of miRNAs found in the present study was between 17 and 25 nucleotides, and most were 22 nucleotides in length (42%) (Supporting information, Fig. S1), which is consistent with other studies of milk and mammary gland miRNAs.28, 34, 45, 46 Most of the miRNAs were known (1004), but 194 novel miRNAs were identified, primarily in the calf blood.

Colostrum miRNAs

The 303 miRNAs identified in bovine colostrum may be derived from the cow's blood, the mammary gland or both. Some of the miRNAs in human milk are most likely produced by the mammary gland as they are not detected in maternal blood. 47 Interestingly, the number of miRNAs in the neonate calf blood of this study (1198 miRNAs) was four times higher than that in colostrum (303 miRNAs). This suggests that most of the miRNAs in neonate blood are not transferred from the colostrum as the mammary gland secrets fewer types of miRNAs than found in blood.

The total number of miRNAs identified in the dam day 0 and pooled day 0–4 colostrum (303 miRNAs) was similar to the number found in previous studies,28, 48 which ranged from 230 21 to 545 miRNAs.28, 48, 49 We found 296 known miRNAs and seven novel miRNAs in the colostrum. The number of novel miRNAs found was much lower than previous studies, possibly because previous studies only mapped sequences to the miRBase database28, 46, 48, 49 and not RumimiR database. In this study, great care was taken to remove potentially spurious data, so that only miRNAs with a minimum of five counts in at least four blood samples or two colostrum samples were retained. This step may have eliminated low abundance sequences, which may also explain the lower number of novel miRNAs found in this study compared with others.28, 48, 49

Of the 303 miRNAs found in colostrum, 76% overlapped between the dam day 0 colostrum and the pooled day 0–4 colostrum. In addition, there were only four miRNAs among the 303 detected which had higher levels in day 0 colostrum compared with pooled day 0–4 colostrum. Hence, most of the miRNAs in the colostrum do not change immediately postpartum. The period from second milking to 5 days postpartum is considered a transition period when colostrum gradually changes composition into milk.1, 11 The change of the miRNA profile between day 0 colostrum and pooled day 0–4 colostrum is consistent with this transition. In a previous study, we found a decline in the levels of five miRNAs (miR‐142‐5p, miR‐150, miR‐155, miR‐181a, and miR‐223) from day 0 to day 3 postpartum, 30 and other studies have shown that many miRNAs increase while others decrease during the transition period from colostrum to mature milk.21, 48 Bovine milk exosomes have been shown to have different levels of 338 miRNAs between day 1 colostrum and day 170 milk, although the levels of only 86 miRNAs were found to differ between day 1 colostrum and day 7 transition milk. 48 The levels of miRNAs continue to change as lactation progresses. For example, 108 of 116 miRNAs have been shown to have higher levels in 7 days postpartum transition milk than in mature milk. 21

In this study, the levels of only four miRNAs (miR‐19b, miR‐193b, miR‐378 and miR‐345‐5p) differed between the two colostrum sources (dam day 0 colostrum versus pooled day 0–4 colostrum). All four of these miRNAs have been reported to be relevant to immune or developmental functions.50, 51, 52, 53 MiR‐19b is part of a regulation pathway that controls NF‐κB signalling, a central pathway in innate immunity and inflammation, 54 and miR‐19b has been also associated with the immune function of bovine colostrum exosomes. 50 The expression of miR‐193b increases during human Th17 cell differentiation, suggesting a role in adaptive immunity and inflammatory T‐cell programming. 55 MiR‐378 is involved in development, and its expression increases during C2C12 myoblast‐to‐myotube differentiation, indicating a role in myogenesis. 53 MiR‐345‐5p has been reported to alleviate the inflammatory response with allergic rhinitis. 52

MicroRNA profile of neonate calf blood at birth

The calves in this study were born with a complex profile of 1163 miRNAs in their blood before receiving any colostrum (day 0). Previous studies have shown that the synepitheliochorial bovine placenta structure prevents the transfer of maternal immune components from mother to foetus during pregnancy,4, 56 although whether miRNAs are transferred is not known. MiRNAs have been found in blastocysts and embryonic tissues,57, 58, 59, 60 and at high levels in embryos, uterus, placenta and dam blood during early pregnancy,57, 61, 62 as well as in newborn tissues and blood at birth.30, 63, 64 Some authors have suggested that transfer of these specific miRNAs may occur.57, 65 There is also growing evidence that the embryo or foetus communicates with the dam via miRNA which is transferred to the blood of the dam via the chorionic binucleate cells in the placenta. 66

MicroRNA profile of neonate calf blood after colostrum consumption

The miRNA profile in calf blood between birth (day 0) and after two feeds (day 1) did not differ greatly (1163 and 1167 miRNAs, respectively), of which 94% (1132 miRNAs) were in common. In a previous study, 21 only 360 miRNAs were found in serum. The higher number of miRNAs detected in blood reported here is most likely because the miRNAs were identified from whole blood rather than in serum. There were 22 miRNAs found to have significantly different levels between day 0 and day 1, of which three had higher levels in the day 1 calf blood samples after feeding colostrum (let‐7a‐3p, miR‐1260b and miR‐12042). However, only the increased level of miR‐1260b was confirmed by RT‐qPCR.

The three miRNAs (let‐7a‐3p, miR‐1260b and miR‐12042) found at higher levels in calf blood at day 1 after two feedings are related to cellular and immune function. Let‐7a‐3p has a role in the inhibition of neurotensin receptor 1, which is involved in cell proliferation and invasiveness of glioblastoma and induces intrinsic apoptosis. 67 The let‐7 miRNA family members affect immunoglobulin A (IgA) level in the digestive tract of the piglet. 68 MiR‐1260b has been related to immune protection in the infant gut. 69 To date, there has been only one study describing a potential role of miR‐12042 in MDBK cells expressing bovine papillomavirus E5 oncogene. 70 As the expression of these miRNAs increases after birth, they may regulate cell growth and immune functions.

It is difficult to differentiate miRNA absorption from colostrum and synthesis of the miRNAs by calves.30, 63 Kirchner et al. 63 found that only three miRNAs of the 15 most highly expressed miRNAs in colostrum exosomes were significantly increased in the calf blood after feeding colostrum (miR‐200‐a/b/c family). However, the increased level was thought to be a result of differential expression by the calves rather than absorption from colostrum because of the role of the miR‐200‐a/b/c family in signal processing. In our previous study, we quantified five selected immune‐related miRNAs by RT‐qPCR in colostrum and calf blood before and after receiving colostrum at days 1, 2, 3 and 7, and there were no significant differences in miRNA levels between these time points. 30

It is possible that the miRNA absorption from colostrum is not detected because the increase in the blood is short lived. In a study on the absorption of miRNA from cow milk in humans, miR‐29b and miR‐200c were shown to be absorbed into human plasma after the cow milk was consumed. 71 However, the levels only increased for a 6 h period after the milk consumption, and then decreased to baseline by 24 h. 71 Interestingly, the increase of miRNA in blood may have a physiological effect as the concentration of runt‐related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), which is a target of miR‐29b, increased 31% in the blood mononuclear cells after milk consumption. 71 In addition, the possible absorption of 16 highly abundant plant miRNAs into human blood has been reported after consumption of watermelon juice or fruits, and again the effect was short‐lived as seven miRNAs reached a peak 1 to 3 h after drinking the juice. 72 The potentially rapid uptake and return to baseline levels of miRNA may explain why the absorption of colostrum miRNAs was not detected in the present study, as the calf blood was only collected once after 24 h. Therefore, to verify whether the identified miRNAs are truly absorbed by neonate calves and determine whether the absorption is short‐lived, future studies using labelling to track these specific miRNAs are warranted.

Pig colostrum has been found to have 13 immune‐related miRNAs at higher levels than milk. 73 Of these miRNAs, 12 were found at higher concentrations in colostrum‐fed piglets compared with those fed mature milk 4 days after birth. 73 It is possible that these immune‐related miRNAs were transferred from colostrum to the piglets. The gastrointestinal differences between monogastrics and ruminants may affect miRNA absorption from colostrum by piglets differently from calves. A more likely cause of the difference in observations is the developmental stage. The piglets were assessed at 4 days after being fed colostrum or milk, 73 while in the present study we examined the effects of the colostrum fed soon after birth at day 1. The differences in the piglet miRNA profiles may reflect differential expression of a more developed immune system in the colostrum‐fed piglets and do not represent absorption differences.

The majority of the miRNAs in colostrum and milk are packaged into extracellular vesicles (exosomes),21, 29, 74 however, miRNAs are also found bound to proteins in the whey serum.75, 76 Specific miRNAs appear to be packaged into exosomes or bound to proteins and taken up by cells,77, 78, 79, 80 although the selectivity of this process is still a matter of debate. The transfer of specific miRNAs may explain why the general uptake of miRNAs from the colostrum was not observed in the neonate calves. Therefore, further studies should verify whether any exosomal or protein‐bound miRNAs in the colostrum are transferred to the blood of neonatal calves and if so, whether this process is selective. In particular, as the turnover of miRNA in calf blood is not known, longitudinal studies of absorption of miRNAs from colostrum at time points earlier than those sampled in the present study are recommended.

CONCLUSION

Both colostrum and calf blood contained a large number of miRNAs, but the number in calf blood was four‐fold higher than in colostrum. A similar miRNA profile was found in day 0 colostrum and pooled day 0–4 colostrum with 76% of the miRNAs expressed in both. There were only four miRNAs which had significantly different levels between the type of colostrum, all of which were higher in dam day 0 colostrum than the pooled day 0–4 colostrum, suggesting that the expression of miRNAs does not vary substantially in colostrum. Of the miRNAs in the calf blood, 94% were detected in the calf blood samples from both day 0 and day 1. Only 22 miRNAs had significantly different levels between the two time points, of which three had higher levels at day 1 (let‐7a‐3p, miR‐1260b and miR‐12042). However, only miR‐1260b was present in high levels and validated as being significantly higher at day 1 by RT‐qPCR. Therefore, unlike the high level of IgG absorption which is evident in neonate calf serum 24 h after initial colostrum consumption, it appears that colostrum miRNAs are not generally absorbed and retained in the bloodstream of neonate calves on day 1 postpartum.
