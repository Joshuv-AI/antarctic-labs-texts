Schisantherin A promotes neural differentiation of stem cells from apical papilla to improve mandibular development via mental nerve repair
Authors: Lingxi Du, Ailian Li, Ziling Tang, Hanxiang Yang, Xinyu Shi, Shengchao Wang, Zuocheng Qiu, Xuesong Yang, Yue Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence shows neural involvement in bone remodeling; regulating maxillofacial nerve repair modulates jawbone. Neural stem cell (NSC) therapy is limited by sources/ethics, but neural crest-derived dental mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) like stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAPs) have strong neuroregenerative potential for NSC transdifferentiation. Schisantherin A (Sch-A), neuroprotective, enhances NSC proliferation/differentiation. This study explores optimal Sch-A concentration/duration for SCAP neural differentiation and effects on rat mental nerve repair/mandibular development.MethodsSCAPs’ mesenchymal stem cell properties were verified via flow cytometry and trilineage differentiation. Effects of different Sch-A concentrations were evaluated using CCK-8, colony formation, scratch assay, qRT-PCR, immunofluorescence, and Western blot. Transcriptome sequencing identified underlying mechanisms and determined optimal. A mental nerve injury model was established in 4-week-old SD rats (five groups; n = 4 per group) to assess neurorepair, functional recovery, and mandibular development following transplantation of Sch-A-induced SCAPs.ResultsTreatment with 10− 9 mol/L Sch-A for 1 week induced robust neural differentiation in SCAPs, with high expression of nestin and NSE. Mental nerve-injured SD rats exhibited reduced lip sensation, abnormal nerve morphology, and inhibited transverse development of the anterior mandibular. Transcriptome analysis revealed Sch-A primarily acts via neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway. Transplantation of induced SCAPs promoted nerve repair and restored mandibular development.ConclusionSch-A at 10− 9 mol/L concentration promotes the transdifferentiation of SCAPs into neural stem cell-like cells primarily through the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway. These Sch-A induced SCAPs effectively repair mental nerve injury and facilitate normal mandibular development.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04950-5.

Introduction

In recent years, mounting evidence has highlighted the nervous system’s role in bone remodeling. Embryologically, neural tissues develop prior to bone tissues and regulate bone growth across stages. Anatomically, nerves course linearly or spirally around blood vessels, forming the structural basis for neural-mediated skeletal regulation [1–4]. Physiologically, metabolic pathways of key neurotransmitters confirm peripheral nerve innervation within the skeletal system. Studies using salamander limb models have shown that, post-injury, neural cells can transition into a regenerative phenotype resembling their precursor states, mediating bone repair and remodeling through paracrine signaling or secreted proteins [5]. Disruption of TrkA signaling reduces sensory nerve innervation, impairing both primary and secondary bone formation - as evident in TrkA Avil-/- mice, where sensory nerve ablation leads to significant bone loss [6, 7]. Additionally, nerve fibers precisely regulate bone regeneration via Schwann cells or endoneurial mesenchymal precursor cells [8]. In long bones, highly selective sensory/motor nerve injury inhibits bone metabolism, inducing osteoporosis. Jawbones, rich in peripheral nerves, rely on neural activation and regeneration to balance the dynamic osteogenic microenvironment; for instance, transection of the inferior alveolar nerve disrupts post-extraction innate immune responses and hinders jawbone healing [8], underscoring the potential of regulating maxillofacial peripheral nerve repair to modulate jawbone growth and remodeling.

Neural stem cell (NSC) therapy is an effective approach for repairing damaged nerves, as NSCs secrete soluble factors, differentiate into neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes [5], replace injured/necrotic neural cells, regulate the neural microenvironment, suppress inflammation, and promote functional recovery. Local or systemic NSC administration thus holds promise for regenerating and repairing neural tissues in cases of nerve injury or neurodegeneration [9–11]. However, NSC sourcing remains a critical challenge in clinical applications: adult NSCs are restricted to limited regions of the central nervous system [12], yielding small sample sizes [13]; they often require high cell doses [14], and standardized protocols for isolating human tissue-derived NSCs are lacking [15], along with ethical constraints. Consequently, transdifferentiating other cell types into NSCs has emerged as a key alternative.

Among cells with NSC induction potential - such as human embryonic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, and dental-derived mesenchymal stem cells (DMSCs) - DMSCs are often preferred. They offer advantages including easy accessibility, minimal invasiveness, wide availability, low oncogenicity/tumorigenicity, fewer ethical concerns, and a neural crest origin that endows them with strong neurogenic potential [16–18]. DMSCs include stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAPs), dental pulp stem cells, periodontal ligament stem cells, dental follicle stem cells, and stem cells from exfoliated deciduous teeth.

The SCAPs, first isolated from developing wisdom tooth germs by Sonoyama et al., are precursors to dental pulp and odontoblast cells. They exhibit pluripotency, differentiating into osteoblasts, odontoblasts, and adipocytes in vitro, and express canonical stem cell markers [19–23]. The SCAPs also show robust proliferation and colony-forming capacities, with osteogenic potential comparable to bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells but weaker adipogenic ability. Under specific induction, they can differentiate toward non-native cell types (e.g., neural cells, chondrocytes, hepatocytes) and, post-transplantation, express multiple neural markers such as βIII-tubulin, GAD, NeuN, nestin, GFAP, NSE, and CNPase [19, 24, 25].

As progenitors derived from the developing apical papilla, SCAPs represent an early progenitor population with inherent tissue regeneration advantages [26]. Having originated from the neural crest and expressing specific neural stem cell markers, they possess exceptional neural regeneration and repair capabilities [27–29]. Kolar et al. demonstrated that SCAPs exhibit stronger neuroaxonal growth potential than other dental stem cells in vitro and exert superior axonal regeneration and neuroprotective effects in rat sciatic nerve injury models [30]. In rat spinal cord injury models, SCAP transplantation promotes functional recovery, reduces gliosis, and facilitates spinal nerve repair [31–33]. Jose Flavio A de Almeida et al. found that SCAPs regulate trigeminal neurite outgrowth and targeting via brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) release [34]. Byung-Chul Kim et al. further showed that SCAPs cultured in 3D organotypic systems form neural-like tissues with axonal and myelin structures [35], highlighting their potential in treating peripheral nerve injuries and neurodegenerative diseases.

Schisantherin A (Sch-A), the most abundant lignan isolated from Schisandra chinensis (molecular formula C30H32O9, molecular weight 536.57 g/mol), exhibits significant neuroprotective activity [36–39]. Additionally, Sch-A maintains neuronal survival and function by regulating mitochondria-lysosome crosstalk [38–40]. It reduces apoptosis in primary rat cortical neurons subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation-reperfusion injury, alleviates neurological deficits, and reduces infarct volume in rat models of middle cerebral artery occlusion-reperfusion [41]. Sch-A pretreatment also protects SH-SY5Y cells from 6-hydroxydopamine-induced damage, preventing dopaminergic neuron loss [42]. Beyond neuroprotection, it exerts anti-inflammatory [43], anti-tumor [44], and liver fibrosis-ameliorating effects [45], establishing it as a promising neuroprotective and neuroinductive agent.

To date, existing studies have not focused on the regulatory role of Schisantherin A (Sch-A) in the neural differentiation of stem cells. Furthermore, although maxillofacial nerve injury is closely associated with bone development, the potential application of neural differentiation of dental-derived stem cells in repairing maxillofacial nerve injury during bone development has not been adequately explored. Based on the reported neurotrophic properties of Sch-A and the neural differentiation potential of stem cells from apical papilla (SCAPs), we hypothesized that Sch-A may promote the neural differentiation of SCAPs and enhance their neuroregenerative capacity. To test this hypothesis, this study aimed to investigate whether Sch-A promotes the neural differentiation of SCAPs and whether Sch-A-pretreated SCAPs are capable of facilitating mental nerve repair, which may be associated with enhanced mandibular development, as well as to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms. This work may provide a theoretical basis for the use of SCAPs as a stable source of neural stem cell-like cells and support their potential application in the treatment of nervous system-related diseases and jawbone-associated disorders.

Materials and methods

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Literature summary

The literature search covered publications up to July 2025, with the following inclusion criteria: (1) articles reporting information based on the keywords ‘Schisantherin A’, ‘SCAP’, and ‘Neural differentiation’; (2) English-language articles; and (3) articles related to neural mechanisms of action. Articles were excluded if they (1) did not align with the research objectives; (2) were irrelevant to the topic; (3) did not report original data (e.g., editorials, reviews, or conference abstracts).

Isolation and expansion of stem cells from apical papilla (SCAPs)

Approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jinan University (Approval No.: JNUKY-2023-0110) with informed consent, third molar tooth germs were extracted from 12–18-year-old orthodontic patients at the oral alveolar surgery clinic [46]. The extracted tooth germs were collected in DMEM (Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% penicillin-streptomycin (PS; Oricell, China), and processed within 4 h. Specifically, sterile nickel forceps were used to gently grasp and collect the dental papilla tissue attached to the apical region of the tooth germ, while the mineralized dental tissues were discarded. The apical papilla tissues were minced, washed 3× with PBS (Solarbio, China), centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Tissues were transferred to PBS-prewetted T25 flasks. Complete medium (DMEM + 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) + 1% PS) was added; cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Flasks were inverted after 4 h and incubated until cells migrated and expanded [33]. For growth curves, healthy P3 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 1000 cells/well (9 groups, 3 replicates/group). Cells were trypsinized, counted daily for 9 days, and a growth curve was plotted.

Alizarin red staining

To verify that the extracted cells possess mesenchymal stem cell characteristics, we performed osteogenic differentiation detection as one of the three-lineage differentiation assays. Passage 3 (P3) SCAPs were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well. At 70–80% confluency, cells were subjected to osteogenic induction culture for 3 weeks using the human-related stem cell osteogenic differentiation medium (Oricell, China), (medium changed every 3–4 days). Mineralization was assessed by Alizarin Red S staining (Solarbio, China) [47].

Alcian blue staining

As part of the three-lineage differentiation assays to confirm the mesenchymal stem cell properties of the extracted cells, chondrogenic differentiation detection was conducted. 4 × 105 P3 SCAPs were pelleted in 15 mL centrifuge tubes. Per manufacturer’s instructions, a chondrogenic differentiation premix (comprising Basal Medium For Cell Culture, Supplement For Human Related Stem Cells Chondrogenic Differentiation I, and Supplement For Human Related Stem Cells Chondrogenic Differentiation II; Oricell, China) was prepared at the specified ratio, followed by 3 weeks of chondrogenic induction culture with medium changed every 3 days. Formed chondrospheres were sectioned and stained with Alcian blue (Oricell, China) to evaluate proteoglycan deposition [47].

Oil red O staining

We carried out adipogenic differentiation detection as one of the three-lineage assays to validate that the extracted cells have mesenchymal stem cell characteristics. P3 SCAPs were seeded in 6-well (CellPro, China) plates at 2 × 104 cells/well. At 100% confluency, per the inducer manufacturer’s instructions, cells were alternately treated with human-related stem cell adipogenic differentiation media A and B (Oricell, China) for 3 weeks (3 days medium A/1 day medium B per cycle). Lipid droplet formation was detected by Oil Red O staining (Solarbio, China) [47].

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was performed to identify surface markers of SCAPs. P3 – P5 cells were trypsinized, washed with PBS, and adjusted to 1 × 105 – 106 cells/mL. Cells were incubated with antibodies against negative markers CD45, CD34, HLA-DR and positive markers CD73, CD90, CD105 (BD Biosciences, USA), and analyzed using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (ACEA/Agilent, USA) for mesenchymal stem cell marker profiling [48, 49].

Experimental animals

Approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Jinan University (20241111-03), twenty 4-week-old male SD rats (Beijing Weitong Lihua Experimental Animal Technology Co, LTD.) (126.8 ± 14.17 g) were randomized into 5 groups (n = 4) by means of the random number table method: Sham, model control (MC), SCAPs treatment (Group A), induction medium-treated SCAPs (Group B), and 10⁻9 mol/L Sch-A-pretreated SCAPs (Group C). All animals were housed under conditions of 50–60% humidity, 22 ± 2℃ temperature, a 12-hour light cycle, and good ventilation, and were provided with a standard soft diet al.ong with equal amounts of food and water. During the experiment, the cages were placed in the same room and on the same rack to minimize mixing. All animal experiments were strictly performed in accordance with international animal welfare standards. During the experiment, every effort was made to minimize animal suffering, while the investigators responsible for animal modeling and drug administration were blinded to the group assignments of the animals. Under anesthesia with 1% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg), the mental nerve was exposed via incision and flap reflection, clamped for 1 min to induce injury; Sham rats underwent only incision and suturing [50]. One day post-modeling, SCAPs in each group were cultured under respective treatment conditions for 7 days. Following trypsinization, the cells were adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/ml — based on previous studies to ensure a sufficient number of viable cells to exert their therapeutic effects. A 100 µL aliquot of the cell suspension was injected locally into the injury site weekly; this administration strategy maximizes cell retention at the target nerve lesion [51, 52]. Animals in the Sham and MC groups received an equal volume of PBS. Four weeks later, animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal administration of an overdose (150 mg/kg) of sodium pentobarbital [52]. Once the rats had lost consciousness and showed no response to painful stimuli, they were dissected, and their mandibles and mental nerves were collected for subsequent analysis.

Behavioral assessment

Behavioral assessments were performed independently by investigators blinded to group assignments, with blinded data analysis. Each week, SD rats were placed in transparent cages with grid floors and allowed for 30 min acclimation. Subsequently, pinprick tests followed von Frey standards, using probes to stimulate predefined facial regions (see Fig. 8A). Responses were scored as: 0 = no response; 1 = sensation; 2 = sensation with withdrawal; 3 = sensation, withdrawal with fleeing or aggression; 4 = grade 3 response plus persistent unilateral facial grooming [53].

Measurement indices of mandibular anatomy

Four weeks post-modeling, fresh mandibular samples were harvested and marked at the following landmarks: L1 (anterior-most lingual alveolar crest of lower incisors), Mef (mental foramen apex), Go (midpoint of mandibular angle), Maf (mandibular foramen apex), Con (condylar apex), and Id (inferior alveolar margin). Seven linear dimensions were measured using vernier calipers: Go - Id (alveolar margin to mandibular angle), L1 - Mef, Mef - Mef (inter-mental foramen distance), Go - Maf, Con - Go (condyle to mandibular angle), Con - L1 (total mandibular length), and Con - Con (bilateral condylar width) [54, 55]. All measurements were performed by two independent researchers in a blinded manner (blinded to the group allocation of samples). Measurements were repeated three times per side, and bilateral averages were calculated.

Scratch wound healing assay

A cross-shaped scratch was created on the confluent cell monolayer using a 200 µL pipette tip [56]. After washing to remove non-adherent cells, medium was replaced with fresh medium containing Sch-A at concentrations of 0, 10− 9, 10− 8, or 10− 7 mol/L. Wound closure was photographed at 6, 12, and 24 h post-scratch for migration rate analysis.

H&E staining

Histomorphological changes at the site of mental nerve injury were observed using hematoxylin-eosin staining. Mental nerves were harvested 4 weeks post-modeling. Tissues underwent gradient alcohol dehydration, xylene clearing, and paraffin embedding. Section (5 μm thick) were mounted, dried, and stored. After dewaxing in xylene and rehydration, sections were stained with hematoxylin for 30 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol, blued in Scott’s tap water for 2 min, and stained with eosin for 2 min. They were then dehydrated in 100% alcohol, cleared in xylene for 10 min, and mounted with neutral balsam [57].

Toluidine blue staining

Histomorphological analysis of the mental nerve injury site was performed using toluidine blue staining. At 4 weeks post-modeling, mental nerves were harvested and sectioned (5 μm). After dewaxing and rehydration, sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and stained with toluidine blue for 20 min. Following rinsing, sections were differentiated in 1% acetic acid for 5 s until axons appeared dark blue. After a final rinse, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped with neutral resin [57].

Clonogenic assay

P3–P5 SCAPs (1000 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates (CellPro, China) and cultured for 2 weeks in complete medium supplemented with Sch-A (0, 10− 9, 10− 8, or 10− 7 mol/L). Colonies were stained with crystal violet, microscopically counted, and analyzed.

CCK-8 assay

To investigate the effect of Sch-A on the proliferation of SCAPs, healthy P3 – P5 SCAPs were seeded in 96-well plates (CellPro, China) at 3 × 104 cells/mL (100 µL/well) and cultured for 12 h. Cells were then treated with Sch-A (0, 10− 9, 10− 8, or 10− 7 mol/L) for 1, 2, or 3 days. Following the Enhanced CCK-8 Kit (Beyotime, China) protocol, a working solution (DMEM: CCK-8 = 9:1) was added (100 µL/well). Absorbance (OD450 nm) was measured, and cell viability was calculated [58].

Immunofluorescence

Cells (1000/well) were seeded on coverslips, induced for 1–2 weeks, fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and incubated with primary antibodies (Nestin 1:250, NSE 1:500; Proteintech, USA) followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000; Proteintech, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Western blot

P3 - P5 SCAPs (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates. At 90–100% confluency, cells were treated per immunofluorescence groups for 1–2 weeks. Total protein was extracted, quantified (BCA Kit; Beyotime, China), and normalized. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to membranes, and probed with anti-NSE (a marker of mature neurons)/Nestin (a marker of neural precursor cells)/SOX2 (a core transcription factor regulating pluripotency and differentiation) [59–61] (Proteintech, USA) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were detected by chemiluminescence, and band intensities were quantified.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Human SCAPs were neurally induced (medium changed every 3–4 days) and harvested at 1, 3, and 7 days. Groups: Blank (complete medium), Control (neural induction medium), 10⁻⁹mol/L Sch-A, 10⁻8 mol/L Sch-A, and 10⁻7 mol/L Sch-A (neural induction medium + respective Sch-A concentration). Total RNA was extracted (FastPure® Kit; Vazyme), and purity/quantity was assessed spectrophotometrically. cDNA was synthesized (HiScript® III RT SuperMix; Vazyme) on a thermocycler (Analytik Jena, Germany), and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed (ChamQ SYBR Master Mix; Vazyme) on qRT-PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA) using gene-specific primers for Nestin, NSE, SOX2 (Sangon Biotech) and GAPDH for normalization [62]. The relevant gene sequences are presented in Table 1. Relative expression was calculated by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Transcriptomic sequencing

SCAPs from three donors (2 × 105 cells/well) were grouped as Blank, Control (neural induction medium), and Sch-A (10⁻⁹mol/L Sch-A + neural induction medium). RNA was extracted at day 7. After quality control (Agilent 2100), ribosomal RNA was depleted, and libraries were constructed through mRNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection (250–300 bp), and PCR amplification. Sequencing was performed on Illumina platforms (PE150). Data analysis included: quality filtering, alignment (Hisat2), differential expression analysis (DESeq2; |log₂FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05), and GO/KEGG enrichment (padj < 0.05) [62].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA) and SPSS Statistics, and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 10, FlowJo (BD Biosciences), and related software. In this study, quantitative data represent ≥ 3 samples/group; experiments were repeated ≥ 3 times, and the centre line represents the mean, the error line represents the standard deviation, and all experimental results are biological replicates. The criteria for determining statistical significance (P < 0.05) are as follows: For the cell experiment section, methods including distribution verification (Normality and Lognormality Tests, Shapiro-Wilk test), homoscedasticity assessment (Brown-Forsythe test, F test), group comparison tests (ordinary one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test, unpaired t test), and Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests were adopted; for the animal experiment section, methods including distribution verification (Normality and Lognormality Tests, Shapiro-Wilk test), homoscedasticity and sphericity assessment (Levene’s Test, Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity, Brown-Forsythe test, F test), group comparison tests (ordinary one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test, unpaired t test), and Sidak test, Greenhouse-Geisser test were used.

Results

Literature reviews demonstrate that Schisantherin A exhibits neuroprotective and neurorepair effects, while SCAPs possess neural crest-derived properties and highly efficient differentiation potential

Following standardized procedures and based on predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria (Fig. 1), the selected literature is summarized in Tables 2 and 3. Most of these studies focus on the application of Schisantherin A in treating neurological disorders. Further analysis reveals that existing research primarily emphasizes the neuroprotective and neurorepair effects of Schisantherin A, rather than its role in neural differentiation (see Table 3). Crucially, stem cells from apical papilla (SCAPs), with their neural crest-derived properties and highly efficient differentiation potential (see Table 2), provide a novel approach to fill this gap. This overlooked research niche has prompted us to investigate whether Schisantherin A can promote the neural differentiation of SCAPs and its application in repairing neural injury models during skeletal development.

Dental papilla tissues were isolated from third molar tooth germs. Sch-A promotes proliferation, migration, and neural differentiation of SCAPs

Treatment with 10− 9 mol/L Sch-A for 24–48 h significantly promoted cell proliferation (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A), with no cytotoxic effects at 10− 9 − 10− 7 mol/L in colony formation assays, the 10− 9 mol/L group exhibited the highest number of colonies (50.33 ± 5.686, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B–B1). Scratch assays showed that 10− 9 − 10− 8 mol/L Sch-A significantly increased migration rate(86.16 ± 6.195% and 88.76 ± 5.880%, respectively), achieving higher 24-hour closure than controls (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3C–C1). Figures 4A and 5A, and 6A demonstrate the gene expression of NSE, nestin, and SOX2 in SCAPs after 1, 3, and 7 days of induction: for NSE (Fig. 4A), the 10− 9 and 10− 7 mol/L groups increased significantly vs. the control at 3 days, while only the 10− 9 mol/L group showed statistical significance vs. the control at 7 days (P < 0.05); for nestin (Fig. 5A), the control, 10− 9, and 10− 7 mol/L groups increased significantly vs. the blank group at 7 days (P < 0.001); and for SOX2 (Fig. 6A), all groups exhibited low expression across all time points. After 1 and 2 weeks of induction, fluorescence intensity assays and Western blot revealed that for NSE (Fig. 4B–D2), all experimental groups had significantly higher fluorescence vs. the blank group at 1 week (P < 0.05) (Western blot only showed an increasing trend), with no intergroup differences at 2 weeks; for nestin (Fig. 5B–D2), both fluorescence and Western blot results increased significantly in the control and all experimental groups vs. the blank group at 1 week (the 10− 9 mol/L group had the strongest fluorescence and a significant increase vs. the control) (P < 0.05), while fluorescence remained higher vs. the blank group at 2 weeks but WB detected no intergroup differences; for SOX2 (Fig. 6B, C), Western blot showed no expression in any group at 1 and 2 weeks. Thus, 10− 9 mol/L Sch-A with 1 week of induction emerged as the optimal condition.

Sch-A-pretreated SCAPs attenuate rat mental nerve injury and mandibular developmental defects

No significant body weight differences were noted between groups before modeling, nor at any post-modeling time point. Thus, mental nerve injury does not impact rat body weight or its changes (Fig. 8B). Model group rats exhibited hypoesthesia in the lower lip, with pinprick scores (2.194 ± 0.7156) lower than sham controls (3.694 ± 0.2732) (P < 0.05). At the 4th week, the therapeutic effect of Group C was superior to that of Groups A and MC, with statistical significance, and the recovery of sensory function was significant (3.5 ± 0.577, P < 0.05) (Fig. 8C). Based on HE and toluidine blue staining results, the morphology of mental nerves in each group was observed. The Sham group showed no compression of mental nerves, while the remaining groups exhibited abnormal twisted and compressed morphology. Microscopically, white gaps appeared between cells in the MC group; groups A and B had a small amount of nerve myelin sheaths and axons, and group C had a large number of nerve myelin sheaths and axons in mental nerves (Fig. 8D, E), nerve structure restoration was evident in H&E and toluidine blue stains. Mandibular morphometry revealed reduced inter-mental foramen distance (Mef-Mef) in model (7.397 ± 0.247 mm) versus sham groups (7.743 ± 0.132 mm) (P < 0.05). Group C (Sch-A-pretreated SCAPs) showed increased Mef-Mef distance (7.987 ± 0.120 mm) versus model (P < 0.05), along with improved ramus length (Con-Go) (12.37 ± 0.225 mm) and total mandibular length (Con-L1) (26.71 ± 0.2198 mm) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8F). Notably, some parameters approached sham group levels, demonstrating that Sch-A-pretreated SCAPs repair mental nerve injury and reverse mandibular developmental impairment.

Discussion

To investigate the effect of Schisandrin A on the proliferation of SCAPs, the present study aimed to identify the optimal concentration and time point of Sch-A for inducing SCAP neurogenic differentiation, and further evaluate the repair capacity of Sch-A-induced neurodifferentiated SCAPs in treating mental nerve injury and their regulatory role in mandibular development in growing rats.

The SCAPs are ectomesenchymal stem cells of neural crest origin. Currently, a consensus on identification markers for dental-derived stem cells is lacking, and the universal criteria for multipotent mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are typically applied. MSCs should express CD105, CD73, and CD90 ((≥ 95% positivity) and be negative for CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79ɑ or CD19, and HLA-DR (≤ 5% positivity) [48], while exhibiting trilineage differentiation potential into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes in vitro. Here, CD105, CD73, CD90, CD45, CD34, and HLA-DR served as identification markers, and trilineage assays confirmed that SCAPs exhibit canonical MSC characteristics.

The SCAPs, isolated from immature permanent tooth apices, exhibit high accessibility, robust proliferation, and potent growth factor secretion. Given their neural crest origin, they express neural stem cell (NSC) markers [58] and possess multilineage differentiation (e.g., odontogenic, osteogenic, neurogenic) [27]. Under induction, these neural crest-derived cells exhibit in vitro neurogenic potential, expressing nestin, NSE, βIII-tubulin, and other neural markers in B27/bFGF/EGF-containing medium [63, 64]. In vivo, SCAPs improve locomotor function and neuroprotection in spinal cord-injured rats [65], enhance axonal regeneration in sciatic nerve injury models [32], and secrete neurotrophic factors (e.g., NGF and BDNF) [33]. Their neurogenic and regenerative capacities may exceed those of bone marrow MSCs [66], positioning them as an optimal source for NSC induction.

Schisantherin A (Sch-A), a bioactive monomer derived from Schisandra chinensis, modulates cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Our experimental data established 10− 9 mol/L as the optimal concentration, which significantly enhanced SCAP proliferation, migration, and neurogenic differentiation. Specifically, assays revealed that low-concentration Sch-A promoted SCAP proliferation without cytotoxicity; this effect was concentration-dependent, being strongest at 10− 9 mol/L and diminishing at higher concentrations (up to 10− 7 mol/L). Furthermore, treatment with 10− 9 or 10− 8 mol/L Sch-A significantly accelerated cell migration at both 12 and 24 h.

In this context, the molecular markers we observed align with established paradigms of neural differentiation. Nestin, a marker of neural precursors and NSCs, denotes early neural lineage commitment. During neuroinduction, pluripotent stem cells transition to enter the neural precursors, where nestin facilitates cytoskeletal remodeling to support proliferation and migration [59, 67]. The Sch-A-induced upregulation of nestin observed in our study is consistent with this role (Fig. 5). Chen et al. observed that H9 embryonic stem cells upregulated neural precursor markers (nestin and PAX6) while downregulating pluripotency marker (OCT4) [68]. The NSE, a neuronal maturation marker, increases alongside synaptic proteins (e.g., synaptophysin) in neuroinduced MSCs, indicating functional neural phenotype acquisition [60, 69, 70]. Our finding of elevated NSE supports the progression of SCAP toward a neuronal phenotype under Sch-A treatment. The SOX2, a core transcription factor maintaining stemness [61, 71], must be downregulated for pluripotency exit [72, 73]. Its expression level dictates function: high levels sustain pluripotency, whereas low levels facilitate lineage differentiation [74]. The SOX2 interacts with hnRNPs to balance pluripotency-differentiation balance via alternative splicing [75]. Although SOX2 and nestin are co-expressed in early neural precursors, SOX2 downregulation marks the shift from self-renewal to lineage-specific differentiation [67, 76–78]. In our study, the downregulation and eventual absence of SOX2 across all Sch-A-treated groups signifies this critical exit from a pluripotent state. The molecular pattern of elevated NSE/nestin and absent SOX2 observed in our model may involve specific signaling pathways. In this study, elevated NSE/nestin and absent SOX2 may involve JNK signaling or ETS transcription factors (e.g., ELK1). Dual-specificity phosphatase 16 (DUSP16) modulates JNK phosphorylation to inhibit SOX2, promoting neural precursor differentiation [79]. Epigenetic regulation may also contribute: histone deacetylase inhibitors enhance histone acetylation, activating neurogenic genes, and suppressing SOX2-mediated stemness [61, 80, 81]. Microenvironmental signals (Wnt, Notch, FGF) may drive nestin+ cell differentiation, with SOX2 downregulation relieving NeuroD1 inhibition [82, 83]. Collectively, Our multi-dimensional validation of Sch-A-induced neurogenic differentiation, integrating transcriptional (qRT-PCR), protein (WB), and phenotypic (IF) data, demonstrated a consistent pattern: upregulation of NSE and Nestin, downregulation of SOX2, and stronger effects after 1-week induction. Thus, the formed evidence chain collectively confirms that 10− 9 mol/L Sch-A most effectively promotes SCAP proliferation, migration, and neurogenic differentiation.

The sciatic nerve injury model, a cornerstone of peripheral nerve research [84, 85], has limited applicability to craniofacial nerve injury due to anatomical and embryological distinctions. Craniofacial nerves (e.g., trigeminal, facial) derive predominantly from neural crest cells (e.g., Axin2+, Prx1+), whereas trunk nerves originate from the neural tube. This divergence links craniofacial nerves to skeletal development; for instance, the trigeminal nerve regulates jawbone homeostasis via sensory-sympathetic innervation [86, 87], unlike the weak nerve-bone interactions in limbs.

The mental nerve, a sensory branch of the inferior alveolar nerve, exits the mandible via the mental foramen to innervate the lower lip, chin, and labial gingiva of anterior mandibular teeth [88]. Its superficial location and isolation (devoid of adjacent major vessels) make it ideal for modeling craniofacial nerve injury and studying mandibular development impacts. Clinically, mental nerve injury occurs frequently due to trauma, apical surgery, endodontic treatment, cystectomy, genioplasty, orthognathic surgery, molar extraction, or dental implant placement [89, 90], causing sensory disorders (hypoesthesia, numbness, anesthesia, hyperesthesia) [91]. Investigating its mandibular developmental consequences and therapeutic interventions is vital for understanding neuro-osseous crosstalk and improving trauma/developmental deformity management.

Four-week-old male SD rats were selected with a 4-week post-injury observation period, as mandibular growth peaks between postnatal weeks 4–8 [54]. Peripheral nerve maturation occurs prenatally; the inferior alveolar nerve is structurally complete at birth, with myelination concluding by 3–4 weeks [92–96]. Thus, 4-8-week-old rats, with mature peripheral nerves and active mandibular growth, are suitable for studying craniofacial sensory nerve injury and bone development. For using human SCAPs in rat models, we referenced prior studies [33, 34, 97, 98] where human dental stem cells (including SCAPs) were directly injected into rats with favorable outcomes and no obvious species incompatibility. Given SCAPs’ paracrine effects [34] and our intermittent administration (once/week for 4 weeks), sustained therapeutic efficacy is ensured even after cell elimination, so no anti-rejection interventions were needed. Notably, mandibular Mef-Mef length in the MC group (injury) versus Sham group, indicating impaired transverse mandibular development. H&E and toluidine blue staining revealed preserved myelin/axon structures in groups A, B, and C, most prominently in group C. Group C exhibited increased Con - Go, Mef - Mef, Go - Maf, L1 - Mef, and Con - L1 lengths relative to MC, with Con - Go and Go - Maf exceeding the Sham level, suggesting Sch-A-pretreated SCAPs not only repair injury but also enhance mandibular development. Similar staining and bone metrics between Sham and MC groups may reflect rapid self-recovery in juveniles or insufficient injury severity (1-minute clamping), masking group A’s effects. Pinprick tests showed that at the 4th week, the therapeutic effect of Group C was better than that of Groups A and MC with statistical significance, while the modest behavioral improvement in other treated groups versus MC without significance., possibly due to delayed sensory versus structural recovery [97, 99] or insufficient observation time (> 4 weeks recommended) and measurement sensitivity.

The SCAPs primarily repair nerve injury via paracrine neurotrophic factors and neural differentiation [98, 100]. Kolar et al. [30] highlighted SCAP paracrine effects, detecting NANOG expression and secretion of NGF, GDNF, BDNF, ANGPT1, and VEGFA. In vitro, SH-SY5Y neurons extended longer axons in SCAP-conditioned medium - an effect abolished by BDNF neutralization (unlike VEGFA). In vivo, SCAP-loaded fibrin conduits enhanced axonal regeneration and protected dorsal root ganglion neurons in a 10-mm rat sciatic defect. Cao et al. [51] reported SCAPs replace injured neural cells, with KDM2B promoting neurodifferentiation via epigenetic regulation (H3K4me3 activation on neurogenic genes and H3K27me3 inhibition relief), potentiating spinal cord repair. Zhang et al. [31] showed that targeting the MLL1/WDR5 complex or HES1 activity enhances SCAP neurogenic potential for spinal repair. Consistent with these, Sch-A-induced SCAPs showed superior mental nerve repair efficacy versus naïve SCAPs.

In the present study, Sch-A at a concentration of 10− 9 mol/L effectively promotes the proliferation and migration of SCAPs. Under the condition of neural induction medium, it significantly facilitates the neural differentiation of SCAPs, with the expression of specific proteins Nestin and NSE. Transcriptome sequencing analysis revealed that Sch-A promotes the neural differentiation of SCAPs primarily by regulating the expression of genes associated with the Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway. When SCAPs induced by Sch-A were applied to a rat model of mental nerve injury, they effectively improved the neural status, rescued the injured nerve, and inhibited the abnormal development of the mandible.

However, the mechanism by which Sch-A induces SCAPs to differentiate into neural-like stem cells requires further verification. Additionally, the mechanism underlying the repair of mental nerve injury by neural-like cells derived from Sch-A-induced SCAPs warrants further investigation. Furthermore, the specific role of the mental nerve in regulating mandibular development deserves in-depth study.
