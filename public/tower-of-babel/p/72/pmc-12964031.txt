Ten-year experience of CD22 CAR T cells for children and young adults with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia∗
Authors: Alexandra Dreyzin, Bonnie Yates, Haneen Shalabi, Sara K. Silbert, Hao-Wei Wang, Constance M. Yuan, Chloe N. Hoang, August A. Culbert, Flavia Gava, Monica S. Nair, Victoria M. ABSTRACT
Key Points•CD22 CAR T cells demonstrated efficacy in relapsed/refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, regardless of disease burden.•Final analysis of this decade-long trial offers insight into IEC-HS management and CAR T-cell pharmacokinetics.

Introduction

Antigen-negative relapse after CD19-targeted immunotherapies remains a leading cause of relapse in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), necessitating alternative therapies.1, 2, 3 CD22, expressed in 60% to 90% of B-ALL samples, is an important target for immune- and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell targeting.4 The first CD22-targeted CAR T-cell construct, developed at the National Cancer Institute (NCI), was tested in a phase 1 trial in children, adolescents, and young adults (CAYA) with relapsed/refractory (R/R) B-cell malignancies starting in 2014.5 As one of the first CAR T cells targeting an antigen other than CD19, this trial provided novel insights into CAR T-cell efficacy, the impact of manufacturing changes, and management of inflammatory toxicities in patients whose leukemia was refractory to all other available therapies.

The initial cohort demonstrated efficacy of CD22 CAR T cells and revealed the importance of antigen density on response and immune escape.5 Subsequently, the addition of CD4/CD8 selection of the apheresis product, to create more homogenous starting material and enhance manufacturing feasibility, resulted in enhanced CAR T-cell potency and toxicity, prompting dose deescalation.6

Additionally, toxicities observed with CD22 CAR T cells differed from those described with CD19-targeted CAR T cells. Despite lower rates of severe cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome,7,8 more patients were observed to have hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)–like toxicities, leading to characterization of this phenomenon.9,10 This supported further defining immune effector cell–associated HLH-like syndrome (IEC-HS) and developing grading system and treatment guidelines.11

Although concurrently developed CD22-targeting CAR T cells exhibited limited antitumor activity, highlighting the importance of CAR structure in efficacy,12 the NCI construct received US Food and Drug Administration “breakthrough therapy designation” in 2019 for treatment of B-ALL that is relapsed after, or refractory to, CD19 targeting. This designation, along with its efficacy in adults with B-cell lymphoma,13 paved the way for future development.

With limited options for patients with R/R B-ALL, this phase 1 trial remained open through 2024. We now report summary results from this decade-long experience highlighting insights on CD22 CAR T-cell pharmacokinetics, preemptive IEC-HS therapy, and additional correlatives.

Materials and methods

CD22 CAR T-cell trial design

The trial design and manufacturing of autologous CD22-targeted CAR T cells incorporating the m971 single-chain variable fragment and 4-1BB costimulatory domain have been described previously.5 The protocol was approved by the NCI institutional review board and the NIH recombinant DNA advisory committee. All patients and/or guardians provided consent and assent when appropriate. Briefly, CAYA with R/R B-cell malignancies were eligible if they had CD22 expression of >80% by flow cytometry or 15% by immunohistochemistry. The initial escalation was up to 3 × 106 transduced CAR T cells per kg. After incorporation of CD4/CD8 selection of the apheresis product, the dose of 3 × 105 CAR T cells per kg was used as the expansion dose.6

With an amendment introduced in March 2021, a preemptive approach to IEC-HS, incorporating the use of steroids and anakinra for patients who developed CRS with a ferritin level >50 000 μg/L, was adopted (Table 2). Data cutoff was 30 May 2024. This trial was registered at www.ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT02315612).

Disease assessment

Baseline medullary disease burden was defined based on morphologic evaluation of bone marrow biopsy before CAR T-cell therapy, augmented with flow cytometric measurable residual disease (MRD) assessments using a threshold of <0.01% of mononuclear cells to identify MRD. All patients also had cerebrospinal fluid evaluations. Extramedullary disease (EMD) was evaluated based on history, physical examination, and imaging with positron emission tomography–computed tomography scans as clinically indicated. Baseline disease burden was categorized as low (<5% bone marrow blasts by flow cytometry) or high (≥5% bone marrow blasts).

Response evaluation

Response was evaluated on approximately day 28 after infusion with bone marrow aspirate/biopsy, lumbar puncture, and follow-up positron emission tomography–computed tomography scan if they had known EMD. Complete response (CR) was defined as resolution of medullary disease by bone marrow morphology and qualified by MRD response. Partial response, stable disease, or progressive disease were based on established criteria but analyzed collectively as “no response” (NR). For documentation of relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS), patients were followed up for a minimum of 3 months after infusion.

Toxicity assessments

CRS and IEC-HS were identified and graded retrospectively based on the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria.11,14 Because the immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome definition cannot be retroactively applied, neurotoxicity was captured based on Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events. IEC-associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT) grading was based on European Hematology Association/European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EHA/EBMT) consensus.15 Two patients were excluded from ICAHT calculation due to insufficient data in the first 30 days (1 died shortly after infusion and 1 was taken off study due to disease progression.) Data on infections and hospital course were captured from day 0 to day 60 after CAR infusion. An infection was defined as a distinct clinicopathological entity based on microbiologic or histopathologic data or as a clinical syndrome of infection and graded by Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events. Patients were censored at day 60 or before if they were treated for progressive disease, underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT), died, or were lost to follow-up.

Pharmacokinetic analyses

CD22 CAR T-cell pharmacokinetics were calculated based on peripheral blood assessments quantified by flow cytometry. CD22 CAR detection was done using a commercially available fluorescently labeled recombinant protein: recombinant human siglec-2/CD22 protein conjugated to Atto 647N (R&D Systems, catalog no. ATM1968-050). Peripheral blood was generally analyzed weekly for the first month after infusion. CAR T-cell persistence was monitored at clinical follow-up time points as feasible.

Peak CAR T-cell expansion (Cmax) was calculated as the percentage of T cells expressing CD22 CAR from the concurrent absolute lymphocyte count. Area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using all available time points from day 0 through day 28. One patient, with insufficient data, was excluded from pharmacokinetic calculations.

Statistical analysis

Toxicity, response, and pharmacokinetics data were reported using descriptive statistics. Patients continued to be followed up on a long-term follow-up protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02315599) for key outcomes of relapse, late toxicities, or death after CD22 CAR infusion, inclusive of post-HSCT monitoring. They were censored at the end of data collection on 30 May 2024. Kaplan-Meier estimates were used for OS and RFS. Kaplan-Meier curves were also used to estimate CAR T-cell long-term survival as a measure of CAR T-cell persistence, as per Mody et al.16

A series of additional correlatives were conducted at trial completion, examining relationships between different toxicities and baseline disease burden on outcomes. Groups were compared using nonparametric t test, χ2 test, Fisher exact test, or log-rank test, as appropriate using GraphPad Prism version 10.1. Pharmacokinetics were compared across doses and stratified by disease burden as well as response. Two-tailed P values of <.05 were considered significant.

The starting dose level (dose level 1 [DL1]) was 3 × 105 transduced CAR T cells per kg; subsequently, dosing was escalated to 1 × 106 CAR T cells per kg (DL2) and 3 × 106 CAR T cells per kg (DL3), as previously reported.5 DL2 was initially selected as the expansion dose based on toxicity at DL3. A total of 17 (21.8%) patients were treated at DL2 before T-cell selection of the apheresis product was introduced. As previously described, the T-cell selection resulted in increased toxicities, prompting deescalation to DL1 with T-cell selection for the final expansion cohort.6 A total of 46 (59.0%) patients were treated at this expansion dose.

A summary of toxicities observed in the overall cohort is illustrated in Figure 2, with full details in supplemental Table 5. There were 2 patients who experienced treatment-related grade 5 events; 1 with multiorgan failure secondary to sepsis, which occurred on day 39 after infusion; and 1 with acute respiratory distress syndrome secondary to CRS, which occurred on day 9 after infusion. CRS

Among 78 patients treated, 66 (84.6%) experienced CRS, with 13 (16.7%) having severe CRS (grade ≥3), including the aforementioned patient with acute respiratory distress syndrome secondary to CRS.6 In the expansion cohort (DL1 with T-cell selection, n = 46), 40 (87.0%) and 9 (19.6%) patients experienced any CRS or severe CRS, respectively. The median onset of CRS was on day 8 after infusion (range, 3-22), with median duration of 5 days (range, 2-13). Importantly, disease burden was not a major factor influencing CRS rates or severity: severe CRS occurred in 2 of 23 (8.7%) patients with low disease burden and in 11 of 55 (20%) patients with high disease burden (P = .32; Figure 3A). Neurotoxicity

Neurotoxicity occurred in 18 (23.1%) patients across the whole cohort, and in 9 (19.6%) patients in the expansion cohort. The median day of onset was on day 5 (range, 0-14) after infusion, with a median duration of 7 days (range, 2-23), incorporating intermittent symptoms. Severe neurotoxicity (grade ≥3) was rare, occurring in only 1 patient in the expansion cohort, and was comprised an intracranial hemorrhage, which has been described previously.6 As previously reported, neurotoxicity was predominantly mild and comprised cognitive or mood changes, auditory or visual hallucinations, or vertigo, all of which were transient, with resolution at 1 month.7 Neurotoxicity did not significantly correlate with severe CRS (P = .48) or disease burden (P = .42).

IEC-HS

Although a detailed characterization of HLH-like syndrome (ie, CAR HLH) across a portion of patients has been reported previously, all patients have since been reanalyzed using the more recent IEC-HS criteria.9,11 Accordingly, IEC-HS was seen in 28 (35.9%) of all patients who underwent infusion and in 20 (43.5%) patients in the expansion cohort, all of whom had antecedent CRS. Overall, IEC-HS onset occurred at a median of 11 days (range, 7-24) after infusion and 4 days from CRS onset (range, 0-16). Half of all IEC-HS cases were mild (14 [50%] patients with grade 1-2), with grade 3 IEC-HS seen in 11 (39.3%) and grade 4 in 3 (10.7%) patients. In addition to hyperferritinemia, common laboratory abnormalities that contributed to IEC-HS diagnosis were hepatic transaminitis (92.9%), cytopenias (82.1%), hypofibrinogenemia (71.4%), and hypertriglyceridemia (89.3%). The median number of IEC-HS manifestations per patient was 5 (range, 2-7). IEC-HS occurred slightly more often among patients with high disease burden and those with severe CRS (Figure 3B-C). Specifically, IEC-HS occurred in 40% of patients with high disease burden and 26% with low disease burden (P = .31). Similarly, IEC-HS occurred in 53.8% of patients with grade ≥3 CRS and in 39.6% of patients with grade 1 to 2 CRS (P = .37). All had full resolution of IEC-HS with exception of 1 patient whose course was complicated by sepsis and multiorgan failure before treatment of IEC-HS.5

Treatment of IEC-HS evolved over the conduct of the study and included steroids and anakinra most frequently, with 2 patients also receiving ruxolitinib and 1 patient receiving etoposide, the latter for CAR T-cell–associated lymphocytosis, which was recently described elsewhere.17 Treatment of IEC-HS is summarized in Figure 4. Toward the end of the trial, we adopted a preemptive approach to IEC-HS management (Table 2), in which preemptive treatment was initiated in patients with rapidly rising ferritin without waiting for the full constellation of IEC-HS symptoms to manifest. Seven patients were treated after the introduction of this amendment, 4 of whom developed IEC-HS. Cytopenias

Cytopenias in recipients of CD22 CAR T cells have been recently reported.18 In brief, 46 (60.5%) patients overall and 28 (60.9%) in the expansion cohort had ICAHT grade ≥3. On day 30 after infusion, grade ≥3 neutropenia was observed in 53 (69.7%) patients and grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia in 38 (50%) patients. In the expansion cohort, grade ≥3 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia occurred in 29 (63%) and 22 (47.8%) patients, respectively. Patients with high baseline disease burden were more likely than those with low baseline disease burden to develop severe ICAHT (73.6% vs 30.4%; P = .0007; Figure 3D).

Infections

Among all patients, 29 (37.2%) had an infection and 15 (19.2%) had a severe infection (grade ≥3) within 60 days after infusion, comparable with infection rates in the expansion cohort with 17 (37.0%) and 8 (17.4%) of 46 patients experiencing any infection or a severe infection, respectively.

Those with grade ≥3 ICAHT were more likely to develop an infection by day 60 (Figure 3E). In addition, those with IEC-HS were more likely to have an infection than those without IEC-HS (Figure 3F). Infections occurred in 17 of 28 (60.7%) patients with IEC-HS and 12 of 50 (24.0%) patients without IEC-HS (P = .0029). Similarly, severe infections occurred in 10 (35.7%) patients with IEC-HS and 5 (10.0%) patients without (P = .014). Among patients with IEC-HS, severe infections were slightly more common for those who were treated with steroids (8 patients [53.3%]) than among those who did not receive steroids (2 patients [15.4%]; P = .055). There was no association between anakinra use and infections in this population (supplemental Table 6). After implementation of preemptive IEC-HS therapy, 2 of 4 patients with IEC-HS developed infections, aligned with overall infection rates among those with IEC-HS.

Clinical response and survival

The overall CR rate among 77 evaluable patients was 70.1%; among 54 responders, 45 (83.3%) were MRD-negative, 8 were MRD-positive, and 1 did not have available MRD data (Figure 5A-B). Among those who were MRD-positive at day 28, 2 additional patients subsequently achieved MRD negativity (supplemental Table 7) Across 46 patients from the expansion cohort, CR rate was similar at 71.7% (n = 33), with 87.9% achieving an MRD-negative CR at day 28, and 1 additional CR at a later evaluation. CD22 expression was retained in patients with NR, although decreased antigen binding capacity was observed in 11 of 23 nonresponders (supplemental Table 8). CR rates in the expansion cohort did not vary with baseline disease burden or based on presence or absence of EMD (Figure 5C-D). There were only 4 patients in the expansion cohort who had low bone marrow disease burden and no EMD, 3 (75%) of whom achieved CR. In the expansion cohort, OS was 13.3 months (95% CI, 8.8-24.9), and RFS was 8.6 months (95% CI, 4.4-15.1; supplemental Figure 1A-B). With median follow-up of 4.5 years (range, 0.3-8.8), 16 patients remain alive, of whom 11 (68.8%) received a post–CAR T-cell consolidative HSCT. Among 54 responders, 24 went directly to HSCT at a median of 65 days after infusion. In a landmark analysis, which included 28 responders in the expansion cohort remaining in remission 2 months after infusion, 18 went directly to HSCT, 13 (72%) of whom relapsed with RFS of 1.08 years after the 2-month mark (95% CI, 0.42 to not reached). Among those who did not go on to HSCT, 10 (100%) relapsed, with RFS of 0.17 years (95% CI, 0.11 to not reached; P = .0002). Patients who were transplant-naïve before CD22 CAR T cells had similar post-CAR HSCT survival (median OS, 2.4 years [95% CI, 1.15 to not reached]) compared with those who had undergone a previous transplant (OS, 1.9 years [95% CI, 1.1 to not reached]; P = .44; supplemental Figure 2A-C). One patient, who underwent 2 transplants before CD22 CAR T cells, developed a second malignancy, papillary thyroid carcinoma, which was thought to be secondary to total body irradiation and etoposide exposure, unrelated to CAR T-cell therapy.

Correlations of response with toxicities

As expected, patients with CR were more likely to have CRS compared with those who had NR (54/54 [100%] CR vs 11/23 [47.8%] of those with NR; P < .0001). Across the expansion cohort, all 33 patients with CR had CRS, compared with 7 of 13 (53.8%) among those with NR (P = .0002). IEC-HS occurred in 24 (44.3%) patients with CR, compared with 4 (17.4%) patients with NR (P = .037). This difference was less prominent in the expansion cohort, in which IEC-HS occurred in 48.5% of those with CR and 30.8% of those with NR (P = .34). Rates of neurotoxicity, ICAHT, and infections did not differ between patients with and without CR (supplemental Table 5)

CAR T-cell pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetic data were available for a total of 77 patients, with missing data from 1 patient who died shortly after infusion (supplemental Table 11). Peripheral blood CAR T-cell concentration measurements beyond the first month were available for 52 patients (67.5%). The median peak CAR T-cell Cmax was 547.5 cells per μL (range, 0-34 069) overall and 546.7 cells per μL (range, 0-34 069) for the expansion cohort. The Cmax varied by dose, with a median of 803.4 cells per μL for DL2 compared with 5.5 cells per μL for DL1 (P = .0034). With T-cell selection in the expansion cohort, Cmax was higher than that of unselected cells (median, 546.7 cells per μL for DL1 with T-cell selection vs 5.5 cells per μL for DL1; P = .0041), although this difference was not observed at DL2. The AUC in the first 28 days followed the same patterns as peak expansion.

Timing of peak expansion was a median of 14 days (range, 12-50) overall with no variation by dose. Longer persistence of CAR T cells was observed at DL2 than DL1 (median CAR T-cell survival of 147 vs 45 days; P = .048), as well as in the expansion cohort (DL1 with T-cell selection) compared with DL1 (131 vs 45 days; P = .0069).

Across 70 patients with flow cytometric evaluation of day 28 bone marrow for presence for CAR T cells, the median percent CAR T cells was 36% (range, 0%-94% overall, and 40.5% [range, 0%-93.6%]) in the expansion cohort. Among 38 patients who had bone marrow evaluations beyond 1 month, median CAR T-cell persistence in bone marrow was 198 days. Flow cytometric evaluation of day 28 cerebrospinal fluid was available for 56 patients and showed a median of 53.5% CAR T cells (range, 0%-92.2%; supplemental Figure 3).

Within the expansion cohort, pharmacokinetic measures did not vary with disease burden, although an interesting observation was presence of expansion in patients who had EMD only (supplemental Table 4). Peak peripheral and bone marrow expansion, day 28 AUC, and persistence were greater for patients with CR than those with NR (Figure 6D,F,H). Discussion

This phase 1 clinical trial of CD22 CAR T cells spanned a decade and provided CAR T-cell therapy to 80 CAYA with R/R B-ALL (n = 78) or B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 2). Collectively, treatment with CD22 CAR T cells resulted in a CR rate of 70.1% overall and 71.7% for the expansion cohort of this highly refractory population; particularly notable in the context of a phase 1 trial in which most patients had experience disease relapse after CD19-targeted CAR T cells, blinatumomab, or inotuzumab. Additionally, although response rates were lower than in CAR-naïve patients receiving tisagenlecleucel, most patients had high disease burden and included young adults up to age 39 years, thus including patients with generally worse prognosis and more high-risk features. Because the application of CD22 CAR T cells will be in the relapsed or combinatorial setting, this older and more refractory population is representative of the target patient population. In this analysis, we report toxicity rates and disease response metrics, examine relationships between disease burden and toxicities, provide an in-depth description of IEC-HS management with a novel preemptive strategy, and demonstrate an approach to cellular therapy pharmacokinetics.

CD22 targeting is an increasingly important part of B-ALL therapy. With the introduction of blinatumomab as part of up-front treatment,19 CD22 targeting may become critical for relapse, especially for those who are CD19−.20 Furthermore, even for those with retained CD19 expression, nonresponse to blinatumomab is associated with poor CD19 CAR T-cell response, suggesting the need for alternative therapies.21 Although inotuzumab ozogamicin has response rates approaching 80% for R/R B-ALL when patients with EMD are excluded,22 response rate in a Children’s Oncology Group study, which included those with EMD, was 58.3%.23 Key limitations of inotuzumab include inefficacy in CNS disease alongside risk of veno-occlusive disease, which was seen in 25% of pediatric patients.22,24,25 Data from CD22 CAR T-cell trials, including the study described here and a recent trial from Stanford,26 demonstrate efficacy in patients with EMD as well as those who have relapsed after CD19 and CD22 targeting.

The response rates and toxicities observed in our study are in line with those reported in 2 other early phase trials of CD22 CAR T cells reported by Schultz et al26 and Myers et al,27 including the lower rates of severe CRS, limited neurotoxicity, and comparable initial CR rates of 74% to 75%. Notably, the trial described by Schultz et al used the same construct that was used in our trial. Although both studies reported high rates of initial response, few patients went on to consolidative transplant, and relapse was common, with median duration of remission at 2.5 and 5.3 months, respectively. In this cohort of patients with refractory and often high burden disease, our correlative studies show similar CAR T-cell expansion and efficacy with high and low disease burden. This is in contrast to previous studies of CD19-targeted CAR T cells, which demonstrated worse outcomes in patients with high baseline disease burden.28, 29, 30 Furthermore, in our expansion cohort, CR rates were similar for those with and without EMD, although patients with non-CNS EMD have generally had worse responses to CD19 CAR T cells.31 Of note, patients with high disease burden comprised most our cohort and some patients with low marrow disease had EMD, which may make them more similar to the high disease burden group. Given the potency of CD22 CAR T cells in this population, we anticipate even better outcomes in patients with truly low burden disease.

The experience from this trial has also enhanced our understanding of IEC-mediated toxicity management. IEC-HS was recognized as an inflammatory process distinct from CRS, and remained prevalent after dose reduction in our expansion cohort.11 We demonstrate here that IEC-HS occurred even in patients with low burden disease and mild CRS, underscoring the importance of continued monitoring and distinction from severe CRS. Rather than waiting for end-organ manifestations, in the context of this construct, our preemptive approach proposes use of combination therapy with steroids and anakinra earlier in the course, in line with the more recent consensus definition of IEC-HS based primarily on elevated or rising ferritin in the appropriate clinical context.11 Preemptive therapy may help to mitigate both severe IEC-HS and adverse effects of prolonged steroid treatment for future patients.9 Importantly, although IEC-HS was associated with increased risk of infections, the increased risk in this population did not correlate with more steroid or anakinra use. Additionally, although previous reports have raised concerns about the effect of steroids on CAR T-cell function,32 patients who received the preemptive steroids continued to exhibit peripheral CAR T-cell expansion. The strategy outlined here is unique to this construct ad prospectively studies in this trial. Applicability to other constructs is unknown. However, as IEC-HS has been observed in the setting of CD19 and B-cell maturation antigen CAR T cells and has been associated with worse outcomes,33,34 prospective study of preemptive therapy to prevent severe IEC-HS is warranted. This trial spanned a decade, providing access to an effective treatment to patients in need, especially those with CD19− disease. Admittedly, the treatments available for relapsed B-ALL changed over this time frame and increasing options may have altered the population referred for CD22 CAR T cells. Additionally, the variability of patients’ clinical course before CD22 CAR T cells as well as in management after CAR T cells make direct comparisons in outcomes challenging. Nonetheless, this broad experience provides a foundation for advancing CD22-targeted CAR T cells beyond early phase trials.

Lessons learned from this trial have already been instrumental in the design of dual-antigen targeting via bivalent and bicistronic CAR T-cell constructs (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT05442515 and NCT03448393),35 along with sequential targeting approaches. Although the registration study for CD22 CAR T cells (firicabtagene autoleucel; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05972720) in adults with B-cell lymphoma recently closed due to suboptimal remission duration, initial remission rates demonstrated efficacy.36 Moving forward, CD22 CAR T cells will be critical for patients who relapse after CD19-targeting therapy or as part of combinatorial CAR T-cell strategies at initial relapse. Increased access will require collaboration with pharmaceutical companies. The importance of CD22 CAR T cells as a potential curative therapy for children with refractory B-ALL remains a compelling reason to develop this therapy for commercial use.
