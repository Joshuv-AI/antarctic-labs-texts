Clinical Value of Peripheral Blood Gene Expression Profile and Donor-Derived Cellfree DNA for Identifying Persistent Rejection
Authors: Raymond L. Heilman, James N. Fleming, Sook H. Park, Christabel Rebello, Steve Kleiboeker, John Holman, John J. Friedewald
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Points Peripheral blood biomarkers may have value in monitoring kidney transplant recipients after treatment of acute rejection.The donor-derived cellfree DNA may be more sensitive to identifying antibody-mediated rejection and gene expression profile may be more sensitive to identifying acute cellular rejection. BackgroundPersistent rejection is an increasingly recognized barrier to long-term kidney allograft survival. A noninvasive method to help identify patients with persistent rejection in need of biopsy would be valuable.MethodsThis was a post hoc analysis of a multicenter observational study. Patients who had a biopsy-proven acute rejection, had another biopsy within 9 months (270 days), and had a biopsy-paired biomarker sample were included.ResultsA total of 64 index rejections in 58 patients with repeat biopsies were identified with a median time to repeat biopsy of 100 days. Persistent rejection was present in 61%; 69% of follow-up biopsies were performed in clinically stable patients. Peripheral blood gene expression profile (GEP) demonstrated a sensitivity of 59%, specificity of 76%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 79%, and negative predictive value of 54%. Donor-derived cellfree DNA (dd-cfDNA) demonstrated a sensitivity of 62%, specificity of 86%, PPV of 88%, and negative predictive value of 56%. For repeat biopsies within 90 days of rejection in clinically stable patients (63% of repeat biopsies), both GEP and dd-cfDNA had specificities and PPVs of 100%. GEP was more likely to be positive in T-cell–mediated rejection while dd-cfDNA was more likely to be positive in antibody-mediated rejection.ConclusionsBoth GEP and dd-cfDNA may have utility in identifying clinically stable patients with persistent rejection in need of biopsy; however, they identify different types of rejections.Clinical Trial registration number:NCT01531257.

Introduction

Persistent rejection after treatment of both subclinical acute rejection (SubAR) and clinical acute rejection (cAR) is common, increasingly recognized, and associated with worse outcomes in kidney transplantation.1,2 Persistent rejection (within 2–9 months of initial rejection) has been identified in 32%–64% of patients after treatment of ≥Banff borderline biopsy-proven acute rejection (BPAR) and 61% of untreated ≥Banff borderline rejection.1,2 There has been a documented dissociation between recovery of kidney function, which most clinicians use to determine treatment efficacy, and persistence of T-cell–mediated rejection (TCMR) by histology.3,4 Furthermore, these episodes of persistent rejection have demonstrated a significant association with increased risk of death-censored and all-cause allograft loss (hazard ratio, 2.98 and 2.30, respectively), highlighting their clinical importance.1 However, repeat biopsies in the absence of clinical concern are not standard practice at most institutions for multiple reasons, including risks associated with invasive biopsies. A noninvasive method to identify patients with persistent rejection in need of a biopsy would be valuable in this setting.

Peripheral blood gene expression profiles (GEPs) have been developed and clinically validated as rule-out tests for SubAR.5 Their focus on gene expression changes that are associated with either normal biopsies demonstrating no immunologic damage to the allograft or biopsies showing rejection in the absence of clinical symptoms could be theoretically useful in the assessment of patients after treatment of rejection to identify continued gene expression consistent with immunologic injury.

Donor-derived cellfree DNA (dd-cfDNA) is a biomarker that has been developed and clinically validated as a rule-out test for cAR. Dd-cfDNA is a marker of damage to the allograft. Although nonspecific to immune-mediated rejection, it has been shown to be more likely to identify antibody-mediated rejection (AMR), and so provides additional utility along with GEP.6–8

We investigated the potential of kidney transplant biomarkers to assist in diagnosis of persistent rejection in a post hoc analysis of a multicenter prospective observational trial with follow-up surveillance biopsies.

Methods

Study Design

This was a post hoc analysis of a multicenter 24-month observational study that included surveillance and for-cause biopsies, along with follow-up biopsies after treated subclinical rejections. The Clinical Trials in Organ Transplantation 08 (CTOT-08) study was designed to develop and evaluate the performance of a novel GEP biomarker in patients with stable allograft function after kidney transplantation.5 The CTOT-08 study enrolled 307 patients who underwent surveillance biopsies 2–6, 12, and 24 months after kidney transplant, as well as for-cause biopsies for acute renal dysfunction. CTOT-08 patients diagnosed with SubAR on a surveillance biopsy also underwent a repeat biopsy at week 8, which was limited to one episode per patient.5 This study was originally approved by the institutional review board at each participating institution. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. The clinical and research activities being reported are consistent with the Principles of the Declaration of Istanbul as outlined in the “Declaration of Istanbul on Organ Trafficking and Transplant Tourism.”

Patients

Patients from the CTOT-08 study with biopsy-paired samples at the time of at least one biopsy (either surveillance or for-cause) were evaluated. Patients who had a biopsy showing BPAR and had another biopsy within 9 months (270 days) and had a biopsy-paired sample for GEP and/or dd-cfDNA for both biopsies were included in the analysis. We used the previously defined period of 9 months for the primary analysis, but also performed a subanalysis of repeat biopsies within 90 days to evaluate results within a time frame more likely to be clinically implemented for assessment of persistent rejection1,2 (Figure 1).

Study Objectives

The primary aim of this study was to determine the diagnostic performance of GEP and dd-cfDNA with subsequent biopsies after an initial rejection event.

GEP Assay

Blood samples for the GEP assay were drawn directly into PAXgene (BD BioSciences, San Jose, CA) tubes. The samples were processed using Affymetrix HT HG-U133+PM Array Plates on the Gene Titan MC instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) (GEO Accession No. GSE107509). The GEPs were analyzed with the TruGraf algorithm, a gene expression algorithm analyzing differential expression of 120 gene signatures that analyze a variety of both immunologic (majority) and nonimmunologic gene expression pathways. The TruGraf assay (Eurofins-Transplant Genomics Inc., Framingham, MA) has a previously defined probability threshold of 0.5 to differentiate the TX (Transplant eXcellence, no subclinical rejection) from the Not-TX (Not Transplant eXcellence, cannot confirm the absence of subclinical rejection).

dd-cfDNA Assay

Blood samples for the dd-cfDNA assay were drawn directly into BD Vacutainer PPT Plasma Preparation Tubes (BD BioSciences). The samples were processed using next-generation sequencing and recipient genotyping. Data were mapped to reference genomes and analyzed for percentage dd-cfDNA by a custom bioinformatics pipeline. The dd-cfDNA results were provided as a percentage of the donor-derived fraction as compared with total cfDNA by using panels of single-nucleotide polymorphisms to differentiate between donor and recipient cfDNA without requiring knowledge of donor genotypes. The Viracor TRAC assay (Eurofins-Transplant Genomics, Inc.) reports the fraction of dd-cfDNA as a percentage, with ≥0.7% considered positive.

Study Definitions

As a post hoc analysis, biopsy classifications as previously defined were used.6 Borderline rejection, TCMR, and AMR/suspicious for AMR were considered positive biopsies.9 TruGraf GEP with a 0.5 threshold was used for dichotomous results (TX [negative] versus Not-TX [positive]) while TRAC dd-cfDNA used a 0.7% threshold for dichotomous results (≥0.7% was classified as positive and <0.7% was classified as negative). Patients were considered clinically stable if they met the definition of the CTOT-08 study (serum creatine <2.3 mg/dl and <20% increase in creatinine compared with a minimum of two or three prior values). SubAR and cAR were defined as reported by investigators within the CTOT-08 trial, with SubAR being diagnosed on scheduled surveillance biopsies in clinically stable patients and cAR occurring on for-cause biopsies in patients with acute renal dysfunction.5

Statistical Analyses

Diagnostic performance characteristics were examined to determine the ability of both TruGraf GEP and TRAC dd-cfDNA to identify patients at highest risk of persistent rejection. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were used to evaluate diagnostic performance. For population-based diagnostic performance measures (PPV and NPV), prevalence reflected the dataset available. Kaplan–Meier method was used to examine the correlation between patients with persistent rejection with patient death or graft loss, as compared with patients without persistent rejection. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS/STAT software (SAS Studio) (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

A total of 64 index rejections (20 cAR and 44 subAR) in 58 patients with repeat biopsies were identified for the analysis (Figure 1). Patient demographics are reported in Table 1. Index biopsies occurred at a median of 233 days after transplant. The reasons for biopsies were surveillance (44 [69%]) and clinically indicated (20 [31%]); all the surveillance biopsies were confirmed to be performed in clinically stable patients by CTOT investigators (Table 2). The index rejections showed AMR or mixed rejection in 53% (34/64). Of the 30 biopsies that demonstrated TCMR, 23 were borderline, 2 had grade 1A, 2 had grade 1B, and 3 had grade 2A rejection (Table 2). Out of the entire cohort, 61% (39/64) of patients had persistent rejection within 9 months, with a median time to repeat biopsy of 100 days (63–245), with no significant differences in days to rebiopsy between those who had persistent rejection versus those who did not (P = 0.648). The reasons for the follow-up biopsies are reported in Table 3. Surveillance biopsies or scheduled follow-up biopsies after treatment of SubAR were performed in 44 patients (69%), demonstrating a 57% persistent positivity rate, and clinically indicated biopsies were performed in 20 patients (31%), demonstrating a 70% persistent positivity rate (Table 3). All 44 of the surveillance biopsies were determined to have been performed in clinically stable patients by CTOT investigators. Among the follow-up biopsies, 25 (39%) demonstrated normal biopsies, 11 (17%) showed borderline rejection, 3 (5%) had TCMR grade ≥1A, 12 (19%) had AMR, and 13 (20%) had mixed AMR and TCMR (Table 3). Biomarker test characteristics for follow-up biopsies within 9 months and a subanalysis of those with follow-up biopsies within 90 days are presented in Tables 4 and 5, respectively. For the primary analysis of biopsies within 9 months, peripheral blood GEP demonstrated a sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 59%, 76%, 79%, and 54%, respectively. Results are also reported dividing clinically stable patients from those who underwent a clinically indicated biopsy to best identify where biomarkers could have utility. In clinically stable patients (n=44), the PPV and NPV were 76% and 56%, respectively. For dd-cfDNA performance compared with biopsies in the same time frame, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 62%, 86%, 88%, and 56%, respectively. Among clinically stable biopsy-paired dd-cfDNA, PPV and NPV were 94% and 64% (Table 4). When evaluating the GEP and dd-cfDNA results together (n=58 biopsies with both results), of the 36 persistent rejections within 9 months of follow-up, both tests were positive in 14 of the rejections and at least one test was positive in 29 (81%) of the rejections, while both tests were negative in 15 of the 22 biopsies that did not show rejection (68%). This means that if both tests were evaluated, they would have an accurate call 76% of the time and would miscategorize seven rejections and seven biopsies without rejection (data not shown). Interestingly, the tests had differing results on the basis of the type of rejection on the follow-up biopsies (Figure 2). This shows that GEP was more likely to be positive in biopsies that had borderline/TCMR, whereas dd-cfDNA was more likely to identify AMR and mixed rejection.

In the subanalysis of biopsies performed within 90 days of the index rejection, GEP had a PPV of 83% and NPV of 53%, whereas dd-cfDNA had a PPV of 89% and NPV of 50%. Among the clinically stable patients, GEP had a PPV of 100% and NPV of 60%, whereas dd-cfDNA had a PPV of 100% and NPV of 56% (Table 5). On focusing on the clinically stable population, where there would be no clinical indication for rejection, each test was positive for seven biopsies in which rejection was present. They each missed four rejections, but both correctly classified all negative biopsies accurately (neither GEP nor dd-cfDNA had any false positives). This would represent seven rejections identified that would not typically be identified in normal clinical practice, without any negative biopsies that would have been performed because of a positive biomarker. To better visualize the changes in biomarkers over time between biopsies, 29 index biopsies were identified that had multiple tests between it and the repeat biopsy as part of the intensive monitoring requirement in the CTOT-08 study. On average, these biopsies had biomarker results on days 14, 30, and 45 after the index rejection and then had biomarkers paired with a repeat biopsy on average at 66 days (range 52–100). Of those 29 repeat biopsies, 18 (62%) had persistent rejection and 11 (38%) had resolved rejection. The median and 25% and 75% quartiles of both the GEP relative score and dd-cfDNA percent at each time point are represented graphically in Figures 3 and 4. In Figure 3, the threshold for positivity is referenced at a score of 50 and 0.7%, respectively, for GEP and dd-cfDNA. These data show that both GEP score and dd-cfDNA percentage decrease at the time intervals between biopsies after treatment of the rejection. In both GEP and dd-cfDNA, the absolute values decrease after treatment of rejection and then separate on the basis of persistent versus resolved rejection. 50% (9/18) of the GEP scores and 35% (6/17) of the dd-cfDNA results are above the positive threshold by the time of the second biopsy in those with persistent rejection, whereas 73% (8/11) GEP results and 90% (9/10) dd-cfDNA results stay below the positivity threshold in those with resolved rejection (Figure 3).

Finally, we collected data on the long-term outcomes for the cohort in 56 of the 58 patients, while the remaining two were lost to follow-up. The overall months (median, 25%–75%) to death, graft loss, or last follow-up for the cohort with persistent rejection was 66.6 (41.1–81.9) months and 73.4 (68.6–89.7) months for the cohort with resolved rejection. There were nine graft losses (n=3 with death, 6 with death-censored graft loss) in the 31 patients with persistent rejection (29%) and four graft losses (n=2 with death, 2 with death-censored graft loss) in the 25 patients with resolved rejection (16%). In a Kaplan–Meier analysis of graft survival (Figure 4), there were no differences in graft survival in the group with persistent rejection (log-rank P = 0.17).

Discussion

With the increasing interest in recurrent and persistent rejection and its effect on allograft survival, this analysis demonstrates a potentially important utility for blood-based biomarkers that are considered rule-out tests in other contexts of use. Because of the risks associated, invasive biopsies are not commonly used after treated rejection unless there are clinical indications of unsuccessful rejection resolution. As demonstrated by Rampersad and colleagues, a re-evaluation of this practice is pertinent to efforts to prolong allograft survival.1 To identify patients most efficiently in need of a repeat biopsy, noninvasive biomarkers could have clinical utility and reduce unnecessary risks in patients with successfully treated BPAR.

Both blood GEP and dd-cfDNA demonstrate excellent capability of identifying patients with persistent rejection after treatment in this small cohort with robust follow-up data. This is especially true for patients who were clinically stable and had biopsies within 90 days of the initial rejection. In this situation, the clinical scenario would not have identified these patients for rebiopsy, and so the high PPV would help identify patients in need of a biopsy who would otherwise be missed. The sensitivity and specificity of the tests are like other studies in the context of surveillance biopsies,5,6,10 representing the effect of prevalence on the population performance characteristics (61% prevalence of persistent rejection versus 25% prevalence of subclinical rejection in a screening population). For example, in previous analyses in the stable kidney transplant population, the TruGraf blood GEP demonstrated a sensitivity range of 38%–43% and specificity range of 78%–85%, both of which fall within the 95% confidence range of our results presented here. This corresponded to a PPV range of 35%–47% and NPV range of 81%–82% with prevalence of SubAR at 25%.6,10 The TRAC dd-cfDNA was validated in clinically indicated biopsies (not including borderline rejection) with a sensitivity of 57% and specificity of 84.5%. Within the clinically stable population, the dd-cfDNA had a sensitivity of 47% and specificity of 88%, both of which fall within the 95% confidence range of our results presented here. This corresponded to a PPV of 56% and NPV of 84% with prevalence of SubAR at 25%.6 Our analysis demonstrates the importance of the prevalence of events on population performance characteristics (PPV and NPV) even when test performance characteristics (sensitivity and specificity) are stable and highlights an important consideration when using available biomarkers in clinical scenarios that may differ from that in which it was initially designed. This provides important implications on what information the tests provide in specific clinical scenarios.

Although there have been one previous case series and one review of observational data from the Circulating Donor-Derived CellFree DNA in Blood for Diagnosing Acute Rejection in Kidney Transplant Recipients study that have documented a decrease in dd-cfDNA after the treatment of acute rejection, neither of those reports included follow-up biopsies to confirm a histologic response.11,12 Our analysis is the first report that carefully correlates the response of peripheral blood biomarkers with follow-up allograft biopsies. It is noteworthy that the frequency of persistent rejection in this prospective cohort remained high at 61%, and it is unknown how many of the patients in these reports had persistent rejection that was undiagnosed.

An additional important finding in the study is the different subtypes of rejections that each biomarker identified. Although the performance characteristics were nearly identical, interrogation of the individual biomarker results paired with each biopsy identified differences in the types of rejections that each biomarker was sensitive to. The overlap of biomarker results further supports that the two orthogonal technologies may identify different classes of rejection, with the GEP more sensitive to borderline/TCMR and dd-cfDNA more sensitive to AMR/mixed rejections.

Our study has several strengths and limitations. Strengths of the analysis include the availability of over 5 years of clinical follow-up as well as detailed clinical and treatment data that can only be observed in well-performed prospective clinical trials, such as CTOT-08. The availability of intensive monitoring time points of the biomarkers between index biopsies showing rejection and follow-up biopsies are limited and valuable for the analysis of the clinical utility of using peripheral blood biomarkers to monitor the outcome of acute rejection. In addition, all biopsy interpretations were performed centrally. Our study also has limitations. First, for the analysis of graft survival, the cohort size was small and underpowered to allow for a valid statistical analysis. In addition, the number of end points was too small to allow for adjusting for time to rejection in the model. However, with these limited data, the follow-up on the persistent rejection cohort does suggest inferior graft survival (Figure 4). In addition, it is a post hoc analysis of a clinical trial, and so was not designed to answer the clinical question under evaluation; therefore, all data generated in these analyses should be viewed as hypothesis generating. However, because the CTOT-08 trial was designed to evaluate the prevalence and clinical consequences of subclinical rejection, including failure of treatment to resolve rejections, it had necessary design elements to help provide more data on persistent rejection. In addition, follow-up biopsies were not performed after all rejections, and not all biopsies had paired biomarker samples, limiting our analysis to a subset of the total CTOT patient cohort. Finally, as an observational study, the biomarker results were not available to clinicians during the conduct of the study, so clinical utility of using the biomarkers in decision making cannot be determined.

Overall, this analysis presents a potentially useful clinical setting of use for GEP and dd-cfDNA. Especially relevant are the biomarker performances in the context of clinically stable patients after treatment of rejection. This is the first analysis of molecular biomarkers as noninvasive identifiers of persistent rejection that includes follow-up biopsies; however, the cohort size is small and retrospective. Therefore, further follow-up and analysis is necessary to confirm our findings.
