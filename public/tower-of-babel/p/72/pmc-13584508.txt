Can family health alleviate income-related health disparity in China? A population-based study
Authors: Haomiao Li, Jinyuan Hu, Qiang Yao, Yibo Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamily health is an important variable that links internal and external resources at the family level, exerting significant effects on individual health. However, there remains a lack of empirical evidence regarding its potential to mitigate health disparities. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore the effect of family health on income-related health disparity in China and analyze the heterogeneous effects of different dimensions of family health across different sub-populations.MethodsUsing nationally representative data from the 2023–2024 Psychology and Behavior Investigation of Chinese Residents [n = 49,885], we employed health-related quality of life (HRQoL) as a proxy variable for individual health and applied the recentered influence function-index-ordinary least squares model to explore the contributions of family health and its four dimensions to the disparity in HRQoL.ResultsOur findings showed that family health had a significantly negative association with the concentration index of health utility index (UI; β = -0.0002, P < 0.05) and EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS; β = -0.0006, P < 0.01). Family social or emotional support was negatively associated with EQ-VAS disparity, and family external social support significantly contributed to lower UI disparity. However, family health resources were positively associated with the disparity in UI and EQ-VAS. Heterogeneity analysis revealed more pronounced effects of family health in mitigating health disparity among females, young populations, and urban residents.ConclusionsThis study emphasizes the importance of alleviating health disparities through family health improvement. In addition, the study findings demonstrate the necessity of reshaping resource allocation at the family level—balancing external supply and internal equity of families among different subgroups—and accelerating the distribution of high-quality medical resources to economically disadvantaged areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-02000-3.Keywords: Family health, Health-related quality of life, Disparity, Recentered influence function-index-ordinary least squares model

Background

Health disparity is the main goal of health system development and an important measure of social civilization [1, 2]. In China, health disparities among different socioeconomic groups presents a dynamic evolution feature, posing challenges to social equity and sustainable development [3]. While the family is the basic social unit exerting lifelong impacts on individual health [4], the potential of “family health” to bridge these SES-related health gaps remains underexplored.

Family health refers to “a resource at the family unit level, shaped by the interplay of each member’s abilities, behaviors, personalities, and interactions, as well as the diverse physiological, social, emotional, economic, and medical resources available to the family, rather than merely the sum of individual health” [5]. Domestic and international studies have investigated the concept, measurement, and effects of family health, validating the reliability of related scales [6–8]. As a crucial unit in health governance, healthy families can improve individual health by optimizing external health resource provisions and social support systems, as well as transmitting health-conscious values and cultivating healthy behaviors within the household [9].

However, most studies on the effects of family health have focused on its direct impact on individual health; a comprehensive explanatory framework of its role in health disparities at the societal structural level remains underdeveloped. This gap in the literature motivates our central research question: does family health bridge the health gap between different socioeconomic groups, and does its effectiveness vary across population characteristics? Particularly in China, where family characteristics exhibit unique patterns, investigating the relationship between family health and health disparity is warranted. Therefore, this study utilized data from a nationally representative cross-sectional survey to explore the association between family health and population health disparity.

Extensive research has established a strong, consistent association between socioeconomic status (income, education, occupation) and individual health [10, 11]. According to Fundamental Cause Theory (FCT), socioeconomic status (SES) is a “fundamental cause” of health disparities because it provides access to flexible resources—money, knowledge, power, and social connections—that higher-SES individuals can deploy to protect their health regardless of changing disease mechanisms [12, 13]. The Health Social Determinants Framework further underscores the role of SES in interacting with other factors to shape health outcomes [14]. The family, as the central domain of an individual’s life, holds a significant position in this model. Family health integrates fragmented population-level health-related concepts, more precisely reflecting overall family behavior and emotional dynamics rooted in internal and external resource access and social support [9]. Thus, it is highly likely to affect individual health and health disparities.

While income groups differ significantly in medical resource access, health knowledge, living environments, and life stress [15–18], the family, as the core life environment, shapes health for all individuals. High-income families leverage economic resources to provide quality medical services and healthy living conditions [15, 17], while low-income families exert positive health impacts through close relationships and effective health knowledge communication [19, 20].

Therefore, we proposed hypothesis H1:

H1: Among people with different income levels, family health exerts a significant positive effect on individual health levels.

Within the FCT and the social determinants of health framework, family health functions as a buffering resource that serves to attenuate the income gradient in health. This buffering effect is further underpinned by social capital theory and resource substitution theory. Family bonding capital (strong internal relationships) offers emotional support and behavioral norms, alleviating stress-related health risks and guiding healthy behaviors [19, 21, 22]. Bridging capital (external weak ties) expands resource access, including health information and medical services [23]. This dual capital empowerment helps low-income groups mitigate material resource shortages and optimize medical service utilization. Resource substitution theory further suggests that market/institutional health resource scarcity amplifies the marginal utility of non-market resources like family support. Thus, we proposed hypothesis H2:

H2: Family health is negatively associated with income-related health disparities.

Additionally, family health may alleviate income-related health disparities through its four key dimensions. As a form of social capital, close family emotional support enhances psychological resilience in low-income individuals, helping them cope with economic hardships [18]. Healthy lifestyles at the family level — such as shared meal planning and use of public spaces for exercise—can partly offset limited financial resources and reduce disease risk [24, 25]. Drawing on resource substitution theory, family health resources (e.g., medical access, health knowledge) likely provide stronger compensatory health benefits for resource-scarce low-income groups [26, 27]. Furthermore, expanded external social support networks offer additional resource channels for disadvantaged families, yielding greater marginal health gains [23]. Accordingly, we propose that family social/emotional support (H3), healthy lifestyles (H4), health resources (H5), and external social support (H6) each mitigate health disparities across income groups. Methods

Data source

Data for this study were derived from the 2023&2024 Psychology and Behavior Investigation of Chinese Residents, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey. It uses a combined sampling method of probability proportionate to population size, multistage sampling, and quota sampling, covering wide geographical areas, diverse social groups, and comprehensive variables to support in-depth empirical research. Further methodological details are published elsewhere [28, 29].

After quota adjustments, the 2023 and 2024 samples consisted of 30,054 and 25,047 respondents, respectively. To account for macro-level social, economic, and policy influences, only samples from mainland China were retained. In addition, 4,638 cases were excluded due to missing key variables or irregularities in responses to the core explanatory variable. Therefore, a final analytical sample of 49,885 cases was included in the statistical analysis. The sample selection process is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Measures

Income-related health disparity

Individual health was measured by health-related quality of life (HRQoL) using the EQ-5D-5 L instrument, which yields a health utility index (UI) based on a Chinese value set and a Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) [30, 31]. Household per capita monthly income served as the socioeconomic status indicator. For analysis, income was categorized into deciles and, for subgroup comparisons, into low- (≤ 5000 yuan) and high-income (> 5000 yuan) groups, with the 5,000 RMB threshold chosen according to the sample distribution and China’s socioeconomic conditions. Income-related health disparity was quantified using the Concentration Index (CI). A positive CI indicates pro-rich health inequality, while a negative CI indicates pro-poor inequality, with larger absolute values reflecting greater disparity [32].

Family health

Family health, serving as the core explanatory variable in this study, was measured using the Chinese version of the Family Health Scale-Short Form (FHS-SF) [33]. Wang et al. tested the reliability of the FHS-SF to develop the Chinese version and provide a quantitative tool for the assessment of family health problems in China [7]. This form includes four dimensions: family/social/emotional health processes, family healthy lifestyle, family health resources, and family external social support. The items are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Wang et al. assessed the Cronbach’s alpha of the Chinese version of the FHS-SF was 0.83, with the Cronbach’s alphas of the four subscales ranging from 0.70 to 0.90. In the present study, the FHS-SF exhibited good internal consistency for the total scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.828), with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.872, 0.829, 0.664 and 0.719 for the four subdimensions, respectively. All four subdimensions were included as separate independent variables in the subsequent analysis to quantify their heterogeneous effects on income-related health disparity.

Covariates

Based on social determinants of health and previous studies, covariates related to individual, family, and social characteristics were included in the analysis, including age, gender, educational level, occupational status, self-efficiency, chronic diseases, health literacy, smoking behavior, alcohol intake, physical exercises, family type, marital status, residential area, hukou status, regions, public health insurance coverage, social support, and self-assessed family socioeconomic status. The definitions or group details are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA 17.0 (α = 0.10). For descriptive analysis, numbers and percentages were used to describe the distribution of different demographic groups, and ‘mean ± standard error’ was used to describe the characteristics within different income groups. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression was used to analyze the effects of family health and its four dimensions on individuals’ HRQoL.

The recentered influence function-index-ordinary least squares (RIF-I-OLS) model was used to analyze the marginal effects of the family health on income-related health disparity. The RIF-I-OLS method uses recentered influence function (RIF) estimates of the disparity index to determine the relationship between the RIF and the explanatory variables and establish the regression function between the disparity index and the explanatory variables to achieve causal recognition [34, 35]. The method is primarily divided into two steps. The first is to estimate the RIF value of the target concentration index, and the second is to use the RIF estimation value as the explanatory variable and various factors X as explanatory variables to implement the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression process. Robustness test

If income-related health disparity can be mitigated by family health, family health may serve as a potential negative moderator in the income-health linkage, thereby attenuating this association. Therefore, we incorporated the interaction term of family health and income into the OLS model, which assessed the effect of income on HRQoL, controlling for all covariates. To address potential multicollinearity, the independent and moderating variables were first centered [36].

Heterogeneity analysis

First, as a fundamental unit of health governance, families involve distinct roles across generations and sexes: males typically provide economic support, females take on caregiving responsibilities, and older members rely more on family support due to physical decline [37]. These role differences lead to varying benefits from family health. Additionally, China’s dual urban-rural structure creates gaps in living conditions, education, and healthcare resources between urban and rural residents. Second, health disparities have multidimensional drivers. Biological differences across age and sex groups result in distinct health needs, while urban-rural disparities are primarily socioeconomic, leading to structural health inequalities [38]. Treating family health as a one-size-fits-all solution may mask its actual effects on vulnerable populations. We thus analyzed heterogeneity by sex, age, and urban-rural residence to explore family health’s differential impacts on health disparities across subgroups.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Sample characteristics stratified by income are shown in Supplementary Table S3. High-income individuals reported significantly higher HRQoL (both utility index [UI] and EQ-VAS) and better scores across all family health dimensions compared to low-income individuals (all P < 0.001). Significant differences were also observed in other individual, family, and social covariates. The characteristics of the study participants across different survey phases were further presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Association between family health and HRQoL

The OLS regression results indicated that family health had a significant positive effect on individual health levels (Supplementary Table S5). Regression analyses conducted separately for low-income and high-income populations revealed that family health had a significant positive effect on individual health in both groups, thus validating H1 (Table 1). A one-unit increase in family health was associated with a 0.004-unit increase in UI and a 0.881-unit increase in EQ-VAS for low-income individuals, whereas high-income individuals experienced a 0.004-unit increase in UI and a 0.760-unit increase in EQ-VAS. Most family health dimensions positively affected HRQoL in both groups. Notably, family external social support had a significant positive effect on low-income individuals but only showed a marginal effect on UI (β = 0.002, P < 0.01) for high-income individuals, with no significant effect on EQ-VAS. Notably, the magnitude of the association between family health and EQ-VAS was larger for low-income individuals (β = 0.882) than for their high-income counterparts (β = 0.761), suggesting that family health may provide compensatory benefits for those with fewer economic resources.

Using the RIF-I-OLS decomposition method, we examined the contribution of family health to health disparity (Table 3). Family health was significantly negatively associated with the CIs of UI (β = -0.0002, P < 0.05) and EQ-VAS (β = -0.0006, P < 0.01), with coefficients opposite in sign to the RIF-CI values. This suggests that family health plays a mitigating role in reducing HRQoL disparity, thus validating H2. Among control variables, higher educational attainment, retirement, freelance employment, non-smoking, and moderate/high physical activity were associated with reduced health disparity (negative coefficients), with nuclear and backbone family types additionally linked to lower EQ-VAS disparity. In contrast, older age, unemployment, divorce/widowhood, intergenerational family type, western regional residence, public health insurance coverage, and chronic conditions (especially ≥ 2 conditions) were associated with increased health disparity (positive coefficients); unmarried status was also correlated with greater EQ-VAS disparity. These findings are broadly consistent with previous research among the Chinese population [31].

Heterogeneity analysis

The distribution of family health scores and HRQoL across subgroups are presented in Supplementary Table S6 and Table S7, respectively. The CIs of UI and EQ-VAS among different subgroups indicated that high-income individuals tended to have better HRQoL (Supplementary Table S8). Subgroup analyses revealed differential effects of family health on individual health disparities.(Fig. 3). Its mitigating effect was more pronounced among women, young populations, and urban residents. For men, older adults, and rural residents, the effects were marginal or non-significant.

Heterogeneity analysis of the association between family health and health disparity across different sexes, ages, and residential areas

Analysis of the four dimensions showed further variation across subgroups (Supplementary Tables S9–S11). By gender, family external social support was significantly linked to lower UI disparity in females (β = -0.0014, P < 0.01) and marginally reduced EQ-VAS disparity in males (β = -0.0014, P < 0.1), while family healthy lifestyle correlated with lower EQ-VAS disparity in females (β = -0.0023, P < 0.1); notably, family health resources were significantly associated with increased UI (β = 0.0005, P < 0.1) and EQ-VAS (β = 0.0008, P < 0.05) disparity in males with no significant female effects. By age, young individuals showed the most consistent benefits, with family social/emotional health processes and external social support both significantly reducing UI and EQ-VAS disparity (all P < 0.05); middle-aged adults had family health resources linked to higher EQ-VAS disparity (β = 0.0008, P < 0.05), and older adults exhibited a positive association between external social support and EQ-VAS disparity (β = 0.0037, P < 0.05). For urban-rural residence, urban residents saw significant benefits: family social/emotional health processes reduced UI (β = -0.0009, P < 0.1) and EQ-VAS (β = -0.0020, P < 0.01) disparity, and external social support lowered UI disparity (β = -0.0011, P < 0.01), whereas no family health sub-dimension significantly mitigated health disparity among rural residents.

Discussion

Utilizing nationally representative data, this study examined the role of family health in mitigating income-related health disparities in China. Key findings and their implications are discussed below.

Family health significantly mitigates income-related health disparity

The results confirmed a significantly negative association between family health and income-related HRQoL disparity, indicating that family health exerts a negative contribution to income-related health inequality (i.e., mitigating the pro-rich disparity to a certain extent). From the RIF decomposition results, a one-point increase in the family health score was associated with a 2.5% relative reduction in UI disparity and a 3.1% relative reduction in EQ-VAS disparity. A substantive improvement in family health (a one-standard-deviation increase of 6.17 points in the score) would expand the relative reductions in health disparity to 15%-20%, which would represent a marked mitigation of the health gap between high- and low-income groups. Additionally, the improvement of family health is also a crucial factor in enhancing individuals’ health-related quality of life.

Social and emotional support among family members, as well as access to external social resources, may be associated with improved physical and mental health status, and thus may contribute to reduced health disparities due to income disparities [9]. Besides, families may indirectly be associated with enhanced health of their members by fostering a healthy environment and cultivating shared healthy lifestyle habits, such as balanced diets and regular exercise [21]. However, in the present study, the dimension of healthy lifestyle did not exhibit a significant association with health disparity. This null finding may stem from limitations in the self-reported lifestyle measures available in our dataset, or it may indicate that the translation of a shared family environment for healthy living into actual individual health outcomes is more complex and heterogeneous than our model could capture. Notably, the impact of external social support on HRQoL in the high-income group was not significant, possibly because high-income individuals rely more on internal family resources, making external social support for their health insignificant. However, for low-income individuals, external social support may serve as a resource substitute, compensating for the lack of internal family health resources [27].

The counterintuitive finding that family health resources widen rather than narrow health disparities can be explained by Fundamental Cause Theory (FCT). As Phelan et al. (2010) argue, health inequalities persist because higher-SES groups are better positioned to utilize new health-promoting resources as they become available. In our study, family health resources—as flexible material resources—are more effectively mobilized by higher-income households. Individual health outcomes are highly sensitive to material resources [39], family health resources, closely linked to household income, directly contribute to divergent health trajectories: high-income families convert economic advantages into high-quality medical services, private healthcare, and professional health knowledge [40], thereby rapidly improving their health status; low-income families, facing material scarcity, cannot rely on non-material family health factors (e.g., social-emotional support) to compensate for this deficiency. This illustrates a distinct Matthew effect [41]—economic privilege translates into unequal access to family health resources, amplifying the income-health gradient. Extending FCT to the family level [42], our findings reveal that family health resources, when tied to material wealth, function as a mechanism for sustaining socioeconomic health disparities rather than as a compensatory resource for low-income groups as hypothesized (H5).

Impact of family health on health disparity exhibits group heterogeneity

The mitigating effect of family health on disparities was more pronounced among women, younger populations, and urban residents. In traditional social divisions of labor, women tend to devote more effort to domestic responsibilities, making their health more susceptible to family environment and support [37]. Moreover, women are more inclined toward long-term health investments, whereas men exhibit lower willingness for such investments due to unhealthy lifestyle habits [43]. Consequently, family health lifestyles play a notable role in alleviating health disparity among women. Men, typically serving as primary breadwinners, rely more on family resource allocation for health maintenance. In high-income households, men can mitigate health risks through comprehensive occupational protections, corporate health benefits, and high-quality medical services. In contrast, low-income groups face greater exposure to hazardous working conditions, increasing their vulnerability to health deterioration [44, 45]. Beyond these behavioral and occupational differences, the gendered division of health labor within families may further explain why women benefit more from family health. Women often serve as the primary health managers for their households—scheduling medical appointments, monitoring family members’ health conditions, and disseminating health information [46]. This caregiving role makes women more responsive to family-level health interventions and more likely to translate family health resources into tangible health improvements for themselves. In contrast, men’s health tends to be more contingent on material resources rather than relational or emotional support, which may explain why family health resources paradoxically widen disparities among men [43].

Disparities in the role of family health and its subdimensions across age groups may reflect intrahousehold resource allocation bias. Family status determines the share of household resources for each member, with healthcare investments often favoring individuals possessing higher income potential and greater human capital investment [47]. As a result, younger individuals generally receive more familial support, whereas older adults are more likely to experience deficits in both emotional and material support. Middle-aged adults primarily function as providers rather than recipients of family support, with both financial and emotional resources being directed toward children and elderly parents [48]. Consequently, the health needs of middle-aged adults are often overlooked. The negative effects of external social support for older individuals result from the interplay between cumulative disadvantage and social network rigidity. Socioeconomic disparities throughout an individual’s life lead to divergent resource acquisition capabilities, which become entrenched in old age due to physiological decline and shifting social roles [49]. Low-income older individuals, constrained by limited social networks and financial scarcity, must rely on basic public services, resulting in markedly inferior quality and intensity of social support, which exacerbates health disparities [50, 51]. Taken together, these age-specific patterns suggest that family health operates through distinct mechanisms across the life course: for the young, it provides emotional and social capital that buffers income shocks; for middle-aged adults, material resources become dominant but are inequitably distributed; and for older adults, external support may inadvertently reinforce existing socioeconomic gradients due to cumulative disadvantage.

In rural populations, the association between family health and reduced health disparity was generally insignificant. Several mechanisms may explain this null finding. First, rapid urbanization has led to massive out-migration of working-age adults from rural areas, leaving behind a predominantly elderly and left-behind population whose family structures are often fragmented [52]. In such contexts, the traditional family unit may no longer function as a cohesive source of health support, diluting the potential buffering effect of family health. Second, rural areas are characterized by a predominance of bonding social capital (strong ties within homogeneous networks) rather than bridging social capital (weak ties that connect to external resources) [53]. While bonding capital provides emotional support, it rarely offers the information, services, or material resources needed to overcome income-related health barriers. In contrast, urban residents have access to more diverse social networks that can mobilize external resources, making family health more effective in mitigating disparities. Third, the scarcity of formal healthcare infrastructure in rural areas, including fewer hospitals, longer travel distances, and lower availability of specialist care, means that even when families possess health resources or social support, they cannot easily translate these into improved health outcomes [54]. This structural constraint is compounded by lower public health system efficiency, which at the macro level has been shown to disproportionately disadvantage less affluent regions [55]. This combined deficit may overwhelm any potential compensatory role of family health. Finally, the homogeneity of socioeconomic status in many rural communities reduces the observable variation in both income and family health, making it statistically more difficult to detect a moderating effect. Collectively, these factors suggest that in rural China, improving family health alone is unlikely to reduce health disparities without concurrent investments in public health infrastructure and economic development.

Policy recommendations

To advance health equity, policies should address disparities exacerbated by unequal family health resources. This includes directing high-quality medical resources to underserved areas, ensuring comprehensive family doctor coverage for vulnerable groups, and establishing dynamic support mechanisms such as health subsidies for low-income families. Efforts should also promote intra-family equity by reducing gender- and age-based biases in resource distribution. Furthermore, given the demonstrated role of social support and healthy lifestyles in mitigating disparities, public health initiatives should prioritize non-material interventions, disseminating health knowledge, fostering community-based mutual aid networks, developing formal institutionalized support such as telecare [56], and leveraging cultural values to strengthen shared family health responsibility and intrinsic motivation for healthy behaviors. The government should integrate family health into the national health strategy, while communities can implement dynamic health monitoring and personalized support. Empowering families with health management tools and knowledge remains essential for sustaining long-term outcomes.

Research limitations

Despite in-depth exploration of family health’s impact on health disparity, this study has several limitations. First, data limitations include the absence of key variables on family and individual health. Family health was measured with a short scale, which overlooks micro-level family interactions like emotional communication and health behavior modeling—factors critical to understanding how family health shapes health disparity. Second, the analysis may not fully control for all confounding factors, leading to potential omitted variable bias. Cross-sectional data also prevents exploration of long-term dynamics in the family health-health disparity relationship and establishment of complex causal links. Furthermore, given the cross-sectional design, we cannot rule out potential reverse causality, which means that individuals with better health may also have higher income, which could influence family health dynamics. Third, socio-cultural influences are not sufficiently addressed. Mechanisms through which family values and health perceptions affect resource allocation and health behaviors across cultural contexts remain understudied, requiring an interdisciplinary analytical framework.

Conclusions

Family-centered health promotion strategies can help mitigate income-related health disparities. Using nationally representative data, this study is the first to demonstrate the relationship between family health and health disparities, suggesting that family health is associated with reduced health disparities. However, it found that certain sub-dimensions of family health are associated with wider health disparities. The impact of family health and its sub-dimensions on health disparities varies across different age groups, sexes, and residential areas. These findings provide a more precise guidance for family health development in China. We recommend integrating the efforts of the government, healthcare systems, and the society to construct a collaborative family health system. This system should promote fairness in resource allocation at the macro level, improve family health services at the meso level, and foster a positive family environment, healthy family lifestyles, and mutual support among families at the micro level.
