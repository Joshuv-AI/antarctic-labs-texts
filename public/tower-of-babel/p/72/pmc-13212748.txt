Tim-3 agonist restrains ILC2 function and attenuates airway hyperreactivity via NLK pathway
Authors: Yoshihiro Sakano, Kei Sakano, Kota Kokubo, Benjamin P. Hurrell, Stephen Shen, Xin Li, Gowri Yeliyur Shivakumara Swamy, Jafar Cain, Vijay K. Kuchroo, Omid Akbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Allergic asthma is promoted by type 2 inflammation involving cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, with group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) playing a key pathogenic role. Here, we identify T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (Tim-3) as a negative regulator of ILC2 function. Tim-3 expression is upregulated in activated pulmonary ILC2s, and engagement with Tim-3 agonists inhibits ILC2 activation, proliferation, and type 2 cytokine production via the Nemo Like Kinase (NLK) signaling pathway and suppression of mitochondrial metabolism. In vivo, Tim-3 agonists alleviate airway hyperreactivity (AHR) and inflammation in both IL-33- and Alternaria alternata-induced AHR models, while ILC2-specific Tim-3 deletion exacerbates AHR. These results are confirmed in human ILC2s and humanized mice, supporting the translational relevance. Our findings establish Tim-3 as an inhibitory checkpoint for ILC2s and suggest its potential as a therapeutic target in allergic asthma and other ILC2-mediated diseases.

Introduction

Allergic asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways primarily driven by type 2 cytokines such as interleukin (IL)−4, IL-5, and IL-13, which are produced by T helper 2 (Th2) cells and group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s)1,2. The global prevalence of asthma remains high, with increasing incidence particularly among children, adolescents, women, and the elderly. While mortality rates and disability-adjusted life years are declining in high-income countries, they are rising in low- and middle-income countries, and asthma remains the second leading cause of death among chronic respiratory diseases3. Severe asthma symptoms affect one-third to one-half of patients, significantly impairing quality of life and underscoring the need for improved treatment strategies4–6. In this context, novel therapies targeting ILC2s, which are central to the pathogenesis of severe asthma, have emerged as promising approaches.

ILC2s are pivotal immune cells involved in the initiation and persistence of type 2 airway inflammation. Upon stimulation by epithelial cytokines such as IL-33 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin, which are released in response to allergens or viral infections, ILC2s become activated and secrete large amounts of cytokines, including IL-5 and IL-137. This cascade promotes eosinophilic infiltration, mucus hypersecretion, and airway hyperreactivity (AHR). Notably, in severe and corticosteroid-resistant asthma, ILC2s often drive inflammation more potently than conventional Th2 cells, highlighting their critical role in disease chronicity8–10. Accordingly, recent studies have focused on molecules that regulate ILC2 activity, including immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif and immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif-containing immune checkpoint receptors11–13.

Immune checkpoint receptors are molecules that regulate immune responses and have attracted significant attention in cancer immunotherapy; more recently, targeting these regulatory mechanisms has emerged as a promising strategy for autoimmune and allergic diseases14. Among these checkpoint receptors, T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (Tim-3) is a negative regulator of immune activation, expressed on a variety of immune cells, including T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells15,16. Tim-3 modulates immune responses by binding to multiple ligands, leading to inhibition of T cell activation and promotion of immune tolerance16. In inflammatory disease models, Tim-3 has been shown to regulate Th1-mediated responses and suppress granulocyte activity17,18. However, its role in allergic asthma is unclear, and the function of Tim-3 in ILC2 biology remains poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated the function of Tim-3 on pulmonary ILC2s in the context of allergic asthma. We find that Tim-3 is expressed on ILC2s at both the transcript and protein levels, and that activation of Tim-3 with a specific agonist suppresses ILC2 activation and cytokine production. Mechanistically, Tim-3 modulates ILC2 function via the Nemo-Like Kinase (NLK) signaling pathway and regulates mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. In vivo, administration of a Tim-3 agonist ameliorates AHR in both IL-33- and Alternaria alternata-induced AHR models, whereas ILC2-specific Tim-3 knockout mice exhibit exacerbated AHR. Importantly, our findings were validated in human ILC2s, in which Tim-3 activation similarly suppresses type 2 cytokine responses and AHR in a humanized mouse model. Together, these results identify Tim-3 as an inhibitory checkpoint on ILC2s and suggest that targeting Tim-3 may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for asthma and other ILC2-mediated diseases.

Results

Tim-3 is upregulated in activated ILC2s, and Tim-3 agonist suppresses ILC2 effector function

To evaluate Tim-3-encoding gene Havcr2 expression in pulmonary ILC2s under both steady-state and activated conditions, we analyzed the single-cell transcriptome of a pure population of FACS-sorted ILC2 isolated from the lungs of mice treated intranasally with PBS or IL-33 and examined the expression of Havcr2, which encodes Tim-319. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) analysis, visualized using UMAP, revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles between naïve (PBS-treated) and IL-33-activated ILC2s (Fig. 1A, left), with IL-33 stimulation inducing a unique population of Havcr2-expressing ILC2s (Fig. 1A, right). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the number of cells expressing Havcr2 mRNA was significantly increased in activated ILC2 compared to naive cells (Fig. 1B). Upregulation of Tim-3 expression was closely associated with increased expression of the cytokines Il5 and Il13, key drivers of AHR20,21 (Fig. 1C). Tim-3 expression (encoded by Havcr2) on ILC2s was significantly upregulated following IL-33 treatment in vitro compared to PBS (Fig. 1F). Flow cytometry further validated a marked increase in Tim-3 protein on ILC2s in the IL-33 airway inflammation model (Figs. 1F and S1A), consistent with the transcriptional data. Additional analysis showed that Tim-3 expression was most prominent on ILC2s compared to other immune cell types, such as CD4⁺, CD8⁺, and CD19⁺ cells, following IL-33 stimulation (Fig. S1B, C), suggesting a unique regulatory role in ILC2s. Subdivision of ILC2s into Tim-3⁺ and Tim-3⁻ populations revealed that Tim-3⁺ ILC2s expressed significantly higher levels of the activation marker GATA-3 and the proliferation marker Ki67 than their Tim-3− counterparts (Fig. S1E, F). These results suggest that Tim-3-targeted therapy may be useful as a treatment for activated ILC2. To directly assess the functional impact of Tim-3 engagement, pulmonary ILC2s were isolated from IL-33-challenged mice and cultured ex vivo and either an isotype control or a Tim-3 agonist (anti-Tim-3 antibody clone 5D12) for 24 h22 (Fig. 1G). Annexin-V/DAPI staining confirmed that the Tim-3 agonist did not induce cytotoxicity (Fig. S1G). Notably, stimulation with the Tim-3 agonist significantly reduced the secretion of IL-5 and IL-13 (Fig. 1H, I), as well as the expression of GATA-3 and Ki67 (Fig. 1J, K), compared to controls. These results confirm that Tim-3 controls the activation of ILC2s. Tim-3 modulates ILC2 function through the NLK signaling pathway

To determine the mechanism by which Tim-3 regulates ILC2 activation, we isolated activated ILC2s and performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of ILC2 cultured for 24 h with an isotype control or Tim-3 agonist. We observed 681 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) induced by Tim-3 agonist treatment (Fig. 2A). To validate the association between Tim-3 signaling and these pathways, Ingenuity Pathways Analysis (IPA) was employed, identifying the downregulation of the Th2 Pathway signaling, NF-κB signaling, and TNFR2 non-canonical NF-κB signaling and upregulation of WNT/β-catenin signaling pathways in Tim-3 agonist treatment ILC2s (Fig. 2B). Our analyses led us to identify Nlk, a gene previously recognized as a repressor of NF-κB in multiple immune cell types23–25. In this study, RNA expression of Nlk was significantly upregulated by Tim-3 agonist stimulation, suggesting a role for Nlk in ILC2s. To further explore the correlation between Nlk and ILC2 activation, we used weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Intriguingly, we found significant negative correlations between Nlk and several markers linked to ILC2 activation, including Il13, Stim1, Cd84, Cd44, Map3k8, and Il7r26–31 (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that Nlk may negatively regulate ILC2 activity. NF-kB has been identified as a critical regulator of ILC2 activation32. In addition, it has been documented that NF-κB is subject to negative regulation by NLK33. Therefore, we evaluated the expression of NLK and NF-κB proteins in activated ILC2 treated with Tim-3 agonists or isotype controls to investigate the relationship between NLK and NF-κB. Agonist treatment significantly increased NLK levels (Fig. 2D) while concurrently reducing NF-κB expression (Fig. 2E), suggesting a potential inhibitory mechanism mediated by NLK. To investigate the causal relationship between Tim-3 signaling and NLK in the regulation of ILC2 activity, we treated activated ILC2s isolated from wild-type (WT) mice with a Tim-3 agonist and NLK inhibitors34, and assessed their effects on cytokine production and ILC2 activation (Fig. 2F). Annexin-V/DAPI staining confirmed that neither the Tim-3 agonist nor the NLK inhibitor induced cytotoxicity in this context (Fig. S2A). Treatment with the Tim-3 agonist significantly reduced the production of cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-13 compared to the untreated group. However, co-treatment with the Tim-3 agonist and NLK inhibitor restored cytokine production, resulting in significantly higher levels of cytokines when compared to the Tim-3 agonist treatment group alone (Fig. 2G, H). Consistent with the cytokine data, expression of GATA-3 (Fig. 2I), Ki67 (Fig. 2J), and p65 (Fig. S2B) was significantly reduced in the Tim-3 agonist alone group compared to the untreated group but significantly increased in the Tim-3 agonist plus NLK inhibitor group. Notably, levels in the co-treatment group were comparable to those observed in the untreated group. To further substantiate these findings, experiments employing morpholino-mediated gene silencing were conducted, which produced concordant results (Fig. S2C, D). Tim-3 activation is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction in ILC2s

Recent studies have increasingly shown that mitochondrial respiration plays a key role in modulating the cytokine secretion capacity of ILC2s35,36. Furthermore, NF-κB has been identified as a regulator of mitochondrial energy production12,37. To explore the link between Tim-3 signaling and mitochondrial function, we found in our transcriptomic analysis that treatment of ILC2s with Tim-3 agonist led to the downregulation of pathways related to respiratory electron transport, oxidative phosphorylation, complex I biogenesis, complex III assembly, and mitochondrial translation. In contrast, transcripts related to the mitochondrial dysfunction pathway were upregulated (Fig. 3A). Using a MitoTracker Green assay we found that ILC2 mitochondrial mass was significantly dampened/reduced/impaired when Tim-3 was activated (Fig. 3B). Transcriptomic analysis further showed broad downregulation of genes associated with the mitochondrial respiratory signature following Tim-3 agonist treatment (Fig. 3C). To validate these transcriptomic findings, we measured mitochondrial respiration in live ILC2s (Fig. 3D). While basal respiration was not significantly affected, Tim-3 agonist treatment markedly reduced both the spare respiratory capacity and ATP production rates (Fig. 3E–G). Notably, while Tim-3 agonism significantly impaired mitochondrial respiration, screening of transcriptomic profiles and glucose uptake assays revealed no significant changes in glycolytic pathways or fatty acid oxidation, suggesting a selective impact on mitochondrial metabolism. Collectively, these results suggest that Tim-3 signaling negatively regulates mitochondrial function in ILC2s.

Tim-3 agonist treatment attenuates IL-33-induced airway hyperreactivity

To investigate the potential of Tim-3 stimulation in preventing IL-33-induced AHR, WT mice were intranasally challenged with IL-33 (0.5 µg) or PBS, followed by intraperitoneal administration of either a Tim-3 agonist (200 µg) or isotype control for 3 consecutive days (Fig. 4A). On the fourth day, pulmonary function was evaluated by directly measuring lung resistance and dynamic compliance in tracheostomized, anesthetized mice. The mice were mechanically ventilated and exposed to increasing doses of aerosolized methacholine. Following the assessment of AHR, the trachea was cannulated, and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid was collected for further analysis by flow cytometry. Tim-3 agonist-treated mice showed significantly lower lung resistance compared to mice treated with isotype control, indicating that Tim-3 agonist treatment alleviates IL-33-induced AHR (Fig. 4B). Consistent with these findings, measurements of dynamic compliance showed marked improvement in the Tim-3 agonist-treated group relative to the isotype control group (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant reduction in pulmonary ILC2 numbers in Tim-3 agonist-treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 4D). Notably, lung inflammation was substantially reduced in the Tim-3 agonist group compared to controls, as evidenced by decreased counts of CD45+ leukocytes and eosinophils (Figs. 4E, F and S3). Additionally, levels of key ILC2-derived cytokines IL-5 and IL-13 in BAL fluid were lower following Tim-3 agonist treatment compared to isotype controls (Fig. 4G, H). Finally, histological examination of lung tissue (Fig. 4I) showed that IL-33 stimulation caused prominent epithelial thickening (Fig. 4J), heightened inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4K), and elevated Alcian blue/periodic acid–schiff (PAS)-positive cells (Fig. 4L, M) in isotype control mice. These pathological features were markedly reduced in the Tim-3 agonist-treated group. Tim-3 agonist treatment attenuates Alternaria alternata-induced AHR

To further evaluate whether Tim-3 activation can reduce AHR, we employed a clinically relevant pulmonary inflammation model using Alternaria alternata (A. alternata), a common fungal allergen linked to allergic diseases. WT mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of Tim-3 agonist (200 µg) or isotype control, followed by intranasal (i.n.) exposure to 100 µg of Alternaria alternata or PBS on days 1–4. On day 5, we assessed lung function, pulmonary ILC2 numbers, BAL cell counts, and histopathology (Fig. 5A). Consistent with our earlier findings, mice treated with the Tim-3 agonist exhibited significantly reduced lung resistance and marked improvement in dynamic compliance compared to controls (Fig. 5B, C). Tim-3 agonist treatment also significantly decreased the total number of pulmonary ILC2s (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the numbers of CD45+ cells and eosinophils in BAL fluid were significantly lower in the Tim-3 agonist-treated group than in the isotype control group (Fig. 5E, F). Levels of the ILC2-associated cytokines IL-5 and IL-13 in BAL fluid were also significantly reduced following Tim-3 agonist treatment (Fig. 5G, H). Histological analysis revealed markedly decreased epithelial thickness, inflammatory cell infiltration, and PAS-positive cells in the lungs of Tim-3 agonist-treated mice (Fig. 5I–M). Based on our findings in WT mice, we next investigated whether targeting Tim-3 could mitigate AHR in the absence of adaptive immunity. To this end, we used Rag2-deficient mice, which lack mature T and B cells, and challenged them with Alternaria alternata using the same protocol described previously to induce AHR. We then assessed lung function and inflammation. Discussion

In this study, we identify Tim-3 as a critical negative regulator of ILC2 effector function. We show that Tim-3 is significantly upregulated in activated ILC2s, and engagement with a Tim-3 agonist suppresses ILC2 effector functions, including cytokine production and proliferation. In mouse models, stimulation with IL-33 or exposure to the allergen Alternaria alternata induces a population of Tim-3⁺ ILC2s characterized by elevated expression of activation markers and type 2 cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-13. Treatment with Tim-3 agonist significantly reduces ILC2 activation, proliferation, and cytokine production without causing cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, we showed that Tim-3 exerts its inhibitory effects via the NLK signaling pathway, downregulating NF-κB activity and mitochondrial function, leading to decreased respiratory capacity and ATP production in pulmonary ILC2s. In vivo, administration of a Tim-3 agonist in mice challenged with IL-33 or the allergen Alternaria significantly attenuates AHR, reduces lung inflammation, and lowers both ILC2 frequency and cytokine levels—effects that are sustained even in the absence of adaptive immune cells. Conversely, specific deletion of Tim-3 in ILC2s leads to exacerbated AHR and increased lung inflammation, underscoring the essential role of Tim-3 in restraining ILC2-driven pathology. This regulatory function is conserved in human ILC2s, where Tim-3 agonist treatment effectively suppresses activation, proliferation, and cytokine secretion both ex vivo and in a humanized mouse model. Collectively, these findings establish that Tim-3 is a pivotal inhibitory receptor in ILC2s and underscore its therapeutic potential in asthma and other ILC2-mediated airway inflammatory diseases.

Tim-3 is an immune checkpoint receptor known to regulate T cell immune responses and has recently garnered significant attention as a potential therapeutic target in cancer immunotherapy38. Tim-3 functions as an inhibitory receptor expressed on various immune cells and has been shown to suppress T cell activation, proliferation, and cytokine production through Bat3 dissociation and activation of signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, and β-catenin39–41. Recent reports have shown that Tim-3 is expressed on immune-related cells, including microglia, and modulates cell activation through multiple mechanisms15. In this study, we investigated the functional role of Tim-3 in ILC2s, key effectors of type 2 inflammation. Our data revealed that Tim-3 engagement suppresses ILC2 effector function through activation of NLK, a downstream component of the β-catenin signaling pathway, which in turn inhibits NF-κB activity. This regulatory mechanism was validated using an NLK inhibitor and aligns with previously characterized pathways in T cells, further corroborated by existing literature.

Tim-3 is a well-established negative regulator of immune activation, and its dysregulation is associated with various inflammatory and autoimmune conditions. While its role in T-cell exhaustion has been extensively studied, our findings identify Tim-3 as a pivotal inhibitory checkpoint for ILC2s that is crucial for restraining AHR. Engagement of this receptor by an agonist mimic ligand induces inhibitory signaling, effectively dampening the pathogenic effector functions of both mouse and human ILC2s.

The therapeutic relevance of this approach is further underscored by the expression profile of endogenous Tim-3 ligands in the context of respiratory disease. Galectin-9, a primary ligand for Tim-3, is expressed by various immune cells and airway epithelial cells, indicating that Tim-3 signaling in ILC2s is likely mediated through interactions with ligands in the airway microenvironment rather than through autocrine mechanisms. Clinical evidence indicates that Galectin-9 levels are significantly reduced in the airways of patients with asthma compared to healthy individuals42,43. This suggests that the Tim-3 inhibitory pathway may be under-activated in asthmatic lungs due to a relative deficiency of endogenous ligands, supporting the rationale for therapeutic targeting of Tim-3 to restore this regulatory checkpoint and suppress ILC2-driven inflammation.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of intracellular metabolism in regulating cytokine production by ILC2s44,45, with mitochondrial energy production serving as a central driver of this process. Efficient mitochondrial function depends on the utilization of key metabolic substrates such as fatty acids and glucose, as well as direct modulation of mitochondrial activity46. Furthermore, in Treg, circulating Treg cells depend on OXPHOS and lipid oxidation for energy, while TIM-3 expressing Treg cells have decreased mitochondrial mass and membrane potential and reduced OXPHOS, suggesting that Tim-3 controls mitochondrial function47. Given prior evidence implicating NF-κB in the regulation of mitochondrial function48,49, we investigated whether Tim-3 signaling influences ILC2s’ metabolism. Through transcriptomic profiling and extracellular flux analysis of ILC2s, we found that Tim-3 signaling significantly suppresses mitochondrial function-related genes, such as respiratory electron transport, oxidative phosphorylation, complex I biogenesis, complex III assembly, and mitochondrial translation, and markedly reduced both the reserve respiratory capacity and ATP production rates. These findings indicate that Tim-3 negatively regulates mitochondrial activity by inhibiting NF-κB signaling, thereby dampening cytokine production and effector responses in ILC2s.

Tim-3 dysregulation has been reported to be associated with multiple sclerosis, Crohn’s disease, and rheumatoid arthritis, and Tim-3 signaling plays an important role in suppressing T cell activity50. However, the role of Tim-3 in ILC2s in autoimmune diseases has not yet been reported. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting Tim-3 in ILC2-mediated AHR, we utilized two established mouse models: IL-33-induced and Alternaria-induced AHR. In both models, administration of a Tim-3 agonist significantly ameliorated AHR and airway inflammation. Notably, comparable therapeutic effects were observed in Rag2−/− mice, which lack functional T and B cells, indicating that Tim-3 acts on ILC2s independent of adaptive immune components. In contrast, deletion of Tim-3 specifically in ILC2s led to a marked exacerbation of AHR and enhanced pulmonary inflammation, underscoring the critical role of endogenous Tim-3 in restraining ILC2-driven pathology and inflammation. Collectively, these findings establish Tim-3 is a critical negative regulator of ILC2 function and highlight its potential as an efficient therapeutic target in allergic airway diseases, where ILC2s are major contributors to disease severity.

In humans, as in mice, Tim-3 is an immune checkpoint molecule expressed on a variety of human immune cells, particularly activated CD4+ Th1 cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), dendritic cells, and macrophages51. It has been reported that Tim-3 is capable of transducing various signals in immune cells and suppressing their activation52. However, the role of Tim-3 in human ILC2 remains to be elucidated. Therefore, to extend our mouse findings to human ILC2s (hILC2s), we investigated the role of Tim-3 in hILC2. Stimulation with IL-33 induced robust Tim-3 expression in hILC2s, and ex vivo activation of Tim-3 significantly suppressed the production of key cytokines, including IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, and IL-13. Tim-3 engagement also reduced the expression of GATA-3 and Ki67, indicating diminished transcriptional activity and proliferative capacity, consistent with observations in the mouse models. In a humanized mouse model, administration of a Tim-3 agonist effectively attenuated AHR and pulmonary inflammation, further supporting the translational relevance of these findings. Collectively, these results suggest that Tim-3-based therapies may represent a promising strategy for modulating ILC2-driven immune responses in human allergic airway diseases.

Targeting Tim-3 for the regulation of ILC2s holds considerable therapeutic promise for asthma treatment by addressing multiple unmet clinical needs. Our data reveal that among various immune cell subsets, including CD4⁺, CD8⁺ T cells, and B cells, Tim-3 expression is most robustly induced in ILC2s. Thus, the effect of Tim-3 agonist was effective not only in Rag2−/− mice lacking T and B cells, but also in studies using WT mice, indicating a high specificity of the target cells. This selective expression profile suggests that Tim-3-based interventions could provide precise immunomodulation with minimized off-target effects, potentially overcoming limitations associated with broad-spectrum immunosuppressive therapies53,54. Importantly, ILC2s are increasingly recognized as central drivers of steroid-resistant and non-eosinophilic asthma phenotypes, which remain challenging to treat with current therapeutic options53–55. Thus, modulating ILC2 activity via Tim-3 activation offers a potential strategy to target these severe patient populations. Moreover, the inducibility of Tim-3 expression by inflammatory mediators positions Tim-3 not only as a therapeutic target but also as a potential biomarker for disease activity and therapeutic responsiveness, enabling more personalized treatment approaches.

Our findings demonstrate that administration of a Tim-3 agonist significantly ameliorates AHR and airway inflammation in both acute and chronic models. The therapeutic efficacy observed in mice, which lack functional T and B cells, confirms that Tim-3 can operate through a mechanism independent of adaptive immune components. Furthermore, the exacerbated inflammation observed in mice underscores the critical and non-redundant role of endogenous Tim-3 in restraining pathogenic ILC2-driven pathology. These results, combined with our observation that Tim-3 is more robustly induced in ILC2s than in pulmonary T-cell subsets, suggest that the Tim-3-NLK axis is a primary regulatory checkpoint for activated ILC2s in the lung.

However, we recognize that our study did not specifically characterize the activation status or cytokine profiles of adaptive T-cell responses in the chronic Alternaria model. Given that Tim-3 is a well-established negative regulator of T-cell function in various inflammatory contexts, it remains possible that a portion of the therapeutic effect observed in WT mice is mediated through the adaptive immune compartment. We acknowledge the lack of a full T-cell assessment in the chronic setting as a limitation of this study. While our data confirms that ILC2-specific Tim-3 signaling is essential for controlling AHR, future research is required to fully delineate the relative contributions and potential crosstalk between innate and adaptive Tim-3 signaling during chronic allergic inflammation.

In summary, our findings reveal a regulatory mechanism by which Tim-3 suppresses ILC2 activity through the activation of NLK, leading to modulation of NF-κB signaling and mitochondrial function. This Tim-3-NLK axis emerges as a critical pathway for restraining ILC2-mediated type 2 immune responses, including AHR, a hallmark of allergic inflammation. Importantly, therapeutic activation of Tim-3 using Tim-3 agonist demonstrated robust efficacy in both mouse and human ILC2 models, highlighting the translational relevance of this pathway. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the molecular and metabolic circuits governing ILC2 activity but also identify Tim-3 as a compelling immunoregulatory target for the development of next-generation therapies to treat ILC2-driven diseases such as asthma and other chronic allergic conditions.

Methods

Mouse strains and housing

WT C57BL/6J mice, Rag2−/− (C.B6(Cg)-Rag2tm1.1Cgn/J), Rag2−/− IL-2Rγ−/− (Rag2−/−GC−/−; C;129S4-Rag2tm1.1Flv Il2rgtm1.1Flv/J), and Nmur1-Cre+/− mice (C57BL/6-Tg(Nmur1-iCre,-eGFP)1Dart/J), aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME). Havcr2flox/flox mice (Tim-3flox/flox) were provided by Dr. Vijay Kuchroo (Ann Romney Center for Neurologic Diseases, Harvard Medical School, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Boston, MA). Age- and sex-matched mice (6–8 weeks old) were used in all studies. Nmur1 Cre+/− Tim-3flox/flox (ILC2ΔTim-3) mice were generated by crossing Nmur1 Cre+/− (ILC2WT) and Tim-3flox/flox mice. Both ILC2ΔTim-3 and ILC2WT mice were utilized in experiments. All mice were maintained in a pathogen-free facility at the Keck School of Medicine, University of Southern California (USC), under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C with 40–60% humidity.

Genotyping PCR

Mouse genomic DNA was extracted from tissues digested with proteinase K (Worthington Biochemical). Genotyping PCR was performed using the extracted DNA, GoTaq Master Mixes (Promega), and primers with the nucleotide sequences listed below. Tim-3 flox forward 5′-GGCCTGTGACCAATGTTCTT-3′; Tim-3 flox reverse 5′-CTAACCCAGCCTCCTGAGTG-3′; NMUR1-Cre forward 5′-TCCCAGTGGCATGTGACTAA-3′; NMUR1-Cre reverse 5′-GCAGGTTGGAGACTTTCCTCT-3′. PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel and visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Isolation and ex vivo culture of mouse lung ILC2s

To isolate mouse pulmonary ILC2s, mice were anesthetized and administered 0.5 µg recombinant mouse IL-33 (rmIL-33; BioLegend) in 50 µL PBS intranasally once daily for 3 consecutive days. On the fourth day, mice were euthanized and subjected to transcardial perfusion with PBS to remove circulating blood cells, after which lungs were harvested and digested with 400 U/mL collagenase Type IV (Worthington) at 37 °C for 1 h. The digested tissue was mechanically dissociated, filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Falcon), and treated with RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) to remove erythrocytes. Single-cell suspensions were stained with antibodies against CD45, ST2, CD127, and a lineage cocktail (CD3ε, CD4, CD5, TCRβ, TCRγδ, CD45R/B220, CD335, CD11c, CD11b, Gr1, FcεRIα, and Ter119), and live, single, CD45⁺ST2⁺CD127⁺Lineage⁻ cells were sorted as ILC2s using a FACSAria III cell sorter (BD Biosciences) to a purity exceeding 95%. Sorted ILC2s were cultured at 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL in 96-well U-bottom plates in complete RPMI-1640 medium (cRPMI) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Omega Scientific), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (GenClone), 10 ng/mL rmIL-2, and 10 ng/mL rmIL-7 (both from BioLegend), and maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. For Tim-3 agonist studies, anti-Tim-3 monoclonal antibody (clone 5D12, Cell Essentials) (Tim-3 agonist)22 or isotype control (mouse IgG1, clone P3.6.2.8.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added at 20 µg/mL for either 1 h or 18–24 h, while for NLK inhibition, OTSSP167 (Medchemicalexpress) (NLK inhibitor) was added at 0.85 nM for 18–24 h34.

Assessment of mitochondrial function in ILC2s

Mitochondrial function was assessed by measuring the real-time oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using the Seahorse Mini HS XF instrument (Agilent) as previously described12. Briefly, 6 × 10⁴ ILC2s were treated with or without Tim-3 agonist for 24 h, after which cells were counted and seeded in triplicate into Seahorse XFp PDL-coated cell culture mini plates. Cells were maintained in FBS- and phenol red-free Seahorse assay medium supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, and 1 mM pyruvate. The metabolic activity was profiled using the Agilent T-cell metabolic assay protocol, with baseline OCR measurements followed by sequential injections of 1.5 µM oligomycin, 2.5 µM BAM15, and 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A. Oxygen consumption was measured in triplicate after each injection, and parameters such as basal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and total ATP production rates were calculated.

In vivo mouse challenge and BAL collection

Mice were intranasally challenged under anesthesia with either 0.5 µg/mouse recombinant mouse IL-33 (rmIL-33) or PBS for 3 consecutive days, following established protocols21. For experiments involving A. alternata, mice received intranasal administration of 100 µg/mouse A. alternata extract for 4 consecutive days under anesthesia, as previously described by our group12. Twenty-four hours after the final challenge, lung tissues were enzymatically digested and processed into single-cell suspensions for downstream analyses. For BAL collection, the airways were flushed three times with 1 mL of cold PBS, and the recovered lavage fluid was centrifuged to pellet cells, with supernatants retained for cytokine assays. Prior to flow cytometry, cells isolated from BAL samples were treated with RBC lysis buffer to ensure optimal staining.

Additionally, PE anti-mouse SiglecF (E50-2440, 1:100, was obtained from BD Biosciences, while PerCP-eFluor710 anti-mouse ST2 (RMST2-2, 1:266) and eFluor450 anti-mouse CD11b (M1/70, 1:200,) were from Thermo Fisher Scientific. For intranuclear staining, the Foxp3 Transcription Factor Staining Kit (Thermo Fisher) was used along with APC anti-mouse Ki67 (SolA15, 1:100, Thermo Fisher), PE anti-mouse/human GATA-3 (TWAJ, 1:100, Thermo Fisher), NLK Antibody Alexa Fluor 647 (B-5, 1:100, Santa Cruz), and PE anti-human/mouse Rela (p65) (532301, 1:100, R&D Systems). Apoptosis was assessed using PE Annexin V (Thermo Fisher) and DAPI (Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. For knockdown studies, the 2.5 mM Nlk 5′-TGGCTCTTGTGCCACAAAGAGACAT-3′ or random control oligo (Gene Tools, LLC, Philomath, Ore) was added as free uptake oligos 24 h as previously described56. For human samples, the following antibodies were used: FITC anti-human lineage cocktail [CD3 (UCHT1), CD14 (HCD14), CD16 (3G8), CD19 (HIB19), CD20 (2H7), CD56 (HCD56), 1:50], with additional lineage markers including FITC anti-human CD235a (HI264, 1:200), FCεRIα (AER-37, 1:200), CD1a (HI149, 1:200), CD123 (6H6, 1:200), and CD5 (L17F12, 1:200). Further, APC-Cy7 anti-human CD45 (HI30, 1:200), PE-Cy7 anti-human CD127 (A019D5, 1:200), and PE anti-human CRTH2 (BM16, 1:200) were purchased from BioLegend. Live/dead discrimination was performed using fixable violet or aqua cell stain kits (Thermo Fisher), and CountBright absolute counting beads (Thermo Fisher) were used for quantification. All samples were analyzed on a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were processed using FlowJo version 10 software (BD Biosciences).

Assessment of pulmonary function and histological analysis of lung tissue

Lung function was assessed using the FinePointe RC system (Buxco Research Systems, Wilmington, NC), with mice anesthetized and mechanically ventilated according to established protocols to ensure animal comfort and well-being during the procedure. Baseline measurements were obtained following aerosolized PBS administration, after which mice were challenged with increasing concentrations of aerosolized methacholine (Sigma) ranging from 5 to 80 mg/mL. Maximal pulmonary resistance (RL) and minimal dynamic compliance (cDyn) were recorded over 3 min after each methacholine dose. For histological examination, the right-sided lung was excised, fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde (PFA), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness for hematoxylin and eosin staining. Composite images were created using Adobe Illustrator software (version 22.1), and stained sections were visualized with a Keyence BZ-9000 microscope (Keyence, Itasca, IL) and analyzed using the ImageJ software package (NIH & LOCI, University of Wisconsin).

Transcriptomic profiling and bioinformatic analysis of ILC2s

ILC2s were harvested and lysed in RLT buffer (Qiagen), and total RNA was extracted using the MicroRNeasy kit (Qiagen). For cDNA synthesis and library preparation, 10 pg of RNA per sample was processed with the SMARTer Ultra Low Input RNA v3 kit (Clontech). Amplified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 system, generating an average of approximately 30 million reads per sample. Raw sequencing reads were processed using Partek Genomics Suite software, version 7.0 (Partek Inc). Read alignment was performed with STAR (version 2.6.1d) against the mouse reference genome mm10 with GENECODE M21 annotations. Following alignment, quantification, and normalization were carried out, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq. Gene enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted with the IPA tool.

Multiplex analysis of cytokine production

Following treatment of ILC2s with the indicated reagents, culture supernatants were collected for cytokine quantification. Cytokine concentrations in these supernatants were measured using the LEGENDplex Mouse Th2 Panel (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Similarly, cytokine levels in BAL fluid supernatants were assessed using the same LEGENDplex protocol. For human samples, culture supernatants were collected and analyzed with the LEGENDplex Human Th2 Panel (BioLegend), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. All LEGENDplex assays were performed on an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Isolation, culture, and in vivo transfer of human ILC2s

Human peripheral ILC2s were isolated with high purity (>95%) from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) obtained from 6–10 healthy donors following informed consent under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the USC (Protocol #HS-19-00247). Fresh human blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS, and PBMCs were isolated using SepMate-50 separation tubes (STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following red blood cell lysis (BioLegend), CRTH2⁺ cells were enriched using the CRTH2 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) as previously described57. Isolated human ILC2s were FACS sorted and phenotypically defined as CD45⁺, CD127⁺, and CRTH2⁺, while negative for lineage markers (CD3, CD5, CD14, CD16, CD19, CD20, CD56, CD235a, CD1a, CD123). These ILC2s were cultured for 72 h at 37 °C (5 × 10⁴ cells/mL) in complete RPMI (cRPMI) supplemented with recombinant human IL-2 (10 ng/mL, BioLegend) and IL-7 (10 ng/mL, BioLegend), with or without IL-33 (100 ng/mL, BioLegend), in U-bottom 96-well plates. For specific analyses, 20 µg/mL of human TIM-3 antibody (344801, R&D Systems) or isotype control (monoclonal rat IgG1 antibody, 43414, R&D Systems) was added to the cultures. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days until the desired number of ILC2s was obtained. Subsequently, a total of 2 × 10⁵ ILC2s were intravenously transferred into Rag2−/−GC−/− alymphoid mice. Recipient mice were challenged intranasally with carrier-free recombinant human IL-33 (BioLegend) at 1.0 µg/mouse in 50 µL PBS for 3 consecutive days under anesthesia, as previously described21. Prior to each intranasal challenge, either human TIM-3 antibody or isotype control was administered intraperitoneally at 200 µg/mouse in 200 µL PBS.

Data analysis and statistics

All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1). Comparisons between two groups were made using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests, while one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups. All experiments, including those shown in the main and supplementary figures, were performed in at least three independent replicates. Unless otherwise specified in the figure legends, data are presented as mean ± SEM from one representative experiment. Sample sizes (n) for each specific experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the IACUC. The human study was reviewed and approved by the IRB of USC, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study.
