Longitudinal cognitive assessment using the Cumulus NeuLogiq platform in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia
Authors: Emmet Costello, Kimberly Kiyui, Ciara Brennan, Niamh Ni Obain, Sophie Leonard, Mark Heverin, Florentine Barbey, John Dyer, Laura Rueda-Delgado, Shannon Diggin, Elizabeth Tunbridge, Hugh Marston, Robert Y.K. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
People living with ALS (plwALS) and/or FTD (plwFTD) often experience cognitive and behavioural changes. However, detection can be confounded due to factors like fatigue and testing anxiety. Cumulus neuroscience developed NeuLogiq(R), a multi-modal neurocognitive platform that can be used in clinic or at home, providing an ecologically valid measure of cognition. This study examined the feasibility and usability of NeuLogiq in plwALS, plwFTD, and controls, and compared performance on gold standard neuropsychological assessments with corresponding NeuLogiq digital assessments. Over 8 months, plwALS (n = 11), plwFTD (n = 7), and matched healthy controls (n = 10) completed longitudinal full neuropsychological assessment, as well as three 25-minute NeuLogiq Platform sessions every 2 weeks in their homes. Participants adhered well to the study schedule, conducting over 32/54 sessions on average. All groups rated usability in the ‘good’ or ‘excellent’ range and had > 80% complete data. Baseline group differences were detectable on both NeuLogiq digital assessments and benchmark neuropsychological assessments of similar cognitive domains. Longitudinal mixed effects models found that the ALS group showed decline on NeuLogiq measures of emotion recognition and speech fluency. These findings suggest that the NeuLogiq platform is feasible and usable for plwALS and plwFTD, and can identify cognitive deficits to a similar extent as benchmark assessments over time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53099-x.

Introduction

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are progressive neurodegenerative conditions, characterised by overlapping genetic, neuropathological, clinical and neuropsychological features1–3. FTD is primarily marked by changes in personal and social behaviour, emotional blunting, apathy, and language deficits, whilst ALS is marked for upper and lower motor neuron death, leading to rapid paralysis and death. However, ~ 14% of people living with ALS (plwALS) develop co-morbid FTD4 while ~ 13% of people living with FTD develop co-morbid ALS5. Furthermore, ~ 35% of non-demented plwALS experience mild cognitive and behavioural changes, while ~ 30% of people living with FTD (plwFTD) display mild motor dysfunction. Consequently, there is growing recognition that ALS and FTD represent two sides of a disease spectrum called frontotemporal-spectrum disorder (ALS-FTSD)2. Despite this, existing assessment tools are typically designed to measure motor or cognitive/behavioural impairment in isolation, rather than capturing the multidimensional nature of ALS-FTSD. This fragmentation can result in incomplete phenotypic characterisation and may reduce the sensitivity of clinical trials to detect change across the full disease spectrum. For example, the ALS functional rating scale – revised (ALSFRS-R)6 is a validated measure of motor disability in ALS but does not assess cognitive or behavioural dysfunction, and is further limited by restricted granularity, multidimensionality, and floor and ceiling effects7. Thus, there is an urgent need for multimodal biomarkers and novel endpoints, encompassing direct measures of neuronal functioning, motor functioning and cognitive and behavioural functioning.

While the detection of cognitive impairment at a single timepoint is relatively straightforward, as individuals are typically screened in multidisciplinary ALS or memory clinics, dynamically measuring cognitive decline is more challenging. Due to the rapidly progressive nature of these diseases, longitudinal studies are often limited by high attrition rates8. This drop-out is often non-random, as those with more aggressive motor decline and/or greater executive dysfunction are less likely to continue in observational studies9,10. It is also unclear if the cognitive assessment tools typically used in ALS studies (e.g., the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screening tool) are in fact sensitive enough to reliably capture real world cognitive or behavioural decline within individuals11. Structural and functional interrogation of the brain (e.g., MRI, fMRI, PET, EEG, MEG) in ALS-FTSD is also subject to selection bias. In imaging studies, those with more severe respiratory dysfunction, sialorrhea or behavioural changes are often unable to lie supine for sustained periods, complicating the collection of MRI data12. EEG and MEG technologies are less burdensome than MRI, but still typically require participants to travel, which is often more difficult for those with more severe progression. Home-based digital tools may address some of these issues by offering a less burdensome assessment, eliminating the need to travel to clinics, while also offering an ecologically valid measure of functioning. Home-based sampling can minimize measurement error that would normally occur due to testing anxiety13, and dynamically capture the effect that fluctuations in mood and stress have on cognitive functioning14. Mass sampling at a pre-baseline phase can max out familiarity effects, enabling better characterization and control of practice effects. Remote, self-administered digital assessments have gained considerable popularity in clinical studies and trials as they enable the collection of objective, ecological and economical digital biomarkers of disease progression and/or treatment effects at scale15,16. However, the usability of these digital platforms must first be demonstrated in the target patient populations, especially when cognitive, motor and behavioural changes are present.

NeuLogiq is an at-home digital neurocognitive assessment platform developed in collaboration with a precompetitive consortium of pharmaceutical companies, including Biogen, Eli Lilly, GSK, Takeda, Boehringer Ingelheim, and Bristol Myers Squibb. The consortium’s aim was to create a platform suitable for home- and clinic-based self-administered assessments that could be used by non-expert individuals, including those with cognitive impairments, to enable the collection of repeated longitudinal measurements. The NeuLogiq platform consists of a 16-channel wireless dry electroencephalography (EEG) headset coupled with a computer tablet for digital assessment presentation, allowing for measurement of neuronal activity and cognitive functioning. In contrast to traditional pen-and-paper based tools, cognition is measured with gamified and passive assessments, improving participant engagement and adherence to study protocols. Importantly, the platform is designed to support multidimensional assessment across ALS-FTSD, simultaneously capturing cognitive performance, neuronal activity, and motor-related measures such as reaction time and speech-derived metrics.

This study aimed to evaluate the usability and feasibility of the NeuLogiq platform17 in plwALS, plwFTD, and healthy controls. We also aimed to assess the ability of the platform to discriminate between the three study groups, and its ability to measure cognitive change or stability over time. This study forms part of a broader effort by the consortium to evaluate the platform across multiple clinical cohorts and indications, and here we assess its applicability and performance in ALS and FTD. Feasibility was measured based on participant compliance with a schedule of assessments over 37 weeks, and the completeness of data during these assessments. Usability and participants’ subjective feedback on the technology were also evaluated using validated questionnaires and a bespoke survey. This paper focuses on the results of cognitive assessment performance, while a follow-up study will outline the findings in respect to EEG data.

Methods

Design

This study utilized a longitudinal, observational, prospective, case-control study design. People living with ALS and/or FTD were identified through routine clinical appointments in Beaumont Hospital, Dublin, or through their treating neurologist. Healthy controls, matched for age, sex and educational attainment, were recruited through existing networks for recruitment, e.g., advertising in local areas and community groups.

Participants

Interested individuals were provided with a patient information leaflet with a minimum of one week to consider participation. If the individual agreed to take part, they were invited to the Clinical Research Centre, Beaumont Hospital, Dublin, where they gave consent, were screened, and on-boarded to the NeuLogiq platform. Participant consent was obtained according to the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was granted by the Beaumont Hospital Research Ethics Committee (REC Reference 21/81). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants.

For people with ALS or FTD, the inclusion criteria were: aged 18–90 years old; a clinical diagnosis of ALS (possible or probable according to El Escorial criteria18 and/or a clinical diagnosis of FTD by a treating neurologist; physically and psychologically fit to participate in the study as confirmed by a member of the participant’s multidisciplinary care team; Wi-Fi available in-home; proficient in English; available caregiver/study partner to assist if necessary; and able to provide informed consent. For healthy controls the inclusion criteria were: aged 18–90 years; Wi-Fi available in-home; proficient in English; and able to provide informed consent.

For all potential participants, the exclusion criteria were: neurological or psychiatric co-morbidities that in the opinion of the principal investigator would affect participation in the study or confound their results; concurrent participation in a clinical trial which would, in the opinion of the principal investigator, result in overburden or confound the results; and barriers to technology integration into their home, e.g., unreliable wireless internet connection.

Procedure

Participants were invited to the Clinical Research Centre, Beaumont Hospital, Dublin for an initial onboarding session during which they were trained on the use of the NeuLogiq platform EEG headset and tablet. This training session lasted approximately 45 min in which participants were shown how to log into the tablet-based app, set up the dry EEG headset, and complete the gamified assessments. Each participant was given a unique username and password to log in to the tablet-based app. Following this, they were provided with hardware for home use and instructed to complete sessions at regular intervals throughout the study. The first two weeks using the platform were termed the ‘familiarisation phase’, intended to allow the participants to become familiar with setting up the equipment and practice completing the digital assessments. During the familiarisation period, all participant sessions were systematically reviewed by the clinical staff through the NeuLogiq study management hub to verify that the headset was worn as instructed, that behavioural performance was consistent with participants’ aetiology, and that their responses demonstrated appropriate understanding of the assessment instructions.

Data from the familiarisation phase were not included in the final analysis. Following this phase, participants were instructed to use the headset and computer tablet 3 times (or sessions) every fortnight, lasting 8 months (37 weeks) in total. Each session on the platform was designed to last less than 25 min, meaning participants spent a maximum of 1.5 h on the platform per fortnight. The at-home assessment-list included 10 digital assessments split across three sessions to limit the burden on participants. Each three-session ‘cycle’ presented a different set of assessments, balanced to avoid exceeding 25 minutes’ duration on any one session.

Separate to the in-home measures, participants were also cognitively assessed using the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS screen (ECAS)19 and a gold-standard neuropsychological battery on three occasions in-person: baseline (t0), week 19 (t1) and week 37 (t2). Benchmark cognitive assessments were typically administered by one of the research staff in a quiet room in the Clinical Research Centre, Beaumont Hospital. A small number of benchmark assessments were conducted in participants’ homes to accommodate individuals with greater physical disability (n = 3). To minimise potential setting-related effects, benchmark assessments were administered by trained research staff using standardised protocols, identical test materials, and comparable testing conditions (quiet environment, minimisation of interruptions) across both settings. Alternate versions of the ECAS (A–B–C) were administered sequentially across visits to reduce practice effects. Other benchmark neuropsychological measures did not have validated parallel forms, which is a common constraint in longitudinal ALS research. Assessments were spaced approximately 4–5 months apart to reduce short-term learning effects. Given the progressive motor impairment characteristic of ALS, particular attention was paid to minimising motor confounding. Demographic and clinical information on plwALS was drawn from the national ALS register20. See Fig. 1 for overview of the study procedure.

Materials

The NeuLogiq platform hardware

The NeuLogiq platform dry EEG headset is a wireless 16-channel dry sensor EEG headset (www.cumulusneuro.com). Flexible Ag/AgCl coated polymer sensors of a comb-design (ANTNeuro/eemagine GmbH) are used to achieve a stable and dermatologically safe contact to the scalp at 16 channels (10:10 locations: O1, O2, P3, Pz, P4, Cz, FT7, FC3, FCz, FC4, FT8, Fz, AF7, AF8, FPz). The left mastoid is used for reference and the right mastoid for driven bias, with single-use, snap-on electrodes attached to wires extending from the headset. The mastoid sensors consist of gel-based single-use disposable Kendall™ electrodes.

The platform also consists of a tablet-based mobile app which was accessed in this study via a Huawei computer tablet (Huawei MediaPad T3 8.0). Upon login, participants are given instructions on how to place the headset, including how to position it on the head, and how to position detachable mastoid sensors. The user is given feedback on the signal quality of each sensor and is prompted to make adjustments to ensure it meets an acceptable standard. Once the headset has been set up, the participant can begin the gamified digital assessment of brain functioning (see headset and tablet in Supplemental Fig. 1). Cloud-based secure methods are used to collect and automatically process data and to share with web-based dashboards that allow the researcher to monitor participant progress and visualize data daily.

Usability questionnaires

Platform usability was measured using the system usability scale (SUS), a ten-item scale of system usability21, ranging from 0 to 100 with scores > 70 indicating good usability, and scores > 80 indicating excellent usability22. Specific feedback on participant experience, including ease of use, confidence in using the technology, fatigue from the sessions and overall opinion of the platform, was captured using a bespoke survey (see supplementary Figs. 2–5).

Benchmark neuropsychological battery

A comprehensive neuropsychological battery is the gold standard for measuring cognition and behaviour, although the specific assessments employed often vary according to clinical context and research aims. The benchmark battery used in this study (see Table 1) is derived from several decades of clinical practice and research within the Academic Unit of Neurology at Trinity College Dublin and has been extensively validated in population-based and longitudinal ALS cohorts23. While designed to be a screening instrument, the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioural ALS Screen (ECAS) is widely regarded as the most established and universally applied assessment tool for cognitive and language impairment in ALS and is increasingly used as a reference framework in FTD research.

Intellectual functioning was measured using the Test of Premorbid Functioning UK (TOPF-UK24; to capture premorbid IQ) and the Two-subscale Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence 2nd Edition (WASI-II25; to capture current IQ). Executive functioning was measured using the FAS verbal fluency assessment (verbal fluency26, the colour word interference assessment (inhibition and cognitive flexibility27 and the digit span assessment (working memory28. The Sydney Language Battery29 captures object naming, object comprehension and semantic association components of language functioning. Verbal memory was measured with the Wechsler Memory Scale-3rd Edition logical memory assessment (WMS-III30 while visuospatial memory was measured with the Rey Complex Figure Test (RCFT31. Social cognition components (emotion recognition and emotion-prosody matching) were measured using the Wechsler Advanced Clinical Solutions - Emotion Recognition subscales32. Finally, depression and apathy symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire – 9 item version (PHQ-931 and Dimensional Apathy Scale (DAS33 respectively).

The NeuLogiq digital assessments were therefore intended to map onto the same core cognitive and behavioural domains measured by the ECAS and the benchmark battery, i.e., executive function, language, verbal fluency, visuospatial functioning, memory, and behavioural change. This domain-level approach allows the interpretation of digital cognitive measures within a clinically meaningful and widely recognised neuropsychological framework, and acknowledges that the platform is intended to complement rather than replace established in-person assessments. Where possible, accuracy-based, or motor-independent outcome measures were prioritised (e.g., total correct responses rather than raw reaction times). For assessments inherently embedding motor speed (e.g., psychomotor speed) within the score, comparable levels of motor demand were aligned between the NeuLogiq platform and benchmark measures to facilitate cross-modal comparison.

NeuLogiq platform digital assessments

NeuLogiq platform measures were composed of ten gamified neurocognitive assessments developed by Cumulus Neuroscience, targeting a wide range of cognitive functions relevant to ALS and FTD pathologies. It included six purely cognitive assessments and four EEG-compatible assessments. The design of each of these measures is based on traditional cognitive assessments well described and validated in the literature. The core principles underlying these cognitive paradigms were maintained during their digitalization and gamification. Each assessment is summarized below, including the benchmark neuropsychological measure they are associated with (see17,33 for more detailed overviews of many of these assessments). The schedule of digital assessments is outlined in supplemental Table 2.

Symbol swap

Based on digit symbol substitution assessment participants match symbols to numbers based on a translational key presented at the top of the screen (see supplemental Fig. 5). The workspace contains nine digits (1–9) and nine symbols, and the participant has 90 seconds to complete as many ‘symbol-number matches’ as possible. The outcome measure considered for analysis was the total number of correct responses in that 90 second timeframe. As this assessment captures several components of executive functioning, including working memory, attention and psychomotor speed, it was associated with the ECAS executive functioning subscale, which captures working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility and social cognition.

Memory match

Based on episodic paired-associate learning assessment, participants are required to learn the location - from 6 possible options - of abstract patterns (see supplemental Fig. 6). The assessment consists of several ‘encoding’ and ‘recall’ stages, which get more difficult until the participant’s ‘lives’ (i.e., chances to make an error and still continue) are consumed. Four lives are available per session.

On each encoding phase, the participant must commit to memory the location of some novel patterns, distributed among six on-screen locations. At the recall stage, these novel patterns plus all patterns presented on previous levels must be recalled. Patterns appear in the middle of the screen, and responses are given by tapping on a location. Feedback (correct vs. incorrect, plus a correction for incorrect responses) is given for each response. The outcome measure considered for analysis was the total number of correct responses. As this assessment measures short term encoding and recall, its benchmark was the Wechsler Memory Scale-III story recall test (total story items recalled correctly).

Double take

Based on the n-back test, participants are presented with a series of playing cards, with each card displaying 1 of 4 different possible shapes. For each trial, the participant must indicate whether the current card matches or doesn’t match the previous card, i.e. the card “one back” (see supplemental Fig. 7). The assessment includes 30 trials, of which 10 are match trials. The primary outcome measure was the percentage of correct responses across trials. As this assessment primarily measures working memory, it was benchmarked against the Digit Span Forward subtest (total correct responses).

Rapid response

This psychomotor assessment measures the speed and efficiency of a person’s response to a stimulus. Participants are instructed to place and hold their finger on the ready button (the fingerprint icon) and wait for the target above to be highlighted by a green circle (see supplemental Fig. 8). Once the target is highlighted the participant must swiftly move their finger and press it. This assessment includes 10 practice trials and 30 timed trials, and captures reaction time, movement time, and full response time. The primary outcome measure was mean reaction time. In participants with ALS, performance was expected to be influenced by motor impairment. The most comparable benchmark metric was the ‘time to complete’ the Rey Complex Figure test which similarly reflects psychomotor speed and motor functioning.

Astrotap

This assessment is a gamified iteration of the classic 2-stumulus visual oddball paradigm. Participants are presented with target stimuli (aliens and stars) and non-target stimuli (astronauts) across five levels (see supplemental Fig. 10). Participants are required to ‘shoot’ the targets by pressing the touchscreen in the bottom corners, and to not shoot the non-targets. Higher points are awarded for quicker responses and for hitting the star targets; and negative points are given if they hit a non-target. Within each session, 30 target stimuli and 70 non-target stimuli are randomly presented across five levels (blocks) of gameplay. The assessment lasts approximately 15 min. It measures reaction time, response accuracy and corresponding EEG correlates of decision-making including the P300 event-related potential (ERP). The primary outcome measure for this analysis was mean reaction time. As the assessment primarily indexes inhibitory control, it was benchmarked against the number of inhibition errors on the Colour–Word Interference Test (CWIT).

Go with the flow

In this gamified Flanker assessment, the stimuli presented are groups of orange fish, facing left or right (see supplemental Fig. 11). Participants must indicate whether the central fish is facing left or right. The central fish may be facing the same direction as the larger group of “flankers” (a ‘congruent’ trial), or the opposite direction (an ‘incongruent’ trial). The assessment consists of 150 trials divided into 5 blocks of 30 trials each, evenly split between congruent and incongruent trials. Flanking stimuli appear on-screen very slightly before the target stimulus. To elicit a suitable proportion of error trials to enable ERP analysis within a reasonable assessment duration, progressive time pressure is applied following consistent correct responses. The response window is shortened slightly with correct responses and lengthened slightly with incorrect or missed responses, and higher points are awarded for maintaining a ‘streak’ i.e., repeatedly not missing a response window. The assessment takes approximately 7 min to complete. The primary outcome measure for this assessment was the Balance Integration Score (BIS), a composite of both accuracy and response time. Further details on the BIS are provided in previous publications17,33. Given that the assessment engages inhibitory control, sustained attention, and cognitive flexibility, it was benchmarked against the number of errors on the inhibition/switching condition of the Colour–Word Interference Test.

Facial expression recognition test (FERT)

This third-party test was developed by P1vital Products and integrated into the NeuLogiq platform34 to measure the accuracy of recognising emotions in facial expressions. In this assessment, participants observe a series (60 trials) of male and female faces expressing various emotional expressions, including happy, sad, fearful, anger, disgust, and surprise (see supplemental Fig. 13). The faces are presented for a brief period (500ms), after which the participant must identify the emotion of the face from a list of emotion words. Facial emotion intensity is varied in 10 levels between neutral and high intensity, producing trials of varying difficulty for each emotion type. The primary outcome measure was the total percentage of correctly identified emotions. This was benchmarked against the number of correctly identified emotions on the Advanced Clinical Solutions – Emotion Recognition task. To explore depressive symptoms, we additionally examined the proportion of neutral faces that were misclassified as negative on the FERT, and compared this Patient Health Questionnaire-9 total score.

Lingo

Developed by Winterlight Labs and integrated into the NeuLogiq platform, this assessment consists of two parts: a semi-structured picture description assessment and an unstructured journaling assessment35. For picture description, participants describe what they see in a black and white illustration presented on the tablet screen (see supplemental Fig. 14). Twelve different illustrations are used across sessions to prevent stimulus familiarity effects. The outcome measure for this analysis was the Moving Average Type Token Ratio (MATTR) per 10 words of speech. This quantifies the number of unique words types in a 10 words segment of speech (e.g., The dog ran through the park and the dog ran home” has six unique words out of 10 total words, giving a score of 0.6). This measure is based on word diversity, and not execution time. As this assesses both language and executive components of verbal fluency, it was the verbal fluency assessment.

Statistical analysis

Feasibility was measured by calculating participants’ adherence to the study protocol, i.e., the percentage of sessions that the participant logged in, at home, to complete out of the total sessions requested (max 54 sessions over the total 8 months). Adherence was examined over time and within each 2-week cycle. Adherence was also compared across the different groups as well as attrition rate (ALS, FTD, and Control). Demographic and clinical factors associated with adherence and session data completeness were explored using Pearson’s correlations and Welch’s t-tests. Usability was measured by generating descriptive statistics of the System Usability Score and the bespoke questionnaire. A mean score greater than 70 on the SUS was considered to indicate good usability22.

The discriminatory power of the NeuLogiq platform at baseline was measured by carrying out one-way Analyses of Variance (ANOVAs). Baseline performance on digital platform assessments was defined as the average score of participants first 3 cycles (6 weeks) following a two-week familiarisation, which was included to reduce early learning effects and technical variability associated with unsupervised home-based testing. ALS, FTD, and control groups were compared using NeuLogiq digital assessments and benchmark neuropsychological measures targeting comparable cognitive functions (e.g., Digit Span Forward and Double Take both assess working memory). For each NeuLogiq task a small set of predefined summary outcomes was selected for analysis based on their correspondence to the primary outcome variables of the benchmark tests (e.g., total correct responses, total items recalled), and their relevance to neuropsychological domains common in ALS/FTD literature and clinical practise. This approach prioritised interpretability and reduced the number of statistical comparisons. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied on ANOVAs conducted across different cognitive domains, as these analyses addressed distinct domain-level hypotheses. Where a significant omnibus effect was observed, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with correction for multiple comparisons using the Holm–Bonferroni procedure. The estimate of effect size of both NeuLogiq platform and benchmark neuropsychological assessments were examined to compare the discriminatory power of both approaches.

Longitudinal cognitive change as assessed by both NeuLogiq and the similar benchmark full battery was measured using linear mixed effect models. Treating either NeuLogiq or benchmark full battery assessments as the outcome variable, the base model included random intercepts (participant ID), random slopes (participant ID | time), a main effect for time (days since baseline) and a main effect for group status (i.e., ALS, FTD, or control). In the model of interest (test model), a group*time interaction effect was added to test if groups had the same slope of decline over time (see model equations below). Given the relatively small sample size, models were intentionally kept simple. Although covariates such as age, sex, and baseline performance were considered, they were not included to avoid overfitting and unstable parameter estimates. Between-subject differences at baseline were partially accounted for through random intercepts, while within-subject longitudinal change was modelled via random slopes. Analyses were completed using pairwise deletion to maximise available data. A descriptive analysis of longitudinal performance was also carried out to examine the effectiveness of the NeuLogiq Platform in tracking cognition over time on a case-by-case basis.

Results

Each group was well matched in terms of age, education and handedness, however there was a notable difference in the gender distribution across groups. Feasibility and usability

Of the 28 participants enrolled in the study, 4 participants (3 plwALS, 1 control) withdrew from some study elements, resulting in an attrition rate of 14.3%. Among them, N = 2 withdrew (both plwALS) before completing the full NeuLogiq schedule, and N = 2 (1 plwALS, 1 control) completed the full NeuLogiq schedule, but later withdrew before completing the final neuropsychological assessments at T2. N = 1 cited lack of time as reason for withdrawal, N = 2 gave no reason, and N = 1 passed away before completing their final visit.

Overall adherence to the schedule of NeuLogiq digital assessment sessions was 41% in plwALS, 59% in plwFTD and 76% in healthy controls. Adherence declined from stage 1 of the study (0–4 months) to stage 2 of the study (4–8 months), and within each 2-week cycle (see Fig. 2). Of the 875 NeuLogiq sessions started by participants, 92% had complete data (96% in plwALS, 81% in plwFTD and 96% in healthy controls). Lower adherence was associated with younger age (r = 0.42, p = 0.03), greater apathy (r = -0.55, p = 0.009) and greater depressive symptoms at baseline (r = -0.42, p = 0.04). Greater apathy was also associated with fewer complete NeuLogiq sessions (r = -0.59, p = 0.004). General cognitive functioning (as measured by ECAS, TOPF and WASI-II FSIQ2) was not associated with adherence or completeness of data. PlwALS with spinal onset (M = 31%) had significantly fewer sessions started than those with bulbar onset (M = 71%), t9= 3.92, p = 0.003, however they also had significantly higher completeness of data in those sessions (M = 99% vs. M = 91%), t9= -4.09, p = 0.003.

At baseline, 100% of controls and 82% of plwALS rated the platform as easy to use, while plwFTD found it more difficult to learn how to use the platform (38% rating it as very difficult and 37% rating themselves as confident to start). Despite their low confidence at baseline, 67% of the FTD group required little or no support to complete each session, and 50% rated the headset and tablet as easy to use. At study completion, 66% of controls, 50% of plwFTD and 75% of plwALS rated their opinion of the technology used as either positive or very positive (see supplementary Fig. 15–17 for bespoke survey responses).

Comparison of NeuLogiq platform and benchmark measures over time

Linear mixed effects models examined cognitive performance over time, as measured by both NeuLogiq platform and benchmark cognitive assessments. The effect of interest in this analysis was the time*group interaction effect, as this tested if all groups had the same slope of performance over time. On all benchmark neuropsychological assessments, the addition of the time*group interaction term did not significantly improve the model, i.e., there was no statistically detectable difference in progression between ALS, FTD and Control groups in terms of their cognitive performance over time on benchmark assessments.

On four NeuLogiq digital assessments, the addition of a time*group interaction effect significantly improved the model: Go with the Flow (χ22= 11.97, p = 0.003), Rapid Response (χ22= 7.61, p = 0.02), FERT (χ22= 8.12, p = 0.02) and Lingo speech fluency (χ22= 11, p = 0.004), i.e., ALS, FTD, and Control groups had different slopes of performance over time (see Table 5 for full model summaries). For Go with the Flow and Rapid Response assessments, the significant interaction effects were driven by FTD group, who started with a lower baseline, and then improved over time relative to ALS and control groups (i.e., differential practise effects, see supplemental Figs. 18 and 19). For FERT (Fig. 3A) and Lingo speech fluency (Fig. 3B), the significant group*time interaction effects were driven by decline in the ALS group whilst controls and FTDs stayed stable or improved.

Case wise analysis of decline

Benchmark assessments (i.e. pen and paper assessments) indicated group level stability for ALS, FTD, and healthy control groups, however cognitive stability was not the case for all participants, with some showing considerable decline during the study (see supplemental Fig. 20). In the following exploratory results section, we present some individual case studies to review how individual cognitive change was captured by the various measures employed in the study. Three participants were selected for individual analysis, examining those who cognitively declined in more detail, and how this was captured by the NeuLogiq assessments relative to benchmark assessments (see Fig. 4).

Case 1 (plwALS)

This participant with ALS declined on several benchmark neuropsychological assessments relative to a matched control, with the largest decline seen on ECAS total, object comprehension and emotion-prosody matching. While there was a high degree of missing data for this participant on NeuLogiq assessments, decline was observed on Symbol Swap, Rapid Response and Lingo assessments, suggesting the NeuLogiq platform was able to capture the broad cognitive and motor decline this participant experienced (see Fig. 4A).

Case 2 (plwALS)

This participant with ALS had broad cognitive decline, with large effects observed on restricted verbal fluency, object comprehension, digit span, and emotion-prosody matching. Performance on the NeuLogiq platform appeared relatively stable across most assessments, except for naming of high intensity emotions, which showed moderate decline. This suggests the NeuLogiq assessments did not appear to identify fluency and working memory decline for this participant, but was able to capture emotion naming deficits that emerged (see Fig. 4B). However, it must be noted that not all the NeuLogiq metrics collected were analysed in this study, and that other outcomes may have identified these deficits.

Case 3 (plwFTD)

This participant with FTD declined on several benchmark neuropsychological assessments, with the largest declines seen on ECAS total, object comprehension, story recall, response inhibition and emotion recognition. Like many of participants with FTD, performance on many NeuLogiq assessments started from a low baseline and then showed improvement over time, possibly due to a change in speed/accuracy trade off strategy or a delayed practise effect. One assessment which did decline for this individual was Double Take performance, suggesting the NeuLogiq platform was able to identify working memory/attentional deficits, but was less able to capture comprehension, verbal memory and emotion recognition decline for this participant (see Fig. 4C). It is also notable that through regular repeated sampling, the Double Take assessment was able to capture considerable variation from session to session for this participant, something that was not possible using less frequent full battery assessment.

Discussion

The NeuLogiq platform demonstrated good usability and preliminary evidence of feasibility. Across all groups, data completeness was high, and overall adherence patterns varied by cohort: adherence was high among controls, moderate among participants with FTD, and modest among participants with ALS. Lower adherence in the ALS group compared to the FTD group may reflect the greater physical burden associated with ALS, including progressive motor impairment and fatigue. While adherence was lower than previous observational studies of the platform (which ranged from 70 to 93%)17,33,36, these studies were carried out in older healthy adults or people with mild Alzheimer’s dementia, and over different durations ranging from 6 weeks to 1 year. The high number of sessions started and completed, particularly by those with ALS, show that the platform is able to capture large quantities of data in people with severely debilitating conditions.

Within each 2-week cycle of three sessions, adherence declined, with the most pronounced decreases observed in plwALS. Overall assessment burden may have been too high for some participants, particularly those with ALS who were more physically impaired. Further studies, with controlled comparisons of different task loads, through reduced session length or total number of tasks, would be needed to better identify adherence limiting factors in this population. Within cycle drop-off meant that assessments placed at the end of each 2-week cycle, such as FERT and Memory Match, had more missing data, limiting their statistical power relative to other assessments. Apathy was strongly negatively associated with adherence (i.e., number of sessions stated) and completeness of data (i.e., completeness of data within sessions), highlighting the need for easy to use, engaging assessments, and positive reinforcement to maximize engagement. Future studies may opt for a more refined catalogue of NeuLogiq assessments to optimise the balance between patient burden and data collection, For example, a study of cognition dysfunction in ALS could prioritize the most sensitive assessments, such as emotion recognition and picture description as they appear to distinguish groups longitudinally.

Participants’ usability ratings of the NeuLogiq platform were in line with previous studies conducted in healthy controls, with high rating in all groups17,33. Interestingly, despite their increased motor impairment and lower adherence, the ALS group had the highest usability ranking of the three groups, scoring in the excellent range37. This was also reflected in participants responses to the bespoke survey, with the majority of plwALS rating NeuLogiq as very positive, easy to use, and requiring little support. The particularly positive rating in plwALS may indicate that they see the NeuLogiq platform as less burdensome than traditional in-person cognitive assessments, which often require long travel distances, long wait times at clinics and less engaging assessments. While plwALS reported finding the platform easy to use, their adherence levels indicate that usability alone is not sufficient to ensure sustained engagement. Several factors may help explain this discrepancy. PlwALS often experience rapidly progressive motor impairment, fatigue, and reduced endurance, which may make optional or repeated assessments more challenging to complete even when the interface itself is perceived as straightforward and enjoyable. These individuals increasingly need to prioritise essential daily activities over discretionary research assessments as disease burden increases. In addition, apathy (a core behavioural symptom of ALS) was strongly associated with reduced adherence, limiting participants capacity to engage, despite viewing the platform as usable. These considerations highlight that usability and feasibility are related but distinct constructs, and that improving adherence may require a minimal assessment burden and increased motivational features.

Baseline cross-sectional analyses revealed that the NeuLogiq platform could emulate benchmark neuropsychological assessments in comparing groups, with similar effect sizes and patterns of differences on post-hoc comparisons. Baseline differences between groups were also in line with a previous meta-analysis, with the largest effect sizes observed on social cognition, verbal fluency and executive functioning assessments, and with greater deficits in plwFTD compared to plwALS38. While the ALS group had lower mean scores than controls across most NeuLogiq and benchmark neuropsychological assessments, a larger sample size was likely required to detect statistically significant differences between these two groups. These preliminary findings suggest that the NeuLogiq platform may be a promising tool for conducting granular group-level analyses and neurocognitive profiling of plwALS or FTD at a single timepoint. However, the small sample size and reduced statistical power of this study warrants caution when interpreting these results.

Longitudinally, benchmark neuropsychological assessments showed no evidence of cognitive decline in any group, potentially due to limited sample size or an insufficient study duration. Even in larger cohort studies, evidence of cognitive decline in plwALS is mixed, with some showing decline across the whole cohort39,40 while others suggesting decline is limited to those with baseline cognitive impairment9,10. NeuLogiq assessments however, benefitting from a much larger number of repeated samples, found several significant differences between groups in their slopes of change over time. For two of these assessments, FERT and Lingo speech fluency, plwALS showed mild decline relative to plwFTD and control groups. Impairment of these functions relative to controls has been commonly observed in cross-sectional studies41–43, but studies showing real-world decline have been more rare9,10. The high number of repeated samples facilitated by the NeuLogiq platform, provided more statistical power, enabling us to capture this decline. However, given the small number of participants involved, these results should be interpreted with caution.

The decline in emotion recognition may be associated with reduced volume in the anterior cingulate cortex and right inferior frontal gyrus44 which imaging studies show to worsen over time45, and with reduced functional activity in the ventral associative bundles that connect occipital, temporo-limbic and orbitofrontal regions in the right hemisphere46. The decline observed on Lingo assessment was more pronounced than that of social cognition, and supports recent evidence that language deficits in ALS are largely attributable to speech dysfluency or lack of lexical diversity (i.e. the number of unique words used per sentence), rather than other language processes such as discourse productivity (absolute words spoken), discourse content (number of content units), syntactic complexity (mean words per sentence) or verb processing (use of verbs in sentences)47.

In the case of Go with the Flow and Rapid Response NeuLogiq assessments, the difference in slopes over time was due to the FTD group starting from a low baseline and then improving over time while the other groups stayed stable. Given that performance on these assessments is highly dependent on psychomotor speed and response timing, the observed improvement in the FTD group may reflect impaired timing and response inhibition, as observed in bvFTD48. This suggests that for these assessments, a more thorough instruction (emphasising speed from the beginning), or a more comprehensive familiarisation phase may be required. The absence of any observed decline in the FTD group may be attributed to the limited sample size, short follow-up duration, and the clinical and genetic heterogeneity of the participants.

Individual case analysis of three decliners (2 plwALS, 1 plwFTD) found that both modalities could detect individual change over time. This is well exemplified by the executive decline observed in Case 1 and the reduction in accuracy of emotion recognition seen is Case 2 using both modalities. However, there was not perfect agreement between the two assessment tools. For example, Case 2 and 3 showed decline on multiple benchmark measures but only declined on one NeuLogiq assessment. However, it is important to note that only a small fraction of the available NeuLogiq outcome measures was analysed in this study, and that other metrics not included may have detected decline. Furthermore, it must be noted that NeuLogiq assessments were developed prior to this study and not designed to be equivalent to this set of benchmark neuropsychological measures used in this study. Thus, the differences in individual trajectories may simply reflect the heterogeneity of cognitive decline within these populations, rather than lack of sensitivity of either modality.

A limitation of the present study is the potential for selection bias related to technology familiarity. As we did not systematically record reasons for non-participation, we cannot determine how many participants declined specifically due to concerns about using digital tools. In our sample, 38% of plwFTD indicated that they felt “not confident at all” using such technology (see supplemental Fig. 15), indicating our sample did not consist exclusively of highly technology-savvy individuals and that low confidence with technology did not automatically preclude participation. In addition, the small sample size limits the precision of our estimates and restricts the generalizability of the usability and adherence findings to the broader ALS and FTD populations, particularly those with more severe disease or lower digital literacy.

As neuropsychological assessment location (hospital/at-home) was confounded with disease severity and group membership, we did not compare performance across settings. While standardised administration procedures were used across locations, assessment setting represents a potential source of variability that should be considered when interpreting benchmark performance. The small sample size also limited the complexity of the longitudinal models and precluded adjustment for additional covariates such as age, sex, and baseline cognitive performance. Future studies with larger cohorts will be better positioned to incorporate these factors and more fully characterise sources of inter-individual variability in cognitive trajectories.

Overall, these findings provide proof-of-concept that the NeuLogiq platform can capture meaningful neuropsychological data, relevant to the heterogeneity of the ALS-FTSD phenotype. While limited in sample size, this study benefits from very rich repeated sampling, allowing for in-depth characterization of individual participants, and increased statistical power to detect longitudinal change. This study was limited by incomplete longitudinal ALSFRS-R data, restricting our ability to compare cognitive data with disease progression. PlwALS and plwFTD found the platform enjoyable to use and tolerated a high volume of sessions despite their illness. Effect sizes of cross-sectional comparisons were similar between NeuLogiq and benchmark neuropsychological assessments and there was some evidence that the platform can capture speech fluency and emotion recognition decline in plwALS, offering potential utility as a digital biomarker of cognitive decline. Future work will investigate the EEG data collected in this study, to detect electrophysiological correlates of cognitive dysfunction, and further validate biomarkers that are scalable. Together, these findings highlight the promise of remote self-administered assessments to empower care decisions and enable quantitative and objective endpoints in the development of novel therapies for people with ALS and FTD.
