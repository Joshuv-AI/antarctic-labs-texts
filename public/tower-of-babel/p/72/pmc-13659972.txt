Clinical and radiological presentations of pulmonary hydatid cysts in Yemen: a retrospective cross-sectional study at a tertiary center

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary hydatid cysts caused by Echinococcus granulosus represent a significant health burden in endemic regions such as Yemen. Limited contemporary data are available on the clinical and radiological characteristics of this disease in the Yemeni population. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical presentation, radiological features, surgical management, and early outcomes of pulmonary hydatid cysts at a tertiary center in Sana’a, Yemen.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Al-Thawra Modern General Hospital between January 2019 and March 2023. The medical records of 114 patients with surgically confirmed pulmonary hydatid cysts were analyzed. Demographic data, clinical presentation, radiological findings, surgical procedures, and early postoperative complications were collected using structured questionnaires. Background

Pulmonary hydatid cysts, caused by the larval stage of the tapeworm Echinococcus granulosus, are among the most geographically widespread zoonotic diseases affecting humans [1]. E. granulosus resides in the small intestine of definitive hosts (dogs and other canids), where gravid proglottids release eggs that are excreted in feces. Intermediate hosts (sheep, cattle, and goats) become infected by ingesting these eggs, after which oncospheres hatch in the intestine, penetrate the intestinal wall, and migrate via the bloodstream primarily to the liver and lungs, where they develop into hydatid cysts [2]. Humans are accidental intermediate hosts, typically infected through contact with dogs or consumption of contaminated food or water.

This disease poses significant medical, social, and economic burdens, particularly in endemic regions, and affects over one million people worldwide [3]. The parasite has the highest prevalence in parts of North Africa, Asia, South America, and Australia, with an annual human incidence exceeding 50 per 100,000 in endemic areas [2].

Yemen, located on the Arabian Peninsula, is a hyperendemic region for hydatid disease owing to its pastoral economy, close human-animal contact, and poor sanitation. The disease is particularly prevalent in rural areas, where livestock farming is the primary occupation, and traditional practices facilitate parasite transmission [4]. Previous studies from Yemen have documented the widespread nature of this parasitic infection, with significant case loads reported in major governmental hospitals in Sana’a [5].

Diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical presentation and imaging results. Chest radiography is the initial screening modality, and typically reveals well-defined round opacities. Computed tomography (CT) is the principal diagnostic tool, allowing for detailed characterization of cyst size, number, location, and complications [6]. Ultrasonography, which is primarily used for hepatic cysts, may occasionally demonstrate pleural-based or peripheral lung cysts [6].

The clinical presentation of pulmonary hydatid cysts varies considerably depending on the cyst size, location, and complications. While small, intact cysts may remain asymptomatic for years, larger cysts or those with complications can present with respiratory symptoms, including cough, dyspnea, chest pain, and hemoptysis [7, 8]. Radiological diagnosis reveals characteristic features such as well-defined cystic lesions with varying internal architectures [9]. Classic radiological signs include the Escudero-Nemerow sign (crescent sign) indicating impending rupture, Camelot sign (water lily sign) representing a detached endocyst, serpent sign representing floating membranes, and air crescent sign indicating bronchial communication [10].

Surgical resection remains the gold standard treatment. Although video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has gained popularity in some centers [11], open thoracotomy with endocystectomy and capitonnage remains the predominant approach in many endemic regions, particularly for complicated cysts, where the risk of intraoperative rupture necessitates meticulous isolation [12].

Despite the recognized burden of hydatid disease in Yemen, there is a paucity of recent comprehensive data on the clinical and radiological presentation of pulmonary hydatid cysts in the Yemeni population. Most available studies are either outdated or focus on general hydatid disease rather than pulmonary manifestations. This knowledge gap hampers the development of evidence-based diagnostic and treatment guidelines tailored to the local disease context of the region.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical and radiological presentations of pulmonary hydatid cysts at a major tertiary center in Sana’a, Yemen, over 4 years. The specific objectives included determining the sociodemographic characteristics of affected patients, documenting clinical presentations, characterizing radiological findings (including cyst number, size, location, peri-cyst changes, and classic signs), and evaluating surgical management outcomes and early postoperative complications.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted at the Al-Thawra Modern General Hospital (TMGH) in Sana’a, Yemen. TMGH is a tertiary referral center with a specialized thoracic surgery unit and serves as one of the primary hospitals for complex thoracic procedures in the country.

Study period and population

This study covered a four-year and three-month period from January 1, 2019, to March 31, 2023. The source population comprised all patients admitted to the thoracic surgery unit with a diagnosis of pulmonary hydatid cysts during the study period.

Participant selection

A total of 140 patient records were initially identified in hospital databases. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) male or female patients up to 60 years of age, (2) patients with a clinical history and radiological findings consistent with pulmonary hydatid cysts; and (3) patients who underwent surgical intervention for pulmonary hydatid cysts. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with recurrent hydatid lung disease, defined as any new hydatid cyst presenting after a previously confirmed complete surgical clearance, regardless of laterality; (2) patients who did not undergo surgical treatment; (3) patients with incomplete medical records or insufficient data for analysis; and (4) patients who were unreachable for telephone follow-up or refused to participate in follow-up.

After applying these criteria, 26 records were excluded: 8 for recurrent disease, 12 for incomplete data, and 6 for refusal to participate in telephone follow-up. The final study sample comprised 114 patients with complete data available for analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of patient selection for the study. Flowchart illustrating the identification, screening, and inclusion processes. Of the 140 identified records, 26 were excluded due to recurrent disease (n = 8), incomplete medical records (n = 12), or refusal to participate in telephone follow-up (n = 6), resulting in a final study sample of 114 patients.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed based on prior research. The questionnaire captured (1) sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, residence, occupation, and social habits), (2) clinical presentation (symptoms including cough, dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, fever, sputum production, nausea/vomiting, loss of appetite, and weight loss), (3) radiological findings (number, size, location, condition of cysts, and peri-cyst changes including mass effect, empyema, pleural effusion, and parenchymal infiltrate), (4) surgical procedures performed, and (5) early postoperative complications and duration of hospitalization.

All patients underwent chest radiography as an initial screening modality (Fig. 2). CT was performed for definitive diagnosis and preoperative planning using a 16-slice CT scanner (Siemens Somatom Emotion; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with the following protocol: slice thickness 1–2 mm, pitch 1.2, 120 kV, and automatic tube current modulation. Intravenous contrast was administered selectively when additional evaluation was required. Ultrasonography was primarily used to identify associated hepatic cysts. MRI is not routinely used for the diagnosis of pulmonary hydatid cysts at our institution.

Surgical procedures

All pulmonary hydatid cyst procedures in this cohort were performed via open thoracotomy. Although our center is equipped with VATS capability and utilizes it for procedures such as thymectomy [13], all hydatid cyst procedures were performed using an open approach. This is due to the high risk of cyst rupture and spillage during manipulation, necessitating meticulous isolation with hypertonic saline-soaked packs and careful controlled endocystectomy, for which our team has extensive experience with the open approach [11, 12]. VATS may offer advantages in terms of reduced postoperative pain and faster recovery in selected cases [11], but the open approach remains the preferred method for complicated cysts in our institution.

Data were extracted from hospital medical records archives. Additional information was obtained by telephone from patients using their recorded contact information. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee for the retrospective review of medical records. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants for the telephone follow-up component of the study.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages for the categorical variables. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for all proportions using the Wilson score method with a continuity correction. Multiple-response analysis was used for variables in which patients could provide multiple responses, such as clinical symptoms and radiological findings. Chi-square tests were used to examine the associations between categorical variables, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, Sana’a University, Sana’a, Yemen (approval no. SU-2023-085). Administrative approval and permission to access patient medical records were granted by the administration of Al-TMGH. All data were anonymized to ensure patient confidentiality. As this was a retrospective study of medical records, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. Verbal informed consent was obtained for telephone follow-ups.

Results

Participant characteristics

The final study sample comprised 114 patients with surgically confirmed pulmonary hydatid cysts. The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. A marked male predominance was observed, with 82 males (71.9%; 95% CI: 63.1%-79.4%) and 32 females (28.1%; 95% CI: 19.9%-36.9%). The largest age group was 19–30 years (n = 49, 43.0%; 95% CI: 34.3%-52.2%). Most patients resided in urban areas (n = 86, 75.4%; 95% CI: 66.8%-82.4%) rather than in rural areas (n = 28, 24.6%; 95% CI: 17.6%-33.2%). Occupation analysis revealed that 35.1% (95% CI: 26.9%-44.2%) were workers or laborers, 33.3% (95% CI: 25.3%-42.4%) were students, and 21.9% (95% CI: 15.3%-30.4%) were housewives. A history of khat chewing was reported by 57 patients (50.0%; 95% CI: 41.0%-59.0%), with no significant association found between khat use and any clinical symptoms (P > 0.05).

Clinical presentation

The clinical findings at the time of diagnosis are shown in Table 2. Respiratory symptoms were almost universal among the patients. Five patients (4.4%; 95% CI: 1.9%-9.8%) were completely asymptomatic at presentation and were diagnosed incidentally on imaging performed for other reasons. The most common presenting symptom was cough, reported in 102 patients (89.5%; 95% CI: 82.5%-93.9%), followed by dyspnea (n = 87, 76.3%; 95% CI: 67.7%-83.2%), sputum production (n = 81, 71.1%; 95% CI: 62.2%-78.6%), and chest pain (n = 74, 64.9%; 95% CI: 55.8%-73.1%). Constitutional symptoms, such as fever, were also frequent (n = 62, 54.4%; 95% CI: 45.2%-63.2%). Hemoptysis was noted in 36 patients (31.6%; 95% CI: 23.8%-40.6%), and the pathognomonic sign of expectorating grape-like material was reported by nine patients (7.9%; 95% CI: 4.2%-14.3%). Loss of appetite was reported by 15 patients (13.2%; 95% CI: 8.1%-20.7%), weight loss by 13 (11.4%; 95% CI: 6.7%-18.7%), and nausea/vomiting by 3 (2.6%; 95% CI: 0.9%-7.4%).

Surgical management and outcomes

Surgical procedures were performed by a dedicated team of thoracic surgeons led by Dr. Mohammed Al-Shehari and Dr. Yasser Abdurabo Obadiel, who performed the majority of the cases, with additional procedures performed by other surgeons in the thoracic surgery unit. The majority of surgeries were elective (n = 109, 95.6%), with five patients (4.4%) requiring emergency surgery due to complications such as empyema or severe respiratory compromise. Preoperative albendazole was administered to 10 patients (8.8% of total patients). Conservative lung-sparing surgery was the primary treatment. Capitonnage was performed in 104 patients (91.2%; 95% CI: 84.6–95.2%). Simple endocystectomy with capitonnage (without fistula closure) was performed in 35 patients (30.7%; 95% CI: 22.8%–39.8%), and an additional 4 patients (3.5%; 95% CI: 1.4%–8.6%) underwent simple capitonnage with fistula closure. Meanwhile, 65 patients (57.0%; 95% CI: 47.8%–65.8%) required complex capitonnage procedures, including additional decortication, drainage, or fistula closure. Non-capitonnage procedures were performed in 10 patients (8.8%; 95% CI: 4.8%–15.4%): endocystectomy alone (n = 3), endocystectomy with drainage (n = 2), wedge resection (n = 1), lobectomy (n = 1), right thoracotomy with endocystectomy (n = 1), concurrent liver endocystectomy via right thoracotomy (n = 1), and decortication alone (n = 1). Single lobectomy was performed for a massive, destroyed solitary cyst; no patient with multiple cysts underwent lobectomy (Table 5).

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive data on pulmonary hydatid cysts from a major tertiary center in Yemen over the past four years. Our findings confirmed that the disease predominantly affects young males from urban areas and is characterized by respiratory symptoms. The radiological pattern shows a predominance of solitary, intact cysts, and surgical management demonstrates excellent outcomes with low complication rates when performed in specialized centers.

The marked male predominance (71.9%) observed in our study is consistent with several regional studies but exceeds the proportions reported in some international studies. Sheikhy et al. [14] reported a 55% male predominance in Iran, whereas Abdennadher et al. [15] found a more balanced sex distribution (51.51% males) in Tunisia. Uchikov et al. [16] reported a male-to-female ratio of 2:1 in their Bulgarian series. The higher male predominance in our study may reflect occupational and cultural factors specific to Yemen, where men are more likely to engage in livestock-related activities and have greater exposure to infected animals than women. However, it should be noted that in some Middle Eastern series, female predominance has been observed in extrahepatic pulmonary hydatid disease, suggesting that sex distribution may vary according to organ involvement and regional factors [17].

The concentration of cases in the young adult age group (19–30 years, 43.0%) aligns with findings from similar endemic regions. Aldahmashi et al. [18] reported an average age of 27.75 years in a multicenter study conducted in Yemen, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia, whereas Thapaliya et al. [19] reported a mean age of 31.11 years in Pakistan. This age distribution likely reflects the long incubation period of the disease and peak exposure during childhood and adolescence in endemic areas. The data were originally categorized during primary data collection based on distinct clinical age groups relevant to our hospital wards: infants (< 3 years), pediatrics/adolescents (3–18 years), young adults (19–30 years), and older adults (31–45 and 46–60 years), rather than continuous numerical ages.

An important finding of our analysis was that most patients (75.4%) resided in urban areas. This contrasts with previous reports from endemic regions, where hydatid disease is traditionally more prevalent in rural areas with livestock farming than in urban ones. This urban predominance in our study likely reflects several factors: (1) referral bias, as our institution is a tertiary center in the capital city of Sana’a, and urban residents have better access to specialized healthcare; (2) internal displacement due to the ongoing conflict in Yemen, which has led to significant migration from rural to urban areas, including Sana’a; and (3) potential underreporting from rural areas due to limited healthcare infrastructure. We acknowledge that this finding may not represent the true epidemiological distribution of pulmonary hydatid disease across Yemen, where the disease is likely more prevalent in rural agricultural communities [4, 5].

The high prevalence of khat chewing (50.0%) represents a purely sociodemographic descriptor of the study population. We wish to emphasize that our study did not establish a causal relationship between khat chewing and pulmonary hydatid cyst disease. Khat chewing was recorded because it is a common social habit in Yemen. Our statistical analysis confirmed no significant association between khat use and clinical symptoms or disease characteristics (P > 0.05). While some studies have explored the immunomodulatory effects of Catha edulis and its active alkaloid cathinone on cellular and humoral immune responses [20], no established biological mechanism has linked khat consumption to increased susceptibility to E. granulosus infection. This finding is consistent with the current understanding that hydatid disease transmission is primarily related to contact with infected canines and livestock, not khat consumption alone.

The clinical presentation in our study, dominated by cough (89.5%) and dyspnea (76.3%), is consistent with that reported in the literature for endemic regions. Sheikhy et al. [14] reported cough in 59.8% and dyspnea in 31.1% of patients, whereas Mohammad et al. [21] found cough in 44.6% of Indian patients. The higher symptom rates in our study may indicate more advanced disease at presentation, possibly due to delayed medical consultation or limited access to health care. Notably, five patients (4.4%) were asymptomatic and were diagnosed incidentally, which is consistent with the known natural history of hydatid disease, in which small, intact cysts may remain clinically silent for years.

The high prevalence of sputum production (71.1%) in our cohort is notable and likely reflects bronchial irritation from the cyst or an associated respiratory infection. This symptom has been variably reported in the literature and may be particularly relevant in endemic regions with high rates of respiratory comorbidities.

The presence of hemoptysis in 31.6% of our patients was notably higher than the 12–21% range reported in most studies [22]. This finding, along with the proportion of ruptured cysts (21.6%), suggests that many patients presented with complicated disease, emphasizing the need for early diagnosis and intervention in endemic areas.

The predominance of solitary cysts (85.1%) in our study is consistent with previous reports. Kocaman et al. [2] reported solitary cysts in the majority of their Turkish pediatric population. The right lung predominance (67.0%) aligns with anatomical factors related to bronchial anatomy and blood flow distribution. In the literature, the right lower lobe is often described as the most commonly affected site owing to its vertical bronchus and greater blood flow [10]; however, in our cohort, the right upper lobe was slightly more frequently involved (Fig. 4A).

The size distribution, with most cysts measuring 5–9.9 cm (62.8%), suggests that patients presented with moderately sized cysts that were symptomatic but not yet at the giant cyst stage. This pattern differs from that reported in some studies that reported higher proportions of giant cysts (> 10 cm), possibly reflecting differences in healthcare access and the timing of presentation [15].

Our study documented several important peri-cyst changes with clinical significance. The mass effect on the surrounding parenchyma was the most common finding (39.2%), reflecting the space-occupying nature of the cysts. Empyema was documented in 5.2% of cases, typically associated with cysts that ruptured into the pleural space. Pleural effusion (2.1%), pericystic parenchymal infiltrate (1.0%), and abscess formation (1.0%) are additional complications that may influence surgical planning and postoperative outcomes.

Classic radiological signs of pulmonary hydatid cysts, although not systematically documented in our retrospective records, deserve discussion because of their educational and diagnostic importance. The Escudero-Nemerow sign (crescent sign) appears as a radiolucent rim between the peri-cyst and endocyst when air penetrates between these layers, indicating impending rupture [10, 23]. The water-lily (Camelot) sign is virtually pathognomonic for hydatid disease, representing a floating detached endocyst within the peri-cyst [10, 23]. The serpent (whirl) sign represents crumpled endocyst membranes floating within the cystic fluid [23]. The cumbo (double-arch) sign appears with further air dissection as an air-fluid level within the endocyst, with a radiolucent rim between the peri-cyst and endocyst [23]. The dry cyst sign appears after complete expectoration of the cyst fluid and membranes, leaving an air-filled cavity [23]. These signs, when present on CT imaging, facilitate accurate diagnosis and may guide surgical timing.

Calcification of pulmonary hydatid cysts is notably rare compared to that of hepatic cysts, occurring in less than 1% of pulmonary cases [23]. No calcified cysts were identified in our series, which is consistent with this observation. The mechanism underlying this difference remains unclear but may be related to the lower calcium metabolism and different tissue environments in the lung compared to those in the liver [10].

The rupture rate of 21.6% for solitary cysts is approaches the lower end of the 24.7%–61% range reported in previous studies [24]. The predominant rupture into the bronchi (57.1%) versus the pleura (38.1%) has important clinical implications, as bronchial rupture typically presents with pathognomonic symptoms, such as hydatoptysis, whereas pleural rupture can lead to serious complications, including empyema, pneumothorax, and allergic reactions [23].

The differential diagnosis of pulmonary hydatid cysts includes several cavitary and cystic lung lesions. Lung abscesses typically present with thick, irregular walls and air-fluid levels without the characteristic well-defined spherical appearance of hydatid cysts. Cavitating tuberculosis usually shows upper lobe predominance, associated with satellite lesions and constitutional symptoms. Cavitating lung malignancies tend to have thicker nodular walls and may show contrast enhancements. Bronchogenic cysts are typically located in the mediastinum and do not exhibit crescent or water-lily signs. Pneumatoceles usually develop in the context of pneumonia or trauma and lack the classic signs of a hydatid disease. The presence of pathognomonic signs, such as the water-lily sign, together with an appropriate epidemiological context (endemic region, animal contact), usually enables an accurate diagnosis [25].

The high success rate of conservative surgical procedures (capitonnage in 91.2% of cases) demonstrates the effectiveness of lung-sparing techniques by experienced surgeons. This approach aligns with the current surgical philosophy, which favors parenchymal preservation, whenever possible [26]. The low rate of anatomic lung resection (lobectomy in 0.9% and wedge resection in 0.9%) compares favorably with other series and reflects both appropriate patient selection and surgical expertise. Notably, while Abdennadher et al. [15] required anatomical resection in 45.46% of giant cyst cases, our series achieved high success with parenchyma-preserving capitonnage (91.2%), even in a cohort with significant cyst diameters. Single lobectomy was performed for a massive, destroyed solitary cyst; no patient with multiple cysts underwent lobectomy.

While the majority of patients in our cohort presented with solitary cysts confined to the lung parenchyma, our series also highlights the efficacy of the open approach in managing exceptionally complex, multi-compartment presentations (Fig. 6). For instance, concurrent involvement of the lung, mediastinum, and pericardium poses a severe surgical challenge that traditionally dictates highly invasive procedures, such as a median sternotomy or staged bilateral thoracotomies. However, our experience demonstrates that a strategically planned single-site approach—such as utilizing a left posterolateral thoracotomy to navigate the aortopulmonary window—can provide adequate exposure to safely clear pulmonary, deep mediastinal, and pericardial cysts simultaneously. This technique significantly minimizes surgical trauma, bypasses the morbidity of a sternotomy, and underscores the necessity of advanced open thoracic expertise in endemic tertiary centers.

Although VATS has been increasingly adopted for pulmonary hydatid cyst surgery in some centers, with reported advantages of reduced postoperative pain and shorter hospital stays [11], our series exclusively utilized open thoracotomy. This decision was based on several factors: the high risk of cyst rupture during manipulation, the need for meticulous isolation with hypertonic saline-soaked packs, the complex nature of many of our cases (ruptured cysts, empyema, and giant cysts), and the extensive experience of our surgical team with the open approach. A recent randomized controlled trial by Kakamad [11] demonstrated that while VATS may provide benefits in terms of postoperative pain and return to work, intraoperative outcomes, including cyst rupture rates, were comparable between the approaches. The choice of surgical approach should be individualized based on cyst characteristics, surgeon expertise, and the available resources [12].

Preoperative albendazole was administered to 10 patients (8.8%). Although albendazole is the preferred medical treatment for hydatid disease [27], its role in the preoperative period for pulmonary cysts remains unclear. Aydin et al. [27] cautioned that albendazole is not recommended for intact cysts during the preoperative period because of its potential to weaken the cyst wall and increase the risk of rupture. Medical therapy is more commonly employed postoperatively to prevent recurrence or in cases in which surgery is not feasible [9, 27].

The overall complication rate of 7.9% and the absence of mortality in our series are excellent and compare favorably with the published literature reporting complication rates of up to 20% and mortality rates of 0–2% [28]. The most common complication of trapped lung (6.1%) is consistent with reports from other centers and typically resolves with appropriate postoperative management.

The short hospital stay (75.4% discharged within one week) reflects both the effectiveness of the surgical approach and good postoperative management protocols. This compares favorably with other series and has important implications for healthcare resource utilization in resource-limited settings.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. The retrospective design inherently carries the risk of information bias due to incomplete or inaccurate medical records. As a single-center study from a tertiary referral hospital, our results may not be generalizable to all of Yemen, and there may be a selection bias toward more severe or complicated cases that require referral to specialized centers.

The use of telephone follow-up for data collection could introduce recall bias, particularly for historical information on symptoms and exposure. The lack of complete serological data for all patients limited our ability to confirm the diagnosis, despite all cases being surgically confirmed. The descriptive nature of this study precludes the establishment of causal relationships, and the relatively short follow-up period does not provide information on the long-term outcomes or recurrence rates of the disease.

A significant limitation was the absence of systematic documentation of classic radiological signs (crescent, water-lily, and serpent signs) in the available records. These signs, while diagnostically important, were not consistently reported in radiology reports at the time of the imaging. Age was recorded in categorical ranges during primary data collection based on distinct clinical age groups relevant to our hospital wards rather than as continuous numerical values, precluding reclassification into standard 5- or 10-year demographic increments.

Clinical implications

Our findings have several important clinical implications. The predominant involvement of young, economically productive individuals highlights the socioeconomic impact of this disease in Yemen. The high symptom burden at presentation underscores the need for increased awareness among healthcare providers, particularly in urban areas where most patients now reside, to facilitate the early diagnosis and treatment of this condition.

The excellent surgical outcomes achieved at this specialized center support the centralization of care for complex cases and underscore the importance of surgical expertise in the management of pulmonary hydatid cysts. The low complication rates and short hospital stays demonstrate that these procedures can be safely performed in appropriately equipped centers, even in resource-limited settings such as ours.

Conclusions

Pulmonary hydatid cysts in Yemen predominantly affect young males from urban areas and present with typical respiratory symptoms, such as cough and dyspnea. Most cases involve solitary, intact cysts located in the right lung, although a significant proportion present with complications such as cystic rupture. Surgical management with capitonnage via open thoracotomy is safe and effective when performed in specialized centers, with excellent outcomes and low morbidity rates. These findings emphasize the importance of hydatid disease as a public health concern in Yemen and support the effectiveness of conservative surgical approaches by experienced surgeons in treating this condition. Future efforts should focus on prevention strategies, early diagnosis programs, ensuring access to specialized surgical care for affected populations, and systematic documentation of radiological findings using standardized reporting.
