Multi-omics analysis reveals distinct responses to light stress in photosynthesis and primary metabolism between maize and rice
Authors: Fengying Duan, Xia Li, Ze Wei, Jing Li, Caifu Jiang, Chengzhi Jiao, Shanshan Zhao, Yu Kong, Mengxiao Yan, Jirong Huang, Jun Yang, Yanmei Chen, Ralph Bock, Wenbin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
High-light (HL) stress is a major environmental factor that limits crop productivity. Maize (Zea mays) and rice (Oryza sativa), two key global crops, can both grow under HL intensities but differ in photosynthetic metabolism; maize is a C4 species, whereas rice is a C3 species. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying their responses to HL stress remain poorly understood. To systematically dissect how HL affects maize and rice growth, we conducted time-resolved multi-omics analyses, examining the transcriptome, translatome, proteome, and metabolome in response to HL treatment. Integration of this multi-omics approach with physiological analyses revealed that rice exhibits a more rapid response to HL stress than maize, with significant alterations in photosynthetic electron transport, energy dissipation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and primary metabolism. In contrast, the higher tolerance of maize to HL stress is primarily attributed to increased cyclic electron flow (CEF) and non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), elevated sugar and aromatic amino acid accumulation, and enhanced antioxidant activity during 4 h of HL exposure. Transgenic experiments further validated key regulators of HL tolerance; for instance, knockout of OsbZIP18 enhanced HL tolerance in rice, whereas overexpression of ZmPsbS in maize significantly boosted photosynthesis and energy-dependent quenching (qE) after 4 h of HL treatment, underscoring its role in protecting C4 crops from HL-induced photodamage. Taken together, these findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of HL stress tolerance in C4 versus C3 species and highlight a set of candidate genes for engineering improved HL tolerance in crops.

Introduction

As sessile organisms, plants have evolved remarkable plasticity to adapt to constantly changing environmental conditions. Light is the most dynamic and variable environmental factor for photoautotrophic organisms; it serves as both an energy source and a signaling factor that influences numerous cellular processes (Slattery et al., 2018). However, during many stages of the life cycle, most plants encounter high-light (HL) intensities that exceed their photosynthetic capacity, leading to stress and potential damage to chloroplasts and cells (Karpinski et al., 1999). Such stress disrupts the balance between photosystem activity and carbon fixation, impairs photosynthetic efficiency, and reduces crop yields (Long et al., 1994; Yamamoto et al., 2008; Li et al., 2020). In addition, excess excitation energy can result in reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, primarily at photosystem II (PSII) and photosystem I (PSI), which accelerates photoinhibition and hinders PSII repair cycles (Asada, 2006; Nishiyama et al., 2006; Raven, 2011).

Plants have evolved various acclimation mechanisms to mitigate HL stress, including modulation of the photosynthetic machinery at different timescales. For PSII, the dynamic degradation and de novo synthesis of the D1 protein are critical for maintaining photosynthetic activity (Edelman and Mattoo, 2008). Non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), which dissipates excess light energy as heat, is a rapid and flexible photoprotective response (Christa et al., 2017). NPQ includes several components, such as energy-dependent quenching (qE) (Krause et al., 1982), photoinhibitory quenching (qI) (Krause, 1988), state transition quenching (qT) (Krause and Weis, 1991), zeaxanthin-dependent quenching (qZ) (Nilkens et al., 2010), sustained antenna quenching (qH) (Brooks et al., 2013; Malnoë et al., 2018), and blue-light-dependent quenching induced by chloroplast movement (qM) (Takahashi and Badger, 2011; Cazzaniga et al., 2013; Li et al., 2018). Among these, qE is the fastest and most important; it is driven by thylakoid lumen acidification, which activates the PSII subunit S (PsbS) and the xanthophyll cycle (Krishnan-Schmieden et al., 2021). Lumen acidification promotes the accumulation of zeaxanthin, a key xanthophyll pigment, further enhancing qE (Bassi and Dall'Osto, 2021; Niyogi et al., 1998),. In contrast, qI is slowly induced and relaxed under long-term HL exposure, caused by photoinactivation of the D1 protein in the PSII reaction center and by zeaxanthin accumulation (Nilkens et al., 2010; Kress and Jahns, 2017). PsbS acts as a sensor of the low lumenal pH generated by photosynthetic electron transfer, triggers conformational changes in the PSII antenna, and ultimately activates NPQ. Increased expression of PsbS and xanthophyll cycle enzymes has been shown to enhance NPQ capacity, photosynthetic efficiency, and crop yields in species such as tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) and soybean (Glycine max) (Kromdijk et al., 2016; De Souza et al., 2022).

In addition to photoprotection, transcriptional and translational networks regulate the expression of genes encoding photosynthetic proteins, including antenna proteins and core components of the photosynthetic apparatus, enabling efficient acclimation to HL (Huang et al., 2019; Schuster et al., 2020). C4 plants, such as maize (Zea mays), exhibit higher photosynthetic efficiency and HL tolerance compared with C3 species, including rice (Oryza sativa), due to distinct biochemical and anatomical traits. Such traits include spatial separation of carboxylation and decarboxylation enzymes in mesophyll and bundle sheath cells, which optimizes energy utilization for carbon fixation under stress conditions (Blatke and Brautigam, 2019; von Caemmerer, 2021; Yabiku and Ueno, 2019). Although many HL responses are shared between C3 and C4 plants, critical differences exist in their regulatory mechanisms, including energy dissipation and acclimation processes.

Maize and rice, as members of the same plant family (Poaceae), provide ideal models for comparative studies of C3 and C4 photosynthesis. In this study, we conducted time-resolved multi-omics analyses, integrating transcriptomic, translatomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data to investigate the HL responses of these two crops over 4 h of HL treatment. Our results provide insights into NPQ-mediated photosynthetic remodeling, metabolic regulation, and antioxidant defenses, identifying key pathways and regulators of HL tolerance in both species. Genetic validation of several potential regulators highlights promising strategies for improving photosynthetic efficiency and HL tolerance through genetic engineering and crop breeding.

Results

Rice is more sensitive to HL stress than maize in photosynthetic electron transport and energy dissipation

To establish the physiological basis for a multi-omics analysis of HL stress responses, we first evaluated the photosynthetic responses of maize and rice to HL intensities. Hydroponically grown seedlings of both species were cultivated under a growth light intensity of 300 μmol photons m−2 s−1 until the five-leaf stage, then exposed to HL intensities of 1800–2000 μmol photons m−2 s−1 for 4 h (10 a.m. to 2 p.m.), simulating midday field conditions (Supplemental Figure 1A). To evaluate seedling sensitivity to HL exposure, we measured the maximum potential quantum efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), a proxy for reversible photoprotective downregulation and/or irreversible photodamage of PSII. Fv/Fm decreased more rapidly in rice than in maize, reaching 0.5 and 0.7, respectively, after 4 h of HL exposure (Supplemental Figure 1B). This observation indicated distinct photosynthetic responses to HL stress between the two species. Based on these findings, we selected the time points 0, 2, and 4 h for further physiological and multi-omics analyses.

To explore mechanistic differences, we analyzed photosynthetic responses in more detail. Dynamic changes of chlorophyll fluorescence were measured, including chlorophyll a fluorescence OJIP transient, photosynthetic electron transport rate (ETR), redox state of the plastoquinone (PQ) pool, maximum photooxidizable fraction of P700 (Pm), Fv/Fm, and NPQ. HL treatment increased fluorescence in the J phase of the OJIP curve similarly in both species, indicating comparable plastoquinone A (QA) reduction levels in PSII reaction centers (Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure 1C). However, fluorescence in the I phase significantly increased only in rice (Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure 1D), suggesting more pronounced impairment of electron transport from PSII to PSI in rice. Consistent with this finding, HL exposure induced opposite changes in ETR(II) and ETR(I) between the species (Supplemental Figure 2A and 2B). Accordingly, PQ pool reduction significantly increased in rice during HL treatment, whereas it remained at a relatively low level for 2 h in maize before increasing at 4 h (Figure 1B), indicating a more severe disruption of PQ-to-PSI electron transfer in rice. Similarly, Pm and Fv/Fm significantly decreased in rice after 2 h of HL treatment but were unaffected in maize until 4 h (Figure 1C and 1D). Moreover, the quantum yield of PSII [Y(II)] prominently decreased in rice but remained unaffected in maize under HL (Supplemental Figure 2C). Although the quantum yield of light-inducible energy dissipation [Y(NPQ)] decreased in both species, the non-regulated energy dissipation [Y(NO)] increased only in rice after 4 h of HL (Supplemental Figure 2D and 2E). These findings suggest that PSII photodamage occurred in rice but not in maize. We further investigated NPQ induction and relaxation dynamics in both species. In maize, NPQ was rapidly and strongly induced under HL, reaching its maximum within 200 s of actinic light (AL) exposure and relaxing to baseline within 150 s after AL switch-off. Conversely, rice displayed significantly reduced NPQ after 2 h of HL; after 4 h, NPQ failed to reach levels comparable to untreated plants (Figure 1E). These results suggest that NPQ induction and relaxation are severely compromised by HL in rice. Notably, the major NPQ component, qE, sharply decreased in rice after 4 h of HL, whereas residual NPQ increased. In contrast, both NPQ components remained stable in maize (Figure 1F). These findings suggest that maize has a stronger capacity to dissipate excess absorbed light energy as heat than rice. Gas-exchange measurements further showed that the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), the maximum rate of carboxylation (Vcmax), and the maximum rate of electron transport (Jmax) significantly decreased in rice but were only slightly affected in maize after 4 h of HL treatment (Figure 1G–1I). Multi-omics analysis reveals distinct molecular responses to HL in maize and rice

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the differential responses of maize and rice to HL, we conducted a comprehensive multi-omics analysis, including transcriptomics, translatomics (ribosome profiling), proteomics, and metabolomics (Figure 2A). In general, both species displayed the strongest responses at the transcriptomic level, followed by translatomics, proteomics, and metabolomics (Figure 2B). Partial least-squares projection to latent structures analysis demonstrated that samples from the same time point clustered closely together across all gene expression levels (transcription, translation, and protein accumulation) in both maize and rice (Supplemental Figure 3A–3C). Principal-component analysis of metabolite profiles revealed distinct metabolic responses to HL in maize and rice, representing 59.59% of the variance along PC1 (Supplemental Figure 3D). Next, we characterized the global responses of rice and maize to HL by calculating the number of differentially expressed variables in each omics dataset (Supplemental Figure 4; Supplemental Table 1). After 4 h of HL exposure, 19.18% and 25.92% of genes were transcriptionally upregulated in maize and rice, respectively (Supplemental Figure 4A). At the translational level, fewer genes were upregulated (9.53% in maize and 10.77% in rice; Supplemental Figure 4B; Supplemental Table 1). Specifically, maize showed 507 and 947 upregulated genes at 2 and 4 h, whereas rice showed 925 and 1681 upregulated genes at these time points. At the proteomic level, maize exhibited a higher proportion of downregulated proteins (64.34%) compared with rice (24.21%), corresponding to 585 and 684 downregulated proteins in maize and 65 and 202 in rice at 2 and 4 h, respectively (Supplemental Figure 4C; Supplemental Table 1). This pattern suggests a faster rate of protein turnover in maize under HL stress. Similarly, a greater accumulation of metabolites was observed in maize than in rice after 2and 4 h of HL exposure (Supplemental Figure 4D).

Coordinated responses across multi-omics levels

To identify regulatory mechanisms underlying the HL response, we searched for genes exhibiting coordinated changes across transcription, translation, and protein-accumulation levels (Figure 2C; Supplemental Dataset 1). This analysis revealed 33 maize genes and 23 rice genes that were significantly altered across all three levels (adjusted [adj.] p < 0.05). When analyzing co-regulation at two levels, we identified 922 maize genes with coordinated transcriptional and translational responses, 151 genes with coordinated transcriptional and protein-accumulation responses, and 45 with coordinated translational and protein-accumulation responses. The corresponding numbers in rice were 1748, 148, and 27 genes, respectively (Figure 2C).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of genes significantly altered at two or more levels revealed enrichment of terms related to photosynthesis, ROS response, and response to temperature stimuli (false discovery rate < 0.01; p < 0.05), indicating that these processes are directly affected by HL stress in both species (Supplemental Figure 5). Additionally, genes involved in thylakoid membrane formation and photosystem biogenesis were enriched in both maize and rice, consistent with the strong impact of HL on the light reactions of photosynthesis (Supplemental Figure 5). Interestingly, rice-specific enrichment was observed for genes involved in translation, protein folding, carboxylic acid metabolism, and transmembrane protein transport, suggesting unique molecular adaptations in rice to HL stress (Supplemental Figure 5B).

Photosynthesis-related genes respond differently to HL in maize and rice

As described above, numerous photosynthesis-related genes exhibited pronounced responses to HL stress in both maize and rice (Supplemental Figure 5). Among these, 71 and 42 nucleus-encoded photosynthesis-related genes were differentially regulated in maize and rice, respectively (Figure 3). Specifically, 36 genes in maize and 28 genes in rice were downregulated at the transcript level, while only two rice genes (OsPsbQ3 and OsPsbS2) were upregulated after 2 h of HL treatment (Figure 3B). Closer inspection revealed that maize required 4 h of HL exposure for significant downregulation of ZmATPF1D2, ZmATPF1G, and several PSI and PSII genes (ZmPsa, ZmLhca, ZmPsb, and ZmLhcb). In rice, similar genes (OsNDHB4, OsPsa, OsLhca, OsPsb, and OsLhcb) were repressed after only 2 h of HL treatment (Figure 3A, 3B, and 3E–3G). To further investigate the relationship between translation and protein accumulation during HL stress, we compared ribosome profiling (translatomic) data with differentially expressed protein datasets. In maize, both translational activity and protein levels of photosynthesis-related genes were simultaneously reduced at 2and 4 h of HL. In contrast, in rice, the abundance of photosynthetic proteins predominantly increased after 2 h of HL; translational activity remained largely unchanged (Figure 3A, 3B, 3E, and 3H). Collectively, these results suggest that maize primarily downregulates photosynthetic gene expression across all levels in response to HL, whereas rice exhibits more heterogeneous regulatory responses across transcriptional, translational, and proteomic levels.

Important roles of primary metabolism during HL stress

To better understand the metabolic adaptations underlying HL-induced changes in photosynthesis, we analyzed primary metabolic pathways—glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and amino acid biosynthesis—by integrating metabolomic data with transcriptomic, translatomic, and proteomic datasets (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 6). HL stress resulted in increased accumulation of several TCA cycle intermediates, including α-ketoglutarate, fumarate, and malate, in both species. Consistent with these findings, genes encoding TCA cycle enzymes were strongly upregulated—five in rice and eleven in maize (Supplemental Figure 6A). However, sugars and glycolytic intermediates (e.g., glucose, fructose, galactose, maltose, and glucose-6-phosphate) showed greater accumulation in HL-treated maize than in rice. Similarly, the accumulation of amino acids, such as tryptophan, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tyramine, was more pronounced in maize (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 6B and 6C). We also detected several metabolites with contrasting accumulation patterns between maize and rice during HL stress. For instance, trehalose rapidly accumulated in maize within 0.5 h of HL exposure but continuously decreased in rice over 4 h. Amino acid metabolism also showed differential responses between species. In maize, the levels of aromatic amino acids (AAAs)—phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan—were substantially elevated, with strong upregulation of ZmCMU (chorismate mutase), a key gene in AAA biosynthesis, after 4 h of HL (Figure 4). In contrast, both AAA levels and the expression of the OsCM (chorismate mutase) gene were significantly reduced in rice under HL stress (Figure 4). Additionally, nitrogen metabolism in maize was enhanced, as evidenced by increased glutamine and glutamate—key intermediates in the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase (GS-GOGAT) cycle—as well as the accumulation of proline, putrescine, and spermine, all of which are common stress markers in plants (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 6D).

Genes encoding antioxidant defense proteins, including glutathione-S-transferase (GST) and peroxidases (Prx), were significantly altered in both species across multiple omics datasets during HL treatment (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 6E). Interestingly, nearly 50% of these genes were transcriptionally upregulated in maize, whereas approximately half were downregulated in rice under HL stress (Figure 4). Considering the pivotal roles of glutathione-S-transferase and peroxidases in mitigating photo-oxidative stress, these findings suggest that antioxidative defense capacity substantially differs between maize and rice. Although many heat shock proteins (HSPs) and their corresponding mRNAs were detected in our datasets, their abundance did not significantly change in response to HL (p < 0.05), indicating that heat stress effects were negligible under the HL treatment conditions (Supplemental Figure 7).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that maize and rice exhibit distinct responses to HL stress at multiple molecular levels. Enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, and antioxidative defense systems are tightly associated with the HL response of maize; rice displays more heterogeneous regulatory changes across transcription, translation, and protein levels.

Efficient remodeling of electron transport and NPQ formation protect maize from photodamage under HL stress

As shown in Figure 3, multiple genes and proteins with significantly altered abundances during HL stress were mapped to photosynthetic electron transport processes, including linear electron flow (LEF) and cyclic electron flow (CEF). Notably, a reduction in LEF coincided with an increase in CEF. For instance, the expression levels of CEF genes, including ZmPetF5/6, ZmPnsB1-2, ZmPnsB2, and ZmPGR5-like2/3, were upregulated after 2 or 4 h of HL, corresponding to a two- to three-fold increase in CEF in maize (Figure 3D–3G; Supplemental Figure 8A). In rice, however, CEF increased 1.5-fold after 2 h of HL but returned to pre-stress levels by 4 h. These changes paralleled the expression patterns of OsNDHB4, OsPGR5, and OsPGR5-like transcripts, as well as the OsNDHM protein (Figure 3G; Supplemental Figure 8A). Considering the pivotal role of the PsbA (D1) protein in the repair of photodamaged PSII, we further examined its expression level by western blot analysis. Our results showed that D1 protein levels decreased more slowly in maize than in rice under HL (Supplemental Figure 8B), highlighting the essential roles of electron transport and PSII repair in mitigating photodamage in maize.

Despite the general downregulation of photosynthesis-related genes under HL stress, PsbS and PetF were upregulated in both maize and rice (Figure 3B–3D, and 3I). The increased expression of PsbS is consistent with the physiological acclimation of maize and rice to HL, given that the PsbS protein and xanthophyll cycle are major regulators of NPQ. In maize, ZmPsbS was highly induced after 4 h of HL, whereas in rice, OsPsbS1 and OsPsbS2 were significantly induced after 2 h (Figure 3I). Among the three key xanthophyll cycle enzymes—β-carotene hydroxylase (Chs; ZmHYD4, ZmHYD5, OsBCH1, OsHYD3), violaxanthin de-epoxidase (VDE; ZmVDE1, OsVDE), and zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP; ZmZEP1, ZmZEP2, OsZEP1)—Chs and VDE were transcriptionally upregulated in maize but remained unaffected in rice. Conversely, ZEP was significantly upregulated only in rice (Supplemental Figure 8C–8F). These results suggest that increased expression of xanthophyll cycle enzymes contributes to HL tolerance in maize by maintaining a high NPQ level.

To further examine the role of NPQ in the differential HL responses, we generated PsbS knockout (KO) mutants (psbs) and overexpression (OE) lines in maize. Under normal light conditions, ZmPsbS-OE plants displayed enhanced growth characteristics, including longer roots, increased plant height, and higher biomass compared with wild-type (WT) plants, whereas ZmPsbS-KO plants exhibited opposite phenotypes (Figure 5A; Supplemental Figure 9A). Under HL stress, the Fv/Fm values remained stable in ZmPsbS-OE plants but significantly declined in ZmPsbS-KO plants after 2 and 4 h of HL (Figure 5B). This suggests that PsbS plays a critical role in HL tolerance because reduced Fv/Fm values in HL-treated psbs mutants likely reflect impaired photoprotection. Other photosynthesis-related parameters, such as photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate, followed similar trends, with the exception of intercellular CO2 concentration (Figure 5C; Supplemental Figure 9B). Interestingly, maize psbs mutants displayed a pronounced increase in the I phase of the OJIP curve compared with the WT, mimicking the HL response observed in rice (Figure 5D; Supplemental Figure 1C and 1D). In contrast, maize PsbS-OE lines showed no significant alterations in J or I phases during HL treatment (Figure 5D), indicating that PsbS positively regulates photoprotection. Consistent with these findings, both ETR(II) and ETR(I) decreased in psbs mutants and increased in OE lines after 4 h of HL (Supplemental Figure 9C and 9D). Furthermore, analyses of NPQ induction and relaxation revealed that NPQ, particularly its qE component, rapidly declined in psbs mutants under HL stress but increased in OE lines and WT plants (Figure 5E). Integrated correlation networks identify key components in HL-responsive regulation of gene expression in maize and rice

To identify candidate genes responsible for HL tolerance and elucidate their interrelationships, we utilized machine-learning methods to construct gene-correlation networks using our multi-omics datasets. Four distinct machine-learning algorithms were employed to determine the weight values of the variables (i.e., genes and proteins) that respond to HL. For this purpose, non-redundant genes from the top 10 000 interactions were clustered based on weight values using the Infomap method (Supplemental Figure 10). The network analysis revealed that numerous transcription factors (TFs) and photosynthesis-related genes act as hub nodes in both maize and rice. For example, the maize network contains 20 TF hub genes representing diverse families, including DOF, CO-like, AP2-EREBP, bHLH, NAC, MYB, TCP, bZIP, WRKY, ERF, and NF-Y TFs. The rice network contains nine TF hub genes, including GRAS, zinc finger, MYB, bZIP, MAD, and NF-Y TFs. In addition, a greater number of photosynthesis-related hub genes was identified in maize; only a few key genes were found in rice, such as OsPsbS2 (Os04g0690800), chlorophyll a/b binding proteins (Os11g0242800, Os01g0720500, Os07g0577600), fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (Os11g0171300), and malate dehydrogenase (Os08g0434300) (Supplemental Dataset 3).

In maize, the analysis revealed seven gene modules integrated into the correlation networks. Three biological processes—photosynthesis, oxidative stress, and metabolic pathways—were highly enriched (Supplemental Figure 10A and Supplemental Dataset 2). Quantitative analysis identified 424 node genes that exhibited significant changes during the 4-h HL stress period (Supplemental Dataset 3). These changes were primarily observed at the transcriptional level (269 of 450 genes) and in protein accumulation (184 of 450 proteins). Among these node genes, 14 showed simultaneous decreases at the transcriptional, translational, and protein-accumulation levels. These included genes involved in photosynthesis, oxidative stress responses, and central metabolism, such as ZmPsb27 (Zm00001d029049), ZmPsaD1 (Zm00001d013039), ZmPsaE1 (Zm00001d005446), peroxidase 5 (ZmPrx5, Zm00001d037550), an auxin-repressed protein gene (Zm00001d029102), and PDK regulatory protein1 (ZmPDRP1, Zm00001d006520). Notably, genes encoding fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (Zm00001d023559) and DNA-directed RNA polymerase (Zm00001d019553) were identified as key nodes connected with multiple modules. Moreover, ZmPsaE genes acted as central hubs in the module associated with photosynthetic electron transport and PSII repair; ZmPsaD1 was part of a module linked to chlorophyll biosynthesis and PSI light harvesting (Supplemental Figure 10A), suggesting that these genes play regulatory roles in the HL response in maize. Such results highlight the tight linkage and extensive reprogramming of photosynthesis and carbon metabolism during HL stress.

In rice, the analysis identified six modules within the gene-correlation networks. Similar to maize, these modules were functionally associated with photosynthesis, oxidative stress responses, and metabolism (Supplemental Figure 10B; Supplemental Dataset 2). Quantitative analysis revealed significant changes in 297 node genes: 251 showed transcriptional changes and 113 exhibited alterations at the translational level. Interestingly, only one gene (Os01g0303000) displayed changes across all three levels (Supplemental Dataset 3). This gene encodes the regulator protein CP12, which is involved in thioredoxin-mediated regulation of the Calvin–Benson cycle in response to light (López-Calcagno et al., 2017). Network analysis demonstrated that genes involved in photosynthesis and metabolic processes clustered within the same modules, reflecting their interconnected roles in HL acclimation. In addition, genes related to the GO terms “response to hydrogen peroxide” and “protein complex oligomerization” clustered together (Supplemental Figure 10B), suggesting a potential interplay between oxidative stress and protein complex formation under HL stress. Importantly, the correlation network analysis identified three TFs—OsbZIP18 (Os02g0203000), OsARP1 (Os11g0671000), and OsHAP5C (Os03g0251350)—as central hubs connecting various modules (Supplemental Figure 10B). This finding suggests that these TFs play pivotal roles in regulating HL responses in rice.

The OsbZIP18 transcription factor regulates HL tolerance in rice

Pronounced changes in gene expression and physiological processes occurred 2 h earlier in rice than in maize during HL stress, suggesting that the two crops have evolved distinct regulatory mechanisms to cope with HL challenges. Given the crucial role of TFs in orchestrating gene expression under stress, we compared the expression patterns of HL-responsive TF genes in maize and rice (Supplemental Dataset 4). In total, 161 maize and 119 rice TF genes strongly responded to HL treatment. These included genes encoding AP2-EREBP, BTF, bHLH, bZIP, ERF, GATA, and MYB TFs, indicating conserved roles in HL-induced gene regulation across both crops (Supplemental Dataset 4). Interestingly, while eight bZIP genes were significantly upregulated in maize after 4 h of HL exposure, only one rice gene, Os02g0203000/OsbZIP18, was induced after 2 h and peaked after 4 h of HL. Remarkably, OsbZIP18 was identified as the central hub gene in our correlation network analysis. Its expression level was negatively correlated with reductions in Fv/Fm and the photosynthetic rate in rice (Supplemental Dataset 3; Figure 1D and 1E).

To determine whether OsbZIP18 contributes to HL tolerance, we generated two CRISPR-Cas9 KO mutants, referred to as Osbzip18-KO1 and Osbzip18-KO2 (verified by polymerase chain reaction [PCR] and sequencing) (Figure 6A). The physiological responses of these KO lines to HL treatment were subsequently compared with those of the WT and the OsbZIP18 OE plants (OE1; Figure 6B). The OE1 line was generated in the Zhonghua 11 (ZH11) background; KO lines were generated in the Nipponbare (NIP) background. Consequently, physiological data from OE and KO lines were compared with data from their respective WT controls. As shown in Figure 6, both Fv/Fm and Pn progressively declined in WT plants with extended HL exposure (Figure 6C and 6D). Notably, the KO lines displayed slower reductions in these parameters than the WT (NIP), indicating increased tolerance to HL. In contrast, the OE1 line exhibited significantly faster and more pronounced decreases in Fv/Fm and Pn; levels at 2 h of HL were comparable to those of ZH11 at 4 h of HL (Figure 6C and 6D), suggesting enhanced sensitivity to HL stress. Further assessment of NPQ and its components revealed distinct responses. Whereas total NPQ in KO lines decreased at a similar rate as in the WT, the qE component declined more slowly; a significant reduction was observed only after 4 h of HL. Conversely, OE lines exhibited more rapid decreases in NPQ and qE, such that significant reductions were evident by 2 h of HL (Figure 6E; Supplemental Figure 11D). These results collectively demonstrate that OsbZIP18 dosage significantly influences HL tolerance, supporting its regulatory role in the HL response.

To further investigate the regulatory function of OsbZIP18, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of WT, KO, and OE lines under HL conditions. Differential expression (4 h HL vs. 0 h) and clustering analyses identified a group of genes (cluster 9 in Supplemental Figure 12) that were downregulated in WT and/or OE lines but remained stable in KO lines. GO enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were involved in photosynthesis, NPQ-related processes, oxidative stress response, and nitrate transport (Supplemental Figure 12). Additionally, analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (adj. p < 0.05) demonstrated that key genes related to light harvesting (Lhca/b), NPQ, PSII assembly, and oxidative stress exhibited distinct expression patterns across WT, KO, and OE lines after HL treatment (Figure 6F). These findings suggest that OsbZIP18 modulates a set of stress-responsive and photosynthesis-related genes, thereby contributing to the regulation of physiological adaptations to HL stress in rice.

Discussion

Light stress at midday, particularly during the summer season, is a common adverse condition that negatively affects plant performance in the field. Exposing plants acclimated to normal growth conditions (300 μmol photons m−2 s−1) to midday light intensity (1800 μmol photons m−2 s−1) represents a relevant experimental approach for breeding or engineering HL-tolerant field-grown crops. Although significant progress has been made in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying plant tolerance to HL, the master regulators determining HL tolerance remain largely unidentified. Here, we utilized a systems biology approach to unravel the physiological and molecular responses to HL stress and to identify the underlying mechanisms in two model crops with distinct photosynthetic metabolism: the C4 plant maize and the C3 plant rice. Although transcriptomic responses to HL stress were extensive (10 897 DEGs in maize and 10 863 DEGs in rice), only 16.4% (maize) and 8.5% (rice) of these genes showed concordant changes at the protein level. Transcriptional and translational responses were broadly aligned, but mRNA–protein correlations were weaker, consistent with prior studies in Arabidopsis (Liang et al., 2016; Li et al., 2022). These results underscore the role of post-transcriptional mechanisms, including translational regulation and protein turnover, in shaping stress responses.

Additionally, we identified conserved and specific responses to HL stress in the two crops. Physiological analyses revealed that photosynthesis was affected by HL in both species; however, Fv/Fm, NPQ, and the photosynthetic rate decreased more rapidly in rice than in maize (Figure 7). This observation is consistent with the higher sensitivity of rice to HL stress. Multi-omics analysis further supported these findings, showing early transcriptional responses (within 2 h) in both crops. Genes involved in photosynthesis and chlorophyll biosynthesis were downregulated, whereas ROS-responsive genes were induced. At the translational level, processes such as protein folding/unfolding and ROS responses were among the most affected. These changes were accompanied by a pronounced reduction in the abundance of photosynthetic complex components (e.g., PSI, PSII), leading to reduced light harvesting and electron transport (Figures 3 and 7; Supplemental Figure 2). The rapid transcriptional and translational regulation observed in rice within 2 h of HL stress suggests that the ROS burst triggered by HL exposure quickly represses photosynthetic processes (Mittler et al., 2022). In contrast, maize largely maintained homeostasis during this period, indicating a more robust tolerance mechanism. Interestingly, whereas photosynthesis-related genes were transcriptionally downregulated in HL-treated rice, their corresponding protein levels increased. This discrepancy could reflect HL-induced structural rearrangements of thylakoid membrane protein complexes (Kim et al., 2020) or the delayed effect of de novo protein synthesis. Taken together, these findings indicate that the C3 plant rice experiences HL stress earlier and initiates a faster response than the C4 plant maize.Figure 7Model of responses to HL stress in maize and rice at various levels.Significant changes in the transcriptome, translatome (ribosome footprints), proteome, metabolome, and physiological parameters are shown in red or blue based on GO enrichment analysis. Plants mitigate HL-induced photodamage by reducing light absorption, quenching excess light energy, repairing damaged PSII, and scavenging ROS (Shi et al., 2022). Our study identified several photoprotective pathways that were differentially regulated in maize and rice under the same HL intensity (300 μmol photons m−2 s−1–1800 μmol photons m−2 s−1). In maize, the abundance of the D1 protein increased (Supplemental Figure 8B), suggesting efficient PSII repair. Additionally, CEF around PSI was elevated in maize under HL (Supplemental Figure 8A), likely providing extra adenosine triphosphate (ATP) for the CO2 concentration mechanism (CCM) operating in C4 photosynthesis (Ishikawa et al., 2016; Munekage and Taniguchi, 2016). Maize also exhibited upregulated expression of PsbS, Chs, and VDE genes, potentially increasing the zeaxanthin pool size and enhancing NPQ (Supplemental Figure 8D–8F). In contrast, both Chs and VDE were downregulated in rice. This finding highlights the prominent role of NPQ as a photoprotective mechanism in C4 plants, which has not been sufficiently appreciated thus far. A previous study in the C4 model plant Setaria viridis detected transcriptional changes in PsbS gene expression after HL treatment, suggesting that upregulation of the NPQ and PsbS genes contributes to photoprotection in C4 plants (Anderson et al., 2021). However, the functional validation of PsbS in C4 crops, especially maize, has not been reported. Our transgenic analyses with both ZmPsbS-OE and psbs mutant lines revealed that ZmPsbS substantially contributes to NPQ induction (Figure 5; Supplemental Figure 9), which is crucial for sustaining photosynthetic capacity in maize under HL. Thus, genetic manipulation of key regulators involved in NPQ formation provides a promising and efficient strategy to improve photosynthesis and agricultural productivity in C4 crops.

Due to their sessile lifestyle, plants depend on metabolic adaptations to achieve stress resilience. In this study, metabolomic analyses revealed significant differences in primary metabolism between maize and rice during HL stress. Maize accumulated higher levels of sugars and sugar conjugates, reflecting its ability to sustain efficient carbon assimilation under HL (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 6B). Trehalose levels were particularly elevated in maize, suggesting its role as an osmo-protectant and membrane stabilizer in the maintenance of cellular integrity under stress (Fernandez et al., 2010). Genetic manipulation or chemical intervention in trehalose metabolism can improve crop yield under stress (Kretzschmar et al., 2015; Griffiths et al., 2016; Paul et al., 2018). Our results suggest that engineering trehalose biosynthesis could be a useful strategy to enhance HL tolerance in crops.

Our study also provided ample evidence that HL stress disturbs the balance between ROS generation and detoxification (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 6E). To examine changes in ROS levels, we used DAB staining to detect H2O2 and NBT staining to measure superoxide (O2−) in maize and rice leaves after HL exposure. DAB staining showed increased H2O2 accumulation after 2–4 h of HL treatment in both plant species (Supplemental Figure 13A); NBT staining revealed significantly higher O2− accumulation in rice, particularly after 2 h of treatment (Supplemental Figure 13B). We also analyzed the expression patterns of oxidative stress-related genes. In rice, SOD, POX, GSR, and GPX genes were significantly upregulated starting at 2 h post treatment; in maize, only SOD and GLPX were upregulated at 4 h, whereas POX/GSR remained largely unchanged (Supplemental Figure 14A–14D). Interestingly, CAT genes were upregulated in rice but downregulated in maize (Supplemental Figure 14E), further supporting the conclusion that rice experiences more severe ROS stress under HL. Additionally, maize showed increased expression of ZmGAD1 and ZmGLN2, genes associated with the glutathione (GSH) and antioxidant defense systems. The GSH/glutathione disulfide (GSSG) redox system contributes to the maintenance of redox homeostasis in plants (Szalai et al., 2009). Further investigation is required to determine whether the higher capacity of maize to maintain redox homeostasis, or the redox state of its GSH pool, differs from that of rice in response to HL.

Our transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets provided evidence of accelerated TCA cycle activity under HL stress in both maize and rice (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 6A). This finding implies that the additional ATP and carbon skeletons generated by the TCA cycle are critical for plants to cope with HL stress (Zhang and Fernie, 2018). In C4 plants, RuBisCO functions almost exclusively as a carboxylase due to suppression of its oxygenase activity (and the resulting photorespiratory pathway) by the CCM, which elevates CO2 levels around RuBisCO in bundle sheath cells. This mechanism is particularly advantageous under harsh conditions such as light or drought stress, where stomatal closure leads to CO2 limitation and reduces photosynthetic efficiency (Moroney et al., 2013). We further investigated photorespiration-related metabolism and found pronounced species-specific differences. Photorespiration is widely regarded as an energetically costly and inefficient pathway, especially under stress conditions. Notably, the glycine-to-serine ratio, a well-established indicator of photorespiratory flux, significantly increased in rice after 0.5 h of HL treatment; it remained very low and largely unchanged in maize (Supplemental Figure 15). These results demonstrate that rice displays greater sensitivity to HL stress and a lower efficiency of energy utilization than maize. Consequently, it is reasonable to assume that maize, as a C4 plant, possesses a greater capacity to maintain the homeostasis of its primary metabolism (including sugar and amino acid metabolism) compared with the C3 plant rice.

Our systems approach and correlation network analysis identified the TF OsbZIP18 as a potential master regulator of HL responses in rice. Transgenic experiments confirmed the critical role of this TF, given that KO mutants (Osbzip18-KO) exhibited improved HL tolerance (Figure 6; Supplemental Figure 11). A homolog of OsbZIP18 in Arabidopsis, ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), is a key regulator of light signaling and a wide range of physiological processes, including photomorphogenesis, root growth, nutrient acquisition, and abiotic stress response. In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), HY5 directly activates PGR5 and VDE, which are critical for photoprotection (Jiang et al., 2020). Previous studies involving rice have identified OsbZIP18 as a positive regulator of branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis and a negative regulator of UV-B tolerance (Sun et al., 2022). Two other homologs of AtHY5 in rice, OsbZIP1 (Bhatnagar et al., 2023) and OsbZIP48 (Burman et al., 2018), were reported to mediate seedling photomorphogenesis via alternative splicing and modulation of GA biosynthesis. The new function uncovered in this study highlights OsbZIP18 as a promising target for genetic engineering to improve photosynthesis under adverse environmental conditions.

In conclusion, our work revealed novel processes and players in maize and rice that had not previously been implicated in HL tolerance, including several genes with unknown functions that merit future investigation. The study highlights the value of integrating physiological analyses with multi-omics datasets and transgenic experiments to identify master regulators of stress responses. Differences in the responses between the C4 plant maize and the C3 plant rice were particularly evident in the early phase after the onset of HL stress, consistent with the higher sensitivity of rice to this stress. Our findings provide a rich resource of genome-wide expression data and reveal both conserved and species-specific aspects of the HL response in maize and rice. These insights can guide future efforts to improve photosynthesis and crop productivity under challenging environmental conditions and in the face of climate change.

Methods

Plant growth conditions and sample collection

Rice (Oryza sativa L. japonica cv. Nipponbare) and maize (Zea mays L. cv. B73) were grown hydroponically in a growth chamber (27°C/20°C, 16 h/8 h day/night, 300 μmol photons m−2 s−1). Rice nutrient solution was based on Yoshida et al. (1976); maize nutrient solution was based on a modified Hoagland solution (Hoagland and Arnon, 1950). The pH was adjusted to 5.9; nutrient solutions were refreshed every 3 days and aerated. Seedlings at the five-leaf developmental stage were subjected to 1800 μmol photons m−2 s−1 HL for 0, 2, or 4 h using light-emitting diode light (model BLS4060, designed by Plantsystem). HL treatment was applied from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. Fans were arranged around the light-emitting diode light source to support heat dissipation, and the room temperature was maintained at approximately 25°C by an air-conditioning system.

As shown in Supplemental Figure 1A, only the leaf segments that received maximum light exposure were sampled and used for multi-omics analyses or physiological measurements. For RNA extraction, ribosome profiling, and proteomic analysis, leaf segments were rapidly excised and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. All experiments were conducted with three or four independent biological replicates per treatment group. For multi-omics analyses, a pooled-sample strategy was utilized, in which multiple individual leaves were combined into a single biological replicate to minimize technical variability and enhance data robustness.

Gas-exchange measurements

Gas-exchange parameters were measured using an LI-6400XT instrument (LI-COR) under 1800 μmol photons m−2 s−1 photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) and 500–600 μmol mol−1 CO2; the leaf temperature was maintained between 28°C and 30°C. For the CO2-response curves (A-Ci), the PPFD was set to 1800 μmol photons m−2 s−1, and the CO2 concentration started at 400 μmol mol−1. When the steady state had been reached, the CO2 concentration was decreased stepwise to 50 μmol mol−1 and then gradually increased to 1500 μmol mol−1, for a total of 12 CO2 concentration values (Long and Bernacchi, 2003). The maximum carboxylation velocity (Vcmax) and the maximum rate of electron transport (Jmax) were calculated by fitting the mechanistic model of CO2 assimilation from the A-Ci curve. Vcmax and Jmax were normalized to values at 25°C, in accordance with previously described equations (McMurtrie and Wang, 1993; Bernacchi et al., 2001). Four biological replicates were analyzed.

Chlorophyll fluorescence and P700 absorption measurements

Fv/Fm and OJIP fluorescence transients were measured using a FluorPen FP100 after 30 min of dark adaptation. NPQ, NPQ components, PSII/PSI fluorescence, and P700 absorption were measured with a DUAL-PAM-100 following standard protocols. Quantum yields (Y(II), Y(NPQ), Y(NO)) and CEF were calculated. The redox state of PQ pools was determined via OJIP transients with far-red pre-illumination. See the supplemental information for details.

Ribosome profiling and RNA-seq analysis

Ribosomes were extracted from ∼2.5 g of leaf tissue using polysome extraction buffer, purified via sucrose cushion centrifugation, and subjected to RNase I digestion. Ribosome-protected fragments and RNA were isolated, sequenced, and analyzed. FASTQ files were mapped to maize (Zea mays B73_RefGen_v4.41) and rice (Oryza sativa IRGSP-1.0) genomes using HISAT2 and processed with Cufflinks. Differential expression was determined using t-tests (p < 0.05). See the supplemental information for details.

Protein extraction and peptide identification by mass spectrometry

Proteins were extracted using phenol-based methods, digested with trypsin, and analyzed via Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometry. Peptides were separated using reverse-phase chromatography, and data were processed with MaxQuant (false discovery rate < 0.01). See the supplemental information for details.

Metabolite profiling

Leaf samples were harvested before and after HL treatment (0.5–4 h) and extracted in methanol with ribitol as an internal standard. Polar metabolites were derivatized with N-Methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) and analyzed using gas chromatography/quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC/Q-TOF-MS). Data were normalized to ribitol and sample weight and then log2-transformed for principal-component analysis. See the supplemental information for details.

Bioinformatics analysis

Ribosome isolation and profiling were conducted in accordance with previous protocols (Ingolia et al., 2012, Li et al., 2013). Total RNA was extracted, and libraries were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq. Data were mapped to Zea mays (B73_RefGen_v4.41) and Oryza sativa (IRGSP-1.0) reference genomes. Statistical analysis identified differentially expressed genes and proteins. See the supplemental information for details.

Correlation network analysis

Machine-learning methods were employed to determine the weight values of genes and proteins in response to HL treatment, using the top 50 differentially expressed and unchanged genes as training datasets. Pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients between genes and proteins were calculated using transcriptome, ribosome profiling, and proteome datasets to construct networks; each interaction was weighted based on gene weights and correlation coefficients. The resulting networks were analyzed for community structure and enriched biological processes using tools such as igraph and KOBAS. See the supplemental information for details.

Transgenic constructs

Stable transgenic materials generated in this study included the maize psbs mutant, maize PsbS OE lines (ZmPsbS-OE), and rice KO lines of the OsbZIP18 (Os02g0203000) gene. The maize mutant and OE lines were generated in the B73 genetic background. The psbs mutant was generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 KO method with vector pXUE411C, in which guide RNA expression was driven by the OsU3p promoter (targeting locus Zm00001d042697) (Xing et al., 2014). Homozygous KO lines were selected by PCR amplification, followed by amplicon sequencing. The ZmPsbS-OE line was generated using the OE vector pBCXUN, in which transgene expression was driven by the maize UBI promoter (Chen et al., 2009), and homozygous plants were obtained by herbicide selection. Rice KO lines of the OsbZIP18 (Os02g0203000) gene were generated in the Nipponbare genetic background. CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing was employed to create the KO lines using a construct generated with the psgR-Cas9-Os vector backbone (Mao et al., 2013). Two KO lines of the OsbZIP18 gene (KO1 and KO2) were selected by PCR and sequencing, then used for phenotypic analyses under HL conditions.

Statistical analysis

In all experiments, at least three independent replicates were used for data collection and analysis. Data were analyzed using SigmaPlot. Error bars indicate ± SD (n ≥ 3) according to Tukey’s multiple test at the p < 0.05 level. Significant differences are indicated by different letters. n.s. means not significant.
