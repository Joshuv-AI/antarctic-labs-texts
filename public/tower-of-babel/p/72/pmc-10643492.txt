Exploring the synergistic effect of fly ash and jute fiber on the fresh, mechanical and non-destructive characteristics of sustainable concrete
Authors: Md. Adnan Hossain, Shuvo Dip Datta, Abu Sayed Mohammad Akid, Md. Habibur Rahman Sobuz, Md. Saiful Islam
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The utilization of waste fly ash and natural jute fiber has drawn attention to producing sustainable concrete. However, there is a lack of studies to analyze the synergistic effect of jute fiber and fly ash on the properties of concrete. Hence, the aim of this study is to investigate the combined effect of fly ash concentration and the different sizes of jute fiber on the fresh, hardened, and non-destructive testing properties of concrete. Concerning the purpose, the concrete mixes were prepared with 0.2 % and 0.4 % jute fiber incorporating 0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % replacement of cement by fly ash in concrete. For the assessment of properties, slump, density, and compacting factor test was conducted at the fresh state of concrete, whereas mechanical properties such as compressive, splitting tensile, and flexure strength test were conducted at 7 and 28 days. In addition, the non-destructive test (NDT) was also carried out to predict the destructive compressive strength by rebound hammer at 28 days. The microstructure property of optimum mix concrete was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). It is observed that the incorporation of jute fiber decreased the slump, density, and compacting factor but increased the compressive, splitting tensile, and flexure strength, whereas the fly ash improved both fresh and hardened properties. Compared to the standard mix, fly ash-based JFRC mixtures have compressive strength improvements varying from 1.7 % to 25.9 %. The 10 % fly ash and 0.2 % volume of jute fiber exhibited a maximum of 11.64 % and 10.72 % splitting tensile and flexural strength enhancement at 28 days, respecting the control mix. In addition, the NDT strength assessment obtained 5.5 % less than destructive strength on average. From the SEM point of view, it is obtained that the 10 % fly ash with 0.2 % jute fiber composition matrix exhibits a better bonding interface and the cracking resistance nature of the concrete mix.

Introduction

The most often used construction material is plain concrete because of its well-known benefits, including its inexpensive cost, inclusive range of uses, and extensive availability. As concrete's strength increases, its nature becomes more fragile as it ages [1]. However, the main downsides of concrete are its relatively inferior tensile strengths and poor resistance to fracture propagation [2,3]. Consequently, the brittle performance of plain concrete can be compensated through fiber-reinforced concrete, which is habitually regarded as a substitute [4,5]. Generally, fibers are categorized as steel, synthetic, glass, and natural in characteristics that have been integrated to make the concrete stronger in tension [[6], [7], [8]]. All of them, steel fiber is the most used reinforced material in concrete [2,[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Conversely, due to its characterization, the steel fiber has a chance to be exposed to a corrosion environment. Therefore, natural fiber is considered a potential substitute to generate fiber-reinforced concrete. Natural fibers are eco-friendly, green, recyclable, reasonable, and manageable [13,14] and are acquired from natural resources such as cotton, coconut trees, banana trees, and jute. These natural fibers are associated with the concrete matrix where inferior diameter and separate irregular fibers circulated arbitrarily all over the concrete composites [5]. Among them, jute fibers originate from annual plants that are easily accessible and may now potentially be employed as a component of the cement matrix. Furthermore, jute fibers provide an acceptable tensile strength of 250 MPa–300 MPa and exhibit around 7 times lighter in weight than steel fibers [15]. According to Zhou et al. [16], the fiber bridging consequence of short distinct jute fiber on the failure enhances strength properties, impact resistance behavior, and cracking resistance of the cement-based matrix. In addition, jute is right behind cotton as the second-most-manufactured textile fiber in the world [5]. In Bangladesh, jute is widely available, has low-price, and is designated as the most durable natural fiber [14]. Hence, Bangladesh became the second-largest producer of jute in the world, generating 33 % of the world's total supply [15]. This jute fiber integrated many useful properties like elevated tensile strength, reasonable fire resistance, and renewability, which brand it as beneficial over other types of fiber [5,14]. These researches also indicated that jute fibers could be used as an acceptable substitute for the customary fibers in concrete materials.

Due to the development of novel concrete forms with improved attributes and sustainability, the growth of modern civilization has created alarming requirements. However, excessive use of cement constituents in concrete affects the environment on a large scale, as 7 % of CO2 emission has ensued in the cement industries [17,18]. The disposal of industrial waste is another issue related to conservation. These waste elements' silica fume, fly ash, and slag may all be used as extra cementitious materials in concrete [19,20]. Hence, according to various experts, fly ash significantly impacts the behavior of new, hardened, and durable concrete [9,10,[21], [22], [23]].

Recently, a few investigations have been performed on the impacts of organic fiber in fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC) regarding fresh behavior, mechanical response, and adverse environmental impact resistance. Among them, Kim et al. [24] stated that jute fibers adversely affect the slump of fresh concrete. Moreover, Islam and Ahmed [5] indicated that the fluidity of the jute fiber reinforced concrete (JFRC) mix was decreased by adding jute fiber in a lower volume, thus resulting in a decrease in the slump. They discovered that jute fiber's larger aspect ratio (fiber length of 20 mm) and volume (up to volume percentage of 0.50 %) had a relatively insignificant impact on the workability of the JFRC mix. Also, they noticed that the shorter jute fibers, when kept at a constant volume, contain more fibers, which has a higher detrimental effect on the slump. Chakraborty et al. [25] and Mondol et al. [26] observed the decreasing trend of fresh density with the volume incorporation of jute fiber. On the other hand, Atiş and Karahan [10] detected that the reduction of workability due to steel fiber addition could be altered by incorporating fly ash particles in the mix. In addition, Akid et al. [9] reported that 5 % and 10 % fly ash replacement of cement recovered the lost slump, compaction factor, and penetration depth of the fresh mixes due to steel fiber incorporation.

The most important elements that fundamentally affect the mechanical properties of the mix are the kind of fibers and their qualities [12]. Islam and Ahmed [5] have discovered that adding 0.25% jute fiber improved the cylindrical compressive strength. Moreover, the tensile strength was apparently more for the 0.25 % and 0.50 % jute fiber. However, Zakaria et al. [27] concluded that compressive, tensile, and flexural strength increased considerably for the volume of 0.1 % and 0.25 % fiber and length of 10–15 mm. They obtained that the maximum increment for tensile strength is around 35 % compared to the control concrete. They also stated that larger fiber content and length affect the mechanical properties adversely. Furthermore, Razmi and Mirsayar [28] denoted that lower-content jute fibers improved the compressive, tensile, and flexural strength with developed toughness, ductility and concrete's hardening mechanism. According to Zhang et al. [29], jute fiber developed comprehensive mechanical properties; therefore, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis verified that suitable incorporation of jute fiber can efficiently seal the micro-cracks in JFRC, decrease the structure's porosity and avoid the opening and circulation of cracks. Conversely, Akid et al. [9] studied the fly ash-based steel FRC, where they obtained that up to 10 % substitution of cement by fly ash and steel fiber enhances strength up to 4%–35 %, whereas 33.6 % modulus of rupture enhancement is observed at the same combination. They also found a similar fashion of compressive strength response of the FRC mix in NDT. However, Topcu and Canbaz [22] reported that the strength properties started to decrease in fly ash-based steel FRC from 10 % to 20 % fly ash substitution. Furthermore, fly ash-based concrete mixes enhanced the possibilities of positive sustainability impact with low-cost materials [30]. Nevertheless, till now, no studies have been developed to distinguish the fresh and mechanical behavior of fly ash-based jute fibers reinforced concrete.

In the recent year, natural fiber inclusion in the concrete mixtures has added a new dimension in sustainability research concept. Only a small number of studies have been published so far on the impact of jute fibers on the characteristics of fresh and hardened concrete. Also, nothing is known about the fresh and hardened qualities of fly ash-based jute fiber from a sustainable perspective. However, prior research was restricted to a small number of data points, so there was insufficient statistical analysis to understand the results of the investigation and to make any comparisons to the standards that were already in place. Moreover, NDT approaches are needed to explore which can fill the gap in the existing literature's findings. From those aspects, the purpose of this investigation is to assess the effect of 0.2 % and 0.4 % volume of jute fiber with 0 %, 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % replacement of cement by fly ash on the fresh properties (slump, density, ball penetration, compaction factor) and mechanical properties (compressive, splitting and flexural strength) at 7 and 28 days. Additionally, the NDT test is performed with sustainability assessment through embodied CO2 and cost assessment. Furthermore, SEM analysis and a full comparison exploration with the standard are performed to determine the microstructure properties and predictable properties of the JFRC mix. Therefore, this research serves as a reference and foundation for further research into normal-strength concrete made from ecologically friendly plant fiber.

Specimen preparation, curing and testing methods

The concrete sample' mixing and curing processes were carried out in accordance with ASTM C192 [33], as presented in Fig. 2(a and b). The concrete specimens were created using prism molds measuring 100 mm × 100 mm and cylindrical molds measuring 100 mm × 200 mm. Concrete samples were removed from the molds after casting for 24 h and placed in water curing tanks for 7 and 28 days. According to ASTM C143, ASTM C360-92, and BS 1881, fresh concrete tests were conducted for slump, density, Kelly ball penetration, and compacting factor [[34], [35], [36]]. On the other hand, mechanical properties such as compressive, splitting tensile and flexure strength tests of concrete were performed following ASTM C39, ASTM C496 and ASTM C78, respectively, at 7 and 28 days [[37], [38], [39]]. The NDT of concrete was also conducted according to ASTM C805 [40] to compare the destructive compressive strength. Besides, the SEM examination was conducted to investigate the changes in the microstructure of concrete following ASTM E986-04 [41].Fig. 2The concrete specimen preparation (a) Mixing of the concrete; (b) Specimen preparation.Fig. 2

Sustainability performance analysis

This study exhibits the sustainability performance of the JFRC mixes through environmental and economic analysis. The environmental valuation was attained due to the reduction of embodied energy and eCO2 emissions by the fly ash and jute fiber. A huge amount of energy consumption has occurred due to the collection, processing and transportation of the raw materials, which finalized the assessment of eCO2 emissions. Raw materials such as coarse aggregate and river sand were brought from riverside industries which were passing through shipping and transportation phases. These material transition aspects were evaluated with the concern of a related supplier of material for eCO2 emissions assessment. Cost analysis

This investigation evaluated the benefits in terms of the production cost of the JFRC on the basis of mixtures mass control considering per m3 of concrete. Table 5 assesses the cost valuation of individual materials in the production stage and transportation stage used in this study. Those practices save space and a toxin-free atmosphere, which are the key positive assistances to assist the social being.

Results and discussion

Fresh properties

Effect of jute fiber and fly ash on the slump

Fig. 3 displays the experimental findings for the fresh parameters of concrete mixtures, such as slump and density. It is evident that the control mixture obtained 110 mm, which is the highest slump of all the mixes, 110 mm. With the addition of jute fiber to concrete mixtures, the slump value of freshly mixed concrete decreased. The incorporation of 0.4 % jute fiber with fly ash in concrete decreased the fluidity of fresh mixes and exhibited a higher loss of slump. This is attributed to the grip and cohesiveness of the mix provided by jute fibers. Moreover, the water absorption properties of jute fiber also enhance the possibility of slump loss in the concrete mix. Similar types of jute fiber concentration effects on slump are observed in Refs. [5,24]. However, the viscosity and slump of a concrete mixture can rise as a result of the high-filled surface area of fibers holding more cement matrix to wrap around [5,6]. In addition, the hydrophilic jute fiber's tendency to absorb moisture harms the workability properties [25,47].Fig. 3Changing the slump value and density along with the mixing types of concrete.Fig. 3

Changing the slump value and density along with the mixing types of concrete.

Contrary to the jute fiber addition nature, the workability of JFRC mixes increases with the incorporation level of fly ash in the concrete mixes, as represented in Fig. 3. In comparison, fly ash replacement ranging from 0 to 15 % intensifies the workability value in the range of 3–12 mm, which is consistent with the research of [21,48,49]. Therefore, the spherical shape of the fly ash elements acts as minuscule ball bearings within the JFRC mix, serving a lubricant effect. In addition, this similar effect also develops JFRC pump ability by decreasing frictional losses throughout the pumping route and flush work finish ability. However, the substitution of cement with fly ash moderates the water demand for a specified slump and advances the cohesiveness with the low segregation behaviour of concrete [21].

Effect of jute fiber and fly ash on density

The test results of the freshly mixed concrete density are represented in Fig. 3, where the density decreases with the addition of jute fiber. The average density decreases by 7 % due to the larger volume of jute fiber incorporation in the tested mix. Subsequently, jute fiber has inferior specific gravity to cement matrix, thus the intensification in fiber content reduces the unit weight of the freshly mixed JFRC. Similar behavior in the case of fresh densities is observed by Chakraborty et al. [25]. However, the incorporation of fly ash in fresh JFRC decreases the density when related to the control mix prepared without fly ash. Therefore, F5J0.2, F10J0.2, and F15J0.2 decrease by 1.62 %, 2.6 %, and 4.63 % fresh density, respectively with respect to F0J0 whereas F5J0.4, F10J0.4, and F15J0.4 mixes show a diminishing of 8.9 %, 10.26 %, and 11.12 % fresh density concerning to the control mix. A similar fashion of decreasing nature of fresh density is noticed in the fly ash-based investigation of [9,10,50]. The large porosity and low specific gravity of fly ash compared to cement affect the density adversely; as a result, the surface strain of cement paste decreases and floccules of cement are commendably distributed.

Effect of jute fiber and fly ash on ball penetration value

The experimental workability investigation of the freshly mixed JFRC mix is also observed with the help of ball penetration value, where the penetration value of the freshly mixed decreases with the addition of the higher volume of jute fiber, as represented in Fig. 4. The penetration value of the 0.4 % fiber concentration JFRC mixes exhibits a decrease of 16.45 % on average compared to 0.2 % fiber concentration JFRC mixes, whereas the control mix displays higher penetration than the JFRC mixes. The greater surface area of fibers can grip more cement matrix to wrap around, which enhances the densified characteristics of JFRC mixes and prevents the ball from penetrating the freshly mixed concrete. In addition, water absorption of jute fiber also generates the chance of less penetration value in the concrete mix. However, the fly ash-based JFRC mixtures recover a little bit of the decreasing slump value due to the jute fiber. Therefore, the fly ash replacement from 0 to 15 % amplifies the penetration value in the range of 5–11 mm. This is similar to the fly ash-based ball penetration investigation of Akid et al. [9]. Consequently, the mini-round shape and even texture of fly ash particles pave the way for ball penetration.Fig. 4Changing the ball penetration and compaction factor along with the mixing types of concrete.Fig. 4

Effect of jute fiber and fly ash on compacting factor

Fig. 4 illustrates how the compacting factor value drops when an increasing volume of jute fiber is added to the concrete mix, complementing the slump and ball penetration test results. Consequently, adding 0.4 % jute fiber to concrete reduces the fluidity of freshly mixed JFRC; thus greater amount of loss in compacting factor is observed in the F5J0.4, F10J0.4, F15J0.4 mixes compared to F5J0.2, F10J0.2, F15J0.2 respectively. Therefore, the adhesion and cohesiveness of the mix provided by jute fibers indicate an inferior compacting factor tendency. On the other hand, fly ash replacement of 0–15 % recovers the compaction factor loss and provides a good compacting range concrete mix with better workability. Since the micro-particles of fly ash can act as a physical filler, it enhances the possibility of improving the flow in the paste and compaction factor. However, a similar tendency is observed between the slump and compacting factor in Fig. 5, representing a linear co-relation of s = 509.3cf – 385.5 with R2 = 0.86. The co-relation is quite similar to the investigation of [9]. On the other hand, ball penetration (bp) and compacting factor (cf) show a linear relationship, as both fresh properties indicate a similar response towards the JFRC mix, as represented in Fig. 5. The linear correlation between the fresh state testing represents an equation of bp = 408.37 cf + 335.5 where R2 = 0.8685. Those relationships between the experimental analyses can be associated with predicting the workability of the JFRC mixes in the absence of any instruments.Fig. 5Relationship between slump, compaction factor and ball penetration of JFRC mixes.Fig. 5

Fig. 6 depicts the impact of the JFRC mix's fly ash replacement and jute fiber concentration enhancement. The uniaxial compressive strength of JFRC increases with curing age, as shown in Fig. 6. The 7-day JFRC mixtures' compressive strength has reached about 73.6 % of the 28-day compressive strength. Nevertheless, the effect of jute fiber addition is observed on both days. At 7 days, progression in compressive strength is observed for 0.2 % amount of jute fiber addition, whereas strength-decreasing behavior is observed for 0.4 % JFRC mixes with respect to the control mix. Related behavior is observed at 28 days where F5J0.2, F10J0.2, and F15J0.2 display 13.41 %, 15.31 %, and 16.46 % more strength compared to F5J0.4, F10J0.4, F15J0.4 mix. A similar trend is also obtained in the investigation of Islam and Ahmed [5] and Zakaria et al. [27]. The increased compressive strength of 0.2 % JFRC mix is due to the fiber bridging action promoting transverse deformation, thus boosting the strength of the JFRC mix. The addition of fibers in a small amount also reinforces the composite elements and strengthens the bond [5,51]. SEM analysis

The study used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine how fly ash and jute fiber affected the surface morphology of concrete. SEM results can confirm changes in concrete's surface microstructures. The SEM images of the control and F10J0.2 specimen are presented in Fig. 13(a) and (b). Concrete samples that had the best mechanical strength were chosen for this experiment.Fig. 13SEM image of (a) control concrete specimen; (b) fly ash and jute fiber added F10J0.2 concrete specimen.Fig. 13

SEM image of (a) control concrete specimen; (b) fly ash and jute fiber added F10J0.2 concrete specimen.

The SEM image of the control sample revealed that the surfaces of the concrete were covered in numerous pores of the C–S–H phase combined with C–H, which may have decreased the overall strength of the control concrete. The image also shows that there are many layers of disoriented hydrates of C–S–H and C–H on the surface microstructure, which reduces the mechanical properties of concrete as well. On the other hand, the SEM image of the concrete with fly ash and jute fiber inclusion showed smooth surfaces that were completely covered in the C–S–H hydrates layer. It was also noted that ettringite crystals were growing through the concrete surfaces, increasing the strength of the modified concrete. This is to be anticipated as the reaction between fly ash and C–H produces additional hydrated (C–S–H) substances. The same finding was also reported in our earlier study of Akid et al. [61]. Concrete with fly ash added has the maximum strength compared to control concrete because of its complete compact packing and the filling of spaces by the fly ash [62]. The incorporation of fly ash increased the sulfate content of the concrete, resulting in a significant buildup of ettringite that filled the structure's enormous cavities. Fly ash also enhanced the quantity of C–S–H gels in the concrete mixture and filled in the gaps, creating a denser microstructure and enhancing the concrete's resistance. It is also obvious that the jute fibers that were added to the concrete caused bonding and interlocking contacts between the fibers and the concrete's components. This bonding helped to transmit stress away from the normal axis and decreased the growth of microcracks, which increased the concrete's strength.

Conclusions

Contrary to conventional concrete, fly ash-based JFRC can offer a number of clear advantages in a variety of structural applications. So, using different concentrations of jute fiber and fly ash, this study investigated the fresh, mechanical, microstructural, and sustainability performance of fly ash-based JFRC. Using the results as a baseline, the following conclusions may be made.•The addition of jute fiber percentage reduced fresh JFRC's workability; however, the workability was somewhat recovered by the addition of fly ash. Recommendations

Furthermore, to study the specimen's properties on a wide scale, different diameters of jute fiber can be combined with ultra-high-performance concrete. More study is needed to show how smaller jute threads and fibers treated with various chemicals alter the properties of conventional and self-consolidating concrete. Further studies can also focus on the comprehensive LCA to quantify the environmental benefits of using fly ash and jute fiber in concrete production. Compare this with traditional concrete to determine the overall environmental impact reduction.
