New knock-in mouse lines Dmp1em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) enable precise osteocyte-specific targeting and visualization
Authors: Yizhong Jenny Hu, Yvonne Qianqin Guo, Ting Hsuan Wang, Manyun Yang, Yuchen Liu, Yingzi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone in adult mammals plays critical structural and endocrine functions, both largely rely on osteocytes—the most abundant bone cells and principal source of bone-derived hormones, such as fibroblast growth factor 23 and sclerostin. The widely used 10-kb promoter-driven transgenic Tg(Dmp1-Cre) and Tg(Dmp1-CreERT2) lines, while widely used and helpful, utilize transgenic approaches with transgene expression driven by the Dmp1 promoter. The transgene expression suffers from positional effects with off-target expression in muscle, brain, and other tissues, limiting their applications, causing a major technical gap in the investigation of osteocyte-specific functions. Here, we generated 2 novel mouse lines—Dmp1em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen)—via CRISPR-Cas9 mediated “knock-in” approaches to insert an internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-CreERT2 or IRES-ZsGreen cassette into the Dmp1 locus, allowing CreERT2 or ZsGreen expression under the genetic control of the endogenous Dmp1 locus while preserving Dmp1 gene expression. We show that the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) enabled highly efficient tamoxifen-inducible recombination (>90% in cortical and trabecular osteocytes) with minimal off-target expression in non-bone tissues. The Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) line showed robust, fixation-, and decalcification-resistant green fluorescence in osteocytes from birth through adulthood, faithfully reflecting endogenous Dmp1 expression. Lineage tracing using the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line further revealed that the cortical osteocytes are long-lived, whereas trabecular osteocytes undergo continuous renewal, uncovering compartment-specific differences in osteocyte lifespan. By eliminating off-target recombination or gene expression in skeletal muscle, brain, gastrointestinal tract, and marrow compartments, the 2 mouse lines offer powerful tools for rigorous studies in osteocyte biology, mechanotransduction, and bone regulation of systemic physiology that impact health, aging, and diseases, while minimizing complications due to off-target transgene expression.

Introduction

Bone is a multifunctional organ that provides structural support, protects internal organs, and enables locomotion through its interactions with muscles and joints. Beyond these mechanical roles, bone also serves as a reservoir for calcium and phosphate, playing a central role in systemic mineral homeostasis.1,2 In recent years, bone has also been recognized as an endocrine organ capable of regulating diverse aspects of other tissues and organs such as skeletal muscle, pancreas, kidney, and body fat.3–5 Bone-derived factors, including fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), which regulates phosphate metabolism,6,7 and sclerostin (SOST), which inhibits Wnt/b-catenin signaling, have been implicated in metabolic regulation and insulin sensitivity apart from their role in bone mass control.8 These bone-derived factors play critical roles outside of the bone. Dissecting the regulation and function of these bone derived factors in bone and systemic control is therefore essential for advancing our understanding of human physiology and diseases.

Osteocytes, the most abundant cells in adult bone, are central regulators of skeletal homeostasis.9 Derived from terminally differentiated osteoblasts that are embedded within the mineralized matrix, osteocytes establish an extensive lacunocanalicular network that senses mechanical loading, integrates endocrine and metabolic cues, and coordinates bone formation and remodeling by modulating the activity of osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and progenitor cell populations.10–12 In addition to their regulatory roles within the bone, osteocytes are the primary source of bone-derived endocrine factors, such as FGF23 and SOST.13,14 Dysregulated osteocyte function contributes not only to osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, osteogenesis imperfecta,15–22 but also to non-bone disorders, such as vascular calcification,23 diabetes,24 and CKD,25 highlighting the importance of elucidating the molecular mechanisms governing osteocyte biology.

The inaccessibility of osteocytes, due to their encasement in mineralized bone matrix, poses a significant experimental challenge. Much of our understanding of osteocyte biology has been enabled by genetic mouse models that allow visualization, lineage tracing, and osteocyte-targeting genetic manipulations.26 During the transition from osteoblasts to osteocytes, early osteoblastic markers, such as alkaline phosphatase (Alpl) and Sp7 transcription factor (Sp7), are downregulated, whereas osteocyte markers, including Dentin Matrix Protein 1 (Dmp1), phosphate-regulating gene with homologies to endopeptidases on the X chromosome (Phex), and Sost, are upregulated in expression.27 Among these, Dmp1 expression increases dramatically in late-stage osteoblasts and remains high in mature osteocytes, making it an attractive genetic locus for osteocyte-specific gene targeting.28,29

Dmp1 encodes a non-collagenous extracellular matrix protein that is critical for proper bone mineralization, osteocyte maturation, and phosphate homeostasis.29–32 Loss-of-function mutations in DMP1 cause autosomal recessive hypophosphatemic rickets in humans and mice, underscoring its clinical and physiological importance.29,33 Several transgenic mouse lines targeting Dmp1 have been developed, using the 10-kb or 8-kb fragment of the Dmp1 promoter to drive Cre, CreERT2, or fluorescent reporter gene expression.34–36 While widely used and helpful, these Dmp1 transgenic lines suffer from limitations inherent to random transgene insertion, including variable expression levels, positional effects, and ectopic activities in non-target tissues.37,38 Both the 10-kb and 8-kb Tg(Dmp1-Cre) lines exhibit recombination not only in osteocytes but also in a substantial portion of osteoblasts, skeletal muscle, bone marrow stromal and hematopoietic cells, brain, and gastrointestinal mesenchyme.26,39–42 Similarly, the inducible 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-CreERT2) line shows variable and substantial recombination in skeletal muscle.43–45 Such off-target transgene expression driven by the Dmp1 promoters complicates interpretation of bone–muscle and bone–endocrine signaling and mechanotransduction studies, particularly given that muscle is itself a mechanosensitive tissue with endocrine functions.

Given the growing recognition of bone as an endocrine and mechanosensory organ, there is a pressing need to fill in the current technical gap by developing highly specific osteocyte-targeting genetic tools that avoid ectopic activity in muscle and other non-bone tissues. Such tools would enable more precise dissection of osteocyte-autonomous mechanisms, bone–muscle crosstalk, and systemic roles of osteocyte-derived factors, as well as provide accurate readouts of osteocyte differentiation indicated by osteocyte marker gene expression. To address these needs, we generated 2 new mouse lines—Dmp1em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen)—using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated “knock-in” of an inducible Cre recombinase or fluorescent reporter into the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of the endogenous Dmp1 locus. This approach ensures that the expression of CreERT2 or the ultrabright ZsGreen is under the control of native Dmp1 regulatory elements at the endogenous Dmp1 locus, thereby avoiding positional effects and minimizing ectopic activity.46 This design preserves endogenous Dmp1 gene expression.

The Dmp1em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) lines are complementary tools that provide both an inducible Cre-driver line for temporally controlled osteocyte-specific genetic manipulation and a faithful reporter line for mapping the spatial and temporal dynamics of endogenous Dmp1 expression. Here, we describe the generation, characterization, and validation of these knock-in lines, and demonstrate that they overcome the off-target limitations of previous transgenic models, offering a more precise research tools for bone biology and diseases and beyond.

Materials and methods

Generation of Dmp1 em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) knock-in mice

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Harvard Medical School IACUC. Mice were housed in a pathogen-free facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Both male and female mice were used, with gender-matched littermates carrying negative genotypes serving as controls. To generate the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) lines, a 2.6 kb internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-CreERT2 cassette or a 1.3 kb IRES-ZsGreen cassette was inserted between the stop codon and 3′ UTR of the endogenous Dmp1 locus using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing46 (Figure S1A). To confirm successful integration of the full gene cassettes at the intended genomic locus, long-range PCR was performed on genomic DNA extracted from ear tissue of CRISPR offspring using primers flanking the region between the last coding exon (exon 6) and a site downstream of the 3′ UTR. The primers used were: Common_F (GGAAGACTACAGAGGTGAGC) and Common_R (GGGTTATCATTTGAGAATGCC). This primer pair amplified a 1449-bp band in WT mice, a 4058-bp band in Dmp1em1(CreERT2) mice, and a 2787-bp band in Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) mice, consistent with the predicted sizes following insertion of each gene cassette (Figure S1B). Among the 40 CRISPR offspring obtained, one founder carried the correctly inserted Dmp1em1(CreERT2) allele, and two founders carried the correctly inserted Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) allele. The full-length PCR products spanning the entire targeted genomic region of the founder mice were further analyzed by Sanger sequencing, which confirmed the correct nucleotide sequences at the genome–insert junctions, including the 5′ and 3′ homology arms with the adjacent genomic sequences, the genome-IRES 5′ junction, the 5′ and 3′ CreERT2 junctions, and the 5′ and 3′ ZsGreen junctions in the targeted alleles. Alignment of chromatograms with the predicted allele sequence confirmed precise recombination boundaries and correct cassette orientation without unintended indels within the sequenced regions (Figure S1C). Raw sequencing trace files are provided in Data File S1. After Sanger sequencing confirmation, these mice were subsequently bred to WT C57BL/6J mice.

For routine genotyping, we designed additional primer sets to detect the presence of IRES, CreERT2, or ZsGreen sequences (Figure S1D): CreER_F (CTACTTCATCGCATTCCTTGC), ZsGreen_F (GCACTTCATCCAGCACAAGC), and IRES_R (GCATTCCTTTGGCGAGAG). Using these primers, mice carrying either knock-in allele produced a 916-bp band corresponding to the IRES region (Figure S1E, primer pair 1: Common_F+IRES_R). Mice carrying the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) allele produced an 820-bp band corresponding to CreERT2 (pair 2: CreERT2_F+Common_R). Mice carrying the Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) allele produced an 877-bp band corresponding to ZsGreen (pair 3: ZsGreen_F+Common_R). As expected, WT mice did not yield amplification with any of these primer pairs.

To further examine whether the gene “knock-in” affected endogenous DMP1 protein expression, Western blot analysis was performed using midshaft cortical bone tissue from the femur and tibia of P21 WT, Dmp1em1(CreERT2)/+, and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen)/+ mice (Figure S1F). Comparable DMP1 protein levels were observed among the 3 genotypes, indicating that endogenous DMP1 expression was preserved. Both founder mice were fertile and produced offspring carrying the “knock-in” allele following expected Mendelian ratio, indicating successful establishment of both mouse strains. Mice carrying either “knock-in” allele exhibited normal body weight and bone mass compared to WT littermates. All experiments were conducted on mice backcrossed to C57BL/6J for at least 2 generations. To assess Cre-mediated recombination, male Dmp1em1(CreERT2)/+ mice were crossed with female Rosa26TdTomato/+ (Jackson Laboratory: Stock# 007909) reporter mice to generate Dmp1em1(CreERT2)/+;Rosa26TdTomato/+ (Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom) offspring.

10-kb Tg(Dmp1-Cre) and Tg(Dmp1-CreERT2) lines

The 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-Cre) mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (stock #023047) and crossed with female Rosa26tdTomato/+ mice to generate the Tg(Dmp1-Cre)-TdTom reporter mice. Tibial sections from the 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-CreERT2)-TdTom male mice were provided by Dr. X. Edward Guo’s laboratory (originally obtained from the Jackson Laboratory, stock #032757). In these mice, tamoxifen was administered daily from P106 to P108, and tissues were collected at P112. For direct comparison, Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom male mice were subjected to the identical tamoxifen injection schedule and tissue collection time point.

Tamoxifen (TAM) injection

Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, T5648) was dissolved in corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich, C8267) at 10 mg/mL by gentle inversion at 37 °C for 1-2 h. For postnatal induction, Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice received 3 consecutive daily TAM intraperitoneal injections at 75 mg/kg body weight and were collected 2 d after the final injection. Pregnant females were injected with TAM at 100 mg/kg body weight at embryonic day 18.5 (E18.5), and the P0 pups were collected for analyses. Dmp1+/+;Rosa26TdTomato/+ mice served as negative controls for TdTom expression. Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice without TAM injection were also analyzed for TdTom expression to determine leaking Cre recombination.

Tissue collection, processing, and imaging

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused transcardially with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. Bones and internal organs were dissected, post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24-48 h and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS. Samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and sectioned using a Leica CM3050S cryostat at 20 μm. Bones from P21 and P60 mice were decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 7-14 d before embedding. Sections were mounted with Fluoroshield mounting medium with DAPI (Millipore Sigma). Fluorescent images were acquired using a fluorescent microscope under identical exposure and contrast settings for each experiment.

Quantification of recombination efficiency

For each timepoint, 3 mice were analyzed, with 5 sections analyzed per mouse. In the cortical bone, matrix-embedded osteocytes were defined as DAPI+ nuclei surrounded by mineralized matrix; surface cells were defined as DAPI-positive nuclei directly lining bone surfaces. The proportion of labeled cells was calculated as the number of TdTom+ cells divided by the total number of osteocytes or surface cells in the region of interest. Quantification was performed in a blinded fashion.

Immunofluorescent staining

Cryosections were permeabilized and blocked in 10% donkey serum/0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies (DMP1: R&D Systems AF4386, working concentration: 5 μg/mL; OPN: R&D Systems AF808, working concentration: 5 μg/mL; Piezo1: Proteintech 15 939-1-AP, working concentration: 5 μg/mL) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min. Autofluorescence was quenched using the TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching Kit before mounting. Sections were imaged with a fluorescent or confocal microscope, and images were quantified using ImageJ.

Western blot analysis

Femur and tibia from P21 mice were collected. After removing marrow, muscle, and connective tissues, bone tissue protein was collected using the Total Protein Extraction Kit for Bone Tissue (Invent Biotechnologies, SA-02-BT). Protein concentrations were quantified by the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and further transferred to the Nitrocellulose Blotting Membrane (Cytiva). Target proteins were detected with the SuperSignal West Pico Plus Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the PXi4 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Syngene). DMP1 and β-actin (DMP1: R&D Systems AF4386, working concentration: 0.1 μg/mL; β-actin: Abclonal AC038, working concentration: 1:1000) were blotted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.4.1). Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated. Each mouse was considered a biological replicate for all analyses. For histological and reporter-labeling experiments, multiple non-adjacent sections per animal were analyzed and averaged to generate a single value per mouse. Sex was recorded for all animals. Because no sex-dependent differences were detected in the endpoints evaluated, male and female mice were pooled; however, groups being compared were sex-matched with similar representation of males and females. For model characterization studies (eg, reporter labeling pattern and labeling efficiency), n = 3 mice per group was used to confirm targeting specificity and reproducibility under standardized imaging and quantification conditions, where the phenotype was highly consistent and within-group variability was low. For μCT analyses, sample size was determined based on effect sizes observed in our prior study.47 Power calculations for a 2-group comparison (2-sided α = .05, 80% power) indicate that a minimum of n = 5 mice per group provides sufficient power to detect large effects of this magnitude. Accordingly, n ≥ 5 per group was used for WT and KO cohorts in μCT analyses. For comparisons between 2 groups, unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. Assumptions of normal distribution and variance homogeneity were assessed within Prism. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ .05. The n values are indicated in the figure legends.

Results

Cre recombination is efficiently and specifically detected in the bone of the Dmp1 em1(CreERT2) mice

To generate genetic tools with improved osteocyte specificity, we inserted CreERT2, encoding the inducible Cre recombinase, or ZsGreen, encoding the ultrabright green fluorescent reporter, into the 3′ UTR of the endogenous Dmp1 locus using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated “knock-in” (Figure S1A and B). In the correctly targeted mice, DMP1 expression was normal (Figure S1F). To assess whether CreERT2 is effectively expressed in osteocytes and the Cre recombinase is induced by TAM, we generated Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice by breeding Dmp1em1(CreERT2)/+ mice with the Rosa26TdTomato/+ mice. Femurs from the Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice were analyzed on postnatal day 7 (P7), P14, P21, or P60 after 3 consecutive days of TAM injection, with the last injection performed 2 d before mice collection (Figure 1A-E). We also analyzed Cre expression in newborn mice by injecting pregnant females carrying Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom embryos on E18.5 and collected P0 pups for analysis.

Dmp1+/+;Rosa26TdTomato/+ mice served as negative controls. Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice without TAM injection were also analyzed at each time point to assess leaky Cre activity. Expression efficiency was quantified as the percentage of TdTom+ cells among DAPI+ cells within the cortical or trabecular bone matrix (F) with TAM injection (N = 3/time point, 2M/1F) and (G) without TAM injection (N = 3/time point, 2M/1F). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Groups not sharing a letter are significantly different from each other (p < .05).

At each stage, TAM administration induced robust TdTom fluorescence only in bone tissue. When TAM was administered embryonically at E18.5, 46.9 ± 6.3% of cortical bone osteocytes and 29.0 ± 11.4% of trabecular bone osteocytes were labeled at P0 (Figure 1F). Postnatal TAM injections markedly increased recombination efficiency, reaching 85.4 ± 8.6% in cortical bone osteocytes and 69.3 ± 4.6% in trabecular bone osteocytes by P7, with high efficiency maintained into adulthood—surpassing 90% in cortical bone by P14 and in trabecular bone by P21. In the absence of TAM, spontaneous TdTom expression was detected in a few osteocytes of the uninjected Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice in an age-dependent manner. TdTom expression was detected in less than 10% of osteocytes before P7 in the cortical bone and before P21 in the trabecular bone (Figure 1G). At P60, reaching young adulthood, spontaneous TdTom expression was detected in 35.6 ± 2.6% of osteocytes in the cortical bone and 25.5 ± 3.9% in the trabecular bone.

Examination of TdTomato expression driven by the 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-Cre) line revealed substantial off-target recombination in skeletal muscle, bone marrow cells, and a significant fraction of endosteal and periosteal bone-lining cells, consistent with previous reports (Figure S2A). We next performed a direct comparison with the 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-CreERT2)-TdTom mice, in which TAM was administered daily from P106 to P108 and tissues were collected at P112. Side-by-side analysis with Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice treated and harvested over the same time window demonstrated markedly improved specificity of the knock-in line for osteocytes. In contrast to the transgenic lines, no TdTom fluorescence was detected in skeletal muscle or bone marrow cells in the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) mice (Figure S2B).

To evaluate Cre recombination activity on extra-skeletal organs, we examined the brain, stomach, intestine, kidney, liver, heart, spleen, testis, skin, visceral fat, and pancreas in P60 mice (Figure 2). Analysis of P60 10 kb Tg(Dmp1-Cre)-TdTom mouse detected substantial TdTom expression in the brain, stomach, and intestine (Figure 2A). We also detected a low level of TdTom expression in the visceral fat, pancreas, and kidney. In contrast, in the P60 Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice after TAM injection, recombination in these tissues was significantly reduced or undetected (Figure 2B), indicating substantially improved bone-specificity. We also detected minimal TdTom expression in the spleen and skin, with no signal detected in the other organs analyzed. Although recombination leakage without TAM was detected in the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) “knock-in” line, the leakage was only found in the bone, with very little ectopic recombination.

Dmp1 em1(CreERT2)-TdTom preferentially labels osteocytes in adult bone

The transition from osteoblasts to osteocytes is a gradual process, making it difficult to pinpoint the exact moment when Dmp1 expression is initiated. Published studies using knock-in/knock-out reporter lines and in situ hybridization have shown that Dmp1 is expressed predominantly in embedded osteocytes, but not in the bone lining osteoblasts.28,29 However, driven by either the 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-Cre) or the 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-CreERT2), substantial TdTom expression was found in both endosteal and periosteal bone-lining cells, indicating that the 10-kb Dmp1 promoter drives gene expression in both osteoblasts and osteocytes (Figure S2). While osteoblasts and osteocytes are biologically and functionally distinct, this overlap in Cre activity limits the ability of the Cre drivers based on the 10-kb Dmp1 promoter to pinpoint osteocyte-specific gene functions. To determine whether the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line can overcome this limitation, we examined TdTom expression after TAM administration in both the bone matrix and bone surface at different stages.

To assess TdTom expression in bone lining cells and embedded osteocytes over time in the Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice, we quantified TdTom+ cells on bone surface and within the bone matrix (Figure 3A). Embryonic TAM administration resulted in TdTom expression in 20.6 ± 2.5% of cortical bone lining cells at P0 (Figure 3B), peaking at 36.8 ± 1.6% at P7 and declining to 20.0 ± 1.4% by P60. Periosteal and endosteal surfaces exhibited similar temporal trends, with consistently higher number TdTom+ cells on the endosteal surface (Figure 3C). Immunofluorescent staining of DMP1 showed strong DMP1 protein expression within the bone matrix in P60 mouse femurs, with minimal expression detected on the bone surface, co-localizing with TdTom expression in Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice (Figure 3D). On the other hand, immunofluorescent staining for Osteopontin (OPN), an osteoblast marker, in TAM injected Dmp1CreERT2-TdTom mice detected TdTom expression in a subset of OPN+ osteoblasts at P14, which was reduced by P60 (Figure 3E). These findings indicate that Dmp1em1(CreERT2) targets a substantially smaller fraction of bone lining osteoblasts in postnatal bone than previous lines and that osteocyte specificity is further enhanced after skeletal maturity in young adults.

Lineage tracing reveals distinct turnover rates of cortical vs trabecular osteocytes

Osteocytes are considered long-lived cells in both humans and rodents, yet their true lifespan within the mineralized bone matrix has not been directly assessed. This knowledge gap has hindered our ability to fully understand osteocyte biology and bone homeostasis. The improved specificity and temporal control of our knock-in Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line provides a unique opportunity to address this longstanding question.

To examine osteocyte longevity, we performed lineage tracing in Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice. TAM was administered to 2-mo-old mice, and bones were analyzed 1 wk, 1 mo, and 4 mo later (Figure 3F). In the cortical bone, TdTom expression persisted throughout the 4 mo period. The fraction of TdTom+ osteocytes remained high at all time points, with little evidence of turnover even after 4 mo. In contrast, in the trabecular bone, the TdTom+ osteocytes were well preserved 1 mo after TAM induction but declined by nearly half by 4 mo after TAM injection. These findings suggest that the trabecular, but not the cortical osteocytes undergo active turnover, revealing a previously unrecognized difference in osteocytes between bone compartments.

To probe the functional significance of this difference, we generated conditional Piezo1 KO mice (Piezo1 cKO, Dmp1em1(CreERT2)/+;Piezo1fl/fl) and induced recombination at 2 mo of age. Immunofluorescent staining of Piezo1 in WT and Piezo1 cKO mice demonstrated effective and specific deletion of Piezo1 in TdTom+ osteocytes, not in surrounding TdTom− cells, such as the bone marrow or skeletal muscle (Figure S3A). Cortical bone analyses at 6 mo revealed significant reductions in bone thickness and volume in the Piezo1 cKO mice compared with littermate controls, consistent with published studies using Tg(Dmp1-Cre) models.48 In contrast, trabecular bone mass was not significantly altered following Piezo1 deletion at 2 mo, suggesting the WT osteocytes found in the trabecular bone may have compensated for the function of the Piezo1 mutant osteocytes. We also performed immunofluorescent staining of DMP1 in WT and Piezo1 cKO mouse bones and found that loss of Piezo1 reduced protein expression of DMP1 (Figure S3B), consistent with our previous finding.47 However, we did not detect significant difference in DMP1 expression in the trabecular bone (Figure S3C), consistent with our observation that trabecular bone mass did not differ between WT and Piezo1 cKO mouse bones. Together, these findings establish the cortical osteocytes as truly long-lived osteocytes that persist for months after they form, whereas trabecular osteocytes undergo active renewal. This distinction has important implications for interpreting genetic manipulations in osteocytes and highlights the value of the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) knock-in model for temporally controlled and cell-type-specific lineage tracing and functional studies.

Robust green fluorescence in the osteocytes of the Dmp1 em2(ZsGreen) mice

To determine whether the Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) mouse line can serve as a readout for endogenous Dmp1 expression and a tool for osteocyte visualization, we analyzed the Dmp1em2(ZsGreen)/+ mice for ZsGreen expression at different developmental stages. Strong ZsGreen fluorescence was observed from P0 through P60 specifically in the bone, indicating that ZsGreen fluorescence in the Dmp1em2(ZsGreen)/+ mice robustly captures Dmp1 expression as described previously28,29 (Figure 4A-E). This expression pattern is consistent with Cre recombination observed in the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line and was further validated by DMP1 immunofluorescent staining, which showed strong colocalization with ZsGreen fluorescence in P60 femurs (Figure 4F). Signal intensity remained robust following standard fixation and decalcification even in adult bone, suggesting that Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) can be used as a robust tool for osteocyte visualization in the mineralized bone matrix.

We further examined ZsGreen signal in matrix embedded osteocytes and bone lining cells, and found that compared with the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line, Dmp1em2(ZsGreen)/+ mice displayed markedly fewer ZsGreen+ bone lining cells at all stages (Figure 4G), consistent with previous findings that the embedded osteocytes express stronger Dmp1.28,29 The Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom line is more sensitive in detecting Dmp1 expression as weaker CreERT2 could still induce recombination to allow TdTom expression from the Rosa26 locus. Similar to the Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice, the ZsGreen signal was not detected in the skeletal muscle, tendon, cartilage, marrow cells, or most of the non-skeletal organs. Minimal expression was observed in stomach, intestine, and skin, consistent with the limited extra-skeletal activity seen in Dmp1em1(CreERT2) mice (Figure 5).

Discussion

In this study, we generated and validated 2 novel “knock-in” mouse lines, Dmp1em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen), targeting osteocytes by inserting the CreERT2 gene or ZsGreen reporter gene into the endogenous Dmp1 locus, without interfering with the normal DMP1 expression. Both lines exhibit highly specific expression of TdTom under the control of CreERT2 or ZsGreen in the bone with minimal off-target expression in non-bone tissues, thereby overcoming key limitations of the existing Dmp1 promoter-driven Cre or GFP transgenic mouse lines. Furthermore, our lines showed improved osteocyte-specificity compared to the existing lines, demonstrating that the “knock-in” strategy provides a faithful representation of endogenous Dmp1 expression and enables precise genetic targeting of osteocytes across postnatal development and adulthood stages.

The Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line allowed for efficient and inducible recombination in cortical and trabecular osteocytes, with recombination efficiencies exceeding 90% following postnatal TAM induction. Importantly, TdTom expression in non-bone tissue after TAM induction of the Dmp1em1(CreERT2)-TdTom mice was minimal, in contrast to that in previous 10-kb Tg(Dmp1-Cre) and Tg(Dmp1-CreERT2) transgenic lines that exhibited substantial recombination activities in brain, skeletal muscle, gastrointestinal tissues, and hematopoietic compartments.26,39–42 Recently, a Dmp1-Cre knock-in mouse line was generated by fusing the Cre gene to the end of the coding region of Dmp1 through a 2A self-cleaving peptide.49 Despite improved specificity compared to the previous lines, recombination in the skeletal muscle was still observed. The reduction of ectopic Cre activity in our model is likely attributable to the targeted insertion into the endogenous locus, ensuring that the recombinase expression remains under the control of the complete and native Dmp1 regulatory context. This specificity is particularly valuable for studies investigating osteocyte-muscle or osteocyte-endocrine cross-talk, where off-target recombination in muscle or visceral tissues can confound phenotypic interpretation. In addition, the off-target Cre activity may also cause lethality, hindering bone study, when gene expression was also altered in other critical tissues or organs, such as the gut.39,50 It is important to note that the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line exhibited age-dependent Cre recombinase activity in bone in the absence of tamoxifen, reaching ~35% recombination in cortical osteocytes by P60. This background activity remained strictly restricted to Dmp1-expressing bone cells and was not observed in muscle, marrow, or visceral tissues, indicating that the leakiness is cell-type specific rather than ectopic. In mature osteocytes, Dmp1 is expressed at high levels, and strong transcription from the endogenous locus likely leads to strong CreERT2 protein expression, such that even a very small fraction of the CreERT2 protein that escapes Hsp90-mediated cytoplasmic retention and undergoes ligand-independent nuclear translocation can be detectable, a phenomenon reported in other highly expressed CreERT2 knock-in systems.51,52 The age-dependent increase in baseline recombination is consistent with progressive increase in Dmp1 expression during osteocyte maturation rather than promoter dysregulation.47 Importantly, TAM administration increased recombination efficiency to >90%, demonstrating that the system retains robust inducibility and dynamic range. We should mention that Cre-induced recombination efficiency is locus dependent53,54 and the Rosa 26 locus in which the floxed tdTom in inserted is shown to have high recombination efficiency.55 Therefore, the recombination leakage in other genes could be less. Nevertheless, inclusion of Cre-positive, vehicle-treated controls is recommended to determine baseline recombination, and early TAM induction can minimize background labeling for temporal lineage tracing studies. In addition, while minimal, reproducible off-target expression in a few visceral tissues, including the stomach, small intestine, and skin was found. While markedly reduced compared to transgenic Tg(Dmp1-Cre) lines, this residual activity may warrant caution in studies involving systemic or whole-body physiological phenotyping.

An important biological insight enabled by our Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line is the ability to trace the long-term fate of osteocytes in vivo. Using tamoxifen-induced recombination at 2 mo of age, we found that cortical osteocytes are long-lived, retaining TdTom expression for at least 4 mo after induction. In contrast, trabecular osteocytes exhibited significant turnover during the same period, with nearly half of the initially labeled population replaced by newly embedded osteocytes. This observation provides direct evidence that osteocyte lifespan is compartment-specific, with cortical osteocytes persisting as long-lived cells while trabecular osteocytes undergo continuous renewal.

These results have several implications. First, they refine the concept of osteocytes as uniformly long-lived cells by showing that turnover dynamics depends on bone type. Second, these findings are consistent with the fact that trabecular bone is intrinsically more metabolically active and undergoes more rapid remodeling than the cortical bone, which likely underlies the faster turnover of trabecular osteocytes. Third, they highlight the necessity of considering compartment-specific osteocyte dynamics when interpreting genetic manipulations, particularly in studies where phenotypic variations occur. For example, in our Piezo1 conditional KO experiments, cortical bone mass was significantly reduced following TAM induction at 2 mo, consistent with sustained Piezo1 deficiency in long-lived cortical osteocytes. In contrast, trabecular bone mass remained unchanged, likely because Piezo1-deficient osteocytes in trabeculae were progressively replaced by WT cells, thereby masking the phenotype. Therefore, to maximize recombination efficiency in the trabecular compartment during gene deletion studies, additional periodic TAM administrations may be required.

The Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) line provided a robust, fixation-, and decalcification-resistant fluorescent readout of Dmp1 expression as osteocyte differentiation and distribution. We observed strong fluorescence in matrix-embedded osteocytes at all postnatal stages examined, with limited fluorescence in bone lining cells compared to the CreER-induced TdTom expression. This confirms that Dmp1 expression is stronger in mature, embedded osteocytes whereas the Cre-mediated recombination driven by the inducible Dmp1em1(CreERT2) line captures transient or weaker Dmp1 expression in mature osteoblasts. The ZsGreen reporter thus serves as both a lineage marker and a dynamic indicator of osteocyte maturation, enabling high-resolution imaging of the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network without the need for antibody staining.

Together, these 2 models provide complementary capabilities: Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) for visualizing and identification of osteocyte populations, and Dmp1em1(CreERT2) for temporally controlled osteocyte-specific genetic manipulation. The combination is particularly well-suited for dissecting the timing of osteocyte gene function, as inducible recombination can be initiated after bone development, avoiding developmental compensation or early lethality. Moreover, because both lines are “knock-ins,” they can be combined without concerns about transgene copy number variation or integration site effects. Mice carrying either allele were indistinguishable from WT littermates in terms of body weight and bone mass, suggesting that the genetic editing did not affect the endogenous function of DMP1, which is crucial for bone mineralization and osteocyte maturation.

Future applications of these lines are broad. The Dmp1em1(CreERT2) model is ideally suited for inducible gene KO or overexpression studies in osteocytes to probe their role in bone remodeling, mechanotransduction, and metabolic regulation, particularly involve cross-talk of bone with the brain, gastrointestinal tract, and metabolic organs, such as the pancreas or adipose tissue. The Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) reporter offers opportunities for live-cell imaging, osteocyte isolation by fluorescence-activated cell sorting, and quantitative morphometric analyses of the lacunocanalicular network. Furthermore, combining these tools with disease models, such as osteoporosis, CKD, or metabolic syndrome, could clarify the causal contributions of osteocyte signaling pathways to bone fragility and systemic homeostasis.

In summary, the Dmp1em1(CreERT2) and Dmp1em2(ZsGreen) knock-in mice provide highly specific, flexible, and physiologically relevant tools for osteocyte research. By faithfully recapitulating endogenous Dmp1 expression and minimizing ectopic activity, these models will facilitate precise genetic dissection of osteocyte biology and advance our understanding of osteocytes in health and disease.
