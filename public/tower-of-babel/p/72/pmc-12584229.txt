Interaction between rasagiline and Pueraria radix in in vitro models of parkinson’s disease
Authors: Eugene Huh, Jin Hee Kim, Seungmin Lee, Myung Sook Oh
Published: 2025

Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterised by motor dysfunction that results from the death of dopaminergic neurones in the brain [1]. Although levodopa, a dopamine precursor, remains the gold standard treatment for PD, it primarily offers temporary symptomatic relief by replenishing the depleted brain dopamine levels and improving motor function [2]. However, this approach has significant limitations, particularly in preventing disease progression. Moreover, long-term levodopa use is complicated by a narrowing therapeutic window, making medication management increasingly challenging [3, 4]. A major concern is the development of levodopa-induced dyskinesia, which significantly affects patients’ quality of life. Consequently, most countries recommend delaying levodopa initiation, or considering alternative or combination therapies [5].

Rasagiline (RASA), a monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B) inhibitor, is an alternative treatment for PD. Unlike levodopa, RASA maintains brain dopamine levels while offering mild neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects, resulting in fewer adverse effects with long-term use [6]. Recent PD treatment guidelines increasingly recommend RASA as a first-line treatment for patients with mild symptoms before initiating levodopa therapy [7]. Given its moderate efficacy as a monotherapy, RASA is frequently used in combination with other medications to enhance therapeutic outcomes, highlighting the importance of understanding potential drug interactions when developing optimal treatment strategies [8].

Drug efficacy is significantly influenced by the metabolism and bioactivation of the digestive system following drug administration [9]. Various factors, including diet and lifestyle, can affect drug effectiveness. In the case of RASA, numerous food interactions have been reported, necessitating careful dietary guidance during treatment [10]. Despite its advantages over levodopa, RASA monotherapy often provides only moderate symptomatic relief for patients with PD, creating the need for combination approaches that can enhance efficacy while maintaining safety profiles. With the growing global interest in alternative medicine, particularly in East Asia, new treatment strategies incorporating natural compounds are being proposed [11, 12]. This trend has led to co-administration of traditional natural medicines with conventional synthetic drugs, creating an urgent need for comprehensive interaction studies to ensure safety and efficacy in clinical settings [13].

Pueraria root (PR), a frequently used natural medicine in East Asian traditional medicine, is known for its efficacy in treating pain and muscle movement disorders [14]. Recent studies have demonstrated its potential in various neurological conditions owing to its ability to modulate synaptic signalling and promote neuronal differentiation [15, 16]. The active compounds in PR, particularly puerarin, daidzein, and genistein, exert neuroprotective effects through multiple complementary mechanisms [17–19] that may enhance the therapeutic action of RASA. Although RASA primarily functions as a selective MAO-B inhibitor to prevent dopamine degradation, PR isoflavones act through parallel pathways, including antioxidant defence enhancement, suppression of microglial activation, and preservation of mitochondrial function. Specifically, daidzein upregulates anti-apoptotic proteins and modulates calcium homeostasis, potentially addressing mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, which RASA alone cannot fully mitigate [18]. Furthermore, genistein has anti-inflammatory properties that may complement the modest effects of RASA on neuroinflammation, which is a critical factor in PD progression [20]. While these compounds have shown promising results in controlling PD progression [17–19], their potential synergistic interaction with MAO-B inhibitors remains largely unexplored despite the increasing concurrent use of herbal and conventional medicines among PD patients.

Based on the complementary mechanisms of action of the MAO-B inhibitory effect of RASA and the neuroprotective properties of PR, we hypothesised that their co-administration would produce synergistic effects on both enzyme inhibition and neuronal protection while maintaining favorable metabolic compatibility. This study evaluated the pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic interactions between RASA and PR to determine whether this combination offers enhanced therapeutic potential for managing PD through improved efficacy, potentially allowing for reduced conventional drug dosages while mitigating side effects.

Methods

Materials

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), foetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) were purchased from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). RASA, 6-hyroxydopamine (6-OHDA), 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)−2,5-diphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) and all other reagents were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) unless noted otherwise.

Preparation of PR extract

PR was purchased from Jung Do Herbal Drug Company (Seoul, Korea) and deposited in the herbarium of the College of Pharmacy, Kyung Hee University, Seoul, Korea (Specimen number: KHU-PR-2023-001). PR extract was prepared by previously described methods [21]. Briefly, PR (100 g) was boiled in 1 L of distilled water for 2 h. Then, the extract was filtered, evaporated using a rotary vacuum evaporator, and lyophilised (yield; 38.14%). The powder was kept at 4 °C before use.

High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of PR extract

The standard compound puerarin (≥ 98% purity) was obtained from Wuhan ChemFaces Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China) and used as a standard compound. For quantification of puerarin in the PR extract, the sample was dissolved in methanol at a concentration of 500 ppm, sonicated for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.2 μm PTFE syringe filter. Standard solutions of puerarin were prepared in methanol at an initial concentration of 100 ppm and subsequently diluted to obtain a calibration range of 10–100 ppm, followed by filtration under the same conditions. HPLC analysis was performed using an Agilent 1200 series with Gemini NX-C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 15% B for 0–10 min, linear increase to 50% B from 10 to 15 min, maintained at 50% B from 15 to 20 min, and returned to 15% B from 20 to 25 min. The injection volume was 10 µL, and detection was performed at 254 nm. Each analysis was repeated four times to ensure reproducibility.

Mao-B Inhibition assay

Liver tissue extraction from Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats was performed in accordance with the National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publications No. 80 − 23) revised in 1996. The experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyung Hee University (KHUASP(SE)−19-008). Six-week-old male SD rats (160–180 g) were purchased from Daehan BioLink Co., Ltd. (Eumseong, Korea). Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and allowed free access to food and water. After an acclimatization period of one week, rats were anesthetized with CO₂ and subsequently euthanized by cervical dislocation. Liver tissues were immediately excised and processed for further analysis. According to the previously described method, MAO-B activity was measured using purified mitochondria from rat liver homogenates [22]. Briefly, liver tissue (5 g) was homogenised in 0.3 M sucrose in potassium phosphate buffer (K-PB) and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to obtain crude mitochondrial pellets. The pellets were suspended into 4 mL of 0.3 M sucrose in K-PB and layered onto 40 mL of 1.2 M sucrose in K-PB. A mitochondrial pellet was obtained by centrifugation at 53,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C. After a single wash in potassium phosphate buffer, the mitochondria were suspended in 40 mL K-PB. The mitochondria sample was then divided into aliquots and stored at − 80 °C until use. To test for specific MAO-B activity, 98 µL of the rat liver homogenate was mixed with 1 µL of PR (0–1000 µg/mL) or RASA (0–100 nM), and pre-incubated with 1 µL of 50 µM clorgyline, a selective MAO-A inhibitor, at 37 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, 100 µL of the mixture was combined with 400 µL of 2.5 mM tyramine and 100 µL of a chromogenic solution containing 1 mM vanillic acid, 0.5 mM 4-aminoantipyrine, and 4 U/mL peroxidase. Subsequently, the mixture (100 µL/well) was transferred to a 96-well plate and subjected to kinetic analysis using a spectrophotometer at 490 nm for 30 min at 37 °C. Absorbance values of the negative control curve were subtracted from those of the MAO-B positive control curve.

RASA concentrations (10–100 nM) were selected to correspond to clinically relevant plasma levels in PD patients receiving standard dosing (0.5–1.0.5.0 mg/day), while maintaining MAO-B selectivity without significant MAO-A interference. PR extract concentrations (100–1000 µg/mL) were chosen based on puerarin content (32.546 ± 1.735 µg/g) to provide bioactive isoflavone exposure (7.8–78 µM) consistent with reported neuroprotective ranges. The concentration ranges were also informed by our laboratory’s screening of various traditional medicinal plants for MAO-B inhibitory activity, which identified PR as a promising candidate for combination studies.

Cell experiments

HepG2 (HB-8065) and PC12 (CRL-1721) cells were obtained from the American type culture collection (Washington, USA). HepG2 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All experiments were performed 24 h after seeding into 96-well plates (4.0 × 104 cells/well) or 6-well plates (2.0 × 105 cells/well). After 24 h, cells were pre-treated with various concentrations of PR or RASA in serum-free media for 24 h. PC12 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% P/S under a 5% CO2 incubation at 37 °C atmosphere. All experiments were performed 24 h after seeding the cells in 96-well plates (1.0 × 104 cells/mL). The cells were pre-treated with various concentrations of PR or RASA for 3 h and then exposed to 75 µM of 6-OHDA for 3 h.

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. After replacing the culture medium with MTT solution (1 mg/mL), the cells were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Formazan was diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR was performed to evaluate the expression of CYP450 isoforms most relevant to RASA and PR metabolism. The selected isoforms included CYP1A2 (primary RASA metabolic pathway) and CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2B6, and CYP2C9 (documented PR isoflavone metabolic pathways). These five isoforms collectively account for >90% of hepatic drug metabolism and represent the most clinically significant interaction potential. qRT-PCR was performed as previously described [23]. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from cells using a RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis from RNA samples was performed using TOPscript™ RT DryMIX (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). cDNA was subjected to qRT-PCR using TOP real™ qPCR 2X PreMIX (SYBR Green; Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) and a CFX Connect real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, United States). Primers were synthesised by Cosmo Genetech (Seoul, South Korea). The primer sequences used in this study were as follows (5`→3`); CYP1A2 Forward: CTG GGC ACT TCG ACC CTT AC/Reverse: TCT CAT CGC TAC TCT CAG GGA; CYP2B6 Forward: TTA GGG AAG CGG ATT TGT CTT G/Reverse: GGA GGA TGG TGG TGA AGA AGA G; CYP2C9 Forward: ACA TCA GCA AAT CCT TAA CCA ATC T/Reverse: GGG TTT CAG GCC AAA ATA CAG A; CYP2D6 Forward: CGC ATC CCT AAG GGA ACG A/Reverse: TTC CAG ACG GCC TCA TCC T; CYP3A4 Forward: CAG GAG GAA ATT GAT GCA GTT TT/Reverse: GTC AAG ATA CTC CAT CTG TAG CAC AGT; and β-actin Forward: CTG GCA CCC AGC ACA ATG/Reverse: GCC GAT CCA CAC GGA GTA CT.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test was performed for multiple group comparisons, and unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction was used for specific group-to-group comparisons. The difference was considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 and shown in each figure. All experiments were performed with n = 3 independent replicates. All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism Software (ver. 8.0.1., San Diego, CA, USA). Interactions between PR and RASA are denoted by the combination index (CI) [24]. The CI analysis was based on the median effect principle and was computed using the following formula: The CI values were calculated using CompuSyn software (ComboSyn Inc., Paramus, USA), which automatically computes combination indices based on the Chou-Talalay median-effect principle. This software-based approach ensures accurate and consistent CI determination for quantitative synergy assessment. The Chou-Talalay CI method was employed to quantitatively assess drug interactions, as this approach provides mathematically rigorous distinction between synergism (CI < 1), additivity (CI = 1), and antagonism (CI >1) based on the median-effect principle. Optimal combination ratios were identified by testing five different RASA: PR concentration ratios, with the ratio producing the lowest CI value selected for subsequent dose-response validation studies.

Results

HPLC analysis of PR extract

The content of puerarin in the PR extract was determined by comparing its chromatographic peak with those of standard solutions. The puerarin peak was identified at a retention time of 6.535 min. For quantification, a calibration curve was constructed using serially diluted standard solutions in the range of 10–100 ppm. The regression equation for puerarin was Y = 24.751X – 87.935. Based on the calibration curve, the puerarin content in the PR extract was calculated to be 32.546 ± 1.735 µg/g (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Concentration-dependent inhibitory effects of RASA and PR on MAO-B activity

Prior to investigating the interaction between RASA and PR in MAO-B inhibition, we individually evaluated the concentration-dependent inhibitory effects of each compound (Fig. 1A). RASA exhibited significant inhibitory activity starting at 10 nM (82.82 ± 0.81% of control), with approximately 16% inhibition of MAO-B activity observed at 100 nM. Similarly, PR demonstrated significant MAO-B inhibition at 100 µg/mL (84.98 ± 1.18% of control), achieving inhibitory potency comparable to 100 nM of RASA at 1000 µg/mL (11.14 ± 2.93% of control). These individual dose-response relationships provided the foundation for selecting starting concentrations for systematic ratio optimization studies.

These ranges were selected to correspond to clinically relevant RASA exposure and bioactive puerarin concentrations. To effectively assess the interaction between RASA and PR, we conducted enzyme reactions using various mixture ratios, starting with the lowest effective concentrations of each compound (10 nM of RASA and 100 µg/mL of PR, Fig. 1B). The highest inhibitory effect was observed when these concentrations were combined in equal proportions (70.38 ± 0.55% of control). To quantitatively evaluate the synergistic potential of this combination, we calculated the CI, a pharmacodynamic parameter widely used to evaluate drug interactions in therapeutic combinations [25]. The systematic evaluation revealed that the 5:50 nM:µg/mL ratio yielded a strong synergistic effect (CI = 0.57091, Table 1), indicating that this combination produces effects significantly greater than predicted by simple additivity.

Synergistic enhancement of MAO-B Inhibition through RASA and PR co-administration

To further validate the synergistic effects on MAO-B inhibition, we examined the concentration-dependent changes in the inhibitory activity during co-administration (Fig. 2). At lower concentrations (10 nM of RASA and 100 µg/mL of PR), the combination (73.60 ± 0.60% of control) demonstrated enhanced inhibition compared to either compound alone (10 nM of RASA; 85.21 ± 1.00%, 100 µg/mL of PR; 83.55 ± 3.27% of control). Moreover, at higher concentrations (30 nM of RASA and 300 µg/mL of PR), co-administration significantly increased inhibitory effects (24.29 ± 1.23% of control). CI calculations at these higher concentrations confirmed synergistic activity (Table 2, CI = 0.86598), suggesting that concurrent administration could be as an effective therapeutic strategy.

RASA and PR co-treatment provides synergistic cytoprotection against 6-OHDA-induced toxicity in PC12 cells

Given that PD involves multiple pathogenic mechanisms, therapeutic strategies that target various pathways are essential. Although RASA and PR individually exhibit modest cytoprotective properties, their combined effects can significantly enhance PD management. We evaluated the protective effects of RASA and PR against 6-OHDA-induced neuronal death by focusing on their individual and combined efficacy (Fig. 3).

Neither compound showed cytotoxicity, individually or in combination, maintaining stable cell viability across all treatment conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). At concentrations effective for MAO-B inhibition, neither RASA nor PR alone provided significant protection against 6-OHDA-induced cell death (6-OHDA-induced cytotoxicity: 49.19 ± 1.46% of control, Fig. 3). However, co-administration significantly increased cell survival rates in 6-OHDA-treated cells (67.75 ± 4.24% of control), with statistical significance confirmed by direct comparison to individual treatments (Fig. 3). CI analysis of the conditions showing significant protective effects revealed a strong synergistic activity (Table 3, CI = 0.00850), further supporting the potential benefits of combination therapy.

Differential regulation of hepatic CYP450 isoform expression by RASA and PR Co-administration

While our pharmacodynamic findings demonstrate promising therapeutic potential, understanding the metabolic interactions in biological systems is crucial for clinical applications. We investigated the effects of RASA and PR on the expression of five clinically relevant CYP450 isoforms in HepG2 cells (Fig. 4). These isoforms were selected based on their established roles in RASA (CYP1A2) and PR isoflavone (CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2B6, CYP2C9) metabolism. As expected, RASA tended to increase CYP1A2 expression [26] (RASA; 3.30 ± 0.93, CON; 1.36 ± 0.75, Fig. 4B). Importantly, co-administration of PR did not significantly alter this expression pattern (2.36 ± 0.43). PR treatment alone did not significantly affect CYP1A2 expression (1.61 ± 0.36).

PR is metabolised by multiple CYP isoforms, including CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2B6, and CYP2C9 [27]. Our analysis revealed that RASA did not significantly affect the expression of CYP2B6 (RASA; 1.27 ± 0.08, CON; 1.01 ± 0.10), nor did it interfere with PR-induced changes in their expression during co-administration (PR; 1.14 ± 0.18, PR + RASA; 1.15 ± 0.06, Fig. 4C). CYP2C9 expression showed a decreasing trend in both RASA and PR treatment groups individually (RASA; 0.54 ± 0.14, PR; 0.53 ± 0.12, CON; 1.07 ± 0.24); however, when both compounds were administered simultaneously, no significant impact on expression was observed compared to individual administration (PR + RASA; 0.48 ± 0.07). Notably, there was no significant difference compared to the PR treatment group alone (Fig. 4D). Regarding CYP2D6 expression, RASA administration had no effect, while PR significantly decreased its expression (RASA; 0.96 ± 0.05, PR; 0.74 ± 0.07, CON; 1.01 ± 0.07). In contrast, concurrent treatment with RASA and PR resulted in CYP2D6 expression returning to control levels, which was confirmed as a significant change compared to PR treatment alone (PR + RASA; 1.05 ± 0.04, Fig. 4E). CYP3A4 expression showed a pattern similar to CYP2D6 (RASA; 0.95 ± 0.12, PR; 0.57 ± 0.02, CON; 1.00 ± 0.04), but the recovery of expression during concurrent treatment with RASA and PR was minimal (PR + RASA; 0.69 ± 0.05, p-value = 0.0506, Fig. 4F). Cell viability remained stable under all treatment conditions regardless of whether the compounds were administered individually or in combination (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the synergistic effects of RASA and PR on MAO-B inhibition and neuroprotection, and investigated their interactions in drug metabolism pathways. This combination exhibited significant neuroprotective effects against 6-OHDA-induced toxicity in PC12 cells. Importantly, our investigation of CYP450 metabolism revealed minimal interference between these compounds, indicating favourable pharmacokinetic compatibility. Our study addresses a critical gap in understanding such interactions, as patients often combine prescribed medications with traditional remedies without adequate scientific guidance [13, 28, 29].

The standardization of our PR extract using puerarin as the primary marker compound (32.546 ± 1.735 µg/g) represents a critical methodological advancement that enhances the reproducibility and translational potential of our findings. Puerarin, the major bioactive isoflavone in Pueraria root, provides a reliable chemical basis for interpreting the observed synergistic effects with RASA and enables other researchers to replicate our combination studies using similarly standardized extracts. While our current approach focuses on puerarin quantification, future investigations would benefit from more comprehensive chemical profiling to identify additional bioactive constituents that may contribute to the RASA-PR synergistic interactions observed in this study.

The application of CI analysis in this study offers a quantitative framework for assessing drug interactions, and has significant implications for development of therapeutics [25]. Widely employed in cancer chemotherapy and antimicrobial combination studies [30], CI analysis allows the precise characterisation of synergistic, additive, or antagonistic effects between compounds. Our findings revealed optimal synergy at specific concentration ratios, with CI values well below 1.0, indicating strong synergistic effects [24]. This mathematical approach validates our empirical observations and provides a rational basis for determining optimal dosing strategies. The observed strong synergy suggests that lower doses of each compound could potentially achieve therapeutic effects comparable to higher doses of either compound alone, potentially reducing side effects and improving treatment compliance. This level of synergistic activity (CI = 0.57–0.86) is comparable to clinically successful combination therapies reported in other therapeutic areas [25].

The systematic dose selection approach employed in this study provides a rational foundation for interpreting our synergistic interaction findings. The RASA concentrations (10–100 nM) correspond to clinically relevant plasma levels in PD patients, while the PR extract concentrations were selected to provide bioactive puerarin exposure (7.8–78 µM) within ranges reported for isoflavone neuroprotective activity. This pharmacologically-guided dose selection strategy supports the translational relevance of our observed synergistic effects and provides a methodological framework for future combination therapy development.

Our investigation of hepatic CYP450 expression revealed favorable metabolic compatibility between RASA and PR. While individual compounds produced modest changes in specific isoforms (CYP1A2 increase by RASA, CYP2D6/CYP3A4 decrease by PR), co-administration generally maintained balanced expression patterns, with RASA demonstrating a normalizing effect on PR-induced changes. The magnitude of expression alterations observed (0.5–3.3 fold) falls within ranges reported in other herb-drug studies that achieved clinical success without significant pharmacokinetic interference [31–33]. Our systematic evaluation of five clinically relevant isoforms (representing >90% of hepatic metabolism capacity) provides a comprehensive initial safety assessment, though future studies should investigate additional interaction mechanisms including transporter-mediated effects and Phase II conjugation pathways to establish complete pharmacokinetic compatibility profiles.

While our study demonstrates clear synergistic effects between RASA and PR at both enzymatic and cellular levels, several potential mechanistic explanations can be inferred from our findings. The observed MAO-B synergistic inhibition may result from complementary binding mechanisms, where irreversible FAD-binding of RASA is enhanced by PR isoflavones’ potential allosteric modulation or alternative binding site interactions. At the cellular level, the synergistic neuroprotection likely involves convergent activation of survival signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt and Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, which may be more effectively engaged by combination treatment than individual compounds. Additionally, effects of PR isoflavones on modulating estrogen receptor signaling may create cellular environments more receptive to protective effects of RASA, while their direct mitochondrial stabilization complements metabolic protection aspect of RASA, providing dual mitochondrial preservation that explains enhanced cytoprotection at sub-effective individual concentrations. These mechanistic insights, combined with our quantitative CI analysis demonstrating true molecular synergism rather than additive effects, establish a foundation for future detailed molecular interaction studies.

While our study provides valuable insights into RASA-PR synergistic effects, the use of PC12 cells has inherent limitations in representing dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra. PC12 cells derive from adrenal pheochromocytoma rather than authentic neurons and lack the complex synaptic connectivity characteristic of midbrain neurons affected in PD [34]. Our cytoprotective findings align with previous reports showing PR provides cytoprotection against oxidative stress [17–19], though our combination approach achieved similar protection at notably lower individual compound concentrations. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating cytoprotective rather than definitively neuroprotective effects, and future validation in primary dopaminergic neuron cultures and in vivo PD models will be essential to confirm the translational relevance of our observed synergistic interactions [35]. Furthermore, evaluating the potential interactions with other medications commonly prescribed for PD would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the clinical applicability of this combination.

Conclusions

This preclinical study established a scientific foundation for investigating the potential therapeutic interaction between RASA and PR extracts in PD models, demonstrating synergistic effects on MAO-B inhibition and cytoprotection with favorable metabolic compatibility. While our in vitro findings provide preliminary evidence supporting this combination approach, translation to clinical applications requires comprehensive validation through primary dopaminergic neuron cultures, in vivo PD models, pharmacokinetic profiling including bioavailability studies, and carefully designed clinical trials to establish safety and efficacy in patients. Our quantitative pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic assessment methodology provides a framework for systematic evaluation of natural product-pharmaceutical interactions, contributing to evidence-based integrative medicine approaches that may potentially enhance therapeutic outcomes while reducing conventional drug dosages, pending successful clinical validation.
