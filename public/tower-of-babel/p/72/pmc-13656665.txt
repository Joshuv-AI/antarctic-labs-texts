Background

Clostridium perfringens is a rod-shaped, spore-forming, gram-positive anaerobic bacterium belonging to the phylum Firmicutes, widely distributed in environmental reservoirs such as soil, sewage, and animal intestines [1]. Based on the production of six major exotoxins—α, β, ε, ι, CPE, and NetB—the species is classified into seven toxinotypes (A–G) [2]. China hosts the largest yak population and the greatest breed diversity worldwide [3]. Environmental stressors or host-related factors that compromise the immune system of yaks lead to clinical manifestations such as acute hemorrhagic enteritis, parenchymal organ hemorrhage, and abdominal distension, often resulting in sudden death and significant economic losses to the yak industry [4]. Specifically, the iota (ι) toxin produced by type E C. perfringens is associated with necrotizing enteritis, diarrhea, gas gangrene, and enterotoxemia in multiple animal species, as well as food poisoning in humans [3].

At present, penicillin, streptomycin and gentamicin are commonly used to treat yak necrotizing enteritis caused by C. perfringens, yet clinical treatment efficacy is often unsatisfactory as observed in this study. Long-term overuse of antibiotics drives the widespread emergence of drug-resistant strains, while there is a lack of strain-specific vaccines targeting toxinotype E strains isolated from Tibetan yaks, which creates major bottlenecks for the prevention and control of yak clostridial diseases. In this study, we performed whole-genome sequencing of C. perfringens strain XZ-E1 and conducted comparative genomic analyses with publicly available sequences. Our aim was to characterize the pathogenic potential of this strain using genomic data, with a focus on virulence-associated genes. Genomic data obtained from this ranch-isolated strain may support the development of novel vaccines that specifically target C. perfringens infections in yaks, thereby preventing of C. perfringens -induced necrotizing enteritis, diarrhea, gas gangrene, and enterotoxemic diseases.

Results and discussion

General genome characteristics

The complete genome of strain XZ-E1 consists of a circular chromosome (Fig. 1) and six plasmids (Fig. 2). The chromosome is 3,349,143 bp in length with a G + C content of 28.12%. The genome harbors 3,445 genes (chromosome and plasmids combined), including 3,361 protein-coding sequences (CDSs), 30 rRNA genes (10 copies each of 5 S, 16 S, and 23 S rRNA), and 95 tRNA genes. The six plasmids, designated as pXZ-E1_1 to pXZ-E1_6, were 54, 930 bp, 345,615 bp, 13,216 bp, 49,338 bp, 15,591 bp, and 12,962 bp in length, respectively.

Comparative phylogenetic analysis and genomic variants among C. perfringens strains

The complete genome sequences and annotated data of 42 C. perfringens strains were downloaded from the GenBank database, with the strain ATCC13124 serving as a reference. The whole-genome DNA- sequence-based average nucleotide identity analysis was conducted to compare these genomes and a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the genomic sequences of the 42 strains (Fig. 3). The dendrogram revealed that strain XZ-E1 was most closely related to strain 21-D-5, forming a cluster that included strains CI-SD and NCTC8346. Comparative genomic analysis indicated that XZ-E1 and the reference strain ATCC13124 shared 2,569 gene families. Additionally, ATCC13124 possessed eight unique gene families, whereas XZ-E1 harbored 158 genes (Fig. 4; Appendix 1). Gene family clustering analysis identified single-copy gene families shared between the sequenced strain and the reference strain. The ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (Ka/Ks) was calculated using the PAML software. Genes with Ka/Ks ratios > 1 were defined as rapidly evolving genes. Five genes in XZ-E1 were identified as rapidly evolving (Appendix 2).

Pathogenesis and virulence factors

Numerous genes associated with pathogenesis and virulence were identified in both the chromosome and plasmids of strain XZ-E1. Comparative analysis showed that XZ-E1 and ATCC13124 shared a set of homologous genes; however, XZ-E1 contained additional genes absent in ATCC13124. In addition to the alpha-toxin (phospholipase C, encoded by cpa/plc), the XZ-E1 genome carried genes encoding mu-toxin (nagH–nagK), sialidases (nanH, nanI, nanJ), collagenase (peptidase_U32), exo-alpha-sialidase (nanI), sialidase (nanH), collagenase (colA, kappa-toxin), perfringolysin O (pfoA, theta-toxin), and alpha-clostripain (cloSI). Alpha-toxin, the most potent extracellular enzyme secreted by type A C. perfringens, exists in all toxinotypes and plays an indispensable role in bacterial virulence [5, 6]. The core protein phospholipase C hydrolyzes two key components of the eukaryotic cell membrane: phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin. Sialidase cleaves terminally linked α-configured sialic acid residues from oligosaccharide chains on proteins and lipids, functioning both as a nutritional factor that supports bacterial growth and as a virulence factor during pathogenesis. Perfringolysin O (a pore-forming toxin) and phospholipase C (a phospholipid-degrading enzyme) act synergistically to induce host cell lysis in C. perfringens‑mediated gas gangrene [7]. Although collagenase and alpha-clostripain are not the major determinants of virulence in clostridial myonecrosis, they are likely to contribute to the disease process mediated by C. perfringens [8]. Based on the presence of both alpha-toxin gene (plc) and iota-toxin genes (iap and ibp), strain XZ-E1 was classified as toxinotype E.

Antibiotic-resistance genes

This study successfully determined the complete genome sequence of the C. perfringens strain XZ-E1 isolated from yak feces. Genomic analysis revealed that the XZ-E1 genome harbors multiple genes encoding virulence factors, as well as an antibiotic resistance gene mprF. The mprF gene encodes an integral membrane protein that modifies negatively charged phosphatidylglycerol on the membrane surface [9]. This modification confers resistance to cationic antimicrobial peptides, such as defensins, which disrupt bacterial cell membranes [10, 11].

Compared with recently reported C. perfringens strains isolated from cattle, sheep and other ruminants, most ruminant-derived isolates belong to toxinotype A, while strain XZ-E1 identified in this study is classified as toxinotype E, which is relatively less frequently reported in yaks. Additionally, XZ-E1 harbours six distinct plasmids, forming a unique plasmid profile that differs from most previously sequenced ruminant isolates. As a native strain recovered from diarrhoeal yaks on Tibetan pastures, its carried iota toxin and multiple virulence factors are closely linked to clinical symptoms including necrotizing enteritis, diarrhoea and enterotoxemia commonly observed in local yak herds. These features make XZ-E1 a representative strain for studying the pathogenesis of toxinotype E C. perfringens in yaks.

Future directions

In this study, we obtained the complete genome sequence of the XZ-E1 strain along with its six associated plasmids. Genomic analysis revealed that the XZ-E1 genome harbors multiple genes encoding toxins and virulence factors as well as the mprF gene, which contributes to antibiotic resistance. Future studies should focus on elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms of this strain through genomic approaches, with particular emphasis on the molecular basis of C. perfringens-induced necrotizing enteritis, diarrhea, gas gangrene, and enterotoxemic diseases in yaks. Such knowledge is essential for the development of effective therapeutic interventions. Ultimately, the genomic data and analytical framework presented here may inform the selection of candidate strains and gene targets for the development of novel vaccines.

Conclusion

This study successfully determined the complete genome sequence of the C. perfringens strain XZ-E1 isolated from yak feces, comprising one circular chromosome and six plasmids. Genomic analysis revealed that the XZ-E1 genome harbors multiple genes encoding toxins and virulence factors, as well as an antibiotic resistance gene, mprF. These findings enhance our understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of C. perfringens in yaks. The comprehensive genomic data provides a valuable molecular foundation for the future development of vaccines specifically targeting C. perfringens infections in yaks.

Methods

Isolation and DNA extraction of C. perfringens XZ-E1

Fresh diarrheal fecal samples were collected from diseased domestic yaks (Bos grunniens) raised at private family farms in Xizang Autonomous Region, China. In mid-summer 2024, numerous yaks in Xizang exhibited symptoms, including abdominal distension and diarrhea. Postmortem examination of the deceased animals revealed an intestinal hemorrhage. Treatment with penicillin, streptomycin, or gentamicin failed to yield satisfactory therapeutic outcomes. Fresh diarrheal fecal samples were collected and sequentially inoculated into Reinforced Clostridial Medium (RCM), Tryptose-Sulfite-Cycloserine Agar (TSC) base, and 5% sheep blood agar. Anaerobic cultivation was performed at 41 °C. Bacterial morphology was observed under a microscope following Gram staining. Subsequently, selected single colonies were inoculated into RCM and incubated anaerobically at 41 °C for 12 h. Genomic DNA of the XZ-E1 strain was extracted using a commercial bacterial genome extraction kit.

Library preparation and genome sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing were performed according to standard protocols. Briefly, high-molecular-weight genomic DNA was sheared using a - gTUBE. The sheared DNA was subjected to damage repair, end repair, and adapter ligation using dumbbell adapters. Following ligation, exonuclease digestion was performed to remove unligated products. Target fragment size selection was performed using the BluePippin system to obtain the final sequencing library. Sequencing was conducted using an SMRT cell, wherein DNA polymerase was bound to the template within the nanopores. During the sequencing reaction, each of the four fluorescently labeled dNTPs was incorporated, and the base identity was determined in real-time based on the characteristic fluorescence wavelength and pulse signal.

Assembly, genome annotation, and genomic analysis

First, de novo assembly was performed using Hifiasm. The resulting contigs were circularized and the origin of replication was adjusted using Circulator v1.5.5. The assembly was subsequently polished with Pilon v1.22, using second-generation sequencing data to obtain a more accurate genome for downstream analysis. Gene prediction was performed using Prodigal v2.6.3. Ribosomal RNA genes (23 S, 16 S, and 5 S rRNA) were predicted using Infernal v1.1.3, and transfer RNAs were identified using tRNAscan-SE v2.0. Functional annotation of the predicted genes was performed using the Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technologyserver Glimmer3 tool. Annotations were further supplemented with the SEED, Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes databases. Pathogenicity-associated genes and antimicrobial resistance genes were predicted using Pathogen Finder 1.1 and Res Finder 2.1, respectively. Environmental factors associated with the strain were searched against the pathogenic bacterial environmental factor database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool with default parameters, retaining only hits with an e-value of zero. Finally, a circular genome map was generated using Circos v0.66.

Quality assurance

A single colony of XZ-E1 was subcultured at least three times to obtain a pure isolate. The strain was confirmed by PCR sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene as previously described. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Qubit fluorometer, respectively. The integrity of genomic DNA was examined using agarose gel electrophoresis. Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled in the PacBio HiFi mode, and circular consensus sequencing reads of insufficient length were filtered out. The genome assembly was further polished with Pilon v1.22 using second-generation sequencing data, resulting in a high-quality final genome.

Animal handling and ethical euthanasia statement

In this study, only non-invasive fresh fecal samples were collected from live yaks, and no animals were sacrificed or euthanized specifically for this study. For the naturally deceased yaks included in this study, postmortem examinations were performed in accordance with the approved ethical protocol to minimize any potential suffering and ensure animal welfare. No anesthetic or euthanasia agents were administered as part of this research, as all sampled animals were either alive and healthy or had already succumbed to natural causes unrelated to this study.
