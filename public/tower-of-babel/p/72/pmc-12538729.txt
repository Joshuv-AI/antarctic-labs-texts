Cervical spine schwannomas in a young female from a low resource setting: a case report

Introduction

Schwannomas are benign tumors arising from the nerve sheath, predominantly affecting the central nervous system in cases of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1, von Recklinghausen’s disease) [1]. However, cases of multiple intracranial schwannomas occurring without neurofibromatosis (NF) are rare [1]. Spinal schwannomas, commonly slow-growing and encapsulated, are extramedullary intradural tumors that rarely exhibit invasive characteristics. Approximately 30% of these tumors extend through the dural root sleeve, involving both intradural and extradural components [2]. Retroperitoneal schwannomas are rare, constituting only 3–5% of all schwannomas, with about 1% of spinal schwannomas occurring intramedullary [3, 4]. These tumors primarily affect the cervical and lumbar regions [5]. Typically, spinal schwannomas are solid, well-defined masses that may involve adjacent bone remodeling. When large, they can align with the spinal cord axis, forming a sausage-shaped mass extending over multiple vertebral levels or protruding through the neural foramen, creating a dumbbell-shaped mass [5].

Clinically, spinal schwannomas often present with pain or paresthesia, especially before expanding into extraspinal tissues. However, they may occasionally extend into vertebral bodies and myofascial layers [2, 6, 7]. Surgical resection is usually feasible without substantial neurovascular compromise, but complete resection of giant invasive spinal schwannomas (GISS) and multiple schwannomas can be challenging owing to their invasive nature, compressive effects, and adherence to nearby neurovascular structures [8]. GISSs span more than two vertebral levels, erode vertebral bodies, and extend into posterior and lateral myofascial planes, making clinical features and surgical outcomes more complex compared with typical spinal schwannomas [8].

This report describes a unique case of two “kissing” cervical spine schwannomas in a young female patient who underwent successful decompression surgery. To our knowledge, this is one of the rarest cases in literature.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old Ugandan Munyankore female patient, presented to our neurosurgery division with a 6-year history of progressive quadriparesis, primarily affecting her lower limbs and rendering her unable to walk. She reported mild headaches but had no neck pain, incontinence, convulsions, or vomiting. Her medical history was unremarkable, and she had no prior trauma or skin lesions.

Clinical findings

On physical examination, she displayed no signs of chronic illness, with stable vital signs and no café au lait spots or iris hamartomas (Lisch nodules). Neurologically, she was alert with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15/15. Quadriparesis was present with upper limb power rated 3/5 and lower limb power rated 1/5. The limbs were hypertonic with hyperreflexia, especially in the lower extremities. Examination of the spine revealed thoracic kyphosis without tenderness. On the basis of these findings, cervical spine magnetic resonance imaging(MRI) (Fig. 1) and blood tests (which returned normal) were ordered. Genetic testing was not done owing to lack of these facilities in the country yet outsourced services abroad are very expensive.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient underwent surgery via a midline posterior cervical incision. A laminoplasty (Fig. 4) was performed from C2 to T2, followed by a dural opening, revealing a sausage-shaped lesion (Fig. 5). The lesion was carefully removed in small sections to decompress the spinal cord, with histology samples collected (Fig. 3). Owing to their proximity to the vertebral arteries, the intraforaminal and extraforaminal components were left unresected. The immediate and early postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was discharged home with her baseline neurological status.

Follow up and outcomes

At 2 weeks post-discharge, she returned with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage (Figs. 2 and 6), neck pain, and a headache rated 6/10 on the numerical analog scale (NAS); however, the patient did not meet the criteria for meningitis. She was readmitted and underwent a re-look surgery with duraplasty at the C6 left foramina. During the wound exploration, CSF leakage was identified at the C6 left foramina and successfully sealed using muscle and Surgicel, with Valsalva testing confirming no further leaks. Laminoplasty (Fig. 4) was then re-performed, followed by myofascial closure.

At 2 months follow-up, her neurological examination showed slight improvement, with lower limb power increasing to 3/5 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Spinal schwannomas, the most common nerve sheath tumors of the spine, are benign extramedullary, intradural tumors typically originating from spinal nerve roots [9]. “Kissing” schwannomas are predominantly documented in the cerebellopontine angle, and there have been no reports of kissing spine schwannomas in the absence of neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2). Double spinal schwannomas are rare, with NF2 more commonly associated with bilateral vestibular or multiple nerve sheath tumors in the brain [10, 11]. In the absence of bilateral schwannomas, excluding NF2 can be challenging, although in this case, there was no known family history of NF2. NF2 involves a mutation on chromosome 22, whereas NF1 maps to chromosome 17.4 [12]. Here, NF2 was excluded on the basis of clinical and histological findings, without genetic analysis confirmation.

According to literature, GISS tumors extend beyond two vertebral bodies [8], while this patient’s tumor spanned eight vertebral levels (C2 to T2), eroding adjacent foramina. While spinal schwannomas are typically diagnosed in the fifth to seventh decade of life [5], young patients with multiple spinal schwannomas may be classified under schwannomatosis, potentially relevant in this case [4]. Approximately 95% of spinal schwannomas are solitary and sporadic [4], and although pain is a common presentation owing to its origin in the sensory root [9], our patient initially presented without it.

Conclusion

This is the first reported case of kissing cervical spine schwannomas in a low- to middle-income setting. In advanced cases, surgical decompression of the spinal cord is crucial, with gross total resection offering curative potential when detected early. Particular attention needs to be taken to prevent postsurgical CSF leaks.
