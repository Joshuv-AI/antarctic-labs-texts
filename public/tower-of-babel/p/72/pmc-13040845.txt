Right vocal cord paralysis caused by a fish bone - a case report
Authors: Zheng Shi, Yang Zha, Chunmiao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVocal cord paralysis (VCP) is most commonly attributed to central or peripheral causes. Peripheral etiologies can be further classified as traumatic, neoplastic, inflammatory, congenital, idiopathic, or other. Esophageal perforation caused by a foreign body leading to VCP, with hoarseness as the initial symptom, is a rare clinical presentation.Case summaryA 49-year-old male presented with a 1-day history of hoarseness. Flexible laryngoscopy revealed right vocal cord paralysis. Cervicothoracic computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a linear hyperdense shadow extending from the superolateral to inferomedial direction adjacent to the right side of the esophagus at the thoracic inlet level, closely related to the esophageal wall, suggesting a cervical foreign body with esophageal perforation. The patient underwent successful transcervical foreign body removal under general anesthesia. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. One-year follow-up flexible laryngoscopy showed normal bilateral vocal cord mobility.ConclusionIn patients presenting with isolated vocal cord paralysis—particularly those without a clear history of trauma—clinicians should consider not only intrinsic laryngeal, cervical, or central nervous system pathologies but also the possibility of foreign body-induced compression of the right recurrent laryngeal nerve. Chest CT is essential to avoid misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old male presented to the Otolaryngology Department of Tianjin Medical University Baodi Hospital on March 26, 2024, with a chief complaint of sudden-onset hoarseness lasting one day. The patient reported accidental ingestion of a fish bone 7 days prior. Initial laryngoscopy at an outside hospital revealed no definitive foreign body. He subsequently resumed a normal diet, experiencing only occasional right facial lancinating pain and mild dysphagia without odynophagia, dysphagia, aspiration, retrosternal pain, heartburn, or fever. Flexible laryngoscopy at our institution demonstrated complete immobility of the right vocal cord (Fig. 1a), indicative of vocal cord paralysis. Cervicothoracic CT (Fig. 1b) revealed a linear hyperdense foreign body traversing from superolateral to inferomedial adjacent to the right esophageal wall at the thoracic inlet, suggestive of a migrated esophageal foreign body causing perforation.

On March 31, 2024, the patient underwent transcervical foreign body removal under general anesthesia at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Operative Procedure: Following satisfactory general anesthesia, the surgical field was prepared and draped. A transverse incision was made over the right neck at the midpoint between the cricoid cartilage and the sternal notch. Subplatysmal flaps were elevated superiorly and inferiorly. After transecting the sternothyroid muscle, dissection proceeded medial to the sternocleidomastoid muscle, separating the thyroid gland and carotid sheath. Access was gained to the tracheoesophageal groove posterolateral to the thyroid. Tissue edema was noted without frank abscess or infected tissue. Blunt dissection and exploration identified a 2.5 cm fish bone (Fig. 2), which was successfully removed. Hemostasis was secured, a drain was placed, and the wound was closed in layers. Estimated blood loss was 5 ml. The patient recovered well and was discharged uneventfully.

Follow-up: One year postoperatively, flexible laryngoscopy at our clinic demonstrated normal bilateral vocal cord mobility (Fig. 3a). Repeat cervicothoracic CT showed a normal esophageal course without dilatation, intraluminal abnormalities, wall thickening, or infiltration of the periesophageal fat planes (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

Vocal cord paralysis results from disruption of the motor innervation to the laryngeal muscles, potentially accompanied by sensory deficits. Clinical manifestations include hoarseness, dyspnea, cough, aspiration, and dysphagia, which can significantly impact quality of life and pose life-threatening risks [1, 2]. Etiologies are broadly categorized as central or peripheral, with peripheral causes including trauma, neoplasms, inflammation, congenital anomalies, and idiopathic factors [3, 4]. Isolated vocal cord paralysis necessitates thorough etiological investigation. Left vocal cord paralysis is more common due to the longer course of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) around the aortic arch, increasing its susceptibility to injury compared to the right RLN [5, 6].

This patient presented with hoarseness and right vocal cord paralysis. Cervicothoracic CT identified a linear hyperdense foreign body adjacent to the esophagus, indicating migration and probable perforation. History revealed fish bone ingestion 7 days prior, confirming the diagnoses of esophageal perforation and cervical foreign body. Fish bone migration is a common otolaryngological emergency. Due to its pronounced symptoms, patients typically seek timely medical attention, leading to prompt resolution. However, if the fish bone, acting as a contaminated foreign body, penetrates the esophagus and treatment is delayed, it can induce severe inflammatory reactions [7] and may be accompanied by the formation of a para-esophageal abscess [8]. Notably, in this case, the absence of neck pain, significant dysphagia, odynophagia, or signs of infection (e.g., fever) despite the foreign body’s migration and 12-day retention is unusual. Hoarseness likely resulted from RLN compression due to adjacent tissue edema. The lack of aspiration symptoms suggested intact superior laryngeal nerve function, supporting the transcervical approach. The absence of abscess or significant infection at surgery after 12 days is uncommon.

For the examination of left vocal cord paralysis, thyroid ultrasound is routinely performed to rule out the possibility of thyroid tumor invading the RLN, and chest CT is conducted to exclude the possibility of mediastinal tumor or lung tumor compressing the RLN. However, for right vocal cord paralysis, the significance of chest CT is often overlooked, which may lead to missed diagnosis of the disease [9, 10]. In this case, through detailed inquiry of the medical history, the patient skipped the thyroid ultrasound and directly underwent neck and chest CT, which revealed a foreign body perforating the esophagus, causing esophageal perforation and a foreign body in the neck. This case highlights the importance of inquiring about the medical history and CT examination, and formulating a detailed examination and treatment plan based on the medical history to avoid delaying treatment.
