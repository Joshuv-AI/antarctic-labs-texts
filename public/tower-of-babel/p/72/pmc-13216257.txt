Impact of delayed first radioiodine therapy on response evaluation in intermediate risk differentiated thyroid cancer
Authors: Medina-Ornelas Sevastian, Granados-García Martin, Vallejo-Casas Juan A
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) generally has a favorable prognosis; however, intermediate- and high-risk DTC may not progress positively. The optimal timing for radioactive iodine (RAI) after total thyroidectomy in intermediate-risk patients remains uncertain. we evaluated whether delaying RAI by > 6 months affected early treatment responses in 132 consecutive intermediate-risk patients with DTC treated between 2018 and 2022. Patients were grouped by surgery-to-RAI interval: ≤6 months (Group 1, n = 69) and > 6 months (Group 2, n = 63). Administered activities were 3,700 MBq or 5,550 MBq. The primary outcome was excellent response (ER) at 6‑month post‑treatment, per ATA‑2015 criteria. Multivariable logistic regression and 1:1 propensity score matching (age, sex, histology, multifocality, nodal status, and extrathyroidal extension) were used to address confounding factors. Sensitivity analyses included dose-stratified models. At 6 months, ER was more frequent in Group 1 than in Group 2 (35.6% vs. 21.2%; p = 0.049), while incomplete biochemical responses were higher in Group 2 (15% vs. 9.8%). After multivariable adjustment and in the propensity‑matched cohort (63 pairs), an RAI delay > 6 months remained associated with lower odds of ER (adjusted odds ratio 1.98; 95% confidence interval 1.05–3.74; p = 0.035). Aggressive histology and nodal metastasis independently reduced the likelihood of ER. The adverse effect of the delay appeared stronger among patients receiving 3,700 MBq, although the dose-time interaction was not statistically significant. The median follow‑up was 18 ± 6 months. In this cohort, RAI administration beyond 6 months was associated with a lower probability of excellent early response in intermediate-risk DTC, particularly in patients with nodal disease or aggressive histology.

Introduction

The rising global incidence of differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) is concerning, with 567,233 new cases reported by 2022, predominantly in females. In Mexico, GLOBOCAN estimates indicated 11,392 new cases, with a notable female predominance of 9,057 cases, ranking DTC as the fourth most diagnosed neoplasm across the sexes. Fortunately, DTC is not the leading cause of mortality1.

Surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of the primary treatment for DTC, while the role of radioiodine therapy (RAI) is well defined for high-risk patients. However, its efficacy in low-to-intermediate-risk patients remains clear2,3. The 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA-2015) recurrence risk classification identified DTC as having an excellent prognosis when classified as low risk. Conversely, intermediate- and high-risk patients may not always experience favorable outcomes owing to factors such as tumor invasion and aggressiveness. These groups can benefit significantly from RAI2–4.

Recent trends in multiple centers suggest withholding RAI from low-risk patients, following a French study that demonstrated its non-inferiority in this group. Moreover, the low recurrence rates observed in several studies have challenged the justification of long-term, low-dose RAI in low-risk patients5–7. Conversely, many centers continue RAI administration across all risk categories, especially for intermediate- and high-risk patients, while reserving lower doses for low-risk patients. The ATA-2015 classifies clinical responses into four categories: excellent response (ER), biochemical incomplete response (BIR), structural incomplete response (SIR), and indeterminate response (IDR)2,8–11.

The intermediate-risk category exhibited considerable heterogeneity, with recurrence rates varying from 3% to 30%. This variability highlights the need for a refined risk stratification and evidence-based guidelines for RAI treatment in this patient population. The intermediate-risk group constitutes about 25% to 35% of all DTC cases, highlighting the clinical importance of addressing these uncertainties2,4,9.

The COVID-19 pandemic has delayed the development of oncological therapies, including RAI, across high-risk groups. The prognosis of intermediate- and high-risk patients remains uncertain, raising questions regarding whether this delay affects patient outcomes, particularly when only TSH suppression is administered during treatment12,13.

Research on the optimal timing of RAI administration across risk groups is limited, with recommendations varying from 3 to 12 months post-thyroidectomy14,15. Consequently, we aimed to investigate whether the timing of RAI administration (before or after 6 months post-thyroidectomy) correlates with treatment response in intermediate-risk patients with DTC, utilizing the dynamic response evaluation framework proposed by ATA-20152.

Methods

Patients

This multicenter, retrospective study evaluated 132 consecutive patients diagnosed with DTC between 2018 and 2022. All patients had at least one intermediate-risk characteristic based on the ATA-2015 classification and underwent total thyroidectomy. The selection of this recent time period was based on two primary considerations: to ensure uniform application of the ATA-2015 classification criteria and to assess the impact of delays in RAI administration that occurred more frequently during this period, partially influenced by healthcare restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic. All patients with low and high-risk thyroid cancer were excluded.

A minimum follow-up of one year was required to assess prognostic factors and perform evaluations aligned with the modified dynamic staging. The TNM classification was revised according to the 8th edition of the Cancer Staging System for Differentiated Thyroid Carcinoma. This study was approved by the ethics committee of our institution, and the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to its retrospective nature and the use of anonymized medical records for analysis.

Patients were divided into two groups based on the timing of RAI administration after total thyroidectomy: group 1 (n = 69) received RAI within 6 months and group 2 (n = 63) received RAI after 6 months. Each group was further subdivided based on the administered dose: 3,700 MBq (100 mCi) (n = 50) and 5,550 MBq (150 mCi) (n = 82).

During the study period, we identified 27 patients with ATA-2015 intermediate-risk characteristics who did not receive RAI therapy for various reasons (patient preference, comorbidities, physician decision and COVID infection). These patients were not included in the primary analysis because the main objective was to evaluate the impact of RAI administration timing rather than the efficacy of RAI versus observation. Of the 63 patients in Group 2, 17 (27%) underwent RAI beyond 12 months post-thyroidectomy (range 12–18 months). Sensitivity analysis excluding these patients showed results consistent with those of the primary analysis.

Diagnostic pretreatment I-131 scans were not performed as part of the standard protocol. The decision to administer RAI was based on risk characteristics according to the ATA-2015 classification. The timing of RAI administration was primarily influenced by logistical factors and service availability rather than by clinical decisions based on disease severity, allowing for a more unbiased assessment of the relationship between treatment delay and outcomes. RAI activity (3,700 MBq vs. 5,550 MBq) was assigned according to contemporaneous clinical criteria. Patients were preferentially offered 5,550 MBq when one or more of the following features were present: tumor diameter > 4 cm, macroscopic nodal disease (≥ 3 involved nodes or any node > 3 cm), macroscopic extrathyroidal extension, extensive vascular invasion, aggressive histologic variant (e.g., tall-cell, hobnail, columnar), or known gross residual disease in the surgical bed. In the absence of these features, 3,700 MBq was administered (Figs. 1 and 2).

To evaluate confounding by indication and a potential dose per timing interaction, we fitted multivariable logistic regression models for the primary outcome (excellent response vs. not), adjusted for age, sex, aggressive histology, number of positive lymph nodes, multifocality and extrathyroidal extension, and included administered dose as a covariate. We additionally performed sensitivity analyses stratified by administered activity and tested a dose per time interaction term. As a further sensitivity analysis, propensity-score matching for timing of RAI was performed including the same covariates.

To achieve appropriate TSH levels for optimal RAI uptake, patients underwent either 28 days of L-thyroxine withdrawal or administration of recombinant human TSH (rhTSH, Thyrogen®, Genzyme Corp., Cambridge, MA) via intramuscular injection (0.9 mg for two consecutive days). A low-iodine diet was instituted 14 days before RAI administration until the TSH levels exceeded 30 mIU/mL. Thyroglobulin (Tg) and anti-Tg antibody (TgAb) levels were concurrently measured. Whole-body RAI scans (WBS) were performed 5–7 days after post-RAI administration using SPECT/CT images acquired from the neck and thorax. The imaging protocol involved 360-degree rotation with a continuous noncircular orbit technique using a 128 × 128 matrix and a 15% window centered at 364 keV with scattering correction. The CT images for attenuation correction were obtained using low-dose parameters. Raw data reconstruction utilized an iterative method with sets and subsets (8 iterations/4 subsets) and a Butterworth filter (cut to 0.5, order 5).

Serum thyroglobulin (Tg) and anti-thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) concentrations were measured using a chemiluminescent immunoassay on the Architect i2000SR analyzer (Abbott Diagnostics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The functional sensitivity of the Tg assay was 0.2 ng/mL. The reference interval in our laboratory for Tg was 0–55 ng/mL. A TgAb cutoff of 115 IU/mL was used, with values ≥ 115 IU/mL considered positive. Blood samples were collected in the fasting state at scheduled visits, centrifuged within 2 h, and serum aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until batch analysis. In patients with positive TgAb, Tg concentrations were interpreted with caution and were not used in isolation for clinical decision-making; imaging and clinical assessment guided management.

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess differences in continuous variables, whereas the chi-squared test was used to evaluate categorical variables. Linear logistic regression was used to analyze the association between the clinical parameters and outcomes. To address the potential selection bias and baseline imbalances, propensity score matching was performed using logistic regression with the following variables: age, sex, histological subtype, multifocality, lymph node involvement, and extrathyroidal extension.

Cox regression was used to calculate quantitative variable associations with time-to-event outcomes. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residual tests (p > 0.05). Data are presented as mean ± SD or frequency (percentage) as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. significant. Multivariate Cox regression with stepwise backward elimination was used, complemented by sensitivity analyses using clinically informed variable selection to minimize the risk of overfitting. A post-hoc power analysis indicated that our sample size provided 80% power to detect a 15% difference in excellent response rates between the groups (p = 0.05). Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 22 and JMP ver. 10.0.

The patients received levothyroxine for thyrotropin suppression and were monitored through regular physical examinations and thyroid function tests. The primary outcome was defined as an ‘excellent response in the initial response evaluation’ 6 months post-RAI treatment, following the ATA-2015 dynamic criteria. This time point was selected because it represents the first formal evaluation after initial treatment and allows the assessment of early biochemical and structural responses, which have been shown to be predictive of long-term outcomes. The initial evaluation included neck ultrasound, TSH-stimulated Tg and TgAb measurements, along with diagnostic WBS using SPECT/CT. Therapy responses were categorized as excellent response (ER), biochemical incomplete response (BIR), indeterminate response (IR), and structural incomplete response (SIR) at each follow-up, conducted at 6, 12, and 18 ± 6 months after RAI.

For patients who did not achieve ER, additional imaging studies such as MRI or 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography were performed when clinical or laboratory findings suggested persistent or recurrent disease. Imaging interpretations were conducted by two nuclear medicine specialists with > 10 years of experience (k = 0.89). Subsequent RAI doses were administered based on the following specific criteria1: evidence of persistent structural disease on the initial post-treatment scan that was not amenable to surgical management2, incomplete biochemical response with stimulated thyroglobulin > 10 ng/mL without evidence of localized structural disease, or3 incomplete structural response with evidence of RAI uptake. The minimum interval between doses was 6 months, according to international guidelines.

To mitigate confounding, a propensity score was estimated using logistic regression including the following covariates: age (≤ 55/>55), sex, histological subtype (including aggressive variants), multifocality, lymph node metastases and extrathyroidal extension. Patients were matched 1:1 using nearest‑neighbor matching without replacement and a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.10 considered acceptable.

All statistical tests are two‑sided. Exact p‑values are reported throughout, including for non‑significant comparisons. Multivariable models present effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and exact p‑values. Post‑hoc power calculations were performed for the primary endpoint (excellent response at final short‑term follow‑up) to assess the minimum detectable effect given the sample size; no formal post‑hoc power calculations were performed for each secondary outcome due to limited event counts.

Following adjustment for age, sex, aggressive histology, nodal status, multifocality and extrathyroidal extension, and after accounting for administered 131‑I activity, delayed RAI remained associated with a lower likelihood of achieving an excellent response. In analyses stratified by dose, the adverse association of delay was more apparent in the subgroup treated with 3,700 MBq; however, the formal dose × timing interaction term did not reach statistical significance.

The number of doses required by patients in Group 2 was higher than that required by patients in Group 1 (18% vs. 11%). Indications for additional surgery included1 persistent structural disease identified at the initial post-RAI evaluation (n = 10), primarily cervical lymph node metastases (n = 7) and local persistent disease (n = 3); and2 lymph node recurrence detected during follow-up (n = 5). Importantly, these interventions primarily represent the management of persistent rather than recurrent diseases. The higher proportion of additional surgical procedures in group 2 (9.5% vs. 3.5%) suggests that delayed RAI administration might be associated with higher rates of persistent disease requiring surgical intervention, although this difference was not statistically significant (Table 4).

Discussion

There is currently no clear consensus on the best time for RAI administration after total thyroidectomy, and most international guidelines do not have a clear time interval for administration; with the exception of low-risk patients, there is no solid evidence in patients with intermediate and high risk. Current guidelines do not specify an optimal interval for initiating RAI after surgery. The present observational data suggest that a ≤ 6‑month window may be preferable for selected intermediate‑to‑high risk patients, and could inform future updates of guideline recommendations. However, recommendations for broad guideline changes should await confirmation from prospective or multicenter studies2,3,15–17.

RAI ablation and that the decision should be made in a multidisciplinary approach according to many societies, the benefit appears most pronounced in those individuals who accumulate multiple intermediate-risk factors, such as a primary tumor size exceeding 4 cm, or tumors larger than 1 cm that also exhibit microscopic extrathyroidal extension, or the presence of more than five metastatic central compartment lymph nodes, or any evidence of lateral or mediastinal lymph node metastases; however, the interpretation of much of this data is complicated by the retrospective nature of many studies, which are inherently susceptible to selection bias. In such studies, patients presenting with more adverse prognostic features are often preferentially selected for adjuvant RAI therapy, a factor that can obscure or even paradoxically invert the apparent efficacy of RAI if not meticulously accounted for in statistical analyses–this reason we conducted randomized study groups with activities recommended in different guidelines1,2,17–19.

This inherent limitation strongly highlights the pressing necessity for prospective randomized clinical trials to definitively delineate the role of RAI and mitigate the impact of such confounding variables19.

The literature on the optimal timing of RAI administration after surgery in patients with DTC is not uniform. Some studies have reported conflicting results or suggested the potential negative impacts of delays beyond 3 months, particularly in certain low-to-intermediate-risk DTC patient subsets14,20. It is possible that the “timing” of RAI administration may be more of a surrogate marker for other unmeasured variables, such as patient comorbidities, healthcare access issues, or evolving disease biology2,18.

We used the four-criteria therapy response evaluation system proposed by ATA-2015, including ER, IDR, BIR, and SIR. This response evaluation system is most commonly used in centers with high-volume patients with thyroid cancer because it most effectively predicts the risk of recurrence and persistent disease and provides dynamic risk and follow-up recommendations2–4.

Our study evaluated the association between the timing of RAI administration (before vs. after 6 months) and treatment outcomes in intermediate-risk DTC patients stratified by the administered dose (3,700 MBq vs. 5,550 MBq). Response rates were assessed based on dynamic staging at 6 and 12 months, and at the final follow-up. We observed higher rates of excellent response in patients who received earlier treatment, although this association should be interpreted within the context of the limitations of our study. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate this relationship using the 2015-ATA classification with stratification by timing and dose, although our findings differ from those of previous studies.

We acknowledge that administered 131‑I activity was not randomized and that dose allocation reflected clinical judgment based on tumor and nodal characteristics, raising the possibility of confounding by indication. To mitigate this, we adjusted for key prognostic variables, included administered activity in multivariable models, performed dose‑stratified analyses and used propensity‑score matching. Although the association between delayed RAI and a lower probability of an excellent response persisted after these adjustments, residual confounding may remain. The observation that the effect of delay was more evident among patients who received 3,700 MBq should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Prospective studies or protocol‑driven cohorts with standardized dose assignment are required to definitively disentangle the independent effects of timing and administered activity.

Our findings suggest that delaying RAI treatment for > 6 months after total thyroidectomy in patients with intermediate-risk DTC negatively affects the initial excellent response rate. This is important because the initial treatment response is a key prognostic factor associated with long-term recurrence and survival in these patients2,8,9.

Several previous studies have evaluated the impact of the timing of RAI administration in patients with DTC, but with mixed results. Scheffel et al. found no differences in the initial treatment response or recurrence rate between patients who underwent RAI before or 6 months after thyroidectomy20. However, this study included low, intermediate and high-risk patients, and used the ATA-2009 response classification, which is less accurate than the updated 2015 version used in our analysis, and they did not explain how they differentiated the doses between the patients, and in the response evaluation they only performed scanning without SPECT/CT, which could have affected the evaluation2,20.

Kim et al., in their study of 720 intermediate-to-high-risk papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) patients, reported no significant differences in therapeutic response, disease-free survival (DFS), or overall survival when RAI was administered between 90- and 180-days post-surgery compared to administration within 90 days, over a median follow-up of 8.6 years21. These findings are clinically pertinent because they offer valuable flexibility in scheduling RAI, accommodating patient recovery, facilitating logistical arrangements for therapy, and allowing adequate time for a comprehensive postoperative risk assessment that often includes mature thyroglobulin levels and neck ultrasonography results that guide RAI decisions16.

An important aspect to consider is that our study specifically focused on patients with intermediate-risk DTC, a group that benefitted from RAI treatment3,4. Previous studies included patients at different risk levels, making it difficult to extrapolate their results20,21. By analyzing a more homogeneous group, our findings suggest that a delay in RAI administration affects intermediate-risk patients more significantly.

Jeong et al. found that low doses (30 mCi/1,100 MBq) resulted in a higher rate of incomplete response and structural incomplete response compared to high doses (100–150 mCi/3,700–5,550 MBq) in a study follow-up period of 10 years. They did not perform univariate analysis of the factors that affected the responses regardless of the timing of administration22. Our stratified analysis by RAI dose revealed that patients who received 3,700 MBq had worse outcomes, especially when treatment was administered late. This is consistent with a report by Jeong et al., who found a higher rate of incomplete responses to low RAI doses, regardless of the timing of administration22. These findings highlight the importance of adequate RAI doses, particularly in patients with adverse risk factors.

Yu et al. evaluated patients with intermediate- to high-risk DTC receiving RAI after total thyroidectomy with fixed doses of 150 mCi/5,550 MBq in two study groups. They demonstrated that time (< 3 months after thyroidectomy) was associated with a greater biochemical response, but had no impact on SIR and delayed initial RAI related to IR. Methodology implemented was similar to our study, evaluation at 6, 12 months and the last evaluation, our last evaluation was done 18 ± 6 months after initial RAI14.

Similar data were obtained in the study by Gomez-Pérez et al., although in their study they included low-risk patients; in the proportion of intermediate-risk patients, they found greater benefit, positive anti-thyroglobulin antibodies, and elevated thyroglobulin levels as adverse factors23.

In a study involving 1224 DTC patients (75.74% were classified as intermediate risk according to 2015-ATA), Cheng et al. found that delaying RAI therapy for more than 3 months (but not beyond 6 months) did not impair prognosis and was associated with a reduced rate of incomplete (biochemical or structural) responses when assessed 6–8 months post-RAI24.

In this comparative evaluation of recent retrospective cohort studies, results regarding the impact of timing of the first post‑thyroidectomy RAI on early response and long‑term outcomes are heterogeneous. Two large tertiary‑center analyses that applied multivariable adjustment or propensity‑score matching20,21 did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between delayed RAI (up to 180 days or > 6 months, respectively) and worse response to therapy, disease‑free survival or overall survival after a median follow‑up of 6–9 years (Kim: DFS HR 1.3, 95% CI 0.9–1.8, p = 0.061; Scheffel: adjusted RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.80–1.19, p = 0.832). By contrast, a single‑center study14 reported a strong independent association between delay ≥ 3 months and biochemical/structural incomplete response (adjusted OR : 6.7, p = 0.001). Jeong et al.22 did not assess timing but found that RAI activity (low vs. high dose) significantly influenced incomplete response rates in an intermediate‑risk Korean cohort (Table 8).

These discrepant findings likely reflect differences in study design, risk‑profile composition, exposure definition and confounder control. Kim used propensity matching to balance tumor biology and pre‑ablative sTg, and Scheffel adjusted for ATA risk and nodal status, whereas Yu’s institutional series reported large, adjusted effect sizes but included fewer patients and a shorter delay threshold (≥ 3 months). Outcome definitions also differ (Tg cutoffs, inclusion/exclusion of WBS), and Jeong shows that dose and inclusion of WBS materially change ‘ablation success’ estimates. Collectively, the evidence does not uniformly support an urgent universal policy to deliver RAI within a fixed short interval for all intermediate/high‑risk patients, but delay may be more consequential in certain subgroups (e.g., higher pre‑ablative sTg, extrathyroidal soft‑tissue invasion). Pending prospective or larger harmonized analyses, individualized decision‑making, integrating risk features, pre‑ablative Tg, RAI avidity and local logistics is prudent; consideration should be given to prioritizing earlier RAI for patients with aggressive features or elevated sTg on a case‑by‑case basis.

Multivariate analysis identified independent variables associated with a lower probability of achieving an excellent response, such as aggressive histological variants, the presence of lymph node metastases, and delayed RAI administration. These risk factors have been previously described as predictors of poor prognosis in DTC25–27. The identification of high-risk patients reinforces the importance of early and aggressive management, including timely and adequate RAI administration.

Our study suggests a negative impact of delaying RAI beyond 6 months and highlights worse outcomes with lower doses (3,700 MBq), particularly when delayed, especially in patients with lymph node metastases or aggressive histology, contributing valuable data to this complex discussion22,25,26. These findings align with some segments of the literature that emphasize the importance of early and aggressive management of patients exhibiting high-risk features, even if broadly categorized within an intermediate-risk framework25. Possible explanations for the poorer outcomes observed with delayed or low-dose RAI in such specific subgroups could include the unchecked progression of micrometastatic disease, potential development of RAI-refractory tumor cell clones over time, or simply an insufficient radiation dose to effectively eradicate all residual diseases, particularly in the context of adverse biological features22,25,26.

Identification and validation of robust predictive factors are crucial for refining risk stratification within the heterogeneous intermediate-risk DTC category. Characteristics such as aggressive histological variants, extensive lymph node metastases, extranodal extension, and molecular markers such as BRAFV600E and TERT mutations are associated with a higher risk and should be considered in RAI decision-making because they are frequently associated with more aggressive clinicopathological features and have been mechanistically linked to RAI resistance, partly through the downregulation of NIS expression2,17,28,29. A multilayered approach that integrates pathological features, dynamic biochemical evidence, and underlying molecular profiles can generate more personalized and effective algorithms for RAI decision-making.

An additional potential source of selection bias arises from the 27 patients in the initial cohort who met ATA‑2015 intermediate‑risk criteria but did not receive RAI and were excluded from the primary analysis. These patients were omitted for reasons including patient choice, comorbidities, physician decision, and active COVID‑19 infection. Excluding them may have biased the analytic sample if the excluded patients differed systematically from those who received RAI (for example, if they had higher comorbidity burden, differing tumor features, or were considered poor candidates for RAI), which could either attenuate or exaggerate the observed association between treatment delay and early response. We attempted to mitigate this by restricting the primary analysis to patients who actually received adjuvant RAI and by performing sensitivity analyses within the treated cohort; however, we did not have complete data to perform formal inverse‑probability weighting or multiple‑imputation models that would include the non‑treated patients. Therefore, residual selection bias related to the exclusion of these 27 patients cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting our results.

Some multivariable effect estimates are imprecise, as indicated by wide 95% confidence intervals. This imprecision reduces the reliability of point estimates and suggests that the true effect may be smaller or larger than observed; accordingly, non‑significant findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no effect. The study was powered for the primary short‑term outcome only, and limited events in subgroups constrain inference for secondary endpoints and interaction testing. Larger cohorts with longer follow‑up are required to obtain more precise estimates and to confirm these associations.

Our findings indicate that delaying RAI therapy beyond 6 months after thyroidectomy is associated with worse outcomes in patients with aggressive histology or lymph node metastases. Clinically, these results support prioritizing RAI within 6 months for patients with these high‑risk features. Multidisciplinary teams (surgery, endocrinology, nuclear medicine) should consider earlier scheduling of RAI for such patients when logistically feasible.

Our study had some limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting the results. First, the retrospective design introduced a potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors. Although we attempted to mitigate these factors through propensity score matching and multivariable adjustment, residual confounding factors cannot be excluded. Second, baseline characteristic imbalances between the groups, particularly the higher prevalence of aggressive histology in the delayed treatment group and more frequent lymph node metastases in the early treatment group, may have influenced the outcomes despite statistical adjustments.

Third, our relatively modest sample size (n = 132), which was adequate for primary analyses, limited the precision of estimates in the subgroup analyses, as evidenced by the wide confidence intervals for some hazard ratios. Fourth, the follow-up duration (median 18 ± 6 months) is insufficient to capture late recurrence events, which can occur up to 8–10 years after initial treatment in DTC. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as predictors of early treatment response and not necessarily as definitive indicators of long-term recurrence or survival.

Additional limitations include the absence of a centralized pathology review, which may introduce variability in histological classification, differences in TSH stimulation methods between patients, which could influence RAI uptake and efficacy, and potential dose allocation bias, as higher doses were not randomly assigned to patients with multiple risk factors. The absence of a comparison group of intermediate-risk patients who did not receive RAI limited the ability to assess the absolute benefits of RAI therapy in this population. Another limitation of this study is the lack of systematic molecular data (e.g., BRAFV600E, TERT promoter mutations). Integration of molecular markers in future research could refine risk stratification and help identify which patients derive the greatest benefit from earlier RAI administration. We recommend that subsequent studies collect molecular profiles and evaluate interactions between molecular status, histopathologic features, and time to RAI. Finally, although our statistical approach is appropriate, some analyses may be susceptible to overfitting of our dataset27–29.

In conclusion, in our multicenter retrospective cohort of intermediate-risk DTC patients, a delay in the first adjuvant RAI beyond 6 months was associated with a lower probability of an excellent early response, especially among patients with lymph node metastases or aggressive histologic variants. These findings highlight that earlier RAI administration may be beneficial in selected high-risk subsets; however, the retrospective design, nonrandomized dose allocation, and relatively short follow-up limit causal interpretation. Prospective, randomized, or protocol-driven studies with standardized dosing, integrated molecular profiling, and longer surveillance are required to determine the optimal timing of adjuvant RAI and to guide practice recommendations.
