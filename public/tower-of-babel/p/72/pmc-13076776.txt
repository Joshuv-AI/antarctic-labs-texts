Identification and detection of toxic substances involved in Gelsemium elegans Benth poisoning case
Authors: Zou Lu, Lin Ye, Wu Anzhong, Huang Junfei, Pan Runsang, Guo Hua, Liu Liya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, in response to a Gelsemium elegans Benth poisoning incident triggered by the consumption of homemade infused wine, the collected infused wine and the patient’s biological samples (blood and urine) were systematically examined using an integrated analytical strategy that combines UPLC-Q-TOF-MS non-targeted screening and UPLC-MS/MS targeted quantification. By constructing a database of Gelsemium elegans Benth alkaloid components, a total of nine major alkaloid components were identified, and a rapid quantitative analysis method was established for five of the key components (Gelsemine, Koumine, Gelsenicine, Gelsevirine, Humantenine). The results of the study showed that the content of Gelsemium elegans Benth alkaloids in infused wine was high, with Gelsenicine amounting to 60.87 µg/mL, which was estimated on the basis of its LD₅₀ (0.1–0.2 mg/kg bw) reported in the literature, and that an adult’s intake of 115–230 mL can be lethal. In the methodological validation, the spiked recoveries of infused wine and urine samples were 87.16%-105.53% with an RSD of < 7%, indicating that the method is accurate, reliable and does not require an internal standard, which makes it suitable for emergency testing in economically underdeveloped areas. In addition, Humantenine was detected in the urine of all poisoned individuals in this study, suggesting its potential as a potential marker for poisoning. Although the blood samples could not be quantified accurately due to matrix interference, the established coupling technology provides an effective analytical paradigm for the rapid identification and mechanism study of poisoning, and provides technical support for clinical diagnosis and public health emergency response.Keywords: Poisoning, UPLC-Q-TOF-MS, UPLC-MS/MS, Gelsemium elegans Benth, Rapid detectionSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Chemistry, Drug discovery

Introduction

Gelsemium elegans Benth, commonly known as the “heartbreak plant,” is an evergreen woody vine belonging to the family Strychnaceae1. It is recognized as one of the most toxic plants due to its high concentration of potent indole alkaloids, such as Gelsemine, Gelsenicine, Humantenine, and Koumine2. These neurotoxic alkaloids act primarily by inhibiting central nervous system function, which can lead to coma and fatal respiratory paralysis in severe cases.

The plant is prevalent in regions with rich biodiversity, such as Guizhou Province in China, where its morphological resemblance to edible plants like honeysuckle poses a significant public health risk3. Local traditions of foraging wild plants for medicinal soups or health wines further increase the likelihood of accidental poisoning. Due to this similarity and generally low public awareness of its toxicity, mistaken consumption has led to repeated poisoning outbreaks5. One tragic incident occurred in Guizhou, China, in June 2025, where four individuals were poisoned after consuming homemade infused wine, resulting in one fatality. This event underscores the urgent need for reliable and accessible detection methods to support clinical diagnosis and public health intervention.

Currently, there is no standardized national method for detecting Gelsemium alkaloids in complex matrices such as infused wine or biological fluids. While liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) offers high sensitivity and accuracy for quantitative analysis, it depends heavily on reference standards, limiting its utility in screening unknown or unexpected toxins6,7. Conversely, high-resolution mass spectrometry techniques such as UPLC-Q-TOF-MS provide excellent qualitative capability for non-targeted screening but often lack the sensitivity and quantitative precision required for trace-level confirmation in forensic or clinical samples8,9. Moreover, many laboratories lack specialized databases and authentic standards, further constraining effective response in poisoning emergencies.

To address these limitations, this study proposes an integrated analytical strategy that combines the broad screening capability of UPLC-Q-TOF-MS with the precise quantification of UPLC-MS/MS. Distinct from previous laboratory-based method development or studies focusing on single matrices, our work is driven by and applied to an actual, acute mass poisoning event. Using samples from the 2025 Guizhou poisoning incident, we first constructed a practical mass spectral database for rapid on-site alkaloid identification, and then performed targeted quantification of key toxins to confirm the source and assess exposure. This approach not only confirmed the etiology but also, through the systematic analysis of patient samples, revealed the consistent presence of Humantenine in urine, suggesting its novel potential as a practical exposure biomarker for similar incidents. This case-driven application demonstrates the direct utility of the integrated strategy in public health emergency response.

The integrated strategy developed here not only provides a reliable technical framework for diagnosing similar poisoning events but also enhances the accuracy and timeliness of public health responses, potentially informing the development of future standard protocols.

Materials and methods

Instrumentation

In sample preparation procedure following appliances were used, AE240 0.0001 g Electronic Analytical Balance (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland), Milli-Q Ultrapure Water System (Merck, USA), 2300TH Ultrasonicator (Shanghai Ampco), 3-30KS Bench-Top High-Speed Refrigerated Centrifuge (Sigma, Germany), MX16-Waring-CB15K Homogenizer (Shanghai Beyotime Co., Ltd.), MS3 Vortex Mixer (IKA, Germany), T460 H Ultrasonic Cleaning Equipment (Beijing Boduoxing Instruments). Data were acquired by Agilent 6545 UPLC-Q-TOF-MS (Agilent Technologies, USA), Agilent 6470 UPLC-MS/MS(Agilent Technologies, USA).

Reagents

Formic acid (mass spectrometry grade, Aladdin, China), methanol and acetonitrile (chromatography grade, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), ethanol (analytical grade, China National Pharmaceutical Company, China), 98% Humantenine (Lot: TC0575-241010, 10 mg), Koumine(Lot: TC0567-240621, 10 mg), Gelsemine (Lot: TC0568-240420, 10 mg), Gelsevirine (Lot: TC0574-240907, 10 mg) reference standards were purchased from Chengdu Reni Company, 98% Gelsenicine (Lot: DG002B401, 20 mg) reference standard was purchased from Beijing Beina Bio Company.

Sample Preparation

All biospecimens for this analysis of poisoning cases were collected in June 2025. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The Human Ethics Committee of Guizhou Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention waived the requirement for informed consent for this study. This waiver was granted because the research involved acute poisoning cases where the situations were emergencies and time-critical. The primary aim of the study is to derive constructive insights to improve protocols for other institutions handling similar public health emergencies. Furthermore, all patient data were fully anonymized prior to our analysis to ensure confidentiality and privacy.

Urine: Measure 100 µL of urine accurately, add 900 µL of acetonitrile to precipitate the protein, vortexed at 2500 r/min for 5 min, evaporation to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen, and re-dissolve with the initial mobile phase(methanol:0.1% formic acid in water = 20:80, v/v).

Blood: 100 µL of blood was accurately measured, 900 µL of acetonitrile was added to precipitate the protein, vortexed at 2500 r/min for 5 min, the nitrogen was blown to dryness, and then re-dissolved with the initial mobile phase.

Infused wine: dilute the infused wine 10 times with methanol, then accurately measure 100 µL of diluted infused wine, add 900 µL of methanol, vortexed 2500 r/min for 5 min, blow it dry with nitrogen, and re-dissolve it with the initial mobile phase.

all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Preparation of standard references

Dissolve the whole bottle of Humantenine, Koumine, Gelsemine, Gelsevirine and Gelsenicine in methanol, and then volume it to 10 mL in a volumetric flask to obtain 1 mg/mL of Humantenine, Koumine, Gelsemine, Gelsevirine and 2 mg/mL of Gelsenicine. Then take 1 mL of 1 mg/mL of Humantenine, Koumine, Gelsemine, Gelsevirine and 0.5 mL of 2 mg/mL of Gelsenicine in the same 100 mL volumetric flask, and then condense to the scale to obtain 10 µg/mL of Humantenine, Koumine, Gelsemine, Gelsevirine and Gelsenicine mixed standard solution. Another 2 mL of 10 µg/mL was taken in a 100 mL volumetric flask and the initial mobile phase was calibrated only to scale to give a 200 ng/mL mixed standard solution. Using this mixed standard solution, a series of standards of 2, 4, 10, 20, 40, 80, 120, 160, and 200 ng/mL were then prepared. The processed infused wine and urine matrix were then used to formulate a 1:1 series of standards to obtain a series of standards of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 ng/mL.

General mass spectrometric ion source conditions

Both mass spectrometric analyses utilized an Agilent Jet Stream electrospray ionization (AJS-ESI) source operated in positive ion mode. The following source parameters were applied on both instruments: sheath gas temperature, 350 °C; drying gas temperature, 350 °C; nebulizer pressure, 45 psi; and sheath gas flow rate, 10 L/min. The spray voltage was set to 3000 V for the Q-TOF system and similarly optimized for the triple quadrupole system.

UPLC-Q-TOF-MS analysis conditions

The sample solution (3 µL) was injected into an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (Agilent 1290 Infinity II) for separation. The separation was performed on an Agilent Eclipse Plus C18 column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) at 35 °C, using a mobile phase composed of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (solvent B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. A gradient elution program was employed as follows: 80% A was held isocratically from 0 to 20 min, then linearly decreased to 20% A between 20.1 and 25 min, and subsequently maintained at 20% A until the end of the run.

The eluent was introduced into a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Agilent 6545 Q-TOF). Ionization was achieved under the general source conditions described in Sect. 2.5, with the following additional settings: capillary voltage, 4.0 kV; fragmentor voltage, 120 V. Full-scan mass spectra were acquired over the m/z range of 50–950. Data collection and processing were carried out using Agilent MassHunter Workstation software.

UPLC-MS/MS analysis conditions

The chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) maintained at 35 °C. The injection volume was 3 µL. A binary mobile phase system consisting of methanol (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent B) was used at a constant flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. The following gradient program was applied: 10% A from 0 to 0.5 min, increasing linearly to 20% A by 1.0 min, then to 90% A by 10.0 min, holding at 90% A until 12.0 min, and finally returning to 10% A at 12.01 min and maintaining this composition until 14.0 min for column re-equilibration.

The eluate was subsequently analyzed using a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. The electrospray ionization source was operated under the conditions specified in Sect. 2.5. Data acquisition was conducted in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The specific mass transitions and corresponding compound-dependent parameters are detailed in Table 1.

Quantitative analysis

Selectivity

Since these nine alkaloids are not consistently present across all parts of Gelsemium elegans Benth., five major alkaloids (gelsemine, koumine, etc.) that are commonly reported in the literature10 and prevalent in the plant were selected for method development and quantification. Following the quantitative analysis conditions described in Sect. 2.3, the standard solutions was injected into the UPLC-MS/MS system for analysis.

Chromatographic column selection

In this paper, three columns of different brands and models were investigated for method development, namely ACQUITY HSS T3 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm), ACQUITY BEH C18 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm), and Agilent RRHD C18 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The comparative procedure was designed to evaluate their selectivity, efficiency, and peak shape for the target analytes under identical chromatographic conditions. The key differences between the columns lie in their stationary phase chemistry and particle technology. The comparison was performed by injecting the same standard mixture on each column under identical chromatographic conditions (flow rate, temperature, and gradient profile).

Linearity, LOD and LOQ

A series of matrix-matched standard solutions with known concentrations were prepared according to Sect. 2.1.2. These solutions were analyzed under the chromatographic conditions specified in Sect. 2.3. For each target component, a calibration curve was established by plotting the mass concentration (X-axis) against the corresponding peak area (Y-axis). From this curve, the regression equation, correlation coefficient (r), and linear range were determined.

To determine the limits of detection and quantification, the mixed standard solution was further subjected to a gradient dilution series with the matrix blank. Each diluted sample was analyzed, and the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) was measured. The limit of detection (LOD) was defined as the mass concentration yielding an S/N of 3, and the limit of quantification (LOQ) was defined as the mass concentration yielding an S/N of 10.

The calibration curves described above were used to determine the analyte concentration (c, ng/mL) in the final prepared test solution. This value (c) was subsequently converted to the concentration in the original sample (C, in µg/mL or ng/mL) using the equations specified in Sect. 2.9.

Accuracy and precision

The matrix-matched standard samples for recovery studies were prepared as follows. Briefly, 100 µL of 10-fold diluted blank wine or urine was accurately measured. Subsequently, the samples were spiked with either 50, 200, or 350 µL of the 200 ng/mL mixed standard solution, each prepared in six parallel replicates. Next, 800 µL of acetonitrile was added to each sample as a protein precipitation solvent. The mixtures were vortexed at 2500 r/min for 5 min. After vortexing, the solutions were dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen. Finally, the residues were reconstituted with the initial mobile phase for subsequent chromatographic analysis. The calculated value, expressed as (measured concentration / nominal concentration) × 100%, represents the accuracy of the overall analytical method. This parameter reflects the combined efficiency of the sample preparation procedure (including extraction recovery), potential matrix effects, and instrumental performance.

Chromatographic column selection

The performance of three commercially available UPLC columns with different stationary phases was systematically evaluated for the separation of the five target alkaloids under identical chromatographic conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 3, significant differences in separation efficiency and peak shape were observed. The Agilent RRHD C18 column resulted in peak tailing and insufficient resolution between two critical analyte pairs. The ACQUITY BEH C18 column exhibited notably poor peak shape and broadened peaks, which would adversely affect sensitivity and integration accuracy. In contrast, the ACQUITY HSS T3 column provided the most satisfactory overall performance. Although baseline separation was not achieved for one pair of analytes, the peak symmetry was excellent and the resolution was sufficient to allow for reliable integration and accurate quantification without interference. Based on these results, the ACQUITY HSS T3 column was selected for all subsequent method development and validation work. It is noteworthy that Gelsenicine and Gelsevirine (peaks 3 and 4) were not fully baseline-resolved, with a chromatographic resolution (Rs) of approximately 1.25. This is attributed to their high structural similarity, which poses a significant challenge for reversed-phase chromatographic separation. However, for UPLC-MS/MS quantification, the primary selectivity is conferred by the unique multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions. Individual standard analysis confirmed the absence of mass spectrometric cross-talk between their designated quantifier ions (Gelsenicine: 327.2 > 265.1; Gelsevirine: 353.2 > 322.2). Consequently, even with partial co-elution, the signals for each analyte are independently and accurately acquired. The excellent accuracy and precision results obtained for both compounds during method validation (see Sect. 3.6.4) confirm the reliability of quantification using this chromatographic condition.

Accuracy and precision

The accuracy of the method were evaluated through a spike-and-recovery experiment. Blank matrices (infused wine and urine) were fortified with the target alkaloids at three concentration levels (low, medium, and high), with six replicates per level analyzed over three separate days. The mean recovery rates for all components, calculated as (measured concentration / nominal concentration) × 100%, ranged from 87.16% to 105.53%. The relative standard deviations (RSDs) were below 7% across all concentration levels. These results, summarized in Table 5, demonstrate that the method possesses good accuracy and repeatability, meeting the acceptance criteria for bioanalytical method validation.

Discussion

The integrated analytical strategy successfully identified and quantified Gelsemium elegans alkaloids in the poisoning samples, confirming contaminated infused wine as the source of poisoning. As detailed in Table 6, the wine contained a high total alkaloid burden (181.51 µg/mL). The most abundant component was Humantenine (74.51 ± 2.44 µg/mL), followed by Gelsenicine (61.06 ± 2.50 µg/mL), which is reported to be one of the most toxic constituents with an LD₅₀ of 0.1–0.2 mg/kg bw in mice11. It is noteworthy that Gelsenicine was also quantified in the urine of one patient (Urine Sample 2, 11.13 ng/mL), confirming its systemic absorption and renal excretion. The concentration of Gelsenicine in the wine (61.06 µg/mL) was exceptionally high. While direct extrapolation of animal LD₅₀ values to humans is not scientifically valid and involves substantial uncertainty, the detected concentration indicates that the wine represented a potentially lethal exposure range. The fatal outcome for one individual likely resulted from the ingestion of a substantial volume of this highly toxic preparation, combined with potential individual variations in toxicokinetics.

A significant challenge in this study was the matrix effect, which differed markedly across sample types. Consistent with challenges reported for complex biological and food matrices12,13, we observed ion suppression in infused wine and ion enhancement in urine and blood. The use of matrix-matched calibration standards effectively compensated for these interferences in wine and urine, yielding excellent linearity (r ≥ 0.9950) and accurate recoveries (87.16–105.53%) for all five target al.kaloids. This demonstrates the method’s suitability for these matrices.

However, the blood matrix presented an extreme challenge. Protein precipitation with acetonitrile, while effective for urine and simple wine matrices, proved insufficient for blood. This is primarily due to the far greater complexity of whole blood, which contains high concentrations of proteins, phospholipids, and cellular components that are not fully removed by a single precipitation step. These co-precipitated or remaining matrix constituents cause severe and variable ion suppression/enhancement in the mass spectrometer, leading to the observed poor linearity, low recovery, and unacceptable accuracy.

Given the emergency nature of this case analysis, our priority was to establish a rapid, unified sample preparation protocol across all matrices. While more selective techniques such as solid-phase extraction (SPE) or dedicated phospholipid removal plates are known to significantly improve sample cleanup for blood14,15, these were not pursued in the initial phase due to time constraints and the need for method simplicity for potential field deployment. Dilution strategies were considered but deemed likely to compromise sensitivity below the required level for detecting these trace alkaloids. This limitation has direct implications for the forensic and toxicological interpretation of the case. The absence of quantitative blood data prevents the determination of peak serum concentrations, which are crucial for correlating exposure levels with clinical symptoms and for estimating the ingested dose through toxicokinetic modeling. Consequently, our risk assessment relied primarily on the extremely high toxin concentrations quantified in the source (the wine) and the confirmed presence of toxins in urine as evidence of systemic exposure. For future incidents where blood is the primary available matrix, the development of a validated SPE-based method, ideally employing stable isotope-labeled internal standards, will be essential to provide the forensically defensible data required for comprehensive toxicological evaluation.

The developed UPLC-MS/MS method for wine and urine achieved high sensitivity (LODs: 0.03–0.10 ng/mL) and precision (RSD < 7%) using an external matrix-matched calibration approach without an internal standard (IS). The decision to forgo an IS was strategic, considering the primary context of this work: rapid emergency response in potentially resource-constrained settings. For this application, matrix-matched calibration provides a practically sufficient level of control by correcting for the average ionization efficiency and sample loss across a batch of similar samples16–18. Our validation demonstrated that this approach yielded excellent accuracy (87.16–105.53%) and precision (RSD < 7%) for the target matrices, meeting the requirements for reliable screening and semi-quantitative to quantitative analysis in a public health crisis.

It is important to explicitly acknowledge that this approach has inherent limitations compared to methods employing isotope-labeled internal standards. A stable isotope-labeled IS is universally recognized as the gold standard in quantitative bioanalysis, particularly for forensic or regulated applications, because it can correct for both matrix effects and extraction recovery on a per-sample basis, thereby providing superior accuracy and robustness against variability19,20. This point is illustrated by a direct comparison: for instance, Wang et al.7 employed a deuterated internal standard for gelsemine quantification in rat plasma, achieving exceptional precision (RSD < 5%). While our method’s precision is comparable for its intended use, the absence of an IS makes it more susceptible to fluctuations in ionization efficiency during long analytical sequences or when faced with highly variable sample matrices. Therefore, we position our method not as a replacement for gold-standard IS-based protocols, but as a pragmatic and accessible alternative for frontline laboratories where speed, cost, and operational simplicity are paramount, and where the validated performance in wine and urine matrices is fit for the purpose of exposure confirmation and source identification. For scenarios demanding the highest possible certainty (e.g., legal proceedings), the implementation of isotope-labeled internal standards is strongly recommended.

Recent studies on Gelsemium alkaloid analysis primarily focus on specific matrices using UPLC-MS/MS. For instance, Yang et al.6 determined Koumine, Gelsemine, and Gelsenicine in hair with LODs around 0.002–0.005 ng/mg, while Wang et al.7 achieved an LOD of 0.05 ng/mL for Gelsemine in rat plasma. Our method provides comparable sensitivity for a broader panel of five key alkaloids in complex, real-world matrices (wine and urine). Furthermore, the concentrations quantified in our case (e.g., tens of µg/mL in wine) are of the same order of magnitude as those reported in fatal poisoning cases involving plant material5. This demonstrates the method’s applicability for the rapid quantification of toxins in real-world poisoning incidents, supporting its potential utility in clinical and public health emergency settings.

It is important to consider the intended application scope of the developed method. As noted in the validation, the method does not employ an internal standard, and the protein precipitation protocol, while rapid and simple, is susceptible to matrix effects which were compensated for but not fully characterized through dedicated experiments. These factors, particularly the absence of isotopically labeled internal standards, limit the method’s suitability for forensic toxicology, where the unequivocal accuracy and legal defensibility of results are paramount19,20. However, for clinical and public health emergency response – the primary context of this study – the priorities are speed, accessibility, and reliable semi-quantitative to quantitative data to guide diagnosis and intervention. The method’s validation demonstrated excellent accuracy and precision for its intended matrices (wine and urine) using matrix-matched calibration, meeting the needs for this purpose. Future work aimed at forensic applications would necessitate the incorporation of internal standards and more extensive validation, including complete assessment of extraction recovery and matrix effect.

This study underscores the value of an integrated analytical approach. UPLC-Q-TOF-MS, with its high-resolution accurate mass capability, was indispensable for the non-targeted screening and preliminary identification of nine alkaloids in the absence of a complete MS/MS spectral library. This rapid screening narrowed the focus to the most prevalent toxins. Subsequent targeted UPLC-MS/MS analysis then provided the selectivity and sensitivity necessary for precise quantification of the five major alkaloids, which is critical for toxicological assessment. This combined strategy is particularly effective for poisoning incidents involving unknown or multiple toxins.

Humantenine was the only alkaloid detected in all urine samples from the three poisoned patients in this incident, suggesting its consistent excretion following Gelsemium elegans exposure. Based on this preliminary observation, we propose humantenine as a promising candidate biomarker for exposure assessment in similar poisoning cases. Its relatively high abundance in the plant and consistent detectability in urine in this small cohort support its potential utility for this purpose. However, this proposal is based on a very limited sample size (n = 3) and requires validation in larger, independent cohorts to establish its sensitivity, specificity, and quantitative relationship with exposure dose. If validated, humantenine could become a valuable target for routine monitoring and a priority for the stockpiling of reference standards in public health laboratories serving endemic regions.

This study has certain limitations. First, as noted in the methodology, the accuracy determined here represents the efficiency of the entire analytical process. The individual contributions of extraction recovery and matrix effect were not separately quantified, as the primary validation criterion met was the reliable measurement of analyte concentrations in the studied matrices. Secondly, the partial chromatographic co-elution of the structurally analogous alkaloids Gelsenicine and Gelsevirine, while effectively addressed by the specificity of MRM detection in this study, represents a chromatographic challenge. Future optimization could explore alternative stationary phases or chromatographic modes to achieve baseline separation. Third, the small sample size (n = 3 for urine) limits the generalizability of the observation regarding Humantenine as a potential biomarker. Fourth, the method’s current inapplicability to blood analysis restricts the assessment of the most toxicologically relevant matrix. Future work should therefore focus on: (1) expanding the sample set to validate Humantenine as a robust biomarker; (2) refining the sample preparation protocol for blood (e.g., exploring alternative extraction sorbents or introducing an internal standard); (3) further optimizing the chromatography to fully resolve all critical analyte pairs; and (4) applying this coupled strategy to a broader range of plant poisoning incidents to test its universality.

In summary, the principal contributions of this study are threefold. First, it provides a complete, case-driven analytical workflow from non-targeted screening to targeted quantification for a real-world Gelsemium elegans poisoning cluster, moving beyond controlled experimental settings. Second, it establishes and shares a practical, compound-specific database that can facilitate rapid toxin identification in future incidents, especially in resource-limited settings lacking comprehensive commercial libraries. Third, it identifies Humantenine as a consistently detected analyte in patient urine, proposing it as a candidate biomarker for exposure assessment that merits further validation. While the analytical techniques employed are established, their integrated application to solve an immediate public health investigation and generate these actionable insights constitutes the core novelty and translational value of this work.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes and validates an integrated analytical strategy coupling UPLC-Q-TOF-MS and UPLC-MS/MS, which was successfully applied to resolve a real-world Gelsemium elegans mass poisoning event. This approach enabled the rapid non-targeted identification and subsequent precise quantification of the principal toxic alkaloids, unequivocally confirming the contaminated infused wine as the poisoning source. The methodology, optimized and validated for wine and urine matrices, provides a reliable, cost-effective tool well-suited for clinical and public health emergency response where rapid toxin confirmation is critical.

The work also yields important toxicological insights: the detection of alkaloids in patient urine confirms systemic exposure, and the consistent presence of humantenine nominates it as a promising candidate biomarker for future exposure assessment, pending validation in larger studies. A key limitation identified is the method’s current incompatibility with blood analysis due to pronounced matrix effects, pointing to the need for advanced sample cleanup or internal standardization in future forensic-oriented method development.

Looking forward, this case-driven analytical framework, along with the generated compound database, offers a practical blueprint for the investigation of similar plant poisoning incidents, with the potential to improve diagnostic speed and public health intervention outcomes.
