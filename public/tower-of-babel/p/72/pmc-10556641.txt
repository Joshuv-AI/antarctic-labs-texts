Digital twin for pandemic monitoring and prevention: Urgent need for agreements for global data sharing
Authors: Anna Mia Ekström, Ole Petter Ottersen
Published: 2023

By combining international air travel passenger data with standard epidemiological modeling, Stenseth et al. (1) show that reduced flying between countries, with varying spread of an infectious agent may be a more efficient step toward infectious disease control than enforcing immigration quarantine. Although these results are based on data accumulated during the COVID-19 pandemic, they could be of fundamental importance also for other pathogens, by indicating how innovative approaches could help build better preparedness for new pandemics and—depending on the pattern of spread–even prevent local epidemics from developing into global ones.

During COVID-19, expert authorities and political decision-makers had to react swiftly to curb the spread of disease. Invasive public health measures were introduced with insufficient evidence both with regard to their direct and indirect consequences relative to other interventions. The next time around tools should be in place to make nonpharmaceutical interventions more precise to prevent unwanted societal impacts. The work of Stenseth et al. (1) is promising and suggests that “digital twins” (2) can be established to closely monitor the development of new epidemics and to inform decisions aimed at containing outbreaks.

Various data combinations or digital twins including a myriad of relevant observations, could be developed well beyond the data used by Stenseth et al. (1). However, there is a significant caveat: Surveillance data must be made accessible, globally and in real time, for a digital twin to be effective and reliable. There must be a full commitment to data sharing across nations and sectors. To this end, international agreements are urgently needed.

Data sharing is a crucial point in the current deliberations aiming at establishing a “pandemic treaty” (3). Effective access to viral genome data is particularly important. To gain robust support for data sharing, it is essential that mechanisms are brought into place that ensure transparency and equitable benefits also of outputs of such data. During COVID-19, some low- and middle-income countries that willingly uploaded viral sequences were punished through trade restrictions and were the last to receive vaccines (4). Such a discord undermines incentives for data sharing. An agreement to data sharing must therefore include a promise for a more equitable distribution of vaccines and medicines, according to need (4, 5).

The approach introduced by Stenseth et al. (1) should be considered in the armamentarium against new epidemics and pandemics. However, the versatility of this approach depends entirely on unfettered data access. Withholding data will thwart any attempt to build digital twins for more evidence-based decisions on nonmedical interventions. With tools in hand and with a looming risk of new pandemics, the case for global cooperation, solidarity, and equity stands as more compelling than ever before.
