To treat or not to treat: The clue lies in multimodal imaging

A 31-year-old Asian-Indian female presented with complaints of diminution of vision and photopsias in her left eye (OS) for the past 2 weeks. She had a visual acuity of 20/20 in the right eye (OD) and 20/30 in OS with normal intraocular pressure in both the eyes. Findings

The multimodal imaging features suggest diagnosis of MEWDS. The “Wreath-like” pattern on FFA and the absence of early hypofluorescence on ICGA are hallmark features and help to differentiate MEWDS from other chorioretinopathies. The serology for syphilis came negative. The patient was followed up weekly for evaluation of the lesions through serial fundus autofluorescence and OCT scans. On subsequent scans, hyperautoflourescent spots decreased on FAF [Fig. 2a and b], and the ellipsoid zone and external limiting membrane recovered with disappearance of the subretinal hyperreflective material on OCT [Fig. 2c]. The lesions completely resolved by 4 weeks with complete restoration of visual acuity without any treatment.

Diagnosis: Multiple Evanescent White Dot Syndrome

Discussion

MEWDS, first documented by Jampol and Sieving1 et al. in 1984, presents with sudden-onset vision loss accompanied by visual field defects like paracentral scotomas or enlarged blind spot and can be accompanied by photopsia and dyschromatopsia.[1]

MEWDS is mostly a unilateral, self-limiting disorder with rapid resolution and can involve both eyes, recur, and occasionally lead to complications such as choroidal neovascularization or late chorioretinal scarring.[2] It may also be associated with or preceded by other inflammatory conditions like acute macular neuro retinopathy, multifocal choroiditis, and panuveitis, or acute zonal occult outer retinopathy, ocular syphilis, recurrent toxoplasmosis, and candida endophthalmitis, sometimes occurring with post-viral infections or vaccinations. In such cases, the diagnosis is labeled as epi-MEWDS.[3]

SUN-2 criteria for diagnosis of MEWDS include multifocal gray-white chorioretinal spots with foveal granularity, wreath-like FA hyperfluorescence, and/or hyperreflective lesions from RPE through the ellipsoid zone into the outer nuclear layer on OCT in the absence of anterior chamber/vitreous inflammation.[4] Recently, Multi-modal Imaging in Uveitis (MUV) task force came up with multimodal imaging guidelines for diagnosis of MEWDS. Minimal imaging criteria for acute MEWDS include: yellowish-gray-white granular lesions (size: 50–200 µm), multifocal ellipsoid/interdigitation zone disruption with overlying outer retinal hyper-reflectivity (“puff balls”) on OCT, and hyperautofluorescent wreath-like spots (short-wave FAF).[5]

While the prognosis for MEWDS is generally favorable, with lesions typically self-resolving and vision returning to normal within weeks to months, it is crucial to consider serious differential diagnosis, such as syphilis (in younger patients) or lymphoma (in elderly patients), which can masquerade as MEWDS and require distinct management approaches.[6]
