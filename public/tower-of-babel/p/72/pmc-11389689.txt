Viscoelastic and Birefringence Relaxation of Individualized Cellulose Nanofibers in the Dilute and Semidilute Regions
Authors: Reina Tanaka, Tadashi Inoue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Viscoelastic relaxation mechanisms of individualized cellulose nanofibers (iCNFs) dispersed in glycerol in the dilute and semidilute regions were investigated by linear viscoelastic and dynamic birefringence measurements. The birefringence relaxation of the iCNFs was described by the orientational and curvature modes of an existing viscoelastic theory for ideal semiflexible polymers (Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory). However, the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory could not fully describe the iCNF viscoelastic relaxation at high frequencies. Considering the results for birefringence relaxation, the experimental tension mode of the iCNFs was evaluated to be higher than the theoretical value. These results show that the viscoelastic relaxations of the iCNFs are different from those of ideal semiflexible polymers, in contrast to cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs). As the iCNF concentration increased, the orientational mode dramatically slowed, which was more drastic than other semiflexible polymers, including CNCs. This anomalous behavior is likely due to the nonideal nature of iCNFs.

Introduction

Elucidating the polymer relaxation processes is essential to understanding their fundamental molecular dynamics. The relaxation of rigid rods and flexible polymers has been extensively investigated by linear viscoelastic measurements, and the molecular theories of the linear viscoelasticity for rigid rods and flexible polymers in the dilute region were established many decades ago.1−5 On the other hand, semiflexible polymers in the dilute region show unique viscoelastic relaxation different from rigid rods and flexible polymers. In the dilute solution of semiflexible polymers, both the storage modulus, G′, and the loss modulus, G″, show a power-law dependence on frequency, ω, at high frequencies (G′∼G″∼ω2/3) for many kinds of semiflexible polymers such as proteins,6,7 xanthan,8 and poly-γ-benzyl-l-glutamate (PBLG).9 The viscoelastic relaxation of semiflexible polymers has long been explained phenomenologically by the hybrid theory combining the theories for rigid rods and flexible polymers because of lack of adequate molecular theories.6−9 The molecular theory considering the thermal motion for semiflexible polymers was proposed in the 2000s by Morse and his co-workers (hereafter denoted as the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory) based on the worm-like chain model whose contour lengths, L, are shorter than persistence lengths q, (L ≤ q), where q is defined as the ratio of the bending rigidity κ to the thermal energy kBT.10 The details are explained in Supporting Information, S1. Unfortunately, studies comparing the theory with experimental data are still limited,10−13 but its applicability to semiflexible polymers has been established at least for synthetic polymers.11,12

Nanocellulose, a new nanomaterial of plant origin, has attracted much interest concerning SDGs. Thanks to superior properties such as high crystal moduli,14−16 high strength,17 low thermal expansion,18 and biological compatibility, nanocellulose is emerging as a sustainable, high-performance material. To effectively utilize nanocellulose made from natural materials in industry and to guarantee their quality, it is essential to accurately characterize nanocellulose at the same level of analysis performed on polymeric materials today. Our research is based on this idea. Nanocellulose is prepared as aqueous dispersions, and nanocellulose products are prepared by processing aqueous dispersions. To design high-performance nanocellulose products, it is necessary to understand the dynamics of the dispersions. In particular, viscoelastic relaxation is a fundamental indicator that reflects the dynamics and properties of nanocellulose in fluids and is essential for the design and optimization of nanocellulose product manufacturing processes. Thus, elucidating the relaxation of nanocellulose will not only deepen the fundamental understandings of nanocellulose but will also contribute to the promotion of its industrial use. Nanocellulose is categorized into rod-shaped cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) and cellulose nanofibers (CNFs). In our previous study, we demonstrated that the viscoelastic relaxations of CNCs in the dilute region are explained by the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory for semiflexible polymers.19 The CNCs show relaxations derived from internal motions or tension and bending, in addition to that derived from the orientation of rods. The q values of softwood CNCs are estimated to be about ten times L values. Based on this knowledge, CNFs, which are more flexible than CNCs, would show more complicated relaxations. In fact, the relaxation behavior of CNFs in the dilute region has rarely been analyzed, partly due to their complexity.20 One of the major uses of CNFs is the filler of polymeric materials.21,22 In such applications, the tension and bending modes would considerably contribute to the reinforcement effects. CNFs cannot take a straight form due to the thermal motion, and this undulated structure reduces the reinforcement effect and also is the molecular origin of the tension mode. Thus, the study of high-frequency modes is quite important to understanding the equilibrium structure and resulting reinforcement effects.

In this study, we aimed to reveal the relaxation mechanism of CNFs in the dilute region by measuring the linear viscoelasticity and dynamic birefringence of their dispersions. For simplicity, individualized CNFs (iCNFs) were used as samples. Previous reports indicate that the combination of linear viscoelastic and dynamic birefringence measurements is effective for understanding the relaxation mechanism for semiflexible polymers.11−13,19 We also investigated the relaxation behavior of iCNFs in the semidilute region and examined the collision effect23 of the iCNFs in comparison with that of CNCs. Since CNFs are typically processed and handled in the semidilute region when preparing CNF products, understanding the dynamics in the semidilute region is necessary for optimizing the CNF product preparation process and improving product performance. The iCNFs with a uniform width (∼3 nm) were prepared from softwood bleached kraft pulp (SBKP) using 2,2,6,6,-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO)-mediated oxidation at pH 10 and subsequent sonication in distilled water.24 The complex shear moduli G*(ω) ≡ G′(ω)+iG″(ω) and complex strain-optical coefficient, K*(ω) ≡ K′(ω)+iK″(ω), of the CNFs were measured using a rheometer and a self-built apparatus for dynamic birefringence measurement on oscillatory shear deformation,25 respectively.

Experimental Section

Materials

A never-dried SBKP (Nippon Paper Industries Co. Ltd., Japan) with a water content of 80% and a ∼ 2 wt % RHEOCRYSTA I-2SXS CNF gel (DKS. Co. Ltd., Japan) were used as starting materials. Other laboratory-grade chemical reagents (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Japan) were used without further purification.

Preparation of iCNF and CNC Samples Dispersed in Glycerol

The iCNFs with a uniform width were prepared from SBKP by TEMPO-mediated oxidation and subsequent sonication in water according to a previously reported method.24 SBKP was oxidized using a TEMPO/NaBr/NaClO system (10 mmol NaClO per 1 g of SBKP) at pH 10, reduced with NaBH4 at pH 10,26 and then sonicated in distilled water at the solid concentration of 0.1% w/w for 8 and 20 min using a US-300E ultrasonic homogenizer (NIHONSEIKI KAISHA LTD., Japan). After removing unfibrillated fractions by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 20 min, the ∼0.1% w/w iCNF dispersions sonicated for 8 and 20 min were prepared (hereafter denoted as S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B, respectively). The 0.1% w/w aqueous S-CNF-A dispersions were diluted with distilled water, mixed with glycerol at a one-to-one ratio by weight, and then evaporated at 50 °C for 5 h in order to yield 0.063–0.315 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersions.27 A 0.252 kg m–3 S-CNF-B/glycerol dispersion was also prepared in a manner similar to the S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersions. The density of glycerol at 25 °C was determined as 1.259 g cm–3 in a previous study.19 CNCs were prepared from SBKP according to a previous study19 and used as a reference sample. This sample was denoted as S-CNC. The 0.126–3.777 kg m–3 S-CNC/glycerol dispersions were prepared in a similar manner to the S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B/glycerol dispersions.

Preparation of iCNF Samples Dispersed in a 60% Sucrose/Water Solution

The iCNFs dispersed in a 60% sucrose/water solution were prepared with reference to ref (20) in order to investigate the effect of dispersion media on the viscoelastic data for iCNFs. A 2.1% w/w RHEOCRYSTA I-2SXS CNF gel was disintegrated at 0.5% w/w using a double-cylinder homogenizer (PHYSCOTRON, MICROTEC Co. Ltd., Japan) at 7500 rpm for 4 min and then sonicated for 8 min using a VP-300N ultrasonic homogenizer (TAITEC Co., Japan). After centrifugation for 20 min, the ∼0.5% w/w sonicated iCNF dispersion was concentrated using an evaporator until the concentration reached ∼1.6% w/w (hereafter denoted as S-CNF-C). The concentrated S-CNF-C was mixed with water and sucrose at designated concentrations (S-CNF-C: 1.29 and 2.58 kg m–3, sucrose: 60% w/w). The mixture was stirred at 40 °C for 2 days and filtrated using nylon net filters (pore size: 11 μm, NY1109000, Merck KGaA., Germany) to remove visible dust. The 1.29 and 2.58 kg m–3 S-CNF-C/60% sucrose dispersions were then prepared.

Analyses

Contour lengths L of ∼200 individual S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B fibers were measured by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL JEM-2000EX, Tokyo, Japan), and the length distribution histogram was obtained (Figures 1 and S1a, respectively). The weighted-mean lengths, Lw, of the S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B were calculated as 355 and 319 nm, respectively, according to a previously reported method.28 The critical concentrations c*29 or the boundary concentrations between the dilute and semidilute regions of the S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B were estimated as 0.084 and 0.104 kg m–3 using the Lw and the previously reported height and width values, respectively (Table S1).30

Dynamic viscoelasticity of the 0.063–0.315 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersions and the 0.252 kg m–3 S-CNF-B/glycerol dispersion was measured at 5 to 45 °C using a stress-controlled rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar GmbH, Austria) with a cone–plate geometry (plate diameter: 50 mm, angle 2°). All of the measurements were performed within the linear viscoelastic region. The complex shear moduli G* of the dispersions were measured as a function of angular frequency ω.

Dynamic viscoelasticity of the 1.29 and 2.58 kg m–3 S-CNF-C/60% sucrose dispersions was measured from −25 to 25 °C using a HAAKE MARS I rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., U.S.A.) with a CC25 double-cylinder type geometry (diameter: 25 mm; length of the cylinder: 37.6 mm) and a CCB25 DIN/SS cup (diameter: 27.2 mm).

The complex strain-optical coefficient, K*(ω), or the complex ratio of the shear component of the refractive index tensor, nxy*(ω), to the oscillatory strain, γ*(ω),31 of the dispersions was evaluated as a function of ω.32 The details of the dynamic birefringence measurements have been reported elsewhere.25

Results and Discussion

Effects of Types of iCNFs and Kinds of Dispersion Media on Their Viscoelastic Data

We investigated the effects of types of iCNFs, in addition to kinds of dispersion media, on their viscoelastic data. First, the effects of types of iCNFs were investigated by comparing the viscoelastic data of the 0.063 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersion with those of the 0.252 kg m–3 S-CNF-B/glycerol dispersion (Figure 2). The difference in concentration, dispersion medium viscosity, and Lw was corrected by using the coefficients of rC, rη, and rL values, respectively (the details are shown in Table S2). Both of the samples showed the power-law type frequency dependence of G′. Such results were also observed for three different types of iCNFs dispersed in glycerol (the details are shown in Supporting Information Figure S6). These results showed that the viscoelastic data of iCNFs showed similar power-law type frequency dependence of G′ if the same dispersion medium is utilized.

Comparison of the viscoelastic data of the 0.063 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersion with those of the 0.252 kg m–3 S-CNF-B/glycerol dispersion. The G″ values include the viscous contribution of the dispersion medium (ωηs).

Next, we investigated the effects of dispersion media on the viscoelastic data of iCNFs by comparing the 0.063 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersion with the 1.29 and 2.58 kg m–3 S-CNF-C/60% sucrose dispersions (Figure 3). The previously reported viscoelastic data for the 2.58 kg m–3 RHEOCRYSTA CNF (R-CNF)/60% sucrose dispersion extracted from Figure 3b in ref (20) using the Engauge Digitizer Software35 are also shown in Figure 3 as a reference data. Here, we corrected the difference in concentration, dispersion medium viscosity, and iCNF average length by using the coefficients of rC, rη, and rL values, respectively (the details are shown in Table S2). The power-law frequency dependence of G′ values at ω ≳ 1 s–1 was observed for the S-CNF-C and R-CNF dispersed in a 60% sucrose solution, similarly to those for the S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B (Figure 2) dispersed in glycerol. These results show that the G′ values of iCNFs at high frequencies show the power-law frequency dependence, regardless of iCNF types and dispersion media of the iCNFs. On the other hand, the terminal relaxation behavior was observed for the S-CNF-C and R-CNF samples dispersed in a 60% sucrose solution at and ω ≲ 1 s–1, respectively. Such terminal relaxation behavior was not found for the S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B dispersed in glycerol. This is attributed to the fact that the measured concentration ranges of the S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B dispersed in glycerol were more than one order of magnitude lower than those of the S-CNF-C and R-CNF dispersed in a 60% sucrose solution, and thus, the viscoelastic data of the S-CNF-A and S-CNF-B/glycerol dispersions could not be obtained in the low-frequency range where the terminal relaxation is observed. In the examined frequency range, we cannot find the effect of the dispersion media of iCNFs on their viscoelastic data at low frequencies.

Viscoelastic Quantities of the iCNFs/Glycerol Dispersions

Matsuo et al. have reported some characteristic viscoelastic quantities including the limiting modulus at high frequencies, G∞, and the inverse of the steady-state compliance, Je–1, for the R-CNF/60% sucrose dispersions.20 Although they have argued that the G∞ value has been found for low-concentration samples including 2.58 kg m–3, their high-frequency data are not enough to determine the limiting value. As shown in Figure 3, the power-law type frequency dependence of G′ in high frequencies is clearly found for the 2.58 kg m–3 R-CNF/60% sucrose dispersion. This result was similarly observed for other iCNF samples regardless of the iCNF types and dispersion media of the iCNFs, which was shown in the previous section. Concerning the high-frequency modes, we also note that the power relaxation of G′ in the high-frequency range is universally observed in the viscoelastic data at a low concentration around the c* as well as at high concentrations (Figures 3b and 5 of ref (20)). Therefore, we consider that the G∞ values cannot be extracted in our data as well as in the data in ref (20) regardless of the iCNF types and dispersion media of the iCNFs. Similar frequency dependencies of G′ were also reported for other semiflexible polymers such as F-actin solutions (G′ ∼ ω)34 and single-walled carbon nanotubes (G′ ∼ ω0.6).35

We used highly viscous glycerol as a dispersant, which enabled us to collect data at high frequencies in a more dilute concentration range. The higher dispersant viscosity enabled us to measure the viscoelasticity and birefringence of the iCNFs/glycerol dispersions in a concentration range more than 1 order of magnitude lower than iCNFs/60% sucrose dispersions. Therefore, we consider that it is difficult to accurately evaluate Je–1 values for the iCNFs/glycerol dispersions, which are determined from G′ and G″ values in the terminal relaxation region, from our data (Figure 2). In the present study, we focused on measuring viscoelastic data for iCNFs in the dilute as well as semidilute regions accurately. When a 60% sucrose solution is used as a dispersant for iCNFs, it is difficult to measure the viscoelastic data of iCNFs in the dilute region because of the low dispersion medium viscosity (∼40 mPa s). Therefore, we conducted the following discussion using the iCNFs dispersed in glycerol, especially S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersions, in the dilute and semidilute regions.

Dynamic Birefringence of S-CNF-A in the Dilute Region

Dynamic birefringence, the frequency dependence of the strain-induced birefringence, for the 0.063 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersion is shown in Figure 4a. The experimental K′ values at ω ≳ 102 s–1 gradually increased instead of leveling off, and the experimental K″ values at ω ≳ 102 s–1 also gradually increased. These trends are different from CNCs, whose K* values have been expressed using the orientational mode for rods in the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory10 only as discussed in a previous study.19 These results indicate that the birefringence relaxation of S-CNF-A cannot be described solely by the orientational mode of the end-to-end vector. This is because the birefringence arises from the orientation of the bond links, which are regrouped into the orientational mode of the end-to-end vector and the curvature mode.12 Therefore, the curvature mode contributes to birefringence with increasing chain flexibility, . Next, the experimental K* values at ω ≳ 102 s–1 were fitted using K*curv,t according to a previous study under the assumption that Young’s modulus of the S-CNF-A, E, was 1 GPa.12 When the coefficient Ccurv,t was fixed at 1 Pa–1 m–1, the experimental K′ values were fitted. However, the viscoelastic data of the 0.063 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersion were not well fitted by the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory; the theoretical storage moduli, Gt′, showed a different trend from the experimental G′ values regardless of any choice of the rod rigidity κ (controlled through E), treated as one fitting parameter (see Figure 5a). Nonetheless, considering that the birefringence relaxation of the 0.063 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersion was described by the orientational and curvature modes in the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory, the experimental tension mode for the S-CNF-A would be higher than the theoretical values. The similar results were also found for the 0.252 kg m–3 S-CNF-B/glycerol dispersion (Figure S5). At this point, we supposed that the iCNFs dispersed in glycerol is a nonideal system unlike CNCs, and the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory, which well describes linear viscoelasticity of ideal semiflexible polymers, cannot fully explain the dynamics of iCNFs. Interestingly, the experimental G′ values of other kinds of iCNFs prepared from a different starting material or by preparation conditions also showed a similar frequency dependence (Figure S6). We believe that this frequency dependence of G′ would be specific for iCNFs.

Dynamic Birefringence and Viscoelasticity of S-CNF-A in the Semidilute Region

In the semidilute regime, the relaxation of semiflexible polymers is retarded due to the hydrodynamic interaction and topological interaction.12,23 The hydrodynamic interaction works for all of the relaxation modes and can be treated as an increase of the effective solvent viscosity, while the topological interaction retards the rotational motion of rods due to steric hindrance between the surrounding rods. Ignoring these effects, we first compared the birefringence data at 0.126 and 0.315 kg m–3 with the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory (Figures 6 and 7, respectively). The E value of S-CNF-A used as a fitting parameter of the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory was assumed to be 1 GPa. When the Cort values were set to 17 and 15 Pa–1 m–1 at 0.126 and 0.315 kg m–3, respectively (Table 1), the intensity of the experimental K′ and K″ values at ω ≳ 1 s–1 was well accorded with the theoretical values. The Cort values are ideally independent of the concentration and temperature. In contrast, it has been reported that there are also some cases where the Cort values depend on the solvent quality and concentration.12

Concentration Dependence of the Rotational Relaxation Times of Rods: The Difference between S-CNF-A and S-CNC

We compared the birefringence data for the 0.126 and 0.315 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersions in the semidilute region (c/c* ≈ 1.5–3.7) with those at 0.063 kg m–3 (c/c* ≈ 0.8). Figure 9a and b shows experimental K′ and K″ values normalized by the volume friction of the S-CNF-A, φCNF, for the 0.063–0.315 kg m–3 S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersions, respectively. The K′φCNF–1 curves became broader at ω ≲ 1 s–1 with increasing φCNF, whereas those at ω ≳ 1 s–1 were almost overlapped regardless of φCNF. These results show that the rotational motions of the S-CNF-A fibers were retarded more with increasing solid concentrations, as shown in the previous section. The limiting birefringence values at high ω, K(∞)′φCNF–1, were slightly lower than the Δn0/5 values, or the upper boundary of the orientational birefringence in shear deformation.12 The intrinsic birefringence, Δn0, of cellulose is reported as 0.09 in the literature.36 For comparison, the K′φCNC–1 and K″φCNC–1 values for the S-CNC in the dilute and semidilute regions (0.126–3.777 kg m–3) are also shown in Figure 9c and d, respectively. The c* of the S-CNC was calculated as 0.537 kg m–3 from the previously reported dimensions (Table S1).19 We have previously reported that the birefringence relaxation of CNCs is described solely by the orientational mode.19 The K′φCNC–1 curves of the S-CNC in the semidilute region (c/c*>1) almost overlapped with those in the dilute region (c/c* ≈ 0.2); the relaxation times of rotational motions for rods, τrod (see eq S4), did not retard in the examined concentration ranges. Moreover, the K(∞)′φCNC–1 values reached Δn0/5, which was different from the results for the S-CNF-A. In fact, the curvature modes are not observed in the birefringence data of CNCs.19

Conclusions

In this study, we examined viscoelastic and birefringence relaxations of the S-CNF-A/glycerol dispersions in the dilute and semidilute regions to clarify the relaxation mechanism of iCNFs. Initially, we investigated the effect of iCNF types and types of dispersion media on their viscoelastic data. The power-law frequency dependence of the G′ values at high frequencies was observed across all iCNF types and dispersion media. In the dilute region, the birefringence data of the S-CNF-A were successfully described by the orientation and curvature modes of the Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory, assuming Young’s modulus, E, of 1 GPa. However, while the viscoelastic data in the dilute region were well described by the original Shankar–Pasquali–Morse theory at frequencies below ∼10 s–1, deviations from theoretical values became significant at frequencies above ∼10 s–1, regardless of the E values. These findings indicate that iCNFs, represented with S-CNF-A, would have a stronger tension mode than the theoretical prediction. This could be natural because iCNFs may have some defects such as kinks37,38 or twists.37,39 In this research, we quantitatively demonstrated for the first time how iCNF dispersions deviate from an ideal semiflexible system. Similar measurements were extended for the semidilute region of S-CNF-A. With increasing iCNF concentration, the relaxation of the orientational mode, which is characterized by τrod(c), became significantly slower. The extent of the slowdown was evaluated by the retardation coefficient, ac(c). The concentration dependence of ac(c), or , for the S-CNF-A could not be expressed by the existing theoretical equation for semiflexible polymers, considering hydrodynamic and topological interactions. This discrepancy may arise because the rods in S-CNF-A tend to aggregate as the concentration increases, resulting in an increase in the contour length.
