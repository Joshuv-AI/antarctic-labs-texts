Clinical Profile and Determinants of Chronic Kidney Disease Progression in Patients With Cardiorenal Anaemia Syndrome in Tanzania: A Descriptive Post Hoc Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Gidion Edwin, Baraka Alphonce, Alfred Meremo, John Meda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe burden of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression remains notably high among patients with CKD, resulting to adverse clinical outcomes. However, there is scarcity of data on CKD progression in patients with cardiorenal anaemia syndrome (CRAS). This study aimed to examine the clinical profile and identity the determinants of CKD progression in patients with CRAS.MethodsWe conducted a descriptive post‐hoc prospective observational study using data from a previously completed prospective cohort study between August 2023 and April 2024 at the cardiology clinic of Benjamin Mkapa Hospital, Dodoma. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with CRAS were included. Baseline demographic and clinical data were analysed. CKD progression was determined based on changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) within 6 months. Determinants of CKD progression were determined and analysed. During exploratory analyses, both descriptive and inferential methods were employed. A statistical significance set at two‐sided p‐value < 0.05.ResultsA total of 112 patients with CRAS with the mean age of 57.84 (14.53 S.D) years were analysed. At baseline, 54.5% (61/112) were ≤ 60 years, and 56.2% (63/112) being female. The median creatinine was 171 (96–351) mmol/L with a mean eGFR of 35.2 (14.46 SD) miL/min/1.73 m2. Most patients, 60.7% (68/112) were in CKD stage 3, and 51.8% (58/112) presented with moderate to severe increased albuminuria. Over the 6‐month follow‐up, CKD progression was observed in 53.57% (60/112). Determinants were included advanced age: 3.62 (95% CI, 1.35–9.65; p = 0.010); albuminuria: 2.78 (95% CI, 1.08–7.16; p = 0.034); poor functional cardiac status (NYHA class III/IV): 6.44 (95% CI, 2.04–20.27; p < 0.001) and iron deficiency: 9.11 (95% CI, 2.92–28.47; p < 0.001).ConclusionThere is high burden of CKD progression among patients with CRAS. Therefore, targeted intervention is beneficial to retard CKD progression in this high‐risk population.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease progression signifies gradual deterioration of renal function based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of ≥ 4 miL/min or gradual increases in serum creatinine of 20% to 25% from baseline in the context of underlying kidney disease [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, in our study, we applied the operational definition of CKD progression based on our inclusion criteria and study duration [5]. There is a progressive increase in global prevalence of CKD ranges between 9% and 14% [6, 7]. However, in low‐middle income countries, the burden is worst, accounting 15.8% [4]. Literature showed CKD progression is a common complication among patients with underlying CKD and other comorbidities [8, 9, 10]. Globally, the incidences of CKD progression is between 20% and 51.6% [6]. In Sub‐Saharan Africa, the magnitude is 49.6% among blacks with underling CKD [4]. Furthermore, literature demonstrated that progressively increasing in CKD progression, necessitated the need for renal replacement therapy which then resulting in high morbidity and mortality [1, 7, 11]. The majority of patients with CKD progression from previous studies were elders, above 65 years with multiple comorbidities and resulting in increased prohibitive costs [9, 11, 12, 13]; in USA up to 24% of Medicare expenditures [6]. Meanwhile, in low and middle‐income countries, the majority were younger patients with a mean age of 57.7 (13.2 SD) years [4, 8, 14, 15]. Previous local study in Tanzania showed the magnitude of CKD progression among patients with CRAS was 51.9%; and among them, 12% benefited for dialysis [15]. CRAS represents a clinical triad occurring in vicious cycle consisting of chronic heart failure regardless of ejection fraction (i.e., cardiac dysfunction); CKD as defined by Kidney Diseases Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria with an eGFR < 60 mil/min/1.73 m2 (i.e., renal dysfunction); and anaemia by WHO criteria, defined as haemoglobin levels < 12 g/dL in females or < 13 g/dL in males [16, 17]. The link between CKD progression and CRAS is clinically important, as it leads to worse outcomes, higher treatment costs, and increased pressure on healthcare systems [6, 11, 15]. Furthermore, the association of clinical determinants for CKD progression included advanced CKD stage, advanced age, comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, iron deficiency and poor functional cardiac status which then leading to poor clinical outcomes [4, 18, 19, 20]. Despite its clinical significance, little is known regarding the prevalence, clinical profile, and determinants of CKD progression in patients with CRAS, in our local setting. Our previous prospective cohort study, has found the magnitude of CRAS was 41.8% and a total of 22.4% had CKD progression among the study population [21]. Therefore, there is a need to identify the clinical profile of patients with CKD progression in patients with CRAS, so as to provide overall insights and then to improve the patients' outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This descriptive post hoc prospective observational study was conducted using data retrieved from a previously completed study at the Department of Cardiology, Benjamin Mkapa Hospital, in Dodoma, Tanzania. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [22]. Details of the study methodology are outlined in the published study protocol, which is publicly accessible [21].

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

We analysed 112 patients confirmed with CRAS, by referring a previous study [21]. Patients with complete medical records were retrieved and analysed in this study.

Variables and Data/Source Measurement

Description of included variables were detailed discussed from a previously published study protocol and preprint manuscript of this study [21]. Age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus), anthropometrics (waist and hip circumferences and waist‐hip ratio), NYHA functional status, and clinical laboratory parameters [such as lipid profile, renal function tests (urine albumin, serum creatinine), RBC indices and iron studies, and left ventricular ejection fraction] were determined. Medication history was a missing variable, it was not documented at baseline in our study [23].

Sample Size and Sampling Procedure

A total of 112 patients with CRAS were included into analysis. No sampling techniques were applied.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using statistical package for social sciences (SPSS) windows version 26 program [17, 24]. Prespecified analysis was employed to determine the prevalence of CKD progression, whereas exploratory analyses were performed by applying both descriptive and inferential statistical methods to establish the clinical profile and determinants of CKD progression. For continuous variables such as anthropometric measurements, clinical and laboratory parameters, were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and age was recorded as mean and standard deviation (S.D); while categorical variables such as clinical characteristics (including hypertensive status, diabetes, CKD staging, sex category), were reported as numbers and proportions [7, 21, 25]. Prevalence of CKD progression as a measure of disease frequency was reported by using absolute frequency and this was a primary outcome. Baseline sociodemographic variables, anthropometrics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters and determinants of CKD progression were determined at baseline. Association of determinants at baseline and CKD progression were analysed and compared by using Pearson Chi square (χ 2) test for categorical variables; meanwhile, Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables or Fisher's exact test for binary variables [15, 26, 27]. Clinical characteristics included age category, sex category, hypertension, obesity, CKD stages, albuminuria category, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, NYHA category, HF phenotypes and iron deficiency were determined and presented by descriptive method. Binary logistic regression analysis with 95% CI was used to establish the association of determinants and CKD progression. A total of thirteen [13] variables were subjected to univariate regression analysis and were presented as crude Odds ratios (COR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Thereafter, nine [9] variables with a p‐value ≤ 0.2 on univariate regression models, were then fitted to multivariate regression models by age and sex adjustment and were presented as adjusted Odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Backward elimination was performed. Key assumption was the absence of high multicollinearity among predictor variables and the linearity of the logit for continuous predictors. A two‐tailed p value of < 0.05 noted a point of statistical significance.

2.6 Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study is a descriptive post hoc analysis therefore, the previous ethical clearance for primary data was used. This study involved human subjects and, it is not a clinical trial therefore, “clinical trial number: not applicable.” Nevertheless, it was conducted in full compliance with ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval and permission to conduct this study were granted from the institutional research review ethical committee (IRREC) under the Vice chancellor's office under University of Dodoma, Tanzania with reference number MA. 84/261/64/134. Furthermore, directorate of training and research at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital in Dodoma, Tanzania has approved the data collection process, with reference number AB. 150/293/01/”A”/34. Informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from all participants, with approval granted by IRREC of the University of Dodoma and the Benjamin Mkapa Hospital in Dodoma, Tanzania.

Discussion

This study is the first to be conducted in our local setting, aimed to provide insights into the prevalence, clinical profile, and determinants of CKD progression in patients with CRAS at the Benjamin Mkapa Hospital, Dodoma, Tanzania; based on a descriptive post hoc analysis from a prospective cohort study conducted between August 2023 and April 2024.

The prevalence of CKD progression in this study was observed to be 53.57%. This is consistent with previous findings in Sub‐Saharan Africa, which was 49.6% [4], where the progression of CKD in patients with underlying comorbidities is frequently high [8, 25]. Its similarity was due to homogeneity of study population and baseline patients' characteristics.

The high prevalence of CKD progression observed in our study could be explained by the presence of CRAS, leading to renal and cardiac dysfunctions resulting to adverse outcomes [28, 29]. In our study cohort, cardiac dysfunction was synonymous to chronic heart failure (especially HFpEF) which was diagnosed based on echocardiographic criteria (ejection fraction) and therefore, the majority of patients had longstanding history of hypertension [30, 31]. It is important to note that nearly 60% of patients were in CKD Stage 3, while a quarter were in Stages 4 and 5, signaling that a significant proportion of the cohort was already at an advanced stage of kidney disease at the time of enrollment.

Our study findings revealed that the mean age of the cohort was 57.84 (14.53 S.D) years, with more than half being female. The majority of participants had hypertension (68.7%) and diabetes mellitus (70.5%), both of which are well‐known risk factors for CKD and its progression [32]. The presence of obesity (56.2%) and dyslipidemia (60.7%) further contributed to the metabolic burden in this cohort. These findings were consistent with previous studies [6, 8, 14, 18]. Additionally, the study revealed a high prevalence of albuminuria, with 54.5% of patients presenting with moderate to severe albuminuria (stage A2 and A3) which is congruent to a previously published study [33]. Albuminuria, a known marker of kidney damage, serves as an important clinical indicator of CKD progression, and its presence in more than half of the patients supports its role as a predictor of adverse renal outcomes. Our findings, comparably similar to local previous study in Africa [4]. The mean baseline serum creatinine was 171 mmol/L, with a median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 34 mL/min/1.73 m², placing many patients in the advanced stages of CKD (Stages 3 and 4). This advanced stage of kidney dysfunction highlights the significant renal burden among CRAS patients and emphasizes the need for monitoring and early interventions to mitigate further decline in renal function. Our findings were congruent to known published literatures [8, 14, 34]. Furthermore, our study cohort revealed high proportions of elevated inflammatory markers, as indicated by CRP, among patients with CKD progression. The elevated CRP could be attributed by the chronic inflammatory state, associated with uremia in CKD as well as the effects of hepcidin, cytokines and interleukins commonly seen in iron deficiency [35, 36, 37].

Our descriptive post hoc analysis identified several clinical determinants of CKD progression in patients with CRAS. Literatures revealed consistent findings similar to our study [4, 6, 8, 15, 38]. However the high odds for determining the risk for CKD progression were due inclusion of patients with later CKD stages and the presence of CRAS. These determinants included:

Advanced CKD Stages (4/5): it is obvious that the risk of CKD progression was significantly higher in patients with more advanced stages of kidney disease. This is consistent with the established understanding that patients in CKD Stages 4 and 5 are more likely to experience further renal deterioration due to the reduced functional reserve of the kidneys [4, 6, 14].

Iron Deficiency: Iron deficiency was identified as a strong determinant of CKD progression by 9‐odds. Iron deficiency is common in CRAS due to both renal anemia and inadequate erythropoiesis in the setting of kidney dysfunction [38, 39, 40]. The finding that iron deficiency was associated with a higher likelihood of CKD progression suggests that iron supplementation or other interventions aimed at improving iron status could be critical in managing CKD in these patients [28, 41, 42, 43].

Albuminuria: Increased levels of albuminuria were found to be a significant predictor of CKD progression in the cohort. Albuminuria is a significant marker of kidney damage, and its presence in patients with CRAS points to the interplay between renal injury, inflammation, and cardiovascular dysfunction [41]. Albuminuria also suggests glomerular filtration barrier disruption, further exacerbating renal decline [1, 4, 41, 44].

Poor cardiac function (NYHA III/IV): Poor functional cardiac status, as assessed by the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, was associated with an increased risk of CKD progression [15, 28]. This finding aligns with the well‐established relationship between heart failure and CKD, particularly in the setting of CRAS. As cardiac function deteriorates, renal perfusion is compromised, which can lead to further renal damage and progression of CKD [11, 15, 18].

Advanced age: Older age was found to be 3.6‐odds as an independent predictor of CKD progression in this cohort. While the mean age of the population was relatively younger than those seen in high‐income countries (57.84 years), advancing age is a well‐established risk factor for CKD progression due to the natural decline in renal function and the increased likelihood of comorbid conditions [6, 14]. However, in our local setting the majority were younger age but, most of the patients succumbed progression of CKD were those in advanced age with multiple underlying comorbid conditions [8, 29, 45].

Secondary Outcomes of CKD progression: such as rehospitalization and mortality rates were not significantly different between patients with and without CKD progression. However, 10% of patients with CKD progression required renal replacement therapy (dialysis), blood transfusions, or erythropoietin administration, highlighting the clinical severity of CKD progression in this cohort. Mortality rates were slightly higher in those with CKD progression, which underscores the importance of timely and effective interventions to slow the progression of kidney disease and improve survival outcomes. These findings were consistent with local published data [4, 15].

Furthermore, there was no significant association between CKD progression and the need for dialysis or blood transfusion, possibly due to the relatively short follow‐up period (6 months) and the fact that many patients may not have reached the point of requiring such interventions during the study period. However, the increase in clinical needs for these interventions in patients with CKD progression suggests that more aggressive management is required to prevent further deterioration. This post hoc single‐center study had some limitations, including the absence of certain key baseline variables, such as medication history, which may have affected the evaluation of CKD progression. Moreover, the use of a 6‐month operational definition for CKD progression may have resulted in underrepresentation of true progression, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

There is high burden of CKD progression among patients with CRAS. Therefore, targeted intervention is beneficial to retard CKD progression in this high risk population.
