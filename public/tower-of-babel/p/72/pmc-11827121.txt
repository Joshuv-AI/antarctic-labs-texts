Acetabular Hydatid Cyst in a Teenage Girl: A Rare Manifestation and Successful Long-Term Management With Combined Surgical and Anthelmintic Approaches
Authors: Mohammadreza Razzaghof, Mohammad Soleimani, Mohammad Poursalehian, Mohammadreza Barzegar, Sirous Jafari, Seyed Mohammad Javad Mortazavi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hydatid disease is a zoonotic infestation caused by the Echinococcus, with extremely rare bone involvement. We present a case of acetabular hydatid cyst in a 16-year-old girl who initially underwent wide surgical excision, adjuvant ablation, and local administration of anthelminthic agent followed by systemic anthelmintic therapy. After 5 years, due to the development of degenerative joint disease and as infection was suspected intraoperatively, a 2-stage total hip arthroplasty was performed. The patient’s prosthesis remained stable and well fixed, and she is now fully functional at the 12-year follow-up. The consideration of hydatid disease in the differential diagnosis of destructive bone lesions, particularly in endemic areas, is crucial for timely detection and effective treatment. This case report highlights the challenges in the surgical treatment of periacetabular bone hydatidosis.

Introduction

Hydatid disease, a global zoonotic infestation, results from infection by the Echinococcus genus, primarily Echinococcus granulosus. Humans, as intermediate hosts, contract the disease by ingesting eggs shed in the feces of carnivorous definitive hosts [1]. The disease is endemic in regions with significant sheep-raising activities, such as South America, Mediterranean Europe, Australia, New Zealand, Africa, India, and the Middle East [[2], [3], [4]].

It is the most prevalent helminthic disease in humans, typically manifesting in the liver (75%) and lungs (15%), attributable to their capillary filtering function [1]. However, bone hydatid disease is rare, accounting for less than 1% of cases, predominantly affecting vascularized areas, including vertebrae, long bone epiphyses, ilium, skull, and ribs [[3], [4], [5]]. Bone hydatid disease is most commonly observed during the fifth and sixth decades of life, with childhood occurrences being exceptionally rare [6].

Radiographically, hydatid cysts often present as well-defined, multiloculated cystic lesions, which can mimic other pathologies such as tumors or infections [[7], [8], [9]]. Diagnosis can be supported by serologic tests; imaging studies such as ultrasound, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging; and confirmed through biopsy or culture [9,10].

Although various surgical techniques have been described for bone hydatidosis in the literature, standard treatment usually involves surgical excision combined with administration of scolicidal agents such as hypertonic saline, povidone-iodine, hydrogen peroxide, and albendazole [[10], [11], [12]]. Hypertonic saline has frequently been used to prevent recurrence in cases of difficult surgical excision despite its potential to cause local injury and the need for careful application [[13], [14], [15]]. These techniques have been associated with a high recurrence rate of up to 71% [16]. In this report, we presented our experience in the surgical management of an acetabular hydatid cyst in a teenage girl with no recurrence after 17 years.

Case history

A 16-year-old girl visited our hip clinic in Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex, Tehran, Iran, presenting with pain, limited range of motion, flexion and adduction contracture in her right hip, inability to walk, and a 3 × 3 × 5 cm posterolateral thigh mass. She lived in a rural area and had close contact with livestock.

Serologic studies revealed elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) (19.2 mg/L, 4+), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (57-80 mm/hour), hemoglobin (10.9 g/dL), and a white blood cell count of 7.7 (103/microliter).

The plain X-ray of the right hip showed an expansile lytic lesion in the right acetabulum with thinning of the adjacent cortex (Fig. 1). The T2 noncontrast magnetic resonance imaging revealed multilocular hyperintense cysts with a low-intensity rim and a daughter cyst appearance, characterized by multiple small cysts within the lumen of the cystic mass (Fig. 2). The musculoskeletal radiologist suggested a diagnosis of hydatid cyst, as these findings were characteristics of bone hydatidosis. After consult with the infectious disease specialist, the patient received oral albendazole for 2 weeks and was then scheduled for surgical excision of the cyst. In the supine position and under general anesthesia, we used the lateral window of the ilioinguinal approach to reach the cyst. The internal table of the ilium was subperiosteally exposed without penetrating the peritoneal layer, and then a cortical window was opened over the cyst. Notably, the internal table of the ilium over the cyst was elevated with a thinned cortex, making intraoperative fluoroscopy unnecessary. Hypertonic saline was injected into the cyst (Fig. 3a), and after 3 minutes, curettage and excision of the cyst were performed (Fig. 3c). Daughter cysts were excised carefully (Fig. 3b). The excised tissue was sent for pathological diagnosis, which confirmed the presence of a hydatid cyst upon follow-up. Meanwhile, the area was carefully suctioned to minimize the spread of scoleces. The site was subsequently irrigated thoroughly with an adequate volume of normal saline. Following this, local ablation was performed 3 times using liquid nitrogen cryotherapy (Fig. 3d), and the procedure was completed by cauterizing the cyst wall. Subsequently, we washed 5 600 mg praziquantel tablets with povidone-iodine 3 times and mixed it with bone graft substitute (Fig. 3e). We used it to fill the void with this mixture. We did not use bone cement since praziquantel is heat-sensitive and cannot be mixed with cement. Finally, a layer of bone cement was added to seal the bony window (Fig. 3f). Praziquantel exerts its anthelmintic effects by blocking voltage-gated calcium channels, resulting in an excessive influx of calcium ions, which causes muscle contraction and paralysis [17]. Additionally, it induces rapid vacuolization and disrupts the helminth’s protective tegument. In hydatid cysts, praziquantel may hinder the evolution of protoscoleces, prevent the formation of secondary cysts, and decrease the viability of small cysts before they undergo differentiation and form an adventitial layer [18].

The patient was put on oral albendazole for the next 2 years. After 5 years, the patient presented with pain and developed degenerative joint disease and was scheduled for total hip arthroplasty (THA) (Fig. 4b). The patient had an ESR of 18 mm/h and a CRP of 8 mg/L prior to surgery.

We used a direct anterior approach to perform THA. However, upon exposing the acetabulum, a small abscess was found. It was excised and debrided, and samples were sent both for culture and pathological examination. The pathology did not show recurrence of a hydatid cyst, and the culture was further found to be negative too. We decided to perform a 2-stage THA for the patient. The abscess was evacuated, and thorough irrigation and debridement were done. Three liters of povidone-iodine-instilled normal saline (17 mL/liter), followed by 6 liters of normal saline, were used for irrigation. Vancomycin and gentamicin were added to bone cement, which was placed in the joint as a spacer (Fig. 4c). The patient received empirical antibiotics for 6 weeks. Before the second-stage surgery, she had an ESR of 10 mm/h and a CRP of 3mg/L. At a follow-up of 12 years, the patient’s prosthesis remained functional, and she returned to her daily activities without complications (Fig. 5b). She has a satisfactory range of motion with painless hip and is a happy mother of 2.

Discussion

Diagnosing bone hydatid disease is challenging due to the lack of pathognomonic clinical or radiographic features [1]. However, early and accurate diagnosis is crucial, as advanced-stage disease is difficult to treat. Considering hydatid disease in the differential diagnosis of destructive bone lesions, particularly in endemic areas, facilitates timely detection.

Bone hydatid disease can remain asymptomatic for extended periods, with cysts lying dormant for 10-20 years [19]. Disease presentations include pain, pathological fracture, deformity, neurovascular lesions, and secondary infection, predominantly with Staphylococcus [6,19].

As cysts enlarge, cortical thinning, expansion, pathological fracture, and soft tissue extension may be observed [19]. Serological studies have limited utility in diagnosing hydatid bone disease [3,4], with definitive diagnosis relying on histopathological confirmation [20]. Characteristic laminated membranes, scoleces, and hooklets can be observed in the pathological examination of curetted bone material.

Various treatment strategies for bone hydatidosis have been employed, including conservative medical treatment, simple drainage, and complete resection [21]. Medical treatment typically involves mebendazole or albendazole administration for up to 2 years following surgery [6,19]. Chemotherapy is also used to close fistulae and alleviate symptoms [21]. However, radical excision with a wide safety margin is the recommended surgical approach [22]. Establishing a safe margin can be challenging, especially in the pelvis, owing to the proximity of vital vascular and neural structures [1]. Some authors even argue that complete excision in the spine and pelvis is virtually impossible [19,22]. Local recurrence rates are high, exceeding 70% in some studies, with incomplete resection cited as the most critical factor contributing to recurrence [6,23].

Surgery only eliminates macroscopic cysts; thus, the local use of anthelmintics or chemical agents in conjunction with wide surgical resection seems necessary. Various therapeutic regimens have been employed, such as extensive curettage, chemical sterilization, and bone grafting to fill the defect in one study or the combination of anthelmintic treatment, wide resection, and defect filling with polymethylmethacrylate in another study [6,24]. Atalay et al. used aggressive curettage or wide resection, polymethylmethacrylate for gap filling, and oral albendazole administration [6]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has used local anthelmintics in the surgical treatment of bone hydatid disease. Our experience demonstrates that surgical excision of bone cysts, local ablation by cryotherapy and electrocautery, and local administration of anthelmintic agents can be beneficial in eradicating bone hydatid cysts, thereby allowing for successful reconstructive surgeries, including THA.

In our study, we described the technique of washing oral praziquantel tablets with povidone-iodine before mixing them with bone graft. This method was based on the need to incorporate praziquantel into the surgical site to enhance its antiparasitic effect. However, we acknowledge the limitations and potential risks associated with this approach. Oral tablets are not produced under sterile conditions, and washing the surface with povidone-iodine may not ensure complete sterility. This presents a risk of introducing contaminants into the surgical site, which may contribute to postoperative infections, as observed in our case. Given the potential for infection and the variability in achieving true sterility, we advise against the replication of this method without further validation and recommend exploring alternative, sterile methods of delivering antiparasitic agents during surgery.

Additionally, it is important to consider the potential causes of early degenerative joint disease observed in this case. This condition could be attributed directly to the presence of the hydatid cyst, which may have caused significant structural damage and inflammation. Alternatively, the aggressive local treatments employed, such as hypertonic saline, cryotherapy, and cautery, may have contributed to joint degeneration. These treatments, while effective in eradicating the parasitic infection, could have resulted in collateral damage to the surrounding tissues. Careful consideration of these factors is essential when developing treatment plans for similar cases to minimize the risk of long-term complications.

Summary

This case report of an acetabular hydatid cyst in a teenage girl highlights the importance of considering hydatid disease as a rare but possible cause of destructive bone lesions, particularly in endemic regions. The challenges in diagnosing and treating bone hydatidosis necessitate a multidisciplinary approach. Our experience demonstrates that a combination of wide surgical resection, local administration of anthelmintic agents for the first time, and oral anthelmintic therapy can yield successful long-term outcomes, as evidenced by the patient’s functional prosthesis at the 12-year follow-up. The presented case underscores a comprehensive treatment strategy to approach acetabular hydatid cysts with optimal outcomes.
