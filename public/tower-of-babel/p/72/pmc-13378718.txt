A NANOBODY molecule that blocks MerTK ectodomain cleavage in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Linde Duprez, Ayse Kilic, Hélène Bruwiere, Karim Fekir, Dennis Vlaeminck, Camille De Craene, Daan Geiregat, Kirsten Paesen, Lien Leutenez, Stijn Lambrecht, Thomas Van De Velde, Aline Moliere, Judith Verhelst, Edith Stuyven, Soraya Hoelper, Dieter Schmoll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe membrane receptor MerTK is critical for the resolution of inflammation and thus is of pharmacological interest. MerTK function is inhibited by the proteolytic cleavage of its extracellular domain leading to the formation of soluble Mer (sMer). We describe here the NANOBODY molecule A0445046C08 and its half-life-extended version A044500050. Both bound selectively to MerTK and blocked lipopolysaccharide-induced MerTK cleavage in primary macrophages without influencing ligand binding or kinase activity of MerTK. A044500050 reduced Zymosan-induced sMer levels in the peritoneal lavage fluid of a mouse model with sterile peritonitis. The study demonstrates that NANOBODY molecules can be generated that selectively inhibit ectodomain shedding and outlines a novel pharmacological approach for targeting membrane proteins where aberrant cleavage plays a pathogenic role.KEYWORDS: ADAM17, ectodomain shedding, efferocytosis, immunoglobulin single variable domains, resolution of inflammation

Introduction

Ectodomain shedding, i.e., the proteolytic cleavage of extracellular domains (ECDs) of membrane proteins, regulates the activity of several cytokines and receptors. Its pharmacological inhibition is of therapeutic interest.1–4 Due to the promiscuous activity and high homologies of the involved proteases, their inhibition with small molecules is challenging and associated with safety concerns.1,3,5,6 MER proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (MerTK) is an example of a membrane protein that is regulated by proteolytic cleavage. MerTK, Tyro3, and Axl constitute the TAM family of receptor tyrosine kinases.7,8 MerTK is predominately expressed by macrophages and critical for efferocytosis, the phagocytic clearance of apoptotic cells.9 By the expression of MerTK, macrophages recognize apoptotic cells via bridging ligands like growth arrest-specific protein 6 (Gas6). Gas6 binds to both MerTK and phosphatidylserine lipids, present in the extracellular membrane leaflet of apoptotic cells. Gas6 binding stimulates the intracellular tyrosine kinase activity of MerTK, leading to the activation of Erk1/2 and subsequently the phagocytosis of apoptotic cells. Efferocytosis is important for the resolution of inflammatory processes as it restores cellular homeostasis at an inflammatory site.9–11 The metalloproteinase ADAM17 inhibits MerTK activity by cleaving the extracellular domain of human MerTK at P490-S491,12 which is adjacent to the membrane-spanning domain starting at F507 (Figure 1). The cleavage leads to the formation of soluble Mer (sMer).12,13 sMer acts as a decoy receptor14 and increased levels are associated with inflammatory diseases.8,15–23 A mouse mutant expressing a cleavage-resistant MerTK shows reduced inflammation and tissue damage in disease models indicating that blocking MerTK ectodomain shedding could be an approach for the treatment of inflammatory diseases.19,24–27 This could be achieved by inhibiting ADAM17 activity. This protease, however, cleaves several proteins besides MerTK, leading to undesired side effects.1,5,6 Here, we describe the inhibition of MerTK cleavage by NANOBODY molecules against MerTK.

Materials and methods

Immunization and selection of MerTK-targeting NANOBODY molecules

The generation of NANOBODY molecules has been described in detail previously.28–30 MerTK-targeting NANOBODY sequences were obtained following immunization of four camelids (according to local Ethical Committee approval) with DNA encoding for the ECD of human MerTK. The animals were boosted with recombinant human MerTK (ACROBiosystems, cat. no. MEK-H52H6). The animals received a second boost with DNA encoding a chimeric MerTK protein that has the alpaca sequence for Ig-like C2-type 1 and 2 and Fibronectin type-III 1 and the human sequence for the remaining domains (Figure 1). After immunization, blood samples were regularly taken to monitor serum titers. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were prepared using Ficoll Hypaque (Greiner, cat. no. 227288). From PBMCs, total RNA was extracted using RNeasy Midi kit (Qiagen, cat. no. 75144) and used for RT-PCR to amplify the NANOBODY protein-encoding DNA segments, as described.30 Subsequently, phage libraries were prepared according to standard protocols. The target-specific NANOBODY proteins were enriched over several rounds of panning using either MerTK protein or MerTK expressing cells.28,29

Engineering of NANOBODY molecules

Monovalent anti-MerTK NANOBODY molecules were designed with a C-terminal FLAG3-HIS6 tag and produced in E. coli. Multivalent NANOBODY molecules were constructed with one or two N-terminal MerTK-specific building blocks and a C-terminal human albumin-binding building block ALB23002 (SEQ ID NO 10 in31), providing the NANOBODY molecules with an extended in vivo half-life.32 The two MerTK building blocks were connected by a flexible (GGGGS)7 linker whereas a (GGGGS)4 linker was used between the MerTK and albumin-binding building blocks. A non-human protein targeting NANOBODY building block was used as negative control, either as a monovalent NANOBODY molecule with a C-terminal FLAG3-HIS6 tag or as a half-life extended NANOBODY molecule with the albumin binding NANOBODY building block ALB23002 at the C-terminus using a flexible 9GS linker (GGGGSGGGS).32 NANOBODY molecules were produced in Pichia pastoris and purified using metal or Protein A affinity chromatography followed by gel filtration.28,29,32

Binding to human and cynomolgus monkey MerTK expressing CHO cells

Binding of NANOBODY proteins to human and cynomolgus MerTK stably expressed on Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells (InScreenX) was examined by flow cytometry. Cells were seeded in 384-well Bio-One V-bottom plates (Greiner, cat. no. 781280) at 40,000 cells/well. After washing with assay buffer (phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 0.05% NaN3), serial dilutions of NANOBODY proteins were prepared. For experiments with A044500050, the assay buffer was supplemented with 30 μM human serum albumin (HSA, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. A8763). Diluted NANOBODY proteins were added to the cells and incubated (3h, 4°C). After incubation, cells were washed and stained with primary detection antibodies (30 min, 4°C). For FLAG-tagged NANOBODY proteins, mouse anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. F1804) was used. For NANOBODY proteins with an albumin building block, ABH0074 was used, an in-house developed anti-albumin monoclonal antibody produced using hybridoma technology. Following washing, cells were incubated with allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. no. 115–135–164) for 30 min at 4°C. After washing, cells were resuspended in 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution (BD Biosciences, cat. no. 564907) to discriminate against viable cells and analyzed using an iQue flow cytometer (Intellicyt). Binding curves were fitted using four parameter logistic regression in GraphPad (GraphPad Software Inc.).

Binding to primary human macrophages

NANOBODY protein binding to primary macrophages was examined by flow cytometry. Cells were thawed, washed with assay buffer (PBS, 2% FBS, 0.05% NaN3) and seeded in 384-well Bio-One V-bottom plates (Greiner, cat. no. 781280) at 10,000 cells/well. After washing, serial dilutions of NANOBODY proteins were added and incubated (30 min, 4°C). For experiments with A044500050, assay buffer was supplemented with 30 µM HSA. Cells were washed and stained with Zombie Aqua Viability Dye (Biolegend, cat. no. 423101/423102), human Fc-block and mouse anti-FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. F1804) or ABH0074 and anti-mouse IgG APC as secondary detection antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. no. 115–135–164). Cell suspensions were analyzed using an iQue flow cytometer (Intellicyt). Binding curves were fitted using four parameter logistic regression as above.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurement

Binding affinity of the purified NANOBODY proteins was evaluated on a Biacore 8K+ instrument at 25°C. Human MerTK with C-terminal Fc tag (ACROBiosystems, cat. no. MEK-H5253) and cynomolgus MerTK with C-terminal Fc tag (ACRO Biosystems, cat. no. MEK-C5253) were captured via immobilized anti-human IgG-Fc NANOBODY protein (produced in-house) on a CM5 chip (Cytiva). Purified NANOBODY proteins were injected for 2 min (flow rate 30 µL/min) at different concentrations. Dissociation was followed for 900s. Between sample injections the surface was regenerated with two pulses of 0.85% H3PO4 (30s, 30 µL/min). Between capture and analyte injections two wash steps with 0.85% H3PO4 followed by a wash step with running buffer were implemented. Data was double referenced by subtracting the reference flow cell and blank buffer injection (1x HBS-EP+, pH7.4 (Cytiva, cat. no. BR100826)). Binding constants were obtained by fitting a 1:1 interaction model (Langmuir binding model) using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software.

ELISA for NANOBODY binding to Tyro3 and Axl

Black 384-well MaxiSorp plates (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 460518) were coated overnight at 4°C with 1 µg/ml human Axl-IgG1 (ACROBiosystems, cat. no. Axl-H52H53) or human Tyro3-IgG1 (ACROBiosystems, cat. no. TY3-H5251) in PBS. Following three washes with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST), plates were blocked with PBS supplemented with 1% casein for 1h at room temperature (RT). After washing, serial dilutions of NANOBODY proteins or recombinant human Gas6-His (produced in-house) were prepared in PBST containing 0.1% casein, added to the wells and incubated (3h, RT) with orbital shaking. For Tyro3 binding experiments, Gas6 was pre-incubated (30 min, RT) with anti-His tag antibody (GenScript, cat. no. A00186) at a 2:1 molar ratio prior to addition to the plate. After washing, plates were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. A8592) for 1h at RT. Human Gas6 bound to Axl-coated wells was detected using HRP-conjugated anti-His antibody (Bio-Rad, cat. no. MCA1396P). Gas6 bound to Tyro3-coated wells was detected using HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (Abcam, cat. no. ab97040). Fluorescence signal was developed using QuantaBlu Fluorogenic Peroxidase Substrate Kit (ThermoScientific, cat. no. 15169) and measured at 325 nm excitation and 420 nm emission using a CLARIOstar reader (BMG Labtech). EC50 values were determined by non-linear regression analysis using GraphPad Software.

Gas6 competition experiments

The effect of NANOBODY proteins on the interaction between Gas6 and MerTK was evaluated using flow cytometry. CHO cells stably expressing human MerTK (InScreenX) were washed with assay buffer (PBS, 2% FBS, 0.05% NaN3) and seeded in 384-well Bio-One V-bottom plates (Greiner, cat. no. 781280) at 40,000 cells/well. Serial dilutions of NANOBODY proteins were prepared in the assay buffer. Dilutions were pre-incubated with recombinant human Gas6 (produced in-house) at a concentration of 50 nM (30 min, RT). The NANOBODY-Gas6 mixtures were added to the cells and incubated (3h, 4°C) with orbital shaking. As positive control, recombinant chicken Gas6 (produced in-house) was included. It binds to human MerTK but is not recognized by the human Gas6-specific detection antibody. After washing, cells were stained with APC-conjugated anti-Gas6 antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, cat. no. 711–136–152) for 30 min at 4°C. Cells were washed, resuspended in DAPI solution (BD Biosciences, cat. no. 564907) and analyzed using an iQue Screener PLUS flow cytometer (Intellicyt). IC50 values were calculated using four-parameter logistic regression analysis as above.

MerTK cleavage assay

Primary human monocytes were isolated from two sources using the EasySep™ Human Monocyte Isolation Kit (Stemcell, cat. no. 19359): either from PBMCs obtained by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation of buffy coats supplied by the Belgian Red Cross (with informed donor consent), or from cryopreserved human Leukopaks (BioIVT, cat. no. HUMANPBMC-0128507). The monocytes were seeded in macrophage differentiation medium (RPMI 1640 (Gibco, cat. no. 72400) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. F7524), 50 ng/ml Human M-CSF (BioLegend, cat. no. 574808), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco, cat. no. 15140–122)) and cultured in 6-well UpCell culture plates (Nunc, cat. no. 174901). After 3 days, the medium was renewed. Macrophages were harvested after an additional 3 days by detachment using the temperature-responsive properties of the UpCell plates and seeded at 10,000 cells/well in 96-well V-bottom plates (Falcon, cat. no. 353263) using macrophage differentiation medium. Plates were incubated at 37°C for at least 1h, before cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 10 EU/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. L2630) and dilutions of NANOBODY protein. As controls, cells were treated with LPS or left untreated. After 30 min at 37°C, cell supernatant was removed, and cells were resuspended in cold Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (D-PBS) supplemented with 2% heat inactivated FBS and 0.05% NaN3. Erk1/2 activation

Erk1/2 activation was determined by assessing its phosphorylation. Differentiated primary macrophages were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells/well in 96-well flat-bottom tissue culture plates (Costar, cat. no. 3596) in macrophage differentiation medium. Plates were incubated for 15 min at RT, followed by medium replacement with serum-free RPMI 1640 supplemented with 30 µM HSA. Cells were equilibrated in this medium (30 min, RT). Dilutions of NANOBODY proteins or human Gas6 (produced in-house) were prepared in the same medium and added to the cells. After 25 min stimulation at RT, medium was removed, and cells were lysed by ice-cold lysis buffer provided in the HTRF kit. Cell lysates were processed using the Advanced Phospho-Erk1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) Cellular HTRF assay kit (Revvity, cat. no. 64ERKPEG). HTRF signals were measured using a CLARIOstar reader (BMG Labtech) with the following settings: excitation at 320 nm, emission at 620 nm (donor) and 665 nm (acceptor), and a delay time of 60 μs. The HTRF ratio (665 nm/620 nm × 10,000) was calculated for each well. EC50 values were calculated using four parameter logistic regression as above.

MerTK surface expression

Primary differentiated human macrophages were seeded at 10,000 cells/well in Upcell 96F microwell plates (ThermoFisher; cat no. 174897 in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, cat. no. 72400) supplemented with 50 ng/ml Human M-CSF, 30 µM HSA and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. A dilution series of NANOBODY proteins was added and cells were incubated for 24h at 37°C. Subsequently, cells were detached by incubation at 4°C for 30 min and transferred to 96-well V-bottom plates (Falcon, cat. no. 353263). For detection of surface MerTK, cells were stained as described above for the MerTK cleavage assay, except that cells were fixed (ThermoFisher, cat. no. GAS001S) after staining with Zombie Aqua.

ADAM17 assay

ADAM17 activity was measured by a fluorescence assay (ENZO, cat. no. BML-AK310) with GM6001 as positive control following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Mouse line and mouse model of Zymosan-induced sterile peritonitis

To assess activity of NANOBODY molecules targeting the human MerTK sequence, homozygous humanized MERTK/GAS6 double knock-in mice (C57BL/6-Mertktm1(MERTK) Gas6tm1(GAS6)/Bcgen, Biocytogen), aged 6–8 weeks, were used. In this line, the murine MerTK gene was replaced by a chimeric construct, comprising the human ECD and murine transmembrane and intracellular sequences, to allow signal transduction. The murine Gas6 gene was replaced by the human gene sequence. A044500050 and control NANOBODY CNB00010 were diluted in PBS to a concentration of 0.3 mg/ml and injected subcutaneously at doses of 3mg/kg and 10mg/kg. After 16h sterile peritonitis was induced by injecting 1mg of Zymosan A (Sigma- Aldrich, Z4250-1G) in 200µl PBS intraperitoneally. 6h after stimulation, the experiment was terminated. To obtain peritoneal lavage, the peritoneal cavity was flushed with 2ml PBS containing 3mM EDTA through a small incision in the abdominal wall. The aspirate was collected and centrifuged (5min, 300g, 4°C). Supernatants were used for detection of sMer levels by the Human Mer DuoSet ELISA (R&D; cat. no. DY6488). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software. Results were assessed for normal distribution with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance was calculated with an Ordinary one-way ANOVA test followed by Dunnett’s multiple group comparisons using CNB00010 as control.

Results

Human and camelid MerTK protein share a high sequence homology (Figure 1). To identify MerTK-cleavage blocking NANOBODY molecules, camelids were immunized with a plasmid expressing the ECD of human MerTK. This was followed by a boost with human MerTK protein and a second boost using a plasmid expressing a human–alpaca chimera of the ECD comprising the human sequence spanning the cleavage site (Figure 1), to increase the chance for obtaining NANOBODY molecules inhibiting MerTK cleavage. RNA was isolated from PBMCs obtained from immunized animals and used to construct a phage heavy-chain variable-domain library. NANOBODY molecules were screened by flow cytometry for binding to CHO cells expressing the ECD of human and cynomolgus MerTK. Subsequent work focused on the clone expressing the NANOBODY molecule A0445046C08.

In flow cytometry experiments, A0445046C08 bound to human and cynomolgus MerTK-expressing CHO cells, but not to parental CHO cells (Figure 2A–C). As a negative control, a non-human protein targeting NANOBODY molecule was used, which bound neither to MerTK-expressing nor to non-transfected cells. A0445046C08 did not bind the other members of the TAM family, Tyro3 and Axl (Figure 2D,E), in contrast to the ligand of the TAM family, Gas6. The interaction of Gas6 with MerTK-expressing cells was not affected by A0445046C08, indicating that the NANOBODY molecule did not interfere with the ligand-binding function of MerTK (Figure 2F). SPR analysis revealed high-affinity binding of A0445046C08 to both human and cynomolgus monkey MerTK, with dissociation constants (KD) of <23 pM and <40 pM, respectively. The upper limit values reflect the detection threshold of the SPR system, as dissociation rates (koff) fell below the measurable range (Supplemental Figure S1).

To generate a half-life extended version enabling in vivo administration, A0445046C08 was re-cloned and fused to an albumin-binding NANOBODY molecule (ALB23002), yielding A044500050. In the presence of a saturating concentration of human serum albumin (HSA), this molecule bound with slightly lower potency to human and cynomolgus MerTK-expressing CHO cells than A0445046C08 (Figure 3A–C). Like A0445046C08, it did not compete with Gas6 binding (Figure 3D). Both A0445046C08 or A044500050 bound to primary human macrophages (Figure 4A,B) with EC50 values like those determined for their interaction with transfected CHO cells. Contrary to Gas6, the NANOBODY molecules did not activate Erk1/2 (Figure 4B,C). This indicated that their binding to MerTK does not activate MerTK kinase activity. We also generated the molecule A044500010 that consisted of two MerTK binding NANOBODY building blocks and the albumin-binding NANOBODY. A044500010 induced Erk1/2 phosphorylation (Figure 4E) and decreased in a concentration-dependent manner the surface expression of MerTK (Figure 4F). The bivalent MerTK binding format was therefore not further pursued.

To assess the ability of the NANOBODY molecules to prevent MerTK cleavage, primary human macrophages were incubated with LPS. As described,12 LPS incubation decreased the surface expression of MerTK, which was prevented by the ADAM17 inhibitor TAPI-1 (Figure 5A). As a control, sMer levels were measured in concentrated cell-culture supernatants (Figure 5B). LPS treatment increased sMer levels, which was blocked by TAPI-1, in line with the notion that LPS induced MerTK cleavage. Either A0445046C08 or its half-life extended form A044500050 blocked LPS-induced MerTK receptor shedding (Figure 5C,D) as measured by flow cytometry. In line with its slightly less potent MerTK binding activity (Figure 4A,B), the IC50 value of A044500050 for the inhibition of MerTK cleavage was consistently moderately higher than that of A0445046C08 in macrophages from different donors (Figure 5C,D). Like ADAM17 inhibition, A044500050 reduced the sMer levels in LPS-stimulated macrophages (Figure 5B). The NANOBODY molecule did not interfere with ADAM17 enzymatic activity (Figure 5E), excluding that its effect on shedding is due to a direct inhibition of the protease.

To assess the ability of A044500050 to inhibit MerTK cleavage in vivo, mice expressing humanized MerTK and Gas6 were stimulated with Zymosan to induce sterile peritonitis. Zymosan treatment increased sMer levels in peritoneal fluid, as described.24 The administration of A044500050 reduced Zymosan-induced sMer levels in a dose-dependent manner, demonstrating that the NANOBODY molecule blocked MerTK cleavage in vivo (Figure 5F).

Discussion

We described NANOBODY molecules capable of specifically blocking MerTK cleavage through direct binding to the MerTK receptor. This achievement is noteworthy given the high sequence homology between human and alpaca MerTK and the proximity of the cleavage site to the plasma membrane. Both A0445046C08 and its half-life extended variant, A044500050, demonstrated selective inhibition of MerTK cleavage without interfering with the physiological Gas6-MerTK interaction. Importantly, these NANOBODY molecules with one MerTK binding building preserved receptor kinase quiescence. In contrast, the NANOBODY molecule with two MerTK binding building blocks activated receptor signaling. This agonism behavior could result from forced receptor dimerization analogous to Gas6-mediated activation of the related Axl receptor.33 The bivalent MerTK NANOBODY also reduced the surface expression of MerTK, most likely by inducing receptor internalization, consistent with observations for a MerTK-targeting antibody.34 Future work is needed to establish whether our NANOBODY molecules inhibit ectodomain shedding by blocking the cleavage site directly via steric hindrance or by allosteric regulation. The half-life extended NANOBODY molecule A044500050 represents a valuable pharmacological tool for investigating the specific role of MerTK cleavage in disease pathogenesis, which will be the subject of upcoming studies. More broadly, our findings address a substantial challenge in targeting ectodomain shedding, a process that regulates numerous plasma membrane proteins including receptors and membrane-bound cytokines.1,2,5,6,35 While direct protease inhibition remains problematic due to promiscuous substrate specificity and associated safety concerns,1,5,6 our NANOBODY-based approach offers a promising alternative strategy for selectively blocking the cleavage of specific membrane proteins. In conclusion, our work establishes a proof-of-concept for NANOBODY-mediated inhibition of ectodomain shedding that could be extended to other therapeutically relevant membrane proteins, potentially opening new avenues for precision medicine approaches in diseases where aberrant protein cleavage plays a pathogenic role.
