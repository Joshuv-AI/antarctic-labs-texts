Bibliometric and Visualized Analysis of Research Relating to Spinal Modic Changes Reported Over the Period 2004–2024
Authors: Haixin Wei, Hui Lu, Lei Li, Changpeng Qu, Hao Zhang, Xuexiao Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveModic changes (MC) are pathological signal alterations occurring in the vertebral endplates and adjacent bone marrow. These changes are frequently linked to degenerative disc diseases and are associated with low back pain symptoms. However, despite increasing research interest, existing studies are fragmented, mostly descriptive, and lack a comprehensive, quantitative assessment of research patterns, hotspots, and collaboration networks in this field. This study aims to evaluate the current research landscape and global trends regarding spinal MC using bibliometric analysis.MethodsWe retrieved relevant publications on spinal MC from the Web of Science Core Collection database, spanning January 2004 to August 2024. Using CiteSpace, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of keywords, co‐authors, institutions, countries, research domains, cited literature, cited authors, and cited journals.ResultsThis study analyzed 603 articles published in 168 journals from 193 countries. China emerged as the leading contributor in terms of publication volume, while the University of Oulu in Finland demonstrated the most significant institutional impact. J. Karppinen was identified as the most prolific author, whereas M. Modic was the most frequently cited. Among journals, Spine accounted for the highest number of publications and citations. Key research hotspots identified through keyword analysis include “degenerative disease,” “bone marrow changes,” “abnormalities,” “type II changes,” “lower back,” “protrusion,” “discectomy,” “paraspinal muscles,” “obesity,” and “overweight”.ConclusionThis study represents the first known bibliometric analysis and visualization of MC, offering clinicians valuable insights into research priorities and directions. Future investigations should prioritize the classification, pathophysiological mechanisms, and clinical significance of different types of MC, especially their roles in pain and functional impairment. Research should also explore the impact of obesity and paraspinal muscles on the progression of MC. Moreover, studies should examine the potential benefits of weight loss and muscle strengthening in alleviating symptoms. Finally, researchers should focus on leveraging artificial intelligence to improve the identification and understanding of MC.Keywords: bibliometrics, CiteSpace, clinical relevance, Modic changes, visual analysis

Introduction

Peri‐disc bone marrow changes in vertebral bodies, commonly referred to as Modic changes (MC), can be visualized using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In 1988, M. Modic and colleagues classified these changes into three distinct types based on signal characteristics in bone marrow adjacent to vertebral endplates [1]. Type I changes display low signal intensity on T1‐weighted imaging (T1WI) and high signal intensity on T2‐weighted imaging (T2WI), reflecting bone marrow edema and inflammation in the vertebral endplate. Type II changes, characterized by high signal intensity on both T1WI and T2WI, represent bone marrow ischemia, where red hematopoietic marrow converts to yellow fatty marrow. Type III changes exhibit low signal intensity on both T1WI and T2WI, indicating sclerotic alterations in the endplate. Among these, Type I changes are strongly associated with accelerated degenerative processes in the spine [2, 3].

Bibliometric analysis provides a quantitative approach for examining publication patterns, citation trends, and literature usage. This method identifies research trends, hotspots, and interdisciplinary connections by leveraging objective bibliometric indicators such as centrality. Unlike subjective evaluations, bibliometric analysis enables a systematic, data‐driven assessment of academic progress. It plays a vital role in research evaluation, academic planning, and knowledge management, offering evidence‐based insights to guide disciplinary development, foster research collaboration, and inform decision‐making. Furthermore, it enhances the allocation of research resources and promotes scientific advancement. Over the past decade, bibliometric analysis has become widely used to evaluate scientific research across various fields, including medicine, geography, and engineering [4, 5, 6]. While numerous narrative and systematic reviews have explored the clinical significance, pathophysiology, and imaging characteristics of MC, no bibliometric or visual scientometric study has systematically analyzed global research patterns in this area. Unlike traditional reviews that rely on manual literature selection and qualitative summaries, bibliometric analysis utilizes quantitative metrics such as co‐citation frequency, centrality, and burst detection to reveal research hotspots, emerging trends, and collaborative structures. Our study not only introduces temporal keyword evolution, co‐authorship networks, journal influence, and clustered knowledge domains, but also provides visualized data‐driven insights that cannot be achieved through conventional methods. This represents a significant methodological advancement over prior reviews and fills a gap in the spine literature.

Therefore, the purposes of this study are:

To quantitatively analyze the global research output on MC over the past two decades, including publication trends, country and institutional contributions, and influential authors and journals.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Search Strategy

The articles included in this study were indexed from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database, which encompasses over 10,000 journals and is widely regarded as one of the most authoritative databases for bibliometric analysis. The WoSCC was chosen because it offers standardized citation data and compatibility with bibliometric tools such as CiteSpace. It provides high‐quality indexing of peer‐reviewed articles and allows for comprehensive mapping of research networks. Although other databases such as PubMed, Scopus, or CNKI also contain relevant literature, WoSCC was selected due to its broader support for co‐citation and keyword burst analyses essential to this study's visual scientometric approach. The search strategy employed was: TS = (“MC” OR “endplate inflammation” OR “vertebral endplate changes” OR “cartilage endplate inflammation”) AND TS = (spine OR spinal OR vertebrae OR vertebral column OR vertebrae), covering the period from January 1, 2004, to August 10, 2024. Since the database updates daily, all data were retrieved within a single day to ensure consistency and prevent potential distortion. We included only original research and review articles, with the search restricted to English‐language publications. A total of 619 articles were retrieved, of which 16 were excluded based on the inclusion criteria, resulting in 603 articles for analysis. Data Extraction and Analysis

CiteSpace (6.3.R1), a visualization software developed by Dr. Chaomei Chen at Drexel University, Philadelphia, USA, was employed in this study to analyze research trends and hotspots. The software identifies the strongest citation bursts, keyword co‐occurrences, and other bibliometric indicators. We used CiteSpace to examine collaboration networks among countries, institutions, journals, and authors. Additionally, we analyzed co‐cited references and authors and generated visual maps of keyword co‐occurrence, clustering, timelines, and time‐zone visualizations.

In these visualizations, each node represents a specific parameter, with larger nodes indicating greater significance. Different clusters are depicted with distinct colors for nodes and connecting lines. Two independent observers (Wei and Li) conducted a thorough review of the studies based on their titles and abstracts. All referenced and cited literature was collected and exported as plain text files, including bibliographic details such as titles, authors, abstracts, funding sources, keywords, and references. Using CiteSpace (6.3.R1), a Java‐based bibliometric analysis tool, we performed a comprehensive bibliometric study of the WoSCC Core Collection. This software is widely recognized for evaluating research trends across global fields. We identified leading authors, institutions, and countries and mapped collaborative relationships among them. To gain deeper insights into research collaboration, we conducted co‐citation network analyses of references and authors.

Results

Publication Output

Between January 1, 2004, and August 10, 2023, we identified 603 articles that met the inclusion criteria. Research output on spinal MC demonstrated a steady increase during this period, rising from one article in 2004 to 72 articles in 2023, with an impressive annual growth rate of 25.24%. The year 2023 marked the highest publication output, with 72 articles published (Figure 2). Although minor fluctuations occurred between 2009 and 2014, the overall trend reflected a growing focus on spinal MC. Keyword and Research Hotspot Analysis

Keywords provide an accurate representation of the research topic. In this study, analyzing high‐frequency and strongly occurring keywords helps identify research hotspots and trends. Figure 10 displays the keyword co‐occurrence network, where nodes represent keywords. The size of each node reflects its weight, with larger nodes indicating higher frequency. The lines between nodes represent co‐occurrence relationships, and the colors within the nodes correspond to different years of publication. Keyword clustering groups terms and phrases with distinct domain characteristics into clusters. This process uses a feature extraction algorithm from text classification to identify domain‐specific and general words, controlling for word frequency. Figure 11 illustrates the 13 clusters identified in this study: “end plates” (#0), “magnetic resonance imaging” (#1), “basivertebral nerve” (#2), “endplate defects” (#3), “infection” (#4), “risk factors” (#5), “intervertebral disk” (#6), “disc degeneration” (#7), “MC” (#8), “cage subsidence” (#9), “disc prostheses” (#10), “patient‐reported outcome measures” (#11), and “neck pain” (#12). The clusters are ordered by size, reflecting how the field is methodically divided into distinct groups. Cluster#4 “infection” is particularly noteworthy, as it reflects the hypothesis that MC may be associated with low‐grade bacterial infection, especially by Cutibacterium acnes. This theory is supported by studies demonstrating bacterial DNA in disc tissue and the presence of endplate edema resembling infection‐related inflammation [12, 13]. These findings have led to clinical trials investigating antibiotic therapy for chronic low back pain patients with MC, making “infection” an emerging area of research within the Modic change landscape. Figure 12 shows the variation in keywords across these clusters over time, while Figure 13 highlights the top 13 keywords with the strongest reference bursts. Light blue and red bars indicate frequently used and rarely used keywords, respectively. The strongest emerging keywords for MC in the spine included “magnetic resonance imaging,” “MRI,” “disc degenerative disease,” “marrow changes,” “abnormality,” “Type II changes,” “follow‐up,” “low back,” “herniation,” “discectomy,” “paravertebral muscles,” “obesity,” and “overweight.”

Discussion

Overview of the Development of the Field of Modic Change and Bibliometric Analyses

Over the past two decades, the number of publications on MC has shown a significant upward trend, particularly after 2020. This increase is likely due to the growing recognition of the clinical importance of MC in spinal disorders. Additionally, advancements in imaging technologies and deeper investigations into the underlying pathological mechanisms have further contributed to this rise. Publication numbers reached a peak of 72 in 2023, followed by a slight decrease to 53 in 2024. This decline may be due to data reporting lags or shifts in research priorities. The trend line in Figure 2 (R 2 = 0.9546) highlights a strong temporal correlation, suggesting sustained interest and ongoing exploration of MC in spinal surgery research. Overall, this field presents substantial potential for future research.

From a national and regional perspective, China, the United States, and Finland are the top three contributors to MC research. China leads in publication output with 160 articles but exhibits a lower centrality score (0.15), which reflects limited global collaboration or influence in cross‐national co‐citation networks. This pattern is not uncommon among emerging research powers, where the volume of publication grows faster than international visibility. In contrast, the United States demonstrates a much higher centrality (0.87), suggesting broader citation recognition, participation in multicenter studies, and the presence of high‐impact authors (e.g., Samartzis, Lotz). Finland, despite its smaller volume, maintains strong international ties through influential figures like Karppinen and active participation in collaborative spinal research consortia. In addition, the discrepancy may partially stem from differences in research orientation. Publications from China in this field have tended to focus on imaging analysis, pathophysiological mechanisms, or observational designs, whereas research from the United States and European countries more often involves clinical trials, outcome evaluations, and meta‐analyses. Such clinically oriented studies generally garner broader international citations and drive collaborative projects, thereby contributing to higher centrality in global networks. These observations highlight the need for increased international collaboration, cross‐institutional projects, and citation integration for Chinese MC researchers to enhance academic influence. Building more transnational teams and publishing in globally visible outlets may help bridge this gap.

Among the top 10 most prolific authors, Jaro Karppinen has made the most significant contributions to spinal Modic change research over the past two decades. A leading expert in spinal health, Karppinen is a Professor of Physics and Rehabilitation Medicine at the University of Oulu, Finland, and Co‐Director of the International Spine and Pain Consortium. His research primarily focuses on low back pain, osteoarthritis, and the metabolic, environmental, and genetic risk factors associated with spinal disorders, with particular emphasis on MC. Karppinen's work has greatly enhanced the understanding of low back pain, which is now recognized as a major global cause of disability. Dino Samartzis, another influential figure in spinal health research, is a Professor of Orthopedics at RUSH University Medical Center and Director of the International Spine Research and Innovation Program (ISRII). His research examines phenotypic analyses of spinal imaging and pain, as well as “omics” studies (e.g., genomics, metabolomics), biomarker discovery, and precision spinal care. Samartzis has authored over 350 peer‐reviewed articles, with notable publications in high‐impact journals such as The Lancet and Arthritis & Rheumatism. His work has earned international recognition, including prestigious awards like the ISSLS Clinical Award and NASA research grants. Samartzis has also contributed significantly to the development of new definitions and grading systems for MC [14].

An analysis of journals and co‐cited journals offers a robust framework for assessing researchers' contributions. Over the past two decades, Spine has emerged as the most frequently cited journal in studies on MC, with a citation frequency of 585 and a centrality of 0.17. This highlights its central role and substantial academic influence in the field of spinal health. The European Spine Journal and Radiology follow closely, demonstrating high citation frequencies and centrality, establishing them as key references in spinal imaging and diagnosis. Journals such as the American Journal of Neuroradiology (centrality: 0.38) and The New England Journal of Medicine (centrality: 0.72) are less frequently cited but play crucial roles in the academic network by fostering connections and disseminating significant research. In contrast, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders and Skeletal Radiology, despite moderate citation frequencies, exhibit low centrality (both 0.02). This suggests that although these journals address a variety of topics, they occupy a less central position within the core academic network, reflecting a broader research scope rather than a focus on specialized academic exchanges.

The cited literature primarily addresses the clinical correlations, pathophysiological mechanisms, and therapeutic strategies related to MC. As imaging technologies evolve, future research is expected to improve the accuracy of Modic change diagnoses, develop personalized treatment approaches, and explore its associations with other spinal disorders. The top 10 most‐cited papers primarily fall within the field of spinal surgery. Interestingly, the lead authors of these highly cited papers do not overlap with those of the most productive or influential papers over the past 20 years. This divergence may result from the broad scope and extensive collaborations of high‐yield authors, which could dilute their focus on specific, highly cited areas. In contrast, the most highly cited works tend to focus on innovative research or trending topics, attracting concentrated academic attention on particular issues at specific times.

Research Hotspot and Development Trend of MC

Current research on MC primarily concentrates on core areas such as spinal degeneration, pathophysiological mechanisms, imaging diagnostics, and clinical correlations.

Spinal Degeneration and Pathophysiological Mechanism

High‐centrality literature, such as that by Mok FPS (2016) [15], found that the overall incidence of MC increases with age. These changes primarily occur in the two lowest lumbar segments. In multivariate analyses, disc displacement and higher disc degeneration scores were significantly associated with MC in the upper lumbar spine (L1/L2‐L3/L4). In contrast, MC at the lower lumbar levels (L4/L5‐L5/S1) were linked to age, the presence of Schmorl's nodes, disc degeneration or displacement, and a history of lumbar injury. Mok FPS (2016) also identified smoking, obesity, and overweight as significant risk factors for MC. Additionally, MC in the lower lumbar spine was found to be associated with chronic low back pain. Dudli S. (2016) discovered that both chemical and mechanical stimulation of pain receptors adjacent to the injured disc endplate likely serve as sources of pain [7]. MC, which are associated with degraded intervertebral discs, typically manifest in three interchangeable types. These types suggest that Modic lesions represent different stages of the same pathological process, characterized by inflammation, high bone turnover, and fibrosis. These studies, central to the academic network, often involve interdisciplinary collaboration and multicenter research, underscoring their significant academic impact.

Imaging Diagnosis

Keywords play a crucial role in locating relevant articles for comprehensive analysis, as they represent the core concepts of a study and reflect the research hotspots within a specific field. This study analyzed the evolution of keywords related to spinal MC over time through superposition visualization (Figure 12), offering a clearer depiction of shifting research trends. This visualization helps identify changes in research focus, providing valuable guidance for the future direction of the field. Recent advancements in imaging technology have led to the emergence of keywords such as “deep learning” and “artificial intelligence,” signaling a shift toward accurate diagnosis and personalized treatment. This trend highlights the growing potential for integrating advanced technologies, such as artificial intelligence and big data analysis, into Modic change research. For example, Kenneth T. Gao et al. [16] achieved 85.7% accuracy in identifying MC within a test set using a deep learning model, demonstrating satisfactory sensitivity and specificity. Similarly, Yuksek et al. [17] employed deep learning techniques (DenseNet and U‐Net) to classify and segment MC in MRI images. Their results showed classification accuracy as high as 98%–100%, with segmentation accuracy reaching 71%, significantly reducing the workload for radiologists.

The integration of emerging technologies will enhance the accuracy of clinical diagnoses and treatments for MC, offering patients more precise management solutions. Dino Samartzis, the second most prolific author in this field, has conducted prospective studies on the application of artificial intelligence and big data in spinal care [18, 19], aiming to advance evidence‐based, personalized treatment models. His research has significantly contributed to understanding the genetic and nongenetic factors of spinal degeneration [20], particularly focusing on the risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment of disc degeneration and cervical degenerative diseases [21, 22, 23]. Samartzis has also explored novel imaging techniques and precision medicine approaches, which promote the personalization and strengthening of the evidence base for spinal care, providing new insights into the treatment of spinal disorders [24]. He has been instrumental in leading large‐scale, multidisciplinary, and multicenter research efforts that combine big data with advanced bioinformatics tools to enable more precise management and treatment of MC in the spine.

Keyword clustering analysis reveals that recent research has prioritized terms such as “deep learning,” “artificial intelligence,” “action,” and “lumbar disc herniation recurrence.” These findings align closely with the results from keyword co‐occurrence analysis. With the continued development of big data and artificial intelligence technologies, future research is likely to focus on integrating these advanced tools to enhance the accuracy of diagnoses and treatments. This trend is consistent with the areas of study of highly cited journals and core authors, underscoring the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and innovative techniques in Modic change research. In addition to earlier efforts, recent study by Liu et al., a two‐stage deep learning system combining Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD) and ResNet18 was developed to automatically detect and classify MC in lumbar spine MRI. The model demonstrated strong performance, achieving a diagnostic accuracy of 86.25%, offering clinical promise for AI‐assisted radiological interpretation [25].

Clinical Relevance

“MC” and “posterior back pain” are the most frequently occurring keywords in research on MC of the spine (Figure 8). These terms highlight the primary focus of current studies on the relationship between spinal degeneration and pain, a theme that aligns with key works by prominent authors in the field, such as Karppinen's collaborative study published in The Lancet. Low back pain affects individuals across all income levels and age groups, and its specific cause is often difficult to identify. Karppinen's research emphasized the roles of central pain regulation mechanisms, psychological factors, and socioeconomic conditions in the persistence of chronic pai [26].

Keyword burst analysis reveals that “paraspinal muscles,” “obesity,” and “overweight” have become key topics in recent years, pointing to emerging research areas. Obesity and overweight may serve as potential risk factors for MC in the spine. For example, Andrew J. Teichtahl et al. [27] conducted 3.0 T MRI examinations on 72 community members to investigate the correlation between lumbar disc degeneration, MC, and high fat content in paraspinal muscles. Their study found that lumbar disc degeneration often coexists with these lesions, suggesting the need for longitudinal studies to explore causal relationships and potential intervention targets. Emel Ece Ozcan‐Ek‐Si et al. [28] conducted a cross‐sectional study involving a small population, which found that patients with severe disc degeneration were more likely to exhibit increased adipose infiltration in the multifidus and erector spinae muscles. This suggests that severe MC in the lumbar spine are closely associated with adipose infiltration in these muscles. Additionally, as highlighted by the keyword cluster analysis, the physiological changes in muscles may be strongly linked to endplate alterations. Emerging studies in exercise medicine and spinal rehabilitation have begun exploring these avenues, indicating improved paraspinal muscle quality and pain outcomes following targeted physical training [29, 30]. Further experimental studies are needed to confirm this relationship.

Keywords such as “randomized controlled trial” and “double‐blind” reflect the ongoing search for effective interventions. Existing studies show no consensus on the treatment strategies for MC (e.g., antibiotic therapy, physical therapy, etc.) [31, 32]. Future research could involve randomized controlled clinical trials to assess the effectiveness of novel treatments, such as minimally invasive surgery or neuroregulatory techniques, for patients with MC. Moreover, future studies should explore the differentiated effects of various treatment options by incorporating patient classification methods, such as imaging features or biomarkers. Regarding prognosis, longitudinal studies suggest that MC, particularly Type I, may predict a higher risk of symptom persistence and disc degeneration progression [9], yet predictive models remain underdeveloped. Recent studies have also begun to explore the relationship between MC and recurrent lumbar disc herniation (rLDH). Ren et al. demonstrated that the presence of MC was associated with a higher risk of early postoperative recurrence in patients undergoing lumbar discectomy. These findings suggest that MC may influence biomechanical vulnerability of adjacent discs and highlight the need for long‐term monitoring in MC‐positive patients undergoing surgery [33].

Limitation

One limitation of this study lies in the exclusive use of the Web of Science database and English‐language publications. This may introduce selection bias by omitting non‐English studies or regional journals indexed in platforms such as CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure) or Scopus. Consequently, some relevant contributions, particularly from non‐English‐speaking countries, may have been underrepresented. However, this trade‐off was necessary to ensure compatibility with CiteSpace and the consistency of citation indexing for robust visual analysis. Future studies could incorporate multilingual or multidatabase sources to improve comprehensiveness and generalizability. Additionally, a further limitation stems from the inability to separately analyze cervical versus lumbar MC, as most included studies did not specify anatomical locations. Future research could address this gap through focused systematic reviews or imaging‐based meta‐analyses of location‐specific data.

Conclusion

This study is, to our knowledge, the first bibliometric analysis focused on MC in spinal surgery. It identifies current research trends and provides insights into future directions in the field. This analysis may offer valuable guidance for clinicians in the years to come. Future research should focus on the classification of MC, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, and the integration of artificial intelligence in diagnosis and treatment. Additionally, studies should explore the clinical significance of different Modic change types, particularly their association with pain and dysfunction. The role of obesity and paraspinal muscle health in Modic change development, as well as the potential benefits of weight loss and muscle strengthening interventions for symptom relief, should also be prioritized.
