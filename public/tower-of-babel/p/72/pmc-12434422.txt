Incidence and risk factor evaluation for postoperative nausea and vomiting in cesarean section under general anesthesia compared to the gynecologic surgery: A retrospective study
Authors: Su Yeon Kim, Seukyoung Hong, Jung‐Won Hwang, Sang‐Hwan Do, Hyo‐Seok Na
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis retrospective study sought to investigate the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and its associated risk factors in patients undergoing cesarean section under general anesthesia.MethodsPatients who underwent cesarean section under general anesthesia or who underwent open gynecologic surgery were assigned to the OB group (n = 291) or the GY group (n = 225), respectively. The primary outcome was the incidence of PONV. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for PONV in the OB group. Propensity score matching was additionally conducted to facilitate a comparison of PONV incidence between the OB and GY groups (n = 66 in each group).ResultsIn the OB group, 21 patients (7.2%) experienced PONV, which was significantly lower than the 68 patients (30.2%) in the GY group (p < 0.001). This difference remained statistically significant after propensity score matching (15.2% in the OB group vs. 31.8% in the GY group; p = 0.019). In multivariable logistic regression analysis of the OB group, abdominal surgical history (p = 0.032), longer surgical duration (p = 0.017), lower fluid intake (p = 0.011), and higher estimated blood loss (p = 0.009) were associated with PONV.ConclusionsPONV may occur less frequently in patients undergoing cesarean section under general anesthesia than in those undergoing gynecologic surgery. In obstetric patients, substantial intraoperative blood loss with inadequate fluid replacement, prolonged surgical duration, and a history of abdominal surgery may represent additional risk factor for PONV.Keywords: anesthesia, cesarean section, general, gynecologic surgical procedures, postoperative nausea and vomiting

INTRODUCTION

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) generally includes nausea, retching, and vomiting after anesthesia and surgery. PONV occurs in 30% of the general population and up to 70% of the high‐risk population. 1 Although PONV does not pose a threat to the patient's life, it lowers the quality of recovery, prolongs hospital stay, causes dehydration or electrolyte imbalance, and induces unpleasant memories of surgery and anesthesia.

Well‐known risk factors for PONV include female sex, previous history of PONV or motion sickness, postoperative use of opioids, and non‐smoking status. 2 The Apfel scoring system, based on these risk factors, is widely used in the clinical field to predict PONV risk. 3 Specific types of surgery, such as strabismus, head and neck surgery, and neurosurgery, also increase the risk of PONV. 4 Volatile anesthetic agents, nitrous oxide, and the duration of anesthesia have been suggested as anesthesia‐related factors that increase PONV. 2 , 5

Female patients undergoing obstetric and gynecologic surgery have an incidence of PONV of between 30%–80%. 6 , 7 However, a decreased incidence of PONV, 8 , 9 between 14% and 56%, has been reported only in the obstetric surgical population, despite technically being a high‐risk population for PONV (female, non‐smoking, and postoperative opioid use). The fact that most cesarean deliveries are performed under spinal or epidural anesthesia is a significant difference. Accordingly, the incidence of PONV is rarely investigated in cesarean deliveries under general anesthesia.

This retrospective study aimed to determine the incidence and specific risk factors for PONV in obstetric surgical patients. First, we compared the incidence of PONV in young female patients undergoing obstetric or gynecological surgery under general anesthesia. Next, we determined the risk factors for PONV in patients who underwent cesarean section under general anesthesia.

METHOD

Study design and patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B‐2204‐754‐104), which exempted the need to obtain informed consent for this study. The inclusion criteria were female patients aged 20–50 years who had undergone cesarean section or gynecologic surgery under general anesthesia. Only cases of gynecologic surgery performed via laparotomy were included, and only those in which inhalational anesthetics were used as the primary anesthetic agent were enrolled. Desflurane or sevoflurane was used as the inhalational anesthetic. Remifentanil or alfentanil was used as the intraoperative opioid. Patients recruited between January 2010 and December 2021were assigned to the obstetric (OB) or gynecologic (GY) group. Patients who did not use intravenous patient‐controlled analgesia (PCA), those who used intravenous PCA with agents other than fentanyl, those who did not receive prophylactic antiemetics, including serotonin receptor antagonists (e.g., ramosetron or palonosetron), and those with incomplete medical records were excluded from the study.

The following variables were extracted from the electronic medical records for analysis: (1) patient factors including age, body weight, gestational age, history of smoking, underlying medical disease, previous abdominal surgery history, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification; (2) surgery and anesthesia factors including operation time, dose of neuromuscular blocking agents, volume of intraoperative infused fluid, volume of estimated blood loss (EBL), urine output, and transfusion of red blood cell (RBC); and (3) postoperative rescue opioid use, other than fentanyl administered via intravenous PCA, which was converted into morphine equivalents dose during the postoperative 2 days and the number of patients who stopped the intravenous PCA due to PONV.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome was the incidence of PONV up to postoperative day 2. In this study, PONV was defined as any manifestation of nausea, retching, or vomiting. Data on PONV were extracted from medical records, which was measured and documented by the nurses in charge of postoperative PCA. As a secondary outcome, the rate of intravenous PCA discontinuation was assessed. In addition, risk factors for PONV were analyzed in the OB group.

Statistics

Continuous variables were evaluated for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test and then compared using Student's t‐test or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Normally distributed data are presented as means and standard deviations, and non‐normally distributed data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were compared using chi‐squared analysis or Fisher's exact test and expressed as the number of patients and incidence (%). Propensity score‐matched data were considered paired data 10 and analyzed using the paired t‐test or McNemar test for continuous or categorical variables, respectively.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to determine risk factors for PONV up to 2 days postoperatively. Initially, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for PONV using 15 potential variables (Table S1). To reduce the risk of overfitting, the final model included only those factors that demonstrated significant associations: abdominal surgical history, operation time, amount of infused fluid, volume of RBC transfusion, and EBL.

A supplemental analysis was conducted to compare the incidence of PONV between OB and GY groups. Because this was a retrospective study and the surgery was different between the OB and GY groups, the patients could not be randomized, leading to differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups. To reduce bias from confounding factors, we performed propensity‐score matching analysis. Propensity scores were calculated by logistic regression using the following covariates: age, smoking, previous abdominal surgery history, operation time, infused fluid, urine output, estimated blood loss (EBL), volume of RBC transfusion, and MED of postoperative rescue opioid. We excluded covariates that inevitably differed between the two groups, such as height, weight, American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) physical status, and dose of neuromuscular blocking agent. Patients in the two groups were matched using nearest‐neighbor matching with a ratio of 1:1 and a caliper of 0.20, based on their propensity scores. We calculated standardized differences (STDs) in the covariates to assess the balance of confounding variables between the two groups. Variables with an STD lower than 0.25 were considered balanced. 11 Propensity‐score matching was performed to balance the differences between the two groups.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.1; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS (version 28; IBM, Armonk, NY). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 804 patients were evaluated in this study, and 516 patients were finally included in the OB (n = 291) and GY (n = 225) groups (Figure 1). The baseline characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. Due to the natural difference between cesarean section and gynecologic surgery, the two groups showed differences in all basal characteristics. A total of 132 patients (1:1) were propensity score‐matched, and the main covariates used were balanced. Following propensity score matching, the variables that initially showed significant differences between groups were balanced, and no significant differences remained. However, body weight remained significantly different between the two groups even after propensity score matching. Since weight gain is inevitable in pregnant women, we did not include it as a covariate for propensity score matching adjustment. There was no significant difference between the two groups when the pre‐pregnancy body weight of the OB group was compared to those of the GY group (58.9 ± 10.5 in the OB group vs. 61.2 ± 12.8 in the GY group; p = 0.263).

In the OB group, 21 patients (7.2%) experienced PONV, which was significantly lower than the 68 patients (30.2%) in the GY group (p < 0.001; Figure 2a). On the day of surgery, postoperative day 1, and postoperative day 2, the incidences were 2.8%, 4.5%, and 1.5% in the OB group, respectively—significantly lower than the corresponding 12.0%, 21.9%, and 6.2% in the GY group (p < 0.05 for all comparisons; Figure 2a).

After propensity‐score matching, the overall incidence of PONV was also significantly lower in the OB group (15.2%) than in the GY group (31.8%), (p = 0.019) (Figure 2b). When comparing the daily incidence of PONV, a significant difference between the two groups was observed only on postoperative day 1 (6.3% in the OB group vs. 21.9% in the GY group; p = 0.021). There were no significant differences in PONV incidence on the day of surgery and on postoperative day 2.

The incidence of intravenous PCA discontinuation due to PONV was significantly lower in the OB group (6.2%) compared to the GY group (21.8%) (p < 0.001). This significant difference persisted after propensity matching, with discontinuation rates of 12.1% in the OB group and 27.3% in the GY group (p = 0.021).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for PONV in the entire OB population (Table 2). Abdominal surgical history (OR, 2.947; 95% CI, 1.118–7.764; p = 0.029), longer surgery time (OR, 3.839; 95% CI, 1.233–11.954; p = 0.020), lower fluid input (OR, 0.256; 95% CI, 0.077–0.856; p = 0.027), and higher EBL (OR, 2.514; 95% CI, 1.280–4.940; p = 0.007) were associated with PONV in the obstetric patients undergoing cesarean section under general anesthesia.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study demonstrated that patients who underwent cesarean section under general anesthesia had a lower incidence of postoperative PONV than those who underwent gynecological surgery. The proportion of patients who discontinued PCA postoperatively was lower in the OB group. In the present study, longer operative time, lower infused fluid volume, greater EBL, and abdominal surgical history were found to be associated with an increased risk of PONV following cesarean section under general anesthesia.

Few studies have investigated the incidence of PONV in patients who underwent cesarean section under general anesthesia. Park et al. 8 showed results similar to ours in that the incidence of PONV was lower in the cesarean section group until postoperative 36 h than in the gynecological surgery group (14% vs. 47%). Jabalameli et al. reported that the incidence of PONV ranged from 18.8% to 34.4% in the postanesthetic care unit in the cesarean section group with general anesthesia depending on the intraoperative use of opioids, which decreased to less than 3% at postoperative 4–24 h. 12 Another study reported that 56.7% of parturients without any rescue medication presented with PONV on postoperative day 1 after cesarean section; however, it decreased to less than 16.7% after preventive medication with a serotonin receptor antagonist. 9 Overall, it seems that PONV does not occur frequently and its duration is not long if an appropriate preventive strategy is applied. The incidence of PONV in our obstetric population was 7.2% with prophylactic serotonin receptor antagonists, which was significantly lower than that in the gynecologic population.

PONV after cesarean section was observed more frequently under general anesthesia than under spinal anesthesia, 13 and to our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the risk factors for PONV in patients undergoing cesarean section under general anesthesia. Several studies have been conducted to identify the risk factors for PONV in patients who underwent cesarean section under spinal anesthesia. According to these studies, PONV in cesarean section under spinal anesthesia was observed in 12–56% and its risk factors were found to be hyperemesis gravidarum, multiple pregnancies, antiemetic administration, motion sickness during pregnancy, or anticipated analgesia requirement. 14 , 15 , 16 Apfel scores do not seem to be good predictors of PONV in parturients undergoing cesarean section. In our population undergoing cesarean section under general anesthesia, longer operative time, lower infused fluid volume, greater EBL, and abdominal surgical history were found to be risk factors for PONV.

Several studies proved evidence that longer operation or anesthesia time is associated with an increased risk of PONV. Choi et al. identified longer duration of anesthesia as a risk factor for PONV in addition to risk factors of Apfel's score. 17 Another review summarized that the risk of PONV increases by 60% with every additional 30 min of surgery. 18 The analysis demonstrated that parturients undergoing cesarean section under general anesthesia had significantly increased odds of PONV (OR = 3.839; 95% CI, 1.233–11.954; p = 0.020).

Previous other studies have reported that intraoperative blood loss is higher in patients with PONV 19 , 20 and that intravenous fluid replacement probably reduces PONV. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 In this study, although there was an imbalance in the number of subjects across subgroups, patients who developed PONV were found to have a significantly lower net fluid balance. These results raise the question of whether internal fluid imbalance or dehydration is related to the risk of PONV in cesarean section under general anesthesia. It has been known that the intraoperative fluid deficit, hypotension, and following gut hypoperfusion may cause PONV, which can be reduced by the appropriate replacement with fluid therapy. 22 , 25 , 26 , 27 However, further research is still required to determine the appropriate threshold of fluid replacement that affects PONV after cesarean section under general anesthesia.

Interestingly, a history of prior abdominal surgery was identified as a risk factor for PONV following cesarean section under general anesthesia. Currently, previous abdominal surgical history has not been identified as a direct and independent risk factor for PONV. Certain types of abdominal surgery, such as bariatric, gynecologic, and intestinal operations, are associated with higher PONV; however, patient history of prior abdominal surgery was not included as a significant variable. 28 Repeat abdominal surgery may be technically more difficult, requiring longer durations of surgery, greater manipulation of the bowel, or higher opioid use, all of which are risk factors for PONV.

This study has several limitations. First, some well‐established risk factors for PONV, such as a history of PONV or motion sickness, could not be assessed due to its retrospective design and the lack of available patient data. Second, the relatively small number of PONV events limited the inclusion of variables in the multivariate analysis and may have led to potential overfitting. Third, this study only analyzed data up to postoperative day 2. PONV can occur up to several days after surgery; therefore, a longer follow‐up period is needed to accurately assess the incidence and risk factors of PONV. Fourth, cesarean sections are generally performed at our center under spinal anesthesia. In many cases, general anesthesia has been performed mainly in specific circumstances, such as fetal distress, expected massive intraoperative bleeding, or severe cardiopulmonary comorbidity. Thus, patients in the OB group may not be universally representative of general parturients who have undergone elective cesarean section. Lastly, this study did not assess patient‐reported outcomes, such as quality of life, patient satisfaction, or impact on daily activities. These outcomes should be considered when evaluating the effect of PONV in patients.

In conclusion, PONV may occur less frequently in obstetric patients undergoing cesarean section under general anesthesia than in gynecologic surgery patients. Substantial intraoperative blood loss with inadequate fluid infusion, longer surgery, and prior abdominal surgical history could be another risk factor for PONV in obstetric patients. Further prospective studies are required to confirm the actual decrease in PONV with the optimization of intraoperative fluid management according to blood loss during cesarean section.
