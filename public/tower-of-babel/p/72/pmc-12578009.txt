Introduction

Moyamoya, also known as moyamoya disorder (MMD), is a progressive brain disorder of unknown cause. The brain’s internal carotid arteries may sustain partial or complete blockage; hence, the development of abnormal blood vessels usually forms at the base of the brain[1,2]. It usually refers to the revised Research Committee criteria published in 2015. It usually entails the use of catheter angiography in the diagnosis of unilateral cases of the condition, while the use of magnetic imaging is applied in cases of refinement in the bilateral cases[2].

Moyamoya disease exhibited a female predominance and two distinct age peaks of incidence, one in early adolescence and another in middle age[2]. A significant proportion of MMD cases exhibit a familial component[3]. The clinical features are as follows: transient ischemic attack, hemorrhage, migraine, tension-type migraine, etc. A significant factor in the development of MMD, the Ring Finger Protein 213 gene has also been identified[3].

The treatment primarily focuses on managing the symptoms and preventing further complications. Medical management involves antiplatelet drugs, while surgical interventions include endovascular treatment and revascularization procedures. Combined revascularization offers both immediate and long-term benefits but carries the postoperative risk of hyperperfusion syndrome[4]. MMD is a majorly idiopathic condition, but it is reported to be associated with Down syndrome by several researchers[5,6]. The prevalence of MMD in Down syndrome children is 3%–5%. The pathophysiology is unclear, but it is hypothesized that genetic predisposition is leading to congenital vascular anomaly and endothelial dysfunction[7]. Importantly, this overlap can mask the early signs of MMD like delayed motor milestones, hypotonia and behavioral changes[7].

Further research is crucial to optimize medical and surgical approaches, minimize complications, and improve long-term outcomes for individuals with MMD.

A case of a 7-year-old boy presented in a multispecialty hospital in private setting with symptoms of recurrent paroxysmal neurological events for 1 month and was diagnosed with Moyamoya disease. The objective of reporting this case is to highlight the diagnostic complexity in overlapping neurodevelopmental conditions. The case lays the importance of early neuroimaging in atypical presentations of Down syndrome, and underscores the need for high clinical suspicion of Moyamoya in patients experiencing ischemic episodes.

This case report has been done according to the SCARE GUIDELINES 2025 criteria[8].

Case presentation

Case history

A 7-year-old boy with Down syndrome presented to the outpatient department with a 1-month history of recurrent paroxysmal neurological events. The Body Mass Index (BMI) of patient was 12.1 kg/m2. These events were characterized by the sudden onset of transient weakness affecting the right upper and lower limbs. During these episodes, the patient was unable to bear weight on his right leg and experienced difficulty lifting his right arm above shoulder level.

Clinical presentation

Associated symptoms included mild left-sided facial weakness, manifested as deviation of the angle of the mouth toward the left. These episodes lasted for approximately 2–3 minutes, followed by immediate neurological recovery. The patient experienced 4–5 such episodes per week over the past month. Importantly, there was no loss of consciousness, delirium, trauma, numbness, tingling, slurred speech, transient vision loss, or dizziness reported.

Other relevant findings included the absence of fever, convulsions, ear discharge, persistent limb weakness lasting more than 24 hours, limb stiffness or flaccidity, and difficulty walking.

Based on the clinical presentation, the paroxysmal neurological events were suspected to be transient ischemic attacks (TIAs).

Past medical history revealed Down syndrome, neonatal imperforate anus requiring colostomy and subsequent corrective surgery, and significant developmental delays. The patient achieved independent walking at 2.5 years of age and exhibits signs of intellectual disability. He was top-fed due to maternal secondary lactational failure.

Family history included consanguineous marriage. The antenatal history was unremarkable.

Further investigations recommended include neuroimaging (brain MRI/ Computer Tomography (CT)), electrocardiogram (ECG), carotid Doppler ultrasound, and blood tests complete blood count (CBC), blood sugar, lipid profile, coagulation studies).

The patient was alert and oriented to time, place, and person on examination. A thorough head-to-toe examination revealed the characteristic morphological features associated with Down syndrome. Visual and auditory function assessments were unremarkable. No neurocutaneous markers were observed on the patient’s body. The remainder of the physical examination findings were within normal limits. Investigations

Laboratory examination

Laboratory investigations revealed normocytic anemia with normal leukocyte and platelet counts, along with a normal coagulation profile. Further investigations to assess potential underlying causes of the TIAs included specific assays for hypercoagulability, anti-nuclear antibodies, antiphospholipid antibodies, and basal homocysteine levels. All of these investigations yielded normal results.

Imaging examination

The patient’s MRI revealed the presence of several acute lacunar infarcts of a non-hemorrhagic nature, located in the left anterior central semiovale. Additionally, multiple poorly defined areas of abnormal signal intensity were observed within the periventricular and subcortical white matter of both cerebral hemispheres. These lesions exhibited a distribution characteristic of a watershed territory, extending into the right deep corona radiata and the right capsular ganglionic region, suggestive of possible old ischemic foci.

MRA (Fig. 1) demonstrated complete thrombotic occlusion of the intracranial segment of the left internal carotid artery, severe narrowing at the bifurcation of both internal carotid arteries, and significant stenosis of the bilateral anterior and middle cerebral arteries. Multiple collateral vessels with flow-related enhancement were observed in the basal cisterns and bilateral basal ganglia cisterns, likely serving as compensatory pathways for the compromised blood supply to the bilateral middle cerebral artery territories. Digital subtraction angiography revealed complete stenosis of bilateral supraclinoid internal carotid arteries, with filling of these vessels primarily through the posterior communicating arteries and extensive collateral circulation from bilateral posterior cerebral arteries and branches of the external carotid artery. Additionally, the left internal carotid artery demonstrated stenosis 1 cm distal to the middle cerebral artery bifurcation. The venous system exhibited normal morphology and physiological circulation. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of Moyamoya disease was established.

Differential diagnosis

Based on the patient’s history of recurrent paroxysmal neurological events characterized by transient weakness in the right upper and lower limbs, associated facial weakness, and the absence of other neurological deficits, along with the results of laboratory investigations, the possible differential diagnoses include TIAs secondary to atherosclerotic cerebrovascular disease, vasculitides such as Takayasu arteritis, sickle cell crisis (though unlikely in this patient), neurofibromatosis type 1, primary angiitis of the central nervous system, thromboembolic disease, and Moyamoya disease.

Management

The management of Moyamoya disease involves the teamwork of neurologists, neurosurgeons, and other professionals. Lifestyle changes the patient can probably undergo include the use of antiplatelet therapy, blood pressure management, or surgeries aimed at bringing blood flow to the brain.

Conservative management

Initial management consisted of conservative measures, including pharmacotherapy with aspirin 75 mg orally daily, carbamazepine 100 mg orally twice daily, and pantoprazole 20 mg orally daily. Patient relatives were counseled on the importance of maintaining adequate hydration and promptly managing fever. They were instructed to seek immediate medical attention if the frequency of TIAs increased.

Surgical management

The patient underwent surgical intervention to establish an alternative blood supply to the middle cerebral artery. This combined procedure involved encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis, which enhances blood flow to the brain surface, and anastomosis between the superficial temporal artery (from the dura) and the middle cerebral artery, creating a bypass for improved cerebral circulation. Outcome and follow-up

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored for signs of hyperperfusion and neurological deterioration during admission. At the three-month follow-up, the patient exhibited significant clinical improvement with no symptoms associated with TIAs. Neurological assessments showed restored strength in the right upper and lower limbs, and the coordination and balance of the patient were also improved. The patient was advised to continue outpatient follow-up with neurology and physiotherapy services to monitor long-term progress and optimize rehabilitation.

Discussion

Moyamoya disease is a chronic condition affecting blood vessels in the brain. It is marked by the gradual narrowing or blockage of the end part of the internal carotid artery. This blockage leads to the creation of unusually small blood vessels at the base of the brain to make up for less blood flow, which appears as a typical puff of smoke in MR angiography[9]. In this case, the use of MRA as one of the forms of new-generation neuroimaging was critical in making the diagnosis. This was further supported by enlargements of the left and right vertebral arteries, but not the basilar artery, as well as evidence of multiple small lacunar infarcts, also consistent with the diagnosis of Moyamoya disease. This is a clear implication of the value of neuroimaging in other investigations of suspected cerebrovascular diseases, mostly in children who present with unusual symptoms. The patient’s family expressed satisfaction with the care and surgical intervention provided. They reported noticeable improvement in the child’s strength, coordination, and overall well-being, and appreciated the timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary approach taken by the clinical team.

Many patients have a family history. The Ring Finger Protein 213 gene is known to be linked to MMD[10]. Clinical symptoms can be categorized as ischemic, hemorrhagic, or neurological. Common symptoms include ischemic deficits, intracranial hemorrhages, sensory issues, involuntary movements, strokes, seizures, and migraines. Asymptomatic MMD can be detected using MRA, which shows silent microbleeds in the carotid arteries[11]. The diagnosis of MMD was complicated in the present case due to the coexistence of Down syndrome. The overlapping of the clinical features might lead to symptoms masking effect, which is also reported in previous studies[8]. Early neuroimaging is important and critical to avoid the delay in diagnosis of ischemic injury.

The medical management of MMD involves antithrombotic therapy. Single antiplatelet regimens like clopidogrel or cilostazol can be used as primary stroke prevention[12]. Antihypertensive drugs such as nicardipine or labetalol can also be used to prevent hematoma expansion in the early stages of cerebral hemorrhage. In MMD, rtPA thrombolytic therapy should be given based on the principle of assessing the risk of bleeding complications in the hyperacute phase of cerebral ischemia[13].

As applied to therapeutic management, it is noteworthy that patients with symptomatic MMD can safely be offered revascularization therapy. Moyamoya vessels’ hemorrhagic aneurysms can be managed conventionally endovascularly or by revascularization surgery[14]. Revascularization techniques include direct bypass, which is preferred for adults; indirect bypass for children; and combined therapies. Postoperative hyperperfusion can occur, and risk factors include preoperative changes in cerebral blood flow and metabolism. Surgical outcomes vary depending on the procedure and patient age, with higher stroke rates observed in adult patients undergoing direct/combined bypass[15]. Another unique feature of this case is the combined surgical procedure used encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis and superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery bypass, which can be critical for this patient. Taking the decision for surgery in a Down syndrome patient requires critical evaluation of congenital heart disease, wound healing, and the risk of anesthesia. The favorable outcomes show revascularization and beneficial healing.

Post-surgical care should include comprehensive physical therapy, as it plays a vital role in restoring mobility and functionality, while helping prevent complications like muscle contractures and falls. Physical therapists work collaboratively with patients to create individualized treatment plans that address recovery’s physical and psychological aspects[16].

The prediction of the course of MMD is difficult due to its unpredictable course, wherein the disease can be asymptomatic show intermittent symptoms, or show a significant neurological decline. The factors that influence the disease course include the involvement of the arteries, the ability to compensate for the reduced blood flow, the age, and the extent of the arterial narrowing. Hemorrhagic manifestations often result from the fragility of newly formed collateral vessels, predisposing to rupture under stress conditions. This mechanism aligns with findings in studies of nontraumatic brain hemorrhages, which highlight sudden vascular ruptures as a common cause of unexpected deaths[17]. In this context, AI is a promising tool for the risk prediction of MMD. The AI models help to know the vessel fragility, and clinical patterns of such events. Recent advances, such as AlphaFold in molecular biology and AI models predicting treatment outcomes from clinical imaging illustrate how computational intelligence can uncover hidden patterns beyond conventional diagnostics[18,19]. MMD has a strong correlation with stroke and cognitive decline, as reduced cerebral perfusion increases the risk of ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes[20,21]. Upon diagnosis, prompt referral to a specialized center is crucial to develop an appropriate treatment plan. This timely referral ensures access to expert care and facilitates the implementation of necessary interventions to manage the disease effectively[16].

Limitations

This case report of Moyamoya disease highlights a rare clinical scenario. The single-patient focus, for management restricts the generalizability of findings. Additionally, follow-up has been reported for only 3 months; the data beyond that were not available at the time of submission, which limits conclusions about the sustained efficacy of surgical intervention and functional outcomes. Future studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up periods are necessary to establish more definitive recommendations for managing Moyamoya disease in children with Down syndrome.

Conclusion

Moyamoya disease is a rare type of cerebrovascular disease that occurs when the last part of the brain’s internal carotid artery narrows or blocks, creating abnormally thin blood vessels near the base of the brain. This case study describes unusual diagnostic and management features in a case of Moyamoya disease in the context of a child with Down syndrome. The masking effect that overshadowed the patient’s cerebrovascular pathophysiologic process in its early, atypical course was the multiple TIAs. This re-emphasizes the need to have a high index of suspicion for Moyamoya disease in children exhibiting neurological signs and symptoms, especially those who otherwise have predisposing factors.
