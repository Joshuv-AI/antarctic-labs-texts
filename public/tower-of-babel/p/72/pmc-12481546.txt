dECM-Supported Printing of Plasmonic Pillars for SERS Monitoring of Chemotherapy in 3D Tumor Models
Authors: Lara Troncoso-Afonso, Paula Vázquez-Aristizabal, Gail A. Vinnacombe-Willson, Yolany M. Henríquez-Banegas, Patricia González-Callejo, Pablo S. Valera, Malou Henriksen-Lacey, Clara García-Astrain, Luis M. Liz-Marzán
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The development of antitumoral drugs is limited by the absence of in vitro platforms that simultaneously offer biological relevance, spatial complexity and analytical sensitivity to evaluate cellular responses. To overcome these challenges, we propose the integration of surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) sensors into three-dimensional (3D) tumor models. We therefore engineered the incorporation of 3D printed SERS-active hydrogel pillars within a bioprinted breast cancer model featuring distinct tumor and stromal compartments. The model is manufactured using a breast-derived decellularized extracellular matrix bioink, loaded with tumoral and stromal cells, which supports cellular growth and provides the mechanical integrity required to preserve the core/shell organization of the tumor-stroma architecture. In parallel, the sensors are produced from a plasmonic hydrogel ink composed of thiolated alginate and methacrylated carboxymethyl cellulose, which can be chemically photo-cross-linked via thiol–ene click chemistry and loaded with plasmonic gold nanorods. This complex ink shows suitable rheological and mechanical properties for the direct 3D printing of pillar-shaped SERS sensors directly within the tumor-stroma model. These SERS-active pillars enabled the detection of the anti-cancer drug 6-thioguanine (6-TG) in the different compartments of the model, revealing asymmetric consumption of 6-TG by tumoral and stromal cells. These differences are proposed to correlate with a higher cytotoxic response in the tumor core. Therefore, our platform allows for real-time tracking of drug dynamics in a tissue-like environment, thereby offering a versatile tool for therapeutic screening.

In vitro tumor models offer opportunities for enhancing the development of effective therapies targeting breast cancer–the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide–because of their potential to recapitulate the main features of tumors in a patient-specific manner. In the context of drug development, preclinical trials of antitumoral candidates provide preliminary evaluations regarding the efficacy, toxicity, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic parameters, as well as safety concerns derived from their use. Accurately determining these parameters mainly depends on the fidelity with which the test model recapitulates the complexity and heterogeneity of the targeted tissue, significantly influencing the outcome of the following stages of clinical trials. Conventionally, preclinical studies are conducted in two-dimensional (2D) cultures of breast cancer cell lines prior to investigation in animal models. However, cell behavior in 2D substantially differs from that in native tissue. On the other hand, animal models face ethical concerns, high cost, and low throughput, and present divergences with humans in terms of physiology, immune system and genetics. Altogether, these limitations largely contribute to high antitumoral attrition rates. As an alternative, three-dimensional (3D) in vitro cellular models (i.e., spheroids, organoids, microfluidic-based tumors-on-chip, 3D bioprinted architectures, etc. − ) offer higher throughput, fewer ethical concerns, and lower cost compared to animal models, and facilitate more accurate cell–cell and cell-matrix interactions than 2D cultures. Thus, in vitro models have the potential to serve as supporting or intermediate-stage platforms for cancer therapy development.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) composition in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and tumor-stroma interactions have been reported to drive tumor progression and metastasis, leading to poor clinical outcomes. , As a result and focusing specifically on breast cancer, research efforts have focused on the fabrication of tumor models incorporating defined tumor and stromal components by means of advanced manufacturing techniques such as 3D bioprinting, thus moving a step closer to reproducing breast TME complexity in vitro. − Recently, our group developed a porcine-derived breast decellularized ECM (dECM) bioink for fabrication of 3D bioprinted stroma-supported tumor core models. The decellularization of animal or human healthy tissue has been shown to preserve key biological components (e.g., matrisomal core proteins) of the native ECM that are absent in other widely used biomaterials (alginate, gelatin, hyaluronic acid, etc.). The porcine derived breast dECM additionally offers favorable mechanical properties for extrusion bioprinting in decellularized support baths. Such 3D bioprinted tumor models exhibit a core–shell like structure comprising a spherical tumoral core surrounded by a stromal bath.

We propose that this model might assist in the identification of cancer biomarkers and high-throughput therapeutic screening. However, studying cellular response to therapeutics in complex 3D cell models with spatial and temporal control remains challenging and requires techniques with high sensitivity. In this regard, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) allows for the identification of relevant biomarkers in complex biological settings. This technique has gained significant attraction in biology and biomedicine because of the low inelastic Raman cross-section of water. , SERS relies on the amplification of the Raman vibrational modes of the chemical bonds within a molecule adsorbed onto (or nearby) the surface of a plasmonic nanostructure. Anisotropic gold nanoparticles, such as gold nanorods (AuNRs), have been shown to be exceptional plasmonic substrates in biological systems thanks to their biocompatible nature and refined synthetic pathways that determine their optical properties. AuNRs can be incorporated into hydrogel networks and lead to printable materials for the manufacture of scaffolds capable of supporting cell growth in 3D while using SERS to track changes in the ECM during cell/tumor progression. ,

Following a similar rationale, we hypothesized that plasmonic hydrogels, designed for in situ biosensing, could be printed within complex 3D cellular models such as the aforementioned core/stroma breast cancer archetype. By printing plasmonic hydrogel pillars at defined locations of the model (e.g., within the tumor core or the stroma), we would be able to track metabolites produced by tumor and stromal cells, as well as probing (cancer and noncancer) cell responses to anticancer drugs. We focused on the response of breast tumors to the chemotherapeutic agent 6-thioguanine (6-TG), a purine analog of guanine with both anticancer and immune-suppressive properties. 6-TG induces cell death by being erroneously incorporated into DNA and is particularly effective against cancers with a high frequency of homozygous deletion of the gene to produce the methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) enzyme. Even though this drug is traditionally used in clinics for the treatment of leukemia, studying its activity against breast tumors could be useful for drug repurposing. Given that our model can be constructed using MDA-MB-231 cells (which lack MTAP expression) in the tumor core, we selected this drug to evaluate its selective activity by SERS. Interestingly, this guanine analog exhibits a highly defined and intense SERS fingerprint. To build the plasmonic hydrogel, AuNRs were loaded into a click cross-linkable combination of alginate and carboxymethylcellulose with good printability and biocompatibility, as well as reliable SERS signals from the analytes eventually embedded in the gel. Breast dECM was used as a support bath for the extrusion of the plasmonic ink, also providing the ECM for the tumoral and stromal compartments of the model. This printing approach takes advantage of the biological and supportive properties conferred by the dECM, retaining the cylindrical shape of the pillars prior to curing the material with ultraviolet (UV) light. Pillars were printed in both the tumoral and stromal compartments of the model, without disrupting the core/shell architecture, and enabled the study of 6-TG consumption by the different cell populations over time.

Results and Discussion

Gold nanorods were chosen as the plasmonic substrate because of their tunable localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) through defined synthesis parameters. In particular, our AuNRs have an aspect ratio of 2.9 ± 0.5, which leads to an LSPR mode at 785 nm (Figure S1A,B), within the first biological transparency window (750–1000 nm). For the hydrogel matrix, alginate (ALG) and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) were considered because of their high printing shape-fidelity, wide availability and biocompatible nature. Alginate is a low cost, natural polymer obtained from the cell walls of brown algae and some bacteria. Although alginate can be cross-linked using calcium ions (Ca2+), this so-called physical cross-linking can lead to degradation over time under physiological conditions. , On the other hand, carboxymethyl substituted cellulose, derived from the plant biopolymer cellulose, features a high density of reactive sites that allow chemical modifications to meet specific biochemical and mechanical properties. With the aim of fabricating biomaterials with long-term stability in cell media, we chemically modified alginate with thiol groups (TALG), whereas methacrylate moieties were incorporated in the carboxymethylcellulose backbone. Subsequently, chemical photo-cross-linking of the printed hydrogel network was promoted through a thiol–ene click reaction (Figure A). The carboxylic acid groups in the guluronic (G) units of alginate were esterified under acidic conditions using thioglycolic acid, while carboxymethylcellulose was methacrylated using the available hydroxyl groups. Both polymers were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), 1H NMR, and Elman’s assay on the thiolated polymer to confirm the success of the functionalization (Figures S2 and S3). The thiolation of alginate was quantified to be around 5%, whereas the methacrylation of CMC was around 21%.

For the preparation of the plasmonic ink (TALG-MCMC ink), TALG and MCMC, both at 2% (w/v) concentration, were added to a colloidal dispersion of AuNRs ([Au0] = 1.5 mM), as schematically depicted in Figure A. The concentration of AuNRs was chosen according to our previous report, which identified 1.5 mM as the concentration yielding the best SERS signal (Figure S4). Subsequently, lithium phenyl 2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was added to promote orthogonal photocatalyzed cross-linking of the resulting bioink mixture (TALG-MCMC). Although the thiol–ene reaction between TALG and MCMC achieves permanent cross-linking of the extruded material, CaCl2 (100 mM) was also added to the ink formulation to increase the viscosity as a pre-cross-linking step to improve the shape fidelity of the extruded structures during 3D printing. The stability of AuNRs in this mixture was confirmed by ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy (Figure S5A), which shows the two characteristic LSPR bands for AuNRs after the production of the ink. Additionally, considering that the goal was to detect analytes in 3D breast cancer models, the stability of the cross-linked TALG-MCMC ink was addressed in complete cell media (cDMEM). The maximum swelling ratio (SR, 1116%) was obtained after 2 h of incubation. A reduction in SR at longer incubation times was assigned to partial loss of the physical cross-linking of TALG subunits due to the slow diffusion of Ca2+ ions into the surrounding medium. Nonetheless, after a week of incubation, the swelling ratio was still significant (905%), which indicated sufficient stability of the TALG-MCMC hydrogel in cDMEM during the time needed for cellular studies (Figure S5B).

To ensure printability, the rheological properties of the resulting mixture were assessed, including viscosity and ink flow, kinetics of the photoinitiated thiol–ene reaction (photorheology), and viscoelastic behavior. The flow curve of TALG-MCMC ink is shown in Figure B. This test is used to study the dynamic viscosity of the ink, as well as to understand the flow properties during extrusion. Ideally, for a hydrogel-like ink to be extruded through the nozzles used in 3D printing, a shear thinning behavior is required. Our ink indeed shows a shear-thinning behavior, with viscosity decreasing by 2–3 orders of magnitude when the shear rate is increased at a similar rate. Amplitude sweeps were also performed to determine the linear viscoelastic region in which the loss (G″) and storage moduli (G′) are independent of the applied strain (Figure S6A). Additionally, real-time photorheometric measurements were performed to study the changes in G′ and G″ upon irradiation with UV light. In Figure C, we can observe that the bioink started forming a gel prior to irradiation (G′ > G″), likely due to the presence of CaCl2 in the ink formulation. Nevertheless, both moduli were found to increase by ca. 2 orders of magnitude upon exposure to UV light, which could be correlated with the formation of new chemical bonds (R-S-R′) in the hydrogel network as a result of the thiol–ene reaction, which contribute to reinforcing the overall structure. UV irradiation was initiated after 40 s, causing both moduli to initially increase and then reach a steady state after 10 s. Therefore, we conclude that the photoinitiated thiol–ene reaction promotes a fast (<1 min) cross-linking of the hydrogel network. Control experiments in the absence of photoinitiator showed that neither the storage nor the loss moduli increased, further supporting the effect of chemical photo-cross-linking of the ink, as intended (Figure S6B). The viscoelastic behavior of the bioink was determined by a frequency sweep test. The assay showed that G′ was consistently higher than G″ across all frequencies, thus reproducing a gel-like behavior (G′ > G″). Moreover, it can be observed that the ink shows elastic behavior characterized by a frequency-independent G′ while the viscous behavior can be observed by the slight increase in G″ (Figure S6C).

Plasmonic pillars were subsequently printed within a bath of porcine dECM using the AuNR-loaded TALG-MCMC ink (Figure A). The printing parameters were optimized to achieve pillars measuring around 3 mm in height and 600 μm in diameter (see the Experimental Section for details). Because the dECM forms a gel within approximately 30 min at 37 °C, a heating bed was employed to achieve a partially cross-linked dECM gel that functions as a supportive bath retaining the cylindrical shape of the pillars during extrusion, while enabling free motion of the printer nozzle through the dECM. In contrast to printing without a support bath, this printing method allowed us to photo-cross-link the entire pillar after its complete deposition, thereby yielding homogeneous cross-linking of the material in selected regions of the model (Figure B). Control experiments conducted without a dECM bath (Figure S7A) and without the pre-cross-linking step (Figure S7B) yielded poor results. The microstructural features of the TALG-MCMC hydrogels were evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of freeze-dried hydrogels, which confirmed a porous microstructure for both the dECM and the pillars (Figure C). At higher magnification, the presence of AuNRs within the pillars was confirmed, retaining a well-defined rod-like morphology, even after the complete preparation and printing processes (Figure D).

The sensing performance of plasmonic pillars embedded in dECM was first tested by incubating the whole system with different concentrations of 6-TG in cDMEM. To do so, the different solutions were added on top of the models and incubated at 37 °C. The mechanism of action of 6-TG is represented in Figure A. In short, cells lacking the MTAP enzyme cannot recycle adenine from methylthioadenosine (MTA), leading to high levels of 5′-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate (PRPP), which promote the conversion of 6-TG into its toxic nucleotide that ultimately inhibits DNA synthesis and causes cellular death. The tumor core of our breast cancer model contains MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cells, which are MTAP-deficient, whereas the stromal compartment is built from human breast fibroblasts (HBF) expressing MTAP. Therefore, we propose that our model could be used to test and evaluate the selectivity of treatment by 6-TG.

The performance of the dECM-embedded plasmonic pillars was evaluated by monitoring the intensities of the characteristic SERS peaks of 6-TG at different regions of the tumor model after incubation with 6-TG in cDMEM. The structure of 6-TG comprises a purine ring bearing an amino and a thiol group that can coordinate with Au, favoring chemical interaction with AuNRs and resulting in a well-defined SERS profile (Figure B). The SERS spectrum shows several characteristic vibrational peaks at 910, 950, 1246, 1274, and 1303 cm–1, as well as the main characteristic peak for DMSO at 680 cm–1 (see assignments in Table S1). , We chose to monitor the intensity of the most intense SERS peak at 1303 cm–1 to map the 3D distribution of 6-TG molecules. Therefore, we recorded the SERS spectra over the surface of the pillars (xy maps) at different z planes. In general, no differences were observed between the outer walls of the pillar and its interior, suggesting that the molecules could diffuse across the entire sensor width (Figure C). The stacking of the xy intensity maps was used to reconstruct the pillar, showing that the maximum signal for the 6-TG peak was detected close to the upper side of the pillars, gradually decreasing as deeper layers of the sensor were mapped (Figure D). By repeating the experiment for decreasing 6-TG concentrations, we determined a detection range from 1 mM down to 10 μM (Figure E), covering the concentration range typically employed in 2D cellular studies (see toxicity analysis in Figure S8).

Next, we aimed to print the plasmonic pillars while preserving the core/shell architecture of the core-stroma model, as well as the viability of the cells embedded in the dECM (Figure A). Therefore, we first printed the tumor core within a supportive dECM bath containing stromal cells and then plasmonic pillars were printed into both the tumor core and the stromal compartment. In more detail, stromal and tumor bioinks were prepared by suspending HBF and MDA-MB-231 cells in dECM, respectively. The tumor core was built by extruding a multilayered circular structure in the center of a 1 cm2 in-house printed well containing pre-cross-linked stromal bioink. Subsequently, the entire model was heated up to 37 °C for 15 min to initiate cross-linking in the tumor core. Finally, the TALG-MCMC ink was then used to print the pillars inside the core and the stromal compartments, and the whole system was incubated at 37 °C to complete the dECM cross-linking. Figure B shows the integration of sensing pillars in the 3D tumor/stroma model, while the core/shell architecture was maintained as well as the cellular integrity (Figure S9). The models including cells and pillars were further characterized using SEM, which revealed (Figure C) the presence of cells with the expected morphology within the pores of the dECM matrix. Shown in Figure D is a representative image of the network of fibrous material within the dECM, similar in appearance to collagen, in agreement with previous studies characterizing the protein content and composition of porcine breast dECM. To further characterize the morphological characteristics of the model, we captured SEM micrographs at different time points. The fibrous network appeared to be characteristic of the dECM itself, being observed in samples cultured up to 7 days (Figure S10). The presence of a fibrous network within a porous material appears to recapitulate some of the structures present in native tissues (Figure S11). Additionally, we observe slight batch-to-batch differences similar to what is found when comparing tumor-to-tumor microenvironments.

Finally, we aimed to demonstrate the application of SERS to monitor the consumption of the antitumoral drug 6-TG. Therefore, the 3D core/stroma model including the sensing pillars was incubated with a 400 μM 6-TG solution (1% v/v DMSO/cDMEM). This concentration was chosen considering the cytotoxicity observed in 2D cell cultures (Figure S8). The sensors in the core and stroma of the model were then used to monitor the presence of 6-TG. SERS maps of three independent samples were collected at different time points after addition of 6-TG (day 1, day 3, and day 5). The results are summarized in Figure . Intensity maps for the peak at 1303 cm–1 of 6-TG, recorded in the tumor core and in the stroma are plotted in Figure A and B, respectively. The area under the peak at 1303 cm–1 was compared at all three time points in the core and the stroma, in triplicate. In Figure C, one can observe how the peak area decreases after 5 days in both compartments, indicating drug consumption by the cells. However, we can see a significantly greater decrease of intensity over time in the core compared to the stroma, which is suggestive of a higher consumption rate of the antitumoral drug by the tumor cells. This observation was confirmed by live/dead staining of the model, which also revealed a higher degree of cell death in the core. Supported by control experiments revealing high viability (Figures D and S12), these results suggest the cytotoxic effect results primarily from the presence of 6-TG.

Conclusions

The present work showcases the use of plasmonic hydrogels to introduce sensors into complex 3D in vitro models. Through the thiolation of alginate and methacrylation of carboxymethylcellulose, orthogonally photo-cross-linked hydrogels can be obtained via formation of carbon–sulfur bonds. This chemically cross-linked hydrogel shows convenient rheological properties for 3D printing and stability in cell culture media, as well as excellent biocompatibility. This material can additionally be used to print sensors inside a dECM, allowing for the in situ detection of 6-thioguanine, with a detection limit around 10 μM. Similarly, plasmonic hydrogel pillars can be printed inside the tumor and stroma compartments of a 3D breast cancer model without disrupting the integrity of the core/shell architecture. The rational distribution of pillars along the different compartments in this tumor model allows monitoring of the differential response to an antitumoral drug exhibited by two different cell populations. Moreover, in combination with fluorescence microscopy we can confirm that tumor cells in the core are more responsive to the drug than stromal cells. Overcoming the limitations of other conventional techniques when probing 3D constructs in detail, these biocompatible plasmonic nanomaterials provide access to evaluating selective 6-TG consumption by MTAP deficient-tumoral cells via SERS sensing. Broadening the scope of antitumoral drugs that can be detected, together with the ability to monitor cell death directly using plasmonic sensors, might further increase the potential of these platforms for preclinical testing, potentially involving optimization of the hydrogel composition.

Experimental section

Materials

Acetone (≥99.5%), alginic acid sodium salt (alginate, MW 150,000 g/mol), l-ascorbic acid (AA, ≥99%), 5-bromosalicylic acid (5-BrSA, technical grade, 90%), calcium chloride (CaCl2, 99.99%), hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, ≥96%), hydrogen tetrachloroaurate trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, ≥99.9%), deuterium oxide (D2O, ≥99), dithiobis 2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB/Ellman′s reagent, ≥98%), ethanol (≥99.5%), 2-iminothiolane hydrochloride (Traut’s, ≥98%), lithium phenyl-2,4–6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP, ≥95%), methacrylic anhydride (≥98%), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 99%), potassium chloride (KCl, ≥99%), potassium phosphate monobasic (KH2PO4, ≥99%), sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, MW 90.000 g/mol, 70% carboxylation), sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥97%), sodium phosphate dibasic (Na2HPO4, ≥99%), sodium phosphate monobasic (NaH2PO4, ≥99%), silver nitrate (AgNO3, ≥99.9%), 6-thioguanine (6-TG, ≥98%) and thioglycolic acid (TGA, ≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. AlamarBlue HS cell viability reagent, cell tracker blue CMF2HC, cell tracker deep red dye, Invitrogen ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt 0.5 M, pH = 8.9, RNase-free (EDTA, ≥99%) and Gibco Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) were purchased from Thermo Fisher. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) was purchased from Scharlau. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin-streptomycin (PS) were purchased from Invitrogen. FBS and PS were used at 10% (v/v) and 1% (v/v) to prepare complete DMEM (cDMEM). Human breast MDA-MB-231 cell line was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC HTB-26). Primary human breast fibroblasts (HBF) were purchased from Innoprot. All chemicals were used as received. All glassware used for AuNRs synthesis was washed with aqua regia, rinsed with water and dried before used.

Synthesis of Gold Nanorods

AuNRs were prepared according to well-established procedures. Gold seeds were first prepared by dissolving 25 μL of 50 mM HAuCl4 in 4.7 mL of 0.1 M CTAB and stirring over 5 min. Then, 300 μL of freshly prepared 10 mM NaBH4 was injected under vigorous stirring and the resulting solution remained under mild stirring for 30 min more to consume excess borohydride. In order to obtain AuNRs, a growth solution was prepared by dissolving 45 mg of 5-BrSA in 25 mL of 0.1 M CTAB followed by the addition of 480 μL of 100 mM AgNO3. The mixture was stirred for 14 min at room temperature before adding 500 μL of HAuCl4 50 mM. Under these conditions, Au­(III) was reduced to Au­(I) causing the solution to change from orange to clear yellow. This process was monitored by UV–vis spectroscopy until the absorbance value at 396 nm was in between 0.80 and 0.85, expected to produce AuNRs with a longitudinal plasmon band around 780 nm. Once the desired absorbance value was achieved, 130 μL of 100 mM AA was added and stirring was increased prior to the addition of 80 μL of the seed solution. The resulting mixture remained undisturbed overnight to allow AuNR growth. Finally, AuNRs were purified by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 30 min and redispersion of the precipitate, first in water and after a second centrifugation in 1 mM CTAB solution. The resulting nanoparticles exhibited dimensions of 56.5 ± 4.5 nm in length and 17.5 ± 3.8 nm in width.

Synthesis of Functionalized Polymers

Synthesis of Thiolated Alginate (TALG)

Thiolated alginate (TALG) was prepared following a reported procedure with some modifications. In a 100 mL round-bottom flask, sodium alginate (1 g) was dissolved in 40 mL of H2O at 80 °C. Then, 4 mL of TGA were added followed by the slow addition of 2 mL of HCl (7M) over a period of 10 min using an addition pump (100 μL/min). The solution was stirred for 3 h before precipitation in pure methanol. The solid was redissolved and the pH was adjusted to 8. The remaining solution was dialyzed against water over a week and the final product was obtained after lyophilization as a white/beige solid.

Synthesis of Methacrylated Carboxymethyl Cellulose (MCMC)

Methacrylated carboxymethyl cellulose (MCMC) was obtained following a reported protocol. In a 250 mL round-bottom flask, carboxymethylcellulose (2.5 g) was dissolved in 125 mL of dH2O. Once the solution became homogeneous, 10 mL of methacrylic anhydride was added and pH was continuously adjusted to 8 with NaOH. The solution remained under stirring for 24 h and the resulting mixture was dialyzed against water for a week. The final product was obtained after lyophilization as a white solid.

Preparation TALG-MCMC Ink

The preparation of the ink required further purification of AuNRs by centrifugation at 8000 rpm and redispersion in MilliQ water to remove excess CTAB. Then, 40 mg of methacrylated carboxymethylcellulose (MCMC) and 40 mg of thiolated alginate (TALG) were dissolved into 1.6 mL of AuNRs colloidal dispersion (1.9 mM). Then, 400 μL of CaCl2 100 mM was added to achieve final volume of 2 mL at 1.5 mM concentration of AuNRs. The concentration of nanoparticles in the ink can be confirmed by measuring the absorbance at 400 nm and considering that a value of 1.2 in absorbance corresponds to 0.5 mM Au0. Finally, 0.1% (w/v) LAP was dissolved in the previous mixture. The resulting solution was homogenized by shaking at 1000 rpm during 60 s using a speed mixer (DAC 150.1 FV–K, Hauschild) right before loading into the printing cartridge.

Characterization of AuNRs

Ultraviolet–Visible Absorbance Spectroscopy

The colloidal dispersions of AuNRs were characterized by UV–vis spectroscopy, using a Cary 60 UV–vis spectrophotometer from Agilent.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

The morphology and dimensions of AuNRs were characterized by TEM with a JEOL JEM-2100F (UHR) transmission electron microscope. All samples were prepared by placing a drop of 3 μL of the colloidal solution onto a TEM grid and drying at RT before imaging.

Characterization of the Polymers

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (1H NMR)

The chemical structure of the synthesized polymers was determined through 1H NMR analysis using NMR-Bruker AVANCE III 500 MHz. All samples were prepared by dissolving 10 mg of the polymer in D2O (residual peak at 4.79 ppm) and the analysis was conducted at 298 K accumulating 64 scans. All spectra were analyzed using Mestrenova software.

Fourier Transform-Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR studies were conducted using ATR-FTIR Bruker INVENIO X. The spectrometer is equipped with an accessory featuring a single-reflection monolithic diamond for measuring attenuated total reflectance (ATR). Both polymers were characterized by directly placing a small amount of the dried sample on the ATR crystal, ensuring full coverage of the crystal surface. The background was automatically corrected by the software and the spectrum was collected from 450 to 4000 cm–1, accumulating 64 scans.

Ellman’s Test

To determine the number of thiols incorporated to alginate, Ellman’s test was performed. First, 40 mg of TALG was diluted into 250 μL of reaction buffer. In parallel, three test tubes were prepared, each containing 50 μL of Ellman’s reagent solution (4 mg DTNB/mL reaction buffer) and 2.5 mL of reaction buffer. Then, the TALG-containing solutions were added into the previous test tubes for incubation for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, 200 μL of each solution were added to a 96-well plate to record the absorbance at 412 nm using Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode Microplate Reader from BioTek. The absorbance values were used to calculate the amount of free thiols according to a calibration curve obtained using cysteine as standard.

Characterization of the TALG-MCMC Hydrogel

Rheology

Rheological measurements were performed using a Physica MCR 302 rheometer equipped with a UV curing lamp (Anton Paar). Two different geometries and measurement configurations were used for the TALG-MCMC ink and TALG-MCMC cross-linked hydrogel. A conventional parallel plate (geometry PP25, Ø25 mm) was used for the TALG-MCMC ink measurements, and 200 μL of the ink was placed between the plate and the measuring plate with 0.1 mm gap. In contrast, a sandblasted parallel plate (geometry PP08/P2, Ø8 mm) was used instead in the case of the TALG-MCMC hydrogel, to prevent slipping. The hydrogel disc (Ø8 mm) was placed between the fixed plate and the measuring plate with a 2 mm gap. In all cases, a solvent trap device was employed to prevent water evaporation. The assays were conducted at 37 °C to reproduce the behavior of both the ink and the hydrogel in cell culture conditions.

The amplitude sweep was performed on the TALG-MCMC ink to identify the linear viscoelastic (LVE) region over a shear strain range of 0.01 to 100%. To study gelation kinetics, the TALG-MCMC ink was subjected to a shear strain of 0.1% within the LVE range and a frequency of 1 Hz. The measurement was performed over 170 s, with gelation initiated by exposure to a UV curing lamp at 365 nm after initial 40 s. For the TALG-MCMC hydrogel, frequency sweeps were conducted in triplicate to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) over an angular frequency (ω) range of 0.1 to 100 rad/s, using a fixed shear strain (γ) of 0.1% within the LVE region, identified from the previously performed amplitude sweep at 37 °C. The flow curve test for ink samples was conducted over a shear rate (γ̇) range of 1–1000 s–1.

Swelling Test

Swelling tests were performed by first freeze-drying three TALG-MCMC hydrogel samples. These were weighed before immersing them in cDMEM during 1 h at 37 °C. After incubation under static conditions, the gels were removed and dried with adsorbent paper to remove excess of water before being weighed to determine their mass. This process was repeated at intervals of 2 h, 1 day, 2 days, and 1 week. Throughout the process, it was ensured that the hydrogels were always fully immersed in cDMEM for optimal diffusion and the experiments were made in triplicate. The swelling ratio (SR%) was calculated according to the following equation SR(%)=WWET−WDRYWDRY·100

Bioprinting

Fabrication of the 3D Breast Cancer Model

A multihead 3D Discovery bioprinter (RegenHU) was used to print the core/shell model with integrated plasmonic sensors. The tumoral compartment of the model contained MDA-MB-231 cells while the stroma was built with HBF. Before bioprinting, both cell types were fluorescently labeled with two different cell trackers: MDA-MB-231 were labeled with Cell Tracker Blue CMF2HC whereas HBFs were labeled with Cell Tracker Deep Red. MDA-MB-231 cells were suspended in ice-cold dECM (1.8% w/v) at a concentration of 6 × 106 total cells/mL. The stromal bioink was obtained by redispersing fluorescently labeled HBFs in ice-cold dECM (1.8% w/v) at a concentration of 3 × 106 total cells/mL. The whole printing process took place on a heating bed at 37 °C. First, 270 μL of the stromal bioink was added to a homemade cubic holder of 0.5 cm3 and heated at 37 °C for 15 min using the heating bed accessory of the bioprinter. Once the stromal bioink was precross-linked, the tumoral core was bioprinted in the center of the holder as a four-layered circular structure with a printing design of 1 mm in diameter. During printing, the tumoral bioink was extruded through a dispensing needle with an internal diameter of 0.3 mm (Nordson), at a pressure in between 0.019 and 0.023 MPa, at a printing speed of 3 mm/s.

Bioprinting of the Pillars in the 3D Tumor-Stroma Model

The previous model was heated at 37 °C for 15 min to allow the extruded dECM to pre-cross-link. Subsequently, the TALG-MCMC ink was used to print the sensors in the tumoral and stromal compartments. The sensors were printed as a cylinder (6 layers) of 0.4 mm in diameter, using a high-precision dispenser plunger through a dispensing needle with an internal diameter of 0.6 mm (20GA) (Nordson), at a plunger velocity of 0.0172 mm/s with no retraction distance (0 mm) and a volume flow rate of 0.780 μL/s. The cylindrical sensors were cross-linked with UV light during 2.4 s upon deposition of the entire cylinder using a light curing cartridge at 405 nm wavelength with a power density of 25.3 mW/cm2. Finally, the tumor-stroma constructs measuring 1 cm × 1 cm × 3 mm (length, width and height) with the integrated sensors (diameter 0.6 mm and 3 mm of length) were incubated at 37 °C to complete gelation of the dECM. After 15 min, cDMEM (500 μL) was added on top of the model. The models were then incubated for up to 14 days. The printing designs and the generation of the G-code were produced using BIOCAD software (RegenHU).

Incubation of the Models with 6-TG

For the incubation of 6-TG within the sensors embedded in dECM, 6-TG (50 mM in DMSO stock) was diluted in dECM (270 μL) to the desired final concentration (5 μM to 1 mM), prior to printing the sensors. After the printing, the models were swollen in complete DMEM to reproduce the conditions of the cellular incubation. For the incubation of breast cancer cell models with 6-TG, 1 day after being printed, the models were either treated with the drug or its vehicle. The treated models were swollen with a solution of 400 μM 6-TG in 1%(v/v) DMSO-containing cDMEM. Control models were incubated with vehicle (1% (v/v) DMSO-containing cDMEM). Both control and treated types of models were kept on culture for over 6 days and subjected to the SERS/fluorescence microscopy measurements. To investigate the differences in drug uptake between the core and the stroma, we calculated the average spectra of the intensity maps at the top of the sensors (highest signal). From these average spectra we calculated the area under the peak at 1303 cm–1 for each time point in the core and the stroma. The experiments were conducted in triplicate and the average area was calculated for each time point in the core and the stroma of the model.

Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS)

Prior to starting the bioprinting process, homemade holders were arranged on top of quartz slides. Measurements were made using the backscattering configuration of the instrument and a Renishaw 1014 CCD Camera using a 1024 × 256 detector with a grating of 1200 l/mm and slit opening of 65 μm. All measurements were conducted through a Leica 40× immersion objective, with a working distance of 3 mm and a numerical aperture of 1.4. The spectra were collected using a 785 nm laser at 100% power (205 mW/μm2) over 3 s and 2 accumulations. In Figure C, the map was acquired using a xy step size of 70 μm × 70 μm, whereas the maps in Figure D were acquired from selected areas (r = 500 μm, xy step size = 100 μm × 100 μm) at a z step size of 100 μm within each sensor. This enabled us to acquire the SERS spectra of a volume of the pillar (∼0.2 mm3) within 3 h. Higher z-resolution is possible, we tested 50 μm in z, but the spectral data were not significantly different, and thus we chose to prioritize overall imaging time over resolution for this study. For the maps in Figure A,B, smaller maps were collected (r = 250 μm, xy step size = 100 μm × 100 μm) to decrease the measuring time and avoid overexposure to the laser. All the collected spectra were baseline-corrected, smoothed and cosmic rays were removed before obtaining the intensity maps using WiRE 4.4. Renishaw Raman microscope software.

Confocal Fluorescence Microscopy Imaging

Confocal laser scanning fluorescence microscopy imaging was performed with a LSM880 microscope (Zeiss), equipped with Ar+, DPSS and HeNe lasers, and Plan-Apochromat 10× (0.45 NA) objective. Tiled imaging was performed in different z planes.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

Sample Preparation

TALG-MCMC hydrogels were freeze-dried. The dECM samples were collected at different time points as indicated (1, 7, or 11 days in vitro) and washed with PBS 3-fold, 10 min each. Then, the samples were fixed with 2% formaldehyde/2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS at 4 °C overnight (∼16 h, the samples were protected from light using aluminum foil). The samples were washed again thrice with PBS for 30 min each, then with Milli-Q water another three times at 30 min each. The samples underwent a final fixation step in an aqueous solution of osmium tetroxide (OsO4, 1% w/v) for 20 min.

Safety Warning

Osmium tetroxide solutions and their vapors are highly toxic and severe irritants. All skin and eye contact must be strictly avoided by following safety precautions: Osmium tetroxide must only be handled in a fume hood and with full PPE (double gloves, lab coat, and chemical splash goggles). Furthermore, all waste should be neutralized with oil (seed oil, corn oil, etc.) prior to disposal.

Lastly, the sample was washed again with Milli-Q water three times at 30 min each. While such long formaldehyde/glutaraldehyde fixations and high concentrations of osmium tetroxide can lead to overfixation, causing cell shrinkage and various artifacts relevant to histology or light microscopy characterization, we applied these quantities and incubation times to ensure that the fixative penetrated the entire 3D volume of the sample. In fact, when shorter fixation times were used, difficulties were encountered identifying cells deep within the sample, and cells appeared damaged.

SEM Measurements

SEM images of the freeze-dried dECMs were obtained with a JSM-IT800HL from JEOL (Tokyo, Japan), equipped with a backscatter electron detector (BSE detector, scintillator-photomultiplier detector design) and an elevated side-view position a detector (SE detector, Everhart–Thornley (E-T) detector design) using 3–5 kV, under high vacuum.

Statistical Analysis

Bar graphs display mean value ± SD. Unpaired Student’s t tests were conducted for single mean comparisons. Significance levels were assigned as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. These analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 software.
