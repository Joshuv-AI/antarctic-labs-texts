Dissolvable antimicrobial microneedles loaded with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived migrasomes for diabetes wound treatment
Authors: Renshengjie Zhao, Shichao Chen, Linsa Zhou, Jin Huang, Xuemin Ma, Tong Li, Shiting Li, Xiaorong Lan, Guangwen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn clinical settings, diabetic wounds present persistent challenges to spontaneous healing, primarily due to excessive inflammatory responses, impaired vascular perfusion, elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and increased susceptibility to infection. In this context, transplantation of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) has shown promising results in enhancing wound healing. Emerging evidence suggests that this therapeutic benefit is closely linked to the extracellular vesicles (EVs) secreted by BMSCs. Among the newly identified EV subtypes, migrasomes (Migs) have recently demonstrated significant roles in embryonic development, immune modulation, and tissue regeneration. Nevertheless, the specific biological functions and underlying molecular mechanisms of Migs under diabetic pathophysiological conditions remain largely unexplored. Previous studies have preliminarily revealed that BMSCs-Migs are capable of chemotactically recruiting ECs and BMSCs, as well as promoting anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization. However, achieving sustained release of these bioactive components at precise dermal depths to maximize their therapeutic effect remains a major obstacle. To address this issue, an innovative antimicrobial microneedle system (MNs@Mig) has been developed for transdermal, controlled delivery of BMSCs-Migs for diabetic wound therapy. This microneedle platform exhibits sufficient mechanical strength for effective skin penetration and enables prolonged release of therapeutic agents. The incorporation of Migs endows MNs@Mig with multifunctional properties tailored for the treatment of chronic diabetic wounds. By preventing bacterial colonization, recruiting various reparative cells, enhancing angiogenesis, reducing ROS accumulation, and modulating macrophage polarization, MNs@Mig facilitates accelerated, efficient, and high-quality wound repair. Introduction

Delayed healing of diabetic wounds has severe clinical consequences, including ulceration, disability, and even life-threatening complications [1–3]. In addition to complex pathological mechanisms such as impaired blood circulation, immunodeficiency, elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and persistent inflammation, the hyperglycemic microenvironment of chronic wounds favors bacterial colonization on the wound surface [4–5]. Conventional wound dressings remain limited by cumbersome application procedures, lack of antimicrobial activity, and their potential to cause secondary or repeated trauma to the wound bed [6]. Even advanced drug-loaded dressings are hindered by poor skin permeability, limited cellular uptake, and insufficient barrier function against bacterial invasion [6–8]. Thus, there remains an urgent demand for novel wound dressings with multifunctional properties to orchestrate and accelerate the diabetic wound healing process.

Microneedles (MNs) have been extensively applied for transdermal drug delivery due to their excellent mechanical properties [9–11]. They can painlessly penetrate the stratum corneum and deliver therapeutic agents to targeted skin depths, where the compounds are readily absorbed into the subdermal capillary network [12–13]. In chronic diabetic wounds, MNs also provide a mechanical strategy to disrupt bacterial biofilms, thereby overcoming their resistance to antimicrobial agents [14–15]. Among various MN platforms, hydrogel-forming microneedles (HFMNs) represent a transformative advancement in non-invasive therapeutics for skin disorders. Upon insertion, HFMNs rapidly absorb interstitial fluid and swell to form a hydrogel conduit, facilitating efficient drug diffusion into the dermal microcirculation [16]. Therefore, HFMNs exhibit substantial promise for the treatment of chronic diabetic wounds.

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) transplantation has demonstrated favorable outcomes in wound healing; however, its underlying therapeutic mechanisms remain incompletely understood [17–19]. Increasing evidence suggests that these therapeutic effects are primarily mediated by the paracrine action of extracellular vesicles (EVs) rather than direct cell engraftment or differentiation, positioning EVs as a more attractive and safer therapeutic modality [20–21]. Migrasomes (Migs), a newly identified subset of EVs generated during cell migration, function as mediators of intercellular communication [22]. Recent studies have demonstrated that Migs act as carriers of CXCL12, promoting stem cell recruitment and contributing to soft tissue regeneration [23–24]. Furthermore, Monocyte-Derived Migs are enriched with pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF-A, which facilitate capillary formation and monocyte chemotaxis [25]. However, the molecular composition and functional properties of cell-derived vesicles are intrinsically linked to their cellular origin. Specifically, migrasomes inherited from different cell types exhibit heterogeneous protein and RNA profiles [26]. Therefore, the cytokine-rich nature of BMSC-Migs and their underlying physiological functions warrant further investigation. Our previous study has preliminary demonstrated that the BMSC-Migs can serve as carriers for CXCL12 and CCL2, thereby promoting the chemotaxis of BMSCs, ECs, and macrophages [27]. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that migrasomes encapsulated within hydrogels exhibit superior efficacy in promoting cell migration compared to free migrasomes [28]. These properties suggest that the Migs may offer innovative strategies for soft tissue engineering.

In this study, a hydrogel MNs system composed of Hyaluronic acid Methacryloyl (HAMA) and Poly-L-lysine Methacryloyl (PLMA) was developed to achieve controlled release of Migs. The MNs@Mig system exhibited favourable mechanical integrity and sustained-release characteristics. The integration of Migs endowed MNs@Mig with multifunctional therapeutic potential for the treatment of chronic diabetic wounds. By preventing bacterial infection, enhancing migration of reparative cells, promoting angiogenesis, reducing ROS accumulation, and modulating macrophage polarization, MNs@Mig facilitated effective, rapid, and high-quality wound healing. Therefore, MNs@Mig emerged as a promising, painless, non-invasive approach for the management of chronic diabetic wounds.

Results and discussion

Isolation and characterization of BMSC-migrasomes

The migrasomes used in this study were isolated from rat BMSCs under strictly controlled conditions (passages 3–5, the same seeding density, culture duration, and isolation protocol as those used in our previous publication [27]). Of note, rigorous characterization of these migrasomes has already been performed in our prior study [27]; therefore, only a brief description is provided here. In brief, titanium (Ti) implants were anodised at 5 V to generate titania micropits/nanotubes (MNT5) structures with an average diameter of approximately 30 nm [29–30] (Figure. 1B). BMSCs were seeded at 60% confluence on the MNT5 surface to enhance Mig formation. Subsequently, Migs were isolated and purified from the MNT5 surface using an ultra-speed density gradient centrifugation technique, followed by structural characterisation and cargo analysis (Figure. 1A). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) revealed extensive aggregation of Migs around the cell bodies (Figure. 1C). In parallel, Migs were visualized using highly intelligent structured illumination microscopy (HIS-SIM) after transfection with a TSPAN4-mCherry plasmid. Consistent with the SEM findings, abundant Migs were observed on the MNT5 surface (Figure. 1D), and the quantification of Migs is presented in Figure. S1. Isolated Migs were further characterised following purification (Figure. 1A). SEM images showed spherical vesicles with diameters of approximately 750 nm (Figure. 1E). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the presence of membrane-bound vesicles connected to hollow retraction fibers (Figure. 1F), a pomegranate-like morphology previously recognized as a hallmark of Migs [31]. Additionally, typical migrasome structures associated with retraction fibers were visualized using HIS-SIM (Figure. 1G). ELISAs were employed to further analyze the Migs, revealing higher levels of CXCL12 and CCL2 in Migs than in cell bodies (Figure. 1H), which aligns with our prior findings [27]. Collectively, these results confirm the successful isolation and purification of Migs.

The preparation and characterization of MNs@Mig

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan and an essential component of the extracellular matrix, possessing excellent water retention and moisturising properties. HA participates in diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, morphogenesis, inflammation regulation, and wound healing [32–33]. HAMA is obtained by introducing methacrylate groups into HA chains to render them photocrosslinkable. HAMA-based biomaterials have been widely applied in biomedical fields, including the fabrication of high-strength and adhesive hydrogels, controlled drug delivery, microneedle development, wound dressings, and biosensors [34–36]. Poly-L-lysine (ε-PL) is an antimicrobial peptide (AMP) produced by Streptomyces bacteria, exhibiting broad-spectrum activity against various bacteria and fungi, including Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) [37]. Unlike conventional antibiotics or metal ions, ε-PL avoids drawbacks such as drug resistance and cytotoxicity, making it suitable for biological tissue engineering applications including antimicrobial wound dressings [38–39]. Methacrylation of ε-PL introduces carbon–carbon double bonds, yielding PLMA. Because of its facile fabrication and excellent biocompatibility, PLMA-based materials have been explored in antimicrobial coatings, wound care, and related applications [40–41].

Previous studies have demonstrated that hydrogels cross-linked using 5% HAMA + 4% PLMA (HP) possess multiple advantageous properties, including hemostatic capability, injectability, strong adhesion, biocompatibility, and antimicrobial activity [42]. However, the incorporation of PLMA increases the viscosity of HAMA solutions, which hampers the fabrication of sharp microneedle tips [43]. Therefore, HAMA-based hydrogel microneedle tips were employed for Migs delivery, whereas HP hydrogel was used for the microneedle substrate to provide robust edge sealing, hemostasis, and antimicrobial performance.

To investigate the feasibility of hydrogel-based soluble MNs as effective Migs delivery vectors, MNs@Mig patches were fabricated and subjected to systematic characterization (Figure. 2A). A Mig-containing HAMA precursor solution was introduced into microneedle cavities and photocrosslinked to form biodegradable needle tips. Subsequently, the HP formulation was filled into the microneedle base and polymerized using UV irradiation to produce composite microneedle patches. Each resulting MNs@Mig patch consisted of a circular array comprising 385 conical MNs with a height of 600 μm and a base diameter of 270 μm (Figure. 2B). Fluorescent imaging confirmed that the tips were uniformly arranged on the supporting substrate (Figure. 2C). SEM further revealed well-defined sharp microneedle tips with Migs attached to the needle surface (Figure. 2D). As illustrated in Fig. 2E, each microneedle exhibited a mechanical strength of approximately 0.22 N, sufficient to penetrate the skin barrier [44–45]. Furthermore, in vitro degradation experiments revealed that blank microneedles exhibit sustained and stable dissolution (Figure. S4).

To compare release kinetics, we evaluated protein release from free Migs and from Migs embedded in MNs@Mig using a BCA assay, with a 100 µg/mL Mig solution as an example. Each MNs@Mig patch contained approximately 70 µg of migrasomes (100 µg/mL × 700 µL per patch). Correspondingly, each patch contained approximately 1102.5 pg of CXCL12 and 638.4 pg of CCL2. Free Migs released nearly all protein cargo within 12 h, whereas Migs incorporated into MNs@Mig exhibited a slower, sustained release profile, reaching 79.2% at 48 h (Figure. 2F), confirming the controlled-release capability of this system.

The antibacterial activity of the HP hydrogel was subsequently examined against S. aureus and E.coil, two frequent pathogens in diabetic wound infections [46]. As shown in Figure. S2a-c, the HP hydrogel significantly inhibited bacterial proliferation compared with the control group. Agar diffusion assays further confirmed effective antimicrobial activity, as demonstrated by clear inhibition zones surrounding the HP hydrogel against both strains (Figure. S2d). Live/dead bacterial staining after 12 h revealed substantial bacterial killing, confirming the potent antibacterial capacity of the HP hydrogel substrate (Figure. S2e).

The compartmentalized design of our MNs@Mig system, featuring a PLMA-integrated backing layer and migrasome-loaded tips, was engineered to achieve synergistic wound management. This structure allows for the precise delivery of migrasomes into the deep dermis to initiate regenerative signalling, while the PLMA backing layer functions as a robust antimicrobial shield to eradicate superficial biofilms and prevent exogenous reinfection. Furthermore, the hydrophilic channels formed by the swollen HAMA tips facilitate the downward diffusion of antimicrobial components into the deeper wound bed. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that the efficacy of this diffusion-based antimicrobial strategy may exhibit depth dependence in the context of extremely deep-seated infections. Future iterations of this platform might incorporate active transport mechanisms or kinetically controlled release to further enhance antimicrobial penetration in full-thickness tissue defects.

Determining the effective concentration of Migs encapsulation

Migs are vesicular structures originating from migrating cells, arising at the intersections or termini of retraction fibers and gradually enlarging into micron-scale vesicles [47]. As a recently identified class of EVs, research on Migs remains limited. Current studies indicate that Migs derived from monocytes contain angiogenic factors and chemokines, including TGF-β3, VEGF-A, and CXCL12 [25]. Similar conclusions have been reported for Migs from other cell types: fibroblast-derived Migs accelerate wound healing through delivery of CXCL12, while adipose-derived stem cell Migs—also enriched in CXCL12—promote stem-cell recruitment and facilitate soft-tissue regeneration [23–24]. Although the biological functions of Migs have been preliminarily characterized, the optimal concentration required to achieve maximal therapeutic effects remains unclear. Our previous work demonstrated that Migs markedly enhanced BMSC migration in a dose-dependent manner, with the strongest chemotactic effect observed at 100 µg/mL [27]. However, considering the sustained-release characteristics of hydrogel MNs and potential differences in cellular responses, the effective concentration of encapsulated Migs required further evaluation. To determine this, MNs were loaded with four graded concentrations of Migs (0, 50, 100, 200 µg/mL) and co-cultured with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH-3T3) (Figure. 3A). Our previous findings demonstrated that Migs, owing to their plasma-membrane origin, can be efficiently internalised by multiple cell types, including BMSCs, HUVECs, and Raw264.7 macrophages [27, 48]; thus, internalization was not the focus of the present investigation. Transwell assays revealed that chemotaxis of both HUVECs and NIH-3T3 cells increased in a concentration-dependent manner but approached saturation at 100 µg/mL; higher doses produced only marginal additional effects (Figure. 3B-C). Similarly, in vitro angiogenesis assays demonstrated dose-dependent enhancement, with the angiogenic effect also plateauing near 100 µg/mL (Figure. 3D). To address concerns regarding potential cytotoxicity at higher concentrations, biocompatibility of Migs-loaded hydrogels was evaluated. CCK8 assays and live/dead staining confirmed that increasing Migs content did not impair cell viability, indicating favorable biosafety (Figure. 3E-F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that 100 µg/mL represents an optimal dose for functional enhancement without compromising biosafety; therefore, MNs@100-Mig was selected for subsequent experiments.

Migs@MN promotes cell chemotaxis and angiogenesis in vitro

Cell migration is a continuous and essential event throughout wound repair. Multiple cell types are involved in tissue regeneration, among which vascular ECs and fibroblasts are key contributors. Angiogenesis and vasculogenesis constitute indispensable components of wound healing, supporting immune cell recruitment, supplying oxygen and nutrients to the hypermetabolic regenerating tissue, and facilitating the removal of toxic metabolites and waste products [49]. Directed ECs migration is fundamental for vascular remodeling and the formation of new vasculature [50]. As another critical regulator of tissue regeneration, fibroblasts actively migrate to injury sites in response to chemokines and cytokines during the early healing stage and synthesize and remodel ECM proteins to preserve structural integrity [51]. In diabetic wounds, cellular migratory capacity is compromised due to hyperglycemia, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered wound microenvironment [52–53]. Thus, enhancing ECs and fibroblast migration at the early stage of diabetic wound healing is considered beneficial for tissue repair. Migs have been reported to transport CXCL12 and CCL2, thereby promoting the migration and recruitment of various cell types [24–25, 27]. Based on this evidence, it is hypothesized that Migs facilitate ECs and fibroblast migration under high-glucose (HG) conditions, a hypothesis subsequently investigated in this study. The migratory behaviors of NIH-3T3 and HUVECs were assessed. Transwell and scratch assays showed that coculture with MNs@Mig markedly enhanced the migratory capacity of both HUVECs and NIH-3T3 under high-glucose conditions. Furthermore, compared to free Migs, those incorporated into MNs exhibited superior effects owing to their sustained-release properties (Figure. 4A-D). In addition to CXCL12, Migs contain angiogenic factors such as VEGF-A and TGF-β [23]. VEGF-A acts predominantly through VEGFR2 and serves as a central regulator of angiogenesis in both physiological and pathological contexts [54]. Meanwhile, TGF-β signalling has been widely implicated in normal vascular development and homeostasis [55–57]. These observations suggest that Migs possess inherent pro-angiogenic potential. Accordingly, HUVECs were employed to further examine the angiogenic effects of Migs. Tube-formation assays demonstrated that coculture with MNs@Mig significantly enhanced the angiogenic capacity of HUVECs (Figure. 4E). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed pronounced upregulation of VEGF-A and CD31 protein expression in HUVECs (Figure. 4F). Free Migs also improved angiogenesis; however, due to the lack of sustained release, most Migs were not efficiently internalised and underwent degradation, resulting in inferior efficacy compared to MNs@Mig. Collectively, Migs promote the migration of ECs and fibroblasts and enhance the angiogenic capacity of ECs. Compared with free Migs, Migs loaded within hydrogel MNs achieve superior therapeutic outcomes due to their sustained-release characteristics.

Migs@MN modulates the macrophage-dominated immune microenvironment and ROS production

Diabetic wounds commonly display dysregulated and persistent pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization [5]. Under hyperglycaemic conditions, these macrophages become further activated and secrete elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, establishing a vicious cycle of sustained inflammatory polarization and chronic inflammation [58–60]. Thus, in addition to glycaemic control, developing therapeutic strategies targeting this persistent inflammatory phenotype is of particular importance. Tumour-derived Migs have been shown to promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization [61–63], although their mechanisms remain insufficiently defined. Our previous work indicated that BMSCs-derived Migs facilitate anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization [29]. To obtain more compelling evidence, a more detailed investigation was conducted. In an in vitro model of hyperglycaemia, both free Migs and MNs@Mig significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory macrophage-related genes (IL-1β, TNF-α, iNOS) and upregulated genes associated with anti-inflammatory macrophages (Arg-1, CD206, IL-10) following coculture (Figure. 5A). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed decreased expression of pro-inflammatory surface markers and increased expression of anti-inflammatory markers in both treatment groups (Figure. 5B). Flow cytometry further demonstrated a marked reduction in CD86+ cells and a significant increase in CD206+ cells in the MNs@Mig and Mig groups (Figure. 5C). Benefiting from the sustained-release properties of hydrogel MNs, MNs@Mig exhibited superior efficacy in promoting anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization compared with free Migs.

Hyperglycaemia induces elevated ROS levels in diabetic tissues, contributing to vascular complications. Due to metabolic shifts toward glycolysis, ROS production is further enhanced in diabetic macrophages [64]. On the one hand, ROS are indispensable for inducing and maintaining pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization by activating MAPK, STAT1, STAT6, and NF-κB signalling pathways [65–67], ultimately promoting pro-inflammatory gene expression and modulating macrophage differentiation via epigenetic (re)programming. On the other hand, excessive ROS accumulation or impaired detoxification induces oxidative stress, inhibits keratinocytes and ECs migration and proliferation, and represents a major cause of impaired healing in diabetic chronic wounds [68–70]. Therefore, antioxidant capacity is an important criterion in assessing wound dressings for diabetic wound management. Both free and encapsulated Migs reduced ROS production in macrophages under high-glucose conditions, with MNs@Mig demonstrating superior efficacy (Figure. 5D). Reduced ROS may partially explain the suppression of pro-inflammatory polarization by free Migs and MNs@Mig.

During the later stages of normal wound healing, macrophages secrete factors such as TGF-β1 and VEGF-A, which regulate keratinocyte, fibroblast, and ECs proliferation, differentiation, and migration, thereby promoting neovascularization, ECM remodelling, and wound closure [71]. However, in diabetic wounds, hyperglycaemia inhibits macrophage secretion of VEGF-A and TGF- β1, impairs ECs, myofibroblast, and keratinocyte migration, and consequently hinders vascularisation and re-epithelialisation, leading to delayed healing [72, 73]. To evaluate the regulatory role of MNs@Mig within the macrophage-dominated immune microenvironment, ELISA was conducted to quantify cytokine secretion. MNs@Mig markedly reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) while enhancing the production of pro-repair factors (VEGF-A, IL-10, TGF-β) (Figure. 5E). Subsequently, supernatants from macrophages treated with different conditions were used to culture HUVECs and NIH-3T3 cells. These supernatants significantly enhanced cell migration under high-glucose conditions (Figure. 5F), confirming that MNs@Mig effectively modulate the wound microenvironment.

Migs@MN regulates macrophage polarization by modulating the JAK2-STAT3 signalling pathway

To further elucidate the mechanisms by which MNs@Mig promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, transcriptome microarray and bioinformatics analyses were performed on macrophages following MNs@Mig treatment. PCA revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles between the HG + LPS and MNs@Mig groups, indicating substantial alterations in gene expression (Figure. 6A). Differential expression analysis identified 4079 DEGs (1980 downregulated and 2099 upregulated), as illustrated by the volcano plot (Figure. 6B). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that cell chemotaxis, regulation of inflammatory response, and macrophage migration were the top enriched terms in the biological process (BP) category, reflecting active chemotactic and inflammatory regulation (Figure. 6C). In the cellular component (CC) category, phagocytic vesicle, focal adhesion, and outer kinetochore were most enriched (Figure. 6C). In the molecular function (MF) category, chemokine receptor binding and cell adhesion molecule binding were enriched (Figure. 6C), suggesting active expression of chemotaxis- and adhesion-related genes. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of multiple inflammation-related pathways, including the NF-kappa B, JAK-STAT, MAPK, and TNF signaling pathways (Figure. 6C). Further gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicated that several pro-inflammatory signalling pathways were suppressed in macrophages after MNs@Mig treatment (Figure. 6D). Consistently, heatmap visualization of DEGs showed downregulation of pro-inflammatory macrophage-related genes and upregulation of anti-inflammatory macrophage-associated genes (Figure. 6E). CXCL12 has been reported to activate the JAK-STAT signaling pathway [74], while the JAK2-STAT3 axis plays a key role in promoting anti-inflammatory macrophage activation [75–77]. Our previous findings showed that the JAK2 phosphorylation inhibitor AG490 (MCE, USA) abolished the Migs-mediated enhancement of osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs [27]. Hence, we hypothesized that Migs promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization by activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. However, GSEA suggested an overall downregulation trend of the JAK-STAT pathway. Further inspection revealed that this downregulation mainly resulted from reduced expression of pro-inflammatory genes within the pathway (Figure. S3). To validate this hypothesis, the JAK2 phosphorylation inhibitor AG490 was used, followed by Western blot analysis. In the MNs@Mig group, the expression of phosphorylated JAK2 and STAT3, as well as Arg-1, was significantly upregulated, whereas CD86 expression was reduced (Figure. 6F). The addition of AG490 reversed these effects (Figure. 6F), a result further supported by immunofluorescence staining (Figure. 6G). These findings confirm that MNs@Mig promote anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization through activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signalling pathway.

Migs@MN promotes diabetic chronic wound healing in vivo

Based on the in vitro findings demonstrating the pro-migratory, pro-angiogenic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects of MNs@Mig, we further investigated their therapeutic potential for enhancing wound healing and preventing infection in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice. Wound closure was recorded on days 0, 7, 14, and 21 using digital photography (Figure. 7A). Representative wound area patterns were outlined at each time point, with different colors denoting unhealed regions. Quantitative analysis revealed that MNs@Mig treatment markedly accelerated wound contraction compared with free Migs or blank MNs (Figure. 7B). However, MNs@Mig did not significantly affect blood glucose levels (Figure. 7C).

Beyond healing speed, the quality of skin regeneration is critical for functional recovery. Scar formation during wound repair often compromises essential skin functions [78]. Therefore, histological analyses were performed on wound tissues collected at days 7, 14, and 21. H&E staining revealed that by day 7, MNs@Mig treatment markedly promoted re-epithelialisation and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration compared to the control group, whereas no significant improvement was observed in the Blank-MNs or Mig groups. By days 14 and 21, both the Blank-MNs and Mig groups exhibited accelerated epithelial regeneration and diminished inflammation—likely attributable to the intrinsic antibacterial property of the HP base and the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of Migs. Notably, the MNs@Mig group displayed more advanced regeneration, with well-organized skin appendages such as hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands evident in the newly formed tissue (Figure. 7D). Masson’s trichrome staining was further conducted to assess collagen deposition and structural organization. By day 7, newly formed collagen fibres were visible in the MNs@Mig group. On days 14 and 21, collagen deposition became more pronounced and well-aligned, suggesting that MNs@Mig promoted ordered ECM remodeling (Figure. 7D).

Macrophages play a pivotal role in normal wound healing and tissue regeneration, which proceed through four overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [79]. During the early inflammatory phase, monocytes are recruited to the wound site and differentiate into classically activated M1 macrophages with a pro-inflammatory phenotype [80]. As healing progresses, these pro-inflammatory macrophages transition toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, marking the shift from inflammation to tissue repair. However, in diabetic wounds, macrophage infiltration is excessive, and their ability to repolarize into the anti-inflammatory phenotype is impaired [81–83]. This phenotypic imbalance leads to sustained inflammation, thereby hindering the regenerative functions of ECs, keratinocytes, and fibroblasts [71]. To evaluate whether MNs@Mig can modulate macrophage phenotypes, we performed immunofluorescence staining on wound tissues. By day 7, the Blank-MNs group showed reduced pro-inflammatory macrophage infiltration, while both the Mig and MNs@Mig groups effectively promoted macrophage repolarization toward the anti-inflammatory phenotype (Figure. 7E). The same trend persisted at day 14 (Figure. 7E), demonstrating that MNs@Mig effectively mitigates pro-inflammatory macrophage infiltration and restores phenotypic balance in diabetic wounds.

Neovascularisation—the formation of new blood vessels—is a key event during wound healing, providing oxygen and nutrients essential for cellular proliferation and tissue regeneration [84]. During the inflammation phase, newly formed blood vessels gradually invade the wound bed [85]. To assess the angiogenic potential of MNs@Mig, we performed immunofluorescence staining for CD31 and α-SMA. On day 7, the MNs@Mig group displayed a higher density of CD31+ ECs, indicative of rapid endothelial activation and robust capillary sprouting. By day 14, an increased number of CD31+/α-SMA vessels were observed, suggesting that MNs@Mig promotes the maturation of capillaries into arterioles (Figure. 7E). These findings indicate that MNs@Mig enhances both angiogenesis and vascular maturation during the proliferative phase of wound healing. This effect may arise from Migs-mediated recruitment of fibroblasts and ECs, as well as the paracrine signalling of reprogrammed macrophages.

Finally, we analyzed the expression of angiogenic and inflammatory factors in mouse skin tissues. ln the Blank-MNs group, TNF-α expression was reduced, but VEGF-A and TGF-β levels remained unchanged, suggesting that blank MNs primarily provided an anti-infective effect. In contrast, MNs@Mig treatment markedly upregulated VEGF-A and TGF-β protein levels, promoting angiogenesis and tissue regeneration (Figure. 7F).

The significantly improved healing efficacy observed in the MNs@Mig group compared to the topically applied Free Mig group is primarily attributed to the sustained-release characteristics of the hydrogel microneedle platform. In the hostile microenvironment of a diabetic wound, characterized by high levels of proteases and constant exudate, free migrasomes are prone to rapid inactivation and clearance [86]. The HAMA-based microneedles act as a protective depot, transforming the burst release of migrasomes into a controlled, long-term delivery process. This sustained availability ensures continuous activation of the p-JAK2/p-STAT3 pathway in macrophages and persistent stimulation of endothelial cells and fibroblasts, ultimately leading to superior tissue regeneration and functional recovery.

Regarding the key functional components of BMSC-derived migrasomes, we believe their multi-lineage therapeutic effects stem from a synergistic ‘signaling hub’ composed of enriched chemokines and pro-regenerative growth factors. Specifically, our results confirm that migrasomes are enriched with key chemokines, such as CXCL12 and CCL2, which play pivotal roles in the coordinated recruitment of endothelial cells and the modulation of macrophage phenotypes toward an M2 anti-inflammatory state. Furthermore, the presence of potent angiogenic factors like VEGF-A within the migrasomes directly stimulates microvascular sprouting and maturation. By encapsulating these synergistic factors within a stable lipid bilayer, migrasomes effectively protect these signaling molecules from the proteolytic environment of diabetic wounds, ensuring their sustained bioactivity and synchronized activation of regenerative pathways, including angiogenesis and immune resolution.

Compared with exosomes, migrasomes possess distinct structural and functional advantages that make them particularly suitable for diabetic wound healing (Table S2). First, migrasomes (0.5–3 μm) are significantly larger than exosomes (30–150 nm), enabling them to carry a more diverse and complex cargo, including multiple chemokines and growth factors [87–89]. Second, migrasomes are enriched in specific proteins such as NDST1, EOGT, and PIGK, which are largely absent in exosomes, and mass spectrometry analyses indicate that migrasomes share only about 27% of their proteins with exosomes [90–91]. Third, migrasomes function as ‘signaling hubs’ by concentrating high densities of key chemokines (e.g., CXCL12 and CCL2), providing strong chemotactic gradients in the signal-diluted wound microenvironment. From a technical perspective, the larger size of migrasomes allows simpler isolation via low-speed differential centrifugation (20,000 × g) rather than the prolonged ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g) required for exosomes. Moreover, migrasomes can be visualized using standard confocal microscopy without the need for super-resolution techniques, facilitating in vivo tracking. These distinctive properties underscore the rationale for using migrasomes over conventional exosomes in our microneedle-based delivery system. In summary, the combination of larger cargo capacity, unique molecular signature, stronger chemotactic activity, easier isolation, and better traceability positions migrasomes as a superior EV subtype for tissue regeneration in chronic diabetic wounds.

Despite the promising therapeutic effects of the MNs@Mig system, several limitations of these animal experiments should be acknowledged. First, the diabetic wound model utilized only male mice, which limits the generalizability of our results across sexes. Given that estrogen plays a significant role in skin physiology and the rate of wound closure, future research including female cohorts is essential to fully understand the impact of sex as a biological variable on migrasome-mediated healing. Second, this study relied on rat-derived BMSCs for the treatment of a mouse model. Although the high molecular homology and the low immunogenicity of MSC-derivatives support the efficacy of this cross-species approach, future studies using human-derived BMSCs are warranted to better predict clinical outcomes [92–95]. Finally, while the STZ-induced diabetic model effectively mimics hyperglycemia, it may not fully capture the complexity of chronic, non-healing ulcers in human patients.

Methods and materials

Specimen preparation

Ti specimens were prepared following procedures described in previous studies. Briefly, pure Ti plates (10 × 10 × 1 mm, Northwest Institute for Non-ferrous Metal Research) were mechanically polished using SiC sandpaper (PT) to obtain a mirror-like surface. The PT surfaces were subsequently etched in 0.5 wt% hydrofluoric acid (HF) for 30 min and anodized in a mixed electrolyte containing 0.5 wt% HF and phosphoric acid. The anodization was performed at a constant voltage of 5 V for 1 h. All specimens were sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol followed by Co60 irradiation. The resulting surface morphologies were examined using SEM (JEOL JSM-6701 F, Japan).

Cell culture

Rat BMSCs (NCA83Ra01, Shanghai Jingfeng Biology and Technology Co. Ltd) were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, 12571063, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, PAN, Aidenbach, Germany) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Mouse embryonic fibroblasts NIH-3T3 (STCC20005P-1, Servicebio, China), murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 (TIB-71, ATCC, USA), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; BFN6021653, Shanghai Cell Bank, China) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Different groups were cultured in specific DMEM formulations: the control group used low-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 11885084, 5.5 mM D-Glucose, USA), whereas the experimental groups used high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 11995073, 25 mM D-Glucose, USA).

All cell cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 using Esco CelMate incubators (Esco, Singapore) to mimic physiological conditions.

Migs generation

Rat BMSCs (NCA83Ra01, Shanghai Jingfeng Biology and Technology Co. Ltd.) were seeded on Ti substrates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL in 24-well plates. After 3 days of culture, the cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series for standard SEM observation (JSM-6701 F, Japan). In a parallel experiment, live BMSCs were transfected with a TSPAN4-mCherry plasmid via lentiviral transduction according to the manufacturer’s protocol (K5910-00, Invitrogen, USA). After thorough rinsing with PBS, the cells were incubated in fresh culture medium. Live-cell imaging was performed using HIS-SIM (CSRbiotech, China) for continuous recording over 10 h. The acquired images were analyzed using ImageJ software to quantify the number of Migs, defined as small vesicular structures either isolated or connected to retraction fibers.

Migs isolation and cargo analysis

Isolation and purification of Migs from the MNT5 surface were performed by ultracentrifugation at 4 ℃ following previously described protocols. Briefly, 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, USA) was applied to detach all biological components from the MNT5 surface. The suspension was first centrifugated at 1000×g for 10 min to remove intact cells, followed by sequential centrifugations at 4000×g for 20 min and 20,000×g for 30 min to obtain crude Migs precipitates. The collected pellets were further purified by ultracentrifugation at 150,000×g for 4 h in extraction buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and the fraction containing purified Migs was collected for downstream analyses. The purified Migs were characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and TEM, including both ultrathin sectioning and direct visualization. After air-drying on glass coverslips, samples were examined using HIS-SIM and SEM, with or without WGA488 labeling. For cargo analysis, Migs were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and the resulting lysates were analyzed with CXCL12 (Elabscience, E-EL-R3027) and CCL2 (Elabscience, E-EL-R0633) ELISA kits.

Preparation of MNs@Mig

Hyaluronic acid methacryloyl (HAMA, EFL-HAMA-150 K) was synthesized by introducing methacryloyl groups onto the hyaluronic acid backbone, as provided by Engineering for Life (Jiangsu, China). Poly-L-lysine methacryloyl (PLMA, EFL-PLMA-001, EFL, China) was synthesized by grafting methacryloyl groups onto ε-poly-L-lysine using glycidyl methacrylate, thereby introducing reactive double bonds. Freeze-dried powders of HAMA and PLMA were dissolved at concentrations of 5% (w/v) and 4% (w/v), respectively, in a 0.25% (w/v) initiator solution containing lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl phosphinate, followed by stirring at room temperature for 1 h. For sterilization, the hydrogel precursor solutions were heated to 80 ℃ for 30 min and then rapidly cooled in an ice-water bath for 5 min; this cycle was repeated once. A mixture of 5% HAMA hydrogel and Migs was loaded into microneedle molds, degassed under vacuum to remove air bubbles, and dried in an oven at 30 ℃ for 12 h. This process was repeated once. When the hydrogel became viscous, a mixture of 5% HAMA and 4% PLMA hydrogels was added to completely fill the molds. The MNs were further dried for 12 h and subsequently photocured under 405 nm UV light for 10 s.

Morphology observation

Bright-field images and the overall morphology of the MNs were examined using a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Japan). Surface microstructures were characterized by SEM (JSM-6701 F, Japan). Fluorescent MNs, prepared by blending HAMA with red fluorescent gelatin methacryloyl (EFL, EFL-GM-RF-30, China), were visualized using a fluorescent microscope (Olympus, BX53, Japan).

Mechanical property

The mechanical strength of the MNP was assessed using a computer-controlled universal testing machine (CMT4503, China). The microneedle tips were positioned vertically against a rigid stainless-steel platform, and the loading sensor was initially placed 1 cm above the tips. The probe was lowered at a rate of 0.1 mm/s until contact was established, after which the displacement rate was adjusted to 0.01 mm/s. The measurement of force and displacement was recorded continuously as the probe advanced 1.5 mm, enabling evaluation of the compressive resistance and structural integrity of the MNs.

Migrasome release analysis

MNs@Mig and a control solution containing free Migs were placed in the upper chambers of Transwell inserts within 24-well plates, with 600 µL PBS added to the lower chambers as the dissolution medium. The plates were incubated at 37 ℃. At predetermined time intervals, 50 µ L of the dissolution medium was withdrawn from the lower chamber and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The cumulative release of Migs from MNs@Mig was quantified using a micro-BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). The release profile was expressed as the percentage of total Migs released over time.

Antimicrobial performance assay

The antibacterial activity of the HP hydrogel was evaluated using the spread-plate method, agar diffusion assay, and Live/Dead bacterial staining. A bacterial suspension with a concentration of approximately 1 × 106 CFU/mL was incubated with UV-cured HP hydrogel for 24 h. Following incubation, the suspension was diluted 104-fold, spread uniformly onto Lysogeny Broth (LB) agar plates, and incubated 37 ℃ for 24 h. The number of colonies formed on each plate was photographed and counted. For the agar diffusion assay, 100 µl of bacterial suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was evenly spread on LB agar plates. After natural air-drying, the plates were inverted and incubated overnight at 37 ℃. Subsequently, HP hydrogel samples were placed in 5 mm-diameter wells at the center of the agar plates. After incubation at 37 ℃ for 24 h, inhibition zones were photographed and analyzed. For Live/Dead staining, bacterial suspensions (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were cultured with HP hydrogel in 48-well plates for 24 h. Subsequently, propidium iodide (PI) and N, N-dimethylaniline N-oxide (DMAO) were added according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the Live/Dead staining kit (Beyotime, C2030S, China).

Transwell assay

Depending on the experimental groups, different MNs, free Migs, or supernatants derived from differently treated macrophages were placed in the lower chambers of 24-well Transwell inserts, and complete medium was also added to the lower chambers. HUVECs or NIH-3T3 cells (5 × 104) were resuspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chambers. After 24 h of incubation, the inserts were gently washed with PBS, and the non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were removed with a cotton swab. The membranes were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (MedChemExpress, HY-B0324A, China) for 5 min. Migrated cells were counted in five random microscopic fields (central, upper, lower, left, and right) per membrane, and the average number was calculated to quantify cell migration.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs were pretreated for 24 h in the lower chambers of Transwell inserts, with different MNs or free Migs placed in the upper chambers. Subsequently, the cells were seeded onto 24-well plates pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, CLS354277, USA) at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. After 6 h of incubation, phase-contrast images were captured using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). The total number of tubular structures and nodes was quantified using the Angiogenesis Analyser plugin in ImageJ software (v1.8.0, NIH, MD, USA).

CCK8 assay

The biocompatibility of the microneedle system was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, C0038, China). MNs loaded with varying concentrations of Migs were placed in the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell plates, while complete culture medium was added to the same chambers. HUVECs (2 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in the lower chambers. After incubation for 1, 3, and 5 days, CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 ℃. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan Go microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Live/Dead cell staining

A Live/Dead cell staining kit (UElandy, L6037L) was employed to evaluate the biocompatibility of the MNs. MNs loaded with different concentrations of Migs were placed in the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell plates, with complete medium added to the same chambers. HUVECs (2 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in the lower chambers. After incubation for 1, 3, and 5 days, cells were stained with 2.0 µM Calcein AM and 4.5 µM PI. The stained cells were observed and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan).

Scratching assay

HUVECs and NIH-3T3 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. Once the cells reached confluence, a 20 µL pipette tip was used to create linear scratches perpendicular to the plate surface. Images of the wounds were taken immediately after scratching to serve as the 0 h control. Blank MNs, free Migs, and Migs-loaded MNs were placed in the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell plates, while the control and HG groups received no additional treatment. After 24 h and 48 h of incubation with the upper chambers immersed in the culture medium, the scratch width was visualized and photographed under a microscope. The migration rate was calculated by measuring the change in wound width over time. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Immunocytofluorescence staining in vitro

Raw264.7 macrophages and HUVECs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Biosharp, USA) for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature (20 ℃), and blocked with 5% goat serum in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1.5 h at 37 ℃. Subsequently, Raw264.7 macrophages were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against iNOS (Huabio, HA722031, 1:100) and CD206 (Huabio, ET1702-04, 1:100), while HUVECs were incubated with primary antibodies against VEGF-A (Huabio, ET1604-28, 1:100) and CD31 (Huabio, ET1608-48, 1:100) under the same conditions. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 ℃ in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Fluorescence images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (BC43 SR, OXFORD, UK).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from RAW264.7 macrophages using the SteadyPure Rapid RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China). Reverse transcription was performed using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) to obtain complementary DNA (cDNA). RT-qPCR was then conducted with the SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS RT-qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method as described by Livak et al. [96]. Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and their sequences are listed in Table S1.

Flow cytometry

Treated RAW264.7 macrophages were collected and filtered to obtain single-cell suspensions. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated on ice for 30 min with fluorescein-conjugated antibodies against CD86 (BD Pharmingen, 553692) and CD206 (Elabscience, E-AB-F1135E). The cells were then washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed using a FACSMelody flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, FACSCanto Ⅱ). The fluorescence intensity of the target markers was quantified, and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA).

Cellular ROS assay

After various treatments, cells were collected and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶/mL. The suspensions were pre-incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of the ROS fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime, S0033S). The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark and then washed twice with PBS to remove excess probe. Finally, the cells were resuspended in pre-chilled PBS and immediately analysed using a FACSMelody flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, FACSCanto Ⅱ). ROS fluorescence intensity was quantified using FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs)

Cell culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation to remove cellular debris. Cytokine levels, including IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10, TGF-β, and VEGF-A, were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Elabscience; IL-6: E-EL-M0044, TNF-α: E-EL-M3063, IL-10: E-EL-M0046, TGF-β: E-EL-0162, VEGF-A: E-EL-M1292) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated according to standard curves.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted from RAW264.7 macrophages using TRIzol reagent (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity, library construction, sequencing, quality control, and alignment to the reference genome were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and subjected to functional annotation through Gene Ontology (GO, http://www.geneontology.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) analyses to elucidate enriched biological processes and signaling pathways. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was additionally conducted to identify key KEGG pathways involved in macrophage regulation. Data visualization, including principal component analysis (PCA), volcano plots, heatmaps, and pathway enrichment plots, was performed using R software (version 4.40).

Western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from RAW264.7 macrophages using a total protein extraction kit (Keygen Biotech, Jiangsu, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay Kit (Beyotime, Beijing, China). Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4℃, the protein lysates were separated on 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST) at room temperature for 2 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4℃ with primary antibodies targeting phospho-JAK2 (CST, 3776, 1:2000), JAK2 (Huabio, ET1607-35, 1:2000), phospho-STAT3 (CST, 9145, 1:5000), STAT3 (Huabio, ET1607-38, 1:2000), CD86 (Huabio, ET1606-50, 1:10000), Arg-1 (Huabio, HA723143, 1:10000), and GAPDH (Affinity, AF7021, 1:1000). After three washes in TBST (10 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG, Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:4000) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Affinity, West Virginia, USA) and imaged with an iBright CL1000 system (Thermo, MA, USA). Semi-quantitative analysis of band intensity was performed using ImageJ software (v1.8.0, NIH, MD, USA).

Diabetic wound model

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest Medical University (Approval No. SWMU20210414) and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Twenty male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 22–25 g) were obtained from the Animal Experimental Centre of Southwest Medical University and maintained in a Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) facility. To induce diabetes, mice received intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ, 100 mg/kg; Boaigang, B2001, China) seven days prior to wound modeling. Blood glucose levels were measured using a glucose meter (Vivachek, China), and mice with blood glucose ≥ 16.65 mM sustained for at least one month were considered diabetic. Diabetic mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal administration of 1% sodium pentobarbital (10 µL/g), and full-thickness excisional wounds (1.0 cm diameter) were created on the dorsal skin using a circular biopsy punch. Mice were provided free access to food and water and allowed unrestricted movement throughout the study. The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (5 mice per group): Control group, Blank-MNs group, Mig group, and MNs@Mig group. MNs@Mig were applied on days 0, 7, and 14, and secured with transparent dressings. The other groups received 100 µL PBS, Blank-MNs, or 100 µL of free Migs (100 µg/mL), respectively. Wound healing progression was documented by digital photography, and all animals were euthanized on day 21 for tissue collection. Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ software.

Histological, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence Staining in vivo

Wound tissues collected on days 7, 14, and 21 were fixed in 4% PFA (Biosharp, USA) overnight. Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), followed by xylene treatment for tissue clearing. The samples were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Sections were floated on warm water, mounted onto glass slides, and air-dried. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to standard H&E staining (Servicebio, G1005, China) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Solarbio, G1340, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Stained sections were examined using an optical microscopy (Olympus, Japan). For IF analysis, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. α-SMA (Abcam, ab5694, 1:200) and CD31 (Huabio, ET1608-48, 1:200 ) were used to label blood vessels. iNOS (Abcam, ab3523, 1:200) and CD206 (Huabio, HA722892, 1:200) were employed to identify pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophages, respectively. After primary antibody incubation, sections were treated with fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature and imaged using a fluorescence microscope. For IHC staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific for VEGF-A (Huabio, ER30607, 1:200), TGF-β (Huabio, HA721143, 1:200), and TNF-α (Huabio, ER65189, 1:200). The sections were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h, followed by visualization with 3,3’diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic solution.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Where ANOVA indicated significant differences among groups, post hoc comparisons were performed using the Student-Newman-Keuls q (SNK-q) test. For data that did not meet the assumption of a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Quantitative results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a dissolvable antimicrobial microneedle system (MNs@Mig) incorporating bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived migrasomes (BMSCs-Migs) for the treatment of diabetic chronic wounds. The MNs@Mig platform, composed of HAMA microneedle tips and a hydrogel-based HP substrate, exhibited sufficient mechanical strength for effective skin penetration and enabled the sustained release of Migs, thereby addressing the challenges associated with targeted and prolonged drug delivery in wound healing. In vitro experiments demonstrated that MNs@Mig effectively recruited reparative cells (HUVECs and NIH-3T3), promoted angiogenesis, reduced ROS levels, and facilitated macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory phenotype via activation of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway. In addition, the HP substrate provided broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against common wound pathogens, including S. aureus and E. coli. In vivo evaluation using STZ-induced diabetic mice further confirmed that MNs@Mig significantly accelerated wound contraction, enhanced re-epithelialization, promoted collagen deposition and neovascularization, and facilitated the regeneration of skin appendages such as hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Collectively, MNs@Mig represents a multi-targeted, non-invasive, and promising therapeutic strategy for promoting efficient healing of diabetic chronic wounds.
