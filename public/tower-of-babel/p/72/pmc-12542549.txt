Development and validation of the musculoskeletal health climate questionnaire
Authors: Kent Jacob Nielsen, Sarah Holm Junge Jensen, Dorte Raaby Andersen, Johan Hviid Andersen, David Høyrup Christiansen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo develop, evaluate and validate the musculoskeletal health climate questionnaire (MHCQ), a multidimensional questionnaire for measuring musculoskeletal health climate.DesignCross-sectional test–retest study including systematic scale development and psychometric validation.Setting and participantsThe questionnaire was developed following the best practice recommendations for scale development outlined by Boateng et al (2017), including item development, scale development and scale evaluation with input from experts, stakeholders and the target population. Validation was conducted among employees in three physically demanding occupations in Denmark (care workers, slaughterhouse workers and residential painters), where a total of 1420 participants were recruited through labour unions. Of these, 796 completed the retest survey 30 days later. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses (EFA and CFA, respectively), internal consistency (Cronbach’s α), test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC)) and SEM were used to assess the psychometric properties. Criterion validity was examined via associations with pain points, pain medication use and sickness absence. Construct validity was assessed using correlations with the prevent for work questionnaire (P4Wq).ResultsEFA and CFA supported a four-factor model (supervisor’s practices, workplace practices, worker involvement practices and workers’ pain practices) with good to excellent fit (comparative fit index, 0.96–0.99; root mean square error of approximation, 0.04–0.06). All scales showed high internal consistency (α=0.80–0.88) and excellent test–retest reliability (ICC=0.86–0.92). Associations with musculoskeletal outcomes supported criterion validity. Weak to moderate correlations with the P4Wq subscales (rho<0.60) indicated acceptable discriminant validity.ConclusionsThe MHCQ provides a validated, multidimensional tool to assess workplace climate related to musculoskeletal health. It can support workplace assessments and prevention efforts by capturing shared perceptions of leadership, support, involvement and pain-related norms. Further longitudinal research and the use of objective outcome data are needed to assess predictive validity and strengthen the instrument’s applicability across settings.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders, Surveys and Questionnaires, Workplace, Psychometrics

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a leading cause of poor physical health globally and represent the most common cause of work-related disability and sickness absence in Europe.1 2

Employees in physically demanding occupations are particularly at risk because of repetitive movements, heavy lifting, pushing or pulling loads and working in awkward postures.3,7 In addition to these biomechanical exposures, individual-level factors, such as somatisation tendencies,8 low mood9 and pessimistic beliefs about prognosis,10 have been associated with increased risk of MSDs.

However, interventions targeting individual-level psychological or behavioural factors have shown limited effectiveness in improving outcomes.11,19 This highlights the need to explore broader, organisational-level determinants of musculoskeletal health – not as a replacement for biomechanical models, but as a complementary perspective that accounts for the social and organisational context in which physical exposures occur. In line with this, recent systematic reviews have reinforced the importance of psychosocial conditions in the development of MSDs, highlighting factors such as low job control, high demands, low decision authority and lack of social support.20 21 However, these factors are typically measured at the individual level, focusing on a single worker’s perception of their environment. This measurement approach may fail to capture the shared norms and expectations that may shape behaviour and health collectively within a workgroup or organisation.

To address this limitation, one emerging approach is the concept of musculoskeletal health climate (MSK climate), introduced by our research group.22 We proposed that the limited success of conventional interventions may reflect a need for models that address shared perceptions, social norms and organisational practices related to musculoskeletal health. MSK climate is defined as the shared perceptions among workers concerning procedures, practices and behaviours in relation to musculoskeletal pain and prevention (eg, employees’ sense of organisational expectations and support concerning workplace musculoskeletal health management). It builds on broader organisational climate theory and is akin to safety climate, which captures shared beliefs about workplace safety policies, procedures and practices23 and organisational health climate that reflects organisational support for the physical and psychological well-being of employees.24 MSK climate places emphasis on how a workplace culture supports or undervalues musculoskeletal health and can be seen as a more targeted application of organisational climate theory, tailored to musculoskeletal health outcomes. This narrower focus allows for more precise assessment of how organisational factors influence pain experience, sickness absence and preventative actions in physically demanding work settings. In two previous studies, we found that two hypothesised dimensions of MSK climate – perceived management prioritisation of musculoskeletal health and workgroup pain acceptance – were associated with both pain outcomes and sickness absence among Danish care and slaughterhouse workers.22 25

A concept like MSK climate must be clearly defined and measured using valid and reliable instruments to have scientific and practical utility. While our prior work established the conceptual foundation, it did not include psychometric validation or a transparent description of the instrument development process. The present study addresses this gap by detailing the development and validation of a multidimensional questionnaire for measuring MSK climate in physically demanding occupations.

Method

This study employed a cross-sectional survey with a test–retest design to develop, evaluate reliability and validate a questionnaire assessing MSK climate in three physically demanding occupations, following the best practice framework by Boateng et al,26 comprising three phases: item development, scale development and scale evaluation.

Item development

Literature review

The first step involved identifying relevant domains and items to be included in the questionnaire. This was informed by two main sources. First, a comprehensive literature review on culture, pain and musculoskeletal health was conducted to identify existing instruments, relevant domains and potential subdimensions. Second, established climate scales from the safety domain were reviewed to identify commonly used dimensions. Based on these reviews, initial domains and sub-dimensions were defined.

Item formulation

Items for each subdimension were then developed using both deductive and inductive approaches. Relevant items were identified from existing instruments27,29 found in the literature and from safety climate scales30 and were either used directly or adapted to fit the current context. In addition, new items were generated based on the sub-dimension definitions, the research team’s prior knowledge of the target population and input from a stakeholder advisory group. This nine-person group included individuals with expertise in musculoskeletal health from labour unions, employers’ associations, relevant Danish Working Environment Sector Councils (BrancheFællesskaberne for Arbejdsmiljø), and researchers with experience in scale development. To ensure the items measured climate – defined as shared perceptions – they were deliberately phrased to refer to ‘our workplace’ or how ‘we’ experience organisational practices.

Content validity

Content validity of the developed items was assessed through direct feedback from the target population and expert evaluation. First, individual face-to-face interviews were conducted, where each item was evaluated by four representatives from the target population (a care worker, a painter, a slaughterhouse worker recruited through local labour unions and an occupational health professional, recruited through a regional public sector occupational health and safety office), focusing on clarity, relevance and the need for adjustments. Based on the participant feedback, the items were retained, adjusted or removed. Second, the remaining items were evaluated by the stakeholder advisory group. Each member was provided with a definition of the relevant subdomain for each item and subsequently rated the item’s relevance to the subdomain using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from ‘not relevant’ to ‘highly relevant’. The content validity index for each item (I-CVI), subdimension (S-CVI) and modified kappa (k*)31 were calculated and used to evaluate the relevance of the items and sub-dimensions. Items with I-CVI <0.65 and/or k* < 0.7 were excluded. Sub-dimensions with fewer than four remaining items and/or S-CVI <0.8 were re-evaluated and either removed or revised by regrouping items based on conceptual similarity to form new sub-dimensions containing at least four items.

Scale development

Pre-testing

The remaining items were included in a pre-test with the target group using individual cognitive interviews32 to evaluate and refine the items and instructions by exploring how the participants interpreted, understood and responded to the items. The interviews were conducted in three rounds with a total of six participants (two care workers, two painters and two slaughterhouse workers recruited through local labour unions). The questionnaire items and instructions were revised after each round based on the feedback.

Survey population and procedure

The questionnaire was distributed to slaughterhouse workers, residential painters and care workers.

The target sample size was determined to ensure robust psychometric testing in line with best practice recommendations.26 Larger samples reduce measurement error and yield more stable factor loadings, replicable factors and results that generalise to the true population structure, although no single respondent-to-item ratio is universally optimal. Because the final factor structure was unknown at study initiation, a large sample was required to provide stable estimates regardless of the number of retained factors. Additional considerations included securing sufficient numbers for retest analyses, where response rates were uncertain, and enabling subgroup comparisons across occupational groups. Rules of thumb often suggest at least 10 respondents per item (>570 for the initial 57-item version of the MHCQ), whereas ≥1000 respondents is considered excellent for scale development.26 To meet these criteria and ensure heterogeneity, we targeted 300–600 participants from each of the three physically demanding occupational groups.

Recruitment was conducted through relevant labour unions. Two unions invited their members to participate by sharing a generic link to the questionnaire via newsletters, Facebook posts or direct emails. The third union, representing slaughterhouse workers, provided the research team with the contact information of relevant members, who subsequently received personalised email invitations containing unique questionnaire links. All participants who completed the questionnaire were invited to retest 30 days later.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Study data were collected and managed using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools hosted at Aarhus University.33

A 5-point Likert rating scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ was used for the developed items with an added ‘do not know’ option to identify difficult items. The questionnaire further contained demographic questions (age, education and region of residence) and four outcome measures: sickness absence in the past 12 months (0, 1–5, 6–30 and >30 days) because of musculoskeletal pain and other illnesses, use of pain medication in the past 4 weeks (several times a day, once a day, 2–6 times a week, 1–4 times a month, less and never) and musculoskeletal pain experienced during the past 4 weeks (not at all, a little, quite a lot and a lot) for each of six anatomical regions (low back, neck, shoulder, elbow/wrist/hand and hip/knee). For the musculoskeletal pain measure, pain points were calculated as the total number of anatomical sites reported to be painful the last 4 weeks (≥a little). To assess discriminant validity, the 20-item prevent for work questionnaire (P4Wq)34 35 was included; it measures four constructs – job satisfaction, mental stress, physical stress and pain catastrophising – that are conceptually related but theoretically distinct from the constructs assessed by the developed scales, meaning that they address related phenomena but represent different underlying concepts.

Missing values were examined to assess their extent and patterns. The primary issue was partial completion of the questionnaire, with some participants dropping out before finishing. The drop-out rate ranged from 9% to 16% across the three occupational groups. No imputation or other methods were applied to address missing data. Partial responses were included in the analyses. Only participants who completed the full baseline questionnaire were invited to participate in the retest.

Problematic or redundant items were identified by examining inter-item correlations within each domain and assessing items for ceiling or floor effects and the proportion of ‘do not know’ responses, with a threshold of 15% set as the acceptable limit. Furthermore, the responses were inspected for careless responding using the long-string method.36 Responses with a long string >10 were deleted (n=129 at baseline and 65 at retest).

Statistical data analysis was performed using STATA 17.0 (StataCorp. College Station. TX) and R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for statistical computing, https://www.r-project.org).

Exploratory factor analyses

Before factor extraction, the baseline responses from the target population were randomly split into two subsamples, ensuring an equal distribution of the three occupational groups across both subsamples. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed on the first subsample using the Oblimin method of oblique rotation based on a polychoric correlation matrix to account for the ordinal nature of the item responses. Two different approaches were applied to the EFA. First, domain-specific EFAs were conducted, each including all subdimensions within the given domain. Second, a single global EFA was conducted across all domains and sub-dimensions. Factor extraction was based on eigenvalues>1, total variance explained, parallel analysis and the requirement of at least four items per factor. Items with low factor loadings (<0.4) or problematic cross-loadings (>0.3) were removed. The results of the domain-specific and global EFAs were compared, and the resulting factors were retained for the confirmatory factor analyses (CFA).

Scale evaluation

Confirmatory factor analyses

The identified factors were validated using an independent subsample by conducting CFA on the second subsample of baseline responses from the target population. CFA was performed separately for each retained factor. Model fit was evaluated using several commonly recommended fit indices: the χ² goodness-of-fit test, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% CI, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) and the comparative fit index (CFI). Model fit was considered acceptable based on conventional criteria: RMSEA ≤0.08 and TLI and CFI ≥ .95.37 38 To further assess the quality of the retained factors, local dependence (LD) was evaluated by inspecting the residual correlations between item pairs. Differential item functioning (DIF) was assessed using a non-parametric approach, wherein partial Spearman correlations were calculated between item scores and relevant grouping variables (age, sex and occupational group), adjusting for the total scale score. This approach allows for the identification of items functioning differently across subgroups while controlling for the underlying trait level. To correct for multiple tests, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate. Items showing substantial LD or DIF were considered threats to unidimensionality and measurement invariance, respectively, and were excluded from further analysis in an iterative process, with the model being re-evaluated after each item removal. Items showing minor DIF were further evaluated using a graded response model plot (GRM)39 to assess whether the DIF had any practical impact warranting item exclusion.

Internal and retest reliability

The internal reliability of the identified factors was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Test–retest reliability was assessed using intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) based on a one-way random-effects model using data from all participants who completed the questionnaire at both the baseline assessment and the 30-day retest. The SEMs was estimated as the SD of the score differences divided by √2. Mean differences were calculated to assess potential systematic bias between time points. Agreement between baseline and retest scores was further evaluated using Bland–Altman plots, in which the differences between scores were plotted against their mean, along with mean differences, 95% CIs and limits of agreement.

Criterion and construct validity

The final step was to assess the validity of the identified scales through analyses of criterion and construct validity. The evaluation of criterion validity focused on predictive validity, which was evaluated by calculating the Spearman rank-order correlations between the identified scales and the outcome measures assessed at baseline. Pain points during the past 4 weeks were the only outcome measured at both time points, allowing for additional prospective analysis by correlating baseline factor scores with the retest pain scores. To evaluate construct validity, we focused on discriminant validity. Based on our literature review during the item development phase, no existing instruments captured the same conceptual domains as the identified scales. As such, convergent validity could not be meaningfully assessed. Discriminant validity was assessed by examining the correlations between the identified scales and the four theoretically distinct subdimensions of the P4Wq. For all analyses, correlation coefficients and corresponding p-values were reported. Thresholds of rho <0.30 and rho <0.60 were used to indicate strong and acceptable levels of discriminant validity, respectively.

Results

Item development

The initial literature review did not identify any existing validated instruments that covered the concept of an MSK climate. However, based on the literature review and review of existing safety climate scales, a total of five initial domains (eg, ‘management commitment’ and ‘pain perception’) and 17 potential subdimensions (eg, ‘training’ and ‘perceived responsibility for the development of pain’) were defined.

The research team formulated items based on the sub-dimension definitions, drawing on existing scales and contextual knowledge, and incorporating input from the stakeholder advisory group. In total, 175 items (8–16 per subdimension) were developed and phrased to reflect shared perceptions of organisational practices (figure 1).

Content validity

Feedback from the target population led to multiple adjustments of items to improve clarity and the exclusion of 55 items based on perceived lack of relevance and/or comprehensibility. The expert evaluation of the remaining 120 items further led to the exclusion of 63 items based on I-CVI, S-CVI and modified k*. This entailed the re-evaluation and merging of domains and sub-dimensions to form three domains (‘management’s prioritisation and commitment to MSD prevention’, ‘perception and management of pain’ and ‘workplace focus on MSD prevention’) and 13 sub-dimensions (eg, ‘training and instruction’, ‘talking about pain’ and ‘compliance with procedures’). After removing items and recombining subdimensions, the scale-level content validity (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.91.

Scale development

Pre-testing

The cognitive interviews did not lead to a reduction in items or dimensions but resulted in linguistic and conceptual adjustments to improve clarity and comprehension. Thus, the remaining 57 items were included in the questionnaire, which was administered to the target population.

Sample characteristics

The sample characteristics from the survey are presented in table 1. A total of 1420 participants responded to the questionnaire, of whom 1237 (87%) provided complete responses. The highest proportion of complete responses was among care workers (91%), followed by residential painters (84%) and slaughterhouse workers (84%). A total of 796 participants (64% of complete responders) also completed the retest. The overall sample included 63.6% women and 36.4% men, with marked sex differences across occupational groups: 94.4% of care workers were women, whereas 77.1% of slaughterhouse workers and 44.9% of residential painters were men. The mean age ranged from 46 years among residential painters to 57 years among care workers. Residential painters reported the highest average number of pain points during the last 4 weeks (mean=4.68), whereas care workers reported the lowest (mean=3.95). Most participants (62%) had no musculoskeletal sick leave within the last 12 months. Use of pain medication was common, with 29% taking analgesics at least once per day and 22% using them 2–6 times per week. It was used most frequently among care workers and least among slaughterhouse workers.

The global EFA, conducted with all 57 items simultaneously, yielded a three-factor solution as the best fit. These three factors corresponded to the first three factors from the domain-specific EFA, although two were shorter because of item exclusions based on cross-loadings across domains. Seven items loaded onto a single factor within the management’s prioritisation and commitment to MSD prevention domain (only one of which was not retained in the domain-specific EFA). Scale evaluation

Confirmatory factor analyses

Four separate CFAs were conducted to evaluate one-factor models for each of the four constructed scales.

For the management’s prioritisation and commitment to MSD prevention factor, three items (1_1, 3_3 and 4_1) were excluded based on evidence of LD and DIF. One additional item (2_2) showed significant DIF by sex; however, its impact was considered negligible, as the GRM plot showed only minimal differences in expected total scores across sex groups. The remaining four-item model showed good fit: χ²(2) = 5.371, p=0.068, RMSEA=0.05, CFI=0.999 and TLI=0.998 (table 3). Based on the content of the items, the factor was labelled supervisor’s practices, reflecting the perceived actions taken by supervisors to prevent musculoskeletal issues.

Given the retained items’ thematic focus on employee participation in preventive initiatives, the factor was termed worker involvement practices.

Internal and retest reliability

The four scales demonstrated good test−retest reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.74 to 0.82 and no significant differences between the baseline and retest measurements. The SEM was low across all scales, indicating a high level of measurement precision (table 4). The examination of the Bland–Altman plots showed acceptable agreement, as approximately 5% of observations fell outside the limits, indicating that the difference between baseline and retest scores reflects random measurement error within acceptable bounds. Furthermore, Cronbach’s alpha for the four scales ranged from 0.80 to 0.88 in the full baseline sample.

Criterion validity

All four developed scales showed significant and theoretically expected small-to-moderate associations with the outcome measures. Correlations observed at baseline support the predictive validity of the scales. Notably, the correlations with pain points were slightly stronger at retest (rho=0.252–0.400) than those at baseline (rho=0.241–0.367), providing preliminary evidence of predictive validity over a 30-day time period. As expected, all four scales showed statistically significant correlations with sick leave because of musculoskeletal pain (rho=0.135–0.196), whereas correlations with sick leave due to other illnesses were weaker (rho = 0.039 to 0.068) and non-significant for all but supervisor’s practice (rho=−0.068, p=0.021). This pattern supports the specificity of the scales in capturing musculoskeletal-related outcomes. Workers’ pain practices demonstrated the strongest correlations with both pain points and pain medication use (rho=0.367, 0.400 and 0.296, respectively), supporting its behavioural relevance (table 5).

Construct validity

All four developed scales showed statistically significant weak-to-moderate associations with the P4Wq sub-dimensions, with all correlations remaining below the 0.60 threshold for acceptable discriminant validity (table 6). Worker involvement practices, workplace practices and supervisor’s practices were negatively correlated with P4Wq subscales, most strongly with job satisfaction (rho = –0.495 to –0.554) and, to a lesser extent, with mental stress, pain catastrophising and physical stress (rho = –0.241 to –0.434). The workers’ pain practices scale showed positive associations with all four sub-dimensions, with the strongest correlations found for pain catastrophising (rho=0.450) and physical stress (rho=0.439).

We examined item-level correlations for scales with scale-level associations of ≥0.45 (online supplemental table S1 and material A). These correlations were not driven by individual items with disproportionately high values but reflected systematic variation across multiple items. The strongest item-level overlap (ρ≥0.40) was observed for item 1 of the P4Wq job satisfaction scale (“Does your job give you the opportunity to improve your skills?”), which correlated with items across three MHCQ subscales. A correlation of ≥0.40 was also observed between item 7_4 of the MHCQ workers’ pain practices factor (“When we are in pain, we take painkillers and continue working”) and item 12 of the P4Wq Catastrophising scale (“I fear the pain might increase”).

Discussion

This study developed and validated the MHCQ, a multidimensional instrument assessing MSK climate through four distinct and reliable scales. Factor analyses and test–retest data supported the structural integrity and temporal stability of the scale. Theoretically expected small-to-moderate correlations with pain points, medication use and sickness absence supported criterion validity, whereas weak-to-moderate correlations with conceptually distinct but related constructs indicated acceptable discriminant validity. Together, these results support the MHCQ as a psychometrically robust tool for use across physically demanding occupations.

Although previous studies have consistently identified psychosocial and organisational factors as important to musculoskeletal health,20 21 40 few have operationalised these factors as measurable workplace climate constructs. Unlike most existing tools that assess individual exposures or coping styles, the MHCQ focuses on shared perceptions at the organisational level – reflecting how norms, expectations and practices shape musculoskeletal health at work. This study addresses that gap by introducing and validating a multidimensional instrument that captures climate-specific dimensions relevant to musculoskeletal pain and prevention.

The final version of the MHCQ, in both English and Danish, is available in the online supplemental material B.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The MHCQ was developed through a systematic, theory-based approach following guidelines established by Boateng and colleagues,26 incorporating literature review, expert input, stakeholder involvement and iterative feedback from the target population. This rigorous process ensured strong content validity and conceptual clarity. Subsequent robust psychometric testing confirmed the structural validity of the scale. The finding that all four scales correlated significantly with sick leave due to musculoskeletal pain, but not with sick leave due to other illnesses, supports the criterion validity and specificity of the MHCQ and indicates that the scales capture workplace factors specifically linked to musculoskeletal health, rather than general absenteeism or diffuse perceptions of the work environment. Another notable strength is the inclusion of three distinct occupational groups – care workers, slaughterhouse workers and residential painters – selected for their physically demanding work and representing diverse sex distributions. This sampling strategy enhanced the ecological validity of the scales and ensured variation in sex composition.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all data were self-reported, introducing the risk of common method bias, which may inflate associations between scales and outcomes and warrants cautious interpretation of the findings. The precise magnitude of common method bias is difficult to determine; however, a review suggested that 18%–32% of the variance can be explained by method factors.41 Although this may have inflated the strength of some of our observed correlations, it is unlikely to explain their direction. Furthermore, because different response formats were applied for predictors (Likert-type climate scales) and outcomes (categorical measures of sickness absence, pain medication use and pain sites), the magnitude of common method variance is likely to have been reduced.41 Consistent with this, the expected lack of, or weaker, associations with sickness absence because of other illnesses compared with absence due to musculoskeletal pain supports the presence of genuine relationships beyond method effects. However, future research must minimise method-related bias by incorporating objective or multi-source outcome measures, such as registry-based sickness absence data.

Second, criterion validity analyses showed small-to-moderate effect sizes in expected directions. Although stronger associations would have been preferable, the findings are acceptable given that the climate perspective is intended to complement, not replace, biomechanical and individual-level models. However, with the exception of pain points at retest, all outcome measures were collected concurrently with the MHCQ, limiting inferences about predictive validity. We strongly recommend that future studies apply longitudinal designs and incorporate objective outcome measures, such as registry-based sickness absence data, to establish true predictive validity.

Third, discriminant validity was generally acceptable; however, interscale correlations were mostly moderate, which preferably would have been <0.3. The strongest associations were observed with job satisfaction, where three scales correlated at or above 0.50. Although such correlations were not anticipated before item selection, they are explainable based on the thematic overlap introduced by the items retained in the final version of the MHCQ. Job satisfaction is generally linked to leadership quality, perceived support and involvement – all core components of those MHCQ dimensions. However, item-level analysis suggests that these overlaps do not indicate content redundancy. For example, P4Wq job satisfaction item 1 (opportunity to improve your skills) is conceptually distinct from musculoskeletal health practices, and its correlation with MHCQ subscales likely reflects broader evaluations of organisational climate, where opportunities for personal development may be perceived as indicative of supportive management and preventive practices. Similarly, the association of MHCQ item 7_4 (use of medication to continue working) with P4Wq item 12 (pain catastrophising) reflects related but distinct aspects of pain coping: a workplace norm versus an individual cognitive appraisal of pain as threatening. Therefore, we interpret these overlaps as indicators of conceptual relatedness rather than redundancy, consistent with acceptable discriminant validity. To improve on this, future validation studies should consider comparator scales with greater conceptual separation at the item level.

Fourth, although the worker involvement practices factor showed partial overlap with management-related constructs in the global EFA, we retained it on strong theoretical grounds. Worker involvement is a well-established principle in occupational health, particularly within participatory ergonomics,42 43 reflecting employees’ agency and active participation in preventive practices, which is conceptually distinct from managerial support. The observed cross-loadings may reflect the close interdependence between worker involvement and managerial practices in real-world settings, making the constructs less distinct empirically than in theory. At the item level, the scale also captures organisational practices for involvement that go beyond individual worker actions and emphasise joint practices between employees and management (eg, item 11_2: “When employees suggest ways to reduce physical strain, management seriously considers their proposals”). Such wording makes some overlap with management-related constructs understandable. Nevertheless, future validation studies should further examine the stability and independence of this factor across occupational groups and, if necessary, explore whether refined item wording could strengthen its discriminant validity.

Finally, although the inclusion of three distinct occupational groups strengthens the study’s ecological validity, generalisability to other sectors and cultural contexts remains to be tested.

Relation to previous research

Each of the four scales reflects a conceptually distinct domain supported by previous research. The supervisor’s practices scale builds directly on our earlier work with the ‘perceived management commitment’ construct,22 25 providing a focused, climate-level measure of supervisor practices. In contrast to more general measures of supervisor support, this scale captures visible, preventive leadership behaviours. A previous study showed that such leadership behaviours are associated with better pain outcomes,44 whereas variation in supervisory practices may result from unclear expectations and limited organisational support.45 This underscores the importance of measuring how preventive actions are experienced by employees. Consistent with this, managerial engagement has been identified as a key factor in the successful implementation of MSD prevention initiatives.46

The workplace practices scale captures shared perceptions of workplace support efforts from co-workers and others aimed at preventing musculoskeletal pain. This reflects findings from a large meta-analysis identifying social support at work – particularly from supervisors and co-workers – as a consistent protective factor for MSDs across body regions.21 By assessing these perceptions at the climate level, the scale extends beyond individual-level measures to capture the broader organisational context for MSK health.

The worker involvement practices scale captures shared perceptions of whether employees feel included in shaping the physical conditions and pain prevention strategies of their work. A meta-analysis identified low decision latitude and lack of participation as psychosocial risk factors for MSDs.20 Despite this, worker involvement is rarely measured explicitly in MSK prevention efforts. By addressing this gap, the present scale provides a novel climate-based measure of perceived inclusion and influence in shaping physically demanding work.

Finally, the workers’ pain practices scale captures shared workplace norms around enduring musculoskeletal pain while working. Although an individual-level study – typically conducted among people living with chronic pain – found that pain acceptance is associated with reduced disability and improved coping,47 our climate-based measure reflects the collective expectations of whether employees are expected to continue working despite pain. This distinction is crucial. In a previous study, we found that workgroup pain acceptance was associated with increased musculoskeletal-related sickness absence among care workers, but not among slaughterhouse workers22; thus, the effects of shared norms may vary by occupation. However, in the present study, the refined scale showed a consistent association between high pain acceptance and increased musculoskeletal-related sickness absence across all occupational groups. This may reflect the more precise operationalisation of pain acceptance as a climate construct, as well as the removal of items displaying DIF, ensuring that the scale functions equivalently across occupational groups. Thus, the observed association is less likely to reflect measurement bias and may more confidently be interpreted as a genuine relationship between shared pain norms and sickness absence. These findings point to a potential paradox: although acceptance may promote resilience at the individual level, at the group level, it may indicate a culture that discourages help-seeking, underreports strain and normalises chronic pain, which may negatively affect individual well-being. The scale offers a novel tool for identifying workplaces where such norms may undermine musculoskeletal health.

Implications

The MHCQ addresses a critical gap in musculoskeletal research: the lack of validated tools that assess how workplace climate, norms and shared perceptions shape the experience and management of musculoskeletal pain. Although previous research has documented the role of psychosocial and organisational factors in the development of MSDs, few studies have translated these insights into practical measurement tools. The MHCQ can support workplace assessments, guide the planning and evaluation of prevention efforts and enable organisational benchmarking across departments or sectors. Occupational health professionals may use it to assess readiness for MSD interventions, whereas researchers can apply it to investigate mediating mechanisms in longitudinal designs. Although further longitudinal and cross-sectoral validation is needed, the MHCQ holds promise as a flexible tool for both applied and academic purposes.

Practical application

Each scale is scored as the sum of its four items (range of 4–20, with individual item responses ranging from 1 to 5), where lower scores indicate areas requiring attention and higher scores reflect favourable conditions. Scale sum scores can be classified as low, average or high using the reference values provided in online supplemental table S2 (online supplemental material C). In practice, low scores can inform targeted interventions, such as participatory ergonomics initiatives, supervisor training, workflow redesign or pain management programmes, whereas higher scores can highlight areas of best practice. A detailed scoring guide and full item wording are provided in the online supplemental material C.

Conclusion

The MHCQ provides a validated, multidimensional tool to assess workplace climate related to musculoskeletal health. By capturing shared perceptions of leadership, support efforts, involvement and pain acceptance, it offers a novel approach to understanding organisational influences on musculoskeletal outcomes. The instrument can support future research, workplace assessments and evaluation of prevention efforts across physically demanding occupations.
