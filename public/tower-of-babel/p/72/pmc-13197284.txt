ABSTRACT
PurposeThe suitability of the white matter centrum semiovale (CS) as a pseudo-reference region for simplified reference tissue model 2 (SRTM2) with [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1 PET has not been evaluated in youth cohorts. We aimed to assess noninvasive quantification methods and determine the minimum scan time needed in a sample of youth with mental health challenges.MethodsThirty-five youth participants (16–25 years old) from the Toronto Adolescent & Youth cohort underwent 120-minute scans with arterial sampling. Binding potential (BPND) was estimated using one-tissue compartment model (1TCM) as a reference method. The SRTM2 was then applied, with k2′ parameter either set to population average (k2 from 1TCM) or estimated individually by initial coupled fitting in a two-step procedure. Standardized uptake value ratio − 1 (SUVR − 1, 60–90 min) was compared with 1TCM-derived BPND. Linear regression and percent bias analyses were performed. Time stability was assessed using several truncated scans (40–100 min).ResultsCS volume (1.7 ± 0.2 mL), VT (4.0 ± 0.6 mL/cm3) and k2 (0.031 ± 0.004 min− 1) were consistent with previously reported values. The population-based k2′ (0.031) and SUVR − 1 closely matched 1TCM results (R2 > 0.9, < 5% mean bias). The coupled-k2′ approach underestimated several regions (R2 > 0.9, > 10% bias in some regions). Minimum scan durations were 60 min for 1TCM, 90 min for fixed-k2′, and ≥ 100 min for coupled-k2′.ConclusionThe population-based k2′ and semiquantitative SUVR–1 provide accurate [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1 quantification in youth with diverse clinical symptoms, supporting their use as noninvasive methods, and allowing scan time reduction to 90 minutes. This will facilitate its clinical use and wider applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00259-026-07818-1.

Introduction

Synaptic pruning is a crucial developmental event from early life through adolescence and early adulthood, involving large-scale brain reorganization that strengthens efficient synapses and eliminates redundant ones [1]. Excessive pruning is hypothesized to contribute to neuropsychiatric conditions [2]. However, evidence is scarce, and mostly derived from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) works that interpret the observed trajectories of biomarkers measured in the brain across the lifespan as synaptogenesis and pruning phenomena [3]. Quantifying in vivo synaptic density has become a critical biomarker strategy in understanding the pathophysiology of neuropsychiatric conditions through PET molecular imaging [4]. The best available proxy is the SV2A protein ubiquitously expressed in presynaptic terminals [5]. [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1 (a.k.a. [18F]SDM-8) has emerged as a promising tool for SV2A quantification due to its longer half-life and strong binding affinity, offering practical advantages over 11C-labelled alternatives such as [¹¹C]UCB-J [6, 7].

Several clinical PET studies have explored SV2A binding and distribution in neurodegenerative and psychiatric conditions [8–13], but very few have tackled youth populations using semiquantitative uptake methods [14, 15] and to the best of our knowledge, none of those have used quantitative methods incorporating arterial sampling. In particular, early monitoring of synaptic changes during adolescence and early adulthood is crucial for advancing personalized intervention strategies [16] such as those informed by the Toronto Adolescence & Youth (TAY) cohort study, which aims to prospectively investigate mental health problems in a large population of youths aged 11–24 seeking care [17–19] using a multimodal approach that includes neuropsychiatric testing and neuroimaging with MRI and [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1 PET.

Accurate PET quantification of radiotracer uptake typically requires arterial blood sampling in the absence of a validated reference tissue model, posing challenges for clinical research. This is particularly pertinent in youth cohorts, where minimizing invasiveness is essential for feasibility and ethical acceptability. The deep white matter area of the centrum semiovale (CS) is the most suitable described reference region for SV2A PET tracers [20] with different approaches proposed to define it [21, 22]. While non-invasive quantification methods have been validated in mid- and late-life cohorts [23, 24], no data to date support the use of reference tissue approaches specifically in younger populations. Notably, age-related differences in tracer kinetics, such as those reported for [¹¹C]UCB-J [25], underscore the need to evaluate whether reference models validated in adults can be reliably generalized to youth.

White matter microstructural maturation —including myelination, increases in neurite density, and fibre organization processes— follows complex trajectories over the first two decades of life, with key changes occurring during adolescence [26–28], supporting the formation of long-range tracts. In clinical cohorts, studies of white matter integrity uncovered abnormalities using diffusion tensor imaging, and reported fractional anisotropy reductions linked to major depressive disorders [29] and cannabis use [30] that could impact the use of the CS for noninvasive methods. Furthermore, pharmacological and pathological influences on SV2A binding challenge the use of the CS as a suitable reference region. For instance, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI, escitalopram) may modulate synaptic density in healthy individuals [31]. Moreover, in a small sample, Holmes et al. [8] found significantly lower SV2A binding in the CS and several ROIs of medication-free major depression disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder patients, particularly in those with severe symptomatology. This underscores the importance of measuring CS kinetics in clinical cohorts, alongside aging effects.

The present study aimed to validate a non-invasive quantification strategy for [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1, using the simplified reference tissue model 2 (SRTM2), in a subsample of the TAY cohort. Two SRTM2 approaches—using a population-based k2′ and a coupled k2′ configuration (defined in the Methods section, below) — to determine which method provides the most accurate and reliable quantification were evaluated against the binding potential calculated from an established one-tissue compartment model (1TCM) requiring arterial sampling [6]. Time stability was also assessed in the adolescent and youth cohort to determine the minimum scan time for these methods. Finally, given the growing interest in using [18F]SynVesT-1 in clinical studies necessitating shorter acquisition times [14, 15, 32], we also evaluated the agreement and bias of a semiquantitative method, the standardized uptake value ratio minus 1 (SUVR − 1), using a 60–90-minute window as previously proposed [6], to provide additional context in informing methodological choices in future population cohorts.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited through the TAY cohort study (https://www.taycohort.ca/), an ongoing prospective longitudinal study aiming to enroll 1,500 youth aged 11–24 years old seeking mental health and/or substance use treatment at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH), in Toronto, Canada [17–19]. The study was approved by the CAMH Research Ethics Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. The capacity to provide informed consent was assessed with the MacArthur Competence Assessment [33]. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in the Methods section of the Supplementary Information (SI).

[18F]SynVesT-1 image acquisition, reconstruction, and arterial blood sampling

[18F]SynVesT-1 radioligand was synthesized [34] including some minor adjustments as previously described [23, 35]. The acquisition details of PET and MRI data are described in the SI, Methods section. Briefly, PET dynamic emission data were collected for 120 min on a GE Discovery 5-ring PET-computed tomography scanner. Arterial blood was sampled automatically from the radial artery for the first 22 min, and manual blood samples were also drawn at − 5, 2.5, 7, 12, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 110 min from the injection time. A T1-weighted MRI was obtained for each subject for anatomical reference (GE Discovery MR750 3T).

Image processing

Details of image preprocessing can be found in SI, Methods section. Interframe motion-correction, co-registration, tissue segmentation, and normalization were performed with PMOD version 4.2 (Zurich, Switzerland). The Hammers atlas [36] was used with masking defined by the Maximum Probability Atlas method, to define 12 regions of interest (ROI): the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes, anterior and posterior cingulate, the insula, hippocampus, amygdala, basal ganglia, thalamus, and gray matter cerebellum. We used the CS ROI provided by Rossano et al. [20] to define the reference region that defined a 2 mL in template space, and then we created an individualized CS mask in each participant’s PET space.

Blood analysis and arterial input function (AIF)

Blood data processing details can be found elsewhere [35]. Briefly, the blood-to-plasma ratio was obtained by centrifuging a portion of each discrete arterial sample and measuring the whole blood and plasma activity concentration in a gamma counter (Wizard 2480, Revvity). Metabolites (parent fraction) for each sample time point were obtained via HPLC [37, 38], on a customized Advion system. Whole-blood gamma counter measurements were used to scale the automatic blood sampling system (ABSS, Comecer PBS-101) measurement and convert to activity concentration in kBq/mL.

The metabolite-corrected arterial input function was obtained by incorporating the blood-to-plasma ratio and parent fraction measurements using an in-house software, AIF Processing Tool [39], followed by corrections for delay and dispersion (10s) [40]. The radiotracer’s plasma free fraction (ƒₚ) was determined via ultrafiltration [41] and measured 3 times for each subject.

Kinetic modelling and data analysis

Time-activity curves (TACs) for the 12 ROIs were input to the PMOD PKIN module to perform kinetic modelling using 1TCM and SRTM2, using CS as the reference for the latter. The 1TCM was used as a benchmark method [6] from which total distribution volume (VT) measurements were obtained. The 1TCM-derived non-displaceable binding potential (BPND) was then calculated as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:B{P}_{\mathrm{N}\mathrm{D}}=\left({V}_{\mathrm{T}}^{ROI}-{V}_{\mathrm{T}}^{CS}\right)/{V}_{\mathrm{T}}^{CS}$$\end{document}. The SRTM2 was applied using two approaches following previous analytical approaches validated for [18F]SynVesT-1 [24]: (1) by fixing k2′ to the population’s average derived from the 1TCM (i.e., fixed-k2′); or (2) using a subject-specific value estimated with the coupled fitting of the 12 ROI TACs to determine k2′ in a 2-step procedure (i.e., coupled-k2′). For the latter strategy, the k2′ determined by means of a coupled fit across ROI TACs (excluding the CS) is fixed, and fitting is repeated across target regions to estimate BPND. This approach can be advantageous when there is no reference population for comparison or AIF for validation.

To assess the agreement between the 1TCM and the SRTM2 approaches, we used correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient [r]) and linear regression analyses, and percent bias (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:100\:\left(B{P}_{\mathrm{N}\mathrm{D}}^{SRTM2}-B{P}_{\mathrm{N}\mathrm{D}}^{1TCM}\right)/B{P}_{\mathrm{N}\mathrm{D}}^{1TCM}$$\end{document}). We also used multiple regression analysis to assess the effect of age and sex on the CS VT, k2 and volume.

As an additional analysis, given the relevance of semiquantitative methods in clinical research, and with the objective of improving enrollment in young cohorts [14, 15], we also computed the SUVR − 1 values from the time activity curves with a 60–90 min window using the CS as reference region [6].

Finally, we performed supplementary analysis with 2TCM to assess the relationship between the ROIs’ VT in the second modelled tissue compartment (ascribed to VND) and the CS VT to provide further investigation into the validation of non-invasive methods for [18F]SynVesT-1. Details regarding the 2TCM analysis can be found in the SI, Methods section.

Time stability assessment

1TCM and SRTM2 using the fixed-k2′ and the subject-specific coupled-k2′ were fitted to the 120-minute acquisition and with TACs truncated to acquisition times (tacq) of 40, 60, 80, 90 and 100 min to determine the required minimum scan time for the reference tissue approach. Thus, we obtained an acquisition-duration specific k2′ value for each subject and the minimum acceptable value of tacq when estimating BPND with the coupled-fit approach. Agreement (by means of coefficient of determination R2, and Pearson’s r correlation), mean absolute difference, percent bias, and parameter identifiability (percent standard error, [%SE]) in BPND values relative to the full 120-minute acquisition were calculated.

Regional estimates from the 2TCM can also be found in the Supplementary Tables 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1–3 in SI.

Non-invasive methods

The mean CS k2 value from 1TCM was determined for the full sample (k2 = 0.031 min− 1), and this value was then used as the fixed population-based k2′ for SRTM2 analysis (Table 2). BPND values obtained with the fixed-k2′ method were compared to 1TCM estimates resulting in excellent agreement (Fig. 2A, r = 0.996, p < 0.001; y = 0.95x + 0.00; R2 = 0.991) and parameter stability across participants and regions (BPND %SE = 1.5% ± 0.3%, range = 0.9% to 2.8%). Mean percent bias was < 5% across regions for all subjects, with values within < ± 10%, except the amygdala for 2 participants (4.9% ± 0.03%, range = − 11% to 2%).

For the coupled-k2′ approach, a higher efflux rate value was obtained, k2′ = 0.041 ± 0.01 min− 1. Correlation (r = 0.986, p < 0.001) and linear regression (y = 0.92x + 0.06; R2 = 0.973) analyses, as well as %SE (1.3% ± 0.3%, range = 0.8% to 2.7%) were also excellent, although with slightly worse agreement than the fixed-k2′ approach (Fig. 2B). Bias across regions was higher than for the fixed-k2′ method (see detailed description in SI, Results section). In the SI Supplementary Results, we also explored the influence of depression diagnosis, psychoactive medication and cannabis use on BPND bias and CS parameters.

Agreement and bias between the SUVR − 1 calculated with a 60–90-minute window and 1TCM-derived BPND estimates were also excellent but slightly lower across regions and participants (Fig. 2C, r = 0.965, p < 0.001; y = 1.00x + 0.08; R2 = 0.931). Percent bias results can be found in SI, Results section.

Time stability analysis

All truncated times were compared to the full 120-minute scan acquisition, except for the participant who was removed at 118 min, in which case the 115-minute mark was used. Figure 3 shows the percent bias in BPND estimates at each tacq relative to the 120 min. Table 3 presents the results summary for the time stability analysis, including microparameter %SE, absolute mean difference, percent bias, correlation and linear regression analyses with respect to the results using the entire acquisition.

Linear regression and correlational analyses showed excellent agreement at tacq 80, 90, and 100 min for all three methods. Truncated tacq at 40 and 60 min only showed good agreement for 1TCM-derived VT and the SRTM2 fixed-k2′ approach. As expected, the percent bias and the mean absolute error increased as the acquisition time decreased for all three methods. Detailed information can be found in SI, Supplementary Results.

Discussion

Noninvasive quantification of SV2A density with [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1 using a population-based fixed-k2′ of 0.031 min− 1 for SRTM2 yielded excellent agreement with the 1TCM in a cohort of 35 adolescents and young adults with diverse psychiatric diagnoses and varied exposure to psychoactive medication and substances. Notably, the fixed-k2′ value was consistent with that reported in an independent sample of adults from a study conducted at a different centre [24]. Additionally, assessment of the time stability of SRTM2 BPND estimates with fixed-k2′ demonstrated that scan duration could be reduced to 90 min, maintaining excellent agreement (R2 > 0.99) and minimal bias (< 2%) compared to the full 120-minute acquisition. Shorter scan protocol acquisitions (60 min) could still achieve good agreement (R2 > 0.95) with acceptable bias (< 5%) with the population-based approach.

Validation studies of simplified quantification methods in specific clinical populations are essential, particularly when reference region assumptions may be challenged. For [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1, the selection and segmentation of the CS is particularly relevant, given its low but non-negligible specific binding and higher susceptibility to noise. Accordingly, and for consistency with previously published studies, we chose the population average segmentation performed by Rossano et al. [20] that optimized the CS to minimize spill-in from gray matter regions. Selecting an optimal level of erosion is crucial because excessive erosion would lead to greater uncertainty in the CS measurements, which would propagate into the reference-based measurements [42]. The presence of specific binding in the CS may negatively bias BPND estimates [6], where it was found to be around 8% of the specific binding in gray matter regions for [11C]UCB-J from the blocking study [20], hence its consistency across subjects becomes crucial, especially when the population-based clearance rate is fixed. Our study achieved excellent agreement and minimal bias for the fixed-k2′ in a clinically heterogeneous cohort.

Previous independent studies have also established white matter CS as a valid reference region in first-episode psychosis [21, 43], autistic adults [44], and cannabis-using patients [11, 21] reporting no statistical differences in CS uptake. Only one study using [11C]UCB-J [8] reported CS VT differences in middle-aged participants with major depressive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder (n = 12), all medication-free for ≥ 4 months, with an average illness duration of 21.5 years. These findings may reflect age-related white matter changes linked to long-term illness/drug effects. In our TAY cohort, the most prevalent diagnosis was depressive disorder, with half of the sample also meeting criteria for trauma-related disorders. Unlike the prior study, our sample included only adolescents and youth with recent diagnoses and lacked an age-matched healthy control group. Importantly, no age effect was observed on CS VT, k2 or volume, and only the CS volume showed a sex effect. Future studies should further examine white matter changes across the lifespan in depression to disentangle the roles of disease, medication, and aging. Notably, elderly patients with no antidepressant exposure still exhibit more white matter hyperintensities than age-matched controls, suggesting depression itself contributes to white matter pathology [45].

Furthermore, additional investigation of VND and VS obtained from the 2TCM confirmed that CS VS was very low (0.8 ± 0.6 mL/cm3), as expected for a reference region. On the other hand, although VND was similar across the 12 ROIs, the CS VT was 22% higher than the ROIs’ VND. Lower gray matter VND with respect to CS VT has previously been reported in [11C]UCB-J [20] and [18F]SynVesT-1 [6] tracers from blocking studies resulting in BPND underestimation; however, the discrepancy was consistent across ROIs, implying that specific binding in CS would have no contribution to group differences in BPND. Overall, our findings support the continued use of a fixed-k2′ SRTM2 strategy with the CS as reference region in neuropsychiatric research, as the assumption of uniform non-displaceable kinetics in the CS remains sufficiently valid.

The coupled fitting routine for estimating individual-level k2′ was found to be less suitable than the fixed-k2′ approach. This is consistent with Naganawa et al. [24], where SRTM with parameter coupling yielded unstable BPND estimates for [¹⁸F]SynVesT-1 and [¹¹C]UCB-J. Improving this method may depend on better ROI selection [24, 46], as it is a suitable approach for determining k2′ in the absence of arterial blood sampling. Clustering algorithms could refine reference region definition at the individual-level [22] when the assumption of uniform non-displaceable binding in the CS may be compromised or to avoid partial volume effects. Similarly, machine learning or deep learning techniques, such as convolutional neural networks for 3D images, could help inform ROI selection for an improved coupled fitting routine. In our study, the coupled-k2′ approach displayed high percent bias in ROIs such as the amygdala or the hippocampus, which could limit k2′ parameter estimation at the individual level due to the presence of noise, and suggesting that these ROIs should be excluded from the ROIs used for coupled-k2′ determination.

A second aim was to determine the minimum scan time required for non-invasive quantification methods in the TAY cohort. Time stability analysis revealed that 60 min were sufficient to obtain stable VT estimates, consistent with prior studies [6, 23]. For SRTM2 with fixed-k2′, a 60-minute acquisition would achieve decent estimates (mean bias of 2.6%), but yielded BPND %SE values > 5%. At 80 min, BPND estimates for the population-based method were similarly good as those reported when we assessed a Parkinson’s disease patients’ sample at the same PET centre [23], but better agreement was achieved at 90 min. For the coupled-fit strategy, a good agreement with minimal bias and mean absolute error was not achieved until 100 min. In summary, arterial methods require at least 60 min, for SRTM2 with fixed-k2′ 90 min are recommended, and the coupled-fit approach requires 100 min to achieve comparable accuracy.

Finally, the use of SUVR − 1 (60–90-minutes window) also produced excellent agreement with respect to the 1TCM-derived BPND estimates as previously reported [6]. It yielded a systematic small negative bias similar to that observed in the population-based approach for SRTM2. In summary, the SUVR − 1 semiquantitative method would be suitable to use in clinical research studies due to its simplicity and feasibility, especially when having short scan durations [47]; however, since bias could have an impact on sensitivity and specificity when small group differences are observed, particularly for smaller ROIs such as the amygdala, SRTM2 with fixed-k2′ using CS as reference is preferred.

As limitations, it is worth highlighting that while it is an established kinetics quantification method, additional noise could have been introduced by subject-specific AIF measurements, some variability across participants was observed in the blood-to-plasma ratio (Fig. 1). We studied 35 TAY participants with available arterial blood sampling but we lacked a group of healthy controls for comparison purposes. Nonetheless, the obtained population-based k2′ was the same (0.031 min− 1) obtained from a smaller sample size and different age range [24], thus providing further support into fixing this k2′ for SRTM2 in [18F]SynVesT-1 across population ages, diagnoses and psychoactive medications.

Conclusion

In summary, population-based k2′ (0.031 min− 1) SRTM2 with the CS as reference region provides BPND estimates closest to estimates using invasive arterial VT, supporting its use for [18F]SynVesT-1 PET in youth. Acquisition protocol analyzed with this reference tissue model method could be as short as 90 min for this cohort without compromising data accuracy. Future work will explore deep white matter segmentation as a means to further refine the reference region and limit potential partial volume effects related to white matter integrity differences in clinical populations. Whenever there is a new study population, the validity of the reference region needs to be investigated, and here we have shown it in a cohort of treatment-seeking adolescents and young adults. Non-invasive quantification methods will help improve patients’ comfort and increase the generalizability of SV2A quantification in clinical research, and improve recruitment and retention for longitudinal studies.
