Conservative management of postoperative bronchopleural fistula in elderly lung cancer patient: a case report with literature review
Authors: Kun Deng, Ke He, Long Wang, Yong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchopleural fistula (BPF) after thoracic surgery is a rare but highly lethal complication due to the tricky nature of its treatment.Case presentationWe reported a case of BPF after pulmonary surgery and a satisfactory outcome with conservative measures such as continuous closed-chest drainage, strong nutritional support, and appropriate antibiotics.ConclusionsIn highly selected patients with stable hemodynamics, adequate drainage, and controlled infection, conservative management may be considered when surgical or endoscopic options are contraindicated or declined.Keywords: Bronchopleural fistula, Thoracic surgery, Conservative treatment

Introduction

Bronchopleural fistula (BPF) is an abnormal channel formed between the bronchial and pleural cavities that often occurs after lung resection and can lead to serious consequences despite its rarity. BPF triggers the exchange of air and secretions between the residual lungs and the chest cavity, leading to symptoms such as coughing, chest tightness and shortness of breath. If not treated in time, it can also lead to serious consequences such as pneumonia, pyothorax and even tension pneumothorax [1]. According to statistics, the frequency of BPF after lobectomies is 0.4% to 4.5%, and the mortality rate can reach up to 71% [2–4]. The main clinical symptoms of BPF include fever, chills, septic cough, chest tightness and dyspnea. Once left untreated, BPF can lead to recurrent lung infections, sepsis, respiratory failure, and even death. Therefore, treatment for BPF is critically important.

Presently, there is no uniform treatment standard for BPF, and more individualized treatment strategies are developed for specific cases [5]. Although aggressively driven early surgical intervention remains the primary approach [6], it imposes substantial trauma and often necessitates staged procedures, thereby creating a significant burden for patients. Endoscopic treatment may be considered for critically sick patients who are not candidates for surgery, but this approach carries a considerable risk of recurrence [7]. Conservative treatment is also an option, but the lower cure rate often makes surgeons very cautious. Previous studies have shown that, for the treatment of bronchopleural fistulas, a combination of conservative and endoscopic therapies results in a slightly shorter average recovery time than conservative therapy alone, although there is no significant difference [8]. In this context, we had the privilege of contacting and curing a patient who developed a BPF after lung cancer resection through conservative treatment, emphasizing the efficacy of a multidisciplinary protocol. This report aims to provide clinicians with an alternative therapeutic perspective for high-risk populations.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old male patient, with a height of 170 cm and body weight of 65 kg, 20 years of experience as a stonemason, and a history of long-term smoking combined with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), underwent minimally invasive right lower lobectomy. Later identified as Moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma of the right lower lobe (pathological stage pT2N0M0, Stage IB according to the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, 8th Edition). No significant air leakage was observed from the postoperative chest tube on the affected side. The right chest tube was removed on postoperative day 7 with stable vital signs. The patient was able to perform routine ambulation without noticeable chest tightness or shortness of breath.

On postoperative day 9, the patient suddenly developed fever accompanied by chest tightness and dyspnea, without chills, with a peak temperature of 38.7°C. Bedside chest ultrasound revealed moderate right pleural effusion with a maximum depth of approximately 8 cm. We immediately performed closed thoracic drainage, placing a chest tube and obtaining pleural fluid and sputum samples for bacterial culture and identification. Antibiotic therapy with meropenem and vancomycin was initiated. Further chest CT revealed a pyothorax cavity (10 cm × 8 cm × 8 cm) containing pleural fluid and the drainage tube within the right lower thoracic cavity (Fig. 1A1-A2). On postoperative day 10, bronchoscopy confirmed a fistula approximately 1 cm in diameter between the right lower bronchial stump and the pleural cavity (Fig. 1A3), leading to a diagnosis of bronchopleural fistula. The dehiscence was complete, and the air leak was continuous (visible bubbling during both inspiration and expiration). Initial bacterial and fungal cultures of pleural fluid and sputum were negative. Subsequently, multiple thoracic effusions and sputum samples were collected for bacterial and fungal culture and identification. Approximately one week into treatment, sputum culture revealed fungal infection, prompting therapy with fluconazole solution prepared in saline. During treatment for the bronchopleural fistula, meropenem and vancomycin were administered for 24 days. Antifungal therapy was administered for nearly two weeks. The patient’s temperature gradually normalized. After the chest tube was reinserted, the drainage was initially purulent (consistent with empyema), with a drainage volume of approximately 300 milliliters during the first 24 h. The volume then decreased daily, and the nature of the drainage gradually became serous.

A2: the right middle lung showed inflammatory infiltration with consolidation status. A3: Bronchoscopic findings: the right lower bronchus was completely dehiscent (blue arrow) and connected to an empty pleural closed pouch (red arrow)

In this case, despite the development of a bronchopleural fistula, the patient’s vital signs remained relatively stable, and the condition had not yet reached a point requiring emergency surgery. Furthermore, the patient’s overall condition was poor (advanced age, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, persistent fever, and poor nutritional status (Serum albumin was 28.1 g/L at diagnosis)) and the family was reluctant to undergo two major invasive procedures within a short period. Endoscopic intervention was considered but deemed high-risk for failure given the large fistula size (≈ 1 cm) and active empyema. Subsequently, we continued the treatment with chest drains to ensure smooth drainage of secretions in the chest cavity. At the same time, appropriate antibiotic treatment was selected based on sputum culture, drainage fluid culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Considering the patient’s advanced age and having recently undergone lung surgery, a nasogastric tube was placed for enteral nutrition and parenteral nutritional support. During the treatment period, we regularly monitored the patient’s blood routine and biochemical and infection indicators. Owing to the efforts of both doctors and patients, the patient’s general condition gradually improved, and the drainage fluid and air gradually decreased. And further investigation with chest CT revealed that the BPF and empyema cavity had been decreased, along with reactive thickening of the right pleural lagers (Fig. 2B1-B2). The chest tube was subsequently removed. It was worth noting that the patient was not mechanically ventilated at any point. During 6-month follow-up involving clinic visits and CT surveillance, the patient has remained healthy.

Discussion

The literature has previously documented right sided resection as a risk factor BPF after lung surgery [9, 10]. This may be due to the anatomical structure of the lungs. The right main bronchus is thicker and straighter than the left main bronchus, leading to increased accumulation of secretions at the bronchial stump. Additionally, the lumen of the right middle lobe bronchus is relatively narrow and forms an almost perpendicular angle with the intermediate bronchus, creating turbulence during coughing. The middle lobe also has limited airflow for clearance, resulting in poor sputum expulsion and infections at the lower lobe bronchial stump, which can lead to BPF. Moreover, the right lung bronchi are mostly supplied by only one artery, and they are prone to injury during lymph node dissection, making the bronchial stump susceptible to ischemia [10, 11]. This patient has a history of working as a stonemason for 20 years, intraoperatively observed calcified hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes; At the same time, the patient has a long history of smoking and is also combined with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which are also at higher risk. These factors may all contribute to the high incidence of BPF after right lower lobectomy.

For patients presenting with early-stage fistula, large defects (> 8 mm diameter), or those refractory to non-operative management, prompt surgical intervention is mandated to avert life-threatening sequelae. However, physiological reserve and comorbidities necessitate rigorous risk stratification prior to re-exploration [12]. Definitive surgical management entails reinforcement suturing of the bronchial stump or re-resection of the dehiscent anastomosis. Following confirmation of airtight fistula closure, vascularized tissue flaps — such as pedicled pericardium, intercostal muscle, or omentum — are routinely mobilized to augment the repair site and mitigate recurrence risk [13]. The omentum, characterized by its secretory, absorptive, protective, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative properties, provides robust vascularization that renders it an ideal material for BPF repair. However, the scope of the surgery is expanded to the abdominal cavity, which can easily lead to abdominal infection, and there is still a risk of recurrence at the fistula, and may cause serious complications or even death [2]. Critical and intolerable surgery patients can use laser cautery and protein glue to block the bronchial fistula under bronchoscopy. But for BPF above 8 mm, the pain point of endoscopic treatment is low success rate but high recurrence rate [14, 15].

We have analyzed the success of this conservative treatment and believe that there are several factors to be taken into consideration. 1. Full drainage is its core role. During the hospitalization management period, according to the actual drainage situation of the patient, flexibly adjust the direction of the drainage tube and add the number of drainage tubes to maintain sufficient drainage, effectively avoiding the vicious cycle of “open fistula-thoracic contamination-delay infection”. 2. Antibiotic selection based on drug sensitivity not only covers common pathogens (such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus), but also targets the removal of Gram-negative bacteria that are colonized by the thoracic cavity. 3. Strong nutritional support. Malnutrition can easily lead to reduced treatment tolerance in patients, resulting in slower recovery after surgery, increasing the risk of adverse reactions [16]. 4. The dynamic pathophysiological outcome of the rest of lung tissue retraction, diaphragm lifting and progressive reduction of the residual cavity is laid for the ultimate successful removal of the chest tube. It has long been believed that the fistula does not heal for a long time and the continuous leakage of the chest cavity requires patients to retain the chest tube for a long time. In this patient, the residual cavity was reduced, and the thickness of the parietal pleura accelerated the viscera-parietal pleura patch, blocking the gas leakage channel. When the patient breathes spontaneously, air in the residual cavity can be freely in and out through the fistula, but is not enough to trigger tension pneumothorax. In this case, we believe the theoretical basis for the patient’s successful extubation lies in the fact that the persistent inflammatory response led to pleural fibrosis of the residual chest wall (with CT values rising to 80–100 HU in this case), forming a rigid encapsulating structure. This effectively limits the expansion of the residual cavity. Even if there is a continuous fistula, the air leakage range is still strictly restricted, creating favorable conditions for the successful removal of the chest tube (Fig. 2B1-B2). This residual cavity can persist for a long time but does not affect the patient’s daily life and therefore does not require deliberate surgical treatment.

B1: chest CT scan showing an empyema cavity measuring 4 cm × 2 cm × 3 cm containing pleural effusion without the tube (white arrow). B2: complete resolution of the inflammatory infiltrates with marked re-expansion of the right middle lobe and the right lower pleura is significantly thickened

To sum up, Choosing the right treatment strategy for BPF is decisive for this rare and life-threatening complication. Prior experience suggests that once the diagnosis of BPF has been made, the first priority is to control the septic state, improve the patient’s health and prevent pulmonary aspiration [13]. Objectively speaking, this case report does not advocate blindly adhering to conservative treatment. It requires objective and calm analysis of postoperative BPF and a realistic attitude. Although the patient was successfully treated, there are negative factors such as long hospitalization, repeated illness, and high expenses, which cannot be ignored. In addition, there is no relevant evidence-based support for whether bronchial pleural fistula after lobectomy in other areas, so timely surgical intervention or endoscopic interventional treatment is worth considering. In short, the conservative treatment of BPF is simple and easy to implement and has certain clinical significance, but it is not foolproof and needs to vary from person to person.
