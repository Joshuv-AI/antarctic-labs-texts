Degradation of hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) by acoustic and hydrodynamic cavitation
Authors: Andraž Zupanc, Martin Petkovšek, Blaž Zdovc, Ema Žagar, Mojca Zupanc
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study aims to investigate the degradation of HPMC on a laboratory scale by acoustic and hydrodynamic cavitation. The effects of temperature and the addition of an external oxidizing agent on the effectiveness of HPMC degradation were systematically investigated by SEC/MALS-RI, FTIR and 1H NMR. The results of the experiments without cavitation show that an external oxidizing agent alone reduces the weight-average molar mass at 60 °C in 30 min for 45.1 % (from 335 to 184 kg mol−1). However, the weight-average molar mass of HPMC decreased significantly more in the cavitation treatment, for 98.8 % (from 335 to 4 kg mol−1) in 30 min at optimal operating conditions of hydrodynamic cavitation (i.e. addition of external oxidant and 60 °C) with a concomitant narrowing of the molar mass distribution, as shown by the dispersity value, which decreased from 2.24 to 1.31. Compared to acoustic cavitation, hydrodynamic cavitation also proved to be more energy efficient. The FTIR spectra of the cavitated HPMC samples without the addition of H2O2 show negligible oxidation of the hydroxyl groups and the glycosidic bonds, confirming that mechanical effects predominate in HPMC degradation in these cases. In contrast, when H2O2 was added, FTIR and 1H NMR show typical signals for cellulose oxidation products, especially when the experiments were performed at 60 °C, confirming that chemical as well as mechanical effects are responsible for the extensive HPMC degradation in these cases. Since treatment methods that lead to lower molar masses and narrower molar mass distributions of the polymers are lacking or require longer treatment times (e.g. 24 h), mechanochemical treatment methods such as cavitation have great potential, as they enable faster polymer degradation (in our case 30 min) through a combination of mechanical and/or chemical degradation mechanisms.Keywords: Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, Acoustic cavitation, Hydrodynamic cavitation, Degradation, Oxidation

Introduction

Cellulose is a naturally occurring linear polysaccharide with a high degree of crystallinity, making it insoluble in water and of limited use [1], [2]. By modifying some of the hydroxyl groups of cellulose with alkyl and/or hydroxyalkyl groups to produce cellulose ether derivatives, the intermolecular hydrogen bonding and thus the crystallinity of cellulose is largely disrupted, which ultimately leads to improved solubility in water and polar organic solvents and extends the area of applications[3], [4], [5].

Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) is a non-ionic, hydrophilic, semi-synthetic cellulose ether derivative with a cellulose backbone consisting of β-d-glucose units with a (1 → 4) bond [4], [6]. The three hydroxyl groups in the monomeric unit of the cellulose are partially O-methylated and/or O-(2-hydroxypropylated) [5]. The hydroxypropyl and methyl groups can be bound to original hydroxyl groups of the cellulose or to newly formed hydroxypropyl groups via an ether bridge [7]. The HPMC grades differ in their molar mass characteristics, the degree and ratio of substitution with methyl and hydroxypropyl groups and the distribution of these substituents along the macromolecular chains [8]. HPMC has unique solubility, thermo-plastic properties and a wide range of viscosity/molar mass and substitution grades [6], [8]. Its solutions are stable at pH 3–11 and undergo a reversible sol–gel transformation with a gelation temperature of 50–90 °C, depending on HPMC grade [9]. HPMC casted from the solution forms flexible and transparent films [6]. As a water-soluble, biodegradable and biocompatible polymer, HPMC is used in medicine for the controlled drug delivery and scaffolds, in the food industry as a stabilizer, emulsifier, and thickener, in cosmetics, textiles, dyes, paints, adhesives, coatings, agriculture, construction industry and as sorbent for wastewater remediation [6], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]. Although HPMC is available in a wide range of grades, its high molar mass and broad molar mass distribution described by a high dispersity value (Đ), and the batch-to-batch variations in the molar mass and the degree of substitution as well as the distribution of substituents along the chains limit its applications [13], [16], [17]. Therefore, the use of HPMC grades with a lower molar mass and/or a narrower distribution in these parameters can be a useful tool for the development of more robust and predictable controlled release matrix tablets. In this context, various methods for degradation of cellulose like polysaccharides, including HPMC, such as chemical [18], [19], [20], enzymatic [13], [21], [22], [23], [24] and mechanical [25], [26] have been extensively studied.

One of the promising mechanical methods is cavitation, a physical phenomenon in which small cavities or bubbles form in a liquid and collapse due to a local pressure drop [27]. This can be caused by acoustic sound waves, known as acoustic cavitation (AC), or by a local increase in velocity, known as hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) [28]. When the cavitation bubble collapses, the energy is released in an extremely confined area in space and time, resulting in the mechanical and chemical phenomena associated with cavitation [29]. The mechanical effects include elevated temperatures (up to several thousand Kelvin) [30], local shear forces [31], microstreaming (high shear flow on a microscale) [28], microjets (with impinging jet velocities of several hundred meters per second) [32] and shock waves (of several megapascals) [33], while the chemical effects typically include the formation of highly reactive species such as •OH radicals [29]. It has been shown several times that cellulose glycosidic bonds can be cleaved by mechanical or chemical effects of cavitation [28], [34], [35]. Mechanical effects cleave macromolecular chains centrally, resulting in a lower average molar mass of the polymer and a narrower molar mass distribution, while chemical effects cleave the chains randomly, also resulting in a lower average molar mass of the polymer but a broader molar mass distribution [36]. For example, microstreaming caused by acoustic and hydrodynamic cavitation has been proposed as the culprit of rapid bending of cellulose fibrils, leading to their fibrillation into nanocellulose [28]. Similarly, Mohod & Gogate [35] have shown that shear forces generated by the rapid movement of the solvent following cavitation collapse control the breakage of the polymer chains, while mechanical forces generated by ball-milling activate the glycosidic bonds of the carbohydrate molecules for hydrolysis reaction [26]. On the other hand, cavitation has also been shown to facilitate the hydrolysis of cellulose ethers by chemical effects [35]. As such cavitation is considered as a mechanochemical approach for the degradation of biopolymer chains that could replace conventional chemical and enzymatic methods. Since various HC devices, static (Venturi or orifice constrictions) or dynamic (rotor-rotor, rotor–stator geometries) have been utilized and studied for various purposes [37], the HC device should be carefully selected depending on the research. The main difference between static and dynamic devices lies in the type of shear generated. In the latter, the influence of shear forces can be expected at two different levels, macro-shear and micro-shear [31], which could be important for the treatment of macromolecules. In addition, the latter offer greater potential for scaling up and industrial use, which is why a rotor–stator geometry was chosen for the purposes of this research.

The aim of this study was to compare the efficiency of AC- and HC-assisted degradation of HPMC and their influence on HPMC molar mass and structural characteristics. In this context, in addition to the type of cavitation, we also studied the effects of temperature and the addition of an external oxidising agent (hydrogen peroxide, H2O2) on HPMC degradation. The degree of degradation of HPMC was monitored by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) coupled with a multi-detection system, while structural changes in HPMC were assessed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopic methods.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The HPMC K4M grade with a viscosity of 2,700–5,040 mPa.s contains 20–24 % methoxyl and 7–12 % hydroxypropyl groups and was purchased from Ashland Global, USA. 30 % solution of H2O2 was purchased from Belinka Petrokemija (Slovenia). High purity Milli-Q water (MQ) with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm and sodium nitrate (NaNO3; ≥99.5 %; Merck, Germany) were used to prepare the mobile phase for SEC measurements.

Experimental set-ups

Two experimental set-ups (Fig. 1) were used to utilize AC and HC to investigate their effects on HPMC degradation. Both experimental set-ups are described in detail in our previous paper [38]. The AC set-up consists of a 20 kHz ultrasonic horn (UH) Cole Parmer 750 W with a titanium tip of 12.7 mm in diameter. The amplitude of the tip movement at maximum power equals 200 µm. The UH tip was immersed in a 100 mL glass beaker positioned approx. 10 mm from the bottom. The submerged cooling coil was used to maintain a constant temperature throughout the experiments. The HC set-up was first used by Stepišnik-Perdih et al. [39] and modified by Petkovšek et al. [38]. The HC rotation generator was connected to the 1-litre container in which a cooling coil was installed to maintain the constant temperature. The HC rotation generator consists of a rotor–stator configuration with a rotor of 50 mm in diameter and a rotation frequency of 10,000 rpm. Both cooling coils were connected to the external portable cooling device DuraChill CA03 with a cooling capacity of 1.28 kW. The temperature was monitored in both cases with a K-type immersion thermocouple.

Cavitation characterization

The characterization of cavitation was performed by high-speed visualization to compare and distinguish the difference between cavitation in pure water and water solution of HPMC with or without added H2O2. The visualization was performed with the high-speed camera Photron FastCam SA-Z 2100 K with 100,000 and 75,000 fps and a resolution of 640 × 256 and 640 × 376 pixels in 12-bit monochrome technique for AC and HC, respectively. By using high power LED illumination, we were able to reduce the shutter time down to 2 and 1 µs for AC and HC respectively, while keeping the aperture half open.

The visualization of cavitation in the AC and HC set-up is shown in Fig. 2 for the cases at 25 °C in distilled water (AC1 and HC1) and with dissolved HPMC and addition of H2O2 (AC2 and HC2). It can be seen that the cavitation is different with pure water and the addition of HMPC and H2O2, as there are many more small bubbles that look like foam. Although the propagation of shock waves (especially for HC) can be observed with HPMC and the addition of H2O2, the intensity of cavitation is expected to decrease due to the cushioning effect. More details on cavitation dynamics can be seen in the Supplementary Material (Suppl. 1) and additional explanation on active cavitation zone given in one of our previous paper [38].

Cavitation appearance for AC1, AC2, HC1 and HC2. Edge of a single tooth in HC case is marked with yellow. (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

To evaluate the energy input from the cavitation devices (similar as in [38]) into the HPMC samples, a calorimetric analysis was performed for AC, which resulted in a power input of 115 W. In the case of HC set-up, an electrical power consumption method was chosen due to complexity of the device design. In order to prevent misinterpretation of calorimetric measurements due to heat generation through bearings, seals, etc., power analyzer Norma 4000 (Fluke) was used to measure the electrical power at the rotation generator of HC, which equaled 670 W at selected operating conditions (with frequency controller and speed multiplier and other mechanical losses subtracted).

They were systematically designed to investigate different experimental conditions, affecting the HPMC degradation. For control experiments (Table 1, experiments C1-C4), HPMC solutions at the concentrations of 2 g L−1 and 1 g L−1 were treated for 30 min at 25 and 60 °C. HPMC solutions in concentration of 2 g L−1 were treated with AC (Table 1, experiments AC1-AC4) and HPMC solutions in concentration of 1 g L−1 were treated with HC (Table 1, experiments HC1-HC4) at 25 °C and 60 °C, which was chosen based on our previous studies on cavitation erosion [40], pharmaceuticals removal [41], and cavitation dynamics under an UH tip [42]. To test whether chemical or mechanical effects prevail during HPMC degradation, H2O2 was added to the samples immediately before the experiments as an external source of •OH radicals. All experiments with H2O2 addition had the same concentration of 1 mL 30 % H2O2 per 100 mL sample, which was chosen based on our previous study [41]. After treatment of the HPMC solution, the entire sample volume was retrieved for further analysis.

Sample preparation and analyses

For cavitation experiments, the HPMC solutions were freshly prepared by dissolving HPMC in distilled water at room temperature (∼25 °C). The molar mass characteristics of the original and treated HPMC samples were determined by size-exclusion chromatography coupled with a multi-angle light scattering detector (DAWN HELEOS-II, Wyatt Technology Corporation, USA) and a refractive index detector (Optilab T-rEX, Wyatt Technology Corporation, USA) (SEC/MALS-RI). The input parameter required to determine the molar mass characteristics of HPMC is a specific refractive index increment (dn/dc), which was determined assuming 100 % mass recovery of the HPMC from the column. Separation of HPMC by size was performed at room temperature on an Agilent 1260 HPLC chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, USA) using a PolarGel-M analytical column (7.5 × 300 mm, 8 μm) with precolumn (both columns from Agilent Laboratories, USA) and 0.1 M NaNO3 solution in MQ as a mobile phase. The nominal flow rate of the eluent was 0.8 mL min−1. The filtered HPMC solutions after AC or HC treatment were injected directly onto the SEC column, so that the mass of the injected sample was ∼100 μg. Each sample was measured in two parallels and the molar mass characteristics given are their average values. Astra 7.3.1 software (Wyatt Technology Corp., USA) was used for data acquisition and evaluation.

FTIR spectra were recorded in an attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode using a Spectrum Two FTIR spectrophotometer (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Scanning was performed at a resolution of 4 cm−1 in a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1.

1H NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker AVANCE NEO 600 MHz instrument (Bruker Corporation, USA) at room temperature in DMSO‑d6. Chemical shifts are given in ppm relative to a DMSO‑d6 residual peak.

Results and discussion

HPMC solutions treated with H2O2 (control experiments)

The extent of HPMC degradation was monitored by SEC/MALS–RI by comparing the molar mass characteristics of the original and treated samples. The weight-average molar mass (Mw) and Đ of the original HPMC (C0) investigated in this study were 335 kg mol−1 and 2.24, respectively (Fig. 3a and b).

(a): Molar mass characteristics (Mw, Đ) and (b): SEC-RI chromatograms of original HPMC (C0) and H2O2-treated HPMC at 25 °C (C1, C3) and 60 °C (C2, C4), with HPMC concentration of 2 g L−1 (C1, C2) and 1 g L−1 (C3, C4).

The HPMC degradation due to oxidation was evaluated by control experiments (C1-C4) in which HPMC solutions with a concentration of 1 and 2 g L−1 were treated with H2O2 at 25 and 60 °C for 30 min without cavitation. Compared to the Mw of the original HPMC, the Mw values of H2O2-treated HPMC decreased by 12–14 % at 25 °C and 45–47 % at 60 °C, while the molar mass distribution of HPMC was only slightly affected, as shown by the dispersity values (Fig. 3 (a)). These results show that higher temperatures (Fig. 3a, C2 and C4) facilitate the H2O2 oxidative cleavage of the β-1,4-glycosidic bonds linking the glucose repeating units in the cellulose backbone, while the solution concentration of HPMC (1 or 2 g L−1) has a much smaller effect. A similar effect of temperature was shown by Chen et al., 2019 [43] for the oxidative degradation of hyaluronic acid, as the Mw decreased more at 60 °C than at 20 °C (68.05 vs. 101.2 kg mol−1).

FTIR was used to evaluate the structural changes of HPMC before and after oxidation with H2O2 in control experiments. The FTIR spectrum of the original HPMC (C0) shows strong absorption bands at the wavenumbers of 3425, 2900 and 1055 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of the O—H, C—H and C—O groups, respectively (Fig. 4). The low intensity band at 1638 cm−1 in C0 is characteristic of the bending vibration of the –OH groups with including water molecules in the amorphous region of HPMC [44], [45]. Compared to the FTIR spectrum of C0, the FTIR spectra of C1-C4 show additional, weak intensity bands at the wavelengths 1739, 1730 and 1623 cm−1 (Fig. 4), which are due to the presence of carbonyl groups in different environments (–COOH, –CHO, –CO-) as a result of the oxidation of the HPMC hydroxyl groups and glycosidic bonds. From the intensity of these bands in the FTIR spectra of C1-C4 and the SEC-MALS results, it appears that at lower reaction temperature and higher H2O2/polymer ratio, i.e. lower solution concentration (1 g L−1, C3 in Fig. 4) the extent of oxidation of HPMC hydroxyl group is higher, while at 60 °C the hydroxyl group oxidation is accompanied to a greater extent by oxidative cleavage of the β-1,4-glycosidic bonds of the HPMC backbone, leading to a greater reduction in HPMC molar mass (C2 and C4 in Fig. 3).

FTIR spectra of original HPMC (C0) and H2O2-treated HPMC at 25 °C (C1, C3) and 60 °C (C2, C4).

HPMC solutions treated with cavitation in the presence and absence of H2O2

The molar mass characteristics of the HPMC treated with AC and HC are shown in Fig. 5, Fig. 6. Compared to the control experiments, a significantly higher degree of HPMC degradation was achieved when the samples were treated with cavitation, regardless of the experimental conditions used (temperature and H2O2 addition). The Mw of AC and HC treated-HPMC in the absence of H2O2 reached 32 and 97 kg mol−1 at 25 °C, while at 60 °C Mw decreased to 122 and 74 kg mol−1, respectively. These results show that temperature has a stronger effect on HPMC degradation in the AC experiments (AC1 vs. AC3 in Fig. 5a and b) than in the HC experiments (HC1 vs. HC3 in Fig. 6a and b), which might be due to higher HPMC solution concentration in AC and/or other specific cavitation effects. Namely, higher temperature can suppress the cavitation effects due to the higher vapour pressure, which consequently causes cushioning effects, resulting in lower intensity of bubble collapse, as previously reported [46]. On the other hand, the kinetic rate constant for the reaction between radicals and polymer increases with temperature, which should lead to higher extent of polymer degradation [46]. Since these two effects compete, it could be that cushioning effect prevails in AC due to the higher concentration of cavitation events under the ultrasonic horn tip, while better mixing predominates in HC, as discussed in detail in [38]. Since it is known that the cleavage of the glycosidic bond during cavitation can occur by chemical (•OH) and/or mechanical effects, it could be that in the HC treatment of HPMC the chemical effects prevail, which is consistent with previously reported results [25], [36], [46]. Furthermore, in the AC experiments, the concentration of HPMC solutions was also higher, which favours the hydrophobic intermolecular interaction at 60 °C and locally even higher temperature, leading to possible phase separation of HPMC, which affects the energy transfer processes between the solvent and the polymer and finally reduces the probability of polymer degradation [35], [38]. Compared to our results, Goodwin et al., 2011 [3] degraded HPMC with a Mw of 119.5 kg mol−1 and a Đ of 4.5 by ultrasonic irradiation of HPMC water solution. Only after 24 h of treatment, they obtained highly degraded HPMC with a Mw of 76.2 kg mol−1 and a Đ of 2.1. On the other hand, Caceres et al., 2020 [13] prepared HPMC samples with comparable low molar masses (Mw below 20 kg mol−1 and Đ values from 3.6 to 5.1) as in our work by using enzyme-assisted hydrolysis as the degradation method in phosphate-citric acid buffer solution at 47 °C for 72 h. These results show that significantly longer treatment times of several hours [3] compared to only 30 min treatment time in our case are required to achieve comparable molar masses of HPMC.

(a): Molar mass characteristics (Mw, Đ) and (b): SEC-RI chromatograms of original HPMC (C0) and HC-treated HPMC (HC1-HC4).

The combination of cavitation with H2O2 showed a synergistic effect on the extent of HPMC degradation at both temperatures. In experiments performed at 25 °C in the presence of H2O2, the Mw values of AC- and HC-treated HPMC decreased to 22 and 12 kg mol−1, respectively, while the Mw values at 60 °C reached 14 and 4 kg mol−1, respectively, indicating high degree of HPMC degradation (>93–94 %, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Chen et al., 2019 [43] also demonstrated that AC in combination with H2O2 and CuCl2 is a highly efficient method for the degradation of hyaluronic acid. Under optimal conditions and at a temperature of 50 °C, they reduced the Mw of hyaluronic acid from 1000 to around 80 kg mol−1 in 30 min. On the other hand, Prajapat and Gogate, 2019 [25] achieved a 75 % reduction in the viscosity of a carboxymethyl cellulose solution after 180 min treatment of solution with HC in combination with UV and potassium persulfate as an external oxidising agent. One of the reasons for the lower effectiveness found compared to our results could be the choice of a static Venturi device, while in our case a dynamic rotor–stator device induced shear at the macro scale (due to the movement of the rotor near the stator) in addition to shear at the micro scale (due to the formation and collapse of cavitation structures). The same analogy regarding the performance of static and dynamic HC devices was seen in our previous studies on pharmaceutical removal [41], [47], where higher removal was achieved more efficiently in the latter. In addition, the contribution of micro-shear was also recognised in the case of waste activated sludge degradation, where its effects were necessary for the breakage of macromolecules released from the sludge flocs by macro-shear [31]. However, to draw more meaningful conclusions, further studies investigating and comparing the degradation of HPMC with static and dynamic HC devices are required.

To evaluate the structural changes of HPMC during AC and HC treatment in the presence and absence of H2O2 FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopic methods were used. Comparing the FTIR spectra of C0, the FTIR spectra of the H2O2-treated HPMC in the AC experiments (AC2 and AC4) show an additional, high intensity band at a wavelength of ∼1730 cm−1 (Fig. 7), which is more pronounced in the FTIR spectrum of HPMC treated at 60 °C (AC4). In the FTIR spectrum of AC-treated HPMC in absence of H2O2 (AC3), the band at ∼1730 cm−1 is barely detected. In addition to the band at 1730 cm−1, the FTIR spectra of the H2O2-treated HPMC in the HC experiments (HC2 and HC4) also show a high intensity band at 1625 cm−1, which is shifted by 13 cm−1 toward lower wavenumber compared to the small intensity band at 1638 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum of the C0 (Fig. 8). The bands at ∼1730 and 1625 cm−1 imply the formation of oxidation products of cellulose and their intensity is consistent with the extent of reduction in the HPMC molar mass. These results indicate that oxidative cleavage of the glycosidic bond plays an important role and that the degradation of HPMC in these cases is caused predominately by •OH radicals in combination with mechanical effects. An addition of external oxidants like H2O2 intensifies the •OH formation during cavitation, leading to chemically enhanced bond scission. Furthermore, the intensity of the bands at ∼1730 and 1625 cm−1 is much higher in the samples treated at 60 °C than at 25 °C for both AC and HC. The reason for this could be higher kinetic rate constant for the reaction between radicals and polymer, which in this case (AC4 and HC4) predominates over the cushioning effects contrary to experiments AC3 and HC3. Moreover, the intensity of these bands is higher in the spectra of the HC-treated samples (Fig. 8) than in those of the AC-treated samples (Fig. 7), most likely due to the high turbulence and consequently good mixing in HC, which transports •OH formed from H2O2 in the active cavitation zone into the bulk liquid more effectively than in AC, as discussed in detail in [38]. Since the entire sample is continuously transported through the cavitation chamber (Fig. 2), the probability of •OH encountering a polymer chain in HC is higher. Another possible reason could be the H2O2/polymer ratio, as more oxidizing agent was available in HC experiments.

The high degree of HPMC oxidation in the H2O2-assisted AC and HC experiments was also demonstrated by 1H NMR spectroscopy (AC2, AC4, HC2 and HC4 in Fig. 9, Fig. 10). The 1H NMR spectrum of the C0 shows the signal at δ 1.02 ppm, which is due to the methyl group of the hydroxypropyl substituent of HPMC. The protons of the C2–C6 cellulose backbone appear together with the methylene and methine protons of the hydroxypropyl substituent and the methoxy group of HPMC in the range δ 2.7–4.0 ppm. The signal of the C1 cellulose proton is at δ 4.4 ppm and overlaps with the signals of the hydroxyl groups, which appear in the range δ 4.0–5.6 ppm. Compared to the 1H NMR spectrum of C0, the 1H NMR spectra of AC2, AC4, HC2 and HC4 show additional signals that are typical for cellulose oxidation products. The broad signal with a chemical shift (δ) of 12.6 ppm and the low intensity signal at δ 9.6 ppm indicate the presence of carboxyl and aldehyde groups. The high intensity signal at δ 8.2 ppm and the low intensity signal at δ 7.3 ppm are most likely due to the formation of trace amounts of formic acid and 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid, respectively, which is consistent with the signal for the carboxyl group at δ 12.6 ppm. The low intensity signals in the methyl region at δ 2.1 and δ 1.9 ppm might correspond to acetic acid and an unknow side product. In addition, the intensity of the signals in the δ 3.8–6.8 ppm region increases, indicating the formation of various other oxidation products in these samples. The intensity of the additional signals in the 1H NMR spectra of the AC- and HC-treated samples in the presence of H2O2 (AC2, AC4, HC2 and HC4 in Fig. 9, Fig. 10) is consistent with the intensity of the ∼1730 and 1625 cm−1 bands in their FTIR spectra, because in both cases the intensity is higher in the HPMC spectra treated at 60 °C than at 25 °C, and also higher in the spectra of the HC-treated than the AC-treated HPMC samples.

1H NMR spectra of original HPMC (C0) and HPMC treated for 30 min with HC at 25 °C and 60 °C in the presence and absence of external H2O2 oxidizing agent (HC1-HC4). Spectra were recorded in DMSO‑d6.

Since the FTIR and 1H NMR spectra of AC- and HC-treated HPMC (AC3, HC1 and HC3 in Fig. 7–10) in the absence of H2O2 do not show the signals/bands typical for cellulose oxidation products at all or with significantly lower intensity and the spectra are more or less similar to the spectrum of the original HPMC, it was concluded that AC- and HC-treated HPMC is mainly mechanically degraded. This is also supported by the lower Đ values of AC1, AC3 and HC1, HC3 compared to the Đ values of the corresponding AC2, AC4 and HC2, HC4 treated in the presence of H2O2 (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). These results reflect slightly narrower molar mass distribution of AC- and HC-treated HPMC compared to AC- and HC-treated HPMC in the presence of H2O2. Regardless of reaction temperature and H2O2 addition, all AC- and HC-treated HPMC samples show lower dispersity values compared to the original HPMC, suggesting that mechanical forces, particularly acting on longer chains, dominate over chemical effects (Fig. 5, Fig. 6).

Conclusions

In the present study, the degradation of HPMC was investigated by acoustic and hydrodynamic cavitation with and without the addition of H2O2 as an external oxidizing agent. Hydrodynamic cavitation proved to be more effective only when H2O2 was added, regardless of the temperature. Under optimal operating conditions, hydrodynamic cavitation also outperformed acoustic cavitation in terms of efficiency and amount of sample treated. The FTIR and 1H NMR spectra of AC- and HC-treated HPMC indicate degradation of HPMC mainly by mechanical forces, while the spectra of HPMC cavitated in the presence of H2O2 show that HPMC is degraded by both oxidative and mechanical mechanisms, resulting in a high degree of HPMC degradation. This indicates that oxidative cleavage of HPMC glycosidic bonds by •OH radicals play an important role, especially at 60 °C and hydrodynamic cavitation. The results of this study reconfirm that mechanical treatments could be used as an alternative to the commonly used chemical and enzymatic methods to cleave the glycosidic bonds, especially since the degree of oxidation of the glycosidic bonds can be manipulated by the experimental conditions. The knowledge gained in this study can serve as a basis for the degradation of other natural polysaccharides such as hyaluronic acid and chitosan.
