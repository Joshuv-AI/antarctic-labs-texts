Predictors of futile recanalisation in patients with large infarct: a post-hoc analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial
Authors: Tingyu Yi, Xiaochuan Huo, Xiaohui Lin, Mengxing Wang, Yan-min Wu, Zhinan Pan, Xiufen Zheng, Ding-lai Lin, Yuesong Pan, Zhongrong Miao, Wenhuo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies on futile recanalisation after endovascular therapy (EVT) for anterior circulation large vessel occlusion with large infarct were scarce. The present study aimed to explore the incidence and independent predictors of futile recanalisation in patients with large infarct.MethodsThis is a post-hoc analysis of the ANGEL-Alberta Stroke Program Early CT (ASPECT) trial. A favourable outcome was defined as a 90-day modified Rankin Scale score of 0−3; successful reperfusion was defined as extended thrombolysis in cerebral infarction 2b, 2c and 3 on final angiogram; and futile recanalisation was defined as unfavourable outcome despite successful reperfusion. We performed multivariate analysis to identify the predictors of futile recanalisation after EVT in patients with large infarct.ResultsA total of 183 patients were included: 91 (49.7%) patients had futile recanalisation and 92 (51.3%) had meaningful recanalisation. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, nonmodifiable factors included older age (age ≥68 years, OR=3.4, 95%CI 1.5 to 7.7, p= 0.003), female sex (OR=2.78, 95%CI 1.28 to 7.27, p=0.01), higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score (NIHSS ≥16, OR=3.1, 95%CI 1.2 to 8.3, p=0.035), diabetes (OR=3.1, 95%CI 1.2 to 8.3, p=0.017) and symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage (sICH) (OR=9.1, 95%CI 1.0 to 80.7, p=0.049), and modifiable factors included larger final infarct volume (FIV ≥174.7, OR=6.2, 95%CI 2.5 to 15.7, p<0.001) and postoperative respiratory failure (OR=14.1, 95%CI 1.6 to 124.8, p=0.018), which were independent predictors of futile recanalisation.ConclusionsFutile recanalisation occurred in approximately half of patients who had an acute stroke with large infarct after EVT in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial. Nonmodifiable factors that included old age, high baseline NIHSS score, diabetes mellitus, sICH and large FIV, and modifiable factors that included respiratory failure were independent predictors of futile recanalisation after EVT for large ischaemic strokes. Stroke-related pneumonia control may improve prognosis.Keywords: Brain, Cerebral Infarction, Cerebrovascular Disorders

Introduction

Endovascular therapy (EVT) is currently the standard treatment for acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) due to intracranial large vessel occlusion (LVO)1; however, the benefit of EVT for patients with a large infarct, defined as an ASPECT score ≤51 or a baseline infarct volume (BIV) ≥50 mL or 70 mL, was uncertain until recently published landmark trial demonstrated the clinical benefit of EVT for large infarcts.2,5 ANGEL-Alberta Stroke Program Early CT (ASPECT) trial (Trial of Endovascular Therapy for Acute Ischemic Stroke with Large Infarct, ClinicalTrials.gov number NCT04551664) showed that successful reperfusion (defined as an extended thrombolysis in cerebral infarction (eTICI) score ≥2b) was 83.6%.4 However, the favourable outcome rate (defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0–3) was 47%, which means more than half of patients failed to have a favourable outcome despite successful reperfusion, which was termed as futile recanalisation. The incidence and predictive factors of futile reperfusion in patients who had a stroke with large infarcts caused by intracranial LVO undergoing EVT have yet to be explored. Thus, in our study, we analysed data from the ANGEL-ASPECT trial to identify the incidence of and factors predicting futile recanalisation in such a group of patients.

Methods

This study represents a post-hoc analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial (NCT04551664), which was a multicentre, prospective, randomised, open-label clinical trial with blinded endpoints. It was conducted at 46 comprehensive stroke centres across China from 2 October 2020 to 18 May 2022.4 The trial’s methodology and patient eligibility criteria have been previously described.6 For the current analysis, patients who were lost to follow-up, did not undergo digital subtraction angiography (DSA) or experienced failed recanalisation (eTICI <2b 50) were excluded.

Measurement

We collected the baseline characteristics, including demographics, medical history, smoking history, wake-up stroke, blood pressure, stroke subtype defined according to the TOAST criteria,7 preoperational intravenous thrombolysis, time metrics and biochemical tests. We also collected various scores related to stroke, including baseline mRS, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and baseline Glasgow Coma Scale score.

Imaging at 36 hours (±12 hours) and 7 days (±1 day) after randomisation or at discharge was performed. Multimodal imaging that included CT+Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA)+CT perfusion (CTP) or MRI+Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA)+Perfusion Weighted Imaging (PWI) was performed at baseline and 36 hours (±12 hours) after randomisation. CT was performed at 7 days (±1 day) after randomisation. All brain images including baseline ASPECT Score (ASPECTS), arterial occlusion site, reperfusion level, target recanalisation level at 36 hours and intracranial haemorrhage (ICH) were adjudicated by the imaging core laboratory that was independent and blinded to all clinical characteristics. RAPID V.5.0.4 (iSchemaView) software was used to assess infarct core volume assessed on baseline diffusion-weighted imaging or CTP. Relative cerebral blood volume (rCBF) <30% based on CTP or apparent diffusion coefficient <620 based on MRI was defined as infarct core volume.

Baseline ASPECTS via noncontrast CT was used to assess infarct extent; the occlusion site was confirmed by digital subtraction angiography, which included the internal carotid artery (ICA), proximal M1, distal M1 and M2, and ipsilateral extracranial ICA. RAPID software was used to autocalculate perfusion maps and ischaemic core volumes. A time to a maximum of the residue function (Tmax) exceeding 6s (Tmax >6 s) was defined as hypoperfusion, and ischaemic core volumes were those with an rCBF <30%. The mismatch volume was defined as the volume with Tmax >6 s minus the volume with an rCBF <30%.

The eTICI score was adopted to assess the reperfusion extent, and successful reperfusion was defined as 2b50, 2b67, 2c or 3.4 Perioperative details and complications including thrombectomy attempts, distal embolism, embolisation into a new territory, arterial dissection, contrast leakage, status epilepticus and respiratory failure postoperation were also recorded. The final infarct volume (FIV) was assessed on follow-up MR on days 5–7 using an automated algorithm.

Statistical analysis

Patients were dichotomised into the meaningful recanalisation group (defined as a 90-day mRS score ≤3) and the futile recanalisation group (defined as a 90-day mRS score >3) according to clinical and angiographic results. Clinical and radiological outcomes were compared between the two groups. Continuous data are summarised as the median and IQR or mean and SD, and t tests (normal distribution and homogeneity of variance) and Mann-Whitney U tests (abnormal distribution or heteroscedasticity) were used to compare group differences. Categorical variables are described as proportions and were compared using the χ2 test. Variables with a p value <0.05 were entered into multivariate logistic regression analysis with a stepwise likelihood ratio model. Data are presented as the OR and its 95%CI. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to explore the cut-off value of predictive factors. All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), with a significance level of p<0.05 (two-sided).

Results

From 2 October 2020 to 18 May 2022, a total of 1504 patients underwent screening at 46 centres. Among them, 456 (30.3%) were enrolled in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial. Specifically, 231 were randomly assigned to the EVT group and 225 to the medical management group. One patient initially assigned to the EVT group had their consent withdrawn by their legal representative, so 230 patients in the EVT group were included in the full-analysis population. After adjudication, 11 patients were excluded. Finally, 219 patients with EVT were included.7 Successful reperfusion was achieved in 183 (83.6%) patients, who were included in the analysis (figure 1).

About 49.7% (91/183) of patients suffered futile recanalisation, and 38.4% (35/91) of them died while 50.2% (92/183) achieved meaningful recanalisation. The baseline characteristics included demography, imaging and procedure, and postoperative of the patients are listed in tables13. Compared with the patients in the meaningful recanalisation group, patients in the futile recanalisation group were significantly older (mean 70.0 vs 64.0 years, p<0.001); had significantly higher baseline NIHSS scores (median 18 vs 14, p<0.001), higher baseline systolic pressures (mean 150 mmHg vs 143 mmHg, p=0.005) and larger baseline and final infarct core volumes (median; baseline: 65.0 mL vs 48.5 mL, p=0.07; final: 148.25 vs 97.34, p<0.001); were more likely to be female (48.2% vs 28.3%, p<0.001), to have diabetes (28.6% vs 12.0%, p<0.006) and to have cardioembolic (60.4% vs 41.3%, p=0.049); were less likely to be current smokers (18.7% vs 35.9%, p=0.01); were more likely to have symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage (sICH) (10.99% vs 1.09%, p=0.02); and were likely to have more respiratory failure (14.3% vs 1.1%, p=0.01). Workflow time metrics were similar between the two groups, except door-to-imagine time was longer (37.5 min vs 32.0 min, p=0.04) and puncture-to-perfusion time was 10 min longer without significant differences (62.5 min vs 72 min p=0.50) in the futile reperfusion groups.

About 67.1% (53/79) of female patients, 65.4% (63/99) of patients aged ≥68 years, 64.4% (65/101) with an NIHSS score ≥16, 70.3% (26/37) of patients with diabetes, 59.1% (55/93) of patients with cardioembolic, 79.6% (39/49) of patients with an FIV ≥174.7 mL and 92.9% (13/14) of patients with respiratory failure suffered futile recanalisation. We also pay attention to patients who were aged ≥75 years which occupied 22.4% (41/183) in such group patients, and only approximately 26.8% (11/41) of them had meaningful recanalisation and 19.5% (8/41) of them had an independent functional outcome. About 6.0% (11/183) of patients had sICH; of these, 90.9% (10/11) suffered futile recanalisation, 54.5% (6/11) of them died, 49 (53.3%) patients with futile reperfusion received intravenous tirofiban and none of them suffered sICH.

ROC analysis showed the optimal cut-off score of age, NIHSS score and FIV to predict a favourable functional outcome as 68 years (area under the curve (AUC)=0.68, 61% sensitivity, 69% specificity, p<0.001), 16 (AUC=0.67, 61% sensitivity, 71% specificity, p<0.001) and 174.7 mL (AUC=0.69, 89% sensitivity, 43% specificity, p<0.001), respectively.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to focus on futile reperfusion in patients with large infarct and who underwent EVT. Approximately half of the patients had futile recanalisation, of whom 38.4% died in our study.

Our study showed that factors including older age, higher NIHSS score, diabetes mellitus, larger FIV, respiratory failure post-treatment, sICH and female sex were associated with futile recanalisation, which was similar in patients without large infarct.8 These can be divided into nonmodifiable predictors that included age ≥68 years, female sex, NIHSS ≥16, diabetes mellitus and sICH and modifiable predictors that included FIV ≥174.7 mL respiratory failure.

Nonmodifiable predictive factors of futile reperfusion

In line with previous studies9 10 conducted in both modern and earlier endovascular eras, older age was strongly associated with futile reperfusion. Advanced age often has more cardiovascular risk factors, which may influence the myogenic tone of blood vessels and the self-regulating ability of arteries10 and attenuate endothelial function. All these changes may lead to poor collateral circulation,11 heighten the likelihood of penumbra tissue transforming into infarcted tissue,11 12 and increase the possibility of vessel tortuosity and leukoaraiosis. Vessel tortuosity may increase the difficulties of endovascular procedures and lead to failure of reperfusion. Of note, the factor of frailty which we did not collect was more important than age in predicting futile reperfusion.13 Age was a predictor of futile reperfusion in both patients with and without large infarct, but the predictive cut-off value is different in these two groups of patients. A predictive cut-off value of poor clinical outcome (defined as mRS score ≥2) in patients without large infarct was 74 years,8 while it was 68 years (defined as mRS score ≥3) in patients with large infarct in our study.

A previous study showed no patients with ASPECTS of 0–5 and aged >75 years achieved functional independence at 90 days,14 but our study showed that approximately 20% of such a group of patients achieved functional independence. The reason may be due to the different reperfusion rates (84.6% vs 100% in our study). Our study findings also suggest that even advanced patients can benefit from successful reperfusion despite the low rate.

Female sex had a nearly threefold chance of futile reperfusion in our study. The influence of sex on the functional outcomes in patients who receive EVT is debatable.15,18 Oestrogen and sex chromosomes had different impacts on stroke severity and clinical outcomes.18 Oestrogen was related to a smaller infarct volume, while the sex X chromosome was related to larger infarct volumes and severe stroke damage.18 At the reproductive stage, the impact of oestrogen is dominant; however, at the reproductive senescence phase, the impact of the sex X chromosome is dominant.18 The average age of the first stroke attack among women was 72.9 years,19 which typically represents the reproductive senescence phase, so the impact of the second X chromosome would be dominant. Furthermore, approximately 60% of women older than 75 years had atrial fibrillation, a condition linked to a higher risk of severe stroke due to embolic events.20 Other factors influencing the clinical outcome in female patients are listed as follows. First, due to very restricted social activities and relative ignorance of their health because of the Confucian understanding of women21 22 and the higher incidence of aphasia and decreased level of consciousness on presentation, women older than 75 years often arrive late to the hospital. Second, at the age when stroke first presents itself, women are often solitary and have limited social support, which often leads to their institutionalisation at discharge.19 22 Third, women have a higher likelihood of experiencing poststroke depression, which can also hinder recovery.18 Fourth, frailty and musculoskeletal comorbidities such as arthritis and osteoporosis are common in female patients.18

Patients with diabetes mellitus had a more than threefold increase in futile reperfusion. Dysglycaemia has harmful effects in patients who had a stroke, including changes in blood-brain barrier permeability, compromised cerebrovascular reactivity within the microvasculature, elevated lactic acid production in ischaemic tissues, antifibrinolytic effects and heightened susceptibility to reperfusion injury. Collectively, these factors can promote infarct, brain oedema and haemorrhagic transformation.23

Modifiable predictors of futile reperfusion

Of note, about 90% of patients with sICH suffered futile reperfusion, and more than half of them died. So, it was very important if sICH could be predicted at the early stage. The TAGE score, which incorporates time, the ASPECT score, glycaemia and early venous filling after complete successful recanalisation, is a readily available set of clinical variables that performs well in predicting sICH following mechanical thrombectomy.24 For each unit increase in the TAGE score, the risk of sICH approximately doubles. Since there is a close relationship between sICH and futile reperfusion, measurements should be explored to control sICH, such as blood pressure lowering, and sedation.

Our study showed that FIV was an independent predictor of futile recanalisation, which is in line with previous studies.25,28 The previous studies showed that there was a onefold increase in dependent outcome (mRS score ≥2) if FIV was greater than 40 mL29 or 50 mL,25 and there was nearly a sevenfold increase in poor outcome (mRS score ≥3) if FIV was greater than 175 mL. So, steps that can minimise the FIV were adopted, and the prognosis of the patient may be improved. For example, the administration of neuroprotective agents,30 other techniques such as regional hypothermia, remote ischaemic conditioning,31 normobaric oxygen32 and intravenous methylprednisolone33 can minimise the FIV and either improve functional outcome or decrease ICH rate or mortality.

Another modifiable predictor was respiratory failure, which was not common with 7.7% occurrence in patients with large infarct, but its occurrence was associated with a 14-fold increase in futile reperfusion. So, steps adopted to reduce the rate of respiratory failure may enhance the prognosis of such group patients. Respiratory failure may be related to stroke-associated pneumonia (SAP) and a large infarct core.34 SAP is a frequent complication following stroke, occurring with an overall incidence ranging from 5% to 26%.35 36 The mechanisms by which pneumonia occurs are multifactorial. First, stroke triggers localised inflammation at the site of injury and systemic immune suppression. These effects can be detected within a few hours following ischaemic onset and persist for several weeks.37 The catecholamine-induced impairments in early lymphocyte activation play a crucial role in the weakened antibacterial immune response observed after stroke.37 Second, aspiration is related to impaired consciousness and dysphagia.38 Third, additional risk factors for pneumonia include severe stroke, immobility, weak cough and placement of a nasogastric feeding tube.34 So, strong steps including usage of antibiotic drugs, arrangement of anti-aspiration and care of the respiratory tract should be taken to control stroke-related pneumonia, which also hints at the importance of postoperative respiratory airway management in patients with large infarcts. Recently, the MARVEL trial showed that intravenous methylprednisolone at 2 mg/kg/day can significantly reduce the pneumonia occurrence in patients who had an AIS and underwent EVT,33 suggesting that the drug may benefit patients with large infarct.

Time is the brain. Workflow time included onset-to-recanalisation time; puncture-to-recanalisation time was related to prognosis in patients who had an AIS and received reperfusion therapy.39 In our study, it was worth noting. Although without significant differences, the workflow time was longer in the futile reperfusion group, suggesting that optimising workflow may benefit patients with large infarct.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strengths of our research lie in the fact that the dataset was derived from a nationwide multicentric randomised controlled trial involving consecutive thrombectomy procedures. Additionally, a comprehensive and independent 90-day follow-up was conducted to assess clinical outcomes systematically. However, there are several limitations to consider. First, despite the prospective registration of the data, the analysis of this subgroup was conducted retrospectively. Second, the sample size was not large enough to explore other variables that are associated with futile reperfusion.

Conclusion

In this post-hoc analysis, approximately half of patients with large infarcts following EVT had futile reperfusion. Advanced age, a high baseline NIHSS score, diabetes mellitus, a large FIV and respiratory failure were independent predictors of futile reperfusion for patients who had a stroke with large infarct cores caused by anterior circulation intracranial LVO who underwent EVT. Neuroprotective therapy administration and postoperative respiratory airway management may alert the status of futile reperfusion.
