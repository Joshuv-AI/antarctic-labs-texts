GP130 Regulates Macrophage Polarization and Functional Recovery after Spinal Cord Injury in Rats: A Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Study
Authors: Mengqi Gao, Danfen Zhang, Mingze Liu, Yezhong Wang, Yanbin Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroinflammation and macrophage polarization play important roles in secondary injury and functional recovery after spinal cord injury (SCI). GP130, the common signal-transducing receptor subunit of the IL-6 cytokine family, is closely associated with JAK/STAT3-mediated inflammatory signaling. However, its role in macrophage polarization after SCI remains unclear. This study investigated whether GP130 inhibition regulates macrophage polarization and improves functional recovery after SCI. A rat model of T10 spinal cord compression injury was established using a standardized microvascular clip compression method. The GP130 inhibitor SC144 was administered after SCI. Functional recovery was evaluated using BBB scoring and inclined plane tests. Histopathological, ultrastructural, immunofluorescence, flow cytometry, qRT-PCR, western blotting, ELISA, and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses were performed to evaluate tissue injury, inflammatory signaling, and macrophage polarization. Primary bone marrow-derived macrophages were further used to assess the direct effects of GP130 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition on macrophage phenotype in vitro. SCI induced severe locomotor impairment, tissue disruption, ultrastructural damage, and activation of GP130-related inflammatory signaling. SC144 treatment improved functional recovery, reduced inflammatory cytokine expression, and attenuated downstream STAT3 activation. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed a prominent monocyte/macrophage-related myeloid-cell response after SCI and transcriptional heterogeneity within the myeloid compartment. Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry showed that GP130 inhibition reduced M1-like marker expression and promoted M2-like marker expression in vivo. In primary macrophages, GP130-siRNA and SC144 similarly suppressed M1-like pro-inflammatory marker expression and enhanced M2-like reparative marker expression. GP130 inhibition modulates macrophage polarization and promotes functional recovery after T10 spinal cord compression injury. These findings suggest that GP130-related inflammatory signaling may be a potential therapeutic target for regulating macrophage-mediated neuroinflammation after SCI.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a catastrophic central nervous system trauma with complex pathogenesis, posing significant challenges to current clinical treatment modalities [1]. The etiology of SCI includes traffic accidents, falls from heights, violent injuries, and sports accidents, leading to severe structural damage of the spinal cord and resulting in permanent or long-term loss of sensation, movement, and autonomic function, imposing profound and far-reaching burdens on patients’ physical, psychological, and social lives [2]. Globally, the incidence of SCI is alarmingly on the rise, with approximately 13 to 53 new cases per million people each year worldwide [3]. In China alone, there are about 60,000 to 80,000 new cases annually, predominantly affecting young adults aged 20 to 40, with a higher prevalence among males [4]. Current clinical treatments for SCI rely on surgery, medication, and rehabilitation training, but these are often ineffective, with limited recovery for patients [5]. Therefore, elucidating the pathogenesis of SCI and exploring more effective treatment strategies are urgent priorities [6].

Following SCI, the spinal cord tissue endures both primary and secondary injuries. Primary injury results from direct mechanical damage due to external forces, manifesting as contusions, lacerations, and bleeding within the spinal cord [7]. Secondary injury builds upon the primary injury, triggered by a cascade of complex pathophysiological factors, with inflammation at its core, persisting throughout the entire injury repair process [8].

Macrophages play a pivotal role in the inflammatory response following SCI, significantly impacting the progression of secondary injury and subsequent repair processes [9]. These cells exhibit notable heterogeneity and plasticity, adapting their functions and phenotypes in response to microenvironmental changes, primarily polarizing into the classically activated M1 type or the alternatively activated M2 type [10]. M1 macrophages typically promote inflammation, highly expressing surface markers such as CD86 and iNOS, and releasing pro-inflammatory mediators like IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and ROS, which exacerbate neuronal and myelin damage [11]. In contrast, M2 macrophages actively participate in anti-inflammatory responses and tissue repair, expressing CD206 and Arg-1, and secreting anti-inflammatory mediators like IL-10 and TGF-β, effectively clearing cellular debris while promoting axonal regeneration and functional recovery [12]. Numerous experimental studies have shown that bone marrow-derived macrophages infiltrate the injured spinal cord in large numbers following SCI, participating in all stages of the inflammatory response [13]. Therefore, precisely regulating the polarization of infiltrating macrophages towards the M2 type has become an important research direction in the field of SCI intervention treatment [14].Macrophage polarization after SCI is regulated by multiple inflammatory and repair-related signaling pathways. For example, cGAS/STING/NF-κB activation has been reported to promote inflammatory microglia/macrophage responses after SCI, whereas pharmacological inhibition of this axis can shift microglia toward an M2-like phenotype and improve functional recovery [15]. In addition, GLP-1R-related signaling can attenuate microglia-mediated neuroinflammation and glial scar formation after SCI [16]. The cannabinoid receptor-2/autophagy/NLRP3 inflammasome axis has also been shown to regulate neuroinflammation after SCI by promoting autophagy-mediated degradation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [17]. These studies indicate that macrophage/microglia polarization after SCI is controlled by several signaling networks, including NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling, inflammasome-related pathways, autophagy-associated signaling, and cytokine-dependent JAK/STAT pathways.GP130, a common receptor subunit for the IL-6 family of cytokines, is widely expressed in various cells and tissues, with ligands including IL-6, IL-11, LIF, OSM, and CNTF [18]. Upon binding to GP130, these ligands activate downstream signaling pathways such as JAK/STAT, MAPK/ERK, and PI3K/Akt, which finely regulate cellular processes like proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammation [19]. Among the signaling pathways involved in macrophage polarization, JAK/STAT3, MAPK/ERK, and PI3K/Akt are canonical downstream branches of GP130 activation. In contrast, NF-κB-, cGAS/STING-, autophagy-, and inflammasome-related pathways are not classical direct downstream pathways of GP130, but they may interact with GP130-mediated cytokine signaling through inflammatory crosstalk and feedback regulation. Therefore, GP130 may influence macrophage polarization mainly through its canonical downstream JAK/STAT3, MAPK/ERK, and PI3K/Akt pathways, while also being associated with broader inflammatory signaling networks after SCI. Studies have shown that following SCI, GP130 and its downstream STAT3 signaling pathway are rapidly activated, playing indispensable roles in neuroprotection, axonal regeneration, and blood-spinal cord barrier repair [20]. For instance, selective knockout of GP130 or STAT3 in astrocytes has been shown to exacerbate tissue damage and motor function loss in SCI mice, indicating the involvement of the astrocyte GP130-STAT3 pathway in endogenous SCI protection mechanisms [21]. Other research has pointed out that persistent STAT3 activation in spinal cord tissue following SCI and the significant limitation of axonal regeneration following neuronal-specific STAT3 knockout suggest that STAT3 is a key regulatory factor in axonal regeneration [22]. Additionally, recent studies have found that conditional knockout of STAT3 in microglia can effectively reduce inflammatory responses in SCI mice and inhibit the polarization of M1-type microglia/macrophages, implying that STAT3 also plays an important role in the polarization regulation of macrophages [23]. However, the specific role of GP130, an upstream molecule of STAT3, in macrophage polarization following SCI has not been fully and systematically reported [24].

Notably, the GP130-STAT3 signaling pathway also plays a crucial role in peripheral inflammatory diseases. In conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, the GP130-STAT3 pathway is continuously activated, driving the polarization of macrophages and other immune cells towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype, leading to the secretion of large amounts of inflammatory mediators and exacerbating tissue damage [25]. Inhibition of the GP130-STAT3 pathway can significantly improve symptoms such as joint swelling and bone destruction in rheumatoid arthritis mice [26]. Given the homology between peripheral and central macrophages, it is hypothesized that the GP130-STAT3 pathway may also be involved in the regulation of macrophage polarization following SCI [27]. SC144 is a first-in-class, orally active small-molecule inhibitor of GP130. Mechanistically, SC144 has been reported to bind GP130, induce GP130 phosphorylation at Ser782 and deglycosylation, abrogate STAT3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, and suppress the expression of downstream GP130/STAT3 target genes. Previous studies have used SC144 as a pharmacological tool to inhibit GP130-mediated signaling in both in vitro and in vivo experimental models, including ovarian cancer, pancreatic cancer, and inflammation-related disease models. These studies support the feasibility of using SC144 to investigate the biological consequences of GP130 pathway inhibition. Therefore, in the present study, SC144 was selected as the pharmacological inhibitor to determine whether suppression of GP130 signaling could regulate macrophage polarization and improve functional recovery after SCI [28].

In summary, macrophage polarization plays a key role in the occurrence and development of SCI, yet our understanding of its regulatory mechanisms remains insufficient. GP130 has the ability to regulate macrophage polarization in several inflammatory contexts, but its specific role in macrophage phenotype switching after SCI remains unclear. In addition, although previous studies have suggested that GP130 participates in inflammatory signaling after SCI, the spatial distribution of GP130 within injured spinal cord tissue, the temporal changes of upstream IL-6 family cytokines, and the downstream pathway alterations associated with pharmacological inhibition remain incompletely characterized. Therefore, this study established a rat SCI model and used SC144-mediated pharmacological inhibition, combined with histological localization, cytokine quantification, single-cell transcriptomic profiling, and molecular validation, to systematically investigate the regulatory effects of GP130 on macrophage polarization and functional recovery after SCI [29].

Methods and Materials

Establishment of Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) Model in Rats

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2025-008) and performed in accordance with institutional guidelines for laboratory animal care and use. Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 200–250 g were used. Rats were randomly assigned to the Sham, SCI, and SCI+SC144 groups using a random-number table, and investigators responsible for behavioral assessment and data analysis were blinded to group allocation.

Rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). After T10 laminectomy, the spinal cord was exposed without damaging the dura mater. A sterile microvascular clip was applied perpendicular to the exposed T10 spinal cord for 2 min to induce standardized compressive SCI. The same clip was used for all animals to ensure consistency of the injury severity. Sham-operated rats underwent the same laminectomy procedure without clip compression. After surgery, rats were kept warm until recovery from anesthesia, and wound healing, body weight, food and water intake, urinary retention, and hindlimb movement were monitored daily. Manual bladder expression was performed twice daily until spontaneous urination recovered. Prophylactic anti-infection treatment was administered for 3 days, and carprofen was administered subcutaneously for 2–3 days for postoperative analgesia.

For GP130 inhibition, SC144 was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, HY-15614). SC144 was dissolved in DMSO and diluted with sterile saline before use. Rats in the SCI+SC144 group received SC144 at 10 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection, with the first dose administered 30 min to 1 h after SCI, followed by once-daily administration for 7 consecutive days. Sham and SCI rats received an equal volume of vehicle containing the same final concentration of DMSO.

A total of 120 rats were used in this study. Thirty rats were used for longitudinal behavioral assessment, with 10 rats in each group. Separate tissue-collection cohorts were used according to sample-processing requirements. For histological and immunofluorescence analyses, 45 rats were used, with 3 rats per group at each time point of 6 h, 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d. These animals were perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. For molecular and single-cell-based analyses, another 45 rats were used, with 3 rats per group at each corresponding time point. Fresh spinal cord tissues were collected without paraformaldehyde fixation. Tissues collected for qRT-PCR, western blotting, and ELISA were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C, whereas tissues collected for flow cytometry and single-cell RNA sequencing at 7 d after SCI were immediately processed into single-cell suspensions.

Isolation and Culture of Primary Macrophages

Primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from adult male Sprague-Dawley rats. Rats were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium and euthanized by exsanguination before bone marrow collection. Under sterile conditions, the femurs and tibias were removed, and attached muscles and connective tissues were carefully stripped. Both ends of the bones were cut, and the bone marrow cavity was flushed with pre-cooled sterile PBS using a syringe to obtain bone marrow cell suspensions.

The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer and layered onto lymphocyte separation medium for density-gradient centrifugation. After centrifugation, the mononuclear cell layer was collected and washed two to three times with sterile PBS. Cells were then resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 20 ng/mL). Cells were seeded in tissue-culture-treated dishes or six-well plates and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

The medium was replaced every 2–3 days to remove non-adherent cells and cell debris. After 7 days of differentiation, adherent macrophages were gently collected using a sterile cell scraper rather than trypsinization to minimize cell damage and loss of surface antigens. Macrophage purity was assessed by flow cytometry using F4/80 and CD11b. Cultures with more than 90% F4/80⁺CD11b⁺ cells were used for subsequent experiments.

Induction of Cellular Polarization

After 7 days of differentiation, BMDMs were used for macrophage polarization and GP130-modulation experiments. Unless otherwise specified, all in vitro experiments were performed in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

For GP130 knockdown, BMDMs at 60–70% confluence were transfected with GP130-specific siRNA at a final concentration of 50 nM or scrambled negative-control siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 6 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with complete RPMI-1640 medium. Transfection efficiency was approximately 85%, and GP130 protein expression was reduced by approximately 70% compared with the control group, as verified by qRT-PCR and western blotting before polarization experiments.

For pharmacological inhibition of GP130, BMDMs were pretreated with SC144 (10 µM; MedChemExpress, HY-15614) or vehicle control containing the same final concentration of DMSO for 1 h before polarization induction. Cell viability after SC144 treatment was assessed using a CCK-8 assay, confirming more than 95% viability at the concentration used.

For M1 polarization, BMDMs were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) for 48 h. For M2 polarization, BMDMs were stimulated with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) for 48 h. The experimental groups were as follows: Control, M1, M2, NC-siRNA+M1, GP130-siRNA+M1, SC144 + M1, NC-siRNA+M2, GP130-siRNA+M2, and SC144 + M2. After polarization, cells and culture supernatants were collected for qRT-PCR, western blotting, ELISA, and flow cytometry analyses.

Histopathological, Ultrastructural, and Immunofluorescence Analyses of Spinal Cord Tissue

Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats aged 8–10 weeks and weighing 220–260 g were used in this study. Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 22 ± 2 °C with 50%–60% relative humidity and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal welfare.

Rats were randomly assigned to the Sham, SCI, and SCI+SC144 groups using a random-number method. For SCI modeling, rats were deeply anesthetized with intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium. After routine skin preparation and disinfection, a dorsal midline incision was made, and the paravertebral muscles were separated to expose the T10 vertebral segment. A laminectomy was performed at T10, and the exposed spinal cord was compressed using a vascular clip to establish the SCI model. In the Sham group, rats underwent the same surgical exposure and laminectomy without spinal cord compression. Successful model establishment was confirmed by immediate postoperative hindlimb paralysis and subsequent behavioral impairment.

For GP130 inhibition, rats in the SCI+SC144 group received SC144 at 10 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection. The first dose was administered 30 min–1 h after SCI, followed by once-daily administration for 7 consecutive days. SC144 was dissolved in DMSO and diluted with sterile saline before injection. Rats in the Sham and SCI groups received the corresponding vehicle solution at the same volume and schedule. Behavioral scoring was performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. Histological, ultrastructural, and immunofluorescence analyses were also performed by investigators blinded to treatment groups. Animals with postoperative death, severe wound infection, failure of model establishment, or obvious surgical complications unrelated to SCI were excluded according to predefined exclusion criteria.

After surgery, rats were placed on a heating pad until recovery from anesthesia and then returned to clean cages. Postoperative care included daily monitoring of body weight, wound healing, activity, and general condition. Manual bladder expression was performed twice daily until reflex bladder function recovered. Analgesia and supportive care were provided according to the approved institutional animal-care protocol. Animals were allocated to different experimental assays and time points using independent cohorts. Behavioral assessment was performed in an independent longitudinal cohort followed from 0 to 14 days after surgery or injury. Separate animals were used for histology, immunofluorescence, transmission electron microscopy, qRT-PCR, western blotting, ELISA, flow cytometry, and single-cell RNA sequencing at the indicated time points to avoid repeated tissue use from the same animal.

Histopathological, ultrastructural, and immunofluorescence analyses were performed using spinal cord tissues collected from independent tissue-collection cohorts at predetermined time points. At 6 h, 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d after surgery or injury, rats assigned to histological analyses were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. A spinal cord segment centered on the T10 lesion epicenter, including approximately 5 mm rostral and 5 mm caudal to the compression site, was carefully removed to include both the lesion core and adjacent peri-lesion regions.

For hematoxylin-eosin staining, spinal cord tissues were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24–48 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4–6 μm transverse sections. Sections containing the lesion epicenter and adjacent peri-lesion regions were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and observed under a light microscope. Representative images were selected from sections showing typical histopathological features of each group.

For transmission electron microscopy, approximately 1 mm³ tissue blocks were dissected from the lesion epicenter and immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Samples were then post-fixed, dehydrated, embedded in epoxy resin, cut into ultrathin sections of 60–90 nm, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a transmission electron microscope. Representative images were captured from the lesion epicenter and peri-lesion areas using consistent imaging parameters across groups. Myelin disruption, axonal structural damage, and mitochondrial swelling were marked in representative images to facilitate interpretation.

For immunofluorescence staining, spinal cord tissues were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated in 30% sucrose overnight, embedded in OCT compound, and cut into 10 μm transverse frozen sections using a cryostat. Prepared sections were sent to Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. for immunofluorescence staining and imaging. After antigen retrieval and blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GP130 and macrophage polarization markers, including CD86 and iNOS for M1-like macrophages and CD206 and Arg1 for M2-like macrophages. After washing, sections were incubated with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Images were acquired from the lesion epicenter and adjacent peri-lesion regions using a fluorescence microscope or confocal microscope at 200× magnification. For each animal, three sections containing the lesion epicenter were analyzed, and three non-overlapping fields were captured from each section using identical exposure settings across groups. Representative images were selected from sections showing typical staining patterns of each group. DAPI-stained nuclei are shown in blue, and macrophage polarization markers are shown in the corresponding fluorescence channels as indicated in the figure legends. For double-stained sections, merged images were used to evaluate the spatial distribution and overlap between GP130 and macrophage polarization markers. Quantitative co-localization analysis was not performed unless otherwise stated.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from both spinal cord tissues and cultured bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). For spinal cord samples, approximately 20–30 mg of tissue centered on the lesion epicenter was collected at the indicated time points, including 6 h, 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d after SCI. For in vitro experiments, approximately 1 × 10⁶ BMDMs per sample were collected after macrophage polarization and GP130-modulation treatments. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, China, 15596018), followed by phase separation with chloroform and RNA precipitation with isopropanol (Acros Organics, USA, 327270010). The precipitated RNA was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, dissolved in RNase-free water, and quantified. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically, and samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.1 were used for subsequent analysis. For cDNA synthesis, the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan, RR037Q) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the synthesized cDNA was stored at − 80 °C until use. RT-qPCR was performed using the TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II kit (TaKaRa, Japan, RR820S), with β-actin used as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences used were: GP130 forward (_F), CATCAACAGAACGGCATCCAG; GP130 reverse (_R), TCACTTTATCCACGGGGTCAA; IL-6_F, CATCCAGTTGCCTTCTTG; IL-6_R, TATCCAGTTTGGTAGCATCC; IL-11_F, GCTGCACAGATGAGAGACAAA; IL-11_R, GCCGAGTCTTTAACAACAGCA; LIF_F, CAGTGCCAATGCCCTCTTTA; LIF_R, GCATGGAAAGGTGGGAAATC; CD86_F, GATTGCAGGTCCCAGTTCACTTC; CD86_R, CCACTGTCCTGCTTGGACTCAC; iNOS_F, GTGAAGGGACTGAGCTGTTAGA; iNOS_R, GCACTTCTGCTCCAAATCCA; CD206_F, GGGTTCACCTGGAGTGATGG; CD206_R, ATTGTCTTGAGGAGCGGGTG; Arg1_F, CTACCTGCTGGGAAGGAAG; Arg1_R, GTCCTGAAAGTAGCCCTGTC; β-actin_F, GTGGGGCGCCCCAGGCACCA; β-actin_R, CTTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGATTTC; GAPDH_F, AAGCCCATCACCATCTTCCA; GAPDH_R, ATGGCATGGACTGTGGTCAT.

Western Blot

Protein extraction was performed using RIPA lysis buffer, and the concentration of the extracts was determined using a Bradford assay. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with loading buffer, boiled for denaturation, and resolved by SDS-PAGE according to the molecular weight of the target proteins. The separated proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose or PVDF membrane, which was then activated and assembled into a transfer “sandwich” for electroblotting at 4 °C. After transfer, the membranes were washed with TBST buffer and blocked with 5% skim milk or BSA in TBST to prevent non-specific binding. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in the blocking solution overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing with TBST and incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in the blocking solution for 1–2 h at room temperature. Antibodies against the following proteins were used: GP130 (32186-1-AP; Proteintech; Wuhan, China; 1:3000), IL-11(ab187167; Abcam; Cambridge, UK; 1:1000), CD86 (13395-1-AP; Proteintech; Wuhan, China; 1:2000), iNOS (ab178945; Abcam; Cambridge, UK; 1:2000), IL-6 (AMC0864; Thermo Fisher; USA; 1:2000), TNF-α (ab307164; Abcam; Cambridge, UK; 1:1000), CD206 (18704-1-AP; Proteintech; Wuhan, China; 1:1000), Arg1 (16001-1-AP; Proteintech; Wuhan, China; 1:5000), IL-10 (60269-1-Ig; Proteintech; Wuhan, China; 1:2000), TGF-β (21898-1-AP; Proteintech; Wuhan, China; 1:2000), STAT1 (ab109461; Abcam; Cambridge, UK; 1:3000), p38 (ab316937; Abcam; Cambridge, UK; 1:1000), STAT3 (ab68153; Abcam; Cambridge, UK; 1:1000), p85 (60225-1-Ig; Proteintech; China; 1:5000), β-actin (ab8227; Abcam; Cambridge, UK; 1:1000), as well as horseradish peroxidase-labeled antibodies against rabbit (C31460100; Thermo Fisher; Waltham, MA, USA; 1:5000) or mouse (C31430100; Thermo Fisher; Waltham, MA, USA; 1:5000) immunoglobulins. The immune complexes were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence reagent and exposed to a digital imaging system. The relative expression levels of the target proteins were quantified by densitometry using image analysis software, normalized to an internal control, and statistically analyzed for differences and temporal dynamics across various groups using appropriate software.

Flow Cytometry

At 7 days after SCI or sham surgery, spinal cord segments centered on the T10 lesion epicenter (including approximately 5 mm rostral and caudal) were collected from independent cohorts of rats (n = 3 per group). Tissues were mechanically minced and enzymatically digested in RPMI-1640 containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type I, 0.5 mg/mL dispase, and 50 µg/mL DNase I at 37 °C for 45 min with gentle agitation. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer and washed with PBS containing 2% heat-inactivated FBS. Single-cell suspensions were blocked with anti-rat CD16/CD32 for 10 min and then stained with fluorescent-conjugated antibodies against CD45, CD11b, CD86, CD206, iNOS, and Arg1 for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Appropriate isotype controls were included. Cells were washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer. Data were collected from at least 1 × 10⁵ live events per sample and analyzed using FlowJo software. Biological replicates were performed independently three times for each condition.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The levels of inflammatory cytokines in spinal cord tissue homogenates and macrophage culture supernatants were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocols without modification. Rat TNF-α (H052-1-1), IL-1β (H002-1–2), IL-6 (H007-1-1), and IL-10 (H009-1–2) ELISA kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Briefly, spinal cord tissue samples were homogenized on ice and centrifuged to collect the supernatants. Macrophage culture supernatants were collected after polarization and GP130-modulation treatments and centrifuged to remove cell debris. Standards and samples were added to pre-coated 96-well plates and incubated according to the kit instructions. After washing, enzyme-labeled reagents and substrate solutions were added sequentially. The reaction was terminated using stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Infinite 200 full-wavelength microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated for each assay.

Single-cell RNA Sequencing

Fresh spinal cord tissues were collected at 7 days after surgery or injury from the Sham, SCI, and SCI+SC144 groups. The collected tissue segment was centered on the T10 lesion epicenter and included adjacent peri-lesion regions. Tissues were processed immediately after collection without paraformaldehyde fixation to preserve cell viability and RNA integrity.

Spinal cord tissues were mechanically minced into small pieces on ice and enzymatically dissociated in a digestion solution containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (50 µg/mL) in calcium- and magnesium-free PBS. The tissues were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle agitation. This dissociation solution was similar to that used for flow cytometry, but the digestion time and handling procedures were optimized separately for single-cell RNA sequencing to maximize cell viability and transcriptomic quality. After digestion, the cell suspension was gently triturated, filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer, and washed with PBS containing 2% heat-inactivated FBS. Red blood cell lysis buffer was used when erythrocyte contamination was observed.

Cell concentration and viability were assessed using trypan blue staining. Only single-cell suspensions with viability greater than 85% were used for library preparation. Single-cell capture, cell lysis, barcoding, and reverse transcription were performed using the 10× Genomics Chromium platform according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, single cells were encapsulated into gel beads-in-emulsion (GEMs), where cell lysis and reverse transcription occurred. The resulting cDNA was amplified and used for library construction, followed by high-throughput sequencing. Downstream data analysis was performed using Seurat, including quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, cell-type assignment, differential expression analysis, and pathway enrichment analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. Error bars in the figures represent SD. The definition of n is specified in each figure legend; for animal experiments, n represents the number of animals, and for in vitro experiments, n represents the number of independent biological experiments.

For comparisons between two independent groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used when data were normally distributed; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups at a single time point, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. For experiments involving both group and time factors, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used. Behavioral data, including BBB scores, inclined plane angles, and inclined plane scores measured repeatedly over time, were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons.

For time-course molecular experiments involving multiple groups and multiple time points, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was applied. For scRNA-seq cell-proportion analysis, biological replicates rather than individual cells were used as the statistical unit. Differential gene expression and pathway-enrichment analyses were performed using the corresponding bioinformatic pipelines, and adjusted P values were used where applicable. Exact P values were reported where possible; otherwise, statistical significance was indicated as follows: *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Compared with the Sham group, rats in the SCI group showed markedly reduced BBB scores, inclined plane angles, and inclined plane scores, indicating severe locomotor impairment (Fig. 1B). Animals treated with the GP130 inhibitor SC144 showed improved functional recovery compared with untreated SCI rats, as reflected by higher BBB scores and improved inclined plane performance during the 14-day observation period (Fig. 1B).

H&E staining showed lesion-associated tissue disruption after SCI, including hemorrhage, cellular infiltration-like accumulation, vacuole-like tissue rarefaction, and neuronal loss-like morphological changes (Fig. 1C). Compared with the untreated SCI group, the SCI+SC144 group showed relatively preserved tissue architecture and reduced lesion-associated structural disruption (Fig. 1C).

TEM analysis showed ultrastructural abnormalities in the SCI group, including myelin disruption, axonal structural damage, and mitochondrial swelling in the lesion region (Fig. 1D). In the SCI+SC144 group, ultrastructural damage appeared less severe, with relatively better preservation of myelin and axonal morphology (Fig. 1D).

Changes in GP130-Related Signaling and Macrophage Polarization Markers after SCI

GP130 is the common signal-transducing receptor subunit for the IL-6 cytokine family and is closely associated with JAK/STAT3-mediated inflammatory signaling. Because IL-6/STAT3 signaling has been implicated in neuroinflammation, macrophage activation, and tissue repair after SCI, we focused on GP130 as a potential upstream regulator of macrophage polarization in the injured spinal cord. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of GP130 could modulate macrophage phenotype and improve functional recovery after SCI.

To evaluate GP130-related inflammatory signaling after SCI, we first examined the expression of GP130 and its related ligands in spinal cord tissues. qRT-PCR analysis showed that SCI increased the mRNA expression of GP130, IL-6, and IL-11 at different time points after injury (Fig. 2A). These qRT-PCR data were normalized to β-actin. Western blot analysis of spinal cord tissues collected at 7 d after SCI showed that SCI enhanced STAT3 activation, as indicated by an increased p-STAT3/STAT3 ratio (Fig. 2B). SC144 treatment reduced the p-STAT3/STAT3 ratio compared with the untreated SCI group, indicating inhibition of downstream STAT3 activation (Fig. 2B). In contrast, total GP130 protein expression was not clearly decreased after SC144 treatment, suggesting that SC144 mainly inhibited GP130-mediated signaling rather than reducing total GP130 protein abundance (Fig. 2B). In addition, IL-6 and IL-11 protein levels were increased after SCI and were reduced after SC144 treatment, further supporting the inhibitory effect of SC144 on SCI-induced inflammatory signaling (Fig. 2C).

Immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate macrophage polarization marker expression in the lesion region (Fig. 2D). CD86-positive staining, representing M1-like pro-inflammatory macrophage marker expression, was increased after SCI, whereas SC144 treatment reduced CD86-positive signals (Fig. 2D). In contrast, CD206-positive staining, representing M2-like reparative macrophage marker expression, was relatively increased after SC144 treatment compared with the untreated SCI group (Fig. 2D). Because the current immunofluorescence images show macrophage polarization marker distribution rather than GP130/macrophage marker double staining, these findings were described as marker distribution patterns rather than definitive GP130/CD86 or GP130/CD206 co-localization.

IHC staining of paraffin-embedded spinal cord sections showed increased GP130 expression in the lesion region after SCI, while SC144 treatment did not markedly reduce total GP130 abundance (Fig. 2E). ELISA analysis of spinal cord tissue homogenates showed a similar pattern of GP130 levels among groups (Fig. 2F). Together, these results suggest that SCI activates GP130-related inflammatory signaling and enhances M1-like pro-inflammatory macrophage marker expression. SC144 mainly suppresses downstream STAT3 activation and inflammatory cytokine expression, rather than directly reducing total GP130 expression, and promotes a shift toward M2-like reparative marker expression.

Unsupervised clustering and cell-type assignment identified eight major recovered cell populations, including monocyte/macrophage-related myeloid cells, bone marrow stromal-like cells, endothelial cells, pericytes, Schwann cell-like cells, neuronal cells, ciliated ependymal-like cells, and oligodendrocytes (Fig. 3A). In Fig. 3B, dot size represents the percentage of cells expressing each marker gene, and color intensity represents the average expression level. The transcriptional heterogeneity of these cell populations was further supported by the heatmap of highly variable genes (Fig. 3C). Analysis of relative cell proportions showed that SCI was associated with an increased proportion of monocyte/macrophage-related myeloid cells among recovered viable cells, indicating a prominent myeloid-cell response after injury (Fig. 3D). The proportions in Fig. 3D were calculated as the number of cells assigned to each cell type divided by the total number of recovered cells in each group.

Because enzymatic dissociation of injured spinal cord tissue may preferentially recover immune and stromal cells while underrepresenting fragile neural or glial populations, these percentages should be interpreted as relative proportions among recovered viable cells rather than absolute in situ cellular composition of spinal cord tissue. In addition, cell labels such as Schwann cell-like, bone marrow stromal-like, and ciliated ependymal-like populations were assigned based on marker-gene expression and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of their absolute abundance in intact spinal cord tissue. The apparent enrichment of Schwann cell-like cells and the low recovery of OPC/astrocyte populations may reflect dissociation-related and marker-based annotation bias.

Because PI3K-AKT signaling was identified as one of the enriched pathways, we further examined AKT, phosphorylated AKT, PI3K, and phosphorylated PI3K in spinal cord tissues. SCI increased the p-AKT/AKT and p-PI3K/PI3K ratios, whereas SC144 treatment reduced PI3K and AKT phosphorylation compared with the SCI group (Fig. 8D). These results suggest that GP130 inhibition may attenuate PI3K-AKT pathway activation after SCI.

Finally, Venn diagram analysis was performed to identify overlapping genes among the PI3K-AKT, MAPK, and Rap1 signaling pathways. Fourteen overlapping genes were shared among these three pathways, including Pdgfra, Angpt2, Ngfr, Pdgfrb, Flt1, Fgf23, Tek, Igf1, Fgf19, Pgf, Fgf7, Fgf18, Pdgfa, and Egfr (Fig. 8E). These overlapping genes suggest potential crosstalk among PI3K-AKT, MAPK, and Rap1 signaling pathways during GP130-related macrophage regulation.

Together, these results suggest that GP130 is associated with macrophage polarization through multiple inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly the PI3K-AKT pathway. However, the pathway enrichment, pathway-intervention, and overlapping-gene analyses should be interpreted as mechanistic clues rather than definitive evidence of causality. Further pathway-specific inhibition, activation, or rescue experiments are required to confirm the precise signaling mechanism.

Discussion

The results of this study provide insight into the therapeutic potential of GP130 pathway modulation for SCI. The major findings are as follows: first, GP130-related inflammatory signaling was activated after compressive SCI; second, pharmacological inhibition of GP130 with SC144 improved locomotor recovery and reduced lesion-associated tissue damage; third, SC144 reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and inhibited downstream STAT3 activation without clearly decreasing total GP130 abundance; fourth, scRNA-seq revealed a prominent myeloid-cell response and transcriptional heterogeneity of monocyte/macrophage-related populations after SCI; and fifth, GP130 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition suppressed M1-like pro-inflammatory marker expression and promoted M2-like reparative marker expression in primary BMDMs.

GP130 is a crucial component of the IL-6 receptor complex and plays an important role in inflammatory responses and metabolic regulation [30]. In the present study, GP130 signaling was upregulated after SCI, consistent with previous reports linking GP130-related signaling to chronic inflammation and multimorbidity. The canonical STAT3 pathway downstream of GP130 has been implicated in tissue metabolism, regeneration, cell survival, and repair responses after injury [31, 32]. In our study, SCI increased IL-6 family cytokine expression and enhanced STAT3 phosphorylation, whereas SC144 reduced the p-STAT3/STAT3 ratio and decreased inflammatory cytokine expression. Notably, total GP130 protein abundance was not clearly reduced after SC144 treatment. This suggests that SC144 mainly interferes with downstream GP130/STAT3 signaling activity rather than directly suppressing total GP130 expression.

The behavioral and histological findings support the functional relevance of GP130 pathway modulation after compressive SCI. SC144-treated rats showed improved BBB scores and inclined plane performance compared with untreated SCI rats. H&E staining and TEM further showed relatively preserved tissue architecture and less severe ultrastructural disruption after SC144 treatment. These results suggest that pharmacological inhibition of GP130-related signaling may attenuate secondary inflammatory injury and contribute to functional recovery after SCI.

Macrophage polarization is closely associated with inflammatory progression and repair after SCI [33]. M1-like macrophages are generally associated with pro-inflammatory mediator production, whereas M2-like macrophages are linked to anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair responses [10]. In this study, SC144 reduced CD86/iNOS-positive M1-like marker expression and relatively increased CD206/Arg1-positive M2-like marker expression in vivo. In vitro, both GP130-siRNA and SC144 decreased M1-like markers, including CD86, iNOS, IL-6, and TNF-α, while increasing M2-like markers, including CD206, Arg1, IL-10, and TGF-β, in primary BMDMs. These findings support a role for GP130 signaling in regulating macrophage phenotype. Although transcription factors such as PPAR-γ and STAT6 have been reported to participate in macrophage polarization [34], further mechanistic experiments are needed to determine whether these factors are directly involved in GP130-mediated macrophage regulation in SCI.

The scRNA-seq analysis provided additional information on the cellular and transcriptional heterogeneity of the injured spinal cord. The data showed a prominent myeloid-cell response after SCI and identified several monocyte/macrophage-related subpopulations with distinct transcriptional features. However, the interpretation of cell proportions should be cautious. Enzymatic dissociation of injured spinal cord tissue may preferentially recover immune and stromal cells while underrepresenting fragile neural or glial populations. Therefore, the reported percentages reflect the relative composition of recovered viable cells rather than the absolute in situ cellular composition of spinal cord tissue.

Pathway analysis suggested that GP130-related macrophage regulation may involve PI3K-AKT, MAPK, Rap1, and JAK/STAT-related signaling pathways. Western blot validation showed that SC144 reduced PI3K and AKT phosphorylation after SCI, supporting the involvement of PI3K-AKT pathway modulation. Because PI3K/AKT signaling is closely associated with macrophage activation, inflammatory amplification, and tissue repair, these findings suggest that GP130 may regulate macrophage polarization at least partly through PI3K-AKT pathway modulation. However, pathway enrichment and overlapping-gene analyses should be interpreted as mechanistic clues rather than direct evidence of causality. Among these pathways, JAK/STAT3 is the most canonical downstream branch of GP130 signaling, and previous SCI studies have shown that JAK/STAT-related signaling participates in secondary inflammatory injury, glial activation, and functional recovery after SCI [22, 35]. MAPK signaling is another GP130-associated pathway and has been implicated in SCI-related inflammation, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and tissue damage [36, 37]. The PI3K/AKT axis is also involved in neuroprotection, angiogenesis, axonal regeneration, and inflammatory regulation after SCI [38, 39]. Unlike previous SCI studies that mainly investigated the individual modulation of the JAK/STAT3, MAPK, or PI3K/AKT pathway, our findings suggest that GP130 inhibition may coordinately influence multiple interconnected signaling pathways during macrophage polarization, indicating that GP130 may function as an upstream regulatory node rather than acting through a single downstream signaling cascade. Compared with these previous studies focusing on individual pathways, our findings suggest that GP130 inhibition may be associated with coordinated changes in macrophage polarization and multiple downstream or interacting pathways, including JAK/STAT3, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT. Nevertheless, these pathways should be interpreted as associated mechanisms rather than definitive causal mediators, and further pathway-specific rescue or genetic validation experiments are needed.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study focused mainly on the acute and subacute phases after SCI, and the long-term effects of GP130 inhibition on functional recovery remain to be determined. Second, only one compressive SCI model and one SC144 administration schedule were used; therefore, future studies should evaluate different injury models, doses, and treatment windows. Third, incomplete dissociation of spinal cord tissue or excessive cell damage during single-cell preparation may have biased the apparent proportions of recovered cell populations. Fourth, cell detachment and staining procedures used for flow cytometry may affect surface-antigen detection and influence quantification of marker-positive cells. Finally, although this study identified changes in macrophage polarization and associated signaling pathways, further pathway-specific inhibition, activation, or rescue experiments are required to confirm the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying GP130-mediated macrophage regulation [40].

In conclusion, this study suggests that GP130 inhibition by SC144 improves functional recovery after compressive SCI, reduces pro-inflammatory signaling in the lesion region, and modulates macrophage polarization both in vivo and in vitro. These findings support GP130-related signaling as a potential target for regulating the inflammatory microenvironment after SCI.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that GP130 plays a critical role in regulating macrophage polarization following spinal cord injury. GP130 expression is significantly upregulated after injury and is closely associated with the inflammatory microenvironment of the injured spinal cord. Pharmacological inhibition of GP130 effectively suppresses pro-inflammatory macrophage activation and promotes a shift toward a reparative phenotype, thereby improving functional recovery after SCI. Mechanistically, GP130-mediated regulation of macrophage polarization involves multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt, JAK/STAT, NF-κB, and MAPK. These findings highlight GP130 as a potential therapeutic target for modulating neuroinflammation and improving neurological outcomes after spinal cord injury.
