Fabrication and characterization of PIB gels as effective adsorbents for methylene blue
Authors: Xiaohang Peng, Hezhong Zhu, Jichen Wu, Jieqiong Ding, Binhua Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The removal of cationic dyes from wastewater remains challenging due to the trade-off between adsorption efficiency and post-treatment solid–liquid separation. In this work, a high-performance thermosensitive polyisobutylene (PIB) gel adsorbent was prepared via the free radical emulsion polymerization of a hypercrosslinked N-isopropylacrylamide-co-butyl methacrylate polymer. Comprehensive characterization including Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and rheological tests confirmed that the gel featured a porous structure, exceptional thermal stability, and a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) of approximately 32°C. The adsorption performance of the PIB gel for methylene blue (MB) was systematically investigated, achieving a removal rate of 88.5% and the adsorption capacity rose to 4.85 mg g−1. The experimental data were best fitted by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and the Slips isotherm model, suggesting that the MB adsorption process was predominantly governed by chemisorption accompanied by heterogeneous adsorption behavior. The adsorption of MB by the gel was driven by the synergistic effects of electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole interactions and physical pore interception. Moreover, the PIB gel exhibited remarkable reusability, maintaining 72.42% of its initial MB removal efficiency after five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles. Notably, its thermosensitive property enables efficient solid–liquid separation through temperature-induced phase transition, which overcomes the separation difficulty of traditional adsorbents in practical applications. All the above results demonstrate that the PIB gel holds great potential to be a practical and efficient adsorbent for the remediation of MB-contaminated wastewater.

Introduction

Methylene blue (MB) is a typical cationic organic dye widely used in textiles,1 papermaking, leather processing, and printing due to its strong coloring ability,2 high chemical stability, and low cost. A large fraction of unfixed MB is discharged into industrial effluents and surface waters.3 Because MB is highly soluble and its sky-blue solution exhibits high chromaticity even at trace levels, it is easily detectable in aquatic environments. More importantly, its cationic structure binds strongly to negatively charged surfaces, leading to significant persistence and bioaccumulation.4 Once in water, MB reduces light penetration, inhibits photosynthesis,5,6 and disrupts the oxygen balance, thereby interfering with the metabolism and reproduction of aquatic organisms. Ecotoxicological studies show that MB exhibits acute toxicity at microgram-per-liter levels, causing oxidative stress, membrane damage, and genotoxicity,7,8 alongside potential carcinogenic and developmental risks to humans. Therefore, the efficient, cost-effective, and sustainable removal of MB is essential for ecosystem restoration and water safety.

Current remediation technologies for MB include biological, chemical, and physical methods. While biological methods (e.g., microbial degradation,9 phytoremediation10) are environmentally friendly, they are generally slow and highly sensitive to temperature, pH, salinity, and heavy metals in real wastewater. Similarly, chemical methods (e.g., Fenton reactions,11 advanced oxidation processes,12 photocatalysis13) face limitations such as strict operational conditions, high energy consumption, and incomplete mineralization that leaves secondary residues.14 Given these drawbacks, adsorption emerges as a highly promising alternative due to its simplicity and efficiency.15,16 The performance of this method fundamentally depends on the design of the adsorbent.17 Generally, an excellent adsorbent requires a well-developed pore structure, rapid adsorption kinetics, good mechanical stability, ease of separation, environmental friendliness, and low cost.18 Specifically for MB, an ideal adsorbent should have abundant negatively charged adsorption sites to enable strong electrostatic attraction.19 Additionally, it must exhibit resistance to acidic/alkaline and high-salinity conditions, high selectivity in complex wastewater, a porous and hydrophilic structure to facilitate mass transfer, and potential for further functional modification20

Polyisobutylene (PIB)-based thermosensitive gels inherently fulfill the general criteria of an excellent adsorbent and offer unique material advantages. First, the PIB gel exhibits excellent chemical stability and reusability, maintaining high performance over repeated adsorption–desorption cycles.21 Second, its proven biocompatibility (e.g., as a vascular embolic agent22) and lack of secondary pollution align perfectly with green chemistry principles. Third, and most distinctively, its temperature-responsive nature enables an elegant “adsorption–separation integration” strategy.23 At room temperature (below the lower critical solution temperature, LCST), the gel swells to capture target molecules; upon gentle heating above the LCST, it rapidly contracts into a compact hydrophobic block.24 This transition simultaneously achieves spontaneous solid–liquid separation, completely eliminating the need for energy-intensive centrifugation or membrane filtration. These intrinsic properties make PIB gels a potentially excellent adsorbent specifically for methylene blue (MB).25 Because MB is a typical cationic dye, the PIB gel can be chemically modified to introduce abundant negatively charged groups, generating strong electrostatic attraction to ensure high capture efficiency even in the presence of competing impurities. Furthermore, the gel's porous, hydrophilic structure below the LCST allows deep penetration of water and bulky MB molecules, fully exploiting internal binding sites and facilitating mass transfer.26 Following capture, the aforementioned thermal contraction above the LCST physically encapsulates the MB within the compact matrix while expelling water, providing an elegant mechanism for both high-capacity adsorption and highly efficient recovery.

Despite these favorable characteristics, systematic research on PIB-based thermosensitive gels for MB adsorption remains nascent. This work reports the synthesis of functionalized PIB-based thermosensitive gels designed to optimize pore structure and surface functionality for the efficient removal of MB from aqueous solutions (Fig. 1). We systematically investigated MB adsorption under diverse conditions (pH, temperature, concentration, salinity) and elucidated the governing mechanisms, including electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and pore filling.27,28 The study culminates in an evaluation of the gel's selectivity and long-term cyclic stability within a simulated industrial wastewater matrix.29

Materials and methods

Materials

N-Isopropyl acrylamide (NIPAM, 98% containing stabilizer MEHQ, Shanghai Maclin Biotechnology Co., Ltd), N, N' -methylenebis (acrylamide) (MBA, 99%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), potassium persulfate (KPS, 99%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd), butyl methacrylate (BMA, 99%, Shanghai Maclin Biotechnology Co., Ltd), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd). All the chemical reagents used in the experiment were of analytical grade.

Instruments

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on an X-ray diffractometer (XRD-6100, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out on a thermogravimetric analyzer (HCT-1S, Beijing Hengjiu Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). The morphology of samples was observed using a scanning electron microscope (EVO, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Freeze-drying was performed on a vacuum freeze dryer (LC-10N-50A, Shanghai Lichen Bangxi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Rheological measurements were performed on a rotational rheometer (RH-30, Baosheng Industry Development Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). The specific surface area and pore size distribution were determined using an automatic specific surface area and porosity analyzer (ASAP 2460, Micromeritics, Norcross, GA, USA). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on an FT-IR spectrometer (Spectrum Two, PerkinElmer (Shanghai) Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra were measured using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Cary 60, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Preparation of PIB gel

The gel was synthesized via free-radical emulsion polymerization under a nitrogen atmosphere (Fig. 2). Initially, N-isopropylacrylamide (2.2600 g, 19.972 mmol), butyl methacrylate (0.168 mL, 1.035 mmol), N, N′-methylenebisacrylamide (0.0324 g, 0.21 mmol), and SDS (0.0320 g) were dissolved in ultrapure water (160 mL) in a three-necked round-bottom flask. Separately, an initiator solution was prepared by dissolving potassium persulfate (KPS, 0.0950 g) in ultrapure water (10.0 mL). The monomer solution was heated to 40°C and stirred under a continuous nitrogen purge for one hour to ensure complete dissolution and deoxygenation. Subsequently, the temperature was raised to 70.0°C, at which point the KPS solution was added to initiate the polymerization. The appearance of a faint blue opalescence (indicating the formation of nanosized polymer particles via Tyndall effect) was taken as the initiation point of the polymerization. The reaction was then allowed to proceed for another 4.5 hours at 70.0°C under a nitrogen atmosphere with constant stirring. After completion, the reaction was terminated by removing the heat source, and the mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature. The reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and transferred to dialysis tubing (MWCO: 14 000 Da). The sample was dialyzed against ultra-pure water for 72 hours, with the water replaced every 12 hours to remove small molecules and residual impurities. The resulting purified gel was then flash-frozen at −80°C and subsequently lyophilized for 10 hours. The obtained white, spongy solid was stored in a desiccator for later use.

Characterization of PIB gel

The chemical structure, crystalline properties, elemental composition, pore structure, thermal stability, surface charge, particle size, and viscosity of the synthesized PIB gel were systematically characterized using multiple techniques. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was performed in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 to identify surface functional groups (e.g., carboxyl, hydroxyl) and verify successful functionalization. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded between 5° and 80° (2θ) at a scanning rate of 5° min−1 to analyze the crystalline structure and phase composition. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) determined elemental composition (C, O, N, etc.) and chemical states of surface atoms, with high-resolution scans for quantitative analysis. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) measurements were conducted via N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K (after degassing at 105°C for 6 h) to calculate specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter. Thermogravimetric analysis (TG) was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere from 30°C to 800°C at 10°C min−1 to evaluate thermal stability and degradation behavior. Zeta potential was measured in aqueous solutions (pH 3–13) to assess surface charge properties related to electrostatic interactions with cationic MB. Particle size distribution and average diameter were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) at 25°C (0.1 mg mL−1 in deionized water). Viscosity was tested using a rotational viscometer at 25°C with a shear rate range of 10–100 s−1, to evaluate the gel's rheological behavior and structural integrity.

Adsorption isotherms

Adsorption isotherms are fundamental for evaluating adsorbent performance, revealing trends and transformations during the adsorption process.32,33 These curves describe the relationship between the amount of adsorbate retained on the adsorbent surface at equilibrium qe and its equilibrium concentration in the solution Ce under constant temperature conditions.34 They facilitate the determination of optimal adsorption concentrations and elucidate adsorption mechanisms, quantify monolayer adsorption capacity, and predict adsorbent efficacy.35 Common isotherm models include the following.36

Langmuir isotherm

The Langmuir isotherm37 is a relationship curve used to describe adsorption phenomena based on the theory of monolayer adsorption, which assumes a uniform adsorbent surface, identical energy levels at all adsorption sites, and no interactions between adsorbate molecules.38Eqn (4) represents the linear transformation of the Langmuir isotherm.4qm represents adsorption capacity, qe denotes monolayer saturation adsorption capacity, Ce is the equilibrium concentration, KL is the adsorption energy-related constant.

The dimensionless separation factor RL, also termed the equilibrium parameter, is employed to evaluate the adsorption process, as defined by eqn (5).5

The magnitude of the dimensionless separation factor RL determines both the adsorption isotherm type and process characteristics: an RL > 1 indicates an unfavorable adsorption process, RL = 1 corresponds to linear adsorption, 0 < RL < 1 signifies reversible adsorption, and RL = 0 denotes irreversible adsorption.

Freundlich isotherm

The Freundlich isotherm model describes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces, where adsorption sites with stronger binding affinity are preferentially occupied. Adsorbate loading increases progressively with rising ambient adsorbate concentration, with equilibrium concentration (Ce) directly correlating to adsorption capacity (qe). The partition coefficient Kf reflects the relative adsorption capability of the adsorbent, where higher Kf values indicate stronger adsorption capacity. The adsorption intensity is governed by the exponent nf in the equation. According to the linearized Freundlich plot of ln qeversus ln Ce, the slope corresponds to 1/nf and the intercept equals ln Kf. An adsorption process is considered thermodynamically favorable when nf > 1, whereas nf < 1 suggests an unfavorable irreversible adsorption mechanism.

Sips isotherm

The Sips isotherm is widely applied in aqueous contaminant adsorption studies, effectively characterizing adsorbent behavior toward diverse pollutants. This hybrid model integrates features of both Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms: it accounts for multilayer adsorption at low concentrations while describing monolayer saturation at high adsorbate concentrations. Desorption and regeneration of adsorbent

The reusability of the adsorbate was investigated through a series of adsorption–desorption experiments.41 In a typical cycle, 30 mg of adsorbent was first saturated with MB by stirring in a 10 mg L−1 solution at 25°C for 2 h. The saturated adsorbent was separated via centrifugation (8000 rpm, 10 min) and subsequently lyophilized. After separation by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 10 min) and lyophilization, desorption was performed by immersing the MB-loaded adsorbent in acidic deionized water (pH 2–3, adjusted with 0.1 mol L−1 HCl) at 50°C for 4 h. Following desorption, the regenerated adsorbent was recovered by lyophilization. This procedure was defined as one adsorption–desorption cycle. The experiment was repeated for five consecutive cycles, with the MB adsorption capacity calculated after each cycle to determine the reusability of the material.

The XRD pattern (Fig. 3C) reveals characteristic diffraction features of PIB gel. With diffraction angle 2θ (in degrees) as the abscissa (0–90°) and relative intensity as the ordinate, a prominently broadened diffraction peak appears in the 10–30° 2θ range, centered near 20°. This peak broadening signifies amorphous domains or nanoscale crystalline structures, indicating low crystallinity and poor structural ordering. Beyond 30°, the intensity progressively diminishes. The high-angle region (50–80°) exhibits weak fluctuations without sharp peaks, further confirming the dominantly amorphous nature of PIB gel with only minor disordered crystalline signals, consistent with its typical non-crystalline microstructure. These XRD-derived properties confirm a disordered molecular chain structure with abundant pores and free volume. Within this amorphous framework, polar functional groups in PIB gel become fully exposed, enabling enhanced interactions with methylene blue molecules via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction. This structural characteristic significantly increases the methylene blue adsorption capacity of PIB gel.

XPS was employed to determine the surface chemical environment of PIB gel. As shown in Fig. 3D, distinct C 1s, O 1s, and N 1s signals are observed. Deconvolution of the C 1s spectrum reveals peaks at 284.4 eV (C–C backbone) and 286.4 eV (polar C–O/C–N functional groups). The N 1s spectrum exhibits a peak near 401 eV, assigned to C–N bonds, indicating nitrogen participation in amine/amide groups. The O 1s peak at 532 eV corresponds to C–O bonds, confirming carbonyl formation. These characteristic binding energies demonstrate the presence of C–C, C–O, and C–N bonds, consistent with nitrogen/oxygen-containing organic compounds or aggregates.

The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms (Fig. 3E) reveal a Type V isotherm with a distinct hysteresis loop, which is characteristic of mesoporous materials. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis quantifies the material's textural properties, showing a specific surface area of 3.67 m2 g−1, an average pore diameter of 0.665 nm, and a total pore volume of 6.11 × 10−4 cm3 g−1. The isotherm's steep uptake at low relative pressures (P/P0 < 0.1) indicates monolayer-multilayer adsorption, while the hysteresis loop at higher pressures confirms the presence of mesopores due to capillary condensation. The inset pore size distribution curve further corroborates this, showing a primary concentration of pores in the mesoporous range. These results collectively demonstrate that the PIB gel possesses a well-defined mesoporous architecture. This structure, providing abundant adsorption sites, establishes a solid foundation for its high efficacy in pollutant removal applications.43

Thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) analysis under N2 atmosphere (Fig. 3F) was carried out.44 The TG curve shows that the material first has a slight weight loss below 100°C, due to adsorbed moisture evaporation. When the temperature rises to 300–500°C, significant weight loss happens. The DTG curve has a prominent peak at 418°C,45–48 corresponding to major decomposition from polymer chain scission. The onset decomposition temperature around 300°C means the material has favorable thermal degradability, remaining stable at relatively high temperatures before substantial decomposition, which is beneficial for thermal-related applications.

The SEM images (Fig. 4) illustrate the morphological changes of the PIB gel before and after adsorption. Before adsorption (Fig. 4A), the gel exhibited a well-defined porous structure with pore diameters ranging from approximately 42 to 75 µm. After adsorption (Fig. 4B), the gel underwent significant shrinkage, resulting in a more compact and irregular architecture: the remaining cavities became fewer in number, highly irregular in shape and size, and were surrounded by thicker pore walls. Consequently, no uniform pore diameter could be assigned to the post-adsorption structure. This combination of an initially ordered porous framework that collapses into a dense, amorphous morphology provides favorable conditions for adsorption and related applications.

Gel rheological properties

Fig. 6 depicts the relationship between temperature and viscosity of the PIB gel. The gel exhibited significant temperature-dependent viscosity changes, characteristic of a thermoresponsive transition. At lower temperatures (20.1–26.7°C), the gel behaved as a low-viscosity liquid, with viscosity increasing only gradually from 5871 mPa s. This was followed by a steep increase in viscosity between 26.7°C and 34.5°C, with the most rapid rise observed at approximately 32.3°C. This critical transition temperature coincides with the onset of turbidity at 31°C, indicating a structural reorganization driven by enhanced intermolecular interactions and restricted chain mobility. During this transition, pronounced viscosity fluctuations were observed, reflecting the dynamic nature of the molecular rearrangement. Upon further heating to 34°C, the viscosity surged to 24 739 mPa s. Beyond 35°C, the slope of the viscosity curve gradually decreased, and the viscosity continued to rise slowly toward an asymptotic plateau (from approximately 23 000 mPa s at 32°C to 26 000 mPa s at 40°C). This moderate post-transition increase is attributed to further hydrophobic aggregation of PNIPAM and PBMA segments, leading to progressive network densification. The overall behavior nevertheless confirms the completion of the gelation process, with the system reaching a highly viscous, stable gel state.49

pH effect

Solution pH is a critical factor influencing adsorption efficiency, as it affects the adsorbate speciation, the surface charge properties, and the particle size of the PIB gel adsorbent. As shown in Fig. 7A, the particle size of the PIB gel increased gradually from 178 nm to 198 nm as the pH rose from 1 to 11. Meanwhile, Fig. 7B show that the gel exhibited a weak negative zeta potential across the entire pH range (pH 1–11), with a minimum (most negative) value around pH 5, followed by a slight increase but remaining negative at higher pH. For adsorption performance (Fig. 7C), the adsorption of cationic methylene blue (MB) by the PIB gel exhibited a distinctive U-shaped trend across the acidic pH range, reaching its minimum at pH 3 and showing higher uptake at both pH 1 and pH 5–11. The diminished capacity at pH 3 (1.52 mg g; 21.36% removal) was primarily attributed to weak electrostatic attraction combined with intense competitive inhibition from H+ ions for binding sites (e.g., carboxylate and amine groups). Conversely, at pH 1, despite the excessive H+ concentration, the complete protonation of the gel's functional groups mitigated selective competition for MB+. Furthermore, the extreme acidity promoted MB aggregation, enhancing intermolecular interactions with the gel and resulting in an adsorption capacity higher than that at pH 3. At pH 11, the deprotonated functional groups endowed the gel with a more negative surface, which enhanced electrostatic attraction toward MB+, leading to the highest adsorption capacity (6.21 mg g−1) and removal percentage (78.63%). Notably, the gradual increase in particle size did not hinder MB uptake; instead, the adsorption performance increased with pH, indicating that electrostatic interactions and pH-dependent chemical adsorption (e.g., hydrogen bonding or chemisorption) dominate over any physical architectural changes. Given that most natural water bodies are neutral (pH 6–8) and excessive pH adjustment increases costs and introduces impurities, no pH adjustment was applied in subsequent experiments to align with practical application scenarios.

Research on maximum adsorption capacity

The effect of initial methylene blue (MB) concentration (C0) on the adsorption performance was investigated. As shown in Fig. 9A, a visual comparison of the MB solution before and after the adsorption process vividly demonstrated the decolorization effect, the initially intense blue hue of the MB solution progressively diminished to an almost colorless state at low C0 values, signifying the highly efficient removal of MB molecules by the gel. Briefly, 20 mg of gel was added to 5 mL of MB solutions with varying C0, and adsorption was conducted at 45°C for 4 h under stirring. As depicted in Fig. 9B, adsorption capacity (orange curve) increased monotonically with C0, from 0.12 mg g−1 to 4.85 mg g−1, driven by the enhanced concentration gradient that promoted MB diffusion into the gel matrix. Conversely, removal percentage (blue curve) exhibited a non-monotonic trend: peaking at 88.5% (C0 = 0.5(mg L−1), dropping sharply to 32.19% (C0 = 10 (mg L−1), then stabilizing at 21.43% at the highest C0. This reflects site saturation: low C0 allows near-complete removal, while rising C0 saturates finite binding sites, reducing relative removal efficiency despite increasing absolute adsorption capacity.33

Adsorption isotherms

The adsorption isotherms of the PIB-based gel were investigated at 35°C, 45°C, and 55°C (Fig. 11). The equilibrium adsorption capacity (qe) increased monotonically with equilibrium concentration (Ce) across all temperatures without a distinct plateau, following the order 45°C > 55°C > 35°C, indicating moderate heating enhanced adsorption while further warming to 55°C slightly reduced capacity.50 Fitting to Langmuir, Freundlich, and Sips models51 (Table 2) revealed that the Sips isotherm provided the highest correlation coefficients at all temperatures, followed by the Langmuir and Freundlich models. The Langmuir model assumes a homogeneous surface and monolayer adsorption with identical adsorption sites, whereas the Freundlich model is an empirical equation that describes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. The Sips model combines features of both Langmuir and Freundlich, and it avoids the unlimited increase in adsorption capacity at high concentrations. Collectively, these results identify 45°C as the optimal temperature and the Sips model as the most suitable for describing the adsorption isotherm behavior. This temperature-dependent adsorption behavior and model preference suggest that the process is governed by a synergistic interplay of electrostatic interactions and surface heterogeneity, which provides a foundation for further exploring the molecular interactions between the PIB gel and target adsorbates.

Mechanism of adsorption

The adsorption mechanism of PIB gel toward methylene blue (MB) arises from a synergistic combination of multiple interactions, including electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole interactions, chemisorption, and physical pore entrapment. Hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole interactions are mediated by amide and ester groups, while amine groups act as chemisorption sites, and the porous structure provides physical confinement, though this is considered a minor contributor given the limited mesoporosity of the material. Among these, electrostatic force is the dominant driving force (Fig. 12). The gel surface, rich in carboxyl and amine groups, exhibits negative electronegativity52 across a wide pH range, enabling robust electrostatic attraction and ionic bonding with positively charged MB cations.

Schematic diagram of the adsorption mechanism of MB onto PIB gel.

Chemical functional groups play a pivotal role in augmenting the stability of the adsorption process. Specifically, amide and ester groups within the gel structure engage in hydrogen bonding or dipole–dipole interactions with MB molecules, thereby enhancing the overall adsorption affinity. Meanwhile, amine groups serve as chemisorption sites, contributing to a more robust binding of MB to the gel matrix. Additionally, the gel's rough and porous architecture, characterized by a particle size of approximately 203 nm, offers a substantial specific surface area conducive to physical pore interception of MB. Notably, at temperatures below 32°C, the swelling of the gel leads to an expansion of pore channels, which facilitates the diffusion of MB molecules and enhances their physical entrapment within the gel network.

Kinetic analysis corroborates that the adsorption process adheres to the pseudo-second-order model, indicating that chemisorption governs the process. Isotherm studies reveal both monolayer and heterogeneous adsorption characteristics, aligning with the Sips model fitting. Thermodynamic parameters further validate the spontaneous nature of the adsorption. Additionally, the wide pH adaptability of the gel's functional groups ensures stable electrostatic interactions across a variety of pH conditions, thereby contributing to its robust adsorption performance, which is further supported by the pH-dependent adsorption experiments where the adsorption capacity increased with rising pH, correlating with enhanced negative surface charge.

Experiment of adsorption regeneration

The reusability and regenerability of an adsorbent are critical parameters for its practical application. To evaluate this, cyclic adsorption–desorption experiments were conducted using methylene blue (MB), a cationic dye. Desorption was achieved under acidic conditions (pH 2–3), which protonates functional groups on the gel and weakens electrostatic interactions, thereby facilitating MB release.53 In Fig. 13, after five consecutive cycles, the adsorption capacity for MB remained at 72.42% of its initial value. This high retention of performance demonstrates that the PIB gel is an excellent, regenerable adsorbent with significant potential for repeated use.

Conclusions

In this study, a thermosensitive N-isopropylacrylamide-co-butyl methacrylate (NIPAM-co-BMA) gel was successfully synthesized via free-radical emulsion polymerization, with full monomer conversion and copolymer structure confirmed by FT-IR and XPS. The MB adsorption process was best described by pseudo-second-order kinetics and the Sips isotherm, indicating chemisorption on a heterogeneous surface. The gel achieved efficient MB removal primarily through electrostatic interactions and chemical adsorption, as evidenced by pH-dependent capacity enhancement and significant suppression by NaCl. Despite a low BET surface area, the material possesses abundant micron-sized pores, confirming that adsorption is governed by surface functional groups rather than mesoporosity. Furthermore, its excellent thermoresponsive behavior (viscosity transition near 32°C) and robust thermal stability up to ∼300°C highlight its significant potential for practical applications.
