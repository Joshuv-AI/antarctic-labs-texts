Misdiagnosis of diabetic ketoacidosis: a case report
Authors: Minghe Zhang, Jian Li, Lianhui Fan, Hongtao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbdominal pain caused by diabetic ketoacidosis is uncommon and can easily be misdiagnosed as abdominal pain caused by ureteral stones.This case highlights the importance of distinguishing between these etiologies, particularly in patients with diabetes, as delayed recognition of diabetic ketoacidosis can result in life-threatening complications. The novelty lies in emphasizing the diagnostic challenges and the necessity for clinicians to consider diabetic ketoacidosis even when imaging suggests alternative causes.Case presentationThis case report describes a 59-year-old Han female patient who was admitted to the hospital for "generalized abdominal pain without obvious cause for 3 days" and was diagnosed with left ureteral stones. Initial imaging revealed a left ureteral stone and hydronephrosis, prompting ureteral stent placement. Despite antispasmodics, analgesics, and stent insertion, her pain persisted. Following comprehensive systemic treatment, metabolic parameters were successfully normalized, and the patient recovered and was discharged from the hospital.ConclusionThe mechanisms and treatment approaches for abdominal pain caused by ketoacidosis and ureteral stones differ significantly. Clinicians treating abdominal pain in patients with a history of diabetes should consider the possibility of ketoacidosis-induced abdominal pain, especially when conventional antispasmodic and analgesic treatments for ureteral stones are ineffective. Prompt identification of diabetic ketoacidosis via comprehensive clinical assessment and laboratory investigations is crucial to avoid delays in appropriate treatment. Background

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is one of the most common diabetic emergencies [1], characterized by hyperglycemia and severe metabolic disturbances. Hyponatremia and elevated blood ketones are key contributors to its pathogenesis. Electrolyte imbalances caused by DKA may lead to intestinal motility disorders and severe abdominal pain [2, 3]. Common clinical manifestations include abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, and vomiting, with some patients presenting with abdominal pain as the initial symptom, mimicking an acute abdomen [4]. The diverse clinical presentations of DKA increase the likelihood of misdiagnosis.

In contrast, ureteral stones typically manifest as severe, intermittent pain in the flank or abdominal region, often accompanied by gross hematuria. The pain may radiate to the ipsilateral groin. This report discusses the case of a patient with ureteral stones complicated by DKA and explores the differences in the mechanisms and treatments of abdominal pain caused by DKA and ureteral stones. This case highlights diagnostic challenges in a patient with ketosis-prone type 2 diabetes, emphasizing the need to consider DKA even when imaging suggests alternative causes. If the patient is not obese and has no typical insulin resistance, latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) should be highly suspected and further autoantibody tests should be conducted for a definitive diagnosis [5, 6]. LADA belongs to a slowly progressive subtype of type 1 diabetes, and its clinical characteristics lie between those of type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes. For patients suspected to have LADA, it is recommended to first detect glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD)-65 antibody and combine the detection of zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) and insulinoma-associated protein 2 (IA-2) antibodies to improve the detection rate and dynamically monitor the C-peptide level. If the blood sugar of a diabetic patient remains poorly controlled, the patient may need to transition to multiple daily injections (MDI). By improving awareness of DKA-induced abdominal pain, clinicians can reduce the risk of misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.

Case presentation

General information

The patient is a 59-year-old Han female who was admitted to the Urology Department of the Northern Theater General Hospital on 1 January 2024, owing to "generalized abdominal pain for 3 days without obvious cause." At 3 days prior to admission, the patient developed generalized abdominal pain without a clear trigger, which progressively worsened and did not relieve. The pain was accompanied by nausea and vomiting, with the vomit being light pink in color. Despite treatment with antispasmodic, analgesic, and antimicrobial medications, the pain persisted. The patient was then given pethidine, which temporarily relieved the pain, but it recurred 6 hours later. The patient had not eaten or passed gas or stool during this period. Upon physical examination, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.8 °C, pulse 78 beats/minute, respiratory rate 18 breaths/minute, blood pressure 111/68 mmHg. The patient was conscious, and no abnormalities were found on cardiovascular and respiratory examinations. The patient’s medical history revealed a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes 12 years ago, for which she had been using insulin, metformin, and voglibose, with good blood glucose control. Laboratory tests confirmed DKA. It was worth noting that her blood glucose level (15.4 mmol/L) was lower than that of typical hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome (HHS). The diagnostic criteria for HHS require a blood glucose level > 600 mg/dL (33.3 mmol/L), which suggests that this case may represent LADA or ketosis-prone diabetes (KPD). The initial blood glucose level of the patient was 15.4 mmol/L and the blood glucose level after treatment was 13.2 mmol/L. The treatment focused on correcting acidosis, replenishing fluids, and using insulin, leading to symptom relief.

Auxiliary examinations

Upon admission, the following results were observed: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) 9.16%, serum creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) 6.4 ng/mL, serum myoglobin (Myo) 128.6 ng/mL, white blood cell (WBC) count 13.5 × 109/L, neutrophil percentage (NEU%) 88.4%, neutrophil absolute count (NEU#) 11.93 × 109/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) 59.12 mg/L; electrolyte levels: potassium 3.71 mmol/L, sodium 132.8 mmol/L, chloride 96.6 mmol/L, bicarbonate 19.3 mmol/L. Urinalysis showed glucose (3+), ketones (1+), hematuria (2+), nine red blood cells per high-power field, and no protein. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a 5 × 4 mm dense shadow in the left ureter, with dilation of the left renal pelvis, calyces, and ureter. No significant abnormalities were found on electrocardiogram (ECG), cardiac ultrasound, or chest CT.

Treatment course

Based on the patient’s clinical presentation and auxiliary examination results, the diagnosis was left ureteral stone with left hydronephrosis and type 2 diabetes. Upon admission, the patient experienced severe abdominal pain that did not alleviate. A consultation with the general surgery department was requested, and after physical examination, no clear abdominal tenderness, muscle rigidity, or rebound tenderness were observed, ruling out acute abdominal conditions. After receiving an intramuscular injection of 50 mg pethidine for pain relief, the pain slightly improved. On the second day of hospitalization, the patient underwent cystoscopy with left ureteral stent placement under local anesthesia to relieve obstruction and spasms. However, 2 hours postsurgery, the patient again experienced severe abdominal cramping. Treatment with intravenous flurbiprofen axetil injection was ineffective in relieving the pain. Considering the patient’s medical history and auxiliary test results, DKA was suspected. Blood gas analysis, lactate, ion analysis, liver and kidney function tests, and D-3-hydroxybutyrate levels were performed, and the diagnosis of DKA was confirmed after consultation with the endocrinology department. The primary objectives of treatment were to restore metabolic stability by addressing the underlying acidosis (closing anion gap), normalizing blood pH, and ensuring adequate volume resuscitation. The patient’s blood glucose was initially managed with oral metformin and voglibose. When oral hypoglycemic agents showed insufficient efficacy, intravenous insulin was initiated to control hyperglycemia. Following treatment, the patient’s abdominal pain gradually improved, and acidosis was corrected. Follow-up tests showed that blood gas analysis, lactate levels, ion analysis, liver and kidney function, and D-3-hydroxybutyrate levels returned to normal (Table 1). The patient was discharged after recovery.

Discussion and conclusion

DKA is a serious metabolic syndrome characterized by severe disturbances in glucose, fat, and protein metabolism, primarily caused by insulin deficiency and abnormal increases in counter-regulatory hormones such as glucagon, catecholamines, and thyroid hormones. Common triggers include acute infections, vomiting, diarrhea, myocardial infarction, poor diet, and psychological factors [7, 8]. In this case, the patient experienced abdominal pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting, and had not eaten or passed gas or stool. The mechanism of the abdominal pain may be related to severe dehydration and hypokalemia caused by nausea and vomiting, leading to paralytic ileus and intense abdominal pain [9]. DKA is typically characterized by hyperglycemia, positive ketone bodies, and a decrease in pH. Abdominal pain caused by ureteral stones can occur in most individuals, especially those with a history of stones. Abdominal pain due to DKA is often diffuse and accompanied by symptoms such as nausea and vomiting [10], but there is usually no significant tenderness. April Bril et al. have also reported similar cases [11]. In contrast, abdominal pain caused by ureteral stones is typically intense, localized to the flank or lower abdomen, and may be accompanied by noticeable tenderness and radiating pain. DKA is often associated with symptoms of metabolic acidosis, such as Kussmaul breathing and altered consciousness. Ureteral stones may present with gross hematuria, frequency, urgency, dysuria, and other urinary tract irritative symptoms. Laboratory tests for DKA can clearly identify abnormalities in blood glucose, ketone bodies, and pH levels. For ureteral stones, laboratory tests may show abnormalities in white blood cell (WBC) and red blood cell (RBC) counts in the urine, while imaging studies can confirm the presence of stones. Leonid Barski et al. [12] suggest that after the onset of DKA, it is crucial to promptly administer balanced electrolyte solutions to correct dehydration and electrolyte disturbances, intravenously infuse insulin to lower blood glucose levels, and correct electrolyte disturbances, especially potassium, to prevent hypokalemia [13, 14]. If alkalinizing treatment is required, care should be taken to avoid over-alkalization. The triggers of DKA, such as irregular insulin use, infections, or underlying diseases, should be identified. Dynamic monitoring of blood glucose, ketone bodies, blood gas analysis, and electrolyte levels is essential in patients with DKA [15, 16]. The long-term management of patients with diabetes is of vital importance, especially for special types such as LADA and KPD. The disease progression needs to be evaluated by regularly monitoring the levels of autoantibodies (such as GAD-65, ZnT8 antibodies) and C-peptide, and the treatment plan should be dynamically adjusted. For patients diagnosed with LADA, the transition to MDI regimen should be made as early as possible. A strict follow-up plan and patient education are the cores for maintaining stable blood glucose and preventing acute and chronic complications. The goal of long-term management is not only to achieve the target blood sugar levels, but also to improve the prognosis through comprehensive intervention. The abdominal pain caused by DKA is similar to that caused by lower ureteral stones, which can lead to misdiagnosis by clinicians [8, 17, 18]. On one hand, diagnostic habits across different specialties may contribute to misdiagnosis, especially in the early stages when symptoms and signs are not typical. On the other hand, patients with DKA may present with atypical early manifestations, with positive signs and tests often lacking, making early diagnosis difficult. Over-reliance on imaging results and a lack of attention to clinical symptoms and signs can also lead to misdiagnosis. In this case, the abdominal CT scan suggested left ureteral stones with left renal hydronephrosis, leading to an initial diagnosis of abdominal pain due to ureteral stones. After left ureteral stent placement, the pain did not alleviate, prompting a search for other causes. The authors of this article believe that further research is needed to determine whether the abdominal pain was solely caused by DKA or if the left ureteral stone impaction contributed to nausea, vomiting, metabolic disturbances, and subsequently DKA.
