Influenza and Human Papillomavirus Vaccine Utilisation in Vulnerable Urban Youth in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada
Authors: Sarah Deck, Se Lim Jang, Rachel Harris, Fariba Kolahdooz, Moutasem Zakkar, Sangita Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThis project explores influenza and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine utilisation among a multi‐ethnic group of youth (aged 11–23 years) in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada.MethodUsing a cross‐sectional design and purposive sampling, influenza vaccine utilisation over 3 years was assessed via questionnaire. Female participants also reported on HPV vaccine utilisation. Multinomial logistic regression was used to explore the factors associated with vaccine utilisation.ResultsIn the project sample, 16.7% of males and 14.7% of females had received the influenza vaccine each year. The lowest rates of influenza vaccination were reported by African/Middle Eastern/Latin American youth (8.3%), Indigenous youth (10.8%), and youth > 18 years (12.6%). For the HPV vaccine, 61.1% of females reported having received all doses, while 21.9% were unvaccinated. Rates of HPV vaccine utilisation were lowest among females of Asian origin, females aged 18–23 years, and females residing in Canada for < 5 years.DiscussionObserved associations between age, ethnicity, and vaccine utilisation in this study highlight potential barriers and warrant further research to inform policymaking.

Introduction

Vaccination is one of the most effective primary prevention strategies for controlling infectious diseases, such as influenza and human papillomavirus (HPV) [1]. The efficacy of the influenza vaccine is higher among children (74%) compared to adults (55%) and seniors (18%) [2]. In Canada, while the influenza vaccine is publicly funded and recommended for all individuals over 6 months of age, the utilisation rate for the annual influenza vaccine is approximately 40%, with utilisation being highest among older individuals (aged 65+ years) [3]. Despite the vaccination target of 80%, the age‐standardised rate for influenza vaccine utilisation in Canada was 42% in 2019–2020, 40% in 2020–2021, 39% in 2021–2022, and 43% in 2022–2023 [4, 5]. Genital warts, as well as cervical, oral, anal, penile, and vaginal cancers, are associated with various HPV strains, with women being more frequently affected by HPV‐related cancers [6, 7, 8]. About 75% of sexually active Canadians contract at least one HPV infection in their lifetime, with the highest infection rates being among individuals between 15 and 24 years old, and an estimated prevalence of 16.8% in Canadian women aged 13–86 years [9, 10]. Evidence has shown that the best practice for the prevention of HPV infection is to administer vaccines targeting oncogenic strains of HPV before the onset of sexual activity. Two prophylactic HPV vaccines (Gardasil and Cervarix) are licenced in Canada, with an efficacy of 100% for HPV infections and 70% for HPV‐related cervical cancer [10, 11]. In Canada, publicly funded HPV immunisation programs for females began in 2007; Alberta introduced the programme for females in 2008, expanding to include males in 2014 [12, 13, 14]. The programs have increasingly utilised school‐based implementation strategies supplemented by opportunistic vaccine delivery through primary care providers [15]. However, since the introduction of these programs, coverage has been well below the goals set by the Canadian Immunisation Committee of 80% of eligible students within 2 years of the programs' introduction and 90% within 5 years [8]. HPV vaccine utilisation varies from 12.4% to 88.2%, which is limiting progress in HPV and cancer prevention [8]. In Edmonton, Alberta, HPV vaccines are given in 3 doses and are available to grade 6 students (male and female) for free within schools, with eligibility for free vaccination lasting up to 26 years of age.

Although global vaccination programs have achieved substantial success, vulnerable populations, including youth, frequently experience lower vaccination coverage and diminished immunogenic responses [16]. The Socioecological Model states that persons' behaviours are shaped by influences operating at the individual, interpersonal, organisational, community, social environment, and policy levels [17]. Understanding how the multilevel factors may affect vaccine utilisation among urban youth is imperative for informing effective public health policy and disease prevention strategies. Therefore, this project aimed to identify factors influencing influenza and HPV vaccine utilisation among a multi‐ethnic group of youth residing in Edmonton, with the broader aim of describing gaps in coverage, highlighting groups at risk for under‐immunisation, and generating evidence to guide targeted, culturally responsive vaccination interventions.

Methods

This project, a part of the larger WHY ACT NOW project [18, 19], took place in Edmonton, Alberta; approximately 24% of this city's residents are foreign‐born, and 6.5% identify as Indigenous (First Nations, Métis, or Inuit) [20, 21].

Purposive sampling was utilised to select a multi‐ethnic group of participants aged 11–23 years, from 12 representative schools/institutions. Youth who were breastfeeding or pregnant at the time of the project were excluded. Schools and institutions were carefully chosen based on the large population size of Indigenous and/or newly arrived students to Canada. Trained research staff collected data by interviewer‐administered questionnaire, which was developed in collaboration with a community advisory board, and it included demographic variables and questions regarding the number of annual influenza vaccines received over the 3 years prior. Further details regarding the development of the questionnaire have been previously described by Kolahdooz et al. (2018) [19]. Female participants were also asked about HPV vaccine utilisation, as the publicly funded HPV immunisation programme was for females only at this time. Participants or parents of participants < 18 years old provided written informed consent before the start of the interview. Participation was voluntary.

Data Analysis

Means and proportions (%) were generated utilising SAS version 9.4. Subgroup analyses were performed by sex (for influenza vaccine only), age group, duration of time residing in Canada (< 5 years, ≥ 5 years, or Canadian‐born), and ethnicity, which was classified into four categories: Indigenous (First Nations, Métis, or Inuit), European, African/Middle Eastern/Latin American, and Asian.

The Chi‐Square Test of Independence and Fisher's exact test with Monte Carlo approximation were utilised to explore the association between the outcomes of interest (self‐reported influenza vaccine utilisation, self‐reported HPV vaccine utilisation), and the independent variables. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was also utilised. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) were used because they provide a clear measure of relative likelihood while accounting for other factors. Given the non‐probabilistic project sample, predicted probabilities were not explored to avoid model overfitting.

Missing observations were assumed to be missing at random and were excluded from each model as appropriate, ensuring that only participants with complete data for the variables under consideration were included. Missing values are reported in the relevant tables. This approach preserves the integrity of the analyses while maintaining transparency regarding the extent of the missing data. Age was categorised into 11–15, 16–17, and 18–23 years to reflect meaningful developmental stages and potential differences in exposure to school‐based vaccination programs and autonomy in health decisions. To simplify analyses and the interpretation of results, the length of residence was categorised as greater or less than 5 years. This categorisation was done to reflect meaningful differences that may exist, as newcomers in Canada for less than 5 years may face barriers such as lower acculturation, limited familiarity with existing healthcare systems, and limited access to vaccination programs.

Results

In total, 557 youth participated. The mean age was 16.7 (SD: 1.8) years, with 58.9% identifying as female, 18.3% as Indigenous, 19.0% as African/Middle Eastern/Latin American, 23.7% as Asian, and 39.0% as European (Table 1).

Of the 557 participants, 30 had missing data regarding vaccine status and were excluded. Of the remaining 527 participants, approximately half (47.6%) reported not having received an influenza vaccine within the last 3 years, with similar proportions by sex (47.4% of males, 47.8% of females). African/Middle Eastern/Latin American youth had the highest rate of not having received the influenza vaccine in the past 3 years (60.8%) compared to European (48.6%), Indigenous (44.1%), and Asian (38.7%) youth. The youngest age group (11–15 years) had a higher rate of receiving the annual influenza vaccine over the past 3 years (17.3%) compared to the oldest age group (18–23 years), of whom 58.1% had not received an annual influenza vaccine in the past 3 years. Canadian‐born youth had the highest rate (16.8%) of getting the influenza vaccine each year for the past 3 years, compared to immigrant youth who had lived in Canada more than 5 years (10.0%) or less than 5 years (10.6%) (Table 2).

The Chi‐Square Test of Independence and Fisher's exact test revealed that age and ethnicity are associated with self‐reported influenza vaccine utilisation, and that age and residency status (i.e., years lived in Canada) are associated with self‐reported HPV vaccine utilisation. The multinomial logistic regression models provided deeper insights. Model 1 (Table 4) shows the factors associated with self‐reported influenza vaccine utilisation. Age group was a strong predictor with youth aged 18–23 years (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.14–0.59, p = 0.0007) having much lower odds of completing multiple flu doses compared to 11–15 year olds (OR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.23–0.96, p = 0.037). Ethnicity was also a predictor of influenza vaccine utilisation. Compared to European, Indigenous youth were more likely to have at least one dose versus none (OR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.03–3.58, p = 0.040), and African/Middle Eastern/Latin American group was less likely to have 3 doses versus none (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.12–0.84, p = 0.020). There were no associations between sex and residency status with influenza vaccine utilisation.

Model 2 (Table 5) shows the factors associated with self‐reported HPV vaccine utilisation among female youth. Age group was a strong predictor of vaccination status. Youth aged 18–23 had substantially lower odds of being vaccinated compared with those aged 11–15, both for completion of all 3 doses (Yes, all 3 doses vs. No: OR = 0.19; 95% CI: 0.09–0.44; p < 0.0001) and for partial vaccination (Yes, but not all 3 doses vs. No: OR = 0.10; 95% CI: 0.03–0.38; p = 0.0007). Residency status was also significantly associated with vaccination. Youth who had lived in Canada for fewer than 5 years had markedly lower odds of completing all 3 HPV vaccine doses compared with those born in Canada (Yes, all 3 doses vs. No: OR = 0.23; 95% CI: 0.09–0.60; p = 0.003). Indigenous youth were more likely than European youth to fall into the ‘Yes, but not all 3 doses’ category compared with ‘No’ (OR = 6.81; 95% CI: 1.61–28.72; p = 0.009). Discussion

The majority of Canadians who receive the annual influenza vaccine are older adults [3]. In this project, influenza vaccine utilisation rates among youth, particularly annual influenza vaccine utilisation rates, were notably low. In line with Socioecological Model [17], different individual factors were associated with lower vaccine uptake among youth in the project.

Population‐based studies in high‐income countries have demonstrated that when the prevalence of HPV vaccine utilisation was > 50%, HPV‐16 and HPV‐18 infections decreased by 68% in girls aged 13–19 years [22]. This prevalence of HPV vaccination is similar to that reported for undergraduate university women in Canada (49%), for whom limited knowledge about the HPV vaccine and its potential side effects was a barrier to receiving the vaccination [23]. The project findings for HPV vaccination revealed that older teens (18–23 years) were far more likely to remain unvaccinated compared to younger teens (11–15 years), and they were also much less likely to appear in either fully or partially vaccinated categories. While the introduction of the school‐based HPV vaccination programme in Canada may explain higher utilisation rates in school‐aged children in this project [24], participants 18–23 years old might not have benefited from school‐based vaccination, and had the lowest utilisation rates of any age group. Strategies to increase HPV vaccine utilisation and acceptance are urgently needed for Canada to achieve its vaccination goal [8].

The low vaccination rates among youth are in line with the vaccination rates found across individuals in Canada [3, 24]. The factors contributing to such low vaccine utilisation rates include historical, sociocultural, environmental, and economic factors, as well as factors such as safety and efficacy concerns, distrust in government and health services, limited knowledge, and previous negative healthcare experiences [17, 25, 26]. Female youth who had lived in Canada for less than 5 years were substantially less likely to complete all 3 HPV doses compared with those born in Canada. Compared to female youth of European background, Indigenous female youth were more likely to be partially vaccinated than unvaccinated, while other ethnic contrasts were not significant. The findings also corroborate previous research that Canadian‐born individuals are more likely than newcomers to both want to get vaccinated and actually get vaccinated [27]. Lower rates of vaccination among newcomers may be the result of limited vaccine campaigns that prioritise such at‐risk populations [28]. Literature also suggests barriers for newcomers to childhood vaccinations, including HPV vaccination, to be limited knowledge and information about vaccines, language barriers, cultural beliefs, and systemic factors such as limited access to and discrimination within healthcare [29, 30, 31, 32]. For influenza, Indigenous youth were more likely to receive at least one annual flu shot in the last 3 years compared with European youth, while youth from Africa, the Middle East, or Latin America were much less likely to receive all 3 annual vaccinations in the last 3 years compared with European youth. These findings are similar to a study of the Canadian Community Health Survey of Canadians aged 12 years and older, which found that the white ethnic group had a lower likelihood of receiving influenza vaccination compared to other ethnic groups, including Indigenous communities, except for individuals self‐identifying as Black [33]. The authors attribute higher exposure to the media against vaccination. Vaccination campaigns highlighting increased risks for severe influenza infection outcomes, such as hospitalisation, may have promoted vaccination in Indigenous communities [34]. Limited or misunderstood information, as well as other factors including socio‐demographic characteristics and social and cultural norms, have been documented as barriers to vaccine utilisation [35, 36]. Focused dissemination of information to newcomers in multiple languages across multiple platforms [37, 38] is recommended to increase awareness of immunisation health benefits and address vaccine hesitancy. Indigenous community members experience barriers rooted in the legacy of colonialism to accessing healthcare, which, when considered in vaccination strategies, may contribute to improving vaccine utilisation rates [39].

Of additional concern, the COVID‐19 pandemic has had an immense global impact, with Canada reporting over 1.6 million confirmed cases and over 27,000 deaths [40]. Children usually gain initial immunity against influenza from mothers during pregnancy, or via vaccinations or infection in the first years of life. The COVID‐19 pandemic has resulted in children missing almost two years of such exposure and immunity building [41]. It is unknown how the COVID‐19 pandemic restrictions will affect children's vulnerability to influenza and other infections [42].

Despite all youth being eligible for the COVID‐19 vaccine in Alberta, barring contraindications, youth have had the lowest vaccine utilisation rates amongst all age groups [43]. The main reasons for refusing the COVID‐19 vaccine amongst Canadians were limited confidence in the safety of the vaccines and the potential risks and side effects [27]. One‐third of the individuals who reported being unlikely to get vaccinated wanted to wait until it seemed safe, while around one‐quarter did not consider it necessary [27]. Given the uncertainty surrounding the COVID‐19 virus and related immunity, strategies are needed to increase vaccine utilisation among Canadians [44]; the development of such strategies may benefit from information regarding both influenza and HPV vaccine utilisation in youth.

The project findings indicate policy implications and key priorities for practice and future research. Policy initiatives should prioritise funding and development of culturally responsive multilingual vaccine campaigns that improve knowledge, health literacy, and safety concerns among target populations with low vaccine utilisation rates, such as youth, Indigenous community members, and newcomers to Canada. The strengthening of school‐ and community‐based vaccination programs, along with tailored outreach to youth who have missed previous vaccination opportunities, may help address gaps in influenza and HPV vaccine coverage. Inclusion and collaboration with community leaders and focused engagement with parents and newcomer families may foster more trusted and relevant dissemination of vaccine information. This could involve engaging older generations, such as Elders within Indigenous communities, in the transmission/dissemination of knowledge [45]. Vaccine campaigns with an increased focus on newcomer families, particularly parents who often make or influence decisions regarding vaccination for youth, would also be beneficial [46]. Future research is required to assess the impact of tailored strategies and to identify approaches for addressing sociocultural and structural barriers to vaccine utilisation.

This project has several limitations. Vaccine history was self‐reported, which may introduce recall and social desirability bias. Participation was voluntary and based on purposive sampling from 12 schools and institutions, which may lead to selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings. Validation against immunisation records was not feasible within the scope of this project, and data for sensitivity analysis were not collected. The non‐probability design and sparse data in some categories resulted in wide confidence intervals, reducing precision. While multinomial logistic regression adjusts for multiple covariates, unmeasured factors may still influence vaccination behaviour. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and observed associations should be interpreted as correlational. Finally, model‐based estimates reflect patterns within this sample and may not represent population‐level probabilities. Despite the limitations, the project is strengthened by a large number of Indigenous and/or new Canadian youth. The project achieved a participation rate exceeding 90% among youth in Edmonton, attributable in part to sustained relationship‐building efforts, including several months of school‐based presentations conducted by the research team prior to data collection to establish trust and engagement with students. This project also provides insights regarding vaccination promotion, programs, and education to healthcare providers. Future research is still needed to gain further understanding of vaccine utilisation within Canada.
