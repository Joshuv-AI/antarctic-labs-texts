Association of functional brain alterations with β-amyloid, tau, and cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Wanwan Guo, Hongda Shao, Yan Zhang, Lin Liu, Hairong Zheng, Dong Liang, Jianjun Liu, Lingyan Zhang, Zhanli Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRegional functional activity plays a critical role in the cognitive performance of both aging individuals and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). To better understand the pathologies and clinical progression patterns of AD, the investigation of the associations of regional functional brain alterations with β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques, tau tangles, and cognitive decline in AD is essential.MethodsWe recruited 179 participants (mean age of 69.7 years, 61% female) who underwent concurrent [18F]-Florbetapir Aβ PET imaging, [18F]-PI-2620 tau PET imaging, resting functional MRI, and cognitive assessments, after which we classified these participants into A-/T- (N = 35), A-/T+ (N = 11), A+/T- (N = 53), and A+/T+ (N = 80) groups according to the thresholds of the Aβ and tau PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR). We subsequently compared the group differences in mean regional homogeneity (ReHo) values and explored the correlations between ReHo index scores and Aβ burden, tau deposition, and cognitive scores using general linear models, including age, sex, education, hippocampus volume, and total intracranial volume as covariates.ResultsWe detected neuronal hyperactivity and hypoactivity in the angular gyrus, cuneus, lingual gyrus, and parahippocampal gyrus, which are mainly distributed across the default mode and salience networks; moreover, they are associated with regional Aβ burden and tau deposition encompassing the AD spectrum. Furthermore, we confirmed that elevated cortical Aβ accumulation and tau deposition mainly induce decreases in regional functional activity in AD. Finally, we observed that Aβ- and tau-related regional functional brain alterations showed significant indirect associations with the relationships between Aβ burden (β = -0.08, [95% ci, -4.22~ -0.75], p = 0.01), tau deposition (β = -0.17, [95% ci, -11.04~-4.07], p < 0.0001), and cognitive performance. These findings indicate that abnormal functional activity is associated with both AD hallmark pathologies and cognitive decline, in a manner consistent with a potential mediating role.ConclusionsThis study revealed that cortical Aβ and tau aggregation may alter functional activity (as reflected by ReHo), thus further leading to cognitive decline in patients with AD. These findings provide novel insights into how Aβ and tau-associated functional activity affect cognitive decline in AD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-01991-z.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the accumulation of β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques and tau tangles that starts to form decades before the occurrence of cognitive decline [1–3]. An understanding of the associations among Aβ, tau, neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline is critical for elucidating the pathology and progression patterns of AD [4, 5]. Previous research has indicated that neuropathological alterations in AD patients are accompanied by specific changes in brain function, such as disruptions of neuronal excitability and synaptic plasticity [6–8]. Recently, animal and computational models of AD have begun to associate protein deposition with cognitive decline at the neuronal and neuronal circuit levels [9–11]. However, the associations of functional brain alterations driven by Aβ burden and tau deposition with cognitive decline in humans has not been adequately investigated.

Positron emission tomography (PET) [12, 13] and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) [14, 15] are commonly used to detect abnormal brain function in patients with AD. Previous studies using FDG-PET imaging [16, 17] and task-based fMRI [14, 18] have demonstrated that individuals with elevated levels of Aβ and tau deposition exhibit hypometabolism and aberrant functional activation predominantly in the posterior cingulate cortex, parietal, and temporal regions. In recent years, resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) has offered a task-free alternative for assessing spontaneous neuronal activity in topographically dispersed but functionally related brain regions [19, 20]; moreover, this technique has been proven to be useful for studying functional brain alterations in patients with AD [21–24]. Furthermore, previous rs-fMRI studies employing regional homogeneity (ReHo) analysis have revealed altered patterns of localized neural synchrony in AD patients, which are characterized by decreased functional coherence in the posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus and increased synchronization in the lingual gyrus and fusiform gyrus [25, 26]. These spatial alterations in intrinsic brain activity have generally aligned with prior neuroimaging evidence obtained from both PET metabolic studies and task-based fMRI paradigms [27, 28]. Although previous studies have investigated resting-state functional alterations, the relationships between functional changes and both AD-related proteinopathy remain incompletely characterized.

The accumulation of Aβ plaques and tau tangles is thought to cause neuronal death, synaptic loss, and changes in brain activity, thereby eventually resulting in cognitive decline [18, 29–31]. Recent research using PET imaging and fMRI has demonstrated that early Aβ burden and tau deposition are correlated with alterations in functional activity and neurophysiological activity, which may contribute to aberrant network activity [32–35]. Notably, a previous study using task-free magnetoencephalography (MEG) and PET imaging revealed that Aβ burden and tau deposition together influence changes in cortical neurophysiological activity, which are also associated with cognitive decline [34]. Although some evidence indicates that AD-related protein deposition is associated with functional network abnormalities [36–38], to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate how Aβ- and tau-associated functional activity (via the assessment of ReHo) affects cognitive decline in patients with AD. This study could bridge the gap between neuropathological mechanisms and the variety of clinical symptoms that are associated with the disease phenotype observed in patients with AD.

The present study aimed to characterize the patterns of abnormal brain activity associated with Aβ burden and tau deposition across different stages of AD, as well as the association of these abnormalities with cognitive decline. Using resting-state fMRI and whole-brain quantitative PET imaging of Aβ and tau, we assessed the associations of abnormal brain activity with Aβ and tau pathology in patients with AD. First, we utilized ReHo to assess regional functional activity, and the mean ReHo values were compared between different groups according to the thresholds of the Aβ and tau PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR). Second, we assessed the associations of regional ReHo index scores with regional Aβ burden and tau deposition by using a generalized linear model that included age, sex, education status, hippocampal volume (HCV), and total intracranial volume (TIV) as covariates. Third, we explored whether altered functional activity mediated the association of Aβ burden and tau deposition with cognitive decline. We anticipated that Aβ and tau deposition would be associated with large-scale neuronal manifestations of hyperactivity and hypoactivity, as reflected by increased or decreased regional synchronization, respectively, which would correspondingly mediate the impact of AD-related proteinopathy on cognitive decline.

Methods

Participants

We included 179 patients who sourced from the memory-clinic cohort, and underwent Aβ PET (18F-Florbetapir [AV-45]), tau PET (18F-PI-2620 [PI-2620]), resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) and 3D T1-weighted MRI scans, which were acquired in the Department of Nuclear Medicine at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated with Ren Ji Hospital. All of the participants completed the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), which were administered by trained clinicians. None had a history of other neurodegenerative diseases and psychiatric disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease, depression, or schizophrenia. Using Aβ PET and tau PET, we classified the participants into four groups: Aβ PET negative and tau PET negative (A-/T-); Aβ PET negative and tau PET positive (A-/T+); Aβ PET positive and tau PET negative (A+/T-); and Aβ PET positive and tau PET positive (A+/T+), according to the thresholds of the Aβ PET and tau PET SUVR. According to the standard protocol of the ADNI cohort [39], 36, 27, and 116 participants were classified as cognitively unimpaired (CU), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD dementia, respectively. Additionally, we identified A-/T- individuals as healthy controls who did not exhibit cognitive impairment.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the ethical guidelines established by the ethical committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated with Ren Ji Hospital (Protocol ID: 2018-191 and 2020-004), as well as the relevant local regulatory authorities. All of the participants provided written informed consent for the scientific evaluation and publication of their data.

Imaging data acquisition

Imaging data acquisition involved structural MRI (3D T1-weighted) and rs-fMRI data, which were simultaneously acquired on a 3.0 Tesla uPMR790 PET/MR scanner (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). High-resolution 3D structural T1-weighted images were acquired as gradient echoes with the following acquisition parameters: repetition time (TR) = 7.9 ms; echo time (TE) = 3.0 ms; TI = 750 ms; flip angle = 10◦; and field of view (FOV) = 256 mm×256 mm. The rs-fMRI data were acquired by using a gradient-echo echoplanar imaging sequence sensitive to BOLD contrast. Whole-brain coverage (including the cerebellum) was acquired and aligned parallel to the anterior/posterior commissure by using the following parameters: TR = 2068 ms; TE = 30 ms; flip angle = 80◦; FOV = 230 mm×230 mm; matrix = 64 × 64; and resolution = 3.59 × 3.59 × 3.50 mm; scanning time = 8 min and 39 s. During the procedures, the participants were instructed to lie motionless, remain awake, and keep their eyes closed.

The Aβ PET radiotracer 18F-AV-45 and the tau PET radiotracer 18F-PI-2620 were used for Aβ PET and tau PET imaging, respectively. Aβ PET and tau PET data acquisition was performed on a uEXPLORER PET/CT or uPMR790 PET/MR scanner (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). The spatial resolution of the PET/CT or PET/MR scanner was quantified via PET imaging of a Hoffman phantom. Participants underwent Aβ and tau PET following standardized clinical protocols to ensure robust SUVR estimation. For Aβ PET imaging, each participant was intravenously injected with 18F-AV45 (375.0 ± 37.5 MBq). Following a 50-minute post-injection uptake period in a quiet environment, PET images were acquired in a 20-minute window. For tau PET imaging, participants were intravenously injected with 18F-PI-2620 (185.0 ± 18.5 MBq). After a 60-minute rest period, imaging was performed during a similar 20-minute window. To minimize the impact of tracer kinetic variability, all scan sessions were monitored to ensure an actual acquisition duration of 15–20 min, strictly adhering to the recommended standard of 20 ± 5 min.

Resting-state fMRI data analysis

All of the functional image processing was performed by using the Connectivity Toolbox (CONN) [40]. The functional and anatomical data were preprocessed by using a modular preprocessing pipeline, including realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, slice timing correction, outlier detection, direct segmentation, MNI space normalization, and smoothing. The functional data were realigned by using the SPM realign & unwarp procedure, wherein all of the scans were coregistered to a reference image (involving the first scan obtained at the first session) by using a least squares approach and a 6-parameter (rigid body) transformation, after which the scans were resampled by using b-spline interpolation to correct for motion and magnetic susceptibility interactions. Temporal misalignment between different slices of the functional data (acquired in interleaved bottom-up order) was corrected following the SPM slice-timing correction (STC) procedure, which utilized sinc temporal interpolation to resample each slice BOLD time series to a common mid-acquisition time. Potential outlier scans were identified by using the artifact rejection tool (ART) as acquisitions with framewise displacement above 0.9 mm or global BOLD signal changes above 5 standard deviations, which was commonly applied in large-scale resting-state studies [41, 42]. For each participant, a reference BOLD image was computed by averaging all of the scans (excluding outliers). To further ensure data quality, we rechecked the motion parameters for all participants and confirmed that the mean FD remained well below 0.3 mm across all groups. The functional and anatomical data were normalized to the standard MNI space; segmented into gray matter, white matter, and CSF tissue classes; and resampled to 2 mm isotropic voxels following a direct normalization procedure by using an SPM unified segmentation and normalization algorithm with the default IXI-549 tissue probability map template. Finally, the functional data were smoothed by using spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full width half maximum (FWHM).

ReHo is used to identify the functional coherence of a given voxel with its nearest neighbors and can be used to evaluate intrinsic brain activity based on the hypothesis that significant brain activity is more likely to occur in the form of a mass or region consisting of many cluster volumes [43]. ReHo was assessed on a voxel-by-voxel basis by calculating Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (KCC) of the time series of a given voxel with those of its nearest neighbors via the CONN toolbox. Higher ReHo values for a given voxel indicate greater regional functional coherence within a cluster made up of the voxel and its nearest neighbors, whereas lower ReHo values reflect reduced regional synchronization. Compared with the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) or connectivity-based measures, ReHo provides a voxel-wise, data-driven index of localized neural activity, which is thus well-suited for exploring early, region-specific dysfunction in AD. Thus, an individual ReHo map during rest was obtained for each subject. We conducted voxel-wise random-effects two-sample t-tests on the group ReHo map to examine the differences in functional activity between each A/T group and the others, specifically comparing A+/T- vs. A-/T-, A-/T + vs. A-/T-, A+/T + vs. A-/T-, A+/T + vs. A+/T-, A+/T + vs. A-/T+, A+/T- vs. A-/T+. Age, sex, years of education, HCV, and TIV were included as covariates. False discovery rate (FDR) corrected for multiple comparisons was set as p < 0.05 at the cluster level.

PET imaging and analysis

The PET images were coregistered with their corresponding structural MR images in SPM12 (Statistical Parametric Mapping, Welcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, London, UK). Sixty-eight Freesurfer-defined cortical ROIs obtained from MRI segmentation were used to extract the regional SUVR of AV45 and PI-2620 PET from the coregistered PET images. The AV45 PET SUVR of AD summary cortical regions (including the posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, frontal lobe, parietal lobe, and lateral temporal lobe) was calculated as a volume-weighted SUVR by dividing the radiotracer uptake value of typical AD brain regions by that of the whole cerebellum based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas [44]. Aβ PET positive (A+) was defined as AD summary cortical regions SUVR of AV45 ≥ 1.11, and AV45 SUVRs were converted to Centiloids as described previously [45]. The PI-2620 PET SUVR of the AD temporal meta-ROI (including the entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, fusiform gyrus, inferior temporal cortex, and middle temporal cortex) was calculated as a volume-weighted SUVR by normalizing the regional mean PI-2620 uptake to that in the whole cerebellum [46]. In this study, the PI-2620 tau PET SUVR threshold was derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis by maximizing the Youden index, yielding an optimal cutoff of 1.18 (sensitivity 72%, specificity 100%, AUC 0.89) [47]. For the voxel-wise analysis, the AV45 SUVR images were intensity-normalized and spatially normalized at the voxel-wise level in the MNI space in SPM12 as described previously [48]. The thresholds of AV-45 (Aβ) PET and PI-2620 (tau) PET SUVR values were used to divide the whole cohort into the following four pathological groups: A-/T-, A-/T+, A+/T-, and A+/T+ groups.

Causal mediation analysis

We conducted a causal mediation analysis to examine whether (and to what extent) the impact of Aβ PET and tau PET deposition on cognitive status is mediated by the ReHo index score, which was calculated as the weighted mean of the ReHo values within the brain regions demonstrating significant differences across the four pathological groups. Two models were constructed using global Aβ PET and tau PET SUVR values as predictor variables to estimate the direct and indirect (mediation) effects of the ReHo index scores on cognitive scores (i.e., the MMSE and MoCA scores). We adjusted for potential mediator-outcome confounders, including age, sex, education, HCV, and TIV. To address the comparability of the data metrics, we standardized each variable in the analysis. All of the mediation analyses were conducted by using MATLAB, and the results are reported as unstandardized effect sizes. We used bootstrap procedures with 5000 replications to compute a 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval (95% ci) for the indirect effects.

Statistical analysis

All of the statistical analyses were performed by using R (v 4.1.1, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The normal distribution of demographical data in this study was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Demographical characteristics among the healthy controls, A-/T+, A+/T-, and A+/T+ groups were compared using a two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test. Two-tailed t tests were used to compare the ReHo values across all of the brain regions/voxels between groups. A False Discovery Rate (FDR) of 0.05 was corrected to identify a set of significantly different brain regions, denoted as D = {d1​, d2​,.,dk}, where k is the number of group-differential regions. The t value (ti, signed) for each region di (which reflects the direction of the group difference [ti > 0: AD pathological group > other group; ti < 0: AD pathological group < other group]) was recorded. For each subject j, the ReHo value was extracted at each differential region di, which was denoted as ReHoj(di). To integrate ReHo information across differential regions while preserving the direction and magnitude of the group differences, a weighted average method was used, with t values being utilized as weights [49, 50]. Generalized linear models were used to compare the regional ReHo index score, regional Aβ PET SUVR value, and regional tau PET SUVR value within the brain regions showing significant ReHo differences between each A/T group and the others. Finally, we explored whether functional activity changes can modulate the association of Aβ and tau PET with cognitive scores using causal mediation analysis. To maintain comparability across models for subgroup and interaction analyses, the same set of covariates (i.e., age, gender, education, HCV, and TIV) was consistently applied. Results

Demographics of the participants

Participant demographics and summary descriptive statistics of the cognitive assessments and the PET data analyses are provided in Table 1. In this study, we included 179 participants (mean age: 69.7 years; 61% female). The number of A+/T- individuals (N = 53) was 4.81 times greater than that of the A-/T+ individuals (N = 11) (odds ratio [OR] = 4.818, [95% confidence interval [ci], 2.517 ~ 9.224], p < 0.001, McNemar χ2 test), thus indicating that Aβ deposition generally precedes tau pathology. However, individual variability was present, and those individuals classified as A-/T + are typically referred to as exhibiting primary age-related tauopathy (PART) [51]. The relatively small sample size of the A–/T+ group limits the statistical power for between-group comparisons and may reduce the robustness of subgroup-specific findings. The A+/T+ group demonstrated lower MMSE scores (p < 0.01) and MoCA scores (p < 0.01), as well as higher levels of Aβ and tau deposition, compared with the other three groups (p < 0.01). No significant difference was observed for age or years of education across the healthy controls and the remaining groups (p > 0.05).

Divergent patterns of altered functional activity across the spectrum of AD

First, we observed that the mean ReHo values were negatively related to the Aβ PET and tau PET SUVR values in the whole brain (Fig. S1). The mean ReHo maps within each group are shown in Fig. 2. The statistical details of these comparisons can be found in Supplemental Table S1-S6. We observed that compared with the healthy controls, the AD patients (i.e., those in the A+/T- and A+/T+ groups) demonstrated altered ReHo patterns that aligned with the default mode (DMN) and salience network (SAN) [52], including the angular gyrus (AG), paracingulate gyrus (PaCiG), and parahippocampal gyrus (Fig. 2A, B; Table S1, S2).

Furthermore, with respect to the deposition of tau, the A+/T+ individuals demonstrated significant ReHo decreases in the right fusiform gyrus (FG), bilateral postcentral gyrus (PostCG), and left PaCiG, as well as increases in the left superior occipital cortex, right AG, left posterior supramarginal gyrus (SMG), and right PaCiG, in direct comparison with those in the A+/T- group (Fig. 2D). We observed that A-/T+ (i.e., PART) individuals demonstrated abnormal ReHo patterns, which were also similar to those of the DMN, including the temporal pole, middle temporal gyrus, and paracingulate (Fig. 2C).

To determine whether there is a difference in the pathophysiology between AD and PART, we compared the A-/T+ group (PART) with other AD pathological groups. We observed that the AD patients exhibited significant ReHo decreases in the right PreCG, left PostCG, right lingual gyrus (LG), and left supplementary motor area (SMA), as well as ReHo increases in the right cuneus, right temporal pole, left LG, right AG, left posterior SMG, left FG, anterior cingulate gyrus, and left superior parietal lobule, compared with PART individuals (Fig. 2E, F).

Association of altered functional activity with Aβ burden and tau deposition

To ensure that functional activity abnormalities are associated with both Aβ and tau deposition, we implemented generalized linear models to investigate the association of altered functional activity with Aβ burden and tau deposition. We observed that global Aβ PET SUVR (β = -0.45, p < 0.001, cluster-level FDR corrected) and tau PET SUVR (β = -0.57, p < 0.001, cluster-level FDR corrected) were negatively related to the global mean ReHo value (Fig. S1). We further examined whether regional altered functional activity is associated with regional Aβ burden and tau deposition. Specifically, based on the mean ReHo values, we identified the brain regions demonstrating significant differences between the groups and then analyzed the associations between the ReHo index scores and the mean Aβ PET and tau PET SUVR values within these group-differential regions. Significant group differences in whole-brain ReHo index scores were observed among the A/T groups, except between the A+/T + and A−/T+ groups (Figure S2).

We observed that the ReHo index scores within group-differential brain regions identified by comparing the A+/T- and A+/T+ groups with the A-/T- group were negatively associated with the regional Aβ PET SUVR values (β = -2.2, [95% ci, -2.86~-1.55], p < 0.001; β = -3.1, [95% ci, -3.75~-2.48], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A, B). However, we observed that the ReHo index scores within the brain regions exhibiting differences between the A+/T- individuals and A-/T+ individuals were positively correlated with regional Aβ PET SUVR values (β = 0.96, [95% ci, 0.08 ~ 1.84], p = 0.036) (Fig. 3F). Similarly, we observed that the ReHo index scores within group-differential brain regions identified by comparing the A+/T-, A+/T+, and A-/T groups with the A-/T- group were negatively associated with the regional tau PET SUVR values (β = -3, [95% ci, -3.76~-2.16], p < 0.001; β = -4.6, [95% ci, -5.45~-3.71], p < 0.001; β = -5, [95% ci, -6.73 ~ 3.34], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A-C). Notably, group-level regional analyses revealed spatial alignment of functional changes with Aβ burden and tau deposition.

Proteinopathy-related functional activity abnormalities are correlated with cognition

Finally, we conducted association-based mediation analyses to investigate the associations among Aβ/tau PET SUVR, ReHo index score, and cognitive scores, including MMSE and MoCA score. By selecting AD pathological features as predictor variables, we observed a significant correlation between global Aβ PET SUVR and cognitive scores (β = -0.38, [95% ci, -14.37~-6.90], p < 0.001). This association was statistically attenuated when the ReHo index score was included in the model, with a significant indirect association identified (bootstrapped average causal mediation effect: β = -0.08, [95% ci, -4.22~-0.75], p = 0.01) (Fig. 5A), thus indicating a pattern consistent with a partial mediation role of functional activity alterations. Similarly, global tau PET SUVR was significantly associated with cognitive scores (β = −0.29, [95% CI, − 17.01 to − 7.33], p < 0.0001), and the inclusion of the ReHo index score revealed a significant indirect association between tau deposition and cognition (bootstrapped indirect effect: β = −0.17, [95% CI, − 11.04 to − 4.07], p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5B). Notably, a partial association showing a trend toward significance was observed between tau deposition and cognitive scores (β = -0.12, [95% ci, -9.91 ~ 0.11], p = 0.055) after incorporating ReHo index score into the model. Moreover, the ReHo index score was significantly correlated with the cognitive scores across individuals, regardless of their Aβ PET status, whereas this correlation was only observed in tau PET-negative individuals (Fig. 5C). In addition, significant positive correlations were observed between the ReHo index scores of the different brain regions exhibiting significant differences (as identified by comparing the A+/T- and A+/T+ groups with the A-/T- group, as well as by comparing the A+/T + and A+/T- groups) and the MMSE and MoCA scores (Fig. S2, S3). Thus, it is suggested that AD-related proteinopathy, functional brain alterations, and cognitive performance are interrelated in a manner compatible with an indirect association via functional activity, while causal interpretations cannot be inferred from the cross-sectional data.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between AD-related protein deposition and changes in regional functional activity, as well as how these neuropathological alterations subsequently contribute to cognitive decline in patients with AD. Aligning with in vivo and computational disease models, our data revealed that decreases in regional synchronization were mainly associated with both global and regional deposition of Aβ and tau; moreover, AD-related proteinopathy and altered functional activity were observed to be colocalized in the human cortex. In particular, we observed that Aβ-positive and/or tau-positive individuals demonstrated widespread functional hyperactivity across brain regions aligned with the DMN and SAN. Furthermore, Aβ burden and tau deposition may be closely linked to altered functional activity and cognitive decline in AD patients. These findings suggest that cortical Aβ and tau aggregation may be an indicator of functional activity, as measured via ReHo, thus ultimately leading to cognitive decline in patients with AD. This study provides novel insights into how Aβ and tau-related functional activity affect cognitive decline in patients with AD.

We observed altered functional activity (as measured via ReHo) in patients with AD compared with that in healthy controls and PART individuals, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that functional changes such as local neurological homogeneity occur in the preclinical stages of AD [25, 53, 54]. Importantly, some studies using rs-fMRI have revealed that the ReHo index is significantly decreased in the right inferior parietal lobule, left precuneus, left inferior temporal gyrus, right supramarginal gyrus, right fusiform gyrus, and cerebellum but increased in the right medial frontal gyrus, bilateral lingual gyrus, bilateral postcentral gyrus, and left cuneus [26, 55]. Our results were consistent with those observed in earlier studies. Compared with healthy controls, both A+/T- and A+/T+ individuals exhibited functional hyperactivity and hypoactivity within DMN- and SAN-related regions, thus reinforcing the central effects of these networks on AD-related network dysfunction. Unlike previous studies, we observed that A+ individuals demonstrated increased local brain activity in more regions than those exhibiting decreased activity, thus potentially reflecting a compensatory mechanism for functional decline.

A considerable body of research initially conducted with AD mouse models has suggested that the aggregation of pathological proteins can disrupt microscopic cellular activity and circuitry, thus leading to functional brain alterations [35, 56]. Consistent with prior research, our findings demonstrated that both global and regional Aβ and tau deposition were correlated with mean ReHo values, thereby reinforcing the notion that AD-related proteinopathy disrupts functional brain activity in humans. Notably, our results indicate that increased Aβ burden and tau deposition are associated with decreased local coherence of brain activity. One fMRI study demonstrated that the ReHo value of the superior temporal gyrus was negatively related to the Aβ level measured via the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [57]. In addition, previous studies conducted in patients with AD have reported that the DMN- and SAN-related regions are selectively vulnerable to early Aβ and tau deposition [58–60]. Aligning with previous findings, we observed that the DMN- and SAN-related brain regions demonstrated functional hyperactivity and hypoactivity (including the angular gyrus, temporal pole, paracingulate gyrus, and parahippocampal gyrus) in AD patients. In particular, consistent with the results of prior studies [12, 18], this study demonstrated that the brain regions exhibiting altered brain activity spatially overlap with the regions exhibiting Aβ and tau deposition. Importantly, these findings provide direct evidence that microscopic alterations (such as neuronal excitation) manifest as aberrant patterns at the macroscopic scale of functional activity.

Earlier studies conducted in patients with MCI and AD have reported that early deposition of Aβ and tau can impair brain activity, thereby eventually contributing to cognitive decline [34, 35, 61]. In this study, a direct association of ReHo index scores with cognitive scores was observed across all of the participants (with the exception of tau PET-positive individuals). Furthermore, we observed that the association between tau deposition and cognitive performance was only partially significant. These findings suggest that with increased aggregation of tau, the coupling of brain activity and cognitive function becomes disrupted, thereby resulting in a disassociation between altered functional activity and cognitive performance in tau-positive individuals [60, 62]. A recent study reported that increased tau deposition was associated with frontal hypometabolism but not with memory decline [16]. Interestingly, after adjusting for age, sex, education, HCV, and TIV, we observed that regional ReHo index scores mediated the association between imaging biomarkers (Aβ PET and tau PET SUVR) and cognitive scores, thereby indicating that AD proteinopathy may impair cognitive function via the disruption of functional brain activity. Recent studies have demonstrated that altered functional activation is associated with Aβ deposition and cognitive performance using task-based fMRI [15, 63]. Consistently, the results of the present study demonstrate that cortical Aβ and tau deposition may alter resting-state functional activity (as reflected by ReHo), which correspondingly contributes to cognitive decline in AD patients. Notably, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than direct causal effects, given the cross-sectional design of this study.

Although previous studies have emphasized that tau pathology is more strongly correlated with cognitive decline compared with Aβ [64], our findings indicate that Aβ pathology also exerts a measurable effect on brain function and cognition, which is possibly achieved by initiating network-level vulnerability that renders certain brain regions more susceptible to tau-related damage [18, 31]. This dual mediation by Aβ and tau deposition highlights the necessity of a multimodal approach to fully capture the continuum of AD pathophysiology (ranging from molecular deposition to functional brain alterations and behavioral manifestations). Furthermore, Aβ burden and tau deposition may be closely linked to altered functional activity and cognitive decline in AD patients. These findings suggest that cortical Aβ and tau aggregation may be an indicator of functional activity, as measured via ReHo, thus ultimately leading to cognitive decline in patients with AD.

Via the combination of PET-derived pathology with fMRI-based brain functional indices, our findings may increase the sensitivity and specificity of AD diagnosis and staging because of the heterogeneity of AD. Although PET imaging directly quantifies molecular pathology, fMRI captures the downstream physiological consequences of these pathological processes on neuronal communication [36]. Compared with the use of Aβ or tau PET alone, resting-state fMRI offers a noninvasive, radiation-free, and relatively more accessible measure that can reveal functional alterations even in the preclinical stages of AD [26]. Furthermore, as functional synchronization is dynamically modifiable, fMRI may serve as a promising biomarker for monitoring disease progression and evaluating therapeutic efficacy, particularly for interventions aimed at restoring neural network function or reducing abnormal excitability [64]. This is important to given that hybrid PET/MRI systems may not be uniformly available in multi-center studies, rs-fMRI can be acquired independently of PET imaging, offering a more accessible and scalable tool for research on imaging biomarkers of AD. Standardized preprocessing pipelines, harmonization strategies, and rigorous confound removal can substantially improve the reliability and biological validity of functional measures across sites [65, 66]. While functional brain changes so far have not been proven to be reliable biomarkers of neurodegenerative disease, advances in high-field MRI (≥ 7 T) provide unprecedented spatial resolution (< 1 mm), enabling the examination of cortical microcircuits and small subcortical nuclei which are critical for early AD pathology [35]. Therefore, this study might imply a more precise integration of structural, functional, and molecular information, even in the absence of PET imaging data, thereby supporting large-scale, multi-center efforts to map disease progression.

However, this study has several limitations. First, an important limitation of the present study concerns the absence of clinical follow-up data. Although participants were classified according to the A/T biomarker framework, this biological categorization does not provide information about clinical stage or future disease trajectory. In particular, biomarker-positive cognitively unimpaired individuals may represent heterogeneous populations, including participants undergoing normal aging as well as individuals in a prodromal phase of AD. In the absence of clinical follow-up data, our results should be interpreted as cross-sectional associations between AD-related proteinopathy, functional brain alterations, and cognition, rather than as markers of disease progression or conversion risk. Considering that clinical longitudinal follow-up is essential to disentangle stable aging trajectories from those leading to mild cognitive impairment [67], future longitudinal studies incorporating serial clinical assessments alongside multimodal biomarkers are critical to determine whether the functional brain alterations identified in this cohort serve as reliable predictors of subsequent cognitive decline or clinical conversion. Second, in the present study, only the MMSE and MoCA were used to assess cognitive performance, which may not be optimal for identifying more specific cognitive impairments. Future studies incorporating a more comprehensive neuropsychological battery will be valuable to further extend our findings. Third, the relatively small sample size of the A-/T+ group limits the statistical power for between-group comparisons and may reduce the robustness of subgroup-specific findings. This limitation likely reflects the inherent rarity of the A-/T+ biomarker profile in the general population, as reported in previous studies [62, 68]. Therefore, the results pertaining to this group should be viewed as preliminary and interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger and more balanced samples are warranted to validate these findings. Finally, this study lacked evidence from fluid biomarkers that can detect pathology earlier than the use of PET and MRI. Future research should incorporate CSF or plasma biomarkers and genetic data to refine AD pathological staging and explore the mechanisms underlying functional network alterations.

Conclusions

Overall, the present study elucidated the association of altered functional activity (which is driven by Aβ burden and tau deposition) with cognitive decline in patients with AD. This study highlights the importance of considering both Aβ and tau pathology interactions with brain functional changes to fully understand AD progression. By identifying the mediating association of functional activity dysfunction with Aβ, tau and cognitive impairment, this study provides a foundation for elucidating the pathology and progression patterns of AD.
