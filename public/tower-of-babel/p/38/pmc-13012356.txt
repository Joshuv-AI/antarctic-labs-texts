Development and validation of the Attitude to Mental Illness-14 (AMI-14) Scale, a brief version of the AMI-20, through a multicentric survey involving medical undergraduates
Authors: Snehil Gupta, Akash Kumar, Rajeev Ranjan, Pankaj Kumar, Aditya Somani, Ajay Kumar, Saurabh Shekhar, Arshi Khanam, Atul Keche, Ahmad Najmi, Ashish Pakhre, Vijender Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The Attitude to Mental Illness-20 (AMI 20) questionnaire is a widely used instrument to assess medical students’ Attitudes Toward Mental Illness and Psychiatry (ATMIP). However, it lacks psychometric assessment, limiting its wider utility. The current study was undertaken to assess the latent structure of the AMI 20 and establish its psychometric properties.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This observational study involved 88 fourth-semester medical undergraduates from three institutes in India. Through an online survey, sociodemographic data along with their ATMIP (attitude toward Psychiatry 30 (ATP-30) and AMI 20 questionnaire) and their willingness to enroll in the Early Exposure Psychiatry Enrichment Program (EEPEP) were collected. Characteristics of the participants were reported through descriptive statistics. Fleiss Kappa coefficient was used for content validity. Reliability of the AMI-14 scale was analyzed using Cronbach's alpha (internal consistency). Concurrent validity (correlating the ATP-30 and AMI-14 and subdomain scores) and convergent validity (bivariate regression analysis with willingness to join the EEPEP and AMI-14 scores as explanatory and responsive variables, respectively) were assessed. The relationship between gender and willingness to enroll in the EEPEP and ATP 30 and AMI-14 scores were assessed by the t-test and ANOVA, respectively.RESULTS:The AMI-14 scale has two dimensions: knowledge (6 items) and attitude (8 items). The scale has moderately good content validity (Fleiss Kappa coefficient = 0.47), acceptable internal consistency (α >0.70), and sound concurrent (r = 0.83, P < 0.001) and construct validity (Estimate = 5.66, 95% CI: 2.7–8.7, P < 0.001).CONCLUSIONS:The AMI-14 scale with its two domains, has reasonable psychometric properties. Greater cross-cultural validity of the scale is recommended.

Introduction

Attitudes Toward Mental Illness and Psychiatry (ATMIP) among medical undergraduates have been widely assessed across the globe, including the Indian subcontinent, with mixed findings.[123456] Researchers have investigated medical students’ and health professionals’ ATMIP through different methodologies and assessment tools. Notably, variations in the research findings across the population and setting, stage of course, type of educational interventions, etc., are also determined by the types of assessment tools used and in which context. Hence, it becomes vital that the scales should be chosen based on their proven psychometric properties and in the right context.

Among the available scales to test the students’ knowledge and ATMIP, Attitude Toward Psychiatry-30 (ATP-30) and Attitude to Mental Illness Scale-20 (AMI-20) are the most widely used.[123789] Although ATP-30 has been cross-culturally validated, the AMI-20 is still elusive in psychometric assessments.[101112] Strikingly, researchers have taken the liberty to combine AMI-20 (with other scales, e.g., ATP-30) or concurrently use it with other scales (e.g., ATP-30, Mental Illness: Clinicians Attitudes Scale) to assess medical pupils’ ATMIP. For instance, Desai and Chavda[9] have clubbed AMI-20 and ATP-30 to form a 50-item scale and assessed students’ attitudes toward mentally ill individuals, psychiatric diagnoses and treatment, and psychiatry as a discipline. The authors found that students had neutral responses to the scales’ many items and had limited changes in their following psychiatry clinical rotations. Pal and Singh, Tharyan et al., and Risal et al.[137] have employed similar flexible approaches.

Remarkably, Singh et al.,[4] the developer of the AMI-20, have cautioned the readers about its unestablished psychometric properties and that it could not be reduced into meaningful factors to be able to capture different dimensions of ATMIP. They also emphasized that attitude is a complex interaction between knowledge, awareness of therapeutic potentialities of psychotropic medications and therapies, and patient contact.[4] Change in attitude, or, for that matter, stigma toward mental illness, involves different dimensions: stereotype (knowledge), prejudice (attitudinal aspect), and discrimination (behavior).[131415]

Therefore, it is imperative to study the factor structure of AMI 20 in terms of psychiatry and mental health-related knowledge, attitudes, and behavior. For instance, item 8 of AMI 20 (Psychiatric drugs are mainly used to control disruptive behavior) and item 14 (Alcohol abusers have no self-control) largely capture the knowledge part; students/health professionals who are not exposed to the basics of psychopharmacology or addiction psychiatry would respond to these items based on their existing knowledge or cultural stereotypes; hence, may give an inaccurate result (negative attitude) toward psychiatric patients or treatment. Similarly, item 2 (The mentally ill should be discouraged from marrying), item 5 (Psychiatric diagnoses stigmatize people and should not be used), and item 17 (Care in the community for the mentally ill puts society at risk) cover attitudinal aspects around rights of persons with mental illness, diagnostic labelling, treatment, and behavior of psychiatric patients.[4]

Therefore, using AMI-20 to compare medical students’ ATMIP based on their psychiatry exposure, comparison across various phases of the medical course or across the professional disciplines, and the impact of educational interventions can be inaccurate and unreliable.

These warrant exploring the latent structure (dimensionality of the scale) of the AMI-20 concerning psychiatry and mental illness. It would be worthwhile to assess the psychometric properties of this scale, so that this useful instrument can be utilized more widely and robustly. Therefore, this study was carried out with the primary objective of exploring the factor structure and psychometric properties (validity and reliability) of the AMI-20 questionnaire. The secondary objectives were to assess the association of gender and baseline willingness of the pupils to join Early Exposure Psychiatry Enrichment Program (EEPEP; discussed later) with their AMI-20 and ATP-30 scale scores.

The current study is part of a larger interventional study aimed at exploring the feasibility and effectiveness of EEPEPs for medical undergraduates in improving the knowledge, attitude, and ethical considerations towards persons with mental illnesses and psychiatry.[16]

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted this cross-sectional observational study across three medical institutions (Centre-1, All India Institute of Medical Sciences [AIIMS], Bhopal; Centre-2, AIIMS, Raipur; and Centre-3, AIIMS, Patna) of national importance located in geographically different regions of India.

Study participants and sampling

We followed a convenient sampling technique to recruit the participants. The participants were 2nd-year (4th semester MBBS) medical undergraduates with no previous psychiatry exposure, except a few sessions on stress management and Attitude, Ethics, and Communication as part of the foundation course. Typically, students in the participating institutes get exposed to psychiatry (whether in the form of theory or clinical rotation), during their 3rd year (5th semester) or later [Figure 1].

Flowchart for the participants’ enrolment in the study and subsequent participation

Data collection tool and technique

All the potential participants (n = 125 in each site) were oriented about the study by the site investigators, including inviting them for early exposure to a psychiatry enrichment program (EEPEP – an early clinical exposure in psychiatry voluntarily, during out-of-routine training hours). The course curriculum involved semi-formal, novel teaching-learning activities (e.g., movie club, self-directed learning, debates, shadow psychiatry residents, etc.) around mental health and psychiatry.[16] The survey form link was shared with them on their official email ID and through their class representative on WhatsApp.

As the survey was limited to 2nd-year MBBS students, all of them naturally became potential participants of the study, provided they were willing to consent to it, and as such, there were no exclusion criteria. The survey form (Kobo toolbox form) included an information sheet, an informed consent form, details of the enrichment program, and the study scale.

Instruments for data collection

The data collection tool included the participants’ sociodemographic and educational details, willingness to enroll in the enrichment program, and their knowledge and attitude toward psychiatry assessed through ATP-30 and AMI-20.

Attitude Toward Psychiatry-30 (ATP-30) is a 30-item self-report scale with a Likert scoring ranging from 1 (“strong agreement” with the statement) to 5 (“strong disagreement”), with 3 representing “neutral/no opinion” about a statement. Many items represent a positive outlook toward psychiatry (4–5, 9–12, 14–15, 18, 20, 23, 25, and 27–29) and hence were reverse-scored. A higher score indicates a more positive attitude. Its psychometric properties have been well-established.[212]

Attitude Toward Mental Illness – 20 (AMI-20) questionnaire evaluates students’ ATMIP (causes, treatment, consequences, and impact on the individual and society). AMI-20 contains 20 items and a Likert scoring ranging from 1 (“strong agreement”) to 5 (“strong disagreement”), with “neutral” and “don’t know” scored as 0. Most of the statements denote a negative expression/attitude toward mental illness and psychiatry (exception: items 10, 15, and 18). Therefore, a strong agreement with the statements reflects a negative attitude toward mental illnesses (e.g., item-9: ECT should be banned). However, its psychometric properties have not been explored, including in India.[4]

Ethical consideration

Students’ participation in this survey was voluntary and had no bearing on their prospective psychiatry training. The research review boards of all three participating institutes approved the research proposal. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

The study was funded by the research cell of the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Bhopal, as part of the intramural research grant (RC/AIIMS-BPL-2022/IM-TA/AP/241).

Statistical analysis method

Participants’ basic details, willingness to enroll on EEPEP, and outcome measures (ATP-30 and AMI-20) scores were analyzed through descriptive statistics. The Pearson’s r test was used for the correlation between ATP-30 and updated AMI-20 domains (AMI-14, henceforth). We used the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess the data’s normality. The test of significance was kept at P < 0.05.

The face validity of the AMI-20 questionnaires was established by seeking the input of the experts (one psychologist and four psychiatrists) and medical students (two). Their responses on individual items’ domain(s) (knowledge, attitude, or both related to mental illness) were represented as n, %.[1718] The content validity was calculated using proportionate agreement on each item and category (knowledge, attitude, and both). The Fleiss kappa coefficient was calculated to measure the level of agreement among the raters, with a score of >0.4 considered moderately good.[19]

The “maximum likelihood” extraction method was combined with “varimax” rotation for exploratory factor analysis.[20] Although, conventionally, a sample size of 5–10 participants per item is required for conducting factor analysis, we also relied on statistical way of determining the datas’ suitability for the item reduction by checking for Bartletts’ test of sphericity (a value < 0.05 is considered suitable) and ascertaining sample’s adequacy by calculating KMO measures (a value > 0.6 is considered a good fit). Root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA, 90% CI) was calculated to assess model fit (RMSEA < 0.06 is considered a suitable model). Inter-item and item-rest correlation were performed to establish the relationships between items within the scales and between the score of an item and the score on the rest of the scale. A correlation coefficient of <0.3 is considered undesirable; therefore, an item with inter-item correlation or item-rest correlation below this cutoff was removed from the final model.[20] A scree plot was also used to assess the latent factors on the AMI-20 questionnaire. The variance accounted for by each factor on the overall model was also assessed.[21]

Reliability testing involved Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency.[2223] Criterion validity was analyzed using concurrent validity, i.e., the correlation between the scores on the ATP-30 and AMI-14 and its subdomains. A product-moment correlation matrix was created to analyze this.[24] As a measure of construct validity, convergent validity was performed using bivariate regression analysis,[2526] wherein a regression model was created and analyzed using the willingness to join the enrichment program as the explanatory variable and AMI-14 scores as the responsive variable. Free version of the Jamovi software was used for data analysis.[27]

The manuscript has been prepared as per the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement and extant literature on tool development and validation.[2028]

The mean ATP-30 and AMI-20 scores were 110 (11.7) and 62.3 (6.9), respectively, with AMI-14 (the scale developed from AMI-20) knowledge domain (possible score range: 6–30) score being 14.3 (2.9) and that of attitude domain (possible score range: 8–40) being 27 (4.2) [Table 1].

Content validity of AMI-20 questionnaire

As high as 10 items (items 6, 8, 9, 11–16, and 20) on the scale explored a substantial degree of knowledge domain among the students. For example, item 6 enquired whether mental illnesses are wrongly diagnosed in women and ethnic minorities. Item 8 talked about how psychiatric drugs are mostly used to control disruptive behavior. Similarly, item 13 explores whether mental illness is the result of adverse social circumstances [Supplementary Table 2]. The Fleiss-Kappa with three categories of responses (knowledge, attitude, and both) was found to be 0.47, revealing a moderately good-good level of agreement among the raters [Supplementary Table 3].

Exploratory factor analysis of the AMI-20 questionnaire

The exploratory factor analysis of the AMI-20 questionnaire extracted two main dimensions that can be labeled as knowledge domain (items 3, 8, 13, 14, 16, and 19) and attitude domain (items 2, 4, 5, 7, 11, 12, 17, and 18) [Supplementary Figure 1 and Table 3]. The two-factor structure of the scale accounted for a 21.4% variance in the overall scale, while three factors led to a 24.7% variance in the total model; however, the latter resulted in many items cross-loading between themselves. Therefore, to come up with the most parsimonious model in terms of internal consistency and latent construct of the scale, we resorted to the two-factor model of the AMI-14 scale.[22]

Item 1 (Psychiatric patients generally speaking are difficult to like) was found to cross-load on both factors; hence, it was removed from the final analysis. Items 6, 9, 10, 15, and 20 did not load to any factor; therefore, they were not involved in the final scale. The criteria for the factorability of the scale (Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² =440, df = 190, P < 0.001)) and sample adequacy (KMO = 0.68, good fit) were met [Table 3]. The model fit was found to be within the statistical criteria (RMSEA = 0.058, Chi-square value = 199, P < 0.05). The Cronbach alpha (internal consistency) for the original scale, AMIQ-20, was 0.40 (which was below the acceptable range of 0.70). However, upon item reduction, the value of the Cronbach alpha improved to 0.70 (within the acceptable range) for the AMIQ-14 and 0.61 and 0.68 for the AMIQ-14 knowledge and attitude domains, respectively. These findings highlight that the item reduction was worthwhile.

Psychometric properties of the brief AMI-14 scale

Criterion validity: We found that the AMI-14 knowledge score did not correlate with the ATP-30 score (r = −0.09, P = 0.41), while its attitude score strongly correlated with the ATP-30 score (r = 0.83, P < 0.001). Moreover, the two dimensions of the AMI-14 (knowledge and attitude) domain did not correlate with each other (r = 0.15, P = 0.16), indicating that the AMI-14 scale comprises two distinct and unrelated sets of attributes [Table 4].

Construct validity: We used bivariate regression analysis to measure the construct validity of the AMI-14. However, the model fit was weak (Adjusted R2 = 0.14, only 14% variability in the dependent variable could be explained by the willingness to join the enrichment program). We found a change from not willing/unsure to join the enrichment program to willing to enroll in the program increased the AMI-14 score by 5.66 (SE = 1.3, 95% CI: 2.7–8.7, P < 0.001) [Table 4].

Reliability: The internal consistency of the scale, including its subdomains, was found to be within the acceptable range (α >0.70) [Table 5].

Discussion

The current study explored the latent structure and psychometric properties of the AMI-20 questionnaire and developed its briefer version, AMI-14. It also analyzed the relationship between medical students’ AMI-14 and ATP-30 scores and their willingness to enroll in the EEPEP. To our knowledge, this is a pioneer work in establishing the psychometric properties of the AMI-20 questionnaire.

The face validity of the AMI-20 questionnaire revealed that it comprises items related to knowledge, attitude, or both domains related to psychiatry and mental illness. Therefore, unlike what previous researchers have done, its score needs to be analyzed contextually and dimensionally rather than simply totaling the overall score to gauge participants’ ATMIP.[137] The AMI-14 was found to have good face and content validity.

The exploratory factor analysis revealed that the AMI-14 questionnaire has two domains: knowledge (items 3, 8, 13, 14, 16, and 19) and attitude (2, 4, 5, 7, 11, 12, 17, and 18). The scale had acceptable content, concurrent and construct validity, and reasonable internal consistency.

In our study, most (71.6%) participants were willing to enroll in the EEPEP. There was no gender difference in terms of attitude (or knowledge) toward psychiatry and mental illness. Willingness to enroll in the EEPEP was associated with greater scores on the ATP-30 and AMI-14 knowledge and attitude domains.

The item reduction phase revealed that item 1 (Psychiatric patients, generally speaking, are difficult to like), a rather abstract statement potentially covering both knowledge and attitudinal aspects, cross-loaded on both the factors, and was thus subsequently removed.[2021] Keeping such items could have adversely affected the scales’ psychometric properties. Moreover, removing such items provided us with a parsimonious model.

We found that item 6 (Mental illnesses are wrongly diagnosed in women and ethnic minorities), 9 (ECT should be banned), 10 (People who take an overdose need compassionate treatment), 15 (Mental illnesses are genetic in origin), and item 20 (Patients with chronic schizophrenia are incapable of looking after themselves) did not load to any of the domains. On a deeper look, we found that these items explored context that may not be relevant in the Indian setting versus the West (item 6),[29] or warranting critical thinking, such as the etiology of mental illness (item 15),[30] treatment or right to receive treatment (item 10), including treatment effectiveness (item 9),[3132] and outcome of mental illness (item 20).[3334] Such understanding may not develop in medical students (or be prudent to assess) unexposed to psychiatry. Greater research is required in the latter area, particularly qualitative and by involving students from different course phases.

Concerning the concurrent validity, the attitude domains of the AMI-14 correlated strongly with the ATP-30 scores (r = 0.83, P < 0.001). In contrast, knowledge domains did not correlate with the ATP-30, informing the two-factor structure of the scale. Mental health-related stigma comprises cognitive (roughly representing stereotype), attitude (prejudice), and behavior (discrimination) aspects.[131535] They may not necessarily go hand in hand, thus requiring different anti-stigma strategies. The two domains of the AMI-14, with differential correlation with the ATP-30 (assesses primarily attitudes of the respondents), make it a comprehensive instrument to assess students’ knowledge and ATMIP.

The construct validity of the AMI-14 showed that AMI-14 scores positively correlated with willingness to join the EEPEP. This supports the notion that those with a positive ATMIP would be willing to explore this subject and remain motivated to learn it early in their training versus those with poorer attitudes. Literature from developed nations on psychiatry-enrichment activities[36] revealed that many students later chose psychiatry as a career.[3738] However, in the present study, this variable only accounts for a 14% variance in the AMI-14 scores, and other potential determinants can influence the attitude of the study participants toward psychiatry.

The internal consistency of AMI-14, including its subdomains, was in the acceptable range.

Limitations and recommendations

The participants were only 4th-semester undergraduate students with no prior exposure to psychiatry; therefore, the more nuanced picture that could have otherwise been obtained involving different phases of the MBBS students is missing. Some of the items (e.g., Mental illnesses are wrongly diagnosed in women and ethnic minorities) got dropped from the final scale; however, this item might be relevant in countries with marked ethnic diversities (e.g., the United Kingdom and the United States) and thus may limit its cross-cultural utility. The final scale was not externally validated, which might limit its generalizability. Lastly, we did not perform a cognitive interview of the respondents, which could have provided useful insights about the research topic.

AMI-14 seems to be a useful tool to assess the medical students’ and health professionals’ ATMIP by using established psychometric properties. Its two domains can provide a comprehensive coverage of students’ knowledge and attitude and their subsequent behavior toward PwMI and psychiatry. Scale’s items or items that have been removed from the AMI-20 during the development of the current scale might not be relevant for some cultures. It is recommended that the scoring of the scale should be done based on the two categories rather than combining them to provide an overall score, as done inadvertently by researchers in the past. However, it requires cross-cultural validation. Furthermore, the scale can be externally validated; similarly, its test-retest reliability can be further assessed through a longitudinal study design.

Conclusion

The present study constructed a briefer version, AMI-14, and established its psychometric properties in the Indian setting. The scale has two domains: knowledge and attitudes. The scale can be comprehensively used among medical students from different stages of medical courses to evaluate their ATMIP and its change following clinical rotation in psychiatry and other interventions to reduce mental illness-related stigma. In addition, external validation and supplementing the scale with qualitative research design are required.
