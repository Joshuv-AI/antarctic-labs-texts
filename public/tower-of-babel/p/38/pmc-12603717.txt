Ferroptosis-associated genes as candidate biomarkers for the identification of patients with severe influenza necessitating mechanical ventilation
Authors: Xiaohui Mei, Jie Hua, Liang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptotic cell death is a highly regulated process characterised by the iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides on cell membranes. However, the role of ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) in severe influenza is not well characterised.MethodsInfluenza-related gene expression data and FRG lists were, respectively, downloaded from the gene expression omnibus and ferroptosis database. Differentially expressed FRGs (DE-FRGs) were then identified by comparing samples from patients with and without severe influenza, respectively, defined by patients who did and did not require mechanical ventilation. Enrichment analyses of these DE-FRGs were performed, and hub genes were subsequently identified, enabling the establishment of hub gene–drug interaction and competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks.ResultsIn total, these analyses revealed 171 DE-FRGs from patients with and without severe influenza. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination algorithms were used to identify eight hub genes from this dataset (ALOX12, MUC1, stearoyl-CoA desaturase, DECR1, EZH2, toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), RICTOR and GSTM1), all of which exhibited good diagnostic utility for severe influenza. Functional enrichment analyses indicated that these genes may influence influenza pathogenesis through the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis approaches indicated that the expression levels for these hub DE-FRGs were negatively correlated with lymphocyte activity, whereas they were positively correlated with inflammatory cell activity. Predictive analyses additionally enabled the identification of 71 drugs targeting five of these genes, while ceRNA network diagrams highlighted the complex regulatory relationships among these genes.ConclusionThe eight hub FRGs established in this study may play an important role in shaping the pathogenesis of severe influenza in individuals and may offer value as biomarkers that can guide the personalised prevention and treatment of this severe form of disease.Keywords: Viral infection, Respiratory Infection

Introduction

Influenza is a highly contagious respiratory disease caused by the influenza virus. Despite marked advances in medical and pharmaceutical technologies, influenza remains a leading cause of infectious illness-related hospitalisation and mortality.1 An estimated 10%–20% of all people experience symptomatic influenza infections each year, resulting in 3–5 million cases of severe disease and 290 000–650 000 deaths annually.2 While many patients experience only self-limiting upper respiratory tract infections, others suffer from severe pneumonia following influenza virus infection.3 4 Patients with critically ill influenza often exhibit symptoms of severe respiratory failure resulting in the need for mechanical ventilation, and these patients can exhibit mortality rates of up to 50%–80%.5 6 Despite the high rates of influenza-related morbidity and mortality, the precise molecular aetiology underlying severe influenza infections is poorly understood.

Ferroptosis is a form of iron-dependent cell death distinct from necrotic or apoptotic death that is characterised by the accumulation of excessively high levels of iron, lipid peroxidation and the eventual rupture of the plasma membrane.7 Recent research has revealed a strong association between influenza virus infection and the activation of ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death.8,10 One of the key mechanisms involves the virus-induced accumulation of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROSs), which disrupts redox homeostasis within host cells. The replication efficiency of influenza A virus has been shown to be closely tied to the host’s redox status, particularly levels of glutathione (GSH), a major cellular antioxidant.8 A growing body of evidence has also highlighted the critical role of intracellular iron in facilitating viral replication.9 Influenza viruses appear to initiate ferroptosis by elevating lipid ROS, depleting GSH reserves and suppressing the expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a key enzyme in the detoxification of lipid peroxides. Moreover, haemagglutinin, a viral surface glycoprotein, has been implicated in triggering ferroptosis through ferritinophagy—a process that degrades ferritin and releases free iron—thereby accelerating lipid peroxidation and impairing the mitochondrial antiviral signalling pathway. This disruption weakens the type I interferon (IFN) response and facilitates enhanced viral propagation.10 Supporting this mechanism, Liproxstain-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, was found to significantly reduce viral replication, underscoring the dependence of influenza viruses on host iron metabolism for efficient replication.11

The present study was developed to explore the utility of ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) as biomarkers associated with the pathogenesis of severe influenza, as a series of bioinformatics approaches were employed to more fully explore the relationships between these particular genes and immune cell activity in samples collected from patients with influenza.

Materials and methods

Data collection

For these analyses, mRNA expression profiles were analysed in the GSE111368 and GSE101702 datasets downloaded from the gene expression omnibus database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). These datasets were both selected as they included only patients>15 years old who were infected with influenza virus as confirmed based on reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analyses of respiratory tract samples and for whom disease severity had been categorised. For these analyses, severe influenza was defined by the requirement for mechanical ventilation, whereas all other patients were classified as having non-severe disease. The GSE111368 microarray dataset consisted of 69 and 160 whole-blood samples from individuals with severe and non-severe influenza, respectively. The GSE101702 microarray dataset consisted of 44 and 63 whole-blood samples from patients with severe and non-severe influenza, respectively, and was used to validate the expression patterns of identified hub FRGs.

Ferroptosis database was the source of FRGs (n=484) analysed in the present study (online supplemental file 1). The drug gene interaction database (DGIdb, https://dgidb.genome.wustl.edu/) was used to identify drugs predicted to target hub FRGs.

Differential expression analyses

Expression data for 285 FRGs in the GSE111368 dataset were successfully extracted, and 171 differentially expressed FRGs (DE-FRGs) were identified by comparing the expression of these FRGs between the two patient groups in R using student’s t-tests (online supplemental file 2) after determining the normality of the data distribution, with p<0.05 as the significance threshold.

Functional enrichment analyses

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the R clusterprofiler package (online supplemental file 3). Analysed GO terms included those in the biological process, molecular function and cellular component (CC) categories. An adjusted p value<0.05 was the significance threshold.

Optimal diagnostic DE-FRG identification

A reduction in the dimensionality of these data was achieved using the glmnet package (V.4.1–8) and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm. Feature selection was achieved by retaining DE-FRGs identified when comparing samples with and without severe influenza, and the LASSO algorithm was then used to identify diagnostic genes associated with severe disease. In addition, the e1071 package (V.1.7–14) was used to implement a support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) model with tenfold cross validation. Optimal diagnostic biomarkers of severe influenza were then selected based on the overlap between the gene sets generated by these two algorithms. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve approach was used to gauge the diagnostic utility of these genes based on the analyses of their diagnostic accuracy, specificity, sensitivity and area under the curve (AUC) values. A logistic regression model was also established based on the eight identified hub DE-FRGs and used to predict sample classification in the GSE111368 dataset with the R glm package. ROC curves were additionally used to assess the diagnostic power of the resultant logistic regression model.

Immune cell analyses analysis

A single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) approach was used to compute the levels of 28 different types of immune cells in individual influenza patient samples relative to healthy samples from the training dataset (GSE111368). Violin plots were conducted to visualise differences in such immune cell infiltration between severe and non-severe influenza groups using the Wilcoxon test (online supplemental file 4). Spearman’s correlation analysis results for the associations between hub genes and immune cell populations were visualised with the ‘ggplot2’ package (V.0.4.0).

ssGSEA enrichment analysis

The R GSEA package (V.1.58.0) was used to implement this analysis. To further explore pathways associated with identified marker genes, we calculated the correlations between these marker genes and all other genes in the GSE111368 dataset. Then, all genes were rank ordered based on the strength of these correlations and served as the testing gene set. The KEGG signalling pathway set was used as a predefined set for gene set enrichment analyses.

Single-gene gene set variation analysis (GSVA) enrichment analysis

The R GSVA package (v 1.44.5) was used to implement a GSVA analysis using the KEGG pathway gene set as a background gene set when performing GSVA analyses for individual marker genes. The R limma package (V.3.52.4) was employed to assess differences in GSVA scores for samples expressing low and high levels of a given marker gene, using the following criteria: |t|>2, p<0.05. If t>0, pathways were considered highly activated in the high-expression group, whereas if t<0, pathways were considered activated in the low-expression group.

Competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network establishment

Predicted microRNAs (miRNA) likely to interact with identified hub DE-FRGs were selected using StarBase. In addition, miRNA target genes were identified with the miRNA Target Prediction Database (miRDB), TargetScan and miRanda tools based on DE-FRG mRNA sequences downloaded from NCBI and miRNA sequences downloaded from miRbase (online supplemental file 5). StarBase was additionally screened for predicted miRNA–lncRNA interactions (online supplemental file 6), and the resultant data were then used to construct a mRNA–miRNA–lncRNA ceRNA network (online supplemental file 7).

Verification of hub genes in the test dataset

Finally, the expression profiles for these identified hub genes were validated in a separate dataset (GSE101702) between patients with and without severe influenza by using student’s t-tests after evaluating distribution normality. P<0.05 was defined as the significance threshold.

Statistical analysis

Data were compared between two groups with student’s t-tests, while correlations among DE-FRGs were assessed with Pearson’s correlation analyses. Venn diagrams were constructed with the Jvenn package, while ceRNA network visualisation achieved with Cytoscape R (V.4.2.0) was used for all analyses, and p<0.05 was the significance threshold.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

DE-FRG identification

When comparing samples from individuals with and without severe influenza in the GSE111368, 171 DE-FRGs were identified, of which 112 and 59 were, respectively, upregulated and downregulated in individuals with severe disease (figure 1A and online supplemental file 2). Correlations among these genes are provided in figure 1B and online supplemental file 2.

DE-FRGs expression levels in severe influenza. (A) The heatmap shows expression patterns of DE-FRGs across samples (15 DE-FRGs displayed). Red colour means upregulation, while blue means downregulation. (B) The correlation of these genes (15 DE-FRGs displayed). Red colour means positive correlation, blue means negative correlation, while white means no correlation. DE-FRGs, differentially expressed ferroptosis-related genes.

DE-FRG functional analyses

The potential biological functions of the identified DE-FRGs in severe influenza were explored through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. In GO analyses, these genes were found to be enriched in GO terms associated with oxidative stress, chemical stress, the regulation of autophagy and CC disassembly (online supplemental figure 1A). KEGG pathway analyses suggested the enrichment of the autophagy, infection and inflammation pathways (online supplemental figure 1B).

Severe influenza-related hub DE-FRG identification

The LASSO and SVM-RFE algorithms were next used to screen the training dataset (GSE111368) for FRGs associated with the incidence of severe influenza. The LASSO logistic regression algorithm was used with penalty parameter tuning performed via tenfold cross validation, ultimately leading to the identification of 29 genes (figure 2A, B). The SVM-RFE algorithm was subsequently employed as a means of identifying optimal combinations of the 171 DE-FRGs identified above, ultimately yielding a refined list of 11 genes (maximal accuracy =0.834 and minimal RMSE =0.166) (figure 2C, D). The overlap between these two sets of genes produced by the LASSO and SVM-RFE approaches was then examined, identifying eight hub DE-FRGs (ALOX12, MUC1, stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), DECR1, EZH2, TLR4, RICTOR and GSTM1) as targets for further study (figure 2E).

(C) and (D) SVM-RFE algorithm to filter the 11 DE-FRGs to identify the optimal combination of feature genes. DE-FRGs, differentially expressed ferroptosis-related genes; LASSO, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator; SVM-RFE, support vector machine-recursive feature elimination.

To clarify the utility of these eight hub DE-FRGs as tools for differentiating between individuals with severe and non-severe influenza infections, ROC curve analyses were conducted in the training cohort, yielding AUC values ranging from 0.582 to 0.796 (figure 3A). The R glm package was then used to construct a logistic regression model, with subsequent ROC curves yielding an overall AUC of 0.909 for this combination of eight hub DE-FRGs (figure 3B). These results, thus, suggested that this combined logistic regression model offers a valuable tool for the accurate identification of individuals suffering from severe influenza, performing more accurately than any individual hub DE-FRG in this context.

(B) Logistic regression model to identify the AUC of disease samples. AUC, area under the curve; DE-FRGs, differentially expressed ferroptosis-related genes; ROC, receiver operating characteristic; SCD, stearoyl-CoA desaturase.

Functional enrichment analyses of hub DE-FRGs

To more fully understand the possible functional importance of these identified marker genes in the pathogenesis of severe influenza, a single-gene GSEA-KEGG pathway analysis was conducted, with the top six pathways enriched for each of these marker genes being compiled in figure 4A–H. Overall, these genes were found to be mainly enriched for the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses.

Single-gene GSEA-KEGG pathway analysis in (A) ALOX12, (B) DECR1, (C) EZH2, (D) GSTM1, (E) MUC1, (F) RICTOR, (G) SCD and (H) TLR4. SCD, stearoyl-CoA desaturase.

GSVA analyses yielded similar results. For example, high MUC1 (including KEGG_PRIMARY_IMMUNODEFICIENCY, KEGG_GRAFT_VERSUS_HOST_DISEASE and KEGG_ALLOGRAFT_REJECTION) and EZH2 (including KEGG_COMPLEMENT_AND_COAGULATION_CASCADES), and low TLR4 expression levels (including KEGG_RIG_I_LIKE_RECEPTOR_SIGNALING_PATHWA) were predicted to possibly be linked to severe influenza through the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses (online supplemental figure 2A-H).

The relationships between immune cell abundance and the expression of hub DE-FRGs

Differences in the levels of specific immune cell populations in whole-blood samples from individuals with and without severe influenza were next compared with a ssGSEA algorithm. This approach indicated that the blood of individuals with severe disease contained more inflammatory cell types, including activated dendritic cells (DCs) and monocytes, together with reductions in the levels of key lymphocyte populations, including activated B cells, memory B cells, activated CD8+T cells, memory CD8+T cells, activated CD4+T cells and memory CD4+T cells (figure 5A).

Immune landscape analysis. (A) Implemented the ssGSEA algorithm to explore the differences in the immune microenvironment between patients with severe and non-severe influenza. (B) Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed that hub DE-FRG expression levels were positively correlated with inflammatory cell populations and negatively correlated with lymphocyte levels. DE-FRG, differentially expressed ferroptosis-related gene; SCD, stearoyl-CoA desaturase; ssGSEA, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis.

Hub DE-FRG expression levels were positively correlated with inflammatory cell populations and negatively correlated with lymphocyte levels. MUC1 expression, for example, was positively correlated with DC, neutrophil and macrophage levels and negatively correlated with activated CD8+T cell, memory CD8+T cell and memory CD4+T cell, and B cell levels. Similarly, SCD expression was positively correlated with macrophage levels and negatively correlated with activated CD8+, memory CD8+ and memory CD4+T cells, while TLR4 levels were positively correlated with monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils and activated DCs and negatively correlated with activated and memory B cells as well as with activated CD8+, memory CD8+ and memory CD4+T cells (figure 5B).

Predictive identification of pharmacological agents targeting hub DE-FRGs

To better understand the potential therapeutic relevance of these hub DE-FRGs, the DGIdb database was leveraged to identify candidate drugs that may target these genes using the default parameters. The resultant data were visualised with Cytoscape, highlighting 71 drugs predicted to target 5 of these hub genes (TLR4, RICTOR, MUC1, GSTM1, ALOX12 and SCD) (online supplemental figure 3).

Construction of a hub DE-FRG-based ceRNA network

These eight hub DE-FRGs were next used to establish a ceRNA network consisting of 470 nodes and 580 edges, with the nodes corresponding to the 8 DE-FRGs, 202 lncRNAs and 260 miRNAs (online supplemental figure 4). This network highlighted complex regulatory relationships associated with these DE-FRGs. For example, 8, 11 and 18 lncRNAs were predicted to control RICTOR expression by competitively binding to the respective target miRNAs hsa-miR-590–3p, hsa-miR-449c-5p, hsa-miR-1972 and hsa-miR-186–5. Similarly, the expression of SCD was predicted to be under the control of 15 different lncRNAs capable of binding hsa-miR-18a-3p, while a further 19 lncRNAs were found to control SCD, GSTM1 and RICTOR levels by competitively binding hsa-miR-149–3p. For further details regarding this ceRNA network, see online supplemental file 7.

Validation of DE-FRG expression in severe influenza

In the test dataset GSE101702, individuals with severe disease exhibited significantly higher levels of ALOX12 (p=0.0016), EZH2 (p=0.01), MUC1 (p=7.3e−05) and SCD (p=1.5e−06) expression, together with significant reductions in RICTOR expression (p=0.00076), consistent with the above results from the training dataset (online supplemental figure 5).

Discussion

While there have been many important advances in available medical technologies in recent years, influenza remains one of the leading viral causes of hospitalisation and mortality throughout the world.1,5 Recent transcriptomic research efforts have highlighted a range of genes and gene expression patterns related to the severity of influenza-related disease.12 Severe influenza is characterised by the high levels of airway epithelial cell injury, together with damage to other tissue and cell types, including cells of the vascular endothelium.13 14 Accordingly, cell death plays a key role in the incidence of influenza-associated pulmonary damage. Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of cell death distinct from apoptosis that is characterised by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. While viral infections are thought to be potential triggers for ferroptotic death,15 16 there have been few studies to date examining the association between influenza and this form of cell death. Here, a series of systematic bioinformatics approaches were used to explore important ferroptosis-associated genes related to patients with severe influenza necessitating mechanical ventilation. Further research efforts then focused on exploring the association among these genes, their potential functions and the levels of immune cell populations in the analysed samples. Together, these data highlight the potentially important role that ferroptotic cell death may play in the pathogenesis of severe influenza.

Of the eight hub DE-FRGs identified in this study, MUC1 was the most reliable predictor of severe influenza based on the calculated AUC values. Higher levels of the glycoprotein mucin 1 encoded by this gene have been reported in many forms of respiratory disease,17 and in vitro MUC1 overexpression in epithelial cells results in the suppression of influenza virus infection.18 Consistently, Muc1-/- mice experience increased inflammation and higher mortality rates when infected with influenza virus.18 MUC1 has also been identified as an important stabiliser of the key intracellular antioxidant system X−C cystine/glutamate antiporter complex, which plays an important role in ferroptotic induction.10 Inhibiting the ability of MUC1 proteins to dimerise can contribute to higher levels of Keap1 expression, thereby reducing GSK3β phosphorylation and inhibiting nuclear Nrf2 entry, thus suppressing GPX4 expression in a manner that can enhance pulmonary lipid peroxidation levels, triggering ferroptotic death and more severe pulmonary damage.19 Consistently, Wang et al20 reported higher plasma MUC1 expression levels in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) relative to individuals without ARDS. By stabilising HIF-1α, MUC1 can suppress TLR4 activation, thus reducing the severity of sepsis-related pulmonary damage and preserving organ function. Higher plasma MUC1 expression was also able to predict whether septicemia patients were likely to develop ARDS in this prior report. The SCD gene exhibited the second most robust predictive utility in this study when identifying patients with severe influenza. SCDs are endoplasmic reticulum (ER) associated enzymes responsible for catalysing monounsaturated fatty acid synthesis, and they are expressed at high levels in certain cancer cell types in which they exhibit antiferroptotic activity.21 Inhibiting SCD in mice reportedly leads to the suppression of germinal B cell responses together with apoptotic T follicular helper cell death and higher levels of ER stress on influenza infection.22 Together, these findings suggest that the upregulation of genes associated with the inhibition of ferroptosis (eg, MUC1, SCD, EZH2 and GSTM1) and the downregulation of proferroptotic genes (eg, TLR4) may play an important compensatory role aimed at limiting the severity of influenza infection and associated tissue pathology. Prior work has highlighted a relationship between severe infection-associated septicemia and the expression of RICTOR and DECR1.23 24 Moreover, the protein encoded by ALOX12 can catalyse molecular oxygen addition to arachidonic acid in a manner that produces biologically active lipids, thereby controlling inflammatory and apoptotic death.25 However, little research to date has explored the association between ALOX12 and influenza or other forms of severe disease.

Immune responses are central to both the pathogenesis and resolution of severe influenza.1314 26,28 Analyses of predicted immune cell levels in the blood samples included in this study revealed two striking results. One was that adaptive immune responses to influenza virus were suppressed in individuals with severe disease, as indicated by reductions in lymphocyte populations, including both active and memory B cells, CD8+T cells and CD4+T cells. In contrast, these patient samples were predicted to contain far higher levels of activated inflammatory cell populations associated with innate immunity, including DCs and monocytes. Consistently, Bermejo-Martin et al26 reported impaired expression of adaptive immune response-related genes in individuals with severe influenza A/H1N1/2009 infections as compared with individuals without severe disease. Moreover, Nguyen et al27 found higher sequential organ failure assessment scores to be associated with reductions in the responses of adaptive IFN-γ secreting CD8+T cells in individuals hospitalised for acute influenza infections. Dunning et al28 further determined that individuals with severe disease presented with higher expression levels associated with inflammation-related cell types, such as DCs, neutrophils and monocytes, together with lower levels of IFN-γ transcription. While severe lung inflammation in these patients was observed and associated with high levels of pulmonary inflammatory cell infiltration, this largely failed to facilitate viral clearance.29 Ferroptotic cell death is a form of regulated necrosis that is highly immunogenic, unlike apoptotic cell death. Several studies have emphasised the potential importance of ferroptosis as a driver of inflammatory responses. Conversely, inflammation and other immune-related responses can also trigger ferroptosis.8 30 31 Consistently, in this study, the majority of the hub DE-FRGs detected in patients with severe influenza were enriched in immune response and inflammation-related pathways. These hub FRGs were also positively correlated with lymphocyte levels, whereas they were negatively correlated with inflammatory cell levels. However, these correlative relationships do not necessarily indicate a causal relationship, underscoring an important topic for future study.

To more fully explore the potential therapeutic implications of these results, drugs with the potential to target these DE-FRGs were identified, and a ceRNA network was constructed. These identified drugs included resatorvid, which is a small molecule TLR4 inhibitor that suppresses lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory mediator production through its ability to bind the TLR4 intracellular domain. Shirey et al32 previously found that resatorvid was able to protect mice against lethal influenza challenge. Eritoran tetrasodium, which is structurally analogous to R. sphaeroides lipid A, can bind competitively to the TLR4-MD2 complex in a non-agonistic manner such that the ability of LPS to promote inflammatory responses is inhibited. Consistently, the use of eritoran to treat bacterial septicemia model animals has been linked to improved survival outcomes.33 Moreover, phases I and II studies have demonstrated the ability of eritoran to inhibit cytokine production in response to LPS challenge, and one phase II study reported possible survival benefits associated with eritoran treatment in some subgroups of patients with septicemia.34 As such, eritoran may represent a viable tool for treating individuals with severe influenza. Host miRNA expression profiles can also be altered in response to influenza virus stimulation, with miRNA dysregulation either indirectly or directly altering the expression of viral genes and the associated induction or suppression of innate immune responses and cell death in the context of ongoing viral infections.35 36 The specific roles that non-coding RNAs may play in the predicted regulatory relationships identified in this study remain incompletely understood and warrant future study.

There are many important limitations to this study. For one, this was a retrospective analysis with a small sample size, limiting the accuracy of the resultant data. In addition, by specifically focusing on FRGs, it is possible that other genes not related to this pathway but that are nonetheless important regulators of influenza pathogenesis may have been overlooked. Finally, while this analysis revealed several FRGs associated with severe influenza, it remains to be determined whether these genes are specifically related to the pathogenesis of influenza or may be more generally expressed in the contexts of septicemia or mechanical ventilation in the absence of influenza. Further in vitro and in vivo work will, thus, be essential to confirm the mechanistic roles that these FRGs play in shaping the onset and progression of severe influenza.

Conclusions

In summary, eight important FRGs potentially associated with the pathogenesis of severe influenza requiring mechanical ventilation were herein identified. Moreover, the application of multiomics technologies has shown great potential as a means of elucidating disease mechanisms and identifying novel drug targets.37 38 These genes can potentially serve as candidate biomarkers to guide the personalised prevention or treatment of this deadly viral disease.
