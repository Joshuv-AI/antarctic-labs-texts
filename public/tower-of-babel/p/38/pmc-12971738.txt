Absence of lumbosacral plexus abnormalities in preschool children with tethered cord syndrome: A multiparametric MRI study
Authors: Shengwei Xu, Shilong Tang, Wushuang Chen, Junya Ma, Xiao Fan, Ling He, Faqi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the correlation between tethered cord syndrome (TCS) and lumbosacral plexus (LSP) abnormalities in preschool children and evaluate the diagnostic utility of multiparametric MRI (mpMRI).MethodsA total of 100 preschool children (mean age 1.72 ± 1.94 years) with suspected or confirmed TCS were enrolled between October and December 2024. All participants underwent mpMRI, including supine sagittal T2WI-FS, T1WI, axial T2WI-FS/T1WI, coronal 3D Cube STIR (for LSP visualization), and prone thin-layer T2WI-SSFSE (for FT motility assessment). Two senior radiologists independently evaluated spinal cord conus position, FT motility, FT thickness, and LSP abnormalities. Statistical analyses included intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and Chi-square tests.ResultsNo LSP morphological abnormalities were observed in all cases (84 non-surgical, 16 postoperative), ruling out LSP’s role in early TCS. FT motility (AUC = 0.81, ICC = 0.82) and FT thickness (AUC = 0.83, ICC = 0.96) were reliable TCS predictors (both P < 0.001), while LSP metrics were non-diagnostic (AUC = 0.50 – 0.52, P > 0.05). Postoperatively, 6.25% (1/16) experienced complications (e.g., CSF leakage), while 18.8% (3/16) exhibited imaging artifacts from tissue edema.ConclusionTCS in preschool children does not correlate with LSP morphological abnormalities. Preschool TCS pathology is confined to the spinal cord/FT, with no LSP involvement. FT metrics are core diagnostic markers, and routine LSP MRI is unnecessary—avoiding prolonged scans/anesthesia risks in young children.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), ChiCTR2500098818; prospectively registered on 13 March 2025.Keywords: Tethered cord syndrome, Multiparametric MRI, Preschool children, Lumbosacral plexus, Filum terminale motility

Introduction

Tethered cord syndrome (TCS) is a common pediatric neurological disorder caused by pathological fixation of the spinal cord due to an inelastic or thickened filum terminale (FT) [1, 2]. Clinical manifestations (pain, sacrococcygeal skin abnormalities, urinary dysfunction, gait disturbances, etc.) may lead to progressive neuronal damage if untreated, which significantly impact children’s quality of life [1–4].

Accurate TCS diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical assessment and imaging confirmation, as specified by authoritative guidelines and consensus [5–8]. The International Children’s Continence Society (ICCS) consensus and NCBI clinical guideline [8] uniformly designate MRI as the preferred diagnostic tool, defining TCS by: (1) Clinical markers (lumbosacral cutaneous stigmata or progressive neurofunctional deficits); (2) Imaging abnormalities (conus medullaris below L2 vertebral body or FT thickness > 2 mm) [9–12].

The lumbosacral plexus (LSP), derived from T12 to S4 nerve roots, regulates lower limb motor and sensory function [13]. Previous hypotheses of mechanical traction-ischemic injury suggested TCS-related spinal cord traction might indirectly damage the LSP via ischemia or compression [14–16], but direct imaging evidence in preschool children remains scarce [17]. Existing studies focus on adults or mixed-age cohorts, neglecting age-specific neurodevelopmental features (e.g., greater FT elasticity, neural plasticity) in preschoolers [18], while traditional MRI lacks sensitivity for LSP fascicular visualization [19, 20].

Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI)—integrating 3D Cube STIR and prone dynamic sequences—has enhanced TCS diagnostic specificity [11, 21], but systematic data on TCS-LSP correlations in preschool children are lacking [18]. Currently, there is a paucity of age-related research on LSP involvement in TCS, particularly a scarcity of systematic data in preschool children.

This study analyzed mpMRI data from 100 preschool children aged 0–6 years with suspected or confirmed TCS, aiming to verify the hypothesis that “pathological damage of TCS in preschool children is confined to the conus medullaris and filum terminale” and evaluate the contribution of LSP abnormalities to early diagnosis and clinical intervention.

Methods

Informed consent

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the institutional review board (No. 2023–234) and prospectively registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) on 13 March 2025 (https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=265089, Registration ID: ChiCTR2500098818). Written informed consent was obtained from guardians of all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included preschool children (aged 0–6 years) who visited our hospital with suspected or confirmed TCS and were scheduled for lumbar and sacral MRI. The inclusion criteria were as follows: preschoolers (0–6 years) with clinically or MRI-confirmed TCS, complete supine/prone MRI sequences, and no metallic implants or contraindications for MRI. The exclusion criteria included the presence of other neurological disorders or spinal deformities, poor MRI image quality that made accurate assessment impossible, and the presence of metallic implants or other contraindications for MRI [22]. All children underwent a comprehensive clinical assessment by a neurosurgeon, and the diagnosis was based on clinical manifestations and MRI results [5–8].

Image acquisition

Sequence design

Given the absence of standardized MRI scanning protocols for TCS diagnosis, our methodology followed institutional guidelines. All scans were performed on a 3.0T MRI scanner (uMR880, United Imaging, Shanghai, China) using a multi-parameter protocol. In the supine position, sagittal plane imaging included 2D T2-weighted imaging with fat suppression (T2WI-FS), which facilitated the assessment of spinal cord morphology, conus medullaris apex position, and filum terminale (FT) characteristics, and 2D T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), which provided inherent soft-tissue contrast for identifying fat infiltration or lipomas. Axial plane imaging comprised 2D lumbar/sacral T2WI-FS and T1WI, which evaluated spinal canal dimensions, FT thickness, intervertebral disc integrity, and thecal sac compression. Coronal plane imaging involved a 3D Cube STIR sequence, which enabled high-resolution visualization of LSP fascicles through multi-planar reconstruction (MPR) and maximum intensity projection (MIP). In the prone position, sagittal plane imaging included a 2D respiratory-triggered single-shot fast spin-echo (SSFSE) sequence, which was acquired to quantify FT mobility by measuring positional changes in the conus medullaris relative to the spinal canal.

Scan positioning

In the supine position, subjects were positioned head-first with their arms at their sides, and dedicated spine coils were used for imaging. In the prone position, subjects were positioned feet-first, with their arms either extended or flexed beneath their heads, and combined spine and flexible phased-array coils were utilized to acquire the images.

All patients underwent mpMRI scans, including supine sagittal T2WI-FS, T1WI, axial T2WI-FS, T1WI, coronal 3D Cube STIR LSP sequence, and prone thin-layer T2WI-SSFSE scan. For detailed scan parameters, see Table 1.

Image postprocessing and analysis

All postprocessing procedures and quantitative analyses were performed using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer software (v2024.1, Medixant, Poznań, Poland). Two board-certified radiologists, with over 8 and over 15 years of MRI diagnostic experience respectively, independently evaluated the MRI datasets without access to clinical outcomes. Discrepancies in interpretation were resolved by consensus to ensure data reliability.

Consistent with ICCS and NCBI criteria, this study defined “confirmed TCS” as concurrent clinical signs and MRI abnormalities. The key postprocessing workflow included morphological analysis, LSP evaluation, and pathology detection. For morphological analysis, the spinal cord conus medullaris apex position was determined on median sagittal images, with the axial plane images used for reference, if necessary, relative to vertebral bodies (e.g., L2 level as a reference for low-lying conus medullaris) [23, 24]. The anterior–posterior diameter of the spinal canal was measured at the conus medullaris apex position (Fig. 1). FT motility was calculated using the formula [11]: FT motility\%=Dsupine-DproneSpinal Canal Width×100\%. Take 10% of the FT motility as the cut-off. FT thickness was measured at the thickest segment on axial T2WI-FS, with values greater than 2 mm defined as pathological thickening [10, 25]. For LSP evaluation, 3D Cube STIR datasets were reformatted via MPR and MIP to enhance fascicular visualization. Nerve root continuity, caliber variations, and perineural fibrosis were qualitatively assessed. For pathology detection, secondary signs, such as spinal lipomas and syringomyelia, were documented using 2D T1WI/T2WI sequences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with continuous variables (e.g., FT motility, FT thickness) expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD) and categorical variables (e.g., gender, clinical symptoms) reported as counts and percentages. Interobserver reliability of quantitative MRI measurements was assessed via two-way random-effects intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for absolute agreement, categorized as excellent (ICC ≥ 0.75), moderate (0.40 ≤ ICC < 0.75), or poor (ICC < 0.40) based on Cicchetti’s criteria [26]. Diagnostic efficacy of FT parameters was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calculating area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), while Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) test examined associations between TCS and LSP abnormalities. The association between clinical features and MRI manifestations was also analyzed using the χ2 test. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Post hoc power analysis was performed using the pwr package in R (version 4.2.0), with a two-sided binomial test at α = 0.05. The effect size was defined as a minimum detectable proportion of 10% abnormal LSP cases, derived from adult TCS studies reporting radiculopathy incidences.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study cohort comprised 100 preschool children with suspected or confirmed TCS (mean age 1.72 ± 1.94 years; 49 males, 51 females). There were 84 cases (84.0%) without surgical intervention and 16 cases (16.0%) undergoing surgical detethering prior to MRI evaluation.

The clinical characteristics and corresponding MRI findings of the study population are summarized in Table 2. Regarding clinical manifestations, the most common symptom was lumbosacral skin dimples or sinus tracts (n = 49), followed by sacrococcygeal masses or appendages (n = 11), urinary and bowel dysfunction (e.g., constipation, dysuria, incontinence; n = 7), and gluteal cleft deviation or asymmetric skin folds (n = 6). Among the MRI findings, filum terminale (FT) lipomatosis was the most prevalent (n = 15), followed by incomplete bony closure of the lamina (n = 11), syringomyelia or spinal cord central canal dilatation (n = 12), postoperative structural changes (n = 10), and spinal cord lipoma (n = 7).

Clinical symptoms and MRI manifestations

FT metrics

Interobserver agreement for FT motility and FT thickness demonstrated excellent reliability, with ICCs of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.74–0.87) and 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.97), respectively (Table 3). ROC curve analysis identified FT motility (AUC = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.71–0.91) and FT thickness (AUC = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.73–0.92) as statistically significant predictors of TCS diagnosis (P < 0.001 for both) (Figs. 2 and 3). No association was observed between these parameters and LSP morphological abnormalities (FT motility AUC = 0.52; FT thickness AUC = 0.50; P > 0.05).

TCS-LSP correlation analysis

None of the 100 patients exhibited morphological abnormalities of the LSP regardless of surgical status (Figs. 4 and 5). Given the absence of observed LSP abnormalities in all 100 participants (0/100), a post hoc power analysis was conducted to evaluate the reliability of negative findings. Assuming an α-level of 0.05 and an expected clinically significant LSP abnormality rate of ≥ 10%, the current sample size (n = 100) provided 89% statistical power to detect such an effect size (Cohen’s h = 0.3) using a binomial test. This indicates a less than 11% probability of Type II error (false-negative conclusion), supporting that the lack of TCS-LSP association is unlikely attributable to insufficient sample size.

Postoperative outcomes and clinical-MRI correlations

Among surgical cases (n = 16), complications occurred in 6.25% (1/16), including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, postoperative wound dehiscence. Postoperative imaging artifacts (edema/inflammatory infiltration) obscured LSP evaluation in 18.8% (3/16). A significant association emerged between cutaneous stigmata (e.g., skin dimples, sinus tracts) and filum lipomatous degeneration (χ2 = 6.42, P = 0.011). Elevated complication rates were confined to complex surgical cases, with CSF leakage occurring in 6.25% (1/16) of patients undergoing combined spinal lipoma resection and filum terminale section. Notably, no complications were observed in simple filum sectioning procedures (0/10). This aligns with prior reports indicating that dural defects are more prevalent in lipoma-associated detethering due to extensive tissue dissection and adhesion release. Postoperative edema was consistently localized to the surgical site (epidural space) rather than the lumbosacral plexus (LSP) itself, suggesting that inflammatory changes arise from surgical trauma rather than neural traction mechanisms.

Discussion

This study systematically explored the correlation between TCS and LSP abnormalities in preschool children using mpMRI, with core findings providing critical insights into early TCS pathophysiology and clinical practice optimization.

Negative findings but positive contributions

The absence of LSP morphological abnormalities in all 100 preschool children (0/100, including 84 non-surgical and 16 postoperative cases) carries substantial clinical significance. This finding directly supports the concept that in early childhood TCS, pathological changes are confined to the spinal cord/conus medullaris and FT, while LSP involvement is not an early feature of the disease.

For clinical practice, this result provides clear guidance for optimizing TCS diagnostic protocols in preschool children. As endorsed by the ICCS consensus and NCBI guidelines, TCS diagnosis relies on clinical markers (e.g., cutaneous stigmata, neurofunctional deficits) and MRI abnormalities of the spinal cord/FT [5–8, 10]. Our data extend this framework by demonstrating that routine LSP-specific MRI sequences (e.g., coronal 3D Cube STIR) add no diagnostic value for this age group. Given that prolonged MRI scans increase anesthesia-related risks (e.g., transient hypoxemia) in young children [1, 27–29] and consume unnecessary healthcare resources, we strongly recommend omitting dedicated LSP imaging from standard TCS evaluation. Instead, diagnostic focus should prioritize FT motility assessment and prone-supine positional MRI — modalities that achieved AUC values > 0.8 for TCS diagnosis in our study, with excellent interobserver reliability.

Notably, this negative finding also clarifies a key clinical misunderstanding: the LSP is an insensitive marker for early TCS damage in preschool children. Clinicians should not rely on LSP integrity (e.g., normal LSP morphology on MRI) to rule out TCS or determine the need for prophylactic surgery. Instead, decisions should be based on FT metrics (thickness, motility) and spinal cord/conus medullaris abnormalities, as these directly reflect the pathological core of early TCS.

Interpretation of negative correlation between TCS and LSP abnormalities

The lack of TCS-LSP correlation challenges the earlier “mechanical traction-ischemic injury” hypothesis [14–16, 30], which proposed that chronic spinal cord traction in TCS might indirectly damage the LSP via ischemia or compression. This discrepancy can be attributed to age-specific neurodevelopmental characteristics of preschool children:

First, anatomical positioning shields the LSP from direct mechanical stress [14, 16]. The LSP nerve roots originate superior to the conus medullaris [31], avoiding the pathological tension exerted on the distal spinal cord by a tethered FT.

Second, despite alterations in the spinal cord/conus medullaris and FT, the LSP remains intact. Young children(especially preschool children) have inherent high neurodevelopmental plasticity, exhibit greater FT elasticity and robust neural compensatory mechanisms (e.g., collagen remodeling, hemodynamic redistribution) [16, 32], which can offset the indirect effects of spinal cord traction and delay or prevent detectable LSP damage. However, this compensatory capacity may not be sustained in later life, which also explains why LSP abnormalities may occur in older children or adult TCS patients.

Additionally, the absence of LSP morphological abnormalities in postoperative cases further confirms that TCS-related pathological processes (rather than surgical trauma) do not affect the LSP: postoperative edema/inflammatory infiltration (6.25%, 1/16) was localized to the surgical site (epidural space) rather than the LSP, ruling out surgical induction of LSP dysfunction.

Diagnostic value of FT metrics and surgical implications

The FT, a fibrous band anchoring the spinal cord to the sacrum, normally allows subtle movement to accommodate physiological changes [33, 34]. In TCS, loss of filum elasticity—often from fibrofatty infiltration or congenital thickening—generates abnormal tension on the spinal cord [10, 27, 33]. This traction elevates mechanical stress, reduces blood flow, and induces metabolic disturbances within the cord, progressing to irreversible neuronal damage if untreated [1, 2, 35].

Our results reinforce the role of FT metrics as core, reliable MRI markers for early TCS diagnosis. FT motility (AUC = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.71–0.91) and FT thickness (AUC = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.73–0.92) exhibited high diagnostic accuracy for TCS (both P < 0.001), with excellent interobserver reliability (ICC = 0.82 for FT motility; ICC = 0.96 for FT thickness). These parameters directly reflect the biomechanical dysfunction underlying TCS — reduced FT elasticity and pathological spinal cord fixation — enabling objective differentiation between physiological and pathological cord tethering, which is critical for avoiding overdiagnosis of asymptomatic conditions.

For surgical management, our postoperative data provide practical insights: complications 6.25%, 1/16) were exclusively observed in complex cases involving spinal lipoma resection, while simple FT sectioning had no complications (0/10). This aligns with prior reports linking extensive tissue dissection and adhesion release to increased dural defects [36, 37], emphasizing that minimally invasive FT sectioning is safer for uncomplicated TCS. Additionally, preoperative baseline MRI is essential to distinguish postoperative edema from true neural pathology, as surgical inflammation does not involve the LSP and should not be misinterpreted as TCS-related LSP damage.

Limitations and future directions

This study has limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, the cohort was limited to 100 preschool children (0–6 years), restricting generalizability to older children or adolescents; expanding to school-age populations (6–12 years) is needed to explore age-related progression of TCS pathology (e.g., whether LSP abnormalities emerge as neuroplasticity declines). Second, LSP assessment relied on morphological MRI, lacking quantitative functional data (e.g., diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics like fractional anisotropy) that may detect early microstructural changes invisible on conventional sequences [13, 38]. Third, short follow-up prevented observation of long-term LSP function, leaving unanswered whether early spinal cord/FT damage eventually leads to LSP involvement.

Future studies should integrate DTI and MR elastography to characterize LSP microstructural integrity [39, 40], conduct longitudinal follow-up of the current cohort to track LSP function over time, and expand to school-age populations to explore age-related progression of TCS pathology. These steps will refine understanding of TCS pathophysiological progression and further optimize age-specific diagnostic protocols.

Conclusion

In preschool children with TCS, pathological damage is confined to the spinal cord/conus medullaris and FT, with no detectable LSP involvement—confirming the LSP is an insensitive marker for early TCS and routine LSP MRI adds no diagnostic value. FT motility and thickness are reliable MRI markers for TCS diagnosis (AUC > 0.8, P < 0.001), and diagnostic protocols should prioritize these metrics over LSP imaging to reduce anesthesia risks and optimize resources. Future research should focus on longitudinal follow-up and functional imaging to explore age-related TCS progression.
