Safety of the contrast enhancement agent sulfur hexafluoride in ultrasound: Analysis of the Federal Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System database
Authors: Courtney N. Walker, Michael B. Andrews, Douglas G. Adler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesSulfur hexafluoride is an inert gas that creates microbubbles to enhance diagnostic accuracy in a variety of ultrasound (US) studies and is widely used in EUS. We analyzed the reported adverse events (AEs) of the US contrast agent, sulfur hexafluoride, extensively used during EUS procedures using a US national database.MethodsFrom December 2008 to January 2024, AEs reported in the Federal Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System database for sulfur hexafluoride were examined.ResultsThere were 1069 individual reports analyzed. Reports were excluded if they contained drugs other than sulfur hexafluoride. Echocardiogram (70.9%) was the common diagnostic study in which sulfur hexafluoride was administered. The most common AE reported was anaphylactic reaction (n = 179, 16.7%), followed by hypotension (n = 162, 15.2%), cardiac arrest (n = 161, 15.1%), and dyspnea (n = 159, 14.9%). The most common gastrointestinal AE was nausea (n = 135, 12.6%). Severity of AEs ranged from nonserious to death. Death associated with sulfur hexafluoride use was reported in 58 patients (5.4% of AE reports).ConclusionsAnaphylactic reaction was the most common AE reported with sulfur hexafluoride use, and severe AEs including death may be more common than prior large, retrospective, observational studies to date have suggested.

INTRODUCTION

Sulfur hexafluoride, marketed as SonoVue (Bracco, Milan, Italy) or Lumason (Bracco, Monroe Township, NJ) is a second-generation ultrasound (US) contrast agent (UCA), first introduced in 2001. Sulfur hexafluoride is an inert, high-density, nonorganic gas injected by IV as a suspension of phospholipid-stabilized sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles.[1] The microbubbles enhance blood echogenicity and are commonly used in US studies to improve the signal-to-noise ratio and increase image contrast and resolution. Common applications include echocardiograms where injection of sulfur hexafluoride helps delineate the left ventricle endocardial border, enhancement of tumor vascularity in EUS, and detection of leaks in anastomoses and endoprostheses on peripheral Doppler US. Contrast enhancement for EUS with traditional Doppler EUS and contrast harmonic EUS is also widely used as it has shown to enhance diagnostic accuracy in pancreaticobiliary diseases.[2] In pancreatic solid tumors, the accuracy of contrast harmonic EUS is comparable with EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration, and the combination of both improves overall accuracy, reducing false-negative results of each individual technique.[3]

Despite being widely available, there is a paucity of data regarding the safety of sulfur hexafluoride. Most publications evaluating the adverse events (AEs) of this drug are retrospective or observational studies. In this study, we aimed to analyze the AEs reported from a national database of sulfur hexafluoride use in US imaging to highlight potential safety concerns associated with use of this agent.

METHODS

Data acquisition and analysis regarding AEs associated with the contrast agent sulfur hexafluoride were performed via use of the Federal Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). FAERS is an open-access, publicly available, national reporting database (https://open.fda.gov/data/faers). The database was created by the FDA as a surveillance program for drug and therapeutic biologic product postmarketing safety concerns. Reports of safety concerns are submitted to FAERS by health care professionals, consumers, and manufacturers. Clinical reviewers in the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research and the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research then review the reports.[4] If a safety concern is identified, additional evaluations are performed.

A search for the chemical compound of the contrast agent SonoVue/Lumason, known as sulfur hexafluoride, was obtained from the FAERS database. Results were reported between December 2008 and January 2024. AE reports were downloaded from the FAERS database and analyzed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA). Reports were excluded if they contained drugs other than sulfur hexafluoride to support analysis of AEs uniquely related to the contrast agent. All “reasons for use” were included in the analysis.

Information obtained from the FAERS database included patient demographics, AEs, or “Reactions” described in terms found in the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, severity of the reaction, country where the AEs occurred, and classification of the reporter (health care professional or consumer). Analysis of AEs was performed using descriptive statistics of categorical variables [n, (%)].

This study is exempt from institutional review board approval as the data analyzed were obtained from the publicly available FAERS database and personal health information is not included. Individual patients cannot be identified from the information in FAERS.

RESULTS

Between December 2008 and January 2024, a total of 1158 AEs were reported following administration of sulfur hexafluoride. Of those, 1069 individual case reports were included in the analysis. AE reports were excluded if they contained reports of other drug administration in addition to sulfur hexafluoride.

Clinical usage

The most common diagnostic study associated with administration of sulfur hexafluoride was echocardiogram (758 reports, 70.9%), followed by “unknown study” (230 reports, 21.5%), unknown cardiac test/imaging (25 reports, 2.3%), and liver US (24 reports, 2.2%). Less common indications for sulfur hexafluoride use included abdominal US, angiogram, bladder US, breast US, carotid US, lung biopsy, lung US, pancreas US, renal arteriogram, renal US, thyroid US, and vaginal US. Altogether, these studies comprised less than 5% of “reason for use” for sulfur hexafluoride (32 reports, 2.9%). EUS use was not specifically mentioned, but may fall under the “unknown study” category as use of contrast-enhanced EUS has evolved significantly since initial conception in 1986 and expanded quickly after the development of EUS color/power Doppler in the 1990s.[5]

Adverse events

The most common AEs reported for all indications of sulfur hexafluoride use were anaphylactic reaction (179 reports, 16.7%), hypotension (162 reports, 15.2%), cardiac arrest (161 reports, 15.1%), dyspnea (159 reports, 14.9%), nausea (135 reports, 12.6%), and hyperhidrosis (114 reports, 10.7%) [Figure 1].

Severity of adverse events following sulfur hexafluoride use.

Beyond nausea, the most common gastrointestinal AEs reported were emesis (63 reports, 5.9%) and pruritus (63 reports, 5.9%). Additional reported cardiopulmonary events included bradycardia (61 reports, 5.7%), chest pain (79 reports, 7.4%), dizziness (84 reports, 7.9%), and tachycardia (70 cases, 6.5%).

Although most AEs reported with sulfur hexafluoride use were cardiopulmonary, skin reactions manifested by skin flushing (89 reports, 8.3%) and erythema (66 reports, 6.2%) were also noted.

The severity of AEs reported with sulfur hexafluoride use was categorized as serious in 742 reports (69.4%) and nonserious in 327 reports (30.6%). Serious AEs included death (58 reports, 5.4%), life-threatening AEs (261 reports, 24.4%), required intervention (12 reports, 1.12%), disabled (7 reports, 0.65%), and hospitalization (117 reports, 10.9%). There were 287 reports (26.8%) of “other serious outcomes.” Included in other serious outcomes were AEs such as pulmonary embolism, cerebral hemorrhage and cerebral infarction, agonal respiration, musculoskeletal stiffness, pulseless electrical activity, ST-segment elevation on electrocardiogram, anembryonic gestation, urticaria, syncope, eructation, angioedema, and injection site scar in addition to the more commonly reported anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest, and chest pain [Table 1].

AEs associated with pulmonary diagnostic studies

There were a total of 4 AE reports from patients undergoing lung biopsy: cardiac arrest, respiratory failure, seizure, and tachycardia. Patients who had a lung US also had 4 AE reports: cyanosis, dyspnea, feeling hot, and “unclear” AE. Contrast-enhanced US (CEUS) is used in lung US and transthoracic biopsy of peripheral lung and mediastinal lesions. CEUS with agents like sulfur hexafluoride has an increased detection rate of pulmonary lesions within atelectasis, necrosis within lesions, and higher overall diagnostic success.[7] Contrast-enhanced lung US may be especially useful in intensive care units due to availability and noninvasiveness.[8]

AEs associated with abdominal diagnostic studies

Nine AEs were associated with patients undergoing abdominal US and include acute coronary syndrome (1 report, 11.1%), cardiac arrest (1 report, 11.1%), cyanosis (1 report, 11.1%), dyspnea (1 report, 11.1%), emesis (1 report, 11.1%), hypotension (1 report, 11.1%), respiratory failure (1 report, 11.1%), seizure (1 report, 11.1%), and shock (1 report, 11.1%).

Liver US had 45 AEs reported. The most common were anaphylactic reaction (5 reports, 11.1%) and “unclear” AEs (5 reports, 11.1%). Hypotension, nausea, and seizure had 3 reports (6.7%) each. A total of 3 AEs were reported with pancreas US: hypotension, muscle spasms, and tachycardia.

AEs associated with renal, bladder, and vaginal studies

There were 14 AEs reported among patients undergoing renal US. There were 2 cases (14%) each of abdominal pain, cough, dyspnea, hyperhidrosis, and “ineffective” AEs reported. There was report of 1 episode each of emesis, hypotension, rash, and syncope. One hypertensive AE was reported in association with a patient undergoing a renal US/arteriogram. Vaginal US had 13 AE reports. Acute kidney injury and dizziness were most common (2 reports each, 15.4%). Followed by 1 report each of the following: abdominal pain, asthenia, emesis, headache, feeling hot, hypotension, seizure, pain at site, and syncope.

AEs associated with miscellaneous studies

“Unknown diagnostic study” had 544 reports of an AE. The following reactions were reported more than 5%: hypotension (44 reports, 8.1%), cardiac arrest (41 reports, 7.5%), ineffective AE (33 reports, 6.1%), dyspnea (31 reports, 5.7%), and nausea (29 reports, 5.3%).

There were a total of 6 AEs reported among patients undergoing breast US. AEs included abdominal pain (1 report, 16.7%), back pain (1 report, 16.7%), breast pain (1 report, 16.7%), hyperhidrosis (1 report, 16.7%), hypotension (1 report, 16.7%), and “unclear” reaction (1 report, 16.7%). There were a total of 13 AEs reported with thyroid US. Most common AEs with thyroid US were abdominal pain (2 cases, 15.4%) and dizziness (2 cases, 15.4%). There was 1 report of anaphylactic reaction, cardiac arrest, cyanosis, dyspnea, erythema, headache, hypotension, seizure, and syncope reported with thyroid US.

DISCUSSION

Sulfur hexafluoride is a second-generation contrast agent used in US imaging to improve the signal-to-noise ratio, which improves image quality. In EUS, characterization of solid tumor vascularization in the pancreas, pancreas mural nodules, liver metastasis; differential diagnosis of lymph nodes; and prediction of malignant behavior of gastrointestinal stromal tumors are improved with contrast enhancement.[2] Additionally, the sensitivity for detecting malignancy is heightened with contrast-enhanced EUS for historically difficult-to-detect lesions in patients with pancreatic cystic lesions and chronic pancreatitis.[3] Our analysis of the FAERS database revealed more than 1000 reported AEs associated with the clinical use of sulfur hexafluoride.

Echocardiogram comprised 70% of sulfur hexafluoride use reported in the FAERS database and is consistent with indication for use found in other studies. UCAs are advantageous in cardiac US as contrast agents improve endocardial border definition. This allows assessment of regional ventricular function for which high image quality is essential.[9] Also included in the FAERS data were carotid US and angiograms. UCA is helpful during angiograms in conjunction with US as the agent detects microvessels and leaks following vascular anastomoses and placement of endoprostheses.[1]

One-quarter of AEs were life-threatening (261 reports, 24.4%). One hundred seventeen reported hospitalization (10.9%), and critically, there were 58 reports of death (5.4%) following sulfur hexafluoride use. One-third (30.6%) of patients experienced nonserious AEs. This is contrary to a large retrospective, observational study by Li et al of 49,000 patients undergoing CEUS with SonoVue/sulfur hexafluoride.[7] In this study, 43 AEs (0.088%) were reported. Of those AEs, 23 (0.047%) were mild, 13 (0.026%) moderate, and 7 (0.014%) were severe. No deaths occurred.[10] Our results from the FAERS database suggest AEs from sulfur hexafluoride are more severe than previously reported.

Echocardiogram was the most performed diagnostic study (1665 studies) in the FAERS database. In our review of AEs associated with echocardiogram, anaphylactic reaction (7.8%) was most common, followed by dyspnea (7%), cardiac arrest (6.4%), hypotension (6.3%), and nausea (6.1%). The observational prospective study by Furtado et al evaluated 1099 pharmacological stress echocardiogram patients with SonoVue/sulfur hexafluoride contrast enhancement and found arrhythmias (24.1%) to be most common.[11] In this study, there were no reports of sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation, whereas our data noted 9 reports (0.5%) of ventricular fibrillation and 10 reports (0.6%) of ventricular tachycardia. Additionally, the study by Furtado et al contained no incidence of myocardial infarction (MI) in the SonoVue group. However, there was 1 report of MI and 2 deaths in the non–contrast-enhanced US group.[11] In contrast, the FAERS database reported 33 (3.1%) acute coronary syndrome events and 58 (5.4%) deaths with SonoVue/sulfur hexafluoride use. The result of this observational study regarding death and MI in non–contrast-enhanced US patients suggests these patients had significant coronary artery disease at baseline. FAERS data would suggest an increased risk of cardiac complications with SonoVue/sulfur hexafluoride use.

A retrospective study of CEUS with Lumason/sulfur hexafluoride in 774 critically ill patients with cardiac and pulmonary comorbidities found no detrimental effect of the contrast agent on cardiac electrophysiology or pulmonary hemodynamics.[12] In this study, there was no difference between in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients who received sulfur hexafluoride CEUS versus those that did not. This stands in contrast to the reports in the FAERS database. More than 1000 AEs were reported, and a significant number were cardiopulmonary in nature, including hypotension (162 reports, 15.2%), cardiac arrest (161 reports, 15.1%), dyspnea (159 reports, 14.9%), tachycardia (70 reports, 6.5%), and bradycardia (61 reports, 5.75).

The most common reported AE in the FAERS database was anaphylactic reaction (179 reports, 16.7%). However, most published studies have not demonstrated anaphylaxis to be a common AE. In a study of 23,000 patients who underwent abdominal US with UCA-enhancing agents (specific agents not mentioned), the incidence of anaphylactic reaction was 0.001%, and no deaths were reported.[13] Anaphylactic reaction was also not common in a retrospective study by Tang et al, which looked at 30,222 patients undergoing CEUS of abdominal and superficial organs with SonoVue/sulfur hexafluoride. In this study, there were 6 AEs (0.02%), and 2 of those were anaphylactic shock (0.007%), much lower than reports in the FAERS database.[14] There were also no reports of death.

Contrast toxicity can occur due to the interaction of a foreign substance with organic tissue, including UCA microbubbles.[15] Contrast reactions are typically anaphylactoid (not anaphylaxis) and mediated through histamine, basophil, and eosinophil mediator release. Reactions are not associated with immunoglobulin E.[16] Sulfur hexafluoride is also one of the heaviest known gases, approximately 5 times heavier than air, and can displace oxygen.[17] Additionally, theoretical concern for cerebral ischemia due to microvascular occlusion following injection is one of the reasons cardiac shunts are listed as a contraindication to UCA use.[18]

Strengths

An advantage of the FAERS database is that it allows users to perform continued risk assessment of drugs after they have been approved by the FDA. The database provides AE information from a wide range of clinical sites and contexts. AEs are also easy to report as accounts are uniformly coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terminology and do not contain complex medical information.[1] Additionally, the database provides information that may help inform whether drug safety labels and indications need to be modified. If reports of AEs are severe enough, then the drug may be withdrawn from the market. For example, the FDA temporarily discontinued the UCA, perflutren, marketed as Definity (Lantheus, Bedford, MA), in 2007 after 11 deaths were reported.[12] Further investigation in 2008 ultimately led to reapproval of the agent with new label warnings.

Limitations

The FAERS database has limitations. Data are reported by volunteers with risk of bias, leading to potentially heterogeneous results. Reporting of discrete information is also limited, restricting the ability to perform complete analysis of reported data. The FAERS database also only shows a perceived relationship between the drug and AEs by end users. Usually, this is because the onset of the AEs occurs after drug administration but is unable to prove that an AE is truly drug related. Additionally, because the FAERS database includes only reported AEs, it is likely that risks attributed to a drug are artificially elevated. This may explain why our study demonstrated higher reports of AEs, AE severity, and death compared with other studies.

CONCLUSIONS

Our analysis of the FAERS database showed a higher incidence of AEs associated with sulfur hexafluoride use across a wide range of US modalities, compared with large retrospective, observational studies.
