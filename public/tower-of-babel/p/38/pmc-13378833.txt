Factors affecting the intention to perform physical activity: A theory-based research
Authors: Samira Sadeghi, Ali Ramezankhani, Mohtasham Ghaffari, Mahshid Namdari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The current physical activity (PA) levels among adolescent girls are below the expected standards. The application of theories and models, particularly in the context of PA, proves effective in identifying and understanding the factors that influence intention, which is a key determinant of behavior. The present study was aimed at determining factors influencing the high-school girls’ intentions to engage in PA, employing the theory of planned behavior (TPB) as the foundational framework.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This study was conducted among 220 randomly chosen female high-school students in Sarpol-e Zahab. The questionnaire gathered information on demographic characteristics and constructs of the theory, including intention, attitude, perceived behavioral control, and subjective norms. Data analysis involved one-way analysis of variance, independent t-tests, descriptive statistics, the Pearson correlation coefficient, and linear regression, all processed in SPSS version 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).(P < 0.05).RESULTS:The regression analysis revealed that both attitude (β = 0.401, P < 0.001) and subjective norms (β = 0.170, P = 0.005) were significant predictors of PA intention, accounting for 25.9% of the variance. In contrast, perceived behavioral control (β = 0.077, P = 0.236) did not significantly predict PA intention.CONCLUSION:TPB, with particular emphasis on both attitude and subjective norm constructs, is recommended, as both are significant predictors of intention. However, attitude has been identified as the strongest predictor. This approach should be prioritized when designing educational interventions aimed at promoting PA among female students.

Introduction

The global increase in non-communicable diseases poses an urgent and multifaceted challenge, which can be addressed by promoting higher levels of physical activity (PA).[1] Physical inactivity is a significant independent contributor to various serious health conditions.[2] Various studies have also shown that engaging in PA provides substantial health benefits for adolescents and children, underscoring the importance of promoting an active lifestyle during adolescence to support healthy puberty,[3] enhance cognitive functions, and improve the learning process.[4] They should engage in a minimum of 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous PA each day. This should predominantly consist of aerobic activities and include high-intensity aerobic exercises, as well as exercises to highly enhance muscles and bones, on a minimum of three days per week.[5] According to global estimates, 1 in 4 adults and 3 in 4 teenagers do not adhere to the global guidelines for PA.[6] There is an urgent need for effective PA promotion initiatives, especially during adolescence, a critical period for developing health-related habits that influence behaviors in adulthood.[7] Additionally, research indicates that PA levels among young individuals are generally low, decrease with age, and are especially lower among girls.[8] It was found in a study in Iran that PA levels among male adolescent students are approximately four times higher than those of female students.[9] Iranian girls are notably less physically active than their male counterparts, a disparity likely influenced by traditional family structures and sociocultural factors that promote more PA among boys at individual, school, and environmental levels.[1011]

Behavior change models and theories are instrumental in understanding health behaviors and identifying strategies to facilitate behavioral change.[12] Numerous studies have validated the efficacy of these theoretical frameworks in modifying PA behaviors.[131415] Indeed, researchers emphasize the necessity of examining the factors that support sustained engagement in PA behavior.[1617] Research into the decision-making process behind regular PA has led to models that incorporate key psychological constructs. These models aim to predict and explain voluntary behavior.[18] Among cognitive-social models, TPB, which extends the theory of reasoned action, is crucial for addressing behaviors with incomplete voluntary control, incorporating constructs such as attitude, subjective norms, intention, and perceived behavioral control [Figure 1].[19] This theory is commonly employed to explain and predict behavior.[20] Furthermore, it is acknowledged as a robust theory for identifying factors that influence behavioral intentions.[21] Perceived behavioral control explains the perceived likelihood of diverse conditions that either facilitate or constrain the behavior,[22] with more perceived barriers being associated with lower PA intention.[23] Intention serves as the direct precursor to the desired health behavior; the greater the individual’s intention, the higher the probability of the behavior being enacted.[24] Intention can be defined as a self-instruction that encapsulates the underlying motivation or commitment to act.[25] Moreover, intentions reflect the motivational drivers behind behavior, representing the effort and determination individuals plan to exert to perform the behavior.[19] In other words, the driving force behind behavior is intention, which is shaped by three other key constructs.[26] As previously noted, three constructs influence the formation of intentions: expectations about the probable outcomes and experiences from engaging in the behavior (behavioral beliefs), which together foster the development of an attitude toward the behavior.[27] Subjective norms represent the perceived social pressures influencing behavior, grounded in beliefs about the impact of significant others and the motivation to adhere to these influences.[28] The last factor, perceived behavioral control, pertains to an individual’s belief in their capability to successfully carry out the behavior and the level of control they perceive over external factors.[29] Several studies have reported that the TPB accounts for 39%–83% of the variance in PA intention,[20303132] though this range remains debatable. Moreover, the intention to engage in PA may be shaped by demographic factors. However, the impact of these factors remains inconsistent across different studies.[122133] Although the relationship between intention and PA is well-established, research consistently reveals a considerable gap between intention and actual behavior; thus, there is room to investigate the factors that effectively influence the intention-behavior relationship.[33] Moreover, it is crucial to conduct studies on students to identify factors influencing their PA and to implement appropriate interventions.[34] Using TPB as a framework, the present study identified the factors that influence the intention to engage in PA among high-school girls.

Methods and Materials

Study design and setting

This descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study was conducted in 2022 on 220 high-school girls in the 10th and 11th grades in Sarpol-e Zahab. This research is part of a broader intervention study.[35] Based on baseline data from the previous studies,[3637] the correlation between intention and the relevant subscales was anticipated to be approximately 0.25. According to the following formula,[38] the required sample size with 90% power and α = 0.05 was 164. Due to cluster sampling and considering a design effect of 1.25, the final required sample size was approximately 205. However, to ensure robustness, a slightly larger sample size of around 220 participants was selected. The sample size from the baseline data of the intervention study was considered appropriate for the objectives of this cross-sectional study.

Study participants and sampling

The study employed a multistage sampling method. Initially, four schools were randomly selected. Within each selected school, one class from each of the 10th and 11th grades was randomly chosen using Microsoft Excel software by generating a random number between 1 and the number of classes in that grade. The sample comprised all students of these classes. Inclusion criteria required the students’ willingness to engage in the study and mandatory parental consent, as all participants were under 18 years of age.

Data collection tool and technique

Data collection was performed through self-reporting using an online questionnaire, which comprised a demographic information section and the TPB questionnaire. The students accessed the questionnaires via an online platform (https://survey.porsline.ir), with links distributed in Shad class groups, a network used to organize virtual classes for students.

Demographic questionnaire: The demographic characteristics questionnaire utilized in this study encompassed items related to age, educational grade, parents’ occupations, and history of sports club membership.

TPB questionnaire

This study utilized the questionnaire developed by Solhi et al.,[39] which has been validated for both reliability and validity. The questionnaire’s content and structural validity were assessed under the direct oversight of health education and promotion experts, while its reliability was established through a pilot study using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding scores of 0.72 for behavioral intention, 0.70 for attitude, 0.73 for subjective norms, and 0.75 for perceived behavioral control. The questionnaire comprised nine questions on attitude (e.g., “Regular physical activity can boost my energy and strength”), six on subjective norms (e.g., “Most people who are important to me believe that I should stay physically active”), four on perceived behavioral control (e.g., “I’m confident I can engage in physical activity next month if I choose to”), and four on intention (e.g., “I expect to be able to exercise three times a week”). Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale, with options spanning from 1, indicating strong disagreement, to 5, indicating strong agreement.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Approval ID: IR.SBMU.PHNS.REC.1400.091). Permissions were also secured from the Regional Education Department and the participating schools. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating students and their legal guardians after providing them with detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential implications. Confidentiality and anonymity of the participants were strictly maintained throughout the research process.

Statistical analysis method

Upon questionnaire completion, data were inputted into SPSS version 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for analysis. One sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov was used for normality test (P > 0.05). Independent t-tests, Pearson’s correlation (to examine the relationship between PA intentions and the constructs of TPB), one-way ANOVA, and descriptive statistics were employed. Linear regression was utilized to assess the constructs’ predictive capability (P < 0.05).

Results

The students in the study had a mean age of 16.31 ± 0.67 years (P = 0.619), with ages ranging from 14 to 17 years. It was indicated by the results that 50.9% of the students were in the tenth grade (P = 0.824), 64.5% of the students’ fathers were self-employed (P = 0.904), and 87.7% of the mothers were housewives (P = 0.847). In addition, 55.5% of students had not participated in sports clubs (P = 0.549). Statistical analyses utilizing independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA identified no significant differences in the average intention to participate in PA when categorized by parents’ education levels and occupations (P > 0.05) [Table 1].

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the determinants that shape high-school females’ intentions to engage in PA, employing TPB as the theoretical framework. As expected, the results revealed that the three constructs of TPB—attitude, perceived behavioral control, and subjective norms —directly affect PA intentions. Additionally, this study observed no significant differences in the mean intention to participate in PA across demographic characteristics, aligning with the findings of a similar study.[31] However, another survey found that age significantly influences PA intentions.[40] Furthermore, the findings indicated that there were no significant differences in the mean intention to engage in PA based on parents’ occupations. This observation contrasts with the results of another study.[41] These contradictions highlight the complexity of factors influencing PA intentions and suggest that more comprehensive research is needed to fully understand these relationships.

The results revealed that the constructs of TPB explained 25.9% of the variance in PA intentions. This result aligns with previous studies, where TPB constructs explained between 11% and 63.1% of the variance in PA intentions.[31424344454647] The higher this percentage, the more effectively TPB predicts PA intention. In other words, a higher variance signifies a greater ability of this theory to explain and forecast behaviors related to PA.[48] The variations among these surveys can be attributed to the diverse populations sampled, different sample sizes, and the use of varying measurement tools. These findings imply that future studies should explore additional factors influencing intentions and PA behavior, as a significant portion of the variance remains unexplained.

In line with the expectations, the study identified attitude as the most significant predictor of the intention to participate in PA. This result is consistent with the findings of other similar studies.[42444950] Attitudes reflect an individual’s beliefs and values about PA. If someone believes that PA is beneficial and enjoyable, they are more likely to intend to engage in it.[51] The present study suggests that high-school students are more likely to be physically active when they are cognizant of its benefits. Conversely, a separate survey highlighted that patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) often view their perceived control over PA as a critical factor in deciding whether to meet recommended guidelines. This divergence may indicate a different underlying mechanism of intention among CHD patients, who might perceive physical constraints as significant barriers to engaging in PA.[52] In other words, participants’ perceptions and physical barriers significantly influence their intentions to engage in PA. High-school students benefit from positive experiences, while individuals with health conditions such as CHD face physical constraints. Tailored interventions can enhance PA across different populations.

The results of the current study demonstrated that subjective norm is also a moderately strong predictor of PA intention, a finding consistent with previous research.[53] Contrary to this result, another study found that subjective norm was not a strong predictor of PA intentions.[54] This may be due to intimidation by highly active peers, who might unintentionally make less active individuals feel they cannot keep up. Additionally, the lack of active role models can significantly diminish the influence of subjective norms, particularly among adolescents, leading to lower intentions to engage in PA.[55] Moreover, the lack of active role models can diminish the influence of subjective norms, particularly among adolescents.[56] In contrast, Hamilton et al.[57] found that subjective norm was a stronger predictor of PA intention than attitude, possibly due to differences in target populations. Therefore, future studies should take these findings into account when designing interventions.

Unexpectedly, the survey results show that perceived behavioral control does not have a notable influence on the intention to participate in PA. This finding is consistent with other surveys, which highlight that substantial perceived barriers—such as lack of time, fatigue, and pollution—can greatly impede PA regardless of an individual’s perceived control. These barriers can diminish the impact of perceived behavioral control on PA intentions, ultimately affecting the likelihood of engaging in regular exercise.[58] However, other studies have demonstrated that perceived behavioral control is a strong predictor of PA intentions. This indicates that variations in welfare levels, capabilities, and independence in overcoming obstacles among diverse populations, in comparison to the participants of the present study, can influence their perceived behavioral control to predict PA intentions.[4459] Hence, further studies should prioritize enhancing the capabilities and skills of high-school students or adolescents to effectively overcome barriers, thereby significantly increasing their intentions to engage in PA.

While this study focused on female high-school students, the constructs of the TPB—attitude, perceived behavioral control, and subjective norms—are universally applicable to various populations and contexts.[30536061] The significant role of attitude as a predictor of PA intentions suggests that similar interventions could be effective in other demographics, including different age groups, genders, and cultural backgrounds. However, the strength and characteristics of these predictors can vary across different populations, necessitating context-specific adjustments and further research. Indeed, among populations with different cultural norms or socioeconomic statuses, the effect of subjective norms and perceived behavioral control may differ.[62] Therefore, future studies should explore these constructs in diverse populations to validate the generalizability of the TPB framework and its applicability in promoting PA across various settings.

When interpreting the study results, several limitations should be taken into account. Due to practical constraints, it was not feasible to conduct this investigation among all high-school students (both boys and girls). The use of self-reporting methods to complete the questionnaires was another limitation of this study. Regarding strengths, few studies have targeted the high-school student population while utilizing the constructs of TPB for PA. Additionally, an online questionnaire platform was employed to facilitate better access to the students.

Conclusion

The findings of this research indicate that TPB predicts 25.9% of the variance in PA intentions among female high-school students. Attitude emerged as the strongest predictor of intention. Given these results, future educational interventions aimed at increasing PA among adolescents should be designed using the TPB framework, with a particular focus on enhancing positive attitudes toward PA. Addressing perceived behavioral control and subjective norms can also help create a supportive environment that encourages PA.

Prioritizing the enhancement of capabilities and skills to overcome barriers will significantly increase the intentions of high-school students to participate in PA. These contradictions highlight the complexity of factors influencing PA intentions and suggest that more comprehensive research is needed to fully understand these relationships.

Overall, the findings of this survey emphasize the significance of using TPB to predict PA intentions among female high-school students. Attitude emerged as a key predictor, underscoring the need to focus on enhancing positive attitudes toward PA. These insights have important implications for the design of educational interventions aimed at promoting active lifestyles, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes for adolescents.

Proposals for future research

Longitudinal studies tracking changes in PA intentions over time among high-school students can offer a deeper understanding of how intentions evolve and identify critical periods for intervention. Moreover, examining the influence of school environments, including physical education programs, facilities, and policies, can inform the design of school-based interventions. Lastly, exploring cultural and gender differences in PA intentions can enhance the effectiveness of interventions across diverse populations by developing culturally sensitive and gender-specific strategies.
