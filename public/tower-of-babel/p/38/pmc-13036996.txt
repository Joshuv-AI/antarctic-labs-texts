Voclosporin ameliorates proteinuria in a model of non-inflammatory glomerular disease
Authors: Yu Kamigaki, Julie A. Dougherty, Amanda P. Waller, Katelyn J. Wolfgang, Linda M. Rehaume, Jennifer L. Cross, Simon Zhou, Laura E. Biederman, Zackary S. Stevenson, Eman Abdelghani, Bryce A. Kerlin, William E. Smoyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdiopathic nephrotic syndrome (INS) is among the most common glomerular diseases in children with standard first line treatment of glucocorticoids. However, there has been no definitive treatment that can completely cure disease with few side effects for steroid resistant INS patients. Voclosporin (VCS) is a second generation calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) approved in multiple countries for the treatment of adults with active lupus nephritis. VCS does not require therapeutic drug monitoring due to an improved pharmacokinetic profile compared to other CNIs. Based on this, we assessed the ability of VCS to ameliorate proteinuria and hypoalbuminemia in a non-inflammatory glomerular disease using an animal model of NS.MethodsRats received 50 mg/kg puromycin aminonucleoside (PAN) IV to induce NS. Rats were gavaged BID with vehicle or VCS (4 mg/kg/dose), a clinically relevant dose. Outcome measures included proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, serum lipid profiles, glomerular podocyte injury, renal tubular injury, and hypercoagulopathy. Human podocytes were also treated with PAN +/- VCS and assessed for cell viability and stress using XTT and LDH release assays.ResultsVCS treatment significantly ameliorated PAN-induced proteinuria (61% median reduction; P < 0.05) and tubular injury (P < 0.05) in rats. VCS also numerically but insignificantly improved hypoalbuminemia (P = 0.16), hypercoagulopathy (P = 0.099), and glomerular podocyte injury (P = 0.11). VCS did not exacerbate serum lipid profiles of rats with PAN-induced disease. In vitro, VCS significantly protected podocyte viability from PAN-induced toxicity and reduced PAN-induced stress, though not significantly.ConclusionsVCS significantly ameliorated proteinuria in a non-inflammatory model of glomerular disease, and compared favorably regarding improvements in hypoalbuminemia, hypercoagulopathy, dyslipidemia, and podocyte injury. These findings suggest that VCS could be clinically efficacious in patients with primary or secondary NS, without need for drug level testing and with decreased risk of exacerbation of known CNI-related dyslipidemia.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04837-w.

Background

Idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (INS) is one of the most common glomerular diseases in children [1]. It is characterized by massive proteinuria, edema, and hypoalbuminemia, often accompanied by dyslipidemia [2]. Although high-dose daily glucocorticoids (GC) are the mainstay treatment, 10–20% of children do not achieve clinical remission after 4–6 weeks of GC therapy [3]. These patients are classified as steroid resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) and are at the highest risk for progression to end-stage kidney disease [4]. For children with non-genetic SRNS, calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) are the first line alternative immunosuppressive treatment [5, 6]. Both cyclosporine A (CsA) and tacrolimus (TAC) have demonstrated efficacy in SRNS [7]. However, CsA may induce gingival hyperplasia, hypertrichosis, and dyslipidemia, whereas TAC may induce glucose intolerance, and both drugs require therapeutic drug monitoring to maintain efficacy and reduce the risk for tubulointerstitial fibrosis [8]. Thus, there is a critical need for the development of more effective and less toxic treatments for children and adults with SRNS.

Voclosporin (VCS) is a second generation CNI, which is currently approved for use only in adults with lupus nephritis (LN) on a background of immunosuppression. The AURORA 1 and 2 clinical trials demonstrated the efficacy of VCS in LN to induce complete renal remission, of which proteinuria reduction is a major component [9–11]. However, VCS is not yet approved for use in children or adults with any other renal diseases.

Based on the above findings, we hypothesized that VCS would also be effective in ameliorating disease in an acute non-inflammatory animal model of NS, puromycin aminonucleoside nephrotic syndrome (PAN-NS) [12]. In the PAN model, podocytes are directly injured, and proteinuria peaks on day 11 [13–16]. CsA and TAC have been shown to directly protect podocytes in animal and cellular studies [17–19]. In this study, PAN-NS rats were treated with VCS and outcomes regarding proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, blood lipid profiles, hypercoagulopathy, and tubular injury, as well as glomerular and in vitro podocyte injury were assessed.

Materials and methods

Animal study design

All procedures were approved by the NCH Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol AR21-00064) and performed in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Six-week-old male Wistar rats (~ 200 g, Hsd: WI, Inotiv, Lafayette, IN) received 50 mg/kg PAN (Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO) or saline via tail vein injection (500 µl total) on Day 0 (Fig. 1) under conscious restraint. From Day 0 to Day 10, rats were gavaged every 12 h with vehicle (VEH) or VCS (4 mg/kg/dose) under conscious restraint. This dose of VCS is approximately equivalent to the clinically relevant dose used in adults to treat LN, based on pharmacokinetic data [10] and allometric scaling recommended by the FDA [20].

Rats were randomized into four groups: (i) Saline + VEH (n = 6), (ii) PAN + VEH (n = 8), (iii) Saline + VCS (n = 5), (iv) PAN + VCS (n = 9) using the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study described in the ARRIVE 2.0 Guidelines (see Supplemental Material).

Drugs and vehicle

VCS and VEH were provided by Aurinia Pharmaceuticals Inc. The VEH consisted of vitamin E-TPGS, MCT oil, Tween 40, and 95% ethanol (4:2:2:1, by weight; see Supplemental Methods). 1X stock was prepared just prior to gavage. Rats were weighed on Day 0 and every other day to adjust gavage volumes for increasing body weight to maintain precise dosing.

Proteinuria measurement

Spot urine samples were collected on Day 0 (before PAN injection), Day 7, and on Day 11 by placing rats in clean, empty cages until adequate volume could be collected. Urine protein-to-Creatinine Ratios (UPCRs) were measured by the clinical laboratory department at NCH, which is CLIA-certified and GLP-compliant, and reported as total protein/creatinine in mg/mg Cr. UPCR was deemed appropriate to assess proteinuria based on our histological evaluation noting limited interstitial and tubular injury.

Rat exsanguination, blood sample processing, and tissue harvest

On Day 11, rats were anesthetized with 3–5% isoflurane with oxygen and the absence of pedal reflex was used to confirm a surgical plane of anesthesia. Terminal exsanguination/ blood collection via the inferior vena cava was performed, as previously published [21]. Organs of interest were removed, and removal of the heart was used as the secondary method of euthanasia. Kidneys were excised, decapsulated, and rinsed with ice-cold PBS. Kidneys were placed in ice-cold PBS and kept on ice until processed for glomerular isolation. To obtain platelet poor plasma (PPP), 3 ml of whole blood was collected in a syringe with a final concentration of 0.32% sodium citrate and 1.45 µM Corn Trypsin inhibitor (Prolytix, Essex Junction, VT), as described previously [21–23], then processed immediately by centrifugation at 2,500 x g at RT for 15 minutes (min). The upper plasma layer was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged again at 2,500 x g at RT for 15 min, PPP was aliquoted, flash frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C. The red cell phase was checked for clots with a toothpick, and if clots were discovered, the sample was not used for coagulation studies. To obtain serum, 3 ml of whole blood was collected in a syringe without additives and allowed to clot at RT for at least 30 min and centrifuged at 2,500 x g at RT for 15 min. The supernatant was centrifuged at 2,500 x g at RT for 15 min to pellet residual red blood cells, and the serum was aliquoted, flash frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C.

Kidney tissue processing and glomerular isolation

A small section of kidney was fixed in 4% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS). The remainder of the kidneys were used to isolate glomeruli using a standard sequential sieving method, as described previously [24].

Histological assessment of injury

H&E sections were examined in a blinded manner by a nephropathologist and scored for the degree of glomerular injury, tubular injury (acute tubular necrosis, vacuolization, flattening of tubular epithelial cells, casts), interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and vasculature changes including vascular sclerosis. Scores of increasing severities (0/0.5±/1+/2+/3+) were given for each category and summed to obtain a Total Injury Score [25–28] for each rat as reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Measurement of serum lipids and plasma albumin levels

The NCH clinical laboratory directly measured serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was directly measured by ELISA (Cusabio, Houston, TX) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Plasma albumin concentrations were determined by a bromocresol purple (BCP) assay per the manufacturer’s instructions (QuantiChrom BCP Albumin Assay Kit, Bioassay Systems, Hayward, CA).

Glomerular podocyte flow cytometry TUNEL assay

Details for glomerular digestion and staining can be found in the Supplemental Methods and were performed as described previously [31]. Glomeruli were digested to single cells and stained with fluorescence labeled anti-rat synaptopodin antibodies (Biosynth Ltd, United Kingdom) for podocyte detection and the DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System (Promega, Madison, WI) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry was performed on a LSR II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). Data was analyzed using FlowJo software (BD, Ashland, OR).

Thrombin generation assays (TGA)

Platelet-poor-plasma was diluted 1:1 with buffer provided in the Technothrombin TGA Kit (5006010, Technoclone, Vienna, Austria) and TGA reagent C Low, and read on a SpectraMax M2 Plate Reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA) at 360/460 nm [21, 23]. Relative fluorescence units were converted to thrombin (nanomolar) generation curves using Technoclone Evaluation Software (Technoclone, Vienna, Austria), which calculates the characteristic coagulation parameters (lag phase, slope, velocity index, peak thrombin, and area under the curve) as summarized in Supplementary Table S2 with correlation to UPCR and Plasma Albumin in Supplementary Table S3.

In vitro podocyte culture, challenge with PAN, and treatment with VCS

Details for culture and experimental conditions can be found in the Supplemental Methods. Briefly, three independent cultures of conditionally immortalized human podocytes (hiPODs) [32] were differentiated for 8 days at 37 °C in maintenance media.

96-well plates were seeded with 4 × 103 cells/well in maintenance media in technical quadruplicate wells for experimental conditions and technical duplicate for media blank controls. Experimental treatments were prepared in FBS-free media with PAN (50 µg/ml final) or saline and VCS or DMSO as vehicle control. VCS doses tested were 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 0.9, and 2.7 µg/ml.

In vitro podocyte viability and cellular stress

Cell viability at 24 h was measured using the XTT Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a SpectraMax M2 Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

After 24 h,100 µl of conditioned media was analyzed for LDH release by stressed cells using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured with a SpectraMax M2 Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

Percent changes in absorbance were normalized to Saline + DMSO and data from 3 independent cultures were analyzed.

Statistical analyses

One-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD test or Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc test were used for multiple group comparisons to analyze rat treatment groups (n = 5–8/group) and for in vitro podocyte viability and cellular stress, as determined by whether distribution and variance assumptions were met or not. Correlations between NS severity and TGA parameters were analyzed by Spearman correlation tests. All tests were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.3.1, San Diego, CA). Uncorrected P values are indicated in the respective figures and figure legends. In all cases, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Voclosporin ameliorated proteinuria and improved hypoalbuminemia in PAN-NS

Acute PAN-NS rats developed proteinuria by Day 7 with a peak on Day 11 (Supp. Fig. S1), as previously reported [22]. PAN injection induced significant proteinuria by Day 11 vs. Saline + VEH controls (Median UPCR 9.8 vs. 2.0 mg/mg Cr, respectively, P = 0.0007; Fig. 2a). VCS treatment significantly reduced median UPCR vs. the PAN + VEH group (61% reduction; Median UPCR 3.8 vs. 9.8 mg/mg Cr, respectively; P = 0.0374) (Fig. 2a). Notably, VCS treatment alone did not increase Day 11 proteinuria in saline-injected rats (Fig. 2a).

Plasma albumin levels usually inversely correlate with proteinuria [21], if there are no other losses, liver dysfunction, or underlying malnutrition [33]. We tested Day 11 plasma albumin levels and found that PAN treatment significantly decreased median plasma albumin levels vs. Saline + VEH controls (22% reduction; 3.00 vs. 3.85 g/dl, respectively; P = 0.0001) (Fig. 2b). VCS treatment increased albumin levels vs. PAN + VEH (9% increase; 3.26 vs. 3.00 g/dl respectively; P = 0.1606, Fig. 2b), but the change did not reach statistical significance. VCS treatment alone did not significantly alter plasma albumin levels in Saline-injected rats (Fig. 2b). Additionally, plasma creatinine and BUN levels were not affected by either PAN or VCS treatment (Supp. Fig. S2).

Collectively, VCS treatment both significantly reduced proteinuria and improved plasma albumin levels in this NS model.

Voclosporin provided protection against renal tubular injury in PAN-NS

While the PAN-NS model primarily reflects podocyte injury and glomerular disease, secondary tubular injury related to proteinuria can also be observed. PAN is known to cause renal tubulointerstitial injury, though mainly at doses of 100 mg/kg or more in the acute model or with repeated PAN dosing in the chronic model [34–36]. At lower doses, like those used in this study, glomerular and renal tubular injury are more subtle [37]. In the current study, we assessed whether VCS treatment could demonstrate reduced renal tissue injury in the PAN-NS model. Rat kidney sections were stained with PAS and assessed for the degree of glomerular injury, tubular injury (acute tubular necrosis, vacuolization, flattening of tubular epithelial cells, and the presence of Tamm-Horsfall protein casts), interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA), and vasculature changes including vascular sclerosis. Neither glomerular nor vascular alterations were seen in any rats. Each parameter was scored based on severity (0, 0.5±, 1+, 2+, or 3+; corresponding to none, < 25%, 25–50%, 50–75%, > 75%) and summed for a Total Injury Score [25–28] (Supp. Table S1). Subtle differences between groups were observed (Supp. Fig. S3) with the most common types of damage observed including acute tubular necrosis and vacuolization (Supp. Table S1). PAN + VEH rats also exhibited Tamm-Horsfall protein casts and IFTA (Supp. Table S1). PAN + VEH-treated rats had a similar median total injury score vs. Saline + VEH rats (2.0 for both). Interestingly, PAN + VCS rats exhibited significantly decreased total injury scores vs. PAN + VEH rats (1.0 vs. 2.0, respectively, P = 0.0469) (Fig. 3). VCS treatment alone did not induce tubular injury (Fig. 3). These results suggested that treatment of PAN-induced NS with VCS resulted in reduced renal tubular injury in nephrotic animals.

In our acute PAN-NS model, PAN + VEH-treated rats had increased values for all lipid parameters vs. Saline + VEH control rats, reaching significance for LDL-C (P = 0.0116, Table 1). VCS treatment of PAN-NS rats increased lipid levels for TG and TC, but these were not significant compared to either PAN + VEH or Saline + VEH (Table 1, Supp. Fig. S4a, b). VLDL-C levels were increased significantly in PAN + VCS rats versus PAN + VEH rats (P = 0.047) (Table 1, Supp. Fig. S4c). VCS treatment reduced median LDL-C levels vs. PAN + VEH rats, but this was not significant (Table 1, Supp. Fig. S4d). Levels of HDL-C remained comparable across all treatment groups (Table 1, Supp. Fig. S4e). Treatment with VCS alone did increase TG, TC, and VLDL-C levels vs. Saline + VEH (Table 1) but these changes were not significant. Overall, these data demonstrate that VCS does not significantly exacerbate NS-induced dyslipidemia, as has been reported for other CNIs [45].

Voclosporin improved nephrotic syndrome-associated hypercoagulopathy

We previously demonstrated that NS-associated hypercoagulopathy correlates directly with NS severity and improves with proteinuria reduction in both rodent models and patients [21, 23]. The thrombin generation assay (TGA) is a global hemostasis assay measuring the tendency of plasma to clot [21]. Fig. 4a shows a representative thrombin generation curve and its related parameters in normal plasma. The most clinically important parameter of this curve related to NS is the Endogenous Thrombin Potential (ETP) [23], where the area under the curve corresponds to the total amount of thrombin formed and is directly proportional to extent of hypercoagulopathy. Representative thrombin generation curves (Fig. 4b) and ETP values (Fig. 4c) are shown for each treatment group. PAN + VEH significantly increased ETP vs. Saline + VEH (P = 0.0046). VCS treatment of PAN-NS rats ameliorated the increase in ETP, though not significantly (P = 0.0996, Fig. 4c). This pattern of increased values with PAN + VEH and improvement in PAN + VCS was seen in other TGA parameters as well (Supp. Table S2). Correlation studies demonstrated significant correlations of TGA parameters with plasma albumin or proteinuria (Supp. Table S3). Treatment with VCS alone did not significantly affect ETP levels (P = 0.1410) (Fig. 4c) or other coagulation parameters (Supp. Table S2). Together, these data suggest that VCS ameliorated NS-associated hypercoagulopathy.

Voclosporin (VCS) protected glomerular podocytes from injury in PAN-NS

To directly evaluate podocyte responses to injury with PAN and potential protection by VCS, we analyzed glomerular podocyte injury with TUNEL assays to assess double-stranded DNA breakage. Isolated glomeruli were digested to single cells, stained with a podocyte marker (synaptopodin), stained for TUNEL, and analyzed via flow cytometry to enumerate the number of podocyte- and TUNEL-positive cells (Fig. 5a) (31). Compared to the Saline + VEH group, all PAN-treated groups had significantly more TUNEL-positive podocytes (PAN + VEH: P = 0.0005; PAN + VCS, P = 0.0377, respectively; Fig. 5b). Median double positivity for the PAN + VCS group was 2.5%, which was lower than the PAN + VEH rats, but not significant (P = 0.1058). Treatment with VCS alone did not significantly increase podocyte TUNEL positivity (P = 0.556) (Fig. 5b). These results suggested that VCS provided protection against direct podocyte injury in this NS model.

Voclosporin (VCS) improved in vitro podocyte viability and decreased cellular stress after PAN treatment

We assessed the ability of VCS to protect conditionally immortalized human podocytes [32] (hiPODs) from PAN-induced toxicity and stress. Differentiated hiPODs were challenged with 50 µg/ml PAN (or Saline control) and treated with VCS (or DMSO vehicle control) over a dose curve (0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 0.9, and 2.7 µg/ml). An XTT assay was used to assess cell viability and LDH release assayed for cytotoxicity and cellular stress. After 24 h, results showed that PAN treatment significantly reduced cell viability to 49% (P = 0.0012, Fig. 6a) vs. No PAN + DMSO treated cells (set at 100%). VCS rescued cell viability in PAN-treated cells at all doses and was significantly increased at 0.03 µg/ml (83%, P = 0.0350) and 0.3 µg/ml (85%, P = 0.0102) vs. PAN + DMSO-treated cells. For cellular stress analyses, LDH release was significantly increased 1.98-fold in PAN + DMSO treated cells (P = 0.0301, Fig. 6b) vs. No PAN + DMSO cells. VCS decreased cellular stress in PAN-treated hiPODs at 0.03–0.9 µg/ml, but this was not significant. The dose-response curve showed that at 2.7 µg/ml VCS treatment in PAN-treated cells significantly increased cellular stress as compared to PAN + DMSO-treated cells (1.46-fold, P = 0.0186) (Fig. 6b). These results demonstrate that VCS protected hiPODs by reducing cell death and cellular stress induced by PAN treatment.

Discussion

In the current study, we hypothesized that VCS could effectively treat non-inflammatory glomerular disease in a rat model. VCS is currently only approved for use in lupus nephritis, an inflammatory glomerular disease, but improved proteinuria in these patients suggested therapeutic potential in other glomerular disease types. The acute PAN model induces significant proteinuria that peaks at day 10–14 and resolves in 4–5 weeks [13, 15, 16]. Based on the proteinuria on day 11, VCS clearly reduced proteinuria, thus altering the clinical disease course. Our data demonstrated that VCS significantly reduced proteinuria in a model of non-inflammatory glomerular disease (i.e., PAN-NS) at a clinically relevant human dose. Our data also demonstrated that VCS did not exacerbate dyslipidemia, which is a known concern for CsA use in humans [39, 46, 47]. Overall, the current findings suggest that VCS could be clinically efficacious in patients with primary or secondary NS, without need for drug level testing.

It has been reported that TC, TG, VLDL, intermediate-density lipoprotein, LDL, lipoprotein(a), and TC/HDL ratio are all elevated in NS (41, 48). Inclinical trials, VCS did not worsen lipid profiles, in contrast to results for TAC [10, 49]. In our study, PAN induced moderate changes in lipid profiles, supporting the clinical relevance of the PAN-NS model as a model of NS-associated dyslipidemia, as observed by others [40, 42–44, 50–56]. VCS tended to ameliorate several lipid parameters.

Currently, the need for repeated blood draws for periodic therapeutic drug monitoring is a significant burden and expense for children being treated with CNIs for NS, with monitoring recommended every 1–3 months to avoid nephrotoxicity [6]. In contrast, due to the improved pharmacokinetics of VCS [57], drug levels do not need to be tested, leading to significant clinical benefit to patients and families.

Our data also demonstrated that VCS protected both glomerular and cultured podocytes from injury, the target cell of injury in NS. We also found that VCS significantly protected kidneys from tubular injury in NS rats, further demonstrating its cytoprotective effects. Studies of other CNIs in NS animal models and cultured cells have demonstrated protective effects via various molecular mechanisms, mainly centered around the actin cytoskeleton. Synaptopodin is a key cytoskeletal protein in podocytes that is stabilized in its phosphorylated state, which can be reversed by the phosphatase activity of calcineurin [17]. Studies in nephrotic rat models [58, 59] and the PAN-NS rat model [60] showed markedly increased CN phosphatase activity in nephrotic glomeruli, which was then ameliorated with TAC [58, 59]. Thus, when CNIs are applied to podocytes they increase the expression of synaptopodin, increase stress fiber content, and prevent apoptosis [17]. Further studies in PAN-treated rats and cultured podocytes revealed that CNIs restored nephrin, synaptopodin, cofilin-1, phospho-cofilin-1, and podocin expression [18, 61]. Another study identified CNI protection against PAN-induced injury via inhibition of Nfatc1 and Angptl4 [19]. Rats and cultured podocytes exposed to adriamycin and treated with CNI also showed protection of the actin cytoskeleton; [62, 63] restoration of FKBP12 [63], transgelin, synaptopodin, and nephrin expression; [64] and amelioration of mitochondrial morphological changes [62]. Future studies will address molecular changes ameliorated by VCS treatment in PAN-NS rats.

A limitation of these studies is our use of only one animal model of non-inflammatory glomerular disease to evaluate the efficacy of VCS. However, the PAN-NS model is the most widely accepted model for acute NS and has been used extensively for nearly 70 years as a reliable model of acute non-inflammatory glomerular disease [65]. This study also confirmed the ability of the acute PAN-NS model to successfully reproduce the generalized features of NS-associated dyslipidemia seen in human NS. Clinical proteinuria was the primary outcome measure in this study and was assessed using UPCR. This was deemed an appropriate method due to the limited interstitial and tubular injury seen in our histological evaluation. For this study, we were unable to quantitate foot process effacement due to lack of tissue for electron microscopy.

In summary, we found that VCS significantly ameliorated proteinuria and tubular injury in a non-inflammatory model of glomerular disease, and also improved hypoalbuminemia, dyslipidemia, hypercoagulopathy, and podocyte health. Together, these findings suggest that VCS could be clinically efficacious in patients with primary or secondary NS, without need for drug level testing and with more favorable lipid profiles than other CNIs.
