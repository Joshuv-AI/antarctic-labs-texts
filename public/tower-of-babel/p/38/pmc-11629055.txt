Eating Disorders in Children, Adolescents, and Young Adults During and After the COVID‐19 Pandemic: A Danish Nationwide Register‐Based Study
Authors: Heidi Sonne, Helene Kildegaard, Katrine Strandberg‐Larsen, Lotte Rasmussen, Rikke Wesselhoeft, Mette Bliddal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo assess incidence rates of clinically diagnosed eating disorders among Danish youth before, during, and after the COVID‐19 pandemic.MethodThis study used a population‐based time series analysis with individual‐level data from Danish healthcare registries. Participants included all Danish individuals aged 6–24 years from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2023. Monthly incident cases of any eating disorder, anorexia nervosa, and bulimia nervosa were analyzed using interrupted time‐series analysis. We estimated the cumulative number of excess cases of incident eating disorder diagnoses and risk ratios (RR) associated with the pandemic and post‐pandemic periods compared with pre‐pandemic predictions.ResultsThe study population encountered a mean of 1,310,542 individuals during the study period. The study included 11,693 individuals diagnosed with an eating disorder, median age 17 (IQR 14–20 years). Incident cases increased during the pandemic (RR 1.29, 95% CI 1.15–1.45) and normalized post‐pandemic (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.91–1.25) compared with pre‐pandemic predictions. Similar patterns were seen for anorexia and bulimia. Increases were significant for ages 13–16 (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.02–1.38), 17–19 (RR 1.51, 95% CI 1.25–1.83), and 20–24 (RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.12–1.64). Post‐pandemically, a continued increase was observed only for ages 20–24 (RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.02–1.71).ConclusionsThe study documents a temporary increase in diagnosed eating disorders among Danish youths during the COVID‐19 pandemic, with rates later returning to pre‐pandemic levels. These results provide insights into the pandemic's impact on adolescents and youths.Keywords: adolescent, anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, child, COVID‐19, eating disorders, epidemiology, mental health, young adult

Summary

Although reverting to pre‐pandemic levels in the initial years post‐pandemic for most age groups, our findings underscore the necessity of considering that significant external threats and their repercussions may negatively impact the risk of eating disorders in adolescents and young adults.

Introduction

Mental health deteriorated among children and adolescents during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Madigan et al. 2023), leading to increased psychotropic medication use and incidence rates (IRs) of anxiety and hyperkinetic disorders in Denmark (Bliddal et al. 2023). Also, more youths, especially adolescent girls, were diagnosed with eating disorders (EDs) during the COVID‐19 pandemic in Norway, the United Kingdom, and the United States (Surén et al. 2022; Taquet et al. 2021; Trafford et al. 2023). It is unknown if this rise in incident ED diagnoses persisted after the pandemic.

In Denmark, the government implemented two major lockdowns during the COVID‐19 pandemic: the first from March 2020, and the second from December 2020, both with partial reopening during the following spring and summer. Measures included restrictions on physical interactions, mandatory mask‐wearing in public spaces, closure of schools and leisure activities, and remote work directives. During these periods, education shifted to digital learning, with partial in‐person education during reopening phases (Statens Serum Institut 2022). Denmark lifted all pandemic‐related restrictions on January 31, 2022.

So far, no studies have examined the IRs of EDs in the period after the pandemic. We examined IRs of diagnosed EDs among Danish children, adolescents, and young adults before, during, and after the pandemic with a follow‐up of almost 2 years after restrictions were abandoned. Based on previous literature, we hypothesized IRs to be higher during the pandemic. We had no hypothesis regarding the post‐pandemic period, making this aspect of our study exploratory.

Method

The study was a population‐based time series analysis. We linked all Danish individuals aged 6–24 years from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2023, to individual‐level data on diagnoses and dates of hospital encounters from the Danish National Patient Registry. The Patient Registry includes both inpatient and outpatient contacts at hospitals (Schmidt et al. 2015). Eating disorders were defined as any primary or secondary diagnosis, registered across psychiatric and somatic hospital settings with International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD‐10) codes of F50.X. We specifically examined the two ED subtypes anorexia nervosa (F50.0 + F50.1) and bulimia nervosa (F50.2 + F50.3), both including typical and atypical variants.

We assessed the monthly IRs of any ED and ED subtypes stratified by age groups (6–12, 13–16, 17–19, and 20–24 years) enabling comparisons with existing literature (Surén et al. 2022; Taquet et al. 2021; Trafford et al. 2023). The IRs were calculated as the number of individuals with a first‐ever ED diagnosis per month per 100,000 person‐years (py).

Interrupted time‐series analyses were used to examine the association between specific time periods and ED diagnoses. Models were based on time‐series of monthly numbers of incident ED diagnoses from January 2015 through December 2023 with a total of 105 data points. Monthly counts were modeled using negative binomial regression with inclusion of Fourier terms to account for seasonality (Bhaskaran et al. 2013). The time series was divided into three periods: (1) before the pandemic from January 2015 to February 2020, (2) during the pandemic from March 2020 to January 2022, and (3) after the pandemic from February 2022 to December 2023. All pandemic‐related restrictions were abolished in Denmark on January 31st, 2022. We modeled the level change (step function) of the pandemic and post‐pandemic periods and estimated the cumulative number of excess cases of incident EDs and corresponding risk ratios (RRs) for each period, comparing the fitted trend of observed numbers with predictions derived from the pre‐pandemic trend. Analyses were performed using STATA 18 (StatCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The study was registered at University of Southern Denmark's repository (record no. 11.106). According to Danish law, purely registry‐based studies are exempt from ethical approval.

Results

The study population encountered a mean of 1,310,542 individuals during the study period. The number of individuals with any incident ED diagnosis was 11,693. The median age at first ED diagnosis was 17 years (interquartile range [IQR] 14–20). The median age was lower for anorexia nervosa (16 years, IQR 14–19) than for bulimia nervosa (21 years, IQR 18–23). Males accounted for 9.0% of incident ED diagnoses.

The overall IRs of any ED increased during the pandemic, from 94 before the pandemic to 116/100,000 py, and subsequently decreased again after the pandemic to 95/100,000 py. This pattern was also observed for anorexia nervosa specifically (IR 50 to 64 to 53/100,000 py). For bulimia nervosa, the IR was on a downward trend in the period before the pandemic (IR 27/100,000 py in 2015 and 16/100,000 py in 2019). However similarly, the IR during the pandemic was higher than expected from the pre‐pandemic trend (19/100,000 py) and the IR decreased again after the pandemic (12/100,000 py) (Figure 1A). Overall, we estimated 653 excess cases (95% CI 368–938) diagnosed with incident EDs during the pandemic compared with the trend before the pandemic corresponding to an RR of 1.29 (95% CI 1.15–1.45) (Figure 1B). For incident anorexia nervosa the number of estimated excess cases was 339 (95% CI 131–548) compared with pre‐pandemic predictions, leading to an RR of 1.27 (95% 1.09–1.48). The corresponding estimates for bulimia nervosa were 130 excess cases (95% CI 58–202) and an RR of 1.38 (95% CI 1.15–1.67). In the 23 months following the pandemic period, the number of individuals with any incident ED diagnosis returned to the expected values (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.91–1.25) (Figure 1B), which were also seen for anorexia nervosa (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.85–1.29) and bulimia nervosa (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.86–1.45).

When stratified by age groups, there was an increase in the number of individuals diagnosed with any ED among individuals aged 13–16 years (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.02–1.38), 17–19 years (RR 1.51, 95% CI 1.25–1.83), and 20–24 years (RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.12–1.64) during the pandemic. There were no deviations from the expected trend for children aged 6–12 years (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.77–1.26). In the period after the pandemic, the number of individuals diagnosed with incident ED exceeded expectations only among individuals aged 20–24 years (RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.02–1.71), while the number of incident ED diagnoses returned to expected values for the remaining age groups.

Discussion

This study documents an increase in the incidence of individuals diagnosed with EDs among Danish 13–24‐year‐olds during the COVID‐19 pandemic. However, the escalation in new cases of EDs was temporary as the IRs approached the pre‐pandemic levels in the subsequent 2 years.

Our findings related to the pandemic align with results from Norway, the United Kingdom, and the United States, which all found that young individuals, particularly females, experienced an increase in the number of diagnosed EDs (Surén et al. 2022; Taquet et al. 2021; Trafford et al. 2023). EDs often occur comorbid to other psychiatric disorders like mood and anxiety disorders as well as attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorders (Yao et al. 2019; Momen et al. 2022), all of which increased during the pandemic, especially among female children and adolescents (Madigan et al. 2023; Bliddal et al. 2023).

Importantly, our extended follow‐up enabled us to document that the pandemic‐related effect on ED rates was reversible in most age groups. The temporary increase in IRs simultaneous with the pandemic may suggest that pandemic‐related factors played a pivotal role in the development of ED diagnoses. These factors include disrupted routines and social isolation (Devoe et al. 2023). Moreover, due to increased vigilance from the parents towards their children's well‐being, this may have led to a shorter time period between the onset of symptoms and diagnosis of an ED. Among the oldest age group, the 20–24‐year‐olds, the number of individuals diagnosed with incident EDs exceeded expectations beyond the pandemic. The sustained increase in IRs in this group could suggest that this age group may have been particularly affected by pandemic‐related stressors or experienced a delay in diagnosis during this period. The possible effects of the pandemic on the risk of EDs underscores the need for attention in times of crisis, with healthcare systems prioritizing mental health support for youths.

This study has strengths in its nationwide coverage with no risk of selection bias, and inclusion of recent data until the end of 2023. Limitations include identification of EDs by hospital contacts only, enabling only identification of the most severe cases of ED. Our findings may thus underestimate the true incidence of EDs. However, and noteworthy, both in‐ and outpatient contacts are included in the registry. Due to the small number of males diagnosed with EDs, we were unable to stratify the analysis by sex.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the number of individuals with a first‐ever diagnosis of an ED increased during the COVID‐19 pandemic, especially among 13–24‐year‐olds. However, the IRs reverted to pre‐pandemic levels in the first years after the pandemic, showing a momentary change only. While reassuring, the findings emphasize that youths were highly susceptible to the pandemic‐related changes in their lives. Our findings warrant attention when planning potential future lockdowns or periods of social disruption.
