Integrating AI in seed science: Toward an intelligent design paradigm
Authors: Ying Zhang, Jianjun Du, Guanmin Huang, Yue Zhao, Peng Man, Anran Song, Yanxin Zhao, Qingmei Men, Chuanyu Wang, Minkun Guo, Xinyu Guo, Chunjiang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal agricultural systems face mounting threats to food security from climate change, population growth, and land degradation, with current productivity gains insufficient to meet the demands of a projected global population of 9.7 billion by 2050. Seeds, as both carriers of genetic information and the foundation of agricultural production, directly determine crop yield, resilience, and quality. Advancing seed innovation is therefore essential for achieving sustainable increases in agricultural productivity. This review traces the evolution of seed science from agrarian civilization to the era of intelligent seed design and summarizes recent advances in AI-based methodological innovations and applications. We introduce the emerging paradigm of AI-driven seed design, outline its core scientific questions and key technologies, and propose integrated technological pathways. Furthermore, we analyze current challenges and highlight future directions in this field. By integrating the latest research and technological developments, this review aims to establish an “AI for Science” paradigm for future-oriented seed research that meets the increasing global demand for sustainable and high-quality seed resources.Key words: seed phenotyping, artificial intelligence, multi-omics, digital twin, intelligent agent

Introduction

Global warming, population growth, land degradation, and environmental pollution are exerting unprecedented pressure on agricultural systems, posing a severe threat to global food security. According to United Nations projections, the world population will reach 9.7 billion by 2050 (Evie et al., 2015). Meanwhile, cultivated land degradation has reached 33% and, together with increasing extreme climate events, is expected to reduce staple crop yields by 3.2%–7.4%. Conventional breeding cycles—often exceeding eight years—are insufficient to keep pace with these escalating challenges. As a result, current rates of growth in food production are unlikely to meet future demand (Committee on Science Breakthroughs 2030 et al., 2019). In response, many countries have elevated seed innovation to the core of national strategy. Australia launched the “Smart Agriculture 2025” initiative (Langridge et al., 2014); Germany initiated the AVATARS project (Advanced Virtuality and Augmented Reality Approaches in Seeds to Seeds, https://www.avatars-project.de/); and China established the Yazhou Bay National Seed Innovation Platform. Together, these efforts aim to propel an interdisciplinary technological revolution in seed science and establish a new paradigm for ensuring global food security.

Seeds, as core carriers of generational continuity in higher plants, contain the embryo, nutrient reserves, and protective structures, and encapsulate favorable traits accumulated through millennia of domestication and natural selection. However, in the face of global climate change and increasing complexity in agricultural production, conventional research paradigms in seed science have revealed significant limitations. Three major bottlenecks are particularly prominent: (1) insufficient systemic analysis of genotype–environment–phenotype interactions, which constrains the efficiency of trait improvement; (2) limited precision in capturing the dynamic life processes of seeds, hindering a mechanistic understanding of stress adaptation; and (3) weak predictive capacity of empirical models, which fails to support breeding decisions under dynamic environmental conditions (Romero Navarro et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2023; Lenzo et al., 2025). With rapid scientific and technological progress, the deep integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and multi-omics technologies is opening new avenues. Multi-omics approaches—including genomics, metabolomics, single-cell omics, and spatial omics—enable the “panoramic, high-definition mapping” of seed genetic and metabolic blueprints, thereby accelerating trait discovery and breeding by design. Advanced non-destructive monitoring and intelligent sensing technologies facilitate dynamic and in-depth characterization of seed life systems. Meanwhile, DL models empower “precise prediction and forward-looking design” of seed responses to environmental changes (Fu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Together, these developments are driving a paradigm shift in seed science—from experience-based to intelligence-driven research—providing a powerful technological engine for unlocking genetic potential and addressing climate-related challenges.

This review traces the evolution of seed science from agrarian civilization to the era of intelligent design and summarizes recent advances in AI-driven methodological innovations and their applications in seed science. In light of mounting global food security challenges and the limitations of conventional breeding, further intensified by rapid advances in AI, we survey the emerging concept of AI-driven seed design. We introduce key definitions and characteristics, analyze its core scientific questions and enabling technologies, and outline the corresponding technological pathways. Overall, this review aims to establish a framework for an “AI for Science” paradigm in future-oriented seed science, offering forward-looking strategies to address the increasingly urgent global challenge of food security.

The historical development of seed science: From agrarian civilization to the intelligent era

The historical trajectory of seed science has been well documented in previous reviews. Notably, Edward Buckler’s 2018 Annual Review of Genetics article outlines the progression from Breeding 1.0 to 4.0, among other frameworks (Wallace et al., 2018; Fu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Building on these perspectives, this section summarizes the progression of seed science from empirical practice to intelligent design, with a focus on shifts in cognitive approaches and research paradigms (Figure 1).

Stage of empirical cognition (ca. 10,000 BCE–late 19th century)

Early seed research was rooted in empirical observation and marked the emergence of artificial selection through trait-based crop domestication. Documented practices include saline seed selection in Roman agriculture and the domestication of rice and millet in ancient China around 7000 BCE (Fuller, 2007; Melinda, 2011). The field transitioned from descriptive observation to analytical investigation in the mid-18th century with the advent of microscopy, which revealed internal seed structures. This shift was further consolidated by Linnaeus’s classification system (Linnaeus, 1753), the formulation of cell theory (1838), and Mendel’s laws of inheritance (Mendel, 1886). During this period, the integration of anatomical, morphological, and genetic perspectives established a foundational framework for modern seed research and accumulated essential empirical knowledge, despite the absence of a unifying theory.

The establishment of quantitative selection theory (late 19th–mid-20th century)

The early 20th-century rediscovery of Mendel’s laws formally introduced genetics into seed technology, providing the theoretical basis for quantitative selection theory. Subsequently, a key milestone was Shull’s proposal of heterosis theory in 1914 and the development of hybrid maize, which launched modern hybrid breeding (Shull, 1948). This progress helped fuel the Green Revolution through high-yielding semi-dwarf wheat and IR8 rice varieties (Khush, 1999; Borlaug, 2007). With the rapid expansion of hybrid breeding, seed quality control became increasingly important. It was further systematized through advances in physiological studies and the establishment of international testing standards (e.g., the International Seed Testing Association, ISTA) (Kloppenburg, 1988; Peng et al., 1999; Baud et al., 2008; Davière and Achard, 2013).

Molecular breakthroughs and precise manipulation (mid-20th–21st century)

With the emergence of molecular biology, seed research entered a new stage characterized by precise manipulation at the molecular level. Whole-genome sequencing and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have enabled the identification and cloning of genes and quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with resistance, stress tolerance, quality, and yield. Elucidation of their molecular pathways and allelic interaction networks has further provided actionable targets for germplasm enhancement (Bai et al., 2007; Zuo and Li, 2014; Li and Li, 2016; Shu et al., 2016; Li et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020). Integrated multi-omics approaches, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, have revealed the genetic regulatory mechanisms underlying key agronomic traits such as grain weight, grain size, spikelet number, and environmental adaptation (Xiang et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). The combined application of marker-assisted selection (MAS) and transgenic technologies gave rise to a transformative technical system for germplasm innovation, enabling precise molecular manipulation (Ruperao et al., 2025). Despite these advances, bottlenecks remained, particularly the limited systemic understanding of genotype–environment–phenotype interactions. Meanwhile, global seed conservation programs were launched, and germplasm resource banks (seed banks) have been established across multiple countries (Table 1). These efforts support the conservation, characterization, and utilization of seed resources at national and regional levels (Li and Pritchard, 2009; Smale and Jamora, 2020).

The era of intelligent design (emerging over the past decade)

The integration of biotechnology and information technology is currently accelerating the transition from molecular design breeding (Breeding 3.0) to intelligent design breeding (Breeding 4.0). AI models, such as DeepFDML and AlphaFold, have substantially improved the accuracy of target gene identification and phenotypic prediction (Abramson et al., 2024). Meanwhile, genome editing tools, including CRISPR–Cas systems and prime editing, have overcome natural genetic constraints, enabling precise and programmable genetic modification. Together, these advances mark a paradigm shift toward “autonomous decision-making,” in which AI-driven approaches help overcome historical bottlenecks, particularly in predictive modeling (Fu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Notably, however, the “intelligent design era” remains largely confined to the research domain, with only a limited number of applications translated into practical breeding (Fu et al., 2025).

The evolution of seed science reveals a clear trajectory of paradigm shifts. Methodologically, it has advanced from qualitative description to quantitative analysis; technologically, from simple tools to intelligent precision systems; scientifically, from phenotypic observation to molecular mechanism elucidation; and practically, from experience-based practice to data-driven decision-making.

The integration of AI, particularly machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), is fundamentally transforming seed science by enabling novel methodologies and accelerating their translation into industrial practice. Advances in AI applications in seed science research

Methodological innovations

High-throughput seed phenotyping, enabled by advanced computer vision and predominantly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has revolutionized the analysis of seed imagery. By integrating data from RGB, hyperspectral, X-ray, and other imaging modalities, these models enable non-destructive, high-throughput quantification of morphological traits (e.g., size, shape, and color), internal structures, and subtle defects. This transition shifts phenotypic assessment from subjective manual evaluation to objective, precise, and rapid digital metrics (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c, 2025d).

Predictive modeling for complex traits, such as ML models including random forests, support vector machines, and gradient boosting algorithms, is increasingly trained on multi-modal datasets that integrate image-derived features, spectral signatures, genetic markers, and environmental variables. These models excel at predicting complex, physiologically relevant traits such as seed viability, germination vigor, and longevity (storability), providing powerful data-driven tools for quality prediction and decision support (Li et al., 2023a; Qi et al., 2024).

Advanced seed health diagnostics based on DL algorithms, particularly deep CNNs, demonstrate superior performance in the early and accurate detection of seed-borne pathogens (e.g., fungi and bacteria), insect damage, and physiological disorders. Their ability to capture subtle visual patterns, often imperceptible to the human eye, enables precise phytosanitary assessment and proactive quality control (Jin et al., 2025).

Traditional QTL mapping methods, such as linkage analysis and GWASs, have played fundamental roles in identifying loci associated with complex traits. However, these approaches often struggle with high-dimensional genomic data, polygenic inheritance, and genotype-by-environment (G×E) interactions. Recent advances in AI and ML provide powerful complementary strategies, enabling more accurate trait prediction, robust marker–trait associations, and efficient feature selection. Notably, recent breakthroughs in ML and AI have enhanced predictive modeling, significantly improving our ability to identify key genes underlying complex seed traits and to optimize breeding strategies (Kassem, 2025; Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025).

Applied deployments and industrial implementation

In practical settings, AI technologies are rapidly transitioning from laboratory research to industrial-scale applications across the entire seed value chain, encompassing processing, quality control, conservation, and distribution. At the production level, intelligent optical sorters equipped with real-time AI-based vision models are increasingly integrated into processing lines. These systems perform multi-parameter classification to efficiently remove off-types, damaged seeds, and impurities, thereby significantly improving the genetic purity and physical quality of seed lots. For quality assurance, fully automated phenotyping platforms employ AI-driven time-series image analysis to continuously monitor germination assays (Farooq et al., 2024). These systems automatically count germinated seeds, classify seedling development (normal vs. abnormal), and quantify early growth metrics, thereby standardizing and objectifying vigor assessment. In germplasm conservation, AI enables the large-scale digitization and management of ex situ collections. Automated image analysis supports the digital cataloging and periodic health monitoring of seed accessions, while predictive models optimize conservation strategies by forecasting viability decline and informing regeneration schedules. In addition, within supply chain management, the integration of AI with blockchain and other digital ledger technologies is being leveraged to establish immutable audit trails. This synergy strengthens seed certification, ensures genetic identity, and enables traceability from breeding programs to end-users, thereby substantially enhancing supply chain integrity (Mahant and Pal, 2025).

Toward an AI-driven seed design paradigm: Historical context, conceptual frameworks, and core scientific challenges

Research bottlenecks in current seed science

In light of mounting global food security challenges and the limitations of conventional breeding, several urgent issues remain to be addressed in seed science. (1) System-level modeling of the complex interaction among genotype, environment, and phenotype: although current studies can identify relationships between specific genes and traits, understanding of system-level associations—such as gene regulatory networks and phenotypic plasticity across diverse environments—remains limited. (2) Breeding bottlenecks in multi-trait optimization: traditional breeding and molecular design often focus on a single or a few traits, whereas practical breeding requires the simultaneous optimization of multiple traits including yield, quality, stress resistance, and resource-use efficiency. (3) Inefficient mining and functional characterization of large-scale genetic resources: despite the availability of extensive germplasm sequencing data, gene function annotation remains incomplete, and the identification of superior alleles is inefficient. (4) High costs of field trials: conventional breeding relies on long cycles, labor-intensive field phenotyping, and is highly susceptible to seasonal and environmental fluctuations. (5) Insufficient precision and predictability in gene editing and synthetic biology: current gene editing techniques still face challenges such as off-target effects, inconsistent editing efficiency, and difficulties in predicting the outcomes of multiplex gene editing.

The emergence of an AI-driven seed design paradigm

Currently, in response to the aforementioned bottlenecks—and accelerated by advances in AI—an emerging research paradigm is taking shape, which we refer to as the AI-driven seed design paradigm. This paradigm builds on advances at the frontiers of crop phenomics, big data analytics, and multi-omics studies, focusing on the in-depth decoding of complex biological systems in crop seeds and aiming to construct a data-driven, intelligent design paradigm (Figure 2A). Combined with biotechnologies (BT), such as gene editing and synthetic biology, as well as AI technologies including embodied intelligence and robotics, this paradigm seeks to enable precise identification, intelligent design, smart creation, and adaptive optimization of seeds. This framework is expected to advance across three dimensions. (1) Scientific dimension: digital analysis of regulatory networks from genes to phenotypes, systematic decoding of genetic regulation in complex biological systems, and elucidation of the molecular basis of seed trait formation. (2) Application dimension: development of technologies for precise trait integration and intelligent environmental adaptation, offering pathways for yield stability under stress and promoting the coordinated improvement of crop yield and quality. (3) Industrial dimension: deep integration of AI and BT across the entire value chain—from breeding to production and deployment—thereby transforming the seed industry toward “customized variety development” and “intelligent cultivation” (Figure 2B).

Specifically, the AI-driven seed design paradigm utilizes perception systems to acquire real-time environmental information, integrates these data with built-in decision-making modules for comprehensive analysis and evaluation, and autonomously formulates breeding strategies. These strategies are then executed through actuating or robotic systems, enabling efficient management and control of complex breeding processes. The diversity and complexity of data sources provide the foundation for the design and implementation of intelligent seed design systems. Key data types include genomic, phenotypic, environmental monitoring data, and management operation data, which together constitute a multidimensional information space throughout the breeding process. However, significant differences in formats and spatiotemporal scales pose major challenges for data fusion and analysis. To address these challenges, it is essential to establish a multi-level, multi-scale modeling framework based on universal representation (UR), enabling the effective integration of heterogeneous data types. Building on this foundation, the construction of large-scale models and digital twins is critical. This involves: (1) developing high-fidelity, dynamically updated virtual models of seeds and their environments to achieve comprehensive digital mapping of the seed life cycle and its surroundings; (2) utilizing IoT and smart sensing technologies to enable real-time synchronization between physical systems and virtual models, ensuring accuracy and timeliness; (3) integrating multimodal data with advanced algorithms to support multi-objective optimization and intelligent decision-making in complex breeding scenarios; and (4) adopting scalable and modular architectures to accommodate diverse breeding and production demands. With continued advances in AI, smart decision-making systems are increasingly integrated with embodied intelligence, enabling them to rely not only on abstract computation and reasoning but also on direct interaction with the physical environment. This capability allows agents to dynamically adjust their actions based on perceptual feedback, forming closed-loop autonomous systems. Such progress is expected to drive seed research toward greater intelligence, precision, and automation.

Core scientific issues to be addressed in AI-driven seed research

The primary objective of AI-driven seed design research is to overcome fundamental bottlenecks in traditional breeding and agricultural production. By integrating cutting-edge interdisciplinary technologies, it aims to enable the precise design of crop genetic traits and enhance environmental resilience—effectively transforming seeds into programmable biological systems and ushering in a new era of intelligent agriculture. Based on the AI-driven seed design paradigm, several core scientific issues must be addressed in future seed science.

Reconstructing the digital representation of the complex seed life cycle

Seeds are the most fundamental “resource repositories” and “gene banks” for crop improvement. More than 1.8 million crop germplasm resources are preserved worldwide, yet the effective utilization rate of elite alleles remains below 10% (Fu et al., 2025). Accurate phenotyping is essential for deciphering the intrinsic dynamics of the seed life cycle and unraveling the regulatory mechanisms underlying seed development (Liu, 2024). To integrate phenomic data with genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic datasets, it is necessary to develop advanced technologies, including automated sensors, robotics, and high-throughput phenotyping platforms. These approaches enable comprehensive digital profiling of complex biological processes in seeds. Furthermore, seed digital twin systems that integrate multi-source heterogeneous data will generate large-scale phenomics datasets, supporting germplasm characterization and in-depth dissection of genetic mechanisms.

Breaking trade-offs among yield, quality, and stress resistance

Extensive research has identified numerous central-hub genes associated with yield, quality, and stress tolerance across different crops. Unfortunately, many of these genes are connected to multiple regulatory networks and exhibit a “more is not better” effect, whereby ectopic expression or knockout often leads to yield penalties or fitness costs (Luo et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Moreover, genome-wide, high-resolution maps of functional variants remain limited in plants. Although GWAS have achieved substantial progress, they often lack sufficient resolution to pinpoint causal single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or insertions/deletions (indels) and fail to provide sufficient molecular information on the potential function of variants, necessitating laborious follow-up analyses (Engelhorn et al., 2025). There is therefore an urgent need for new approaches that can systematically identify functional genomic “switches” with high resolution and quantify the impact of genetic variation on them.

Engineering seeds with intelligent responses to environmental changes

Seeds, as the starting point of the crop life cycle, directly influence yield formation through their sensitivity and adaptability to environmental changes (Chen et al., 2023; Thenveettil et al., 2024). A 2°C climate-warming scenario is expected to further exacerbate average crop losses by 3%–13%, yet few heat-tolerant staple-crop varieties are currently available to meet future food demands (Lou et al., 2025). There is an urgent need to develop “climate-smart” seeds that achieve higher yields under optimal conditions and stable yields under heat stress, although significant breeding bottlenecks remain (Hickey et al., 2019; Reynolds et al., 2021). Rational design of regulatory regions using gene-editing tools to achieve tailored, climate-smart expression patterns could enable the rapid deployment of such genes in breeding, thereby enriching the breeding gene pool and substantially improving breeding efficiency.

Creating novel germplasm resources through synthetic biology

The integration of CRISPR–Cas-based high-precision editing tools with AI-driven biodesign approaches is enabling synthetic biology to systematically engineer crop pangenomes through the creation of standardized bioparts (South et al., 2019). This approach facilitates the development of synthetic genomes and novel germplasm with diverse functionalities, providing technological support to address population growth and climate challenges. However, seed-based synthetic biology still faces several challenges, including the design of functional bioparts, as well as the construction and optimization of biological systems for specific metabolic pathways. Looking ahead, the convergence of standardized libraries of functional genetic elements, modular toolkits, novel environmental sensors, and efficient heterologous metabolic pathway engineering will open sustainable routes for addressing challenges in food quality and security, agricultural production, and energy–environmental systems (Yu et al., 2024).

Progress in addressing these scientific and technological challenges requires the cross-disciplinary integration of biology, information science, and engineering. Such convergence will drive seed science from empirical breeding toward computation-driven precision design, enabling the development of intelligent seed systems and providing innovative solutions for food security and sustainable agriculture.

Integrated technological pathways for AI-driven seed design

Research within the AI-driven seed design paradigm relies on the integration of multiple disciplines, combining cutting-edge technologies such as high-throughput phenotyping, biotechnology, and AI to enable end-to-end processes—from in-depth analysis and precise identification of germplasm resources and multi-omics analysis to seed digital twins, intelligent breeding systems and smart production platforms. Built on omics-scale big data, this effort pioneers an intelligent paradigm characterized by “BT + AI + Robots.” It aims to overcome key technological bottlenecks in seed science and to transform breeding and seed production from experience-driven practices to intelligent design and smart manufacturing. The technical strategies include the following.

Seed phenotyping platforms based on imaging omics

The construction of a robust data perception layer is fundamental to AI-driven seed research, relying on the comprehensive acquisition, precise recording, and efficient management of highly complex biological information. Seed imaging omics, integrating “imaging + AI,” forms the core of this layer, shifting trait phenotyping from a “manual” to an “intelligent” mode (Table 3). This transition generates massive, multi-source, heterogeneous, and time-series datasets that serve as critical inputs for downstream AI models. For example, Germany’s AVATARS project employs high-resolution MRI and X-ray CT technologies to construct 3D models spanning the full life cycle of rapeseed seeds, integrating genotype data with organ-specific transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and epigenetic information into virtual seed models. In China, the Digital Germplasm Bank project utilizes micron-scale 3D modeling and multispectral scanning to digitally reconstruct hundreds of thousands of germplasm resources, enabling their preservation in both physical and digital forms. Powered by these rich perceptual datasets, AI-driven image recognition and DL are propelling seed science toward a “what you see is what you get” paradigm. Previously invisible, immeasurable, and uncontrollable biological traits are now being transformed into quantifiable, integratable, and predictable digital assets. Furthermore, these technologies are improving the classification of seed resources, quality prediction, and health monitoring, providing a precise basis for optimized preservation and supporting a shift from extensive to precision-based collection.

Construction of seed large models: The brain of intelligent seeds

Seed large models function as the “brain” of intelligent seeds, responsible for understanding requirements, analyzing data, providing decision-making guidance, and generating interactive outputs. The development of core computational models for AI-driven seed design fundamentally relies on the effective integration of multimodal data. In this context, a UR refers to a framework that constructs a unified latent representation space. Its core objective is to align and integrate heterogeneous data from diverse modalities—including genomics (sequences), phenomics (images, spectral data, and environmental sensor readings), and physicochemical profiles (metabolomics and proteomics)—thereby enabling the discovery of cross-modal associations and underlying biological mechanisms. The technological trajectory for integrating seed multimodal data has evolved significantly from early-stage simple feature concatenation to DL-based multi-modal alignment (e.g., contrastive learning approaches that bring paired data from different modalities closer in embedding space), and more recently to sophisticated fusion architectures leveraging transformers. These advanced architectures, including transformers, CNNs, diffusion models, and graph neural networks (GNNs), are increasingly employed to process and integrate multimodal, multi-scale biological data (Bunne et al., 2024). Currently, the use of large models to integrate multi-source data has led to a parallel ecosystem comprising specialized seed models (such as SeedLLM for rice; Wild Bioscience) (Yang et al., 2025; https://www.wildbioscience.com/) and general open-source models (such as “Sinong”) (https://modelscope.cn/models/NAULLM/Sinong1.0-32B). It is important to acknowledge that although UR and related multimodal AI approaches have achieved breakthroughs in fields such as computer vision and natural language processing, the development of a truly end-to-end, interpretable, and widely generalizable UR framework remains at an early stage in seed science. Most current applications are still limited to specific, narrow tasks such as seed vigor prediction based on imaging and spectral data.

Construction of the seed digital twin system

The seed digital twin system constructs a high-fidelity digital replica of physical seeds in virtual space through multidimensional data integration and dynamic modeling. Unlike traditional statistical or empirical models, the digital twin system offers dynamic, data-driven, and real-time simulation, functioning as a precise “virtual clone” of the seed. For example, AVATARS relies on high-throughput seed phenotyping platforms (e.g., HIS and X-ray CT) and IoT sensors to collect real-time data on seed morphology, physiological and biochemical traits, and environmental interactions (Rolletschek et al., 2015). Through the integration of multi-omics data and mechanistic models, such as seed dormancy–germination regulatory networks, the system employs 3D geometric modeling to simulate dynamic internal processes, including water transport and nutrient metabolism. Furthermore, by incorporating AI algorithms (e.g., LSTM and transformer), predictive models can be developed to enable forward simulation and optimized decision-making for seed vigor, germination, and stress resistance (Liu et al., 2017; Herrero-Huerta et al., 2021; Kikukawa et al., 2023). These efforts have successfully digitized certain seed states and enabled offline simulations; however, they largely lack the capability for real-time, closed-loop, bidirectional interaction and predictive optimization with physical systems (Lucido et al., 2025). The integration of large AI models as central cognitive units with digital twins as their real-time, data-driven counterparts is poised to enable a new generation of autonomous systems. Such systems will possess integrated capabilities spanning environmental perception, data analysis, strategic decision-making, and action execution.

Genome editing modules: Precise programming of seed genomes

Genome-editing technologies have initiated a revolution in the precise programming of seed genomes. CRISPR and its derivatives use short programmable RNAs to guide DNA-cutting enzymes, such as Cas9, to specific genomic loci (Eisenstein, 2024). In 2025, Wang et al. integrated AlphaFold-based protein structure prediction with CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing to modify the soybean GmSWEET10b gene and successfully generated new soybean lines (sw10b-ho-1 and sw10b-ho-2) with significantly increased oil content under field conditions (Wang et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c, 2025d). Currently, global agricultural biotechnology companies are actively advancing seed innovation to enhance productivity and environmental adaptability, such as SEEDesign (https://inari.com/) and CPGET (https://www.cibus.com). In addition, PrimeRoot, a method based on prime editing, enables the targeted insertion of DNA fragments of up to 20 kb in rice and maize, further advancing CRISPR-based plant genome engineering (Sun et al., 2024).

Synthetic biology modules: Constructing “artificial life components” in seeds

Beyond precise genome editing, the creation of artificial life components within seeds has emerged as a frontier in AI-driven seed research. If genome editing represents the “modification” of seeds, synthetic biology aims at the “creation” of new seeds. Synthetic biology plays a pivotal role in improving seed traits and enhancing bioproduction by applying engineering principles through iterative “design–build–test–learn” cycles, ultimately improving bioproduction based on input genetic circuits (DNA, RNA, and proteins) (Zhang et al., 2025). The use of synthetic metabolic engineering strategies for biofortification to increase the nutrient content of crop seeds is attracting increasing research attention. Through synthetic biology, “Golden Rice” (enriched in β-carotene), riboflavin-rich rice, and astaxanthin-fortified rice have been developed using rice seeds as the biological chassis (Ye et al., 2000; Paine et al., 2005). Shi et al. further demonstrated the potential of crop seeds as platforms for melatonin biosynthesis by engineering multiple BUFFER genetic circuits using synthetic transcriptional regulators (Shi et al., 2025).

SynBio-based breeding typically involves five main steps. First, the mechanistic basis—including genetic and epigenetic regulatory mechanisms, as well as protein functions—must be elucidated. Next, AI is used to predict genetic targets and estimate enzyme activities to design the desired pathways. Third, genetic circuits are synthesized or optimized, including improving enzyme specificity, reconstructing/engineering biosynthetic pathways, and applying directed evolution. The fourth step involves genome editing, including modifying genes associated with agronomic traits and introducing novel regulatory elements or modification sites. AI-driven tools such as CRISPR-GPT have the potential to significantly assist in this step (Huang et al., 2023). The final step is chassis engineering, which mainly involves synthesizing organelles/cells, (re)introducing novel pathways into the chassis, and enhancing or reengineering the microbiome.

Embodied intelligence: Serving as a bridge between “perception” and “action”

In AI-driven seed design, embodied intelligence serves as a bridge between “perception” and “action,” enabling the system not only to understand seeds but also to actively modify seeds and their environmental responses through prediction and planning. As an “actor,” embodied intelligence—integrated with seed large models, genome editing, and synthetic biology—collectively forms a closed-loop, automated, and intelligent “R&D–creation–testing” system for seed science. In 2025, the introduction of “GEAIR” and the breeding robots it represents exemplifies embodied intelligence, in which robotic platforms leverage AI (e.g., visual recognition and decision-making algorithms) to advance cutting-edge biotechnology (Xie et al., 2025).

The six major technological strategies described above are interconnected and co-evolving, collectively forming an integrated technical system that spans from observational characterization to functional reconstruction. Within this framework, digital phenotyping provides the data foundation, AI algorithms offer computational support, and the integration of large models with digital twins serves as the intelligent brain. Meanwhile, embodied intelligence, combined with gene editing and synthetic biology, enables functional innovation through SeedAgent. Together, these elements constitute a forward-looking technological system for intelligent seed development (Figure 3).

Challenges and future development recommendations in AI-driven seed design

As an emerging cross-disciplinary research paradigm, AI-driven seed design presents significant opportunities alongside considerable challenges. Achieving a paradigm shift from traditional breeding to intelligent design requires not only a deep understanding of current scientific and technological bottlenecks but also the systematic planning of future development pathways. Identifying key issues and strategically deploying technological innovations will drive advances in intelligent seed research and support food security and sustainable agriculture.

Key challenges

The core value of AI-driven seed design lies in transforming breeding from a “trial-and-error” empirical practice into a “computable” design science. However, its development must overcome bottlenecks spanning data, algorithms, validation, ethics, and industrial application.

Standardization and integration of multi-phenomic data

The acquisition of seed phenotypic data faces significant fragmentation, with a lack of uniform standards across research teams in phenotyping equipment, measurement protocols, and data formats (Zavafer et al., 2023). High-throughput phenotyping platforms generate multi-scale, multi-modal datasets, creating technical challenges in spatiotemporal alignment and integrated analysis. Furthermore, robust mechanisms for quality control and reproducibility validation of phenotypic data have yet to be established. Data sharing and integration across platforms and laboratories face both technical and administrative barriers (Raubach et al., 2022). These challenges severely constrain the systematic understanding and precise characterization of the seed life cycle.

Difficulties in analyzing genotype–environment interactions

Seed phenotypic traits are governed by complex genotype–environment interactions involving multi-layered regulatory networks. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying G×E interactions remain poorly understood; the dynamic effects of environmental variables on seed development are difficult to quantify accurately; and the role of epigenetic regulation in environmental adaptation has yet to be fully elucidated. These challenges are further exacerbated under climate change, where predicting seed performance under multiple concurrent stresses becomes increasingly difficult (Gallusci et al., 2023; Boye et al., 2024). The lack of deeper understanding of these complex interactions constrains our ability to design smart seeds with enhanced environmental adaptability (Zandalinas and Mittler, 2022).

Biological interpretability of AI models

Although AI models have substantially improved predictive accuracy, their biological interpretability remains limited. They struggle to integrate prior biological knowledge into model design, and the “black box” nature of DL undermines their credibility. The handling of high-dimensional, sparse biological data also poses challenges for model generalization. Moreover, standardized frameworks for model evaluation and biological validation are lacking. These limitations restrict the deeper application of AI in seed research (Capobianco, 2022).

Precision control in genome editing and synthetic biology

Genome editing and synthetic biology technologies still face significant challenges, including low efficiency in multi-gene improvement for complex traits, incomplete strategies for metabolic network optimization, insufficient control of off-target effects, and difficulties in assessing the physiological impacts of genetic modifications (Li et al., 2021). There is also a lack of standardized biopart libraries and modular design platforms, and the development and application of novel editing tools encounter technical bottlenecks. These gaps limit the application of genome editing and synthetic biology in intelligent seed development (Buecherl and Myers, 2022).

Construction of digital modeling systems

The construction of digital seed models remains challenging. Accurate mapping between physical and digital systems is still immature; real-time synchronization of heterogeneous data is difficult; predictive models often deviate from real-world outcomes; and methods for modeling complex biological processes remain inadequate (Wu et al., 2021). In addition, computational efficiency and stability must be improved, and issues of standardization and interoperability of digital twin platforms remain unresolved.

Future perspective

To address these challenges, AI-driven seed design requires integrated advancements in data standardization, sensing technologies, and AI interpretability.

Establishing a unified omics data standardization framework

High-quality, standardized datasets form the foundation of SeedAgent research. A framework based on “three unifications, two platforms, and one system” is recommended: unified data acquisition standards to regulate phenotyping equipment and procedures; unified format specifications to establish common data models; and unified quality control to implement validation mechanisms. This framework should be supported by phenotypic data management and cross-institutional sharing platforms. Two key platforms should be constructed: a phenotypic data management platform for data storage, cleaning, and annotation, and a data sharing platform to enable cross-institutional interoperability. These efforts should culminate in a standardized technical system encompassing phenomic, genomic, metabolomic, and other multi-omics data. Current initiatives, such as the EU’s “Digital Europe Programme” (https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/activities/digital-programme) and China’s “Digital Germplasm Bank” (https://www.caas.cn/xwzx/nkyw/07fff2648acf4eef831cd9a73f27d76d.htm), provide valuable references for global standardization.

Advancing seed–environment sensing and modeling technologies

To address challenges in deciphering seed–environment interaction mechanisms, a technical framework based on “multi-dimensional sensing, deep modeling, and systematic validation” is proposed (Lakhera and Bhandari, 2025). This involves developing novel biosensors and environmental monitoring systems to enable accurate measurement of key physiological indicators in seeds and environmental variables across growth stages. DL-based predictive models for environmental response should be established to integrate multi-level omics data and unravel gene-by-environment interaction networks. Furthermore, standardized environmental simulation platforms are needed to support phenotypic validation under diverse conditions. For example, next-generation IoT technologies can be deployed for dynamic monitoring of environmental variables; graph neural networks can be used to construct multi-level biological network models; and digital twin technology may be employed to simulate responses to environmental stresses. Advances in these areas will significantly enhance our understanding of complex seed–environment interactions (Zhang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c, 2025d).

Developing interpretable biological AI frameworks

To tackle the issue of insufficient biological interpretability of AI models, we propose a computational framework that integrates knowledge-driven, data-driven, and experimental validation approaches. This framework involves encoding existing biological knowledge as prior constraints to guide the design of DL models; developing specialized algorithms tailored to the characteristics of biological big data to improve performance on high-dimensional, sparse datasets; and establishing biological validation systems for model predictions to support the transition from statistical correlation to causal inference (Wysocka et al., 2023; Rodriguez-Mier et al., 2025). For instance, knowledge graph technologies can be employed to integrate biological principles into biologically constrained neural network architectures, while multi-omics data can be used to experimentally validate model predictions (Yan et al., 2024). Such a framework is expected to substantially enhance the interpretability and reliability of AI models, thereby promoting deeper integration of artificial intelligence in smart seed research.

Creating intelligent synthetic biology toolkits

To overcome the difficulties in achieving precise control with synthetic biology tools, we propose developing an integrated technical system comprising a standardized parts library, an AI-enabled design platform, and precision editing tools (Bao et al., 2021). This system should include: (1) a standardized library of functionally validated biological parts to enable modular and predictable design; (2) an AI-powered platform for optimizing metabolic networks and designing genetic circuits, thereby improving the efficiency of multi-gene trait engineering; and (3) advanced precision-editing tools to minimize off-target effects. For example, ML can be applied to optimize the selection of gene-editing targets; synthetic biology approaches can be used to construct intelligent, response-regulating components; and systematic phenotypic evaluation frameworks should be established (Koblan et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). Advances in these areas will significantly enhance the effectiveness of synthetic biology in developing smart seeds.

Building holistic seed digital twin systems

To overcome the challenges in constructing digital twin systems, we propose the development of a holistic framework centered on data integration, model coupling, and dynamic optimization. This system should achieve real-time synchronization and fusion of multi-source, heterogeneous data to enable precise mapping between physical and digital entities; establish a multi-scale, multi-level coupled modeling architecture to improve the simulation accuracy of complex biological processes; and implement feedback-driven dynamic optimization mechanisms to continuously enhance predictive performance. For example, blockchain technology could be employed to ensure trustworthy data transmission, federated learning could enable secure data sharing across platforms, and reinforcement learning could be used to optimize decision-making strategies (Kim and Heo, 2024). Such a comprehensive digital twin system would provide robust technical support for the development and application of smart seeds.

The future is already unfolding: driven by the Breeding 5.0 frameworks, AI-driven seed design is poised for rapid advancement (Fu et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). On one front, the development of seed digital twin systems and large models will enable in-depth analysis and dynamic prediction of genotype–phenotype–environment interactions and genetic regulatory networks. On the other, modular tools such as genome editing and synthetic biology, combined with embodied agents, will be used to design intelligent regulatory circuits capable of responding to biotic and abiotic stresses, thereby creating adaptive “living sensor” seeds. Ultimately, AI-driven seed design systems will function as core nodes within agricultural ecosystems. Through integrated sensing, decision-making, and execution, they will provide fundamental technological support for adaptation to global climate change and ensuring food security.
