Nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategies in lung cancer: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Guoxiang Hu, Binu Koirala, Lufei Young, Cailing Liang, Wei Zhou, Qi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDyspnoea is a common and distressing symptom in lung cancer patients. While various dyspnoea-relief strategies show promise, their comparative effectiveness remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials on nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategies in lung cancer patients, using studies published until 12 June 2025 from the databases PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. The primary outcome was dyspnoea score post-intervention.Results4138 participants from 46 randomised controlled trials were included in the analysis. Among 10 nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategies in lung cancer patients, acupressure (surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) 98.7%, Hedges’ g −1.96, 95% CI −2.92 to −0.99) demonstrated the highest efficacy, followed by fan (SUCRA 83.7%, Hedges’ g −1.11, 95% CI −1.72 to −0.50), and multicomponent interventions (SUCRA 80.7%, Hedges’ g −1.01, 95% CI −1.49 to −0.52). In post-operative lung cancer patients, multicomponent interventions (SUCRA 97.4%, Hedges’ g −1.55, 95% CI −2.16 to −0.94) were the most effective, followed by cognitive behavioural therapy (SUCRA 74.8%, Hedges’ g −0.90, 95% CI −1.75 to −0.05). For patients with pre-existing dyspnoea, acupressure (SUCRA 90.9%, Hedges’ g −1.72, 95% CI −3.01 to −0.42) and fan therapy (SUCRA 77.3%, Hedges’ g −1.18, 95% CI −2.10 to −0.27) were the top interventions.ConclusionsAcupressure is the primary strategy for alleviating dyspnoea in lung cancer patients, including those with pre-existing dyspnoea, followed by fan therapy, while multicomponent interventions are recommended for post-operative patients.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths worldwide in 2022 [1]. Dyspnoea, a common and distressing symptom throughout the disease, affects 81.2% of early-stage and 79.8% of advanced-stage lung cancer patients [2]. Nearly 70% of patients are still experiencing dyspnoea after lobectomy [3]. According to the American Thoracic Society, dyspnoea arises from complex physiological processes, where signals from pulmonary and peripheral receptors are perceived as breathing discomfort [4]. In lung cancer, the disease and its treatments directly impair respiratory function, including lung fibrosis, restricted lung volume, and reduced gas exchange [5]. Despite its prevalence, dyspnoea is often underestimated and poorly managed.

Multiple nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategies have been proposed in lung cancer patients. Pharmacological approaches can provide short-term benefit, but are often limited by adverse effects with sustained use [6]. Increasing attention has therefore turned to dyspnoea relief such as fan therapy, breathing techniques, exercise and patient education. Evidence suggests these interventions can effectively alleviate dyspnoea in cancer populations [7], and clinical guidelines from the European Society for Medical Oncology [8] and the American Society of Clinical Oncology [9] recommend them as first-line management. However, these guidelines provide little clarity on which strategies are most effective or how they should be prioritised in clinical practice.

Previous network meta-analyses have tried to evaluate dyspnoea interventions in cancer patients, but most have included mixed cancer types, limiting the generalisability of findings to lung cancer [10]. Additionally, a critical gap in the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery guidelines for pulmonary surgery is the lack of guidance on managing post-operative dyspnoea [11]. Recent meta-analysis and clinical trials suggest that breathing techniques and exercises are effective in improving lung function and reducing dyspnoea symptoms in post-operative lung cancer patients [12, 13]. Therefore, a network meta-analysis is crucial to establish the comparative efficacy of these various interventions. Moreover, the severity of pre-existing dyspnoea at baseline has been identified as an independent predictor of survival, suggesting that treatment effects may vary based on symptom severity [14, 15].

Therefore, the objective of this study was to conduct a systematic review and network meta-analysis to compare and rank the effectiveness of nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategies specifically in lung cancer patients. To enhance clinical relevance, subgroup analyses were conducted for post-operative patients and for those with pre-existing dyspnoea, aiming to inform tailored and evidence-informed symptom management strategies.

Methods

This study was registered on PROSPERO (www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO identifier number CRD42024609495). Our review report followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Network Meta-Analysis checklist [16].

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review and network meta-analysis searched literature from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, ClinicalTrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform from inception to 12 June 2025, without language restrictions. The search strategy utilised a combination of Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms. Representative terms included “lung neoplasms” “pulmonary cancer” and “lung tumor” for the population, and “dyspnea”, “shortness of breath” and relevant assessment scales (e.g. Borg Dyspnoea Scale) for the outcomes, alongside standard randomised controlled trial (RCT) filters. In addition, reference lists of relevant articles were screened manually. The full search strategy is detailed in the supplementary material.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: 1) adults (aged ≥18 years) diagnosed with lung cancer; 2) received nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategies (e.g. exercise, breathing techniques, education, fan, acupressure, acupuncture, mind–body therapy, or multicomponent interventions); 3) compared with usual care or another strategy; 4) reported dyspnoea using a validated questionnaire; and 5) used an RCT design. Exclusion criteria were studies with mixed cancer types; comparisons of different doses or delivery methods of the same strategy; and abstracts, protocols or letters.

Data collection

All records were managed using EndNote X9. After removing duplicates, two reviewers (G.X. Hu and C.L. Liang) screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text screening. Discrepancies during full-text review were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (Q. Zhang) if needed.

Two reviewers (G.X. Hu and W. Zhou) independently extracted data using a standardised form. Extracted information included study details, participant characteristics, intervention and control information, outcomes, and adverse events. Dyspnoea scores (mean±sd) at baseline and first follow-up were collected after the intervention. For crossover trials, post-washout data were used. If data were unavailable, we contacted authors, calculated estimates using standard formulas [17], or extracted values from graphs via GetData Graph Digitizer (v2.24). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (Q. Zhang).

Following relevant guidelines [8, 9], interventions were classified into 10 types of dyspnoea-relief strategies, including fan, exercise, breathing techniques, exercise-breathing techniques, education, acupressure, mind–body therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), multicomponent therapy, and acupuncture. Definitions are provided in supplementary table S1.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Study quality was assessed using Version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2) [18]. Two reviewers (G.X. Hu and C.L. Liang) independently assessed six domains; disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (Q. Zhang).

Data analysis

We used Hedges’ g to calculate standardised mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals to compare interventions. Based on Cohen's guidelines, values of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 indicate small, moderate and large effects, respectively. Significance was set at p<0.05. We used Review Manager 5.4 for pairwise meta-analyses with a random-effects model to compare interventions, to directly compare the treatment effects of any two dyspnoea-relief strategies. Heterogeneity was assessed with the I2 statistic: <40% (low), 30–60% (moderate), 50–90% (substantial) and 75–100% (considerable) [19].

We performed a frequentist random-effects network meta-analysis using STATA 18.0 to synthesise direct and indirect evidence. A network geometry plot visualised intervention comparison, with line thickness representing study numbers and node width indicating sample size. Heterogeneity was assessed using tau-squared (τ2). Global consistency was assessed via inconsistency models, and local inconsistency through node-splitting and loop-specific analyses [20]. Subgroup analyses were stratified by post-operative lung cancer patients and those with baseline dyspnoea. We used surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) scores (0–1) to rank interventions [21], with higher values indicating greater effectiveness in relieving dyspnoea. Funnel plots and Egger's test assessed publication bias using R 4.4.2.

To assess robustness, we conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding trials with high risk in one or more key domains and recalculating effect sizes [19]. Differences in estimates and confidence interval overlap were used to evaluate the impact.

Certainty assessment

We evaluated evidence certainty using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) tool, assessing six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence [22]. Each pairwise comparison was classified into one of four tiers: high, moderate, low or very low, based on established criteria.

Results

8029 potentially eligible records were identified; 4509 were screened after duplicate removal. Out of 107 full-text articles assessed, including five additional trials from reference lists, 61 were excluded due to non-RCT design, ineligible populations or abstract-only publications (supplementary table S2). Finally, 46 RCTs were included in the review and network meta-analysis (figure 1) [23–68].

Characteristics of the included studies

Included studies were published between 1996 and 2025, involved 4138 participants across 15 countries (table 1). Most were two-arm RCTs (n=40), with six using three-arm designs [37, 46, 48, 49, 58, 59]. In two three-arm trials, placebo [58] and combined intervention [46] arms were excluded to maintain network relevance and statistical power. 36 were single-centre and 10 were multicentre trials. Parallel designs predominated, with one crossover trial [55]. Data sources included 43 journal articles, one clinical trial registry [33], one preprint [62], and one thesis [47]. 40 studies were published in English, one in Korean [41], and one in Chinese [28]. Supplementary data for four studies were obtained via email from authors [23, 34, 40, 50]. 13 (28.3%) trials targeted post-operative dyspnoea, and 18 (39.1%) included patients with baseline dyspnoea.

Intervention duration, frequency, and dosage varied across trials. Fan sessions lasted 5 min twice daily, with total durations from 10 min [55] to 5 days [26] or 14 days [42]. Acupressure interventions lasted between 3 days [58] and 4 weeks [32]. Exercise interventions included 30–60 min supervised or home-based sessions over 4–16 weeks [50, 59]. The nurse-led multicomponent follow-up was the longest, delivered three times per month for 12 months [53]. Full details are in supplementary table S3.

Quality of the included studies

Out of the 46 trials, 17 (37.0%) were rated as high overall risk of bias (supplementary table S4). Specific concerns included the randomisation process, with three trials showing inadequate sequence generation or allocation concealment, leading to baseline imbalances [27, 39, 44]. One trial had substantial crossover between groups, resulting in a high risk for deviations from intended interventions [38]. 10 trials were high risk for missing outcome data due to substantial, unaddressed loss to follow-up [24, 30, 31, 36, 40, 42, 52, 53, 57, 67]. Additionally, four trials were high risk of selective reporting, as negative outcomes were not fully reported [29, 36, 50, 58].

Assessment of inconsistency

Between-study heterogeneity was τ2=0.17. Global inconsistency was nonsignificant (Chi-squared=7.02, p=0.72) via the design-by-treatment interaction model. Node-splitting identified significant inconsistency in only one out of 17 comparisons (usual care versus education, p=0.048), indicating good overall network consistency. Loop-specific assessment of all seven closed loops showed no significant inconsistency (supplementary tables S5 and S6), supporting the use of a consistency model.

Publication bias

Egger's test showed no significant asymmetry (p=0.26), and the funnel plot (supplementary figure S1) indicated low publication bias.

Pairwise meta-analysis

Pairwise meta-analyses (supplementary figure S2) showed significant dyspnoea reductions versus controls for several interventions: exercise-breathing techniques (SMD −0.32, 95% CI −0.54 to −0.10), breathing techniques (SMD −0.36, 95% CI −0.54 to −0.18), CBT (SMD −0.65, 95% CI −0.92 to −0.39), education (SMD −0.45, 95% CI −0.70 to −0.21), mind–body therapy (SMD −0.59, 95% CI −0.99 to −0.19) and multicomponent interventions (SMD −1.20, 95% CI −2.04 to −0.35).

Dyspnoea-relief strategies in lung cancer patients

As shown in figure 3a, eight strategies significantly improved dyspnoea compared to usual care: acupressure (Hedges’ g −1.96, 95% CI −2.92 to −0.99), fan (Hedges’ g −1·11, 95% CI −1.72 to −0.50), multicomponent interventions (Hedges’ g −1.01, 95% CI −1.49 to −0.52), CBT (Hedges’ g −0.63, 95% CI −1.11 to −0.14), education (Hedges’ g −0.63, 95% CI −0.97 to −0.29), mind–body therapy (Hedges’ g −0.49, 95% CI −0.87 to −0.12), breathing techniques (Hedges’ g −0.40, 95% CI −0.73 to −0.08), and exercise-breathing techniques (Hedges’ g −0.37, 95% CI −0.69 to −0.05). Exercise and acupuncture showed no significant effect. The league table of pairwise comparisons is provided in supplementary table S7. SUCRA rankings (figure 4a) identified acupressure (98.7%) as most effective, followed by fan (83.7%) and multicomponent interventions (80.7%). Exercise ranked lowest (SUCRA, 18.6%).

Dyspnoea-relief strategies in post-operative lung cancer patients

We analysed 13 trials on post-operative lung cancer patients, depicting the impact of seven dyspnoea-relief strategies and routine care on dyspnoea (figure 2b). Multicomponent interventions (Hedges’ g −1.55, 95% CI −2.16 to −0.94), CBT (Hedges’ g −0.90, 95% CI −1.75 to −0.05) and breathing techniques (Hedges’ g −0.43, 95% CI −0.84 to −0.03) significantly improved dyspnoea (figure 3b, supplementary table S8). SUCRA rankings (figure 4b) identified multicomponent interventions (SUCRA, 97.4%) as the most effective, followed by CBT (74.8%) and education (58.3%).

Dyspnoea-relief strategies in lung cancer patients with pre-existing dyspnoea

We conducted subgroup analyses on lung cancer patients with pre-existing dyspnoea, involving seven strategies and routine care across 18 trials (figure 2c). Only acupressure (Hedges’ g −1.72, 95% CI −3.01 to −0.42), fan (Hedges’ g −1.18, 95% CI −2.10 to −0.27) and education (Hedges’ g −0.57, 95% CI −1.13 to −0.01) showed significant symptomatic relief (figure 3c, supplementary table S9). SUCRA rankings (figure 4c) identified acupressure (90.9%) as most effective, followed by fan (77.3%) and multicomponent interventions (56.2%). The ranking of interventions in this subgroup aligned with the overall population, indicating that baseline dyspnoea did not impact therapeutic priority.

Sensitivity analysis

After identifying 17 studies at high risk of bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess their impact on primary findings. The results showed no significant change in strategy effects after excluding these studies (figures 2d, 3d and 4d, supplementary table S10).

Certainty of evidence

The CINeMA assessment showed low confidence for most network comparisons due to within-study bias, heterogeneity and imprecision, except for 1) direct acupressure versus usual care (moderate confidence), and 2) indirect comparisons of acupressure versus acupuncture, breathing techniques, exercise-breathing techniques, and exercise (all moderate confidence) (supplementary table S11).

Adverse events

Safety reporting was limited. Of the 46 trials, only 14 (30.4%) provided adverse event data. Among these, eight trials reported no intervention-related events, indicating a generally favourable safety profile, while six trials documented mild to moderate intervention-related events. Reported events included leg muscle soreness (four patients) and musculoskeletal complaints (four patients) from exercise-based interventions [31, 50]; fatigue, chest soreness (one patient), headache, confusion (four patients) and one readmission from resistance inspiratory muscle training (two trials) [25, 51]; localised sensitivity and pain from acupressure (two patients) [32], and tape allergies from acupuncture (two patients) [68].

Discussion

This network meta-analysis is the first to compare nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategies in lung cancer. Among 46 RCTs, acupressure emerged as the highest ranked intervention for overall efficacy, suggesting that it may substantially reduce dyspnoea compared with other strategies. Multicomponent interventions appeared most effective post-operatively, while acupressure and fan therapy were potentially superior for patients with pre-existing dyspnoea at baseline. These preliminary findings may inform tailored, evidence-based strategies for managing dyspnoea in lung cancer patients. However, they are driven by a limited number of RCTs and heterogeneity, necessitating cautious interpretation and further validation.

Acupressure, a noninvasive traditional Chinese medicine therapy, may relieve dyspnoea through neurophysiological and anti-inflammatory mechanisms [69]. Targeted stimulation of lung-related acupoints (e.g. BL13, RN17, LU1) may activate the peripheral sympathetic nervous system, relax bronchial smooth muscle and reduce airway resistance [70]. Additionally, stimulation via the vagus nerve may trigger neuroimmune regulation, decreasing inflammatory mediators such as C-reactive protein, tumour necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-10 [71, 72]. However, as only two acupressure trials were included in our review, the conclusion should be interpreted with caution. Doğan and Taşcı [32] reported that applying acupressure to LU-1, LU-10 and p-6 twice daily for 4 weeks significantly relieved dyspnoea. However, the study by Strong et al. [58] involved only four participants over a 3-day intervention using Vaccaria segetalis seeds on the auricular points ShenMen, Kidney and Lower Lung. While findings suggest potential benefits, more high-quality RCTs are needed to confirm efficacy and determine optimal acupoint selection, duration and dosage.

Subgroup analysis showed multicomponent interventions appeared most effective for post-operative dyspnoea in lung cancer. Although based on only two RCTs, both strategies consistently integrated three core elements: physiological training, psychological support and education [62, 64]. This aligns with evidence supporting multimodal prehabilitation in lung cancer for improving functional outcomes [73]. A recent RCT confirmed that multicomponent interventions, pre- or post-operatively, improve 6-min walk distance and lung function post-lobectomy [74]. However, as both RCTs focused on the post-operative phase, evidence remains insufficient to determine optimal intervention timing. Future dual-arm RCTs with controlled confounding factors and comprehensive outcome measures are needed to inform timing decisions. While promising, multicomponent interventions face challenges in routine practice due to the need for coordination, patient engagement, and resource allocation, especially in resource-limited settings like outpatient clinics.

Fan therapy ranked second in effectiveness for relieving dyspnoea in lung cancer patients, showing consistent benefits even in those symptomatic at baseline. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, fan therapy, typically delivered via a fan, probably stimulates cold-sensitive receptors in the facial skin or upper airway mucosa innervated by the trigeminal nerve, which may modulate respiratory centre activity and reduce dyspnoea perception [75]. An RCT by Kako et al. [76] found that directing fan to the facial area innervated by the trigeminal nerve reduced dyspnoea scores by 1.35 points compared to fan to the legs. A prior systematic review confirmed the immediate effectiveness of fan in relieving dyspnoea [77]. While evidence for sustained benefit is limited (maximum intervention duration: 14 days) [42], fan is safe, low-cost, noninvasive, and fast-acting, making it ideal for acute relief in low-resource settings like home palliative care or emergency departments.

Clinical implications

Effective dyspnoea management is essential in the care of lung cancer patients. The limited evidence from this network meta-analysis indicates acupressure could be a promising nonpharmacological strategy, particularly due to its noninvasive and low-cost nature [78]. However, the current evidence base is too limited and uncertain to draw definitive conclusions. Given its practical advantages, acupressure merits further investigation in robust, well-designed RCTs. Future trials should establish standardised protocols for acupoint localisation and technique to ensure safety and reproducibility. Multicomponent interventions, including physical training, psychological support and patient education, were found to be highly effective, particularly for post-operative dyspnoea. These should be coordinated within an interdisciplinary framework to ensure continuity and comprehensiveness of care. Fan therapy offers rapid and low-cost symptomatic relief and represents a key noninvasive option [75], especially in patients with pre-existing dyspnoea. It can be readily incorporated in various clinical settings, including palliative care, home-based management, and emergency departments for immediate alleviation of dyspnoea. Patients and caregivers can be easily educated to use simple tools such as handheld fans or portable airflow devices effectively with minimal instruction. The safety, accessibility, and ongoing investigation into its mechanisms support its adoption as a frontline dyspnoea-relief strategy.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the intervention rankings in this network meta-analysis are based on the best available post-intervention data. Given the variability and often short follow-up durations, firm conclusions regarding sustained effects cannot be drawn. Future studies with long-term outcomes are needed to assess intervention durability. Second, sparse evidence for key interventions, such as acupressure and fan therapy, resulted in fragile network geometry, meaning that observed effect estimates and treatment rankings should be interpreted with caution. Third, variations in treatment duration, frequency, and dosage across studies may limit the clinical generalisability of our findings. Future studies should clarify dose-response relationships and optimal intervention parameters for different patient populations. Fourth, most comparisons were made against usual care, offering limited insight into the relative effectiveness of active interventions. Future trials should prioritise active-controlled designs, particularly for promising dyspnoea-relief strategies, to strengthen evidence and inform clinical decision-making. Fifth, the credibility of our findings is constrained by the high risk of bias in many included studies, primarily due to incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and methodological shortcomings, resulting in low certainty for most comparisons and underscoring the need for rigorously designed trials. Finally, the limited reporting of adverse events restricts conclusions regarding intervention safety, emphasising the need for future studies to systematically collect and report safety outcomes.

Conclusion

Our systematic review and network meta-analysis shows that acupressure may be among the most effective nonpharmacological dyspnoea-relief strategy in lung cancer patients, followed by fan therapy. Both strategies appeared effective even in patients with pre-existing dyspnoea. Multicomponent interventions showed potential benefit for post-operative dyspnoea. However, due to limited evidence, treatment rankings may lack stability, requiring cautious interpretation. Future large-scale RCTs with longer follow-up times are needed to confirm efficacy, explore underlying mechanisms, and identify optimal multicomponent interventions for specific patient subgroups.
