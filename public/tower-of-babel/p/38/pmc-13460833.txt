Reimagining Perinatal Care in Alabama Through Innovative Partnerships
Authors: Allison Shorten, Elizabeth G Muñoz, Christian Ketel, Candace C Knight, Michele H Talley, Cynthia S Selleck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildbearing families in the United States, especially in rural communities, face inconsistent access to high‐quality maternity care, with many experiencing sparse and fragmented services. In response to long‐standing disparities in Alabama, we created the Women and Children Health Initiative (WACHI), a coordinated hub that advances midwifery workforce growth, expands practice partnerships, and catalyzes policy change to support collaborative, risk‐appropriate care. Central to WACHI is integrating certified nurse‐midwives (CNMs) within systems that have historically imposed practice barriers. Grounded in the belief that Alabama can do better, WACHI convenes academic fields, health systems, public health sectors, and community partners to codesign and implement evidence‐informed interventions. Program pillars include (1) reopening and expanding the state's nurse‐midwifery education pathway, (2) embedding Nurse‐Family Partnership home visiting with integrated behavioral health, (3) launching the Moms and Kids Mobile Health Clinic to reach underserved rural areas, and (4) building durable CNM‐physician collaborations and standardized protocols for team‐based care. These coordinated strategies align workforce development, health care delivery, and policy to increase first‐trimester prenatal entry, improve postpartum follow‐up, and reduce preventable complications. Early results include growth in the CNM workforce, new rural practice partnerships, and health‐system engagement with respectful, team‐based models that prioritize patient preferences and cultural safety. In this article we describe the WACHI model and early outcomes to inform adaptation and support replication by diverse regional coalitions and systems nationwide.Keywords: certified nurse‐midwife, community partnerships, maternal‐infant outcomes, perinatal maternal health, rural health, workforce development

INTRODUCTION

Access to high‐quality perinatal health care is crucial for healthy birth outcomes, yet this is not the reality for numerous women in the United States. In 2024, approximately 5.5 million women in the United States lived in counties with limited or no access to quality perinatal health care. 1 The situation is critical in rural southern states like Alabama, where significant perinatal inequities have existed for decades and continue to result in a decline in access to services. The number of rural counties in Alabama without active labor and delivery units continues to climb. Currently, only 15 of 55 rural Alabama counties have hospitals providing birthing services, leaving women without access to skilled health care providers and needing to travel long distances for perinatal care, important determinants of maternal and infant outcomes. 2 , 3 According to the March of Dimes 2025 report, 19.3% of Alabama women received inadequate prenatal care. 4

Improving the health and well‐being of women, infants, and children remains an important public health goal. Despite this, maternal and infant mortality and pregnancy‐related complications remain significant concerns nationally and in Alabama. The state consistently ranks among the worst in the United States for maternal and infant health outcomes. Despite a recognized need for change and efforts to bridge care gaps, the March of Dimes again awarded Alabama a grade of “F” on its 2025 Report Card. 4 Alabama has the fourth‐highest preterm birth rate (12.7%), and with an infant mortality rate of 7.6 infant deaths per 1,000 births, Alabama is 1 of 8 states with the highest infant mortality rate among all 50 states. 5 These statistics are even worse for Black women and infants in Alabama, with the March of Dimes reporting a Black preterm birth rate of 17.0% and a Black infant mortality rate of 12.1 in its 2025 Report Card. 4 Preterm birth and its complications are a leading cause of infant mortality in the first year, second only to congenital disabilities. 6 Maternal mortality (deaths while pregnant or within 42 days of when the pregnancy ends) increased significantly during the COVID‐19 pandemic, but by 2023, the US ratio of 18.7 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births had almost returned to prepandemic levels. 7 However, states in the south, including Alabama, continue to report significantly higher maternal mortality ratios, likely due in part to the lack of Medicaid expansion and the many rural areas impacted by hospital obstetric unit closures and maternity care deserts. 7 Alabama has the nation's sixth‐highest maternal mortality ratio at 35.1, and 34.3% of its counties qualify as maternity care deserts. 4 , 8 Again, racial and ethnic disparities exist across maternal health outcomes. The state's Maternal Mortality Review Committee, established in 2018, reported a maternal mortality ratio for Black, non‐Hispanic women of 77.0 and found preexisting chronic conditions such as cardiovascular‐related events, as well as substance use, infections, and hemorrhage, to be the leading causes of maternal death. 9

Childbearing families in Alabama continue to languish under outdated, ineffective, and fragmented approaches to maternity care. Further, their social and economic disadvantage creates barriers to overcoming disparities without coordinated assistance. The 2025 United Health Foundation's America's Health Rankings 2025 Health of Women and Children Report indicated that social and economic factors continue to impact women and children, and that glaring disparities exist across states and populations. Alabama, the nation's 7th poorest state, ranked 45th overall among the 50 states in the 2025 report. 10 , 11 The Maternal Vulnerability Index (MVI) is a county‐level tool used to identify where pregnant women in each state are most vulnerable to poor maternal health outcomes. Scores range from 0 to 100 (very low to very high). Alabama is one of 10 states, almost all in the south, with very high MVI scores. 5 In fact, 55 of Alabama's 67 counties (82.1%) have high or very high MVI scores due to their clinical risk factors and other social, contextual, and environmental factors. 4 , 5

Clearly, barriers exist to providing vital comprehensive maternity care to pregnant women and their infants across Alabama. Shortages of health care providers, including inadequate numbers of certified nurse‐midwives (CNMs) in the state, restrictions to the full scope of practice and reimbursement, and social determinants of health all contribute to access gaps and inadequate care. Social determinants of health, defined as the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, as well as their access to power, money, and resources, profoundly influence health, quality of life, and risk across their life span. 12 These nonmedical drivers of health have a major impact on health, well‐being, quality of life, and opportunity to thrive across the life span, including during pregnancy and childbearing. Ideally, according to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, maternal health care should be risk‐appropriate, delivered by a skilled workforce, coordinated or integrated with behavioral, economic, and social supports, accessible and affordable for pregnant women and families, and equitable. 13

Over decades, the perinatal workforce has diminished across Alabama, particularly in rural counties, as providers have either left the state or retired, and hospitals have closed obstetric units. 2 , 3 Within Alabama, the nurse‐midwifery profession has been historically marginalized, with some CNMs leaving the state for more practice‐friendly states where integration of midwifery models of care is supported. Although The University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) School of Nursing (SON) started a nurse‐midwifery program in 1991, it was placed on inactive status in late 1996 due to low enrollment, the high cost of the faculty‐to‐student ratio required, and a lack of physician acceptance. 14 Midwives serve as mainstream health care providers of routine prenatal care in most countries. In the United States, especially in southern states like Alabama, they remain the exception rather than the norm. National organizations such as the March of Dimes have long recommended expanding access to midwifery care. 4 Evidence consistently shows that midwifery models reduce preterm births and primary cesarean births while improving breastfeeding rates and satisfaction with care. 15 , 16 Yet Alabama continues to underutilize nurse‐midwives. In fact, Alabama has the second‐lowest number of CNMs and the second‐lowest percentage of CNM‐attended births among all 50 states. 17

A critical connection exists between the health of women and children and the health of a family, leaving the health of Alabama families at high risk. Despite evident disparities in outcomes and repeated calls for change, few efforts have focused on designing and implementing innovative, evidence‐based, collaborative models of maternity care. Existing approaches rarely address the specific needs of diverse and vulnerable rural communities. New approaches are needed to ensure access to highly effective coordinated models of care delivered across Alabama. This article focuses on perinatal initiatives successfully launched under the Women and Children Health Initiative (WACHI), with the goal of inspiring others committed to improving maternal health to use it as a model for building collective momentum and leveraging resources to achieve greater impact in their states or communities.

STRATEGY

WACHI Structure and Programs

UAB SON has a tripartite mission focused on education, research, and practice, which serves as the foundation for WACHI's growth. The SON leadership felt compelled to focus its expertise and effort on securing a better future for women, children, and families in Alabama. As a result, the school launched WACHI in 2020. It was born out of a desire to make a difference and ensure a future in which women and children in Alabama are not only healthy but also thrive and reach their highest potential. With the help of dedicated community partners, the WACHI model has provided an innovative hub for strategic partnership development, driving successful clinical and education programs that are already improving women's and children's health, safety, and well‐being.

WACHI brings together SON leadership and faculty passionate about maternal and child health and dedicated to improving outcomes for women and children across Alabama (https://www.uab.edu/nursing/home/wachi). The leadership group of approximately 20 members began in 2020, meeting monthly to develop a mission, vision, and strategic priorities focused on (1) building a diverse perinatal workforce; (2) developing, implementing, and sustaining models of care that improve health care access and outcomes for women and children; and (3) securing partnerships and resources to advance the WACHI mission and vision. Monthly meetings create momentum by keeping everyone focused on the strategic plan, reviewing progress on individual and group projects, and offering meaningful, ongoing advice and support to one another. This consistent focus, support, and synergy has resulted in successful, nurse‐led, interprofessional perinatal initiatives, including (1) development and launch of a nurse‐midwifery pathway; (2) expansion of nurse home‐visiting during pregnancy, postpartum, and early childhood (Nurse‐Family Partnership [NFP]) with wrap‐around mental health services; (3) creation of mobile rural health services for women and children (Moms and Kids Mobile Health Clinic [MKMHC]); (4) collaborative partnerships between physicians, nurse‐midwives, and others in multiple regions of the state (Improving Alabama Maternal Outcomes through Multi‐Disciplinary Services [AL‐MOMS]); and (5) funding for additional partnership initiatives focused on improving maternal‐child health (Rural Maternity and Obstetrics Management Strategies [RMOMS] and Rural Maternal Disparities Consortium). Each initiative and project has its own evaluation process and outcome measures, and the entire WACHI group regularly tracks outcomes based on the WACHI strategic plan. INTERVENTIONS

Perinatal Workforce Development

The UAB SON and WACHI leaders have actively worked to increase the perinatal workforce in Alabama. In 2022, the only nurse‐midwifery pathway in the state reopened at the SON after a 26‐year hiatus, completing pre‐accreditation with the Accreditation Commission for Midwifery Education. The nurse‐midwifery pathway has successfully graduated 2 cohorts of midwives (4 and 6 students, respectively) with a third cohort (10 students) graduating in August 2026. A generous $1.1 million donation from Blue Cross Blue Shield of Alabama supports the program's growth. The funding provides scholarships for nurse‐midwifery students who commit to practicing in rural Alabama for 3 years after graduation. The SON Board of Visitors and Junior Board of Visitors also raised funds to support nurse‐midwifery students traveling to rural and remote locations for clinical experience. Leaders across Alabama have secured additional funding to expand and integrate nurse‐midwifery services into health care settings throughout the state. The Transforming Maternal Health Model was awarded to Alabama Medicaid, providing 10 years of funding (up to $16.5 million) to improve maternal health outcomes. 18 Access to midwifery services is central to the Transforming Maternal Health Model. Hospital systems that once sidelined nurse‐midwives are now encouraged to place them at the heart of care—a clear reflection of the growing recognition and support for CNMs across Alabama. While CNM numbers are still small, the number practicing in Alabama has grown from 20 in 2020 to 62 today. 19

Nurse‐Family Partnership and AL‐MOMS

In 2017, in response to state and county infant mortality rates among the nation's worst, the SON launched an NFP program in collaboration with Jefferson County, Birmingham Department of Health, and several local philanthropic organizations. 20 NFP serves low‐income, vulnerable, pregnant women and their children up to 2 years of age. During the 8 years since that initial program launch, many maternal and child health indicators for NFP‐engaged families have improved. In particular, the preterm birth rate of 10.0% for NFP‐engaged families is lower than both state (12.7%) and county (12.9%) levels, and breastfeeding initiation is higher for children of women engaged with NFP 80.5% vs 76.1% for the state of Alabama (C. Knight, PhD, oral communication, November 3, 2025).

With early improvements in outcomes and community acceptance and engagement, the NFP program quickly grew from 4 nurse home visitors (NHVs) in one county to 13 NHVs across 8 counties. In addition, the model incorporates a layered approach that integrates behavioral health services to address the mental health needs of the families served. This approach consists of a psychiatric mental health nurse practitioner for diagnosis and treatment of preexisting and perinatal mood disorders and a licensed independent clinical social worker for therapy. The availability of these services has been highly successful in decreasing depression and anxiety screening scores among pregnant women working with NFP, with 78% of clients showing improved depression screening scores and 75% showing improved anxiety screening scores after treatment. 21

As rural hospital obstetric units continued to close, faculty proposed adding CNMs to the expanding NFP model. This addition aimed to increase access to maternity care, especially in Alabama's rural regions. The model AL‐MOMS was a product of collaboration within WACHI, between the school's NFP and nurse‐midwifery pathway leadership, and in 2023, the SON received an $8.8 million Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) Integrated Maternal Health Services grant to expand the model. There are now 23 NHVs in 18 counties, which will grow to 44 NHVs in 35 of the state's 67 counties by 2028. In addition, 2 CNMs have been added, with 3 more planned in rural areas of the state by 2028.

Moms and Kids Mobile Health Clinic

Given Alabama's need to improve maternal and child health outcomes, in 2025 the SON was awarded almost $2.4 million from UAB's Strategic Investment Fund to launch a 3‐year project entitled “Rural Reach: Mobile Health for Maternal and Child Care” to reach more women and children in 17 vulnerable, rural counties while building and enriching the nursing workforce. The MKMHC was born out of the WACHI strategic development model, and it is partnering with the school's NFP program to center maternal health care across the perinatal continuum, bringing respectful, nurse‐led care to rural communities facing the highest access barriers. Currently, the MKMHC is focused on pediatric services (school physicals and immunizations) and will begin providing maternal health care services in 2026, including prenatal visits; blood pressure, weight, and fetal growth tracking; anemia and gestational diabetes screening; and timely management of hypertensive disorders with clear escalation pathways. The MKMHC will provide postpartum follow‐up within 7 to 14 days, comprehensive 6‐week to 12‐week visits, lactation support, and counseling on birth spacing and contraception (including initiation, when appropriate). Because maternal morbidity rarely occurs in isolation, visits will include screenings for psychosocial needs, perinatal mood and anxiety disorders, substance use, and intimate partner violence, paired with brief interventions and warm handoffs to community partners. The team will emphasize continuity with a registered nurse and CNM dyad that coordinates referrals (eg, ultrasound, high‐risk pregnancies), closes loops on laboratory results and medications, and addresses transportation. Education will be integrated into every encounter, covering topics such as pregnancy warning signs (headache, vision changes, bleeding), safe sleep practices, nutrition, and chronic disease self‐management. The goal is for women to leave each visit informed, empowered, and supported. Data from the prenatal visits will guide outreach, track timely prenatal care, and drive quality improvement. The MKMHC aims to reduce preventable complications, increase prenatal and postpartum visit completion, and narrow rural gaps in maternal health care outcomes by meeting women and families where they are.

Perinatal Consortium and Networking Resources for Rural Reach

Another WACHI outcome is success in securing 2 additional HRSA grants in 2025 to expand risk‐appropriate perinatal care across rural Alabama. First, the RMOMS award ($4 million over 4 years) establishes a rural maternal network serving high‐need Alabama counties, integrating mobile prenatal and postpartum clinics (MKMHC), home visiting (NFP), and telehealth‐enabled care with a shared data backbone to coordinate referrals and monitor outcomes in real time. The model will train and deploy CNMs and family medicine physicians with obstetrics training to initiate referrals to maternal‐fetal medicine for high‐risk pregnancies, embed perinatal behavioral health screening with rapid linkage, and close gaps for geographically isolated patients. RMOMS will also standardize evidence‐based care bundles for hypertensive disorders and diabetes in pregnancy; expand lactation support and contraception counseling; and connect patients to transportation, the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, and social services through community health workers and doulas. A focus on respectful, needs‐responsive maternity care and patient navigation is part of the design to reduce disparities for rural populations, increase early prenatal entry, and improve postpartum follow‐up and continuity.

Second, the HRSA Maternal and Child Health Research Consortium award ($1.2 million over 4 years) builds a Rural Maternal Disparities Consortium with the Alabama Department of Public Health and community partners to coordinate services statewide, beginning in the southwest part of the state and scaling. The Consortium aligns governance, data sharing, and quality improvement across hospitals, federally qualified health centers, and community organizations, using mobile health (MKMHC), tele‐obstetrics, and home visiting through NFP to strengthen care transitions from preconception through one year postpartum. Both projects are designed to work synergistically with hospitals, rural health care providers, and community members to codesign and implement activities that align with local priorities and capacity. This work explicitly drives efforts to improve maternal and infant outcomes in rural Alabama. Key objectives include increasing first‐trimester prenatal care entry, reducing severe maternal morbidity and preventable readmissions, improving timely postpartum blood pressure monitoring, expanding depression and substance use screening with treatment engagement, increasing breastfeeding initiation and continuation rates, and reducing preterm birth and low birthweight rates. Together, these 2 HRSA awards will operationalize a coordinated rural maternal ecosystem that improves access, quality, and continuity, accelerating progress toward closing Alabama's persistent maternal‐infant equity gaps.

DISCUSSION

Under the WACHI vision of a future in which women and children in Alabama are healthy and achieving their highest potential, the outcomes of this collective, coordinated work will be far‐reaching and life‐changing for women and infants across the state. By integrating nurse‐midwives, nurse home‐visiting programs, rural health mobile units, and the development of a statewide maternal health consortium, this work has established a foundation with the potential to help reverse the poor maternal mortality ratio and infant mortality rate that have plagued Alabama for decades. Through statewide partnership building and a shared commitment to expanding the perinatal workforce, system leaders in Alabama are poised to shift priorities from traditional silos toward genuine interprofessional collaboration. Midwives are finding their voice to lead changes within the maternal health system, with supporters acknowledging that if Alabama continues to approach maternal and infant health disparities through the same medical‐only lens and intervention‐driven birth culture used for decades, outcomes will not improve. The realization that new models are needed has been a long time coming. Recent federal funding to Alabama through the Rural Health Transformation Program also includes planned efforts to increase the number of nurse‐midwives in the workforce as mainstream, integrated health care providers. Additionally, WACHI's partnership development has gained traction in creating an innovative, purpose‐driven network of midwifery and nursing services, integrated with other statewide services and supported by physician colleagues to achieve genuine interprofessional teamwork. By leveraging shared governance, continuous quality improvement, and blended funding, WACHI offers a replicable pathway to strengthen access, quality, and continuity of perinatal services while advancing the well‐being of women, infants, and families. The initiative provides an actionable, scalable framework that builds collective momentum and concentrates resources to improve maternal and child health in Alabama.

CONCLUSION

New innovative solutions are needed to address long‐standing problems. WACHI uses a collaborative, integrated interprofessional approach to spark change in models of care centered on evidence‐based workforce development, innovative technology, expanding relationship‐based care, and growing clinical partnerships and networks. WACHI programs have demonstrated the capacity to increase access to highly effective midwifery models of care delivered across rural and urban communities in Alabama and have gained momentum to reduce maternity care deserts. Sustained organizational commitment to strategic, innovative, future‐focused hubs, such as WACHI, can expand the midwifery workforce, grow productive partnerships, and increase access to care within homes and communities where people live and work. The WACHI model is an example of how a dedicated group of people with collective expertise, passion, and “out of the box” thinking can leverage established programs and develop new programs to secure resources for a better future where women and children can thrive and reach their healthful potential.
