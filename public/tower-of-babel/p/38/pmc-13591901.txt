Foldable capsular buckle scleral buckling as an approach to retinal detachment in a pregnant patient

ABSTRACT

Background

RRD is a common retinal emergency requiring prompt surgical intervention to prevent permanent vision loss [1]. The annual international incidence is estimated to be 12.17 per 100,000 population [2]. Standard management options for RRD are scleral buckling (SB) and pars plana vitrectomy (PPV), with success rates exceeding 90% [3]. However, RRD management in pregnancy warrants special considerations regarding surgical approach, anesthesia, and perioperative positions [4]. However, reports on RRD surgery in pregnant women remain limited, highlighting the need for a safe, effective technique that does not compromise pregnancy or fetal health.

FCB scleral buckling is a modified scleral buckling. The FCB is a redesign of the foldable capsular vitreous body (FCVB) AV-10P model [5], independently developed by Vesber Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). It is approved as a Class III medical device by the Chinese State Drug Administration (Registration No. 20173123296) and holds CE (EU), TGA (Australia), and MHRA (UK) certifications. Notably, FCB and FCVB have not yet obtained FDA approval, which limits their global accessibility. The material of FCB is the same as that of FCVB, which is made of FDA-approved pure silicone that has been safely used in the human body for more than 20 years with good biocompatibility. The device comprises a thin lumen-shaped balloon and a tube-valve system (Fig. 1). Balanced salt solution (BSS) can be injected into the capsule membrane via the tube-valve system to indent sclera. Compared with conventional SB, FCB surgery is less invasive, easier to perform, associated with fewer complications, and better tolerated by patients [6]. Herein, we present a pregnant RRD patient treated with FCB scleral buckling.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old, 28-weeks pregnant, myopic female without other ocular history reported 1 days of a " shadow” in the left eye. On examination, her best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/20 in the right eye and 20/40 in the left. Fundus examination revealed RRD from 12 to 3 o’clock involving the macula, with lattice degeneration and multiple holes between 11:30 and 2 o’clock (Fig. 2A). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) confirmed retinal detachment (Fig. 3B).

The management option of scleral buckling was discussed with the patient and her family. Given her third-trimester status, prolonged supine positioning was challenging, and concerns existed regarding anesthetic effects on the fetus. Finally, the patient was given the option of a novel scleral buckling—FCB scleral buckling. FCB scleral buckling is a modified scleral buckling procedure which can be performed under topical anesthesia and is not time-consuming [6]. They agreed to try this new method, and written informed consent was obtained from patients and her family.

Surgery was carried out under subconjunctival anesthesia. Following a minimally invasive conjunctival incision, a folded FCB was introduced through a tri-folded balloon. A dissection tunnel was prepared in Tenon’s capsule, and the FCB was positioned with its convex surface against the sclera, covering the retinal breaks. The drainage tube was anchored to the sclera, and saline was infused via the valve [7]. Intraoperative fundus evaluation confirmed adequate FCB placement and successful retinal reattachment. The valve was buried, and the conjunctiva and Tenon’s capsule were closed in layers. Cryotherapy was not employed during the procedure, as detailed in a previous report [7].

The procedure was completed within 30 min without patient discomfort. Only tobramycin dexamethasone eyedrop prescribed postoperatively. Due to extensive lattice degeneration, laser photocoagulation was applied around non-detached zones preoperatively and to remaining lesions postoperatively.

The patient resumed normal activity without restrictions the next day. The day after the surgery, Significant subretinal fluid absorption was observed (Fig. 2B). Swept-source OCT (SS-OCT) showed minimal residual subretinal fluid under the macula. Regular long-term follow-up was scheduled at postoperative day 1, week 1, month 1, 3, 6, with annual surveillance thereafter to monitor PVD progression, new retinal breaks and potential redetachment. 2 months postoperatively, she delivered a healthy female via Cesarean at 38 weeks. 4 months after the surgery, the retina was stable, and the FCB was removed. 9 months after FCB removal, all the subretinal fluid was absorbed, and BCVA recovered to 20/20 (Figs. 2D and 3D).

Intraocular pressure and refractive status remained stable throughout the follow-up period.

Discussion and conclusion

RRD can be managed with SB or PPV with gas/silicone oil tamponade. For this patient, SB was preferred over PPV to avoid accelerated cataract progression in a phakic eye and postoperative positioning requirements. However, traditional SB has drawbacks, for instance, it requires cryotherapy, deeper anesthesia, longer operation time, risk of oculo-cardiac reflex and so on [8].

Ophthalmic surgery in pregnancy should prioritize minimizing anesthetic exposure, avoiding forced prolonged supine posture, reducing cryotherapy-related inflammation and limiting perioperative medication use, all of which constitute a targeted fetal safety protection strategy.

The Lincoff balloon, developed in the 1970s for temporary indentation under retinal breaks, saw limited use due to externalized tubing and lack of commercial availability [9]. FCB represents a modern modification, with the drainage tube folded and sutured under conjunctiva. Compared to conventional SB, FCB scleral buckling eliminates retrobulbar anesthesia, muscle traction, intraoperative positioning, and cryotherapy [6, 10], shortening surgery time and reducing complications—attributes well-suited to pregnant patients.

Pneumoretinopexy and single quadrant sutureless air buckle are also feasible alternatives for uncomplicated RRD [11, 12]. Although they are rapid and technically simple, they still need strict postoperative prone or lateral positioning, which is poorly tolerated by third-trimester pregnant women. In addition, intraocular gas may induce IOP elevation and interfere with emergency cesarean delivery under general anesthesia. By contrast, FCB avoids intraocular gas and mandatory constrained positioning, permits early unrestricted activity, and is more compatible with the physiological demands of pregnant patients.

In conventional SB, inverted fundus visualization through an indirect ophthalmoscope complicates surgical mastery. The surgeon needs to locate the holes in the surgery. FCB implantation utilizes three-dimensional modeling from preoperative imaging for precise tamponade placement, obviating intraoperative positioning. Additionally, muscle manipulation is avoided. In this case, ultra-widefield photography clearly visualized retinal breaks.

Furthermore, conventional SB may alter retinal circulation and induce ocular inflammation, often requiring postoperative steroids [13]. Medication use in pregnancy necessitates caution. Here, laser photocoagulation was used instead of cryopexy, minimizing inflammation; only topical antibiotic-steroid drops were administered. Laser-induced inflammation is generally milder than cryopexy [14].

In additional, Refractive changes may follow conventional SB due to directional pressure from sutured buckles, especially beneath rectus muscle [15]. FCB scleral buckling appears to induce fewer refractive shifts [10]. In our case, refraction remained stable, and FCB removal at 4 months likely prevented long-term ocular deformation or muscular/vascular effects.

In conclusion, FCB scleral buckling may represent a promising alternative in selected pregnant patients with uncomplicated RRD; however, larger studies are needed to establish safety and efficacy.

Limitations

This technique avoids retrobulbar anesthesia, muscle traction, intraoperative positioning, and cryotherapy, aligning with fetal safety goals and reducing SB-associated complications. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized widely. Second, the sutureless FCB placement relies heavily on intact Tenon’s capsule adhesion; its stability and reproducibility may be compromised in patients with conjunctival scarring, previous ocular surgery or weak Tenon’s capsule. Third, FCB is not FDA-approved, has limited global accessibility, relatively higher cost, and lacks sufficient long-term follow-up evidence. Further large-sample multicenter trials are warranted to verify its efficacy and safety in pregnant populations.
