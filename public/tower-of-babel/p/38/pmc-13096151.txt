Interpretable predictive model for listed companies ESG greenwashing based on XGBoost and SHAP
Authors: Zhang Jianfeng, Qi Tiantian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prediction of ESG greenwashing among listed companies is crucial for ex-ante control of deceptive ESG information disclosures by companies and for early warning of ESG investment risks. Machine learning techniques are commonly used to predict corporate behavior, but their application in ESG greenwashing is limited and lacks interpretability. This study proposes a predictive model for ESG greenwashing in listed companies using the enhanced XGBoost algorithm and SHAP interpretation method. Using a dataset of Chinese listed companies from 2009 to 2022, this study selects 16 indicators of corporate and external pressure characteristics as the model’s input variables, combines five-fold cross-validation and grid search parameter tuning, and constructs two company ESG greenwashing prediction models. The prediction performance is compared with Random Forest, SVM, LightGBM, BP neural network, and three XGBoost methods. The SHAP method is used to explain the contribution of the main indicators in predicting the company’s ESG greenwashing. The results show that the one-period-lagged corporate characteristics model achieves 86.82% prediction accuracy, and corporate financial characteristics have a greater impact on prediction results than corporate governance characteristics. Further analysis shows that the one-period-lagged corporate characteristics model performs better in non-heavy-pollution industries and state-owned enterprises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42004-1.

Introduction

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) performance is an important standard for measuring listed companies’ sustainable development ability and long-term value. In order to cope with external environmental pressure and improve their green image, some companies begin to selectively or falsely disclose ESG information to the public, and cover up defects by processing ESG reports, that is, implementing ESG greenwashing. ESG greenwashing leads to stock mispricing and preempts government subsidies or green credits1,2. ESG greenwashing predictions can make enterprises more inclined to substantive improvement, and the pressure of such predictions can prompt enterprises to use third-party audits to enhance their credibility. However, the prediction of corporate ESG greenwashing remains at the theoretical level and rarely involves specific methods. With the rapid development of artificial intelligence technology, machine learning methods can supplement the shortcomings of traditional statistical methods in high-dimensional data and nonlinear relationships3. Thus, they are widely used in the field of corporate behavior prediction.

The existing research mainly explores a single factor that influences greenwashing behavior from the listed companies’ internal and external aspects. The internal influencing factors of corporate greenwashing mainly include financial and governance levels. Enterprises with poorer financial performance are more likely to disclose false environmental information, and the probability of ESG greenwashing is higher after receiving the letter of opinion on financial reports4–6. The influencing factors at the corporate financial level include growth, company size, and profitability. Growing enterprises have stronger sustainable development ability, but they also face greater internal and external pressure. In the process of balancing external pressure and internal demand, enterprises may engage in greenwashing5. Company size has an important impact on CSR. Small enterprises’ returns on green investments are higher than those of large enterprises; large enterprises have a higher exposure to greenwashing and thus a stronger sense of environmental responsibility5,7. Non-high profitability is an essential driver of corporate greenwashing. The factors influencing corporate governance include ownership structure, board characteristics, etc. From an ownership structure perspective, increasing the number of independent directors and institutional investors can reduce corporate ESG greenwashing behavior8. From the perspective of the board characteristics, companies with more gender-diverse boards tend to engage less in ESG greenwashing9. When the company faces the pressure of institutional legitimacy, it is easy to have the decoupling behavior of seemingly complying with the system but actually not complying with it10. Institutional legitimacy pressure can be divided into three dimensions: regulation, norm, and imitation legitimacy pressure11,12. Mandatory policies encourage enterprises to implement more substantive ESG initiatives and reduce the occurrence of ESG greenwashing; when government regulation weakens, corporations face less coercive legitimacy pressures and are likely to gain legitimacy through symbolic ESG disclosure13,14. The pressure of normative legitimacy conveys relevant social actors’ expectations for enterprises’ ESG performance through newspapers, magazines, television, and other channels. On the one hand, in highly competitive industries, the enterprises profit margins are usually low, and enterprises devote more energy to cost control than to environmental protection; on the other hand, fierce market competition drives enterprises to pursue differentiated development strategies to gain competitive advantages15,16.

To summarize, the existing literature has employed theoretical and empirical analytical methods to examine a single factor affecting corporate ESG greenwashing from different perspectives and has applied machine learning to predict corporate behavior, providing a theoretical and methodological basis for this paper. However, no literature has applied machine learning algorithms to construct multi-dimensional models to predict corporate ESG greenwashing behavior, nor has it clearly explained the multiple internal and external drivers of corporate ESG greenwashing. The prediction of ESG greenwashing remains at the theoretical level. Specific forecasting methods are less involved.

This paper selects important corporate characteristics and external pressure characteristics to construct an interpretable prediction model of ESG greenwashing of listed companies based on the XGBoost algorithm and the SHAP interpretation method. We explore the model’s predictive ability for ESG greenwashing and analyze the contribution of each feature and its predictive pattern. The rest of this paper proceeds as follows: Section “Research design” details the research method, data, and sample treatment. Section “Prediction results” presents the construction of the prediction model and the prediction results. Section “Interpretive analysis of prediction results” makes the importance analysis of characteristics and the analysis of feature prediction parrern. In section “Further analysis”, we conduct further analysis of the results, including heterogeneity analysis, classification of greenwashing into three categories, and robustness tests. In section “Conclusion and discussion”, we discuss the meaning of our results and present suggestions to investors, regulators, and companies.

This study makes three significant contributions. (1) From the perspective of ex-ante forecasting, selecting important corporate characteristics and external pressure characteristics to construct a prediction model for ESG greenwashing of listed companies, exploring the relationship between characteristics of different dimensions and ESG greenwashing, and overcoming the limitations that the existing literature selects a relatively single characteristic index. We provide a basis for investors to manage ESG investment risks and for regulators to monitor ESG greenwashing practices of listed companies by predicting corporate ESG greenwashing behavior, thereby achieving the goal of standardizing corporate ESG disclosure. (2) Comparing the prediction performance of the model under different time windows, analyzing the company’s financial and governance characteristics in terms of their importance, and explaining how each feature affects company ESG greenwashing, deepening the research results in the field of greenwashing. (3) Introducing the XGBoost algorithm and the SHAP interpretation method to construct the prediction model and perform the attribution analysis, which improves the prediction efficiency of ESG greenwashing. Traditional feature importance measures cannot ensure the local accuracy of feature attribution17. The SHAP interpretation method can address the black-box problem of machine learning model outputs by calculating the marginal contribution of each characteristic to the model output.

SHAP interpretation method

SHAP is an additivity interpretation method based on cooperative game theory, which is mainly used for interpreting the outputs of tree-based machine learning algorithms. The main idea of SHAP is to interpret the model at both global and local levels by calculating the marginal contribution of the characteristics to the model’s outputs.

AUC value

Since the ROC curve reflects the model’s predictive performance independent of the threshold setting, this paper evaluates the model’s performance by using the AUC value, i.e., the area under the ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve. The horizontal axis is the false positive rate, which refers to the percentage of companies that the model incorrectly recognizes as greenwashing but are not. The vertical axis is the true positive rate, which refers to the percentage of companies the model recognizes as greenwashing among all greenwashing companies. Under the same false positive rate, the larger the true positive rate is, the more the model’s curve is skewed to the upper left and the better the model’s prediction performance.

Variable definitions

Dependent variable

In this paper, ESG greenwashing (GW) refers to the gap between the content disclosed in an ESG report and the company’s actual performance, resulting from selective or false disclosure or exaggerated publicity; that is, the company says more than it does about environmental, social, and governance issues. We follow the methodology of Lin et al.1, Liao et al.6, and Yu et al.8 to construct the ESG greenwashing index using the rating data from third-party rating agencies.

This study uses Bloomberg ESG data to measure the degree of symbolic corporate ESG disclosure, i.e., the corporate ESG disclosure score1,6,8. Bloomberg’s ESG disclosure score is based on publicly disclosed information, such as company annual reports, sustainability reports, and news reports. All ESG information disclosed by a company is included in the score, regardless of whether it is positive or negative. The score reflects the quantity of ESG data the company has disclosed to the public, but does not assess its ESG performance. A higher Bloomberg ESG score indicates that a company discloses more non-financial information to the public.

This study selects Huazheng ESG data to measure the degree of substantive corporate ESG disclosure, i.e., the corporate ESG performance score1. Huazheng ESG rating system draws upon international mainstream methodologies and practical experience, and integrates China’s national conditions and capital market characteristics. The ratings are categorized into nine grades, from C to AAA. Huazheng ESG systematically measures the ESG performance of Chinese listed companies across environmental, social, and governance dimensions using its ESG big data platform. A higher Huazheng ESG score indicates that a company has better ESG performance. During robustness test, we replace the ESG performance score with the MSCI and Wind ESG performance scores and compute two alternative ESG greenwashing variables.

In this paper, we use the difference between the standardized ESG disclosure score and the ESG performance score to determine whether the company is greenwashing. The sample is divided into greenwashing and non-greenwashing groups. We use GW as a dummy variable to indicate whether the company is ESG greenwashing. If the ESG greenwashing index is more than 0, it means that the degree of symbolic corporate ESG disclosure is more significant than its degree of substantive disclosure, and there is ESG greenwashing, and the dummy variable GW = 1. If the ESG greenwashing index is less than 0, it means that the degree of substantive corporate ESG disclosure is more significant than its degree of symbolic disclosure, and there is no ESG greenwashing, and the dummy variable GW = 0.

Independent variable

The previous literature review indicates that corporate ESG greenwashing behavior is the result of both internal motivations (driven by a company’s financial and governance characteristics) and external pressures (originating from the institutional environment). Company financial, governance, and external pressure characteristics are highly correlated with corporate greenwashing behavior4–16. Based on the text analysis results, nine company financial and four governance characteristics are selected as independent variables at the company level. The company’s financial characteristics primarily reflect its profitability, growth potential, solvency, operating efficiency, market value, debt structure, agency cost, and company size. In contrast, the company’s governance characteristics primarily reflect its equity and governance structure. This paper focuses on the enhancement effect of introducing external pressure characteristics on the prediction results. According to institutional theory, government regulation, media monitoring, and market competition all impact corporate greenwashing behavior. Therefore, three external pressure characteristics are selected as the independent variables at the external level.

To analyze whether the sixteen selected corporate characteristics and external pressure characteristics correlate with ESG greenwashing behavior, this study employs the Pearson correlation test using Stata. Column (4) of Table 3 presents the correlation coefficients between the sixteen characteristics and ESG greenwashing. Eleven characteristics exhibit a high linear correlation with corporate ESG greenwashing (Lev, Size, ROA, Tbq, CR, Nshrsms, Owncon1, Govern, Media, HHI, Growth), while the remaining five characteristics show no linear relationship with it (IDT, TAT, AC, Bdnum, Nshrstt). Although some characteristics show insignificant correlations with ESG greenwashing in the preliminary analysis, this merely indicates the absence of a simple linear relationship between them. The core strength of the XGBoost model employed in this study lies in its ability to effectively capture complex nonlinear relationships and interaction effects between characteristics and outcomes18. Many of China’s ESG-related policies remain in the developmental and refinement stages. Policy signals may take over a year to be transmitted from the central government to companies and fully internalized. Unlike internal financial and governance characteristics that can directly influence corporate ESG greenwashing behavior, external pressure characteristics require a longer transmission time to alter a company’s ESG disclosure strategy ultimately. At the same time, a single external pressure event may be insufficient to compel companies to alter deeply ingrained behaviors. Only when pressure persists and reaches a certain level will companies deem greenwashing unsustainable, prompting substantive changes or more covert tactics. One year is insufficient for external pressure characteristics to translate into signals detectable by the predictive model.

Precision and recall affect each other, and in specific practice, different requirements should be put forward for both according to the needs of the classification problem. Thresholds should be set to meet the needs. For example, when the regulator needs to further analyze the companies with the possibility of greenwashing, the model should have a high precision. When it needs to find greenwashing companies on a large scale, the model should be required to have a high recall.

By comparing the prediction results of different models in different periods, it can be found that the corporate characteristics model in period t-1 has the best prediction performance. The corporate characteristics model in period t-1 has an accuracy and recall of about 87% and a precision of about 86% for greenwashing prediction, and it can predict nearly 87% of greenwashing companies from all greenwashing companies.

Therefore, this study carries out feature importance analysis, feature prediction pattern analysis, and further analysis based on the corporate characteristics model in period t-1 constructed by the XGBoost algorithm.

In the corporate characteristics model, the company’s financial characteristics have a greater impact on the prediction results of greenwashing. This paper presents SHAP dependency diagram for each financial feature in order of importance, as shown in Fig. 8.

(1) CR is a financial indicator that measures a company’s short-term solvency. Its SHAP value is positively correlated with the sample value. In the dependency diagram, most sample points with CR < 0 are concentrated in the region with SHAP value < 0, indicating that a lower CR value serves as a signal for predicting a company’s “non-greenwashing” behavior. As the CR value increases, the contribution of this feature to predict “non-greenwashing” gradually diminishes. (2) The SHAP value of the feature AC does not show an obvious linear relationship with the sample value. (3) Tbq is a financial indicator reflecting the market capitalization of the company. The model predicts that companies with excessively high or low Tbq are more likely to engage in greenwashing. Based on the distribution of the samples, the density of the samples within x ∈ [-1,0] is larger. (4) TAT is a financial indicator that measures a company’s operational efficiency. When TAT < 0, its SHAP value is positively correlated with the sample value. However, when TAT > 0, its SHAP value and the sample value do not show an obvious linear relationship. (5) Lev is a financial indicator to measure the company’s long-term solvency. Companies with extremely high or low Lev have a higher predicted probability of greenwashing. Debt financing can mitigate the agency conflict between managers and shareholders, providing the company with financial leverage. However, too high debt will increase the company’s pressure to pay off its debts, which may prompt the company to engage in greenwashing. (6) IDT is a financial indicator that measures a company’s debt structure. Its SHAP value is negatively correlated with the sample value. When a company’s interest-bearing liabilities are higher, the model is more likely to predict that it is not engaging in greenwashing. (7) The SHAP value of ROA does not show an obvious linear relationship with the sample value. (8) Size reflects a company’s size, and its SHAP value is negatively correlated with the sample value. The larger the company’s size, the more likely the model is to predict that it is not engaging in greenwashing. This may be because the exposure and cost of greenwashing are higher for large companies, thereby strengthening their ability to avoid risk. (9) Growth is a financial indicator to measure the growth ability of the company, and its SHAP value is negatively correlated with the sample value. The stronger the company’s growth ability, the more likely the model is to predict that it is not engaging in greenwashing.

Company governance characteristics

Governance characteristics have less impact on the prediction results of corporate greenwashing behavior. This paper presents the SHAP dependency diagram of each governance feature in order of importance, as shown in Fig. 9.

(1) Owncon1’s SHAP value is negatively correlated with the sample value, as the shareholding ratio of the company’s first largest shareholder increases, its probability of being predicted as greenwashing decreases. The increase in the shareholding ratio of the largest shareholder can inhibit the company’s tendency to greenwashing. (2) Nshrsms’s SHAP value is positively correlated with the sample value, and with the increase in the shareholding ratio of the company’s supervisory layer, the model tends to predict greenwashing. (3) Nshrstt’s SHAP value is negatively correlated with the sample value, as the proportion of state-owned shareholding increases, it contributes a negative impact on predicting greenwashing. The increase in the proportion of state-owned shareholding inhibits the tendency to greenwashing of the company. (4) Bdnum’s SHAP value does not show an obvious linear relationship with the sample value.

Results indicate that both alternatives pass robustness tests, though the model based on Wind ESG data performs better. We attribute this to three factors: (1) Wind ESG data exhibits stronger relevance to the Chinese market, with the Wind ESG score more accurately reflecting the genuine ESG performance of Chinese companies within their specific institutional environment. MSCI fails to fully incorporate policy contexts and regulatory practices unique to the Chinese market, including China-specific environmental disclosure guidelines, industry transformation pressures under the dual carbon goals, and institutional factors. (2) MSCI typically relies on voluntarily disclosed English-language materials and global databases, which results in delayed or incomplete capture of China-specific unstructured ESG information during assessments, such as local regulatory documents, Chinese media reports, and community environmental disputes. In contrast, domestic rating platforms like Wind have a more timely and comprehensive reflection of corporate ESG performance within the local institutional environment. (3) Wind ESG data shares the same source as companies’ financial and governance characteristic data, ensuring consistency in data extraction, calculation, and matching, thereby reducing integration bias.

Conclusion and discussion

Conclusion

This paper selects important corporate characteristics and external pressure characteristics to construct an interpretable prediction model of ESG greenwashing of listed companies. Using a dataset of Chinese A-share listed companies from 2009 to 2022, this study uses the XGBoost machine learning algorithm and SHAP interpretation method to study the predictive ability of the two types of characteristics on the company’s ESG greenwashing, and explores the importance ranking of different types of characteristic indicators and the interpretation ability of prediction results.

The research results show that, firstly, the machine learning models constructed by corporate characteristics and external pressure characteristics can predict ESG greenwashing of listed companies to a certain extent, and the model with one-period lagged corporate characteristics has the best prediction performance, with high recall and precision. Secondly, the current ratio, the proportion of majority shareholders’ ownership, and the agency costs rank in the top three in the importance ranking of characteristics, and the contribution of the company’s financial characteristics to the model prediction is greater than that of governance characteristics. Finally, the larger the company size is, the higher the interest-bearing debt ratio is, the higher the growth rate of total operating revenue is, and the higher the shareholding ratio of major shareholders is, the lower the tendency of greenwashing is predicted by the model. The higher the liquidity ratio is and the higher the shareholding ratio of regulators is, the higher the tendency of companies to greenwash is predicted by the model. It is further found that the corporate characteristics model has better predictive performance in non-heavy pollution industries and state-owned enterprises, with AUC values of 95.49% and 95.95%, respectively. In addition, the paper uses the ESG rating data from Wind and MSCI and sample from the Hong Kong stock market to conduct a robustness test on the prediction model, thereby enhancing the accuracy of ESG greenwashing calculations and the model’s generalizability.

Discussion

Based on the research findings, this paper presents the following suggestions to investors, regulators, and companies. First, for investors: (1) Investors should pay attention to both corporate ESG disclosure information publicly disclosed when making investment decisions, but also combine multi-dimensional information, such as the company’s financial situation, governance structure, and external pressures, to dynamically adjust their investment strategies and avoid investing in ESG greenwashing companies. (2) Based on knowledge of finance and other aspects, investors can use machine learning methods to assist decision-making to improve investment efficiency.

Second, for regulators: (1) Regulators should further refine and standardize ESG disclosure standards for listed companies, requiring detailed corporate ESG disclosure of ESG-related data sources, calculation methods, and implementation processes. Strengthen the audit and supervision of information disclosure, severely penalize greenwashing companies, and raise the violation cost. (2) Regulators can use machine learning methods to monitor and prevent corporate behavior of greenwashing beforehand, implement stricter regulatory measures for companies with a higher risk of greenwashing, and regularly check and assess the ESG performance of these companies.

Third, for the companies: (1) Companies should incorporate ESG into their corporate strategic planning and daily operation and management, comprehensively improve their management level in various aspects such as environment, society, and corporate governance, focus on the long-term sustainable development of the company, and avoid greenwashing due to short-term interests. (2) Actively communicate with the government, media, investors, and other stakeholders, proactively accept external supervision, and enhance the company’s credibility. Strengthen communication and cooperation with companies in the same industry to jointly promote the improvement of ESG level in the industry and form a favorable industry ecology.

Although this study provides some important insights, it still has some limitations that can be addressed in future research. First, given the difficulty in obtaining data, our research focuses on emerging capital markets such as China, while its unique market structures and policy arrangements may limit the applicability of our conclusions. In future research, we will confirm the applicability of our findings in more efficient capital markets. Second, to ensure sufficient training samples for model training, this study first performs SMOTE-ENN resampling on the entire dataset before splitting it into training and test sets. While this approach may introduce minor discrepancies between the test set distribution and the proper distribution, all models are trained under the same process, ensuring the results remain fair.
