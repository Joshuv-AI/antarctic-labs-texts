The Impact of Ambient Heat on Perinatal and Neonatal Health in the Context of Climate Change: A Scoping Review
Authors: Eline Holvoet, Paul Lokubal, Matthew Chersich, Veronique Filippi, Debra Jackson, Darshnika Lakhoo, Shobna Sawry, Shuchita Gupta, Stanley Luchters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of ReviewClimate change is driving rising temperatures and more frequent heatwaves, raising concerns about the impacts of heat on neonates. Introduction

Climate change is among the greatest global health challenges of the 21st century [1]. In 2024, global average temperatures already exceeded preindustrial levels by 1.5 °C, the threshold established in the Paris Agreement in 2015 [2, 3]. As global warming intensifies, the incidence of heat-related illnesses and deaths is projected to rise substantially [4]. From 2012 to 21, global heat-related deaths averaged about 546 000 per year, around 63% more than the ~ 335 000 deaths annually in 1990–99 [3]. In Europe, extreme heat contributed to an estimated 48,000 deaths across 2023 alone, with disproportionate impacts on vulnerable populations [5].

Pregnant women and newborns are among the populations most vulnerable to heat stress due to physiological, behavioral and social factors that heighten susceptibility, even in temperate climates [6]. Most existing research has examined heat exposure in the prenatal period, which has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, preeclampsia, and gestational diabetes [1, 7]. These pregnancy outcomes in turn increase the risk of neonatal complications via prematurity or intrauterine stress. However, exposure to heat in the neonatal period can also pose direct threats to neonatal health [8]. Newborns, particularly those born preterm or with low birth weight, have immature thermoregulatory systems and a high surface-area-to-mass ratio, making them especially prone to dehydration, heat stress, and heat-related morbidity or mortality [9]. Despite these concerns, no comprehensive review has yet examined heat-related risks across the full perinatal–neonatal continuum, allowing the intersection between prenatal and postnatal exposure windows, outcome heterogeneity, and early-life vulnerability to be considered together.

To address this gap, the aim of this scoping review was to map the impact of heat exposure on perinatal and neonatal health outcomes across the full early-life continuum, from pregnancy through the neonatal period. Expanding beyond the well-studied outcome of preterm birth, the aim was to map the landscape of all associations and commonly used heat metrics, and to synthesize emerging evidence on potential physiologic mechanisms and causal pathways linking heat exposure to adverse outcomes. In doing so, the review also considers climate-justice and vulnerability dimensions, examining which populations are most at risk and how inequities modify heat–health impacts.

Methods

Search Strategy and Study Selection

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews [10]) and reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines [11]. A systematic search of Pubmed, Embase, and Scopus was performed on July 16, 2025, without language or date restrictions to retrieve all relevant articles. The search strategy included terms related to heat exposure, prenatal and neonatal periods, and perinatal and neonatal health outcomes. The complete search strategy is provided in Appendix (1) The PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided in Appendix (2) In line with the iterative nature of scoping reviews and current methodological standards, the review was not preregistered.

Two reviewers (EH and PL) independently screened titles and abstracts in duplicate, followed by full-text screening, using Rayyan screening software. Following the JBI PCC framework, studies were eligible if they: included fetuses in late gestation, stillbirths, or neonates aged 0–28 days or reported disaggregated neonatal data (Population); examined ambient heat exposure and reported at least one perinatal or neonatal outcome (Concept); and were situated in any geographical or care context (Context). Only primary empirical research was included (observational or interventional studies). We excluded preprints, conference abstracts, reviews, editorials, and opinion/commentary papers. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion.

Outcome Definitions and Exclusions

Perinatal and neonatal outcomes included stillbirth (fetal death ≥ 28 weeks’ gestation, or as defined by individual studies), early and late neonatal mortality (0–28 days), and neonatal morbidity outcomes. Outcomes included, but were not limited to congenital anomalies, fetal growth measures, small or large for gestational age, low birth weight (< 2500 g), APGAR scores, neonatal distress, and admission to neonatal hospital or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Maternal health outcomes, including gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, anemia, malaria, and mode of delivery (including caesarean section), were not included in the scope of this review.

Given the extensive existing literature on heat exposure and preterm birth, studies focusing exclusively on preterm birth were excluded to specifically examine less well-characterized perinatal and neonatal outcomes beyond prematurity. Several recent systematic reviews, including Lakhoo et al. (2025), have already comprehensively synthesized this evidence [1]. However, studies reporting multiple perinatal or neonatal outcomes were retained, and all eligible non–preterm birth outcomes were extracted and analyzed, while preterm birth outcomes were not reported in this review. Infant health outcomes occurring beyond 28 days of life were excluded.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data was extracted from included studies using a standardized form by one reviewer (EH), with 8 articles (13.1%) independently extracted by a second reviewer (PL) for quality assurance. Extracted information included: study characteristics (author, year, country, design, and sample size), population characteristics, heat exposure definitions and metrics, exposure windows assessed, neonatal health outcomes examined, and key findings. Information on hypothesized or discussed physiological mechanisms linking heat exposure to perinatal and neonatal outcomes was also extracted where reported. The full extraction table can be found in Appendix 3. In accordance with the JBI scoping review methodology, no formal quality assessment was performed.

Results

Search Overview

A total of 18,339 articles were identified across all databases. After removing duplicates, 8,457 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 8,184 articles were excluded, and 138 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full text reading, 69 articles were excluded for focusing on the impact of heat on preterm birth only and an additional eight articles were excluded for addressing irrelevant outcomes (e.g., adaptation strategies rather than heat exposure outcomes). The final review comprised 61 articles. The PRISMA diagram is shown in Fig. 1.

Study Characteristics

The distribution by country and publication year of the 61 included studies is shown in Fig. 2. The studies were published between 2012 and 2025, with a sharp rise in research output over the last five years; more than three quarters (n = 47; 77.0%) of the studies appeared from 2020 onward. Geographically, even though in recent years there has been a marked shift toward including more data from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), still almost two-thirds (n = 39; 63.9%) of the studies originated from high-income countries (HICs), mainly from the United States of America (n = 17; 27.9%), and with only one article from a low-income country (LIC), the Democratic Republic of the Congo [12]. Among LMICs, China accounted for a large share of studies (n = 6, 27.3% of the 22 studies from LMICs). Six multi-site studies expanded geographic coverage to a total of 65 countries [13–17]. Notably, this review found almost no studies from the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, except for one study done in Israel [18].

Distribution of included studies (n = 61) by country and publication year, illustrating the increasing number of studies over time and the emergence of research from lower- and middle-income countries in the last five years

Study Designs

The 61 studies in the review included a heterogenous mix of epidemiologic designs. One study employed a qualitative approach (community-based interviews and focus group discussions exploring perceived heat impacts on neonates and postpartum care in rural Kenya)[19], while the remaining 60 were quantitative and examined associations between heat exposure and health outcomes through spatial–temporal reanalysis or modeling. Among the quantitative studies, most were based on retrospective analyses of routinely collected health registry data (n = 30; 49.2%) or retrospective cohort designs using existing records (n = 17; 27.9%), whereas only six studies adopted a prospective cohort design. Study settings were frequently unspecified, particularly among registry-based studies. The remaining studies were community-based cohorts (n = 15; 24.6%) or hospital-based cohorts (n = 16; 26.2%).

Heat Exposure Metrics

Across the 61 included studies, seven distinct metrics were used to quantify heat exposure. Ambient temperature was the most common (n = 36; 59.0%), followed by heat waves (n = 7; 11.5%), Heat Index (HI) (n = 6; 9.8%), Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) (n = 5; 8.2%), apparent temperature (n = 4; 6.6%), and the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) (n = 2; 3.3%). One study employed a qualitative measure of “perceived heat”. Geographical patterns emerged in heat metric selection, with humid tropical and subtropical regions (e.g., India, Ghana) more frequently employing humidity-adjusted metrics such as Wet Bulb Globe Temperature, Heat Index, or Apparent Temperature to capture combined heat-humidity stress. In contrast, studies from temperate climates (e.g., Northern Europe, Canada) predominantly used ambient temperature metrics such as daily mean, minimum, or maximum temperature.

Most studies (n = 56; 91.8%) examined heat exposure during the prenatal period, primarily assessing how maternal exposure to elevated temperatures influenced fetal development and birth outcomes. Only three studies focused specifically on the neonatal period [20–22]. Another one included both pregnancy and neonatal exposure [19], and one assessed exposure specifically at the time of delivery (lag time 0–1 day) [23]. First trimester heat exposure was most often examined in relation to congenital anomalies, second trimester for fetal growth outcomes (e.g., small for gestational age), and third trimester for outcomes related to labor, stillbirth, and acute neonatal distress. Perinatal and Neonatal Health Outcomes

The included studies assessed a broad spectrum of outcomes across the prenatal, intrapartum, and neonatal periods, also shown in Table 1. Perinatal outcomes were the most frequently examined, with low birth weight (LBW) as the most studied outcome (n = 28; 45.9%), followed by stillbirth (n = 7; 11.5%). Almost all studies reported positive associations between heat exposure and adverse health outcomes, with only one study reporting no significant association [24]. One low birth weight study reported regional variation in associations [25], and another observed higher overall odds of low birth weight but no significant association among the ’LEAP 1000’ study infant subgroup (a maternal–child nutrition intervention cohort) [26]. Indicators of impaired fetal growth were also commonly assessed. Small for gestational age (SGA) was reported in eight studies (13.1%) [27–34], and fetal growth metrics in two studies (3.3%) [35, 36], both showing consistent positive associations. Notably, despite exclusion of studies focusing solely on preterm birth, adverse birth outcomes collectively appeared in 52.5% of all included papers, indicating their central role in the literature.

Neonatal outcomes were less frequently investigated. Neonatal mortality was assessed in seven studies (11.5%) [12, 14, 15, 20, 22, 37, 38], with all showing increased health risks as a result of heat exposure. Hospital admissions/NICU admissions were reported in three studies (4.9%) [39–41], demonstrating peaks during heat events. Neonatal distress under heat exposure was perceived to involve skin injury, discomfort, feeding difficulties, and impaired caregiving [19]. APGAR score was reported in two studies (3.3%) [23, 42], both of which assessed heat exposure around the time of delivery and found that higher heat exposure was associated with lower (5-minute) APGAR scores. Congenital anomalies were examined in six studies (9.8%) [43–48], including heart defects, neural tube defects, renal agenesis, gastroschisis, and hypospadias. All reported positive associations, particularly with heat exposure during early gestation.

Several studies explicitly discussed or hypothesized physiological pathways linking ambient heat to adverse neonatal outcomes, based on prior biological knowledge and interpretation of their findings rather than direct mechanistic measurement. The most frequently cited mechanism was inflammation (n = 17, 27.9%), followed by dehydration (n = 14, 23.0%), circulatory effects such as vasodilatation and reduced uteroplacental blood flow (n = 10, 16.4%), and impaired thermoregulation (n = 12, 19.7%). A smaller number referred to hormonal imbalance (n = 5, 8.2%). An overview of the proposed pathways is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Across the included studies, several contextual factors were identified. Lower socioeconomic status, indexed by income, education, or neighborhood deprivation, consistently amplified heat-related impact on health outcomes related to neonatal health, such as low birth weight and stillbirth [23, 29, 32, 49]. Community-level vulnerability indices similarly indicated stronger heat effects in climate-vulnerability neighborhoods [27], and among pregnant women working under heat stress due to financial necessity [12]. Racial and ethnic disparities were also observed, with higher risk estimates among African American, Hispanic, and Brown/Parda mothers relative to Caucasian mothers [23, 26], suggesting structural inequities rather than inherent biological susceptibility. Evidence for maternal age and parity was mixed, with some studies identifying elevated heat-associated risk among older mothers [32] or very young mothers [36], and increased vulnerability among nulliparous women [50], though results were inconsistent across settings. Fetal sex showed possible outcome-specific factors, with one study reporting higher heat-related preterm birth risk among female fetuses [51], while others found no sex differences for fetal growth outcomes [52]. Overall, vulnerability appears to operate more strongly through social disadvantages and environmental conditions than through isolated biological traits. One included study suggested that social support (cash-transfer intervention) attenuated heat-related reductions in birth weight [26], indicating that resilience can be increased through targeted interventions.

Discussion

This scoping review found that heat exposure is predominately associated with a spectrum of adverse perinatal and neonatal health outcomes beyond preterm birth, including low birth weight, stillbirth, neonatal mortality, and congenital anomalies. These associations were consistently amplified in socioeconomically disadvantaged and geographically vulnerable populations. However, evidence on direct neonatal heat exposure and its effect on neonatal health outcomes (neonatal mortality, APGAR score, neonatal distress, NICU admission) remains sparse, meaning that current understanding of neonatal vulnerability is still largely inferred from prenatal pathways rather than robust neonatal-specific data. The few null or window-specific associations reported likely reflect heterogeneity in exposure metrics, timing, and population susceptibility, potentially compounded by publication bias against null findings, rather than an absence of heat-related effects. Although potential physiological pathways (e.g., impaired thermoregulation, dehydration, placental dysfunction) were frequently proposed, definitive mechanisms and critical exposure windows remain poorly defined.

Heat Exposure Definitions and Measurement Approaches

As mapped in Table 1, the 61 included studies employed seven distinct heat exposure metrics, with ambient temperature predominating and considerable variation in thresholds, averaging periods, and exposure windows. This heterogeneity has important implications for interpretation. The wide variation in heat exposure definitions likely reflects genuine differences in harmful heat conditions across climates zones and settings. For example, several studies reported increased risk of adverse birth outcomes at moderately elevated temperatures, including in temperate regions where associations were observed at thresholds as low as 20 °C [21, 22, 29]. Notably, a recent report from Scotland showed similar findings, showing increased heat-related deaths starting to increase already at 18.2 °C [53]. These findings highlight the importance of context-specific adaptation and support the use of locally relevant heat definitions rather than a single standardized cut-off for heat or heatwaves, which may also vary across seasons within the same setting.

Although over 160 thermal metrics exist [54], most studies rely on simple indices such as ambient temperature, WBGT, UTCI, or heat index. However, these metrics vary substantially in their theoretical basis and physiological relevance, often failing to account for key factors such as duration of exposure, clothing, activity, and individual heat tolerance, including the relatively limited time spent outdoors in many high-income settings [54]. In addition, there remains limited evidence regarding which thermal metrics are most relevant for different climate zones or for specific health outcomes, underscoring the need for more context-specific validation. Most studies also use retrospective designs, yet this overlooks major individual-level differences in cooling strategies, living conditions, and behavior that can significantly modify heat exposure [55]. In addition, ambient outdoor temperature may be a poor proxy for actual exposure for neonates admitted for care, at least in HICs, as neonates in a hospital setting are often cared for in thermally regulated environments using incubators or radiant warmers that actively control temperature and humidity [56]. In high-income settings, indoor conditions are typically climate-controlled and stable, whereas in many LMIC facilities cooling may be inconsistent, and infants may be more directly exposed to ambient heat. Newborns in community or home settings also experience wide variation of heat exposure depending on wrapping practices, ventilation, bedsharing, or use of fans [56]. Without integrating such complexity, current projections risk oversimplifying human vulnerability and may provide limited guidance for public health decision-making.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms Linking Heat and Neonatal Outcomes

Among the 61 included studies, 17 (27.9%) discussed inflammation, 14 (23.0%) dehydration, 12 (19.7%) impaired thermoregulation, 10 (16.4%) circulatory effects, and 5 (8.2%) hormonal imbalances as proposed pathways (Fig. 3). Importantly, these are mechanisms hypothesised within the included epidemiological studies based on biological plausibility and interpretation of findings. Dedicated mechanistic studies were outside the scope of this review’s search strategy, with only a few included studies that examined proposed mechanisms directly.

Heat is one of the earliest recognized teratogenic agents [7], with fetal heat balance entirely relying on maternal body temperature and circulatory regulation [57], and fetal core temperature closely following maternal temperature, but consistently about 0.4–0.6 °C higher [58]. During pregnancy physiological adaptations exist, such as increased plasma volume, enhanced skin blood flow, and a lower sweating threshold, to help maintain core temperature within safe limits, even during exercise in warm and humid conditions [58, 59]. However, under extreme heat stress these adaptational mechanisms may be exceeded. The proposed pathophysiological mechanisms linking maternal heat exposure to adverse pregnancy outcomes include dehydration, impaired thermoregulation, circulatory system effects, hormonal imbalance, and inflammation [57, 58, 60].

Only a few studies have directly examined some of the proposed pathophysiological mechanisms. Field studies in The Gambia already observed significant heat-related increases in physiological strain among pregnant women, but relied only on a few indicators (heart rate, temperature, dehydration) and lacked biomarker data, limiting mechanistic interpretation [61, 62]. A controlled climate-chamber study in pregnant women demonstrated no evidence of impaired heat dissipation during 45 min of moderate to high-intensity exercise at 32 °C and 45% relative humidity [63]. Although its highly specific design (short duration of heat exposure and only inclusion of physically active participants) limits generalizability to real-world conditions, its findings suggest that thermoregulation itself may not be the main pathway of heat vulnerability, pointing instead toward other physiological mechanisms [31]. Although both prenatal and neonatal pathways are included in Fig. 3, current evidence indicates that most perinatal and neonatal outcomes are likely as a result of prenatal heat effects, whereas evidence for direct neonatal heat exposure remains comparatively sparse. Across studies, exposure windows varied widely, from acute periods (e.g., the week prior to delivery) to trimester-specific and full-pregnancy averages, limiting comparability and increasing uncertainty regarding the most biologically relevant periods of susceptibility.

Vulnerabilities

Climate change exacerbates social, environmental, and intergenerational injustices, as it is not gender-neutral and disproportionately affects more women, disadvantaged communities, and low-income countries [64], which was also evident in the analysis of this scoping review [23, 32, 49].

To fully understand heat vulnerability during gestation and the neonatal period, it is essential to integrate wider contexts such as adaptive behaviors, socio-economic status, and acclimatization status, alongside the pathophysiological pathways. For example, adequate hydration and fan use are central to maintaining thermal balance: fan use improves comfort under euhydrated conditions but exacerbates heat strain when dehydrated, underscoring the importance of fluid balance during heat exposure [65]. Regular physical activity enhances heat tolerance through improved cardiovascular and thermoregulatory efficiency [66, 67], while shorter work–rest cycles can mitigate heat strain during prolonged heat exposure [68]. However, socioeconomic status and structural inequities, including limited access to cooling, inadequate housing, and occupational exposure, influence these adaptive behaviors [69–71]. Furthermore, seasonality seems to have an impact, where “warm” pregnancies (50% of gestation in warm season) have been associated with higher rates of preeclampsia [72]. Moreso, acclimatization status, shaped by residence in warm climates, may also modulate physiological heat responses. While physiological adaptation may occur over time, biological limits, especially during pregnancy, suggest that acclimatization alone cannot offset rising heat risks. Therefore, interventions must complement, rather than rely on, acclimatization by enhancing structural resilience, cooling access, and awareness of heat risks. These findings reinforce that heat exposure functions as a threat multiplier for disadvantaged groups, and that equity-focused adaptation strategies are essential to mitigate its impact on neonatal health.

Research Gaps and Future Directions

Substantial evidence demonstrates that higher ambient temperatures adversely affect perinatal and neonatal health across a range of settings. Nevertheless, this review highlights several substantive gaps in the evidence base. Empirical data on neonatal heat exposure are almost absent, despite strong physiological reasons to expect high vulnerability in the neonatal period. Evidence on longer-term consequences of gestational or neonatal heat exposure (e.g. neurodevelopment, immune function, post-neonatal growth) is similarly limited, with only isolated cohort data suggesting possible effects. Methodological heterogeneity in exposure assessment, including divergent heat metrics, thresholds, and averaging periods, currently precludes robust cross-study comparison and underscores the need for a minimum core set of standardized indicators alongside improved individual-level exposure measurement, including indoor temperature. Important geographical gaps persist, particularly in low- and middle-income countries and in regions projected to experience the greatest future heat stress. In addition, the literature is dominated by observational designs, with very few mechanistic or intervention studies, limiting both causal inference and our understanding of effective adaptation strategies.

To address these limitations, future work should combine refined epidemiologic designs, mechanistic investigations, and intervention or implementation studies, ideally embedded in diverse geographic, climatic and socioeconomic settings and linked to climate projections. Although the preconception period was not a primary focus of this review, at least one included study (Khalili et al., 2025) assessed heat exposure during preconception and found associations with adverse birth outcomes [27]. The preconception window is a biologically plausible but currently understudied exposure period, and future work should explicitly incorporate it into study designs to determine whether heat-related risks begin before conception. A better understanding of which heat metrics are most appropriate for specific health outcomes, and climate zones may also help capture complex exposure profiles, including duration, intensity, and cumulative heat load. Future studies should evaluate adaptation measures, such as ensuring adequate thermal regulation and cooling capacity in neonatal units where ambient temperatures may affect indoor conditions, targeted heat alerts for pregnant women, and locally adapted behavior change interventions on heat adaptation are urgently needed to protect maternal and newborn health in a warming world. Improved exposure assessment could also be supported through routine monitoring of indoor temperature and humidity, and, where feasible, wearable sensors to capture individual heat exposure. Evidence for community-level neonatal thermal protection remains lacking but may offer an important direction for research and intervention design.

Limitations of the Scoping Review

Several limitations should be noted. Although no language restrictions were applied, all included studies were published in English, likely reflecting database coverage and potentially excluding relevant research published in other languages. Grey literature, such as government reports, conference proceedings, and unpublished data, was not included, and some eligible studies may therefore have been missed. Most included studies were conducted in high-income countries, which limits generalizability and underscores the need for more research in LMICs and climate-vulnerable populations. In accordance with JBI methodology for scoping reviews, no quality appraisal of included articles was conducted, which may limit interpretation of findings. An important methodological limitation is that the search strategy was designed to identify epidemiological studies examining associations between heat and perinatal/neonatal outcomes. Purely mechanistic or physiological studies (e.g., animal models, in-vitro studies, controlled human experiments not linked to clinical outcomes) were therefore not systematically captured. The physiological pathways discussed in this review reflect mechanisms proposed or hypothesised within the included epidemiological studies, rather than a dedicated mechanistic synthesis. This limits the depth of mechanistic interpretation and should be considered when reading the pathophysiological discussion. Finally, discrepancies in the number of studies addressing outcomes such as stillbirth and congenital anomalies compared with previous reviews [1, 73] are likely differences in scope, inclusion criteria, and the search strategy used in this scoping review.

Conclusions

Heat exposure poses a growing threat to perinatal and neonatal health, with this scoping review of 61 studies revealing consistent associations between ambient heat and adverse outcomes such as low birth weight, stillbirth, neonatal mortality, congenital anomalies, and hospital admissions. Risks are especially pronounced in socially and geographically vulnerable populations, raising climate justice concerns. While prenatal exposure dominates the literature, neonatal vulnerability remains underexplored, and critical exposure windows are poorly defined. To protect newborns in a warming world, research must further elucidate physiological mechanisms and target the neonatal period directly, while health systems prioritize adaptation strategies such as heat alerts and cooling measures. This review underscores the urgent need for coordinated research, policy, and clinical responses to mitigate the growing burden of heat on neonatal outcomes.
