Quantitative evaluation of spatial heterogeneity in dentin attrition using combined spectroscopic and elemental profiling
Authors: Sibi Charan, Ramya Ramadoss, Krishnasamy Nitya, Sandhya Sundar, Suganya Panneer Selvam, K. Hema Shree
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAge-related attrition in dentin is associated with gradual mineral loss and collagen degradation, affecting its structural and functional integrity. However, there remains a need for standardized, quantitative indices to assess these degenerative changes.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the structural, chemical, and elemental changes in healthy versus attrited dentin using a multimodal analytical approach and to introduce novel quantitative indices for assessing dentin degradation.MethodsHuman teeth exhibiting signs of attrition were collected, processed, and analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Four novel indices—Crystallinity Loss Index (CLI), Mineral Integrity Index (MII), Collagen Degradation Ratio (CDR), and Structural Degradation Index (SDI)—were developed and applied.ResultsAttrited dentin showed significantly reduced crystallinity (CLI = 25.2 %), decreased mineral integrity (MII = 0.38), and marked collagen loss (CDR = 0.60) compared to healthy samples. Elemental analysis revealed a decreased Ca/P ratio and lower concentrations of Zn and Sr, with elevated Fe and Cu levels in aged dentin. The SDI (0.237) provided a composite representation of overall dentin degradation.ConclusionThe combined use of spectroscopic and elemental analyses, along with the application of novel quantitative indices, enables a robust evaluation of dentin degradation associated with attrition. These metrics hold potential in clinical diagnosis, forensic dentistry, and restorative treatment planning.

Introduction

Dentin, the vital tissue forming the bulk of the human tooth, lies beneath the enamel and surrounds the pulp chamber, serving as both a mechanical support structure and a conduct for sensory stimuli.1 Composed of approximately 70 % inorganic material (mainly hydroxyapatite), 20 % organic matrix (predominantly type I collagen), and 10 % water, dentin possesses a unique hybrid structure that renders it both strong and flexible. However, over time, physiological aging and functional stresses such as mastication, parafunctional habits, and dietary factors result in progressive dentin attrition.2,3 This process is characterized by the gradual loss of structural and mechanical integrity, posing challenges for restorative interventions and long-term dental health.

Attrition-induced dentin degradation involves complex biochemical and structural alterations. On a microscopic level, aging is associated with changes in mineral crystallinity, reduction in the degree of polymerization, collagen cross-linking disruptions, and altered elemental composition.4 These changes compromise the bonding interface for restorative materials, increase brittleness, and reduce the tooth's resistance to further damage or caries. Despite the clinical implications, conventional diagnostic tools remain limited in their ability to accurately characterize these subtle yet progressive changes in dentin microarchitecture.

Advancements in analytical instrumentation have opened new avenues for studying dental hard tissues. X-ray diffraction (XRD) allows for the examination of crystal structure and degree of crystallinity, while Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) enables the identification of chemical bonds and matrix components.5,6 Additionally, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), including laser ablation ICP-MS (LA-ICP-MS), provides highly sensitive detection of both major and trace elements in dental tissues.7, 8 These techniques not only aid in understanding the pathophysiology of dentin aging but also hold promise in forensic age estimation, biomarker identification, and personalized dental care.

To improve the interpretability and diagnostic applicability of such data, the current study proposes a set of novel, integrative indices: Crystallinity Loss Index (CLI), Mineral Integrity Index (MII), Collagen Degradation Ratio (CDR), and Structural Degradation Index (SDI). These metrics aim to distill complex analytical outputs into meaningful indicators of dentin integrity, allowing for objective comparisons between healthy and attrited dentin.

Aim

To evaluate the structural, biochemical, and elemental differences between healthy and attrited dentin using advanced spectroscopic and elemental analysis techniques, and to develop novel quantitative indices that accurately characterize the degree of dentin degradation associated with aging and attrition.

Materials and methods

Sample size estimation

Prior to the study, a sample size calculation was performed to determine the minimum number of specimens required per group. A two-sample, two-sided t-test with equal group sizes was assumed. In the absence of variability data from previous studies, calculations were carried out across a range of plausible standard deviations. This approach indicated that approximately 10 specimens per group would provide adequate power at the 5 % significance level.

The formula used was:n=2(Z1−α/2+Z1−βδ/σ)2Where:

α = 0.05 (two-sided), corresponding to Z1−α/2 = 1.96

β corresponds to the desired power

δ = expected difference between group means

σ = assumed common standard deviation.

Specimen collection and preparation

Human teeth with clinical signs of attrition and age-related wear were collected following ethical approval and informed consent. Only teeth free from caries or restorations were included. Each tooth was sectioned longitudinally using a diamond disc under water cooling. Dentin was separated from enamel and pulp, and specimens were subsequently polished with successive grades of silicon carbide abrasive papers (grit 400–1200) followed by alumina suspension. Finally, all specimens were ultrasonically cleaned and air-dried.

Teeth in the attrition group were selected based on clinical evidence of occlusal/incisal wear facets consistent with mechanical attrition, irrespective of patient chronological age. To minimize confounding effects of physiological aging, healthy control teeth were age-matched to the attrition group (±5 years). This ensured that observed changes could be more confidently attributed to mechanical wear rather than chronological aging alone.

Elemental analysis (ICP-MS and LA-ICP-MS)

Dentin samples were digested in ultrapure HNO3 and H2O2 using microwave-assisted digestion, then diluted for analysis. Major (Ca, P, Mg) and trace (Zn, Sr, Fe, Cu, Pb) elements were quantified using an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS system with internal standards (e.g., Rhodium). Spatial elemental distribution was mapped using LA-ICP-MS with precise control over ablation depth and pattern.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

Crystallographic evaluation was performed using a Rigaku MiniFlex diffractometer (Cu-Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å). Samples were scanned from 10° to 80° (2θ) at 0.02° intervals and 2°/min. Diffraction data were analyzed to determine hydroxyapatite crystal structure, peak profiles, and crystallinity indices across sample groups.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Chemical composition was assessed using ATR-FTIR (Bruker Tensor II), with spectra recorded between 4000 and 400 cm−1 at 4 cm−1 resolution and 64 scans per sample. Peaks corresponding to phosphate, carbonate, and amide groups were analyzed to estimate mineral-to-matrix and carbonate-to-phosphate ratios, and collagen content.

Methodology for deriving quantitative degradation indices

To enhance the interpretability of compositional and structural differences between healthy and attrited dentin, we developed and applied four novel indices based on quantitative and semi-quantitative parameters obtained from X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. These indices were formulated to encapsulate both mineral integrity and organic matrix preservation into measurable constructs for comparative analysis.

Crystallinity Loss Index (CLI)

This index quantifies the relative reduction in crystallinity in attrited dentin compared to healthy dentin. Crystallinity values were extracted from XRD data using the Crystallinity Index (CI), calculated from the ratio of peak intensities corresponding to hydroxyapatite reflections. The CLI was computed using:CrystallinityLossIndex(CLI):(Chealthᵧ−Cattᵣᵢted)/Chealthᵧ×100Where Chealthᵧ and Cattᵣᵢted are the crystallinity percentages of healthy and attrited dentin, respectively.

Mineral Integrity Index (MII)

The MII combines crystallinity data with qualitative peak sharpness assessments from XRD profiles to provide an integrative measure of mineral phase integrity:MineralIntegrityIndex(MII):(Crystallinity/100)×SharpnessScore

Sharpness scores were assigned based on peak morphology: 1.0 for sharp, well-defined peaks (healthy dentin) and 0.6 for broader, attenuated peaks (attrited dentin).

Collagen Degradation Ratio (CDR)

To assess collagen matrix degradation, FTIR spectra were examined for changes in the intensity of the amide I (∼1640 cm−1) and amide II (∼1550 cm−1) bands. Based on visual inspection and qualitative analysis of FTIR band prominence:CollagenDegradationRatio(CDR):Collagenattᵣᵢted/Collagenhealthᵧ

The healthy dentin spectrum was used as a reference (baseline = 1.0), while the relative decrease in amide signal in the attrited sample was scored at 0.6.

Structural Degradation Index (SDI)

A composite metric, SDI, was devised to summarize both mineral and organic matrix deterioration into a single value:StructuralDegradationIndex(SDI):1−((Crystallinityattᵣᵢted/Crystallinityhealthᵧ+CDR)/2)

This index reflects the average reduction across two structural axes—mineral phase integrity and collagen preservation—thus offering a synthetic measure of overall dentin degradation.

Data input and calculation

All input data (e.g., crystallinity percentages, peak sharpness estimates, amide band intensity observations) were drawn directly from experimental XRD and FTIR datasets. Calculations were performed using standard arithmetic operations in Microsoft Excel and validated using Python for reproducibility and visualization.

Results

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

XRD analysis demonstrated clear structural distinctions between healthy and attrited dentin. In healthy dentin, the diffraction pattern exhibited sharp and symmetrical peaks corresponding to key hydroxyapatite (HA) crystallographic planes—namely (002), (211), and (300)—with a high crystallinity index of 84.7 %. This profile reflects a well-organized mineral matrix with minimal lattice strain and robust long-range order, indicative of a structurally intact and mechanically competent tissue. The amorphous content was relatively low at 15.3 %, suggesting limited organic matrix degradation.

In contrast, attrited dentin showed broader and less intense diffraction peaks, reflecting smaller crystallite size and disrupted lattice orientation. The crystallinity index decreased to 63.4 %, while amorphous content increased to 36.6 %. These changes point to a loss of mineral phase organization, typical of aged or mechanically worn dentin. The attenuation of peak sharpness, particularly at (002) and (211), suggests increased microstrain and reduced mineral maturity. Collectively, these findings imply a compromised structural framework, potentially impacting the dentin's mechanical resilience, acid resistance, and bonding performance in restorative treatments (Fig. 1A and B).Fig. 1X-ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis of A- Normal dentin, B-Attrited dentin.Fig. 1

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectral analysis provided complementary insights into the chemical composition and matrix integrity of dentin. In healthy samples, well-resolved phosphate absorption bands at ∼559 and 894 cm−1 confirmed the presence of a highly crystalline hydroxyapatite phase. A strong carbonate band at 1407 cm−1 indicated B-type carbonate substitution, consistent with biological apatite. Minimal expression of amide I and II bands (∼1640–1550 cm−1) suggested a dense mineral matrix with a preserved collagen network and minimal organic exposure.

In contrast, attrited dentin displayed broader phosphate bands and a slightly shifted carbonate peak (∼1410 cm−1), suggesting a less ordered mineral phase. More notably, the amide I and II bands were more pronounced, reflecting collagen degradation and a weakened mineral–organic interface. These molecular-level changes correlate with increased brittleness and reduced bonding potential, especially in cases involving restorative or adhesive procedures (Fig. 2A and B).Fig. 2Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) of A- Normal dentin, B-Attrited dentin.Fig. 2

Elemental composition of dentin

ICP-MS quantification

ICP-MS analysis revealed notable differences in the elemental composition between healthy and attrited dentin. Major elements—calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P)—were present in high concentrations in both groups, consistent with the hydroxyapatite-rich nature of dentin. However, attrited dentin exhibited a reduction in Ca and P levels compared to healthy samples, indicating partial mineral loss due to mechanical wear and aging.

The Ca/P molar ratio, a marker of mineral phase stability, averaged 1.65 in healthy dentin and decreased to 1.48 in attrited dentin, suggesting demineralization and altered crystal composition.

Trace element analysis showed: Zinc (Zn) and strontium (Sr) levels were significantly reduced in attrited dentin (p < 0.05), implying reduced biochemical reinforcement and altered remineralization capacity. Iron (Fe) and copper (Cu) levels were slightly elevated in aged samples, potentially reflecting systemic influences or environmental accumulation. Lead (Pb) was undetectable in healthy dentin but present in trace amounts in some attrited samples, possibly due to cumulative environmental exposure over time.

LA-ICP-MS mapping

Spatial distribution mapping by LA-ICP-MS further confirmed the loss of mineral uniformity in attrited dentin. Healthy samples demonstrated a homogeneous distribution of Ca and P across the dentin structure, while attrited dentin exhibited heterogeneous elemental patterns, with localized zones of Ca depletion and elevated Mg and Fe concentrations.

Zinc distribution, typically associated with collagen cross-linking and anti-degradative roles, was notably sparse in attrited dentin, reinforcing the evidence of organic matrix degradation.

The elemental alterations observed via ICP-MS and LA-ICP-MS support the structural degradation findings from XRD and FTIR. The decreased Ca/P ratio, trace element depletion, and elemental heterogeneity in attrited dentin reflect cumulative mineral loss and aging-related compositional shifts. Crystallinity Loss Index (CLI)

This index quantifies the relative loss of mineral crystallinity due to mechanical wear. Given crystallinity values of 84.7 % (healthy) and 63.4 % (attrited), the CLI was calculated as 25.2 %, indicating a significant reduction in mineral organization.

Mineral Integrity Index (MII)

The MII synthesizes crystallinity with peak sharpness from XRD to estimate the overall quality of the mineral phase. Using a qualitative sharpness score (1.0 for healthy and 0.6 for attrited), the MII dropped from 0.847 in healthy dentin to 0.380 in attrited samples, reflecting over a 55 % decline in mineral integrity.

Collagen Degradation Ratio (CDR)

Based on the attenuation of amide I and II bands in FTIR spectra, the CDR measures the preservation of the organic matrix. Qualitative spectral analysis suggested a relative intensity of 0.6 in attrited dentin (vs. 1.0 baseline), indicating a 40 % loss in collagen content.

Structural Degradation Index (SDI)

This synthetic index combines mineral and collagen degradation into a composite structural integrity score. Discussion

The present study utilized advanced analytical techniques—XRD, FTIR, ICP-MS, and LA-ICP-MS—to examine structural and compositional changes in dentin associated with age-related attrition. Our findings reveal significant mineral disorganization, collagen degradation, and elemental imbalance in attrited dentin compared to healthy samples. These alterations were quantitatively summarized using novel indices (CLI, MII, CDR, and SDI), which allowed for an integrative assessment of dentin degradation.

The decline in crystallinity index from 84.7 % in healthy dentin to 63.4 % in attrited dentin (CLI: 25.2 %) reflects a substantial disruption in the hydroxyapatite lattice. Previous studies have similarly shown that aging and mechanical stress lead to reduced crystallite size and peak broadening, indicative of lattice strain and disordered mineral growth.9,10 This loss of mineral organization adversely affects dentin's mechanical properties, including its elastic modulus, hardness, and fracture resistance.11 Conversely, some reports have argued that dentin may retain some structural integrity with aging if functional loading remains moderate and if the collagen matrix is well-preserved.12 However, our elevated SDI and CLI values indicate that, in the context of significant attrition, mineral deterioration is unavoidable.

FTIR spectral analysis further supported this degradation by showing broader phosphate peaks and a shifted carbonate band in attrited dentin. The increased expression of amide I and II bands points to collagen matrix exposure and breakdown (CDR = 0.60), aligning with studies by Tjäderhane et al.,13 who reported that collagen fragmentation and cross-linking loss are hallmarks of aged dentin. Degradation of the organic matrix compromises the dentin's ability to absorb stress and maintain bond strength with restorative materials.14 However, some authors propose that enzymatic cross-linking or remineralization therapies may mitigate collagen loss in aging dentin 15, which offers a potential avenue for therapeutic intervention.

Elemental analysis also revealed a notable decline in Ca and P concentrations in attrited dentin, with the Ca/P molar ratio dropping from 1.65 to 1.48. A similar trend has been reported by Meredith et al.,16 who linked demineralization to increased porosity and solubility. Zinc and strontium depletion observed in our study may further contribute to impaired remineralization, as these elements play vital roles in collagen stabilization and apatite crystal growth.17,18 Notably, lead was detectable only in attrited samples, suggesting environmental accumulation over time, a concern highlighted in toxicology and forensic dentistry literature.19 While trace element enrichment (e.g., Fe and Cu) may occasionally occur due to environmental or systemic exposure, their functional role in dentin aging remains controversial.20

Spatial heterogeneity detected by LA-ICP-MS confirms that attrition not only causes bulk compositional changes but also induces localized mineral loss. These zones of elemental depletion may serve as initiation sites for microcracks and secondary caries, as suggested by Kinney et al.21 Furthermore, diminished zinc distribution corresponds with collagen breakdown, supporting the validity of the CDR index. Although some authors argue that dentin's tubule-rich structure enables self-repair through intratubular mineralization,22 our data suggest this response is insufficient in advanced attrition cases.

The integrative approach employed in this study, combining spectroscopic and elemental indices, provides a robust framework for assessing dentin degradation. The SDI value of 0.237 encapsulates both mineral and organic damage, offering a novel metric for clinical diagnostics or forensic age estimation. Compared to isolated metrics such as microhardness or scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which provide limited biochemical context, these composite indices deliver a more holistic view of tissue deterioration.23

Thus, the results of our study substantiate the progressive and multifactorial nature of dentin attrition. While mineral dissolution and collagen degradation appear interdependent, the application of novel indices allows quantification of these changes and facilitates comparison across age groups and clinical contexts. Future research could explore biomimetic remineralization strategies to restore both mineral integrity and collagen functionality, potentially reversing or slowing the attritional cascade.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that age-related dentin attrition is characterized by significant mineral disorganization, collagen degradation, and elemental imbalance, as revealed by XRD, FTIR, ICP-MS, and LA-ICP-MS analyses. The introduction of novel indices—Crystallinity Loss Index (CLI), Mineral Integrity Index (MII), Collagen Degradation Ratio (CDR), and Structural Degradation Index (SDI)—allowed for a comprehensive and quantifiable assessment of these changes. Collectively, the findings highlight the multifactorial nature of dentin degradation in the context of aging and attrition, offering potential clinical and forensic applications for these integrated analytical parameters in evaluating dental tissue integrity.
