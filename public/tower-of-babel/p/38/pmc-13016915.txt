Designing a rational approach for the assessment of peripheral blood lymphocytosis in the acute care setting
Authors: Charles Yin, Janika Reyes, Bryony Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLymphocytosis is a common criterion used in hematology laboratories to refer a peripheral blood smear for pathologist review. Most centers employ a threshold absolute lymphocyte count above 5.0 × 109/L, but this approach lacks evidence and has poor specificity, especially in the acute care setting, where reactive causes for lymphocytosis are common. We sought to adjust the existing criteria to enhance laboratory efficiency, reduce the number of smears requiring pathologist review, and contribute to resource savings and improved patient care.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed all consecutive cases of lymphocytosis over a 1-year period at a single center that met criteria for pathologist review. Differences in age, sex, and hematologic parameters were compared between patients with a neoplastic vs reactive cause for their lymphocytosis to establish new criteria for referral for pathology review. The impact of implementing these novel referral criteria on the volume of smears referred to pathology as well as their sensitivity and specificity for detection of previously undiagnosed lymphoproliferative disorders were assessed in the original patient cohort and in a validation cohort.ResultsIn 436 identified cases, pathologists noted concern for a previously unknown lymphoproliferative disorder in just 13 instances. Patients with a lymphoproliferative disorder had a higher absolute lymphocyte count and a lower neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio. Applying novel referral criteria based on patient age and neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio, we determined that the number of cases that required pathologist review could be reduced by more than 60% without compromising identification of true cases of ­lymphoproliferative disorder.ConclusionRational design of criteria for pathologist review of lymphocytosis has the potential to greatly reduce workload for pathologists and laboratory staff without ­substantially affecting detection of true cases of lymphoproliferative disorder in the acute care setting.

INTRODUCTION

The accurate interpretation of peripheral blood smears by medical laboratory technologists and pathologists is a vital part of identifying previously undiagnosed hematologic disorders.1‐3 Most laboratories have standardized referral criteria by which peripheral blood specimens must be reviewed by a qualified technologist or pathologist, with many following the guidelines established by the International Consensus Group for Hematology Review.4 One such criterion for referral for pathology review is lymphocytosis, with most centers using an absolute lymphocyte count above 5.0 ×109/L as the cutoff in adults.4 Peripheral blood lymphocytosis has many potential causes, with most being reactive in nature. Reactive lymphocytosis is most often associated with viral infections and is usually self-limiting.5,6 Lymphoproliferative disorders, however, and in particular monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis and chronic lymphocytic leukemia, can be a less common cause of peripheral blood lymphocytosis.7

At our center, the hematology laboratory uses a 2-step approach to screen peripheral blood specimens for the presence of a potential lymphoproliferative disorder. First, an automated hematology analyzer and relevant middleware recognize an elevated absolute lymphocyte count and flag the specimen for further review. A technologist then confirms the result by a manual slide review, after which the specimen undergoes review by a pathologist. Although highly sensitive, this approach exhibits poor specificity, leading to the referral of many more cases for review than necessary and resulting in inefficient use of limited laboratory resources.8,9

Few studies have specifically investigated the optimal absolute lymphocyte count cutoff for flagging a peripheral blood specimen for review. One study of 1194 cases of peripheral lymphocytosis by Tseng et al8 showed that increasing the absolute lymphocyte count threshold above 10.0 ×109/L for patients younger than 50 years of age did not compromise test sensitivity but substantially reduced the number of smears sent for pathologist review. A smaller study of 71 patients by Sun et al9 showed that a absolute lymphocyte count cutoff above 7.0 ×109/L may be more appropriate for patients of all ages. Finally, a recent publication from Freeman et al10 examining results of follow-up flow cytometry immunophenotyping in patients with peripheral blood lymphocytosis found that 95% of patients with an absolute lymphocyte count above 10.0 ×109/L had an identifiable clonal lymphocyte population vs just 14% of patients with an absolute lymphocyte count below 5.0 ×109/L.

Common reactive etiologies for lymphocytosis include infection, surgery, stroke, myocardial infarction, and autoimmune disease.11,12 These conditions often result increased peripheral blood neutrophil counts, as well. Indeed, the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio is a widely accepted marker of general systemic inflammation, and reactive causes of lymphocytosis would be expected to have a higher neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio than a lymphoproliferative disorder would.13,14 To our knowledge, no one has explored using the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio as a criterion for triggering pathology review for lymphocytosis, but a similar approach using a monocyte to neutrophil ratio was examined by Schillinger and colleagues15 as a means of more efficiently distinguishing cases of chronic myelomonocytic leukemia from reactive monocytosis.

Through a retrospective case-control analysis of lymphocytosis cases at our institution, our study aimed to adjust the existing criteria to enhance laboratory efficiency, reduce the number of smears that require pathologist review, and contribute to resource savings and improved patient care.

METHODS

Case selection and study methodology

The cases included in this study were drawn from inpatients and patients seen in the emergency department who had complete blood cell counts measured at the core hematology laboratory at a single 885-bed tertiary-care hospital. During the study period, the core hematology laboratory operated 2 Sysmex XN-9100 Automated Hematology Systems (Sysmex America Inc). Ethics approval for this study was provided by the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board–Health Panel under study identifier Pro00126774.

We identified all cases for which a peripheral smear specimen was referred to a pathologist for morphologic review because of lymphocytosis with an absolute lymphocyte count above 5.0 ×109/L within a single calendar year (November 1, 2021, to October 31, 2022). A separate validation cohort was created from cases referred for pathology review for lymphocytosis between November 1, 2022, and October 31, 2023. Patients younger than 18 years of age were excluded from this study because they had different absolute lymphocyte count thresholds for referral to a pathologist for review. Cases were screened independently by 2 separate individuals (J.R. and C.Y.); duplicate instances were manually removed.

Pertinent clinical and laboratory data, including patient demographic information such as age, sex, and clinical presentation, as well as hematologic indices such as absolute lymphocyte count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute monocyte count, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume, and platelet counts were documented. Peripheral blood smear review comments by the pathologist, any available flow cytometry or tissue biopsy findings, prior diagnosis of a lymphoproliferative disorder, and final clinical diagnosis and outcome were also recorded.

Diagnostic criteria

Patients were considered to have a diagnosis of lymphoproliferative disorder if they had either flow cytometry findings or tissue biopsy results deemed consistent with a lymphoproliferative disorder, as evaluated by a pathologist. Cases were considered to be suspicious for a new lymphoproliferative disorder if (1) the patient did not already have a known diagnosis of lymphoproliferative disorder or (2) the patient had a prior diagnosis of a lymphoproliferative disorder, and the disease was considered to be in complete remission at the time of peripheral smear review. For patients who did not have a diagnosed lymphoproliferative disorder, a reactive cause for their lymphocytosis was assigned according to the final clinical diagnosis from review of the electronic health record (EHR).

Development of rational referral criteria

The literature on this subject is limited, so we established 2 new criteria for referral of lymphocytosis cases for pathologist review:

For patients aged 18 to 49 years, the absolute lymphocyte count cutoff to trigger referral for pathology review will be increased from above 5.0 ×109/L to above 10.0 ×109/L.

For patients aged 18 to 49 years, the absolute lymphocyte count cutoff to trigger referral for pathology review will be increased from above 5.0 ×109/L to above 10.0 ×109/L.

For all patients older than 18 years of age, pathology referral for lymphocytosis will occur only if the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio is below 1.0.

For all patients older than 18 years of age, pathology referral for lymphocytosis will occur only if the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio is below 1.0.

We retrospectively applied these 2 new criteria both independently and in combination to our initial cohort of cases and to a validation cohort to assess for test sensitivity and specificity as well as impact on laboratory workload.

Statistical analysis

We confirmed non-normality of the absolute lymphocyte count and lymphocyte to neutrophil ratio data using the Shapiro-Wilk test of normality. Statistical significance was determined using either the Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons between 2 groups or the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn post hoc analysis for comparisons between multiple groups. P = .05 was the threshold for statistical significance. All comparative statistical testing was performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10.1, software (GraphPad Software). Descriptive statistics and data visualization were performed using Microsoft Excel 2021.

RESULTS

Case characteristics and outcomes

Within the initial 1-year study period, a total of 436 peripheral blood smears were referred for pathology review for lymphocytosis (Figure 1). Of these, only 13 (3.0%) were noted as having features concerning for a previously unknown lymphoproliferative disorder. Of the remaining 423 cases, 56 (13.2%) were from patients with a previously diagnosed lymphoproliferative disorder; in the remaining 367 (86.8%) cases, the lymphocytosis was deemed to be reactive. Of the cases of lymphocytosis deemed to be reactive, the most common causes identified were infection (108/367 [29.4%]), postoperative status (34/367 [9.3%]), cardiac arrest (32/367 [8.7%), autoimmune disease (29/367 [7.9%]), and trauma (23/367 [6.3%]).

Within the subset of 13 cases deemed to have features concerning for a new lymphoproliferative disorder, only 6 (46.2%) cases received workup, with a total of 4 cases receiving a final diagnosis of a new lymphoproliferative disorder. Hematologic parameters of lymphoproliferative disorder vs non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases

We compared hematologic parameters of cases deemed not to be suspicious for a lymphoproliferative disorder and cases deemed to have features suspicious for a previously undiagnosed lymphoproliferative disorder or known lymphoproliferative disorder (Figure 2). Lymphoproliferative disorder cases were found to have substantially higher absolute lymphocyte counts and lower absolute neutrophil counts with overall higher total white blood cell counts than non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases. Interestingly, although absolute monocyte count and hemoglobin level were not substantially different between lymphoproliferative disorder and non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases, mean corpuscular volumes were higher in lymphoproliferative disorder cases, although platelet counts were lower.

In examining the distribution of absolute lymphocyte count in lymphoproliferative disorder and non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases (Figure 3A), the vast majority of non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases had an absolute lymphocyte count below 10.0 ×109/L, with most actually having an absolute lymphocyte count between 5.1 and 6.0 ×109/L. In contrast, lymphoproliferative disorder cases showed a much greater absolute lymphocyte count distribution. Given that absolute neutrophil count was found to be substantially decreased relative to absolute lymphocyte count in cases of true lymphoproliferative disorder vs reactive causes of lymphocytosis, we also examined the differences in the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio between lymphoproliferative disorder and non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases (Figure 3B). As expected, a majority of non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases were found to have a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio of 1.0 or higher, while lymphoproliferative disorder cases more often demonstrated a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio below 1.0. To validate the use of a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio below 1.0 as a means of discriminating between lymphoproliferative disorder and non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases, we constructed a receiver operating characteristic curve (Figure 4). We determined that using a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio threshold of 1.0 maintained excellent test sensitivity (approximately 0.8) while providing for reasonable specificity (approximately 0.5).

Impact of novel referral criteria

The impact of a higher absolute lymphocyte count threshold for patients younger than 50 years of age and a lower neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio cutoff on the number of cases that met criteria for pathology review in the initial cohort was examined (Table 2). Within this cohort, 215 of 436 (49.3%) cases reviewed were in patients younger than 50 years of age. With the addition of a higher absolute lymphocyte count threshold for patients younger than 50 years of age, the number of cases referred decreased from 436 to 293 (a 32.8% reduction), with all 13 cases deemed to have features suspicious for a lymphoproliferative disorder included (100% sensitivity; 30.7% specificity). When a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio cutoff below 1.0 was implemented, the number of cases decreased to 231 (a 47.0% reduction), with 12 of 13 (92.3%) cases concerning for a lymphoproliferative disorder included (92.0% sensitivity; 45.3% specificity). When both criteria were applied together, there was an overall reduction to just 161 cases from the original 436 (a 63.1% reduction), with 12 of 13 (92.3%) cases concerning for a lymphoproliferative disorder captured (92.0% sensitivity; 61.9% specificity).

DISCUSSION

Referral of peripheral blood smears with lymphocytosis for pathologist review is primarily intended to identify a previously undiagnosed lymphoproliferative disorder.3 In the acute care setting, however, there are many reasons for lymphocytosis, with the vast majority being reactive in nature.5,6 As a result, many peripheral smears end up being needlessly referred for review, resulting in poor use of pathologist and technologist time.8 In addition, overutilization of pathology review for mild peripheral lymphocytosis in the absence of specific symptoms increases the rate of overdiagnosis of premalignant conditions such as monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis. Early detection of such conditions has not been shown to lead to any improvement in overall patient outcomes; on the contrary, it often results in unnecessary patient anxiety.10,16

In this study, we reviewed all consecutive cases of peripheral smears referred for review based on lymphocytosis within a single calendar year to better characterize features that distinguish between actual cases of lymphoproliferative disorder from reactive non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases and to design a more rational set of criteria for pathology review for lymphocytosis. Of a total of 436 cases referred for review, just 13 were judged to have features suspicious for a previously undiagnosed lymphoproliferative disorder; after further workup, only 4 cases of new lymphoproliferative disorder were diagnosed. On average, cases of true lymphoproliferative disorder had higher absolute lymphocyte counts and lower neutrophil to lymphocyte ratios than did non–lymphoproliferative disorder cases. This finding is likely due to lymphoproliferative disorders causing much more pronounced peripheral lymphocytosis than reactive causes, while reactive causes of lymphocytosis are generally inflammatory states that result in an increase in multiple white blood cell count ­subsets (and in particular, neutrophils) as opposed to just lymphocytes.17,18

Another key criterion for differentiating between a true lymphoproliferative disorder and reactive lymphocytosis was patient age. It is well known that the incidence of lymphoproliferative disorders increases with age, especially in chronic lymphocytic leukemia, the most common lymphoproliferative disorder to be identified by peripheral blood lymphocytosis.16,19 Indeed, Tseng et al8 showed that increasing the absolute lymphocyte count threshold from 5.0 to 10.0 ×109/L in patients younger than 50 years of age led to a substantial reduction in the number of cases sent for pathologist review without noticeably reducing the ability to adequately identify cases with features suspicious for true lymphoproliferative disorder.

Using a combination of an increased absolute lymphocyte count threshold for patients younger than 50 years of age and a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio below 1.0 led to a 63.1% reduction in the total number of cases being referred while maintaining a sensitivity of 92% for identifying those cases that were originally deemed to have features suspicious for a lymphoproliferative disorder by pathologist review. Furthermore, in reviewing the single case the pathologist deemed suspicious for potential lymphoproliferative disorder, the comment simply recommended further workup with flow cytometry should there be persistent lymphocytosis given that no previous lymphocyte counts were available in the EHR. The patient in question was admitted with an acute coronary syndrome, but ultimately no further workup was pursued because the patient was ultimately lost to follow-up. The validation cohort saw a similar reduction in the number of referred cases without loss of a single case deemed suspicious for a potential new lymphoproliferative disorder.

This study has several limitations, including being limited to a single center and a low number of actual cases of previously undiagnosed lymphoproliferative disorder cases identified, which may have limited the ability of this study to identify meaningful differences in hematologic parameters between cases of true lymphoproliferative disorder and reactive lymphocytosis. Another important limitation was the lack of follow-up for many of the cases deemed by a pathologist to be suspicious for a previously unknown lymphoproliferative disorder; a final diagnosis could not be rendered for these cases.

Nevertheless, this study illustrates that the use of the commonly applied criteria of absolute lymphocyte count above 5.0 ×109/L for all patients for referral of peripheral blood smears for pathology review is likely inappropriate. By incorporating 2 additional criteria—absolute lymphocyte count above 10.0 ×109/L for patients younger than 50 years of age and a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio below 1.0 for all patients—the number of cases that would have been referred could be reduced by almost two-thirds without any meaningful reduction in the ability to identify true cases of lymphoproliferative disorder.
