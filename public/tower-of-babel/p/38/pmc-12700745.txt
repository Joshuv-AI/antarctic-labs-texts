cGAS–STING: From immunology and oncology view
Authors: Xiangxiang Liu, Chengshi Ding, Jun Lu, Na Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway is a cornerstone of host innate immunity, playing a central role in detecting cytosolic double-stranded DNA of both endogenous and exogenous origins. Upon activation, cGAS synthesizes the second messenger 2′3′-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), which binds and activates STING to trigger downstream immune responses, including the production of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines. Emerging studies highlight the cGAS–STING pathway as a promising therapeutic target for preventing and treating diverse pathologies, with particularly transformative potential in anticancer therapies. In this review, we dissect the key findings, functions, and associated components of the cGAS–STING pathway. In addition, we emphasize the factors that upregulate or downregulate the pathway, as well as the role of the cGAS–STING pathway in health and disease. By integrating mechanistic insights with clinical perspectives, this review aims to bridge fundamental discoveries with therapeutic applications of cGAS–STING biology.

Introduction

The innate immune system is an important component of the human immune defense, which resists foreign pathogens, monitors the aging process, and protects against tumor cells, in order to maintain internal environment stability. Historically, the conceptual understanding of innate immunity emerged subsequent to that of the adaptive immune response in immunological history. Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), the key effector molecules of innate immunity, specifically recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns. The latter are ubiquitously present in pathological contexts, such as infection, cellular stress, and tissue injury.[1] Notably, malignant cells continuously release elevated damage-associated molecular pattern levels during tumor initiation and evolution.[2] These endogenous danger signals activate downstream signaling cascades through ligand-receptor interactions with PRRs, initiating innate immune programs, including proinflammatory cytokine secretion and recruitment of immune effector cells.[3] Type I interferons (IFN-I) and other cytokines are precisely regulated by PRRs to counteract viral or microbial infections, preserving cellular equilibrium.

The cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase (cGAS) is a recently identified PRR class involved in cytosolic DNA sensing. Initial studies revealed that stimulator of interferon genes (STING), the upstream initiator of type I IFNs, lacks the direct ability to bind immunostimulatory DNA.[4] As ligands, bacterial-derived cyclic dinucleotides (CDN) (e.g., c-di-GMP and c-di-AMP) bind STING, with subsequent investigations establishing cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), synthesized by cGAS, as the novel secondary messenger for STING activation.[5] Originally, analysis of the cGAS–STING pathway focused on host defense against DNA-containing pathogens.[6] However, emerging evidence demonstrates cGAS–STING pathway’s broad expression in non-immune and malignant cells.[7] Beyond triggering IFN-I signaling to mediate innate immunity, the cGAS–STING pathway participates in diverse cellular processes, including cell death, autophagy, and senescence. Furthermore, accumulating data indicate that cGAS–STING pathway activation restricts tumor progression through the induction of tumor-specific immunity.[8] Consequently, preclinical investigations targeting the cGAS–STING pathway may establish a theoretical foundation for clinical interventions, potentially alleviating symptoms, improving quality of life in autoimmune or inflammatory disorders, and extending the survival of cancer patients.

Key Findings of cGAS–STING

STING, identified in 2008 as a critical innate immunity component, detects viral infections and triggers IFN-I responses.[9,10] Functionally, the CDN c-di-GMP and c-di-AMP of bacteria induce transcriptional profiles similar to cytoplasmic dsDNA.[11,12] STING is a direct sensor for c-di-GMP.[13,14] However, this PAMP is not produced by viruses. Emerging evidence underscores the cGAS–STING pathway as a dual-function pathway critical not only in antimicrobial host defense but also in therapeutic oncology. This pathway is increasingly recognized as both a drug target and a pivotal mediator activated during diverse antitumor interventions.[20,21] Radiotherapy and genotoxic chemotherapeutics induce DNA damage, resulting in cytoplasmic dsDNA accumulation that engages cGAS–STING pathway to potentiate tumor antigen presentation, immune cell recruitment, and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling.[22–24] Similarly, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), oncolytic viruses, and cancer vaccines have been shown to depend on cGAS–STING activation for amplifying immune responses, particularly through T cell priming and dendritic cell (DC) mediated antigen cross-presentation.[25–28] Conversely, pathway inactivation or STING mutations facilitate tumor immune evasion and confer therapeutic resistance, collectively highlighting the therapeutic imperative of balanced cGAS–STING activation in sustaining antitumor immunity.[29–31] These insights aim to inform the development of novel therapeutic modalities and advance mechanistic understanding of cancer immunotherapy.

Functions of cGAS–STING and Associated Components of the Signaling Pathway

Structural domain and function of cGAS protein

Like canonical proteins, cGAS adopts a bipartite architecture comprised an N-terminal domain (residues 1–159) and a C-terminal catalytic core (residues 160–522). The C-terminal region harbors the nucleotidyltransferase domain and two DNA-binding motifs that cooperatively facilitate cGAS dimerization, mediating sequence-agnostic binding to dsDNA via 2:2 stoichiometric interactions with the sugar-phosphate backbone.[32–35] Reverse-transcribed ssDNA sub-optimally activates cGAS.[36] Structural analysis found that unpaired guanosine residues serve as a critical structural determinant enabling partial cGAS activation, suggesting context-dependent sequence recognition within this otherwise DNA structure-selective sensor.[36]

Human cGAS (hcGAS/MB21D1), a 60,000 Da Mab-21 domain-containing protein, exhibits distinctive structural organization. The N-terminal segment (residues 1–160) constitutes an intrinsically disordered region with low cross-species conservation, whereas the C-terminal catalytic module (161–522) forms the evolutionarily conserved Mab-21 functional core.[37] Within this domain, three hydrophobic residues (E225, D227, and D319) coordinate Mg2+ to establish a catalytic triade essential for converting adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and guanosine triphosphate (GTP) into the second messenger cGAMP.[38,39] A zinc ribbon motif assembled through Zn2+ coordination by histidine H390 and cysteines C396/C397/C404 creates a molecular platform that stereospecifically recognizes the B-form dsDNA topology.[40,33] Notably, backbone residues Lys187 and Leu195 stabilize hcGAS to enhance sensing of long DNA fragments (>45 bp), enabling self-nonself discrimination between endogenous short DNA debris and pathogenic long nucleic acids, preventing autoimmunity.[41] Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that surface residue substitutions at DNA length-sensing regions modulate species-specific activation thresholds.[41,42] Mechanistically, hcGAS achieves sequence-independent DNA recognition and catalytic competence through synergistic utilization of Mg2+-dependent catalytic sites and Zn2+-stabilized DNA-binding interfaces [Figure 3]. This dual-metal coordination mechanism not only underpins cGAS’s immunological centrality but also provides a structural rationale for its evolution of stringent self-DNA avoidance strategies.

Structural domain and function of the STING protein

STING, encoded by the TMEM173 gene, is a homodimeric transmembrane protein. Each monomer folds into four transmembrane α-helices distributed across N-and C-terminal domains, which respectively anchor the dimer to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondrial outer membranes. The C-terminal domain extends into the cytosol as a dynamic conformation module that orchestrates innate immune signaling.[43] In its basal state, STING exists as an ER-localized dimer. Upon cGAS activation via liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) with dsDNA in cytoplasmic condensates, catalytic conversion of ATP and GTP generates the cyclic dinucleotide second messenger 2′3′-cGAMP.[44,45] cGAMP binding induces STING oligomerization into active tetramers,[46] which subsequently traffic along sulfated glycosaminoglycan-modified pathways to perinuclear Golgi networks.[47]

During this spatial redistribution, C-terminal palmitoylation of STING recruits and activates TBK1. Autophosphorylated TBK1 enhances docking to interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), triggering its phosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and cooperative interaction with NF-κB to drive transcriptional programs encoding IFNs-I and immunoregulatory mediators.[48–51] Signal termination is achieved through lysosome-dependent degradation of STING, restoring immune homeostasis.[52] Strikingly, STING exhibits cGAS-independent functionality in the DNA damage response (DDR). It nucleates complexes containing TRAF6, p53, ATM kinase, PARP1, and IFI16 to activate NF-κB via TRAF6-dependent ubiquitination cascades. The spatiotemporal regulation of this pathway is unknown and requires further mechanistic elucidation.[53–57]

Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway

The activated cGAS–STING pathway orchestrates innate immunity through multilayered regulatory nodes. This pathway not only induces nuclear translocation of IRF3 but concurrently activates both canonical and noncanonical NF-κB signaling cascades.[58] STING cooperatively phosphorylates IRF3 with TBK1, facilitating its dimerization and nuclear translocation to orchestrate transcriptional programs, including interferon-β (IFN-β1). Simultaneously, STING recruits mitogen-activated protein kinase 14 (MAP3K14/NIK) and the IκB kinase (IKK) complex to bifurcate NF-κB signaling. NIK phosphorylates NF-κB subunit 2 (NFKB2/p100), promoting its proteasomal processing into p52 that complexes with RELB to form the p52/RELB heterodimer, thereby initiating the noncanonical NF-κB pathway.[59,60] Conversely, IKK phosphorylates NF-κB inhibitor alpha (NFKBIA/IκB), liberating the p65/p50 heterodimer from cytoplasmic sequestration to drive canonical NF-κB activation.[61]

Transcription factors (IRF3, p52/RELB, and p65/p50) synergistically coordinate immunogenetic responses: IRF3 directly activates IFN-β1 transcription, inducing IFN-I binding to IFNAR1/IFNAR2 receptors, triggering JAK-STAT pathway [Figure 4]. Phosphorylated STAT1/STAT2 forms a trimeric complex with IRF9, transactivating cGAS expression and establishing a self-reinforcing loop that amplifies cGAS–STING pathway.[62,63] This hierarchical regulatory architecture ensures rapid antiviral defense while maintaining response magnitude through autoregulatory reinforcement.

Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway orchestrates immune responses and cellular fate through dual regulatory mechanisms. Primarily, it initiates innate immune activation by triggering the NF-κB and STING pathway, which drive the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) and activation of innate immune cells, including lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells (APC), thereby inducing localized and systemic inflammatory responses.[64] Paradoxically, sustained hyperactivation of this pathway exerts immunosuppressive effects by impairing immune cell proliferation and inducing programmed cell death. Specifically, chronically activated NF-κB signaling markedly compromises T lymphocyte proliferative capacity through downregulation of cell cycle regulatory proteins.[65] During adaptive immune responses, the pathway coordinates with pro-apoptotic factors, such as IRF3 and p53, to activate mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic cascades. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced in aberrantly activated T cells within TMEs, suggesting its critical role in maintaining immune homeostasis.[66,67] These seemingly contradictory bidirectional regulatory outcomes (proinflammatory/anti-proliferative and immune activation/apoptosis induction) likely reflect an evolutionarily conserved balancing mechanism between pathogen clearance and prevention of immune-pathologic damage.

Factors that Upregulate or Downregulate the cGAS–STING Pathway

Factors that upregulate the cGAS–STING pathway

The dual role of cGAS–STING in tumors: Antitumor mechanisms

Emerging insights reveal the dual regulatory role of the cGAS–STING pathway in tumorigenesis, positioning this innate immune regulatory hub as a critical molecular target for cancer therapeutics [Figure 5]. The immune cells in the TME play an important role in tumorigenesis. It has been known that these tumors associated immune cells may have tumor-antagonistic or pro-tumor functions. Although tumor-antagonistic immune cells in TME tend to target and kill cancer cells in the early stages of tumor development, cancer cells appear to eventually escape immune surveillance and even inhibit the cytotoxic function of tumor-antagonistic immune cells through various mechanisms. The immune evasion ability, as a new hallmark of cancer, inadvertently provides an opportunity for new strategies in cancer treatment, namely the use of immune cells to combat cancer cells. Notably, the pathway exerts antitumor effects through coordinated cell-autonomous mechanisms and microenvironment-dependent regulation. In the context of cellular senescence, while cell cycle arrest was historically viewed as tumor-suppressive, recent evidence demonstrates that senescent cells drive complex cell-intrinsic and paracrine effects via the secretion of pleiotropic senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors.[68,69] SASP components are dynamically evolving mixtures of proinflammatory cytokines, proteases, and immunomodulators that can disrupt tissue homeostasis.[70] Mitochondrial dysfunction exacerbates this process by downregulating oxidative phosphorylation-related nuclear genes, activating ROS-JNK retrograde signaling cascades, triggering cytoplasmic chromatin fragmentation, and SASP development. Each of these are mechanistically linked to cGAS–STING-mediated cytosolic DNA sensing.[71–73] Quantitative analysis confirmed that senescent fibroblasts exhibit significantly elevated extranuclear DNA levels compared to young cells, with cGAS–STING activation accelerating senescence through innate immune responses.[73] Clinically, aged aortic endothelia show upregulated cGAS, STING, and phosphorylated IRF3 (p-IRF3) expression, implicating pathway hyperactivity in age-related vascular dysfunction, offering novel therapeutic avenues for geriatric cardiovascular disease treatments.[74] Strikingly, cytoplasmic DNA accumulation aligns with the elevated proinflammatory cytokine levels observed during aging. In neurodegeneration, cognitive decline correlates causally with excessive IFN-I signaling, while IFN-I receptor blockade reduces hippocampal neurogenesis deficits in aged mice.[75] Notably, cGAS–STING hyperactivation is a feature of progeroid syndromes, such as Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS), with progerin-induced replicative stress driving genomic instability, activating the cGAS–STING pathway via STAT1-dependent IFN responses. These observations highlight a central role for the cGAS–STING pathway in premature aging.[76]

Autophagy, an evolutionarily conserved homeostatic mechanism, governs cytoplasmic component degradation and recycling while enabling tumor cell adaptation to oxidative stress, ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutrient deprivation.[77] Recent breakthroughs uncovered a bidirectional regulatory network between cGAS–STING pathway and autophagy, opening new frontiers in anticancer strategy development. Mechanistically, Drp1 overexpression induced mitochondrial fission that promoted mtDNA leakage into the cytosol, activating cGAS–STING to trigger protective autophagy in esophageal squamous carcinoma.[78] Functional studies in gastric cancer models demonstrated HER2 receptor knockdown to enhance autophagic flux via cGAS–STING activation, significantly suppressing tumor cell migration.[79] At the molecular level, STING precisely modulates autophagy through ULK1 phosphorylation and transcriptional regulation of autophagy-related genes.[80] In cancer immunotherapy, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-deficient murine models revealed that cGAS–STING activation critically governs radiation-induced abscopal antitumor effects. PD-L1 expression by primary tumors amplifies CD8+ T cell activation in both irradiated and distant lesions. Intriguingly, autophagy inhibition exacerbates cytosolic dsDNA accumulation and potentiates cGAS–STING-dependent IFN-β secretion in irradiated cells, activating CD8+ T cell IFN-γ release via paracrine signaling to enhance abscopal antitumor effects efficacy.[81]

Apoptosis, a highly regulated programmed cell death mechanism, maintains tissue homeostasis and exerts dual effects in tumor proliferation control and therapy-induced tumor regression.[82–84] Notably, STING activation exhibits pro-apoptotic potential in malignant cells. Endogenous apoptotic stimuli, including oncogene activation, DNA damage, hypoxia, and growth factor deprivation-trigger mitochondrial apoptosis via Bax/Bak-mediated mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, releasing mtDNA to activate the cGAS–STING pathway and induce IFN-I production.[85] STING agonists induce apoptosis through dual mechanisms by suppressing anti-apoptotic BCL-2 expression while enhancing pro-apoptotic Bax activity. Malignant T cells show marked sensitivity to STING-mediated death signals, suggesting a therapeutic potential for T-cell malignancies.[86] Furthermore, tumor-intrinsic STING activation not only directly induces apoptosis but also promotes DC maturation and immune effector recruitment via IFN-I signaling, orchestrating multidimensional antitumor immunity.[87]

Dual role of cGAS–STING in tumors: Cancer-promoting mechanisms

The cGAS–STING pathway exhibits dual regulatory roles in tumor biology, functioning not only as a tumor-suppressive mechanism but also driving malignancy progression within specific pathological contexts.[88] Tumors continuously promote the fusion of surrounding tissues during initiation and development, thereby forming a favorable microenvironment for tumor growth, further promoting tumor immune escape.[89,90] It is worth noting that this pathway can promote tumor metastasis via the TME. In breast and lung cancer brain metastasis models, tumor cells expressing protocadherin-7 facilitate gap junction formation with astrocytes through connexin 43-mediated intercellular communication. These specialized junctions enable metastatic cells to transfer the second messenger, cGAMP, to astrocytes, activating their STING pathway and inducing secretion of inflammatory cytokines IFNα and TNFα. These cytokines act in a paracrine manner on metastatic tumor cells, enhancing their invasive potential and chemoresistance through activation of STAT1 and NF-κB.[91] As a master regulator of inflammation, NF-κB critically governs cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis, with its aberrant activation implicated in both inflammatory disorders and oncogenesis.[92] Chromosomal instability (CIN) has been shown to chronically activate the cGAS–STING-NF-κB pathway, fostering a proinflammatory microenvironment that augments cancer cell migration and invasion.[93] This tumor-promoting effect is validated in DMBA-induced skin carcinogenesis models, where chronic STING activation accelerates tumor growth, emphasizing the need for precise pathway modulation in inflammation-associated oncogenesis.[94] Recent DDR advances found that chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., etoposide and camptothecin) and oxidative stressors (e.g., H2O2) induce nuclear translocation of cGAS, which impairs homologous recombination-mediated DNA repair, a mechanism that may contribute to tumorigenesis.[95] In the nucleus, cGAS is recruited to dsDNA breaks, interacting with PARP1 via poly (ADP-ribose). The cGAS–PARP1 interaction impedes the formation of the PARP1–timeless complex, and thereby suppresses homologous recombination. These results show that nuclear cGAS suppresses homologous-recombination-mediated repair and promotes tumor growth.[95] This newly discovered nuclear function of cGAS provides critical insight into its dual roles in genome stability maintenance and malignant transformation.

Tumor cells employ multiple strategies to evade cGAS–STING-mediated immune surveillance. Frequent loss-of-function mutations and epigenetic silencing of cGAS and STING promoters have been observed in specific cancer lineages, resulting in pathway suppression.[96] Lactate accumulation within lung TMEs markedly attenuates STING-dependent IFN-I production in dendritic cells, compromising their antigen-presenting capacity.[97] Mechanistically, alternative lengthening of telomeres via extrachromosomal telomere repeat (ECTR) DNA not only enables telomere proliferation but also downregulates STING expression in cancer cells.[98,99] Emerging evidence highlights the nuclear localization of cGAS, where it promotes oncogenesis by inhibiting DDR processes.[100] Intriguingly, ENPP1-mediated hydrolysis of extracellular cGAMP not only blocks STING activation in immune cells but also generates immunosuppressive adenosine, synergistically dampening antitumor immunity and facilitating metastasis.[101] The pathway’s dual immunomodulatory roles in TMEs are underscored by its capacity to enhance antitumor responses via myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) inhibition,[102] while paradoxically promoting MDSC infiltration and radio resistance following radiotherapy.[103] Notably, STING activation in low-antigenicity tumors upregulates indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) expression to drive tumor progression.[104] Furthermore, cellular senescence, while enforcing permanent cell cycle arrest, has been linked to enhanced tumor aggressiveness and poor clinical outcomes.[105]

Factors that downregulate the cGAS–STING pathway

The activation of the cGAS–STING pathway is influenced by multiple factors that inhibit tumors or promote tumor development. Similarly, the inhibition of the cGAS–STING pathway is also influenced by multiple factors. Investigations have shown that chronic inflammation can promote tumor development, so anti-inflammatory therapies may reverse the pro-tumor effect of cGAS–STING activation. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (such as aspirin) can slow the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and induce the production of inflammatory cytokines.[106] For tumors with high CIN, the cGAS–STING pathway is continuously activated, and with pathway inhibition tumor progression can be inhibited. Research has found that small molecule inhibitors targeting cGAS and STING can inhibit the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway. For example, XQ2 specifically binds to cGAS, blocks the binding of dsDNA to cGAS, and inhibits dsDNA induced cGAS activation. However, XQ2 exhibits strong cytotoxicity and poor solubility in vitro. G150 and G108 can block the ATP/GTP binding pocket of cGAS, preventing downstream signal transduction. Further, in human monocytes low cytotoxicity can specifically inhibit the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway by dsDNA.[107] Similarly, STING inhibitors can block STING activation and prevent inflammatory cascade reactions.[108,109] These inhibitors can reverse the tumor-promoting effects of chronic cGAS–STING activation. For future tumor treatment, these inhibitors may improve the tumor’s inflammatory microenvironment.

Tumor heterogeneity can also inhibit the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, thereby affecting the efficacy of targeted therapy. Both genetic mutations and epigenetic changes can lead to abnormalities in the cGAS–STING pathway, resulting in different roles in different types of cancer.[96] In melanoma, due to promoter methylation of STING, cGAS–STING pathway is often impaired, which inhibits its ability to activate an IFN-I response. However, DNA methylation inhibitors can restore STING function, enhance antigenicity, and tumor immunogenicity.[110] In addition, in gliomas, STING silencing mediated by high methylation of CpG site cg16983159 within the STING promoter may reduce its sensitivity to cGAMP, further inhibiting the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, the effect can be reversed by the use of DNA methyltransferase inhibitors.[111]

More and more studies have shown that various protein post-translational modifications of cGAS, such as phosphorylation, ubiquitination, glutamylation, and SUMOylation, have profound effects on the function of cGAS. Cyclin dependent kinase 1 phosphorylates the S291 site of cGAS during mitosis, thereby preventing host genomic DNA from activating cGAS.[112] B-lymphocyte kinase is phosphorylated at the Y215 site of human cGAS, promoting cytoplasmic retention of cGAS.[113] The E272 and E302 sites of mouse cGAS are glutamylated by TTLL6 and TTLL4, respectively. The E272 multi-glutamylation mediated by TTLL6 inhibits the DNA binding activity of cGAS, while the E302 single glutamylation mediated by TTLL4 blocks the enzymatic activity of cGAS.[114] In addition, the polyubiquitin chains connected by STING K48 and K63 can be simultaneously modified by death associated protein kinase 3 (DAPK3). When DAPK3 is absent, the signaling axis is disrupted, IFN-β levels decrease, and tumor growth is promoted.[115] The small ubiquitin-related modifier protein is a group of ubiquitin-like proteins, including SUMO1-4. According to reports, the ubiquitin ligase TRIM38 targets mouse cGAS for SUMOylation at K217 and K464 sites.[116]

Crosstalk of cGAS–STING Pathway

Molecular crosstalk between mitochondrial autophagy and cGAS–STING pathway

The intricate interactions between cellular processes ensure the maintenance of homeostasis and appropriate responses to stressors.[117] In the field of neuroinflammation, the crosstalk between mitochondrial autophagy and the cGAS–STING pathway has attracted widespread attention.[118,119] Recent studies of the molecular interactions between mitochondrial autophagy and the cGAS–STING pathway have provided insights into cellular defense mechanisms and the complexity of in vivo homeostasis. Proteins, such as PINK1 and Parkin, label damaged mitochondria for autophagic degradation. This mechanism ensures the effective elimination of dysfunctional mitochondria, balancing mitochondrial fission and fusion to optimize function and prevent damage accumulation.[120] Compared with nuclear DNA, mtDNA is more exposed and lacks protective histones, increasing sensitivity to ROS damage generated during cellular respiration.[121] When mitochondria are damaged, there is not only a risk of impaired energy production, but also a danger of mtDNA release into the cytoplasm.[122] The regulation of autophagy/mitochondrial autophagy by the cGAS–STING pathway is a key aspect of cellular homeostasis and immune response. This complex regulatory mechanism plays a crucial role in how cells respond to DNA damage, infection, and other stressors.[123] The activation of the cGAS–STING pathway marks the beginning of a complex signaling cascade that triggers immune responses and significantly affects mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy, forming a feedback loop that can amplify or inhibit cellular responses.[124]

Crosstalk between pyroptosis and the cGAS–STING pathway

Pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of cell death, is initiated by inflammasome activation and culminates in gasdermin-D (or -E) cleavage to generate N-terminal domains that oligomerize and form plasma membrane pores, leading to lytic cell death.[125–127] Inflammasomes are multiprotein complexes comprised Nod-like receptor family members (e.g., NLRP3 and NLRC4), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), the adaptor apoptosis associated speck-like protein containing a CARD domain, and pro-caspase-1. Upon sensing pathogenic or sterile cytoplasmic perturbations, pro-caspase-1 undergoes autocatalytic activation within the inflammasome, generating active caspase-1 to execute pyroptosis.[128] Emerging evidence reveals intricate crosstalk between the cGAS–STING-IFN-I pathway and inflammasome pathways. The cGAS–STING pathway enhances AIM2 protein expression, potentiating robust innate immune responses during murine cytomegalovirus infection in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs).[129] Conversely, innate immune homeostasis requires balanced regulation among cytosolic DNA sensors. For instance, AIM2 inflammasome-activated gasdermin-D suppresses IFN-β responses to cytosolic DNA in BMDMs via K+ efflux.[130] Accordingly, AIM2-deficient BMDMs and dendritic cells exhibit amplified cGAS–STING driven IFN-β production upon cytosolic DNA challenge.[131] Further complexity arises from cGAS–STING-NLRP3 pathway activation, which suppresses AIM2-mediated DNA sensing in human myeloid cells through lysosomal pathway-induced cell death and subsequent K+ efflux-dependent NLRP3 inflammasome activation.[132] Further, cGAS-mediated IFN signaling upregulates caspase-1 and caspase-11 expression, exacerbating IL-1β release and pyroptosis during Chlamydia trachomatis infection.[133] Intriguingly, caspase-1 directly cleaves cGAS during canonical inflammasome activation to restrict the cGAS–STING pathway.[134] Collectively, these studies delineate a sophisticated regulatory network between cGAS–STING and inflammasome pathways to prevent hyperinflammation.

Interference between the cell cycle and the cGAS–STING pathway

The cell cycle is a highly regulated process involving a series of ordered events that ensure faithful genome duplication and segregation. Replication stress promotes cytoplasmic DNA accumulation and activates cGAS–STING pathway. For instance, the endonuclease MUS81 suppresses CIN caused by stalled replication forks due to cleavage of DNA structures, facilitating cytoplasmic DNA generation and ultimately activating STING.[135] Mitotically coordinated molecules also exhibit modulatory effects on the cGAS–STING pathway. Barrier to- auto-integration factor 1, a chromatin-binding protein essential for nuclear envelope formation, competitively inhibits cGAS-DNA binding during nuclear envelope rupture to restrict DNA-cGAS complex formation.[136] Depletion of the MutLα subunit MLH1 induces loss of MutLα-specific regulation of exonuclease 1 during DNA repair, triggering unrestricted DNA resection that culminates in chromosomal abnormalities and cGAS–STING pathway activation.[137] The nuclease TREX1 can limit cGAS activation at micronuclei by degrading micronuclear DNA upon envelope rupture.[138] Therefore, elucidating the crosstalk between cell cycle regulation and the cGAS–STING pathway may provide novel insights into new advancing cancer therapeutics.

Functions of cGAS–STING in Health and Diseases

Role of the cGAS–STING pathway in anti-pathogen immunity

The discovery of phagocytosis in 1883 profoundly advanced our understanding of innate immunity, with phagocytosis the host’s primary defense against diverse pathogen invasions.[139] As a central component of innate immunity, cGAS plays a pivotal role in anti-pathogen defense through cytosolic DNA surveillance.[140] It specifically recognizes exogenous DNA from intracellular or extracellular sources, enabling the host response to DNA viruses, retroviruses, bacteria, and DNA-containing parasites. Clinical studies demonstrated cGAS–STING deficiency leads to a severely compromised immune response to pathogens.[141–144]

Viral infections activate cGAS–STING-mediated immunity through distinct molecular mechanisms. Herpes simplex virus infection induces a mtDNA stress response, destabilizing mtDNA and causing its cytosolic leakage. Mislocalized mtDNA fragments activate the cGAS–STING pathway to drive cascading production of IFN-I and proinflammatory cytokines.[145] Beyond DNA viruses, RNA viruses like Dengue virus and SARS-CoV-2 also provoke mtDNA release, with their immunogenicity primarily dependent on host-derived cytosolic mtDNA rather than viral genomic material for cGAS–STING activation.[146,147] During retroviral infections, HIV-1 and related viruses exploit capsid protein interactions with host adaptor protein polyglutamine-binding protein 1 (PQBP1) to specifically recruit cGAS to viral particles, initiating STING-dependent antiviral signaling.[148–151] Notably, pathogens have evolved sophisticated immune evasion strategies through co-evolution, including structural protein mutations and interference with downstream signaling to subvert cGAS–STING surveillance, enabling persistent infections.

The cGAS–STING pathway serves as a core mechanism for detecting cytosolic bacterial invasion, inducing pathogen-specific activation patterns and biological outcomes. Mycobacterium tuberculosis triggers host cytoplasmic stress responses via ESX-1 secretion-mediated phagosomal membrane damage, driving hyperactivation of type I IFNs through the STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway, a process linked to tuberculosis-associated immunopathology.[152] In contrast, Burkholderia pseudomallei employs a type VI secretion system 5 (T6SS5) to induce host membrane fusion. While activating cGAS–STING to promote IFN-I gene transcription, it paradoxically suppresses protein translation while inducing autophagy-dependent cell death.[153] Beyond canonical immune regulation, STING activation participates in host-bacterial interactions through noncanonical pathways like metabolic reprogramming.[154,155] Recent studies reveal that intracellular Gram-positive pathogens (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria) and extracellular species (e.g., Streptococcus pyogenes) secrete cyclic di-AMP to activate STING, eliciting novel cell-autonomous defenses that amplify IFN-I responses. Conversely, Gram-negative bacteria predominantly activate innate immunity via STING-independent pathways through their mRNA.[156,157] These findings collectively delineate the dual roles of cGAS–STING in bacterial infections, serving as a conserved pathogen recognition hub and a dynamically regulated network shaped by pathogen specificity and host microenvironmental factors.

The cGAS–STING pathway exhibits multidimensional regulatory functions in anti-parasitic immunity. In schistosomiasis models, IFN-I-dependent signaling suppresses Th17 cell activation mediated by IL-1β and IL-23 through downregulation of CD209a+ DC activity, while enhancing Th2 cytokine responses that restore immune equilibrium and mitigate immunopathology.[158] Toxoplasma gondii has evolved a unique evasion strategy by secreting effector ROP16 that directly binds STING via a Signal P domain. This binding inhibits K63-linked poly-ubiquitination in a nuclear localization signal (NLS)-dependent manner, blocking IFN-I signaling. Genetic knockout studies demonstrated that ROP16-deficient PRU tachyzoites induce potent STING-dependent IFN responses, effectively constraining parasite proliferation.[159] Intriguingly, Trypanosoma cruzi exhibits stealth infection dynamics, eliciting minimal IFN-I responses and limited transcriptomic perturbations in host cells, suggesting that STING may mediate organ-specific protection through noncanonical IFN-I-independent mechanisms.[160] These studies collectively reveal the duality of the cGAS–STING pathway in host-parasite interactions, functioning as both an antimicrobial executor and a target for pathogen-encoded effectors to achieve immune evasion.

Role of the cGAS–STING pathway in the autoimmune response

The etiological factors underlying various autoimmune disorders remain incompletely understood, yet aberrant upregulation of IFN-I has been mechanistically linked to a spectrum of conditions termed “interferonopathies”.[161] This association underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting the cGAS–STING pathway, a major IFN-I production hub, for autoimmune disease management. Notably, cGAS–STING-mediated inflammation has been implicated in propagating inflammatory cascades across multiple pathologies. The pathway’s evolutionarily conserved defense mechanism exhibits biological duality in that cGAS activation requires only physical contact with dsDNA, irrespective of sequence specificity,[162] enabling indiscriminate detection of both exogenous pathogen-derived DNA and aberrantly localized self-DNA from nuclear or mitochondrial sources.[163] In physiological conditions, stringent compartmentalization mechanisms sequester cellular DNA within nuclear or mitochondrial membranes. However, radiation-induced damage or pathological stress disrupts these barriers, allowing cytosolic self-DNA accumulation that activates cGAS–STING pathway, thereby driving chronic inflammation and autoimmune manifestations, such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis.[164–169] This autoimmune activation paradigm is particularly pronounced in nucleic acid metabolism disorders, such as mutations in nucleases (e.g., DNase II, TREX1/DNase III, and RNase H2) that impair cytoplasmic DNA clearance, resulting in persistent IFN-I signaling in the Aicardi–Goutières syndrome.[170]

A prominent STING-associated autoimmune disorder is coatomer subunit α syndrome, an early-onset autosomal dominant condition characterized by arthritis, interstitial lung disease, and pulmonary hemorrhage. Patients exhibit elevated Th17 cell populations and dysregulated cytokine profiles, including IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-I overproduction.[171] The syndrome stems from heterozygous mutations in coatomer protein I complex subunits, impairing ER-resident protein sorting and retrograde trafficking.[172] Recent advances have also uncovered the pathway’s pathogenic role in the neurodegeneration of Parkinson’s disease-associated PINK1/PARKIN mutations that disrupt mitophagy, leading to mitochondrial DNA leakage that activates cGAS–STING pathway, ultimately establishing a neuro-inflammatory phenotype characteristic of Parkinson’s pathology.[173] Similarly, STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy manifests with acral skin ulceration, febrile episodes, and pulmonary fibrosis, accompanied by IgM deposition, variable autoantibody titers, and excessive IFN-I production.[174] STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy arises from gain-of-function mutations in STING, causing ligand-independent ER-to-Golgi translocation, sustained IRF3 phosphorylation, and constitutive IFN-I activation.[174]

Emerging evidence positions cGAS–STING pathway as a critical driver of inflammatory pathogenesis beyond classical autoimmunity. Accumulating studies identify the pathway as a key mediator of chronic inflammation and functional decline during aging, with therapeutic targeting shown to mitigate neurodegenerative processes in aged organisms.[175–179] Cytoplasmic TDP-43 accumulation is a hallmark of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis that correlates with neuroinflammatory cytokine profiles linked to NF-κB and IFN-I pathway activation. Mechanistically, mitochondrial invasion by TDP-43 triggers DNA release through permeability transition pores, engaging cGAS to initiate inflammatory cascades.[180] In Huntington’s disease (HD), striatal neurons display cGAS upregulation concomitant with enhanced inflammation and autophagic responses, while cGAS depletion ameliorates pathology, nominating it as a potential HD therapeutic target.[181,182] Broadening its clinical relevance, the pathway contributes to myocardial infarction, acute pancreatitis, silicosis, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, which underscore its systemic role in sterile inflammation.[183–192]

Challenges and solutions for targeting the cGAS–STING pathway in clinical treatment

Immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment by mobilizing the host immune system to specifically recognize and eliminate malignant cells, demonstrating superior targeting precision and durable efficacy compared to conventional surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy.[193,194] Clinically approved approaches, such as ICB and chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapies, have made remarkable progress in oncology.[195–197] However, immune evasion remains a critical barrier limiting therapeutic outcomes.[198–201] Emerging evidence highlights the cGAS–STING pathway as a pivotal regulator for overcoming immune evasion, with its targeted modulation offering novel therapeutic opportunities.[36,202–204] Pathway activation induces robust IFN-I production, enhances APC functionality, and promotes immune cell infiltration, thereby amplifying antitumor immunity.[205–212]

Mechanistic studies reveal that tumors evolve diverse strategies to subvert cGAS signaling. Across lung, colorectal, ovarian, gastric, and melanoma malignancies, systemic dysregulation of cGAS–STING components is observed, characterized by impaired STING Golgi trafficking, epigenetic silencing, and mutations/deletions at IFN gene loci. These collectively diminish immune recognition of cancer cells,[213–217] establishing cGAS–STING inactivation as a hallmark of tumor immune escape. Consequently, precision reactivation strategies targeting this pathway may be a transformative improvement to approach to potentiate current immunotherapies.

Challenges to the cGAS–STING pathway in clinical applications

The clinical translation of cGAS–STING-targeted therapies has multiple challenges, primarily centered on achieving spatiotemporal precision in pathway modulation. A core dilemma arises from the pathway’s dual nature in oncotherapy. In that, cGAS–STING activation enhances antitumor immunity by promoting IFN-I and proinflammatory cytokine release, resulting in inflammatory risk, triggering immune-related adverse events (irAEs), including autoimmune reactions, tissue damage, and cytokine-driven fatigue.[218,219] The risk is particularly pronounced in patients with preexisting STING-hyperactive inflammatory disorders, such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis, necessitating the development of spatially controlled activation strategies.[220]

Another critical barrier stems from TME heterogeneity. Genetic and epigenetic alterations drive STING signaling variability across cancer types, resulting in divergent therapeutic responses.[221] At the level of drug delivery, STING agonist development has multiple pharmacokinetic issues in that most candidates exhibit poor bioavailability, metabolic instability, and inadequate cellular uptake.[221,222] Systemic administration further creates a therapeutic paradox in that suboptimal intratumoral drug concentrations coupled with off-target toxicity highlight the urgent need for intelligent delivery systems that enable tumor-selective drug accumulation while minimizing systemic exposure.

Potential therapeutic strategies for targeting the cGAS–STING pathway

Innovative strategies in STING agonist development have transcended conventional small-molecule paradigms, achieving multidimensional technological breakthroughs. Analysis of cGAMP, a natural second messenger, and its structural analogs has unveiled novel immunomodulatory pathways through unique intercellular transmission mechanisms. This anionic, water-soluble molecule facilitates signal propagation via gap junctions with dual therapeutic efficacy in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma models, inducing regression of established tumors[223] and blocking malignant transformation of premalignant lesions.[224] However, clinical translation is hindered by pharmacokinetic liabilities, including poor targeting, rapid clearance, and inefficient cytosolic delivery, driving the development of STING-activating nanoparticles (STING-NPs).[225] Challenges include their large molecular mass, strong polarity, susceptibility to enzymatic hydrolysis, and limitations in co-delivery systems. This pioneering formulation enhances cGAMP bioavailability through intratumoral or intravenous administration, enabling precise pathway modulation while amplifying tumor immunogenicity.[225]

The rediscovery of 5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid (DMXAA) exemplifies the scientific value of target repurposing in that DMXAA was initially developed as an anti-angiogenic agent and has now been shown to activate IFN-I via STING-dependent mechanisms.[226,227] DMXAA did not show antitumor activity in phase III clinical trials of non-small cell lung cancer, with this failure attributed to DMXAA activation only in mice. However, it is currently unclear how DMXAA entered phase III trials if it cannot activate the human STING pathway.[228,229] Current clinical stage candidates include MIW815 (ADU-S100), which has manageable safety in dose-escalation trials for advanced solid tumors, but with minimal efficacy in patients with highly refractory cancers.[230] Results of that trial are not supportive of further advancement to phase II/III studies. Pharmacologic optimization of intratumoral therapeutics will be needed to improve systemic efficacy.[230] STING agonist E7766 showed high safety and effectiveness in a phase I clinical trial of advanced solid tumors, but the pharmacodynamics of E7766 were not dose-dependent, so it was difficult to adjust the dose to achieve the desired therapeutic effect.[231]

Target engagement strategies are shifting upstream of STING. Compared to direct STING agonism that is prone to dose-limiting toxicity, cGAS agonists exploit endogenous signal amplification mechanisms, offering superior therapeutic predictability. The high interspecies homology of cGAS (92% human–mouse conservation) significantly enhances preclinical-to-clinical translation.[232] Concurrently, epigenetic exploration has identified microRNAs (e.g., miR-378b and miR-29a) as precision regulators of the TBK1/IRF7 node, enabling temporally controlled activation of the STING pathway that has informed next-generation gene therapy approaches.[233] These multilayered targeting strategies are redefining the technological landscape of cancer immunotherapy.

Potential combination therapy with GAS-STING pathway targeting

Strategic integration of the STING pathway is redefining multimodal oncology therapeutics, with its unique immunomodulatory properties increasingly recognized for cross-therapeutic synergy. Converging preclinical and clinical evidence demonstrates that precision modulation of the STING pathway not only amplifies existing treatment efficacy but also overcomes biological limitations of conventional therapies through immune remodeling mechanisms. In surgical oncology, innovative STING agonist applications are transforming tumor resection into an immunoprotective intervention.[234] Preclinical studies demonstrate intraoperative implantation of sustained-release STING agonist hydrogels at resection sites achieves triple therapeutic effects, eradicating micrometastatic deposits via localized innate immune activation, establishing systemic immune memory against distant metastases, and suppressing pulmonary metastasis progression by 62% in advanced breast cancer models.[234,235] This perioperative immunotherapeutic strategy converts mechanical debulking into durable systemic immune protection, endowing radical surgery with prophylactic therapeutic properties.

By chemotherapy synergism, the STING pathway engages multidimensional molecular crosstalk. TCGA multi-omics analysis demonstrates enhanced taxane sensitivity in cGAS-high lung and ovarian cancers,[236] suggesting that microtubule-stabilizing agents activate cGAS–STING through mitotic stress induction. For platinum-resistant high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, STING agonist-carboplatin combination therapy achieves transformative outcomes in murine models with 78% tumor burden reduction and 2.3-fold survival extension through malignant ascites suppression.[237] Notably, the smart combinatorial regimen of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and cGAMP unveils a novel chemoimmunotherapeutic paradigm that maintains DNA damage while reshaping the TME via cytokine storm induction, reducing drug toxicity by 40% while tripling antitumor efficacy.[238] This synergy transcends mere additive effects, reflecting logical restructuring such that STING evolves from an isolated target into a molecular bridge connecting cellular stress responses with immune surveillance systems. From paclitaxel-induced mitotic catastrophe to 5-FU-triggered genotoxic stress, chemotherapy-mediated cytotoxicity is converted by STING into systemic immune activation, marking a paradigm shift from “cytoreduction” to “immune priming”. Such a biological signal amplification cascade provides a mechanistic foundation for developing temporally precise combination therapies.

Potential therapeutic scheme for cGAS–STING pathway combination with radiotherapy

The immune-radiotherapeutic synergy mediated by the STING pathway is redefining paradigms in radiation oncology, transcending conventional localized cytocidal effects. Radiation-induced DNA damage not only disrupts tumor genomic stability but also activates cGAS–STING pathway to initiate immunologic cascades. Extrachromosomal DNA fragments generated by irradiation infiltrate DC cytosols, triggering STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway activation and IFN-I storm formation. This immunologic amplification enhances radiotherapy efficacy through dual mechanisms that promote DC maturation and prime tumor-specific CD8+ T cells,[239] establishing systemic “abscopal” antitumor effects via spatiotemporally controlled cGAMP delivery.[239]

A critical balance between radiobiological dosing and immunomodulation underpins this process. Single-fraction doses exceeding 12–18 Gy aberrantly activate the DNA exonuclease Trex1, degrading cytosolic DNA to create immune-evasive barriers.[240] In contrast, hypofractionated regimens (e.g., 5 × 2 Gy) circumvent Trex1 activation thresholds, progressively accumulating cytosolic DNA to boost IFN-β secretion, with Batf3-dependent DC recruitment mechanism synergizing with ICB, inducing systemic tumor regression in preclinical models.[240] Advanced cGAS–STING-activating nanocomposites, designed using these principles, demonstrate transformative efficacy when combined with radiotherapy, achieving three-dimensional therapeutic control with simultaneous ablation of primary lesions and distant metastases through spatiotemporal coupling of DNA damage repair and immune activation.[241]

Notably, this radio-immunologic network faces dynamic equilibrium challenges. In hepatocellular carcinoma, radiation-activated STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway induces adaptive PD-L1 upregulation, generating protective immunosuppressive niches.[241] This double-edged phenomenon necessitates chronologically optimized combination strategies, in which maximal clinical benefit occurs when STING-driven T-cell infiltration peaks temporally align with programmed cell death-1(PD-1)/PD-L1 blockade administration. These insights provide foundational support for developing tripartite therapeutic models integrating radiation dosing, immunomodulation, and checkpoint inhibition. Conclusions and Future Prospects

The cGAS–STING pathway, is one of the most extensively studied pattern recognition pathways, serving a core function in the detection of aberrant cytosolic DNA localization. In this manner, DNA detection mediates host protective immune responses against pathogen invasion. The molecular regulatory mechanisms and biological framework have been systematically elucidated. Despite demonstrable value in immune regulation and antitumor therapy, translation of cGAS–STING targeted clinical interventions has faced three critical bottlenecks, tumor heterogeneity-driven therapeutic response variability, insufficient drug delivery efficiency, and the immunosuppressive nature of solid tumors. Overcoming these barriers is essential to fully realize the pathway’s clinical potential. Future advancements will focus on precision medicine-driven personalized therapeutic design and multimodal combination strategies to achieve dynamic equilibrium between immune activation efficacy and toxicity mitigation.

Emerging preclinical evidence demonstrates that STING agonists significantly enhance synergistic antitumor effects with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, providing mechanistic validation for cGAS–STING modulation as a novel anticancer strategy. Notably, the pathway exhibits spatiotemporally specific dual biological effects. Transient STING activation suppresses malignant progression by recruiting immune effectors via IFN-I responses, whereas chronic activation paradoxically accelerates disease progression by establishing pro-metastatic microenvironments. This phenotypic switch correlates with tumor evolutionary stages, CIN levels, and STING signaling intensity. Thus, clinical implementation requires precision assessment systems integrating tumor molecular subtypes and pathway activation thresholds to determine optimal treatment timing. For chronic dosing regimens, dynamic dose optimization and pulsed administration strategies are imperative to avert systemic inflammation and organotoxicity risks associated with sustained pathway activation.

This review systematically delineates the multidimensional roles of cGAS–STING pathway in innate immunity, tumorigenesis, antitumor immune regulation, and clinical translation. As molecular regulatory networks and spatiotemporal dynamics of the pathway are further deciphered, targeted modulation of the cGAS–STING pathway promises transformative advances in tumor immunology and will catalyze paradigm-shifting innovations in clinical oncology.
