Performance of a novel modified bag-valve-mask ventilation technique during manual ventilation and cardiopulmonary resuscitation: a randomized crossover manikin study
Authors: Xintong Wu, Yu Huang, Yulin Lu, Qing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe conventional one-handed grip during bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation often fails to achieve guideline recommendations. We evaluated a modified BVM ventilation method that allows a single practitioner to compress the bag while maintaining a two-handed mask seal and compared it with the conventional BVM ventilation method in simulated manikin in non-CPR and CPR settings.MethodsIn a randomized, crossover manikin study, we compared the two methods in two scenarios: Manual Ventilation-No CPR (n = 60) and Manual Ventilation-CPR (n = 56). In each scenario, participants used both BVM methods in a randomized order. The primary outcome was the proportion of ventilations with adequate tidal volume. A ventilation with a tidal volume between 350 mL and 600 mL was considered adequate. Tidal volume, inspiratory time, and ventilation rate, chest compression quality metrics, chest compression fraction (CCF) were also evaluated.ResultsIn Manual Ventilation-No CPR scenario, the modified method significantly increased the proportion of adequate ventilations (p < 0.001). In Manual Ventilation-CPR scenario, the modified BVM ventilation method significantly increased the proportion of adequate ventilations (p < 0.001), with a comparable performance in chest compression quality (chest compressions depth, rate and recoil) and no compromise to the CCF.ConclusionsOur simulated manikin study demonstrated that a modified BVM ventilation method improves ventilation performance within an adequate range in both CPR and non-CPR settings. Future studies are warranted to further assess this proposed method.Keywords: Bag-valve-mask, Ventilation, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Resuscitation quality, Simulated, Tidal volume

Introduction

Ventilation is a critical life-saving intervention in the initial management of patients whose respiratory drive is insufficient to maintain adequate ventilation and oxygenation. Such patients commonly present with acute respiratory failure or cardiac arrest. The importance of ventilation has been further emphasized by current evidence.1, 2, 3 The 2025 American Heart Association (AHA) Guidelines highlight the importance of providing adequate ventilation for patients experiencing either cardiac arrest or respiratory arrest.4

Bag-valve-mask (BVM) remains a first-line tool for providing ventilatory support during basic life support and has been shown to achieve outcomes comparable to those of advanced airway strategies.5, 6, 7 The 2025 AHA Guidelines recommend that either bag-mask ventilation or an advanced airway strategy may be considered during CPR in any setting.8 However, BVM ventilation is a technically demanding skill with the primary challenge in achieving an adequate mask-face seal, particularly when one-handed EC grip technique is used to secure the mask and open the airway.1, 4 Multiple studies have shown that BVM performance with one-handed ventilation rarely meets guideline targets.9, 10 Two-handed mask-seal techniques generally provide a superior mask seal, higher delivered tidal volumes, and improved ventilation consistency compared with one-handed techniques.10, 11, 12 The 2025 AHA Guidelines suggest that, when two rescuers are available, it is reasonable for one rescuer to use both hands to secure the mask while the second rescuer squeezes the bag.4 However, this approach is not always feasible or ideal in resource-limited settings, such as when staffing is limited or when space at the head of the bed is constrained.

In this context, we designed a modified BVM ventilation method that enables a single rescuer to compress the self-inflating bag while simultaneously maintaining a two-handed mask-face seal, and we conducted this simulated study to evaluate the efficacy of this novel modified BVM ventilation method in both non-CPR and CPR settings, compared with the conventional one-handed technique.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a randomized, crossover comparative study with volunteers at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Jiaotong University, Sichuan, China. The study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards for Reporting Trials Statement and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu (Fig. 1). We followed the guidelines for health care simulation research for reporting.13

Consolidated standards of reporting trials diagram of participant enrollment, allocation, follow-up, and analysis.

CPR: cardiopulmonary resuscitation, BVM: bag-valve-mask.

Participants

We recruited a convenience sample of 116 volunteers from the hospital. Eligible participants included attending and resident physicians, as well as respiratory therapists and nurses specializing in intensive care, emergency medicine, internal medicine, general surgery, neurology, and infectious diseases. All participants had successfully completed an AHA Basic Life Support course in CPR within the preceding two years. We excluded individuals with physical impairments that hindered use of a conventional BVM ventilation method. We obtained verbal consent from all participants who were blinded to the study hypothesis and objectives (see details in Supplement A). Before the study, participants completed a brief questionnaire for baseline characteristics (Supplement A).

Interventions

When using the modified BVM ventilation method, participants used a two-handed mask-face seal technique of their choice, either the double EC grip or the double thenar eminence (TE) grip.8 The self-inflating bag was placed on either forearm, and ventilation was delivered by leaning the chest forward to compress the bag against the forearm, with participants allowed to make fine adjustments to bag placement and force application to accommodate individual differences in height and body habitus (Fig. 2). Ventilation was performed with a 1500-mL bag (Laerdal® Silicone Resuscitator, Laerdal, Norway) attached to a medium size adult anesthetic mask (Chongren, Fujian, China), a 2000 ml oxygen reservoir was attached to the bag, and an oxygen flow of 15 L/min was supplied according to usual clinical practice. No PEEP valve was used (see details in Supplement B).

When using the conventional BVM ventilation method, participants created a mask-face seal over the manikin’s nose and mouth using a single EC grip with one hand. The self-inflating bag was held in the opposite hand and manually compressed to deliver ventilation.

Protocol

The study consisted of two scenarios: Manual Ventilation-No CPR and Manual Ventilation-CPR. The recruited participants were randomly assigned to the two scenarios using a randomization sequence generated using Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation).

All participants received standardized instructions on the two BVM ventilation methods and underwent practical training, including both ventilation techniques and other hands-on content related to the basic life support protocol. Participants practiced the two ventilation methods and CPR while receiving constant real-time feedback via the QCPR training app (Laerdal, Stavanger, Norway), connected to the training manikin (Resusci Anne, Laerdal, Stavanger, Norway). Parameters related to the quality of chest compressions and ventilations were monitored by the manikin simulator, and the app provides real-time feedback of the parameters through a visual system. Participants were instructed to deliver breaths with a tidal volume of 350–600 mL until each felt comfortable using both methods (up to 1 h).

One week later, upon the start of the tests, the participants performed a brief 3-minute roller refresher using both ventilation methods with the feedback system. After that, the real-time feedback display was not available to the participants, and they were blinded to the metrics during the tests.

Manual Ventilation-No CPR

Participants assigned to this scenario were randomly assigned to two groups with a 1:1 ratio using blocked randomization. A randomized allocation list with blocks size of 4 was generated using Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation). Then the participants were asked to perform manual ventilation on the manikins at a rate of 10–20 breaths per minute for 3 min. One group used the conventional method, while the other used the modified method. After a 1-hour rest to prevent fatigue effects, the participants performed the same procedure using the alternative method. After completion, participants completed a questionnaire for subjective assessments.

Manual Ventilation-CPR

Each participant was assigned to perform one-rescuer CPR using either the conventional or the modified method using blocked randomization with blocks size of 4. Chest compressions and ventilations were delivered at a ratio of 30:2 for 4 min. After a 1-h rest, participants repeated the same procedure using the alternative method, then completed the same questionnaire.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of adequate ventilations in both scenarios. A ventilation with a tidal volume between 350 mL and 600 mL was considered adequate. The lower limit of 350 mL was selected based on the 2025 AHA Guidelines (approximately 5–7 mL per kilogram) and the pathophysiological perspective of approximately 150 mL of anatomic dead space.4 The upper limit of 600 mL was adopted based on 2020 AHA Guidelines to reduce the risk of hyperventilation during CPR and align with lung-protective ventilation principles.4, 14 We considered the manikin to be hypoventilated or hyperventilated when the tidal volume was under or over the adequate range of 350–600 ml, respectively (see more details in Supplement C).

We evaluated the absolute tidal volume and inspiratory time as secondary outcomes for both scenarios. For the Manual Ventilation-CPR scenario, we also evaluated chest compressions with adequate depth, rate, and recoil, pause time, and chest compression fraction (CCF). Metrics were directly monitored by the manikin simulator and recorded by the QCPR App, a practice that was employed and validated by previous studies.15, 16 Participants’ subjective evaluations were recorded for both scenarios. The Borg Category-Ratio 10 (CR10) assessed fatigue.17 Mental stress and perceived ease of learning were each rated on a 10-point Likert scale. Preference for the ventilation methods was also recorded (see details in Supplement C).

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was primarily based on the Manual Ventilation-No CPR scenario. Assuming a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, approximately 75 participants were required to detect an increase of 15% in the proportion of adequate ventilation. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and were reported as mean (standard deviation [SD], 95% confidence interval [CI]) or median (interquartile range [IQR], 95% CI). Baselines characteristics were compared using Pearson χ2 test or Fisher's exact test (Categorical variables), and Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U test (Continuous variables), respectively. Subjective rating of participants was tested using Mann-Whitney U test. To take into account the repeated measures of each participant, a generalized estimating equations (GEE) model was performed to evaluated variables related to ventilation and CPR quality. The period and sequence variables were added into the model to assess whether the effect of the ventilation methods was independent of the order effects. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation).

Results

Manual Ventilation-No CPR

A total of 116 participants were enrolled, and 60 participants were assigned to the Manual Ventilation-No CPR scenario (Fig. 1). Their baseline characteristics were comparable between groups (Table 1). The proportion of adequate ventilations significantly increased from 82.4% (Q1: 55.0%; Q3: 92.6%; 95% CI: 70.9–87.3%) with the conventional method to 97.3% (Q1: 91.4%; Q3: 100%; 95% CI: 96.4–100%) with the modified method (p < 0.001). The proportion of hypoventilation decreased significantly with the modified method, from 17.6% (Q1: 7.4%; Q3: 45.0%; 95% CI: 12.7–29.1%) to 2.6% (Q1: 0%; Q3: 8.6%; 95% CI: 0–3.6%) (p < 0.001). No significant difference in hyperventilation was observed (p = 0.15) (Table 2).

The period and sequence effects were not statistically significant for all the outcomes, indicating that the order in which the two methods were presented did not affect their efficacy on the outcomes (Supplementary Table 1).

Manual Ventilation-CPR

56 participants were assigned to this scenario (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics did not differ significantly (Table 1). The modified method group had a significantly higher proportion of adequate ventilations (100% [Q1: 92.9%; Q3: 100%; 95% CI: 93.8–100%] vs 86.7% [Q1: 68.8%; Q3: 100%; 95% CI: 73.2–90.6%], p < 0.001) and a significantly lower proportion of hypoventilation (0% [Q1: 0%; Q3: 6.7%; 95% CI: 0–3.1%] vs 13.3% [Q1: 0%; Q3: 31.1%; 95% CI: 9.4–26.8%], p < 0.001). The frequency of hyperventilation did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.09) (Table 2).

Mean tidal volume was significantly greater in the modified method group. There was no significant difference in median inspiratory time, while all inspiratory times remained below 1 s. No significant differences were found in chest compression quality metrics. There were no significant differences in pause time and CCF between groups (Table 3). Consistent with the Manual Ventilation-No CPR scenario, the efficacy of the methods was not affected by order effects (Supplementary table 1). Moreover, subjective evaluations yielded results aligned with the Manual Ventilation-No CPR scenario (Fig. 3) (see details in Supplement D).

Discussion

In the present study, we compared the performance of a modified BVM ventilation method with the conventional method in simulated non-CPR and CPR settings. First, in non-CPR setting, our findings demonstrated that the modified method improved ventilation performance compared with the conventional method. Delivering tidal volumes within an appropriate range is essential to avoid physiological harms when performing ventilation.3, 4, 18, 19, 20, 21 In the context of the provision of supportive treatment of respiratory failure, excessive ventilation may conflict with lung-protective ventilation principles, increase the risk of lung injury, reduce venous return, and elevate right ventricular afterload.18 At the same time, hypoventilation is harmful and has been associated with decreased survival, as it reduces alveolar ventilation and impairs oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange.4 Thus, when many previous studies had focused on tidal volume, we placed greater emphasis on ventilation performance in terms of the proportion of adequate ventilations.11, 12 The modified method demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of adequate ventilation, largely because of a substantial reduction in hypoventilation. The modified method also delivered higher tidal volume. These findings are consistent with existing literature showing that the conventional one-handed EC-clamp method frequently leads to hypoventilation due to mask leak, and a two-handed technique generally provide a more effective mask seal and reduce leakage.11, 12, 22 Thus, the advantages of our modified method likely stem from its improved seal.10 Although the modified method could theoretically increase the risk of excessive ventilation, as chest-driven bag compression can generate greater force and the operator’s viewing angle makes chest rise less apparent, our results showed that it did not increase the risk of hyperventilation, and even the maximal volume (612 mL) is generally unlikely to cause additional lung injury except in conditions such as ARDS (<10 mL/kg).23, 24

A previous study proposed a novel two-handed BVM method using a pedal-operated compressor that allows a single practitioner to achieve effective ventilation, but this approach requires specialized equipment.21 In essence, compared to existing methods, our modified BVM ventilation method enables a single healthcare practitioner to achieve equivalent ventilation performance that typically requires two practitioners without any adjunctive device. As for whether other adjunctive devices that have been promoted in clinical settings would affect the efficacy of the modified method (e.g. portable ventilation feedback device such as EOlife), further study might be suggested.

Second, we further evaluated the performance of the modified BVM ventilation method during CPR. In the context of rescuing individuals in cardiac arrest, hyperventilation increases intrathoracic pressure, reduces cardiac output, and impairs coronary perfusion. It can also lead to gastric inflation, regurgitation, and aspiration.3, 4, 19, 20 On the other hand, hypoventilation has also been shown to decrease survival rates in the treatment of cardiac arrest.4 In our study, the modified method was shown to improve ventilation performance while providing comparable chest compression quality without compromising the efficiency of CPR (particularly in terms of CCF), during one-rescuer CPR, compared to the conventional method. The modified method showed a trend of a longer pause time (no significant difference), which might be associated with the longer time required to establish an effective mask seal, position the balloon, and compress it. However, our findings showed that the minimum CCF target recommended by the 2025 AHA Guidelines (60%) was achieved when using either modified or conventional methods (60% vs 60%), and whether higher CCF values within the range of 60–80% are associated with incremental clinical benefit remains controversial.25, 26, 27, 28 Thus, such a minor difference in pause time would not be expected to result in any meaningful clinical benefit. Nevertheless, further study with larger sample size might be helpful to evaluate whether the modified method would provide potential challenges to deliver ventilation in a timely manner, and whether it could be improved with additional training.

2025 AHA Guidelines state that when only one rescuer is available during 30:2 CPR with a bag-mask device, a one-handed technique is necessary to seal the mask and open the airway.4 However, our modified BVM ventilation method enables a single rescuer to maintain a two-handed mask seal while still compressing the bag when performing CPR, offering a potential alternative to current guidelines recommendations.

Our results further suggested that the modified method may reduce both cognitive and physical burden in both two scenarios, and the improved performance with the modified method may be partly associated with lower fatigue and stress levels. Participants also rated the modified method as easier to learn and preferred it over the conventional method, suggesting that the modified method may not pose a significant technical barrier, even for less-experienced practitioners. The subgroup analysis further indicated that the efficacy of the modified method remained constant by variations in body size.

Limitations

First, this study was conducted using a manikin model in a controlled environment. Clinical practice often involves patients with challenging airway characteristics such as redundant soft tissue due to obesity or craniofacial abnormality, and less controlled environments. Our study environments were quiet, well-lit, and free of distractions. In our study, the manikin was placed on a hospital bed with its head being adequately positioned at the top of the bed. This could be more pertinent for the context of in-hospital cardiac arrest, other than out-of-hospital, in which the patient may be lying on the floor for most of the time. Thus, our study scenario may have limited generalizability. Second, all participants were recruited from a single center using a convenience sample. We did not recruit highly experienced anesthetists or other healthcare providers. We also allowed either double EC or double TE grip during the modified BVM ventilation, which might introduce variability in mask seal and leakage. However, too few participants used double TE grip, making it not feasible to perform a subgroup analysis by grip method. Thus, there may be a potential lack of representativeness of our participants. Third, we did not measure physiological metrics such as intrathoracic or gastric inflation, we also did not measure clinical outcomes such as return of spontaneous circulation or survival. Fourth, the pre-specified sample size was not fully met, which might weaken statistical power.

Future studies enrolling more participants with a more diverse range of professional backgrounds and focusing on more physiological metrics and clinical outcomes, are warranted.

Conclusions

In this simulated manikin study, a novel modified BVM ventilation method demonstrated improved ventilation performance within an adequate range in both CPR and non-CPR settings. Future studies are warranted to further assess this proposed method, particularly in the case of clinical settings.
