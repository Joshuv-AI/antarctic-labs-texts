The impact of nanosized graphene oxide on the rheological, physical, mechanical, and antibacterial properties of condensation silicone dental impression materials
Authors: Mohsen Fakoori, Saeed Hesaraki, Nader Nezafati, Majid ghiass
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCondensation silicone dental impression materials are widely used but are limited by polymerization shrinkage, poor wettability, and susceptibility to bacterial contamination. This study evaluates the incorporation of nanosized graphene oxide (GO) as a multifunctional additive to address these issues.MethodsGO was incorporated into a self-formulated silicone matrix at concentrations from 0 to 0.5 vol%. The resulting composites were comprehensively characterized for their physical, mechanical, rheological, and antibacterial properties.ResultsThe incorporation of GO yielded significant, concentration-dependent improvements. Dimensional stability was markedly enhanced, with as little as 0.05% GO reducing 12-hour shrinkage from over 2% to just 0.06%. Handling characteristics were improved, with prolonged working times and enhanced flowability. Mechanical properties were also significantly affected, enabling targeted modification of hardness and flexibility; 0.1% GO increased Shore A hardness by over 14%, while 0.3% GO increased elongation at break by over 55%, demonstrating the ability to optimize the material for either rigidity or flexibility. Surface wettability was improved, with the contact angle decreasing by nearly 19%. Crucially, the composites demonstrated potent antibacterial activity, with 0.2% GO achieving up to 40% efficacy against S. mutans.ConclusionGO is a highly effective multifunctional additive that simultaneously addresses the key limitations of condensation silicones by enhancing dimensional accuracy, tailoring mechanical properties, improving handling, and increasing hygienic safety. These findings highlight the potential for developing a new class of advanced, high-performance dental impression materials.Keywords: Graphene oxide, Condensation silicone, Dental impression materials, Dimensional stability, Antibacterial activity

Introduction

The accuracy and precision of dental impressions are paramount in ensuring the fabrication of well-fitting prostheses, orthodontic appliances, and dental restorations [1]. Condensation silicones have emerged as popular materials because of their favorable handling characteristics and ability to replicate intricate anatomical details [2]. However, these materials present certain limitations. The release of volatile byproducts during polymerization can lead to dimensional inaccuracies, specifically shrinkage, which can compromise the fit and longevity of dental prostheses [3]. To reduce this shrinkage and achieve greater accuracy, clinicians often employ a two-step impression technique, utilizing a high-viscosity putty for an initial impression followed by a low-viscosity wash material to capture fine details [4]. While this technique enhances accuracy, it can be time-consuming and technique-sensitive, potentially introducing additional challenges in clinical practice [5]. Furthermore, the hydrophobic nature of these materials can impede the optimal wetting of oral tissues, sometimes resulting in incomplete impressions or voids [6, 7].

Furthermore, all dental impression materials, including condensation silicones, inevitably become contaminated with oral bacteria during the impression process, harboring potentially pathogenic microorganisms [8]. This necessitates precise disinfection procedures, which introduce further complexities. Disinfection protocols vary widely, with ongoing debates surrounding optimal agents, concentrations, and exposure times. Moreover, disinfection processes themselves can negatively impact impression materials, potentially altering their dimensional stability and surface characteristics [9–11]. These challenges underscore the need for innovative approaches to enhance the safety and performance of dental impression materials [12].

To enhance the properties of impression materials, researchers have explored the incorporation of particulate fillers. For instance, studies have successfully incorporated nanoparticles such as silver (Ag) and titanium (Ti) into polyvinyl siloxane, and silver vanadate (AgVO3​) into irreversible hydrocolloids to impart antimicrobial properties [12–14]. Similarly, other work has demonstrated that adding zinc oxide (ZnO) and chitosan nanoparticles can modify the physical and antimicrobial characteristics of vinyl siloxane ether impression materials [15]. The addition of fillers like diatomaceous earth (DE) and ZnO to condensation silicone has also been shown to tailor material properties, with ZnO notably improving dimensional stability and antibacterial activity [8]. Among advanced nanomaterials, graphene oxide (GO) has garnered significant attention as a multifunctional additive. GO is a two-dimensional, carbon-based nanomaterial created through the oxidative exfoliation of graphite [16]. Its structure is uniquely decorated with reactive oxygen-containing functional groups (hydroxyl, epoxy, carboxyl), which, combined with its high surface area and planar structure, makes it a promising candidate for polymer reinforcement [17]. Previous work has shown that incorporating GO into various polymer matrices can lead to significant improvements in mechanical properties, including tensile strength and stiffness, due to strong interfacial interactions with polymer chains [18–20].

Crucially, GO has an established track record of enhancing other dental biomaterials. Its incorporation has been shown to improve the physicomechanical properties of restorative materials like dental cements and resin composites [21–23], provide potent antibacterial activity in adhesives [24] and implant coatings [25], and demonstrate favorable biocompatibility for regenerative applications [26]. Despite its proven success in reinforcing dental composites, adhesives, and implants, the systematic investigation of GO as a multifunctional additive in condensation silicone impression materials has been largely overlooked. This represents a significant research gap, as GO’s known properties are ideally suited to address the specific challenges of these materials. Its high-aspect-ratio nanosheets could act as physical barriers to mitigate byproduct evaporation and subsequent shrinkage; its hydrophilic functional groups could directly improve poor wettability; and its intrinsic antibacterial nature could enhance the hygienic safety of the final impression [27–30].

Therefore, this study aimed to test the central hypothesis that incorporating nanosized GO would concurrently modify the handling characteristics, dimensional accuracy, mechanical properties, and antibacterial efficacy of a self-formulated condensation silicone. To achieve this, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the GO-modified silicones, assessing their surface morphology, rheological behavior, dimensional stability, tensile properties, hardness, wettability, and antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Streptococcus mutans. This research seeks to provide valuable insights into GO’s potential as a multifunctional additive, contributing to the development of advanced dental materials with improved performance and safety.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The pastes were prepared using the following materials: silica (SiO2, 25 μm average particle size, 2.65 g/cm³ density; Neutron® Pharmaceutical Chemical Company, Iran); calcium carbonate (CaCO3, 2.71 g/cm³ density); hydroxy-terminated polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS-OH, 3500 cSt viscosity, 0.97 g/cm³ density; Sigma-Aldrich®, Germany); graphene oxide (GO, 1.8 g/cm³ density, 3.4–7 nm thickness, 6–10 layers, 99% purity; US Research Nanomaterials); and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 208.33 g/mol molar mass; Sigma-Aldrich®, Germany). A commercial condensation silicone activator (Speedex Universal Activator, Coltène, Switzerland) was used to initiate the setting reaction.

To investigate the effects of GO, a series of pastes with compositions detailed in Table 1 were formulated. The volume percentages of PDMS-OH (39 vol%), TEOS (1 vol%), and CaCO3 (5.4 vol%) were held constant, while the GO content varied from 0 to 0.5 vol%. The SiO2 content was adjusted to maintain a total volume percentage of 100%. This concentration range was selected based on preliminary experiments. Specifically, our preliminary work showed that concentrations below 0.05 Vol% yielded minimal and statistically insignificant effects on key properties like setting time and dimensional stability. Conversely, concentrations above 0.5 Vol% led to a marked increase in paste viscosity and tackiness, which significantly hindered manual mixing and workability, likely due to nanosheet agglomeration. Therefore, the 0.05 to 0.5 Vol% range was identified as the optimal window for detailed characterization, balancing the potential benefits of GO with clinically acceptable handling characteristics.

Achieving a homogeneous dispersion of GO within the silicone matrix is crucial to prevent aggregation and ensure optimal performance. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a two-step dispersion protocol was employed. Initially, the required mass of GO was dispersed in 100 g of ethanol via probe sonication (15 min, 80% duty cycle). This step ensures the initial breakdown of GO aggregates. Following sonication, this GO/ethanol dispersion, along with SiO₂ and CaCO₃ powders, was ball-milled in a zirconia jar with zirconia balls (2 h, 400 rpm). This enhances GO deagglomeration and ensures thorough mixing of all fillers, which is essential for achieving the desired properties in the final silicone paste. The mixture then underwent a drying process in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h to remove the excess ethanol. To prepare the final silicone paste, amounts of PDMS-OH and TEOS (corresponding to the vol% values in Table 1) were slowly added to the dried mixture and stirred for 2 h using an IKA® RW 20 digital mechanical stirrer. This gradual incorporation and final stirring step ensures thorough and uniform dispersion of the polymer and crosslinker within the filler while minimizing air entrapment, resulting in a homogeneous, clump-free paste.

Die preparation

The tensile properties were evaluated via stainless steel molds, which were precisely laser-cut into the standard dumbbell shape specified by the Die C dimension in ASTM D412 (Fig. 2a). These molds were then attached to a polished HDPE substrate via an adhesive. The prepared pastes were loaded into the molds, with the HDPE sheet serving as the upper plate. To assess dimensional stability, a separate ring mold and ruled block were fabricated from PMMA. PMMA was chosen to ensure a smooth surface finish, and the design adhered to the ANSI/ADA No. 19 and ISO 4823 standards (Fig. 2b). The prepared pastes were loaded into the ring mold and then carefully shaped and compressed using the ruled block.

Characterization

Mixing, setting, and working times

The mixing, working, and setting times were determined for each formulation at 25 °C in accordance with ISO 4823 standards, with five repetitions performed for each test (n = 5). The process was initiated by dispensing the base paste and the commercial activator (Speedex Universal Activator) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixing time was measured using a stopwatch, recording the duration from the first contact of the components until a homogeneous mixture, indicated by a uniform color and texture, was achieved through manual kneading. The working time was measured concurrently, starting at the beginning of mixing and concluding when the material became noticeably less fluid and began to exhibit elastic properties, signifying the end of its clinical manipulability. The final setting time was determined using a Gillmore apparatus. A portion of the freshly mixed paste was placed into a ring-shaped mold. An initial-set type Gillmore needle, with a mass of 113.4 g and a flat tip diameter of 2.12 mm, was gently lowered onto the paste’s surface at 15-second intervals. The setting time was defined as the time point at which the needle tip no longer left a visible indentation on the material’s surface.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface morphology and elemental composition of the composites were analyzed using a TESCAN VEGA3 scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDAX) detector. Samples were examined 24 h after setting to assess the fully cured material. Elemental mapping was performed to visualize the distribution of key elements and confirm the homogeneity of the filler dispersion within the silicone matrix.

Rheology

The rheological properties of the uncured pastes were investigated using an Anton Paar Physica MCR 301 rheometer. All measurements were conducted at a controlled temperature of 25 °C using a 25 mm parallel plate geometry (PP25) with a fixed 1 mm gap. To characterize the flow behavior, rotational tests were performed by applying a logarithmically increasing shear rate from 0.1 to 1000 s⁻¹. To evaluate the viscoelastic characteristics, oscillatory frequency sweeps were conducted from 500 down to 0.05 rad/s. A constant strain amplitude of 0.3% was applied during these tests, which was confirmed to be within the linear viscoelastic region for all samples via preliminary strain sweep experiments. Each formulation was tested in triplicate (n = 3) to ensure the reproducibility of the results.

Dimensional stability

To evaluate dimensional stability, impressions were made via a standardized ruled block. Each impression was prepared by mixing the paste and activator at 25 °C. The resulting paste was then loaded into a ring-shaped mold and shaped using the ruled block. Following ISO 4823 guidelines, five replicate impressions (n = 5) were created for each GO concentration. After the impressions had set, they were analyzed via an OLYMPUS DP72 optical microscope. The distances between parallel lines (a, b, c, d1, d2) on the surface were measured (Fig. 2b). These measurements were taken at two time points: 30 min and 12 h after setting, to assess both short-term and long-term dimensional changes.

Tensile

The tensile properties of the materials were evaluated via a SANTAM STM-20 Universal Testing Machine (Iran) with a 2-ton capacity. The tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM D412 standards. Five samples (n = 5) were prepared for each GO concentration. Testing was conducted 24 h after setting to assess the fully cured samples. Each sample was initially subjected to a 1-Newton preload at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min. Following the preloading, the crosshead speed was increased to 500 mm/min until sample rupture occurred.

Shore a hardness test

Hardness was measured 24 h after setting via a Shore A-type analog portable durometer (Model SHD, SANTAM, Iran), following ASTM D-2240. Specimens were prepared with a thickness of at least 6 mm, as recommended by the standard. For each GO concentration, three samples were prepared (n = 3), and five hardness measurements were taken at different points on the flattest surface of each sample. All mechanical testing was performed at room temperature (25 °C).

Wettability

The surface wettability of the set impression material was assessed by measuring the static contact angle 24 h after setting. For each GO concentration, contact angle measurements were performed on five samples (n = 5) via an MTN9.3‒2021 optical tensiometer (from the Wetting and Interface Laboratory, Materials and Energy Research Center). The tensiometer was equipped with IrCA94 software for automated analysis and reporting.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

To analyze the crystalline structure of the samples, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed. A Seifert 3003 pts diffractometer (Germany) with a copper anode and Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.542 Å) was used to conduct the analysis on representative powder and composite samples 24 h after setting.

Antibacterial

The antibacterial activity of the composites was evaluated quantitatively against Gram-negative Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922) and Gram-positive Streptococcus mutans (IBRC-M 10682) based on the ISO 22196:2011 standard, with modifications. The test was initiated 24 h after specimen setting to ensure that the measured antimicrobial effects were attributable to the stabilized material and the incorporated GO, rather than the transient bactericidal effect of the evaporating ethanol byproduct. A fresh bacterial culture was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and diluted in 1/500 Nutrient Broth (NB) to a final test inoculum of approximately 1.5 × 10⁶ CFU/ml. This suspension was applied to the surface of each sample, which was then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and ≥ 90% relative humidity. After incubation, viable bacteria were recovered and serially diluted in sterile 0.9% NaCl solution before being plated for enumeration. For this, Plate Count Agar (1.5% w/v, 15 g/L) was dispensed into sterile 100 mm Petri dishes. The test was performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the average results are reported. Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2021. Pairwise comparisons between each experimental GO-containing sample and the Control sample were conducted for all quantitative properties using two-tailed independent samples t-tests, assuming unequal variances. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. In figures and tables, results significantly different from the control are noted as such (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Mixing, working, and setting times

The incorporation of graphene oxide (GO) significantly influenced the processing times of the condensation silicone, as shown in Fig. 3. The mean mixing time for the control sample was 22 s. All GO-modified composites exhibited a significantly shorter mixing time of approximately 19 s (p < 0.01), a reduction of nearly 14% (Fig. 3a). In contrast, GO’s addition led to a statistically significant increase in the working time (Fig. 3b). The working time increased from 46 s for the control to a maximum of 52 s for the 0.5% GO sample, an extension of approximately 13% (p < 0.01). The setting time demonstrated a clear concentration-dependent increase with higher GO content (Fig. 3c). While the 0.05% GO sample showed no significant difference from the control (189 s vs. 186 s), setting times were significantly prolonged at GO concentrations of 0.1% and higher. The most substantial effect was observed with the 0.5% GO sample, which had a setting time of 246 s, representing a 32% increase compared to the control (p < 0.001).

Surface morphology and elemental analysis

SEM analysis first characterized the morphology of the individual filler powders used in the formulation (Fig. 4). Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) particles were irregularly shaped with a rough texture and sizes ranging from approximately 1–10 μm (Fig. 4a). Silica (SiO₂) particles were angular with sharp edges and sizes ranging from approximately 0.5 to 10 μm (Fig. 4b). Graphene oxide (GO) appeared as characteristic thin, wrinkled, sheet-like structures with a high surface area (Fig. 4c). Analysis of the set composites revealed that GO content influenced the final surface topography (Fig. 5). The micrograph of the control sample, without GO, exhibited a relatively smooth and uniform surface (Fig. 5a), while the addition of GO led to a progressive increase in microscale surface roughness in the corresponding micrographs as its concentration increased from 0.05% to 0.5% (Fig. 5b-d). EDAX analysis confirmed the elemental composition, with spectra for all samples showing peaks for silicon (Si), calcium (Ca), and oxygen (O). The intensity of the carbon (C) peak in the spectra increased proportionally with the GO concentration. Furthermore, elemental mapping showed an intense and uniform distribution of the carbon signal with increasing GO content, alongside a uniform distribution of Si and Ca in the samples (Fig. 6a-d).

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of the composites are summarized in Fig. 7. All samples exhibited pronounced shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) behavior, as their viscosity decreased with an increasing shear rate (Fig. 7a). The control sample consistently showed the highest viscosity at low shear rates. The addition of GO resulted in a clear, concentration-dependent reduction in viscosity, torque, storage modulus (G’), loss modulus (G’’), and complex viscosity across the entire range of shear rates and frequencies tested (Fig. 7a-e). For all samples, the storage modulus (G’) was greater than the loss modulus (G’’) across the entire frequency range tested.

Dimensional stability

The addition of GO significantly enhanced the dimensional stability of the condensation silicone (Fig. 8). For instance, in the a-c measurement set (Fig. 8c), the control group exhibited a shrinkage of 2.05% between the 30-minute and 12-hour time points. In stark contrast, the 0.05% GO sample showed a shrinkage of only 0.06% over the same period, demonstrating a marked improvement in stability. This trend of significantly reduced shrinkage for all GO-containing samples was observed across all measurement sets, particularly at the 12-hour mark (p < 0.001) (Fig. 8a-d).

Tensile properties

The tensile properties of the composites were significantly influenced by the GO concentration, as shown in the stress-strain curves (Fig. 9) and summarized in Table 2. The control sample exhibited the highest tensile strength, with a force at break of 27.96 ± 0.8 N. The incorporation of GO at all concentrations resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the force at break (p < 0.001 for all samples), with the 0.05% GO sample showing the lowest value of 15.21 ± 0.6 N. In terms of ductility, the 0.3 Vol% GO sample demonstrated the highest elongation at break (56.97 ± 2.0%), a highly significant increase of over 55% compared to the control (p < 0.001). Based on the initial slope of the stress-strain curves, the control sample was the stiffest (elastic modulus of 4.8 ± 0.2 MPa), and all GO concentrations resulted in a significantly more flexible material, as evidenced by a lower elastic modulus (p < 0.05 or better for all samples). The 0.3 Vol% GO sample was the most flexible, exhibiting a 58% reduction in its elastic modulus to 2.03 ± 0.1 MPa (p < 0.001).

Wettability

The addition of GO significantly decreased the contact angle of the silicone surface, as shown in Fig. 10. The control sample was hydrophobic, with a contact angle of 109.23° ± 2°. A non-linear trend was observed with the addition of GO. The largest reduction in contact angle occurred with 0.05% GO, which reduced the contact angle by nearly 19% to 88.93° ± 1°. At higher concentrations, the contact angle gradually increased again, reaching 98.20° ± 1° for the 0.5% GO sample. Compared to the control, the reduction in contact angle for all GO-modified samples was statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Antibacterial activity

As shown in Table 4; Fig. 12, the GO-modified samples exhibited significant antibacterial activity in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas the control samples showed minimal activity (~ 7%). The antibacterial efficacy peaked at the 0.2% GO concentration, producing the highest activity against both E. coli (27 ± 2%) and the key oral pathogen S. mutans (40 ± 2%). This optimal concentration also corresponded to one of the highest levels of statistical significance (p < 0.001). At concentrations above this 0.2% peak, the measured antibacterial activity against both bacterial strains decreased. Nonetheless, a statistically significant reduction in bacterial growth was observed for all GO-modified composites when compared to the control.

Discussion

Influence on material processing and curing kinetics

The addition of GO created a clinically relevant trade-off by simultaneously prolonging the material’s workability and its final setting time in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3b, c). This delay in curing kinetics is attributed to GO interfering with the condensation polymerization reaction [31]. The numerous oxygen-containing functional groups on GO’s surface create steric hindrance and engage in competitive hydrogen bonding with the PDMS-OH oligomers, reducing the availability of reactive sites for crosslinking with TEOS [32, 33]. While a longer working time is clinically advantageous, the prolonged setting time can be a limitation. Conversely, GO significantly reduced the mixing time (Fig. 3a), an effect driven by a notable reduction in the paste’s initial viscosity (Fig. 7a). The GO nanosheets act as internal lubricants, physically separating the PDMS-OH polymer chains and disrupting intermolecular forces like hydrogen bonding. This action reduces the material’s internal friction, facilitating a more rapid and uniform dispersion during manual mixing [34].

Microstructure and composition analysis

The SEM analysis provides crucial insight into how microstructure influences the material’s bulk properties. While the irregular and angular morphology of the conventional CaCO₃ and SiO₂ fillers (Fig. 4a, b) establishes the baseline viscosity and mechanical reinforcement, the introduction of GO, with its high-aspect-ratio, sheet-like structure (Fig. 4c), fundamentally alters these particle-polymer interactions. Increasing GO content leads to a progressive increase in microscale surface roughness (Fig. 5) is a direct result of these embedded nanosheets disrupting the polymer surface [35]. This altered topography is a key factor contributing to the significant improvement in wettability (discussed in Sect. 4.5). Furthermore, the elemental mapping (Fig. 6) provides clear evidence of a homogeneous carbon dispersion, indicating the effectiveness of the two-step sonication and ball-milling protocol in preventing large-scale agglomeration. This uniform distribution is critical, as it not only prevents the formation of large agglomerates that could act as stress concentration points but also ensures that GO can effectively interact with the polymer chains to influence rheology and curing kinetics [31].

Rheological behavior and flow characteristics

The pronounced shear-thinning behavior observed in all samples (Fig. 7a) is highly desirable for a dental impression material, as it allows the paste to flow easily into fine anatomical details under the pressure of application, yet maintain its shape without slumping at rest. The key finding from this analysis is that GO acts as an effective rheological modifier, significantly reducing the overall viscosity and viscoelastic moduli of the paste [36]. This enhanced flowability can be attributed to a lubrication effect at the particle level. As schematically shown in Fig. 13, the high-aspect-ratio GO nanosheets likely adsorb onto the surfaces of the larger SiO₂ and CaCO₃ filler particles. This process can reduce inter-particle friction and shield the fillers from the polymer matrix, disrupting strong particle-polymer entanglements. This action facilitates easier particle movement within the paste under shear, lowering the material’s internal friction and resistance to flow [37, 38]. However, while this reduction in viscosity is clinically beneficial, the rheological effects are complex. The observed increase in the loss modulus (G’’) at higher GO concentrations, particularly at lower frequencies, suggests increased energy dissipation, likely due to the breakdown of minor particle agglomerations under oscillation. This finding indicates that careful optimization of the GO content is necessary to achieve an ideal balance of properties [39, 40].

Mechanism of enhanced dimensional stability

The enhanced dimensional stability is a critical outcome, as it directly addresses the primary limitation of condensation silicones: polymerization shrinkage from the evaporation of volatile byproducts like ethanol [41]. Our results show that GO effectively mitigates this shrinkage by acting as an internal barrier within the silicone matrix [42]. We propose a multi-faceted mechanism where the high-aspect-ratio GO nanosheets first create complex and tortuous pathways, physically hindering the diffusion and escape of ethanol molecules [43, 44]. Simultaneously, stability is enhanced through chemical and physical trapping; the abundant oxygen-containing functional groups on GO surfaces capture byproduct molecules via hydrogen bonding [45–47], while the layered structure of the nanosheets allows for their intercalation [48, 49]. This trapping leads to a more controlled release of byproducts over time, mitigating the abrupt shrinkage [50], seen in the control sample and improving long-term accuracy [51, 52]. The non-linear improvement in stability seen in Fig. 8 suggests this mechanism is concentration-dependent, likely due to the saturation of available trapping sites [51] —both functional groups and intercalation layers—on the nanosheets at higher loadings [53].

Mechanical properties under tension

The tensile test results reveal a complex, dual role for GO in modifying the material’s mechanical behavior. The general trend of decreasing tensile strength and elastic modulus can be attributed to GO’s interference with polymer network formation [36]. By sterically hindering the hydroxyl active ends of the PDMS chains, GO appears to disrupt the crosslinking reaction, leading to a lower crosslink density. A less crosslinked network inherently possesses fewer chain entanglements, which directly weakens the material and makes it more flexible, resulting in a lower modulus. This proposed mechanism is strongly supported by the longer setting times observed at higher GO concentrations [54]. However, this weakening effect is countered by a significant increase in ductility, especially for the 0.3% GO sample, which suggests an additional mechanism is at play. At this optimal concentration, the well-dispersed GO nanosheets may act as slip planes or spacers between polymer chains [55]. This configuration, combined with the reduced entanglement from incomplete crosslinking, facilitates chain slippage under load, allowing the material to stretch more before failing and thus enhancing its elongation at break.

Hardness and reinforcement effects

The non-linear relationship between GO concentration and hardness suggests a dual role for the nanosheets, modifying the baseline hardness established by the conventional inorganic fillers (calcium carbonate and silica). The initial increase in hardness, which peaks at the 0.1% GO concentration, is attributable to a classic reinforcement mechanism. At low concentrations, well-dispersed, rigid GO nanosheets act as effective fillers, resisting indentation and increasing the composite’s overall hardness [56]. However, this reinforcing effect is overtaken by two competing factors at higher concentrations. First, as GO content increases, the nanosheets are more likely to agglomerate, creating stress concentration points that reduce the matrix’s resistance to indentation [31]. Second, as established in the tensile analysis, excessive GO can interfere with and reduce the overall crosslink density, leading to an intrinsically softer material [57]. This interplay explains the existence of an optimal concentration (0.1% GO) to achieve maximum hardness. Furthermore, the hardness observed in the control sample is attributable to its calcium and silica content, which was confirmed through EDAX analysis, aligning with the well-established role of these fillers in enhancing the mechanical properties of polymers [58–60].

Surface wettability

The inherent hydrophobicity of the control sample is expected, given the nonpolar methyl groups on the PDMS polymer backbone [61–64]. The significant improvement in wettability upon GO addition is a direct result of the hydrophilic, oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl, carboxyl) present on the GO nanosheets [65]. When well-dispersed, these groups orient at the surface and interact with water molecules, lowering the contact angle [66]. However, the non-linear trend, where wettability slightly decreases at the highest GO concentration (0.5%), is also significant. This is likely because at higher loadings, the nanosheets tend to aggregate—a phenomenon confirmed by our SEM analysis (Fig. 5d)—due to van der Waals forces and π-π interactions [67]. This agglomeration can reduce the effective surface area of the hydrophilic functional groups by masking them within the aggregates, thus slightly diminishing their positive effect on the overall surface wettability [68]. This explanation is further supported by molecular dynamics simulations in the literature, which show that as the concentration of oxygen-containing groups on a surface increases, their interaction ranges can overlap, leading to a less pronounced effect on wettability at higher concentrations [69].

Crystallographic analysis

The XRD analysis confirms the composite’s basic composition and clarifies the role of the GO filler. The patterns (Fig. 11) verify that the primary inorganic fillers are crystalline quartz and calcite, providing baseline mechanical reinforcement [60]. While a distinct peak for GO itself is not expected due to its very low concentration and poorly crystalline nature, the absence of any new diffraction peaks in the GO-modified samples is a significant finding. This confirms that GO acts as an inert physical filler and does not chemically react to form new crystalline compounds. This finding supports the central hypothesis that GO’s influence stems from physical interactions rather than chemical reactions within the matrix.

Antibacterial properties and mechanisms

The results demonstrate that GO imparts significant, concentration-dependent antibacterial activity [70], a finding consistent with previous studies [25, 71, 72]. At lower concentrations, the limited availability of GO nanosheets restricts their interaction with bacterial cells. As the concentration increases towards the observed optimum of 0.2%, more nanosheets become available to damage bacteria through several well-established mechanisms [26, 73–75]. These include (1) physical disruption, where the nanosheets’ sharp edges cause structural changes, lipid extraction, and membrane destabilization, leading to cell death [76]; (2) oxidative stress from the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [77]; and (3) interactions involving oxygen-containing functional groups, which can directly disrupt metabolic processes or hinder bacterial adhesion [24]. The subsequent decrease in activity at concentrations above 0.2% is likely due to the agglomeration of GO nanosheets [70], which reduces the effective surface area for bacterial interaction and limits ROS generation, thereby diminishing the overall antibacterial effect [78].

Conclusion

In this study, the incorporation of nanosized graphene oxide (GO) into a condensation silicone dental impression material was shown to be a highly effective strategy for multifunctional enhancement. The addition of GO yielded composites with significantly improved dimensional stability, targeted modifications to mechanical properties with optimal concentrations for both hardness and flexibility, enhanced surface wettability, and potent antibacterial activity, all while improving flowability. From a clinical perspective, these findings are highly significant. The marked improvement in dimensional stability directly addresses one of the primary drawbacks of condensation silicones, promising more accurate impressions and better-fitting final restorations. The imparted antibacterial properties could reduce the risk of cross-contamination and the need for aggressive disinfection protocols that can degrade impression accuracy. However, this study identified important trade-offs, namely the reduction in tensile strength and the prolonged setting times, which must be considered. Furthermore, these results are based on in vitro analyses, and the material’s long-term stability and biocompatibility require further investigation before clinical application. Future work should focus on optimizing the GO concentration to balance the material’s properties and confirming its biocompatibility for clinical use.
