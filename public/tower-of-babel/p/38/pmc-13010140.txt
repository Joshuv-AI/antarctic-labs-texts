YqeK governs the acquisition and removal of nucleoside tetraphosphate caps in Firmicutes
Authors: Deblina Basu, Shakti Ingle, Daniel J Luciano, Joel G Belasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNucleoside tetraphosphate (Np4) caps have heretofore been reported in only one bacterial species: the model Gram-negative organism Escherichia coli, where RNAs acquire this epitranscriptomic modification during disulfide stress or when the dinucleoside tetraphosphate (Np4A) hydrolase ApaH is eliminated by mutation. Here, we show that caps of this kind also appear on RNA 5′ ends in the prototypical Gram-positive organism Bacillus subtilis upon deleting the yqeK gene, which encodes an unrelated Np4A hydrolase. Present on diverse mRNAs irrespective of the identity of their 5′-terminal nucleotide, these caps are acquired in B. subtilis at least in part by Np4A incorporation at the 5′ end of nascent transcripts during transcription initiation. YqeK suppresses Np4 capping by functioning both as an Np4A hydrolase that reduces the availability of Np4As for incorporation into RNA and as a decapping enzyme that can deprotect Np4-capped mRNA in B. subtilis by converting those caps to 5′-terminal diphosphates, thereby accelerating capped mRNA degradation. Disulfide stress increases Np4 capping in B. subtilis ∆yqeK mutants. Moreover, the elevated level of Np4 capping in these mutants is accompanied by an increased ability of B. subtilis to survive disulfide stress, suggesting a physiologically protective impact of Np4As and/or Np4 capping under these conditions.

Introduction

First discovered more than 50 years ago, dinucleoside 5′, 5′′′-P1, P4-tetraphosphates (Np4As) are now known to be present in all realms of life [1–3]. Comprising two nucleosides linked 5′-to-5′ by a tetraphosphate bridge (Fig. 1A), they are produced by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases when the aminoacyl-adenylate intermediate reacts with a nucleoside triphosphate rather than with transfer RNA (tRNA) [1, 4]. In eukaryotes Np4As have been implicated in neuronal signaling, immune responses, and cardiovascular function [5], while in bacteria their cellular abundance has been correlated with a number of interesting physiological changes, including reduced motility, greater sensitivity to heat shock and oxidative stress, increased vulnerability to aminoglycoside antibiotics, reduced sporulation, impaired control of biofilm formation, and a diminished ability to invade mammalian cells [6–11]. However, the mechanisms by which elevating the cellular concentration of Np4As results in these effects are not clear.

Because their concentration rises in bacterial cells experiencing various stresses, especially disulfide stress induced by treatment with cysteine crosslinkers [12], Np4As are sometimes described as alarmones, i.e. as second messengers for stress [13]. However, no signaling receptor to which they bind and elicit a cellular response has been identified in bacteria [14]. Indeed, the only bacterial protein whose activity is known to be affected by Np4A binding is the metabolic enzyme inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, which catalyzes an early step in the biosynthesis of guanosine nucleotides and is non-competitively inhibited by Ap4A in Bacillus subtilis [15].

Recently we reported that, in Escherichia coli cells containing elevated concentrations of Np4As as a result of disulfide stress, nucleoside tetraphosphate (Np4) caps (Fig. 1A) are observed at high levels on the 5′ termini of a variety of transcripts, including messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and small RNAs (sRNAs) [16]. These caps are acquired during transcription initiation when RNA polymerase incorporates an Np4A instead of an NTP at the 5′ end of a nascent transcript [17]. They can subsequently be removed by either of two Np4-decapping enzymes, the PPP family member ApaH or the Nudix protein RppH [16, 18, 19], both of which also function as Np4A hydrolases [6, 20, 21]. Of this pair of enzymes, ApaH is the principal source of these two activities in E. coli, which normally keep the cellular concentration of Np4As and Np4-capped mRNA extremely low [16, 22]. However, ApaH is inactivated by inducers of disulfide stress, such as cadmium ions or diamide, which explains how exposing E. coli to these stress inducers causes a marked increase in the cellular concentration of Np4As and a concomitant increase in the level of Np4-capped mRNA, such that their concentrations come to rival those of NTPs and uncapped mRNA, respectively [16, 23]. These effects of disulfide stress can be mimicked in unstressed E. coli cells by deleting the gene that encodes ApaH [16, 22]. In contrast, disulfide stress has only a modest impact on RppH activity [19].

In E. coli, Np4 cap removal by ApaH triggers mRNA decay via a 5′-end-dependent pathway in which ApaH-mediated decapping generates an RNA 5′ terminus that is diphosphorylated, which is then converted by RppH to a monophosphorylated 5′ end that can stimulate mRNA degradation by recruiting the endonuclease RNase E. Consequently, in E. coli mutants lacking ApaH, Np4-capped mRNAs that would otherwise be targeted for rapid degradation via this pathway decay more slowly than their uncapped counterparts [16]. The changes in protein synthesis resulting from the increased stability of these capped transcripts may help to explain the various physiological properties described above as being correlated with high Np4A levels, which are each phenotypes of proteobacterial apaH deletion mutants.

Whether elevated Np4A levels lead to Np4 capping in bacterial species other than E. coli has not been reported. Although orthologs of ApaH are widespread in Gammaproteobacteria like E. coli and Betaproteobacteria like Neisseria meningitidis, they are absent from most other bacterial species [24]. In Firmicutes such as Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus mutans, Np4A levels are instead controlled by YqeK, an entirely unrelated Np4A hydrolase that is a member of the HD protein family [24]. Functionally, YqeK resembles ApaH in that they both cut Np4As symmetrically to generate two nucleoside diphosphate molecules [20, 24, 25]. However, structurally and catalytically the two enzymes are quite different. Whereas ApaH comprises a combination of α helices and β sheets and relies on two Mn2+ ions for catalysis [26, 27], YqeK is predominantly helical and harbors two Fe3+ ions in its active site [25, 28]. In B. subtilis mutants lacking YqeK, levels of Np4As increase markedly, and biofilm formation is affected [24, 29]. Similarly, deleting the yqeK gene of S. mutans impairs biofilm formation and exopolysaccharide production while also altering gene expression [30]. Little else is known about the biological function and physiological impact of YqeK.

The ribonucleases that participate in mRNA degradation also differ significantly between E. coli and B. subtilis [31]. Although both employ a low-specificity endonuclease for mRNA turnover (RNase E in E. coli and RNase Y in B. subtilis), these enzymes are unrelated to one another. Moreover, whereas E. coli takes advantage of the affinity of RNase E for monophosphorylated 5′ ends to facilitate the endonucleolytic degradation of RNA decay intermediates bearing a 5′ monophosphate [32], B. subtilis lacks an ortholog of RNase E and instead relies for this purpose on RNase J1, a 5′-monophosphate-dependent exonuclease that is absent from E. coli [33, 34].

To better understand the molecular mechanisms by which YqeK impacts the physiology of Firmicutes, we have now investigated its influence on mRNA 5′ ends in B. subtilis. Our experimental results indicate that, in B. subtilis mutants lacking YqeK, a variety of mRNAs become Np4-capped as a consequence of Np4A incorporation during transcription initiation. Like ApaH, YqeK functions as both an Np4A hydrolase and an Np4 decapping enzyme. Therefore, in its absence, Np4 capping impedes the decay of mRNA ordinarily degraded in B. subtilis by a 5′-end-dependent mechanism. Together with previous studies in E. coli, these findings indicate that, despite considerable enzymatic diversity, the ability to install Np4 caps on RNA is widespread among disparate bacterial phyla.

Materials and methods

Strains and plasmids

All of the experiments in B. subtilis were performed with strain 1A2 (a Trp+ derivative of strain 168 [35]) and derivatives thereof from which the yqeK, rppH, and/or yhxA gene had been deleted. These in-frame gene deletions were made by transforming 1A2 with PCR-amplified genomic DNA from Bacillus Genetic Stock Center strain BKE25630, BKE30630, or BKE09260 and then excising the erythromycin-resistance cassette by transformation with the temperature-sensitive plasmid pDR244 [36], which was subsequently removed. Complementation of the yqeK deletion was accomplished by integrating a copy of the yqeK gene encoding the wild-type protein or a catalytically inactive mutant (yqeK-H109A) into the amyE locus. In each case, the complementing yqeK translational unit, including its natural Shine–Dalgarno element, was first placed under the control of the Pspac promoter in a variant of integration vector pDR66 [37] from which the intrinsic Shine–Dalgarno element downstream of the Pspac promoter had been deleted to prevent overexpression of yqeK. The B. subtilis ∆yqeK strain was then transformed with each of the resulting plasmids, and chromosomal integrants were selected on plates containing 5 µg/ml chloramphenicol and confirmed by PCR.

Plasmid pBYGfl contains a copy of the wild-type yhxA-glpP operon along with kanamycin- and ampicillin-resistance genes and replication origins compatible with B. subtilis and E. coli [34]. To examine the effect of nucleotide substitutions near the transcription start site of the yhxA-glpP operon, pBYGfl was modified with point mutations either at position –1 (C → A, G, or T) or at positions –1, +1, and + 2 (CAG → GNC, where N = A, G, C, or T) (Supplementary Table S7). Besides making it possible to analyze the effect of sequence variation at positions –1 and +1, the use of these plasmids facilitated the detection of cellular yhxA-glpP mRNA, which otherwise would have been present in RNA extracts at a concentration barely detectable by northern blotting.

Plasmids pPlacYqeK and pPlacBsRppH6 are derivatives of pRNG3 [38] that were used to produce hexahistidine-tagged YqeK and B. subtilis RppH (BsRppH) in E. coli under the control of an IPTG-inducible lacUV5 promoter (Supplementary Table S7). Codon-substitution variants of these two plasmids enabled the production of catalytically inactive YqeK-H109A and BsRppH-E68Q in E. coli.

Growth conditions

Before extracting RNA, B. subtilis cells were grown to mid-log phase (OD650 = 0.3) at 37°C in basal limitation medium (BLM) [39] containing 0.75% fructose (w/v) and 0.4 mM KH2PO4 but no additional amino acids. To measure mRNA half-lives, RNA was extracted from B. subtilis at time intervals after treating a mid-log phase culture with rifampicin (0.2 mg/ml) to block transcription. To induce disulfide stress, diamide (2 mM) was added to a mid-log phase culture, and RNA was extracted 0 and 30 min thereafter or the cell density was monitored at 650 nm for an additional 3 h.

RNA extraction

Total RNA was isolated from B. subtilis by treating cells with lysozyme (10 mg/ml) for 1 h in ice-cold buffer A (0.3 M sucrose, 0.01 M sodium acetate, pH 4.5, 10 mM EDTA) and then extracting RNA with hot phenol, as described previously [40].

Detection and quantification of capped and uncapped RNA

Capped RNA was separated from uncapped RNA by site-specific RNA cleavage with a deoxyribozyme 51–76 nt from the 5′ end followed by electrophoresis through polyacrylamide gel matrix covalently modified with 3-acrylamidophenylboronic acid. The transcript of interest in total cellular RNA (10 µg) was first annealed to a complementary 10–23 deoxyribozyme (400 pmol; Supplementary Table S8) [41] by heating the mixture (18 μl) to 85°C for 5 min in water and then cooling it slowly to 30°C. A solution (2 μl) containing Tris–HCl, pH 7.5 (500 mM), MgCl2 (100 mM), and dithiothreitol (10 mM) was then added, and RNA cleavage was allowed to proceed for 3.5 h at 37°C. The reaction was quenched by adding 20 µl of a formamide-tracking dye mixture containing 20 mM EDTA, and the products were subjected to electrophoresis on a denaturing gel containing 5.6% acrylamide:bisacrylamide (19:1), 0.25% 3-acrylamidophenylboronic acid [42], 7 M urea, and 0.1 M Tris-acetate, pH 9.0, which also served as the running buffer. The gel was electroblotted onto a positively charged nylon membrane, and the blot was probed with a 32P-labeled oligonucleotide complementary to the 5′ fragment of the transcript of interest (Supplementary Table S8). Radioactive bands were visualized with a Typhoon RGB Imager (Cytiva), and the band intensities were quantified by using ImageQuant TL software (Cytiva). The percentage of the transcript that was capped was calculated from the ratio of band intensities (capped versus uncapped). mRNA half-lives were calculated by linear regression analysis of band intensity as a function of time after blocking transcription.

RNA synthesis by in vitro transcription

Ap3-, Ap4-, and Ap5-capped yhxA RNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription with T7 RNA polymerase (New England Biolabs) in the presence of Ap3A, Ap4A, or Ap5A, respectively, using a PCR-amplified DNA template (Supplementary Table S8) encoding the first 404 nt of yhxA mRNA and bearing a T7 ϕ2.5 promoter [16]. Their monophosphorylated, diphosphorylated, and triphosphorylated counterparts were synthesized by in vitro transcription in the presence of AMP, ADP, or ATP, respectively. In each case, the concentration of the ApnA (3 mM) or mononucleotide (5 mM) intended for 5′-terminal incorporation in place of ATP was well above that of ATP (0.25 mM). The resulting transcripts were purified by gel electrophoresis, extraction of the band of interest into a 10 mM EDTA solution at 4°C overnight, and ethanol precipitation.

Ap4-capped, triphosphorylated, diphosphorylated, and monophosphorylated AG(CU)13 bearing a 32P-labeled phosphate between the first and second nucleotide was synthesized by in vitro transcription with T7 RNA polymerase in the presence of CTP, UTP, [α-32P] GTP, and either Ap4A, ATP, ADP, or AMP, respectively, using a chemically synthesized DNA template (Supplementary Table S8) [16]. The resulting transcripts were purified by gel electrophoresis, extraction of the band of interest into a 10 mM EDTA solution at 4°C overnight, and ethanol precipitation.

Protein purification

YqeK, YqeK-H109A, B. subtilis RppH (BsRppH), BsRppH-E68Q, E. coli ApaH, Schizosaccharomyces pombe Pce1, and E. coli RppH, each bearing an amino-terminal hexahistidine tag, were overproduced in E. coli BL21 (DE3) rne-131 ∆rna containing plasmid pPlacYqeK, pPlacYqeK-H109A, pPlacBsRppH6 [34], pPlacBsRppH6-E68Q [34], pET-ApaH [16], pSMT3-Pce1 [43], or pPlacRppH6 [44], respectively (Supplementary Table S7), and were purified by affinity chromatography on TALON beads (Takara Bio). Hexahistidine-tagged T4 DNA ligase was purified from E. coli strain MIC2067 [45] containing plasmid pPlacT4L [46] by affinity chromatography on TALON beads followed by anion-exchange chromatography.

esCAPade

To determine the number of phosphates between the cap nucleoside and the first transcribed nucleoside of capped RNA, total RNA extracted from B. subtilis ∆yqeK ∆yhxA cells containing plasmid pYhxA-GlpP C-1A (10 µg) was analyzed in parallel with Ap3-, Ap4-, and Ap5-capped yhxA RNA standards that had been synthesized by in vitro transcription (5 ng spiked into 10 µg of total RNA from B. subtilis ∆yqeK ∆yhxA cells). All samples were analyzed in triplicate, including biological replicates of the cellular RNA. To convert any uncapped 5′ ends to unreactive hydroxyls, the RNA samples (10 µg) were first treated with Quick CIP (10 units, New England Biolabs) for 1 h at 37°C in a solution (20 µl) containing rCutSmart buffer and rRNasin (2 units/µl, Promega). The reactions were quenched with EDTA (215 µl of a 3.5 mM solution), and the RNA was recovered by phenol extraction and ethanol precipitation. Next, the RNA samples were decapped with ApaH (100–200 nM) for 1 h at 37°C in a solution (20 µl) containing of HEPES, pH 7.6 (20 mM), MgCl2 (5 mM), dithiothreitol (1 mM), and rRNasin (2 units/µl). The reactions were quenched with EDTA (215 µl of a 3.5 mM solution), and the RNA was recovered by phenol extraction and ethanol precipitation. The RNA samples were then analyzed by PACO and PABLO to quantify the fraction of the decapped yhxA-glpP 5′ ends that were diphosphorylated (able to be recapped by S. pombe Pce1) or monophosphorylated [able to undergo splinted ligation to a DNA oligonucleotide by T4 DNA ligase (Supplementary Table S8)], respectively [47]. As standards for comparison, triphosphorylated, diphosphorylated, and monophosphorylated yhxA RNAs that had been synthesized by in vitro transcription and spiked into total RNA from B. subtilis ∆yqeK ∆yhxA cells were analyzed in parallel. The resulting Northern blots were probed with a 5′ end-labeled oligonucleotide complementary to yhxA mRNA (Supplementary Table S8). Bands were visualized on a Typhoon RGB imager (Cytiva) and quantified by using ImageQuant TL software (Cytiva) (Supplementary Fig. S1). Calculations based on these relative band intensities (Supplementary Tables S1–S6) were performed by using ciCalc software (doi: 10.5281/zenodo.17966367), as previously described [16].

RNA decapping by purified ApaH, YqeK, and B. subtilis RppH

Total B. subtilis RNA (10 µg) from wild-type, ∆yqeK, or ∆rppH cells was treated or not treated at 37°C with E. coli ApaH (2 μM) in a buffer (20 µl) containing HEPES, pH 7.6 (20 mM), MgCl2 (5 mM), dithiothreitol (1 mM), and glycerol (1% v/v). In other experiments, total B. subtilis RNA (10 µg) from ∆yqeK ∆yhxA cells that contained a plasmid-borne copy of the yhxA-glpP operon bearing a C-1A promoter mutation was treated at 37°C (i) with YqeK or YqeK-H109A (1 µM) in a buffer (20 µl) containing HEPES, pH 7.6 (40 mM), NaCl (250 mM), and dithiothreitol (1 mM) or (ii) with BsRppH or BsRppH-E68Q (2 μM) in a buffer (20 µl) containing HEPES, pH 7.6 (20 mM), MgCl2 (10 mM), dithiothreitol (1 mM), and glycerol (1% v/v). Alternatively, Ap4-capped yhxA RNA (2 ng) synthesized by in vitro transcription was combined with total RNA (10 μg) from B. subtilis ∆yhxA cells and treated similarly with YqeK, YqeK-H109A, BsRppH, or BsRppH-E68Q. In each case, the reaction was quenched after 2 h with EDTA (210 μl of a 3.5 mM solution), and the RNA was recovered by phenol extraction and ethanol precipitation and then analyzed by deoxyribozyme cleavage, boronate gel electrophoresis, and northern blotting.

Phosphorylation state of the RNA product of decapping by YqeK

Ap4-capped AG(CU)13 (1,800 cpm) bearing a 32P-labeled phosphate between the first and second transcribed nucleotides was treated at 37°C with purified YqeK or YqeK-H109A (200 nM) in a buffer (20 µl) containing HEPES, pH 7.6 (40 mM), NaCl (250 mM), and dithiothreitol (1 mM). After 2 h, the reaction was quenched by adding EDTA (5 μl of a 100 mM solution), and the reaction product (10 μl) was subjected to alkaline hydrolysis by adding NaOH (5 μl of a 0.2 M solution) and heating the mixture at 95°C for 15 min. The hydrolysis reaction was quenched with acetic acid (5 μl of a 3 M solution), and 5 μl was spotted onto a PEI-cellulose TLC plate beside a set of markers generated by alkaline hydrolysis of triphosphorylated, diphosphorylated, monophosphorylated, and Ap4-capped AG(CU)13 that had not been treated with YqeK. The plate was developed with a solution containing 0.8 M ammonium sulfate, pH 5.5, and the radiolabeled spots were detected with a Typhoon RGB imager (Cytiva).

Analysis of capped 5′ ends by RLM–RACE

To confirm the transcription start site of individual capped transcripts in B. subtilis, total RNA from ΔyqeK cells (10 μg) was first treated with Quick CIP (10 units, New England Biolabs) for 1 h at 37°C in a solution (20 μl) containing rCutSmart buffer and rRNasin (2 units/µl, Promega) to convert all uncapped 5′ ends to unligatable hydroxyls. The capped 5′ ends were subsequently converted to monophosphates by treatment with E. coli RppH (2–10 μM) for 2 h at 37°C in a solution (20 μl) containing HEPES, pH 7.6 (20 mM), MgCl2 (10 mM), dithiothreitol (1 mM), and rRNasin (2 units/µl). The decapped RNA was then ligated to a chimeric DNA–RNA oligonucleotide (RACE 1; Supplementary Table S8) by treatment with T4 RNA ligase 1 (5 units, New England Biolabs) for 2 h at 37°C in the manufacturer’s buffer (25 µl) supplemented with RACE 1 (1 μg), ATP (1 mM), and rRNasin (1 unit/µl). The ligation products were reverse transcribed by treatment with SuperScript IV reverse transcriptase (300 units, Thermo Fisher) and a transcript-specific primer (5 pmol; Supplementary Table S8) for 1 h at 55°C in a solution (20 μl) containing SSIV buffer and rRNasin (1 unit/µl), and the resulting DNA was amplified by two successive rounds of PCR with nested pairs of primers specific for the chimeric DNA–RNA oligonucleotide and the transcript of interest (Supplementary Table S8). Finally, the PCR products were purified by agarose gel electrophoresis and sequenced.

Results

Despite the discovery of Np4 capping in the Gram-negative proteobacterium E. coli and recent progress in elucidating its origin and regulatory impact, there have been no reports of this epitranscriptomic modification in any other bacterial species. We therefore decided to investigate whether Np4 capping of RNA occurs in B. subtilis, a prototypical Gram-positive organism.

Presence of capped RNA in B. subtilis cells lacking YqeK

A previous study has indicated that the HD protein YqeK is the principal Np4A hydrolase in B. subtilis and other members of the Firmicutes phylum of bacteria, where its absence causes Np4As to accumulate to high levels [24]. To determine whether this accumulation of Np4As is accompanied by a parallel increase in the cellular concentration of capped transcripts, we examined the 5′ end of two B. subtilis mRNAs in wild-type and ∆yqeK cells: the monocistronic cspD transcript, which encodes a cold shock protein, and the dicistronic yhxA-glpP transcript, which encodes a protein of unknown function (YhxA) and a transcription antiterminator (GlpP) important for regulating the synthesis of glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase [48]. This was accomplished by extracting total RNA from isogenic B. subtilis strains containing or lacking the yqeK gene and subjecting each of these transcripts first to site-specific cleavage in vitro with a deoxyribozyme [41] that cuts 60–80 nt from the 5′ end and then to gel electrophoresis and northern blotting. Two types of denaturing gels were used: an ordinary polyacrylamide matrix and a polyacrylamide matrix modified with boronic acid side chains, which selectively retard the migration of capped RNAs by transiently forming a cyclic diester adduct with the 2′,3′-cis-diol in the ribose component of the cap [49].

The 5′-terminal deoxyribozyme cleavage product of both cspD and yhxA-glpP mRNA ran as a single band on an ordinary polyacrylamide gel, indicating a unique transcription start site for each. However, when analyzed instead by electrophoresis on a boronate gel, a second, slower-migrating band appeared for both transcripts when extracted from the ∆yqeK strain but not from the wild-type strain (Fig. 1B). Representing 16%–21% of the total, this second band indicated that, in cells lacking YqeK, a substantial percentage of each mRNA bore a 5′-terminal cap. Consistent with this interpretation, the additional band was eliminated by pre-treating cellular RNA in vitro with the E. coli decapping enzyme ApaH (Fig. 1B), and it co-migrated with the corresponding band of synthetic yhxA-glpP transcripts bearing an Ap3, Ap4, or Ap5 cap (Fig. 1C), whose ribose component contains a 2′,3′-cis-diol (Fig. 1A). By contrast, no such band was evident for either cspD or yhxA-glpP mRNA extracted from mutant cells lacking the B. subtilis RNA pyrophosphohydrolase RppH (Fig. 1B).

To confirm that the accumulation of capped RNA in ∆yqeK cells resulted from the absence of YqeK catalytic activity, the ∆yqeK mutation was complemented with an ectopic copy of the yqeK gene encoding either the wild-type enzyme or a catalytically inactive mutant lacking an active-site residue critical for metal-ion coordination (H109A). As expected, the restoration of catalytically competent YqeK to the mutant strain eliminated the capped form of cspD mRNA, whereas the catalytically inactive enzyme had no effect (Fig. 1D).

Structural characterization of the RNA caps

The accumulation of capped RNA in mutant cells lacking the principal Np4A hydrolase of B. subtilis suggested that these were likely to be Np4 caps. To test this hypothesis, we subjected yhxA-glpP mRNA from ∆yqeK cells to analysis by esCAPade (Enumeration of Shielded Cap-Associated Phosphates by Analysis of Decapped Ends), a procedure that determines the number of bridging phosphates in a cap by comparing the 5′ phosphorylation state of the RNA products generated by using ApaH to decap cellular RNA versus a set of synthetic Ap3-, Ap4-, and Ap5-capped standards [16]. This method takes advantage of the regioselectivity of decapping by ApaH, which releases the cap primarily (though not exclusively) as a nucleoside diphosphate irrespective of the phosphate content of the cap. Consequently, the number of phosphates in the cap can be deduced from how many remain on the RNA 5′ terminus after ApaH-mediated cap removal. This can be determined by using two other assays, PACO and PABLO, to ascertain the percentage of the resulting RNA 5′ ends that are diphosphorylated or monophosphorylated, respectively. PACO (Phosphorylation Assay by Capping Outcome) measures the percentage of diphosphorylated 5′ termini on the basis of their susceptibility to capping in vitro by the S. pombe guanylyltransferase Pce1, whereas PABLO (Phosphorylation Assay By Ligation of Oligonucleotides) measures the percentage of monophosphorylated 5′ ends on the basis of their ability to undergo splinted ligation to a DNA oligonucleotide [47, 50, 51].

When applied to RNA extracted from ∆yqeK cells, these measurements (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Tables S1–S6) revealed that the products of ApaH-mediated decapping of yhxA-glpP mRNA were 103 ± 9% diphosphorylated and 31 ± 4% monophosphorylated, similar to the percentages observed for the decapped products of the Ap4-capped yhxA standard (106 ± 8% diphosphorylated and 22 ± 3% monophosphorylated) (Fig. 2A). (Discrepancies versus the theoretical maximum of 100% resulted from the need to correct mathematically for incomplete PACO capping and PABLO ligation and the imperfect specificity of PACO for diphosphorylated 5′ ends and of PABLO for monophosphorylated 5′ ends [46, 47]). By contrast, treatment of the Ap3-capped standard with ApaH generated 5′ ends that were primarily monophosphorylated, while about half of the 5′ ends resulting from ApaH-mediated decapping of the Ap5-capped standard appeared to be triphosphorylated. From these measurements, we calculate that 92 ± 14% of the RNA caps in ∆yqeK cells contain four phosphates and 10 ± 4% contain three phosphates (Fig. 2B).

Np4 cap acquisition

In principle, cellular transcripts could acquire Np4 caps either during transcription initiation by 5′-terminal incorporation of an Np4A containing a nucleobase able to pair with a complementary nucleotide at the transcription start site (position +1) of the DNA template (Supplementary Fig. S2) or post-transcriptionally if aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases can catalyze the reaction of aminoacyl-adenylates not only with the 5′ triphosphate of NTPs but also with the triphosphorylated 5′ end of RNA. To distinguish between these possible mechanisms, which are not mutually exclusive, we investigated the effect of the promoter sequence on cap acquisition, as this untranscribed region of the gene could affect acquisition of the cap only if capping occurs during transcription initiation.

The promoter of the B. subtilis yhxA-glpP operon contains a C at position –1 of the coding strand, one nucleotide upstream of the transcription start site. We cloned the wild-type operon into a shuttle plasmid, created point mutations that changed the DNA sequence at position –1 from C to A, G, or T, and introduced each of the resulting four plasmids into a strain of B. subtilis from which both the yqeK gene and the chromosomal yhxA gene had been deleted. Total RNA was extracted from these cells, and the percentage of yhxA-glpP transcripts that were capped was determined by boronate gel electrophoresis and blotting, which revealed that Np4 capping in B. subtilis is markedly affected by the identity of the untranscribed promoter base pair at position –1 (Fig. 3). Levels of capping were more than twice as high when the coding strand nucleotide there was a purine (43%–45%) versus a pyrimidine (16%–21%), a finding consonant with previous studies of Np4 capping in E. coli [17]. None of these promoter substitutions altered the site of transcription initiation, as determined by RNA ligase-mediated rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RLM–RACE) after treating cellular RNA first with alkaline phosphatase to dephosphorylate uncapped 5′ ends and then with E. coli RppH to convert the capped 5′ ends to ligatable monophosphates (Supplementary Fig. S3). We conclude that Np4 caps are acquired in B. subtilis, at least in part, by Np4A incorporation into nascent transcripts during transcription initiation.

Effect of the 5′-terminal transcribed nucleotide on capping

Capped yhxA-glpP and cspD mRNA each begin with a 5′-terminal adenylate (A), as shown by RLM–RACE (Supplementary Fig. S4). Nevertheless, the structural symmetry of Np4As (Np4A = Ap4N) and the elevated concentration of at least three of them (Ap4A, Gp4A, and Up4A) in B. subtilis cells lacking YqeK [24] may enable Np4A incorporation by RNA polymerase at the 5′ end of transcripts that begin with nucleotides other than A. To determine whether transcripts bearing other 5′-terminal nucleotides become capped in ∆yqeK cells, we examined the 5′ end of the rpsT and rpsU transcripts, which encode ribosomal proteins S20 and S21 and begin with U or G, respectively. Total RNA from wild-type and ∆yqeK cells was treated with a deoxyribozyme specific for rpsT or rpsU mRNA, and the 5′ deoxyribozyme cleavage product was examined by boronate gel electrophoresis and northern blotting. An additional band was evident for each of these transcripts only when extracted from ∆yqeK cells, and this slower-migrating band was eliminated by pre-treating the cellular RNA with ApaH in vitro (Fig. 4A). Analysis by RLM–RACE confirmed the 5′-terminal sequence of each capped transcript (Supplementary Fig. S4). We conclude that mRNAs with diverse 5′-terminal nucleotides become capped in B. subtilis cells lacking YqeK.

Total RNA from wild-type B. subtilis and an isogenic mutant lacking the yqeK gene was treated with either of two deoxyribozymes that cut rpsT or rpsU mRNA site-specifically 51 or 65 nt, respectively, from the 5′ end and analyzed by boronate gel electrophoresis and northern blotting, with or without prior decapping by purified ApaH. (B) Capping of yhxA-glpP variants that begin with G, C, U, or A. Total RNA was extracted from ∆yqeK cells that lacked the chromosomal yhxA gene and instead contained a plasmid-borne yhxA-glpP operon in which the first transcribed nucleotide (position +1 of the coding strand) was G, C, T, or A, the second transcribed nucleotide (position +2) was C, and the nucleotide at position −1 of the coding strand was G. In each case, the yhxA-glpP transcript was cleaved site-specifically 61 nt from the 5′ end with a deoxyribozyme and analyzed by boronate gel electrophoresis and northern blotting, with or without prior decapping by purified ApaH.

To determine the effect of the 5′-terminal nucleotide on the percentage of 5′ ends that are capped, we next compared the level of capping of yhxA-glpP sequence variants that began with A (wild-type), G, C, or U at the RNA 5′ end (position +1). In each case, this nucleotide was preceded in the yhxA-glpP gene by a purine (G in place of C) at position –1 of the coding strand to enhance levels of capping and followed by a pyrimidine (C in place of G) at position +2 to discourage RNA polymerase from altering the location of the RNA 5′ end by initiating transcription there. Plasmids encoding each sequence variant were introduced into a B. subtilis strain lacking both the yqeK gene and the chromosomal yhxA gene. Total RNA was extracted from the resulting transformants, treated with a yhxA-specific deoxyribozyme, and analyzed by boronate gel electrophoresis and northern blotting. All four sequence variants were found to be capped, with the A-initiated transcript capped at almost twice the level of the other three (43% capped for A versus 20%, 27%, and 27% capped for G, C, and U, respectively) (Fig. 4B). RLM–RACE confirmed the identity of the 5′-terminal nucleotide of each variant (Supplementary Fig. S5). These findings indicate that, while any primary transcript in B. subtilis has the potential to be Np4-capped, irrespective of its 5′-terminal nucleotide, those that begin with A have the greatest propensity to acquire a cap of this kind in ∆yqeK cells.

Np4 cap removal

The structural resemblance of Np4-capped 5′ ends to Np4As raised the possibility that YqeK might function not only as an Np4A hydrolase but also as an Np4 decapping enzyme. To test this possibility, we examined the effect of purified YqeK on Np4-capped yhxA-glpP RNA that had been extracted from ∆yqeK cells or synthesized by in vitro transcription. YqeK was able to decap both transcripts, whereas a catalytically inactive YqeK mutant (H109A) was not (Fig. 5A).

We also investigated the decapping activity of B. subtilis RppH. This RNA pyrophosphohydrolase has been shown to trigger the degradation of triphosphorylated yhxA-glpP mRNA in wild-type B. subtilis cells by converting the 5′-terminal triphosphate to a monophosphate, thereby enabling attack by the 5′ exonuclease RNase J1, which preferentially degrades monophosphorylated RNA [34]. However, unlike YqeK, B. subtilis RppH did not detectably remove the Np4 cap from either the cellular or synthetic transcript (Fig. 5A).

To determine the phosphorylation state of the RNA product of Np4 cap removal by YqeK, we synthesized an Ap4-capped RNA substrate—Ap4AG(CU)13—bearing a single radiolabeled phosphate between the first and second transcribed nucleotides, together with triphosphorylated, diphosphorylated, and monophosphorylated forms of AG(CU)13 that were similarly radiolabeled. Alkaline hydrolysis of these RNAs yielded Ap4Ap, p3Ap, p2Ap, and pAp, respectively, as the predominant radiolabeled products, separable by thin layer chromatography (Fig. 5B). Decapping Ap4AG(CU)13 with purified YqeK and then subjecting the RNA product to alkaline hydrolysis generated p2Ap (ppAp) as the radiolabeled product, evidence that YqeK cuts between the β and γ phosphates of the Ap4 cap to produce diphosphorylated AG(CU)13, an outcome reminiscent of the manner in which this enzyme hydrolyzes Ap4A to generate two molecules of ADP. As expected, catalytically inactive YqeK failed to remove the cap.

Consequences of Np4 capping for mRNA degradation in cells lacking YqeK

In view of previous evidence that the degradation of uncapped yhxA-glpP mRNA in wild-type cells begins with conversion of the 5′ triphosphate to a monophosphate [34] and the expected deficit of decapping activity in B. subtilis cells lacking YqeK, we compared the decay rates of Np4-capped and uncapped yhxA-glpP mRNA in ∆yqeK cells. At time intervals after arresting transcription with rifampicin, total RNA was extracted from ∆yqeK ∆yhxA cells containing a plasmid-borne copy of the yhxA-glpP operon, and the amount of capped and uncapped yhxA-glpP mRNA remaining as a function time was determined by boronate gel electrophoresis and northern blotting (Fig. 6A). Np4-capped yhxA-glpP mRNA decayed with a half-life of 8.7 ± 0.4 min, much more slowly than its uncapped counterpart, whose half-life of 3.9 ± 0.2 min in ∆yqeK cells resembled its short half-life in wild-type cells [34] (Fig. 6B). The decay of Np4-capped yhxA-glpP mRNA was no slower in a double mutant strain lacking both YqeK and RppH (half-life of 8.1 ± 1.7 min) than in the strain lacking only YqeK (Supplementary Fig. S6), as expected from the resistance of Np4 caps to removal by B. subtilis RppH in vitro. These findings indicate that, in the absence of YqeK, Np4 caps protect mRNA from 5′-end-dependent degradation.

Effect of disulfide stress on Np4 capping in B. subtilis

In E. coli, levels of Np4As and Np4 capping increase sharply in response to disulfide stress induced by treating cells with diamide, a chemical reagent that inactivates the Np4A hydrolase and Np4 decapping enzyme ApaH [16] by joining cysteine thiols to create disulfide bonds [52]. Because diamide is also known to induce disulfide stress in B. subtilis [53, 54], we investigated its effect on the level of Np4 capping in that Gram-positive organism. RNA was extracted from B. subtilis containing or lacking the yqeK gene, both before and after treating the cells with diamide, and capping of the yhxA-glpP, cspD, and rpsT transcripts was examined by boronate gel electrophoresis and northern blotting (Fig. 7A). Diamide treatment alone was not sufficient to elevate the cellular concentration of capped RNA to detectable levels in B. subtilis. However, in conjunction with deletion of the yqeK gene, diamide treatment approximately doubled the percentage of yhxA-glpP, cspD, and rpsT mRNA that was capped. These findings show that, in B. subtilis, disulfide stress can increase levels of Np4 capping by a YqeK-independent mechanism.

Effect of YqeK on tolerance of disulfide stress

The elevated percentage of mRNA that is Np4-capped in ∆yqeK cells exposed to diamide raised the possibility that capping under these circumstances might be a cellular response that helps B. subtilis to tolerate disulfide stress. To test this hypothesis, we compared the survival of wild-type and isogenic ∆yqeK cells experiencing disulfide stress. This was accomplished by treating log-phase cultures with diamide and monitoring OD650 as a function of time thereafter (Fig. 7B). Interestingly, despite their mutant genotype, B. subtilis cells that lacked YqeK and therefore contained increased levels of Np4As and Np4-capped mRNA were significantly more tolerant of disulfide stress than were wild-type cells, with a half-time for cellular survival after exposure to diamide that was more than twice as long as that of their wild-type counterparts.

Discussion

Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria are thought to have diverged in evolution about three billion years ago [55], long before the emergence of metazoans. As a result, they have many characteristics that are distinct from one another, including various ribonucleases critical for RNA processing and degradation [31]. They also employ different enzymes for hydrolyzing Np4As. For example, E. coli, a prototypical Gram-negative species, primarily utilizes ApaH for this purpose, whereas the Gram-positive paradigm B. subtilis and other Firmicutes rely on YqeK, a member of an unrelated protein family with an entirely different fold and metal-ion requirement [22, 24–28]. Our data now show that, in B. subtilis mutants lacking YqeK, a variety of mRNAs become Np4-capped as a result of Np4A incorporation during transcription initiation. YqeK functions not only as an Np4A hydrolase that can suppress the availability of Np4As for 5′-terminal incorporation into nascent transcripts but also as a decapping enzyme that can trigger mRNA degradation by deprotecting Np4-capped 5′ ends. Interestingly, the increase in Np4 capping observed in ∆yqeK cells is accompanied by a parallel increase in the ability of B. subtilis to tolerate disulfide stress. Two other cellular metabolites, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and 3′-dephospho-coenzyme A (dpCoA), can likewise become incorporated into RNA as 5′ caps in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria [56–61].

In principle, Np4 caps could be acquired either during or after RNA synthesis. Our finding that the identity of an untranscribed promoter base pair can affect the percentage of transcripts that are Np4-capped in B. subtilis favors the former mechanism, in which this epitranscriptomic modification is acquired during transcription initiation by RNA polymerase when an Np4A is incorporated in place of an NTP at the 5′ end of a nascent transcript by base pairing with a complementary nucleotide at the transcription start site (position +1) of the DNA template. This conclusion is consistent with the ability of E. coli RNA polymerase to install Np4As at the 5′ terminus of nascent transcripts, both in vivo and in vitro [16–18]. The propensity of B. subtilis RNA polymerase to do so is more than twice as great when the nucleotide at position –1 of the DNA coding strand is a purine rather than a pyrimidine, another tendency that is consonant with previous studies of Np4A incorporation by E. coli and Thermus thermophilus RNA polymerase [17, 62]. This preference has been attributed to the influence of the complementary nucleotide at position –1 of the DNA template strand on the environment of the proto-cap nucleoside of the Np4A bound to the active site. The amino acid residues of the RNA polymerase β-subunit in this vicinity are well conserved in these and other bacterial species [62]. This effect of the promoter sequence is not specific for Np4 caps, as the presence of a purine at position –1 of the DNA coding strand also facilitates the incorporation of NAD in place of ATP to create a noncanonical cap at the 5′ end of A-initiated transcripts in E. coli [59, 63]. By contrast, 5′-terminal NAD incorporation by B. subtilis RNA polymerase is aided by the presence of either a purine or T at this position [60].

Due to the symmetry of Np4As, either of the component nucleosides has the potential to base pair with a complementary nucleotide at position +1 of the DNA template strand. As a result, Np4As can be incorporated at the 5′ end of any transcript, irrespective of the 5′-terminal transcribed nucleotide. This property explains why, in B. subtilis mutants lacking YqeK, Np4 caps have been observed at the 5′ end of naturally occurring transcripts that begin with a variety of nucleotides and at the 5′ end of yhxA-glpP variants engineered to begin with A, G, C, or U. In the case of the yhxA-glpP variants, which differed in sequence only at position +1 of the transcript, steady-state levels of Np4 capping were twice as high for the A-initiated transcript as for its G-, C-, and U-initiated counterparts. The higher percentage of A-initiated transcripts that were capped is consistent with their greater potential for cap acquisition, as all four Np4As can be incorporated at the 5′ end of such transcripts to generate mRNAs with diverse caps (Ap4, Gp4, Cp4, and Up4), whereas only a single Np4A (Gp4A, Cp4A, or Up4A) can be incorporated at the 5′ end of G-, C-, and U-initiated transcripts, respectively, resulting exclusively in Ap4-capped mRNAs. In theory, these disparities in steady-state cap levels could have other explanations as well, such as sequence-dependent differences in the decay rate of uncapped/decapped transcripts.

Our results show that YqeK not only hydrolyzes Np4As but also can remove Np4 caps from RNA. In both roles, it cuts between the β and γ phosphates, generating a pair of diphosphorylated products: either two NDPs or a diphosphorylated RNA and an NDP. This regioselectivity resembles that of E. coli ApaH [16, 27], even though the two enzymes are structurally unrelated. Both B. subtilis and E. coli also contain another enzyme, the Nudix hydrolase RppH, that is capable of converting diphosphorylated and triphosphorylated RNA 5′ ends to monophosphorylated 5′ ends vulnerable to ribonucleolytic attack [34, 44, 46]. However, unlike E. coli RppH [16, 19], B. subtilis RppH, which is only distantly related [64], appears to lack appreciable Np4 decapping activity and therefore does not contribute to Np4 cap removal in vivo. An entirely different enzyme, NudC, is principally responsible for removing NAD caps from A-initiated transcripts in E. coli [58]. Although no NudC ortholog is present in B. subtilis, B. subtilis RppH has been reported to remove NAD caps in vitro, but only very slowly unless Mn2+ is added to the reaction at a high concentration (0.1–2.0 mM) [60].

In B. subtilis mutants lacking YqeK, capped yhxA-glpP mRNA is degraded more slowly than its uncapped counterpart, suggesting a possible role for YqeK-mediated decapping in the control of capped mRNA lifetimes. Previously, triphosphorylated yhxA-glpP mRNA has been shown to decay in B. subtilis via a pathway in which RppH converts the 5′ triphosphate first to a diphosphate and then to a monophosphate, which enables rapid 5′-to-3′ degradation by the 5′-monophosphate-dependent exonuclease RNase J1 [34]. We propose that, in wild-type B. subtilis, YqeK-mediated deprotection of Np4-capped yhxA-glpP transcripts generates a diphosphorylated intermediate that is then converted by RppH to a monophosphorylated RNA susceptible to rapid degradation by RNase J1. By contrast, in mutant cells lacking YqeK, this pathway is blocked, and Np4-capped transcripts are degraded more slowly, presumably via a pathway that bypasses the 5′ end and relies instead on internal RNA cleavage by the B. subtilis endonuclease RNase Y. Therefore, the greatly increased abundance of Np4-capped mRNA in ∆yqeK cells appears to result from the additive effect of two consequences of this mutation: an increased supply of Np4As for 5′-terminal incorporation into mRNA during transcription initiation and a reduced rate of capped mRNA decay due to the absence of an enzyme capable of deprotecting the 5′ end of the resulting Np4-capped transcripts.

The exposure of wild-type E. coli to inducers of disulfide stress like diamide inactivates ApaH, which contains three cysteine residues near the catalytic center [26, 27], thereby causing mRNA to become Np4-capped at a high level [16]. Although treating wild-type B. subtilis cells with diamide is not alone sufficient to increase Np4 capping to detectable levels, perhaps because the sole cysteine residue of B. subtilis YqeK is located far from the active site [28], exposing ∆yqeK cells to diamide results in a two-fold increase in the percentage of B. subtilis RNA 5′ ends that are Np4-capped. Interestingly, despite its genetic defect, a strain of B. subtilis lacking the yqeK gene is better able than its isogenic yqeK+ counterpart to survive disulfide stress induced by diamide. Together, these findings raise the possibility that increased Np4 capping may be an adaptive response that helps B. subtilis to tolerate this stress, possibly by prolonging mRNA lifetimes.

Since they were first observed more than 50 years ago, the biological function of Np4As has largely remained an enigma. This is especially so in Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria like B. subtilis and E. coli, where their increased abundance in yqeK and apaH mutants is correlated with a variety of intriguing phenotypes that have never been explained. The discovery that Np4As serve as precursors for Np4 caps that become prevalent in such mutants and can protect mRNA from degradation raises the possibility that these phenotypes result from altered mRNA lifetimes and consequent changes in gene expression.
