Elimination of halo gloss transition surface defects on injection-molded parts
Authors: Mijin Kim, Joohyeong Jeon, Byungohk Rhee, Lih-Sheng Turng, Jae Hyuk Choi, Jinsu Gim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a novel method to eliminate halo gloss transition surface defects commonly found on large-scale injection-molded parts. Due to the increasing importance of product appearance and its influence on consumer decisions, the halo defects that deteriorate surface appearance and quality should be eliminated. The effect of the main injection molding process parameters on the halo defects was compared and investigated by correlating the injection molding simulations and defect specimens. It was found that a drastic increase in flow front speed (FFS) is the main cause of the halo defects. A multi-step injection speed approach that minimizes a drastic increase of FFS due to the reduction of flow front area according to the filling patterns is proposed. The proposed approach was experimentally validated for a simple test part and a real-world, commercial home appliance part, respectively. This approach can contribute to reducing the time for setting defect-free injection molding conditions before mass production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42688-5.Keywords: Halo defects, Gloss defects, Surface quality, Injection molding, Multi-step injection speedSubject terms: Chemical engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Due to the increasing importance of product appearance on consumer decisions1, surface appearance and quality have been one of the most important objectives for research and development of injection molded plastic parts such as appliance housings and automotive interior/exterior parts2,3. Injection molding has been widely used for producing plastic parts because of its capacity to efficiently mold complicated and curved geometry with high dimensional accuracy4,5. For the sake of product cost competitiveness, post-molding processes such as painting or coating that aim at improving surface quality are becoming less desirable. However, the complex flow behavior of polymer melts and the process characteristics of injection molding tend to result in various surface defects, such as gloss transition6–8, tiger stripes9–11, ghost marks12,13, and bubble marks14–16 on the injection-molded surface. Removing these surface defects and improving surface quality are the main factors for increasing process setup time before mass production of appearance parts.

Halo defects are one type of gloss transition defects manifesting as different surface gloss in a circular shape. It has been commonly found in parts with a large surface area, which is typically filled by circular flow patterns from the melt entrance (gate). Figures 1a and b present an example of halo gloss transition defects on a large home appliance part. As shown in the contrast enhanced images, Figs. 1c and d, a noticeable gloss transition of a circular shape around the gate is recognizable. Figure 1e is the distribution of apparent intensity along the solid red line in the gloss transition region, as marked in Fig. 1d. Because the light source is not in the specular direction of the region, the reflected lights shown in Figs. 1d and e are in the form of diffused lights. Lower and higher intensities of diffused light can be regarded as having higher and lower specular gloss, respectively. Therefore, the apparent intensity transition shown in Fig. 1d indicates a transition of specular gloss in the area.

Halo gloss transition defects have not been studied widely in contrast to the well-known tiger stripes defects, which exhibit alternating bands of gloss and dull regions. Several causes resulting in the tiger stripes have been proposed, namely, snake-like flow generated by flow instability9–11,17,18 and differences in microscale sub-surface morphology9,10,18. According to the rheological studies on the flow instability and strain hardening at the flow front, Weissenberg number, 19,20 has been suggested as an index for the occurrence of the tiger stripes. However, gloss transition defects such as halo defects have not received extensive attention. A lack of understanding of the cause of halo defects makes injection molding processes rely on rules of thumb such as applying too high mold temperature, which increases cycle time and reduces productivity.

Recent studies have focused on the underlying mechanism, prediction, and control methodology for gloss transition defects. Microscale shrinkage difference due to inhomogeneous material morphology22,23 or crystallization23,24 was proposed as a main cause for the rough topography of polymer surface that results in low gloss. Surface topography that scatters the incident light onto the polymer surface was responsible for the difference between low and high gloss regions and hence gloss transition defects6. The stiffness recovery of polymer surface due to cooling and melt pressure pushing polymer against the mold surface were pointed out as resisting and driving factors, respectively, for mold surface replication resulting in final surface topography. The ratio of the driving and resisting factors determining the final surface topography, the replication factor, was proposed as an index to identify the stable process window for acceptable surface gloss7,8. However, computing the replication factor is complicated because it requires material characteristics such as the shear viscosity model, the pvT (pressure-specific volume-temperature) model, and the shear storage modulus as a function of temperature. Therefore, implementing the proposed methodology on the shop floor is practically difficult.

This study aims to propose a novel methodology that can be easily implemented on the shop floor to prevent and eliminate the halo defects. The degree of halo defects was analyzed by image processing of specimen image taken by a simple photography setup. The effects of main process parameters identified by previous studies on the halo defects were compared. By comparing the defects and computer-aided engineering (CAE) simulation results, the main cause of the halo gloss transition defects was confirmed, and a multi-step injection speed strategy to eliminate the defects was proposed and validated based on the results of a simple test part and a real-world, commercial home appliance part. The future applications and developments will be discussed at the end of this paper.

Recently, computer vision techniques based on artificial intelligence (AI) have been increasingly adopted for the automated detection of injection molding defects25–27. While these AI-based approaches offer high efficiency, their performance heavily relies on the availability of high-quality, labeled datasets. The quantitative image processing and defect characterization methodology proposed in this study can provide a robust foundation for generating such datasets, bridging the gap between conventional visual inspection and advanced AI-driven quality control.

Methodology

Material and equipment

A simple flat plate-shaped test part with dimensions of 230 ⋅ 110 ⋅ 2 (width ⋅ height ⋅ thickness) mm was used for this work, as shown in Fig. 2a. This simple cavity geometry is better suited for investigating the cause of halo defects in terms of actual molding condition inside of the cavity (in-mold condition). Moreover, it was expected that the result from the selected part could be applied to other appearance parts because the overall geometry of appearance parts such as the backside cover of TVs, is similar to that of the tested simple part, featuring a small thickness and a large surface area. A hot runner gate with a diameter of 2 mm was located 55 mm from one of the shorter edges. The mold surfaces were highly polished to reveal subtle surface quality variations such as gloss transition or other surface defects.

The material used in this study was a polymer blend of polycarbonate (PC) and acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene (ABS)-copolymer (PC/ABS, Lupoy HR 5007 A, LG Chem Ltd., Republic of Korea), which is widely used for home appliance parts but is prone to producing gloss transition defects due to the inhomogeneous morphology of the butadiene rubber phase. The material was sufficiently dried according to the manufacturer’s recommendation to prevent swirl marks and silver streaks on the surface. A high-speed electric injection molding machine (LGE 150IIIDHS, LS-Mtron Ltd., Republic of Korea) was used for the molding trials. The tested samples were collected after at least 20 cycles to ensure sufficient stabilization of the molding process28–31.

Halo defects analysis

Halo defects were analyzed by image processing. Image processing is a relatively easy and faster test method on the shop floor than other surface test methods, such as confocal microscopy32,33, interferometry6,34,35, and scanning probe microscopy36–38, even though it cannot analyze precise surface characteristics such as surface topography. This is because it requires only a digital camera, a reliable light source, and constant ambient conditions, which are easily prepared. Since the image processing results are based on the photographic image of the sample, the results can be regarded as similar to the perceived surface quality by the naked eye. Due to these benefits of image processing, it has been applied to analyzing filling imbalance39, interfacial defects40, ghost marks13, and birefringence41 of injection-molded parts.

An easy and practical photography studio setup was implemented in this study. To capture diffusely reflected light from the injection-molded surface, a reliable digital camera (EOS 700D with EF-S 18–55 mm, Canon Inc., Japan) was aligned to the normal direction of the surface, as shown in Fig. 2b. A diffusing LED light source was aligned at a 45˚ angle to the normal direction. To prevent the effect of light reflection from other light sources, the sample images were taken in a dark room. An ideal gloss surface that reflects all light to the specular direction (cf. Fig. 2c) should show complete darkness in the image taken from the normal direction. A semi-gloss surface, which can be regarded as exhibiting a mixed reflection of perfect diffuse (Lambertian) and ideal specular reflection, reflects a portion of the inciden light in the normal direction (cf. Fig. 2d). This approach is consistent with the physical principles of the Blinn-Phong reflection model, which characterizes surface appearance by separating reflected light into diffuse and specular components. While the standard Blinn-Phong model utilizes a halfway vector to account for surface micro-topography in rendering applications42, its underlying framework aligns with our methodology of focusing on intensity variations induced by surface roughness. By specifically isolating and quantifying the diffuse component through our photography setup, we can more sensitively capture the subtle micro-roughness variations that constitute halo defects—features that are frequently overlooked by conventional specular-based gloss measurements. Therefore, the semi-gloss surface resulting from the gloss transition defects can be easily detected by the intensity of the image. The sampled images were processed by MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., CA, USA).

Intensity difference at the halo defects was used to measure the degree of halo defects. First, the color images were converted into monochromatic images in gray value ranging from 0 to 255, as shown in Figs. 2e and f. It is difficult to perfectly remove dust or prevent scratches on the shop floor. Therefore, a single intensity profile across the halo defects is susceptible to such disturbances. A median filter widely used for dust removal from the image would not be effective for unexpected large scratches. For this study, averaging intensity profiles extracted from different positions along the peak intensity line (cf. the yellow dash line in Fig. 2f) was applied and resulted in smooth intensity distribution without noise, as shown in Fig. 2g. The position of the intensity profiles was adjusted by the peak intensity position in each profile as a position reference. Therefore, intensity profiles were aligned by the same peak intensity position and then averaged. This image processing was effective in removing the noise from the scratches without blunting the averaged intensity profile. The degree of the halo defects was quantified by intensity difference in gray value, ΔG, from the stable gloss surface without the halo defects, as shown in Fig. 2g. Lower and higher Δ values correspond to a weak and strong halo defect (cf. Fig. 2h and i), respectively.

Design of experiment (DoE)

According to the previous studies6–8 on the gloss transition defects, melt temperature, mold temperature, and flow front speed were identified as the main influential factors on local molding conditions that result in differences in mold surface replication. Both melt and mold temperatures are related to the contact temperature of the polymer melt on the mold surface, affecting the cooling effect. Flow front speed as well as melt temperature affect the melt pressure development due to their influence on shear rate, viscous heating, and shear viscosity. In the simple part mold, the flow front speed can be controlled directly by the injection speed. Therefore, melt temperature, mold temperature, and injection speed were selected as the main experimental variables for a design of experiments (DoE) molding trial to investigate the effects of process parameters on the halo defects. Because the surface of injection-molded parts is largely determined during the filling stage, the effects of process parameters related to the post-filling stage, such as packing pressure and packing time, were not significant on the surface quality6. Therefore, the packing stage was omitted and the filling stage was stopped before completely filling of the cavity. The full factorial DoE design and the effects of the selected process parameters on the part surface are tabulated in Table 1. Because the halo defects are subtle and expected to be difficult to quantify, other DoE methods such as fractional designs that sacrifice DoE analysis accuracy, were not acceptable for this study. The ranges of melt and mold temperatures were referred to the recommended process conditions from the material manufacturer. The detailed experimental conditions are provided as Table S1 in the Supplemental Information S1.

CAE simulation

Injection molding simulation software was used to investigate the detailed molding conditions inside the cavity as it is difficult to gather detailed molding conditions in the cavity without multiple in-mold sensors43, unless a specialized mold such as a transparent mold44–46. The injection molding CAE software employed in this study was Moldex3D (CoreTech System Co. Ltd., Taiwan). In the CAE simulation, several assumptions are applied because the polymer melt is a non-Newtonian, viscoelastic fluid, under non-isothermal conditions with a phase transition (liquid to solid, solidification)47. The polymer melt in the simulation was assumed to be a Generalized Newtonian Fluid (GNF). The modified-Cross model using an Arrhenius temperature dependence was used as the shear viscosity model of the polymer melt. The modified Tait model was used to represent the specific volume of the polymer melt as a function of pressure and temperature (pvT behavior). In the filling stage of the injection molding process, the polymer melt was assumed to be incompressible. The detailed model coefficients, mesh, and boundary conditions are tabulated in the Supplemental Information S2. The same molding conditions as the actual molding trials were used as the input data for the computer simulation.

Results

Influential factors

The degree of halo defects, Δ was measured by the proposed image processing methods utilizing the diffused light reflection from the injection molded surface. The measured Δ values are presented in Table S1 and Fig. S1a of Supplemental Information S1. The deviation of measured Δ was appropriately small to distinguish the degree of halo defects induced in different injection molding conditions. The variance of Δ, which is the difference between the measured Δ and the average of each condition in all injection molding conditions, is presented in Fig. S1b and the standard deviation was sufficiently smaller than Δ differences. Therefore, the proposed image processing method was capable of measuring the halo defects.

Based on DoE main effect plots, melt temperature and injection speed were influential process parameters affecting halo defects, as shown in Figs. 3a, c, and d. The effect of mold temperature on halo defects was negligible based on the selected experimental conditions, as shown in Figs. 3b and d. The detailed analysis of variance (ANOVA) results and interaction plots are presented in Table S2 and Fig. S1, respectively, in the Supplementary Information S1. In comparison with the ranges of melt temperature and injection speed, the mold temperature range was relatively narrow due to the recommended process condition. As a result, high mold temperature conditions typically recommended for high surface quality injection molding to reduce the halo gloss transition defect were not employed for this case.

Main effect plot of a melt temperature, b mold temperature, and c injection speed, respectively, on halo defects. d Comparison of relative effect of process parameters on halo gloss transition defects.

According to the previous research6–8, melt temperature affects the shear viscosity of the polymer melt, which, in turn, influences the pressure development. A low melt temperature increases melt viscosity and results in a higher injection pressure under the same injection speed condition. Therefore, it increases the degree of mold surface replication. At the same time, the polymer melt at a low melt temperature cools down faster and the surface stiffness of polymer melt as a resisting factor to mold surface replication recovers more rapidly. Therefore, it is difficult to determine the net effect of melt temperature on halo gloss transition defects due to the competing effects from pressure and cooling. However, injection speed simply affects pressure development in short flow-length parts. Under high injection speed conditions, the flow front speed of the polymer melt in the filling stage is high, and it results in faster development of melt pressure pressing the polymer melt against the mold surface. The dominating effect of injection speed is well described in Fig. 3d, which shows the overall effect of injection speed on surface gloss was nearly twice that of the melt temperature. Therefore, it is recommended to optimize those process settings that affect the injection speed for control of halo gloss transition defects.

To specify the detailed effect of injection speed on the surface quality, the process condition of filling stage was analyzed by computer simulation. Figure 4a plots the predicted filling pattern of the injection-molded specimen under a constant injection speed of 100 mm/s. Figure 4b and c show the specimen surface image with halo gloss transition defects and position of the defects, respectively. The mold and melt temperature were 60 ˚C and 270 ˚C, respectively. The size of halo gloss transition defects was a ring of 55–75 mm in radius at the middle of the specimen.

Even though the injection speed or profile is set as constant with injection molding machines, the actual flow front speed inside of the cavity may not be a constant due to the geometry of the part, as shown by the filling patterns in Fig. 4a. In the early filling stage, the cavity is being filled through a radial flow fpattern with the gate in the center. As flow length increases, the circumference of the flow front and the cross-sectional area of the flow front (i.e., the flow front area) increase. Therefore, the flow front speed (FFS) decreases rapidly as shown in Fig. 4e. After the flow front reaches the left side of the cavity, the cross-sectional area of the flow front decreases suddenly by about 75% because the melt front area to the left of the gate ceases to exist. Therefore, the FFS increases rapidly under a constant machine injection speed profile, as shown in Fig. 4e. Interestingly, the experimental location of the halo gloss transition defects coincide with therapid increase in FFS predicted by the CAE simulation (cf. Fig. 4c and e). This implies that the drastic increase of FFS is the primary cause for the onset of halo defects.

For additional explanation, the velocity values in the y-direction over the filling time are shown in Fig. 5. The reason for observing the velocity in the y-direction is that the product’s length aligns with the y-axis, making this component the most representative value for FFS. Measurement points were set 10 mm to the left and right of the gate, and the velocity values at these points were analyzed. Up to 0.5 s, there is a minimal difference in velocity between constant injection speed control and multi-step injection speed control. However, at 0.7 s, a significant difference in velocity is observed at Point B. The point in time when the left side of the product, relative to the gate, becomes fully filled is approximately between 0.5 s and 0.7 s. Under constant injection speed control, even after the left side is completely filled, the machine continues to inject resin at a constant rate, causing a rapid increase in flow rate toward the right side of the gate, and consequently, an increase in flow velocity. This rapid increase in FFS ultimately triggers the onset of halo defects, making it a critical contributing factor. In contrast, when multi-step injection speed control is applied, the velocity at Point B at 0.7 s is calculated to be 201 mm/s, which is approximately 70.7% lower than the value of 688 mm/s observed under constant injection speed control. Furthermore, no halo defects were observed under the multi-step injection speed control condition, demonstrating that this approach is effective in preventing halo defects.

Multi-step injection speed approach

To eliminate the halo defects, the rapid increase of FFS should be avoided. FFS is directly related to the flow rate of polymer melt into the cavity and the cross-sectional area of the flow front (flow front area). The flow front area is difficult to control, because it is determined by the filling patterns and the cavity geometry. Therefore, the only controllable factor for FFS is the flow rate of the polymer melt injected into the cavity.

To control the flow rate of the melt, one needs to adjust the injection speed as a machine (process) parameter. A multi-step injection speed profile with various injection speeds according to the screw position can be employed instead of using a constant injection speed. The screw positions of the injection speed profile correlate with the positions of the flow front during the filling stage. Therefore, a reduction of injection speed at the screw position corresponding to the contact of the circular flow front with the upper, left, and lower sides of the cavity can alleviate the drastic increase in FFS and thus the halo defects. The injection speed should be reduced to about 1/4 of its initial speed when the melt front contacts the sides of the cavity because 3/4 of the flow front area is gone. To implement this approach with the process setting, the injection speed was changed to 50% and then about 20% of the initial injection speed. Maintaining the reduced injection speed results in an increase in filling time, which can cause an increase in cycle time and an excessive increase in injection pressure. Therefore, the injection speed should gradually recover to the initial injection speed.

The effects of multi-step injection speed profile on the filling patterns and FFS are presented in Fig. 4d and e. The proposed approach eased the drastic increase of FFS as shown in Fig. 4d, in contrast with the constant injection speed that led to the drastic increase of FFS at the middle of the specimen (cf. Fig. 4a), as detailed in Fig. 4e. The reduction of the injection speed effectively eliminated the drastic increase of FFS in the 55–75 mm range from the gate, which corresponded to the halo region as shown in Fig. 4e.

The elimination of halo gloss transition defect by the proposed multi-step injection speed was experimentally validated, as shown in Fig. 4f. In comparison with the defective specimen molded by constant injection speed (cf. Fig. 4b), the molded specimen using multi-step injection speed profile did not exhibit any visible halo defects. To enhance the reliability of the experimental results, ten samples were collected, and a comprehensive analysis was performed based on these samples. The degree of the halo defects was reduced from 7.60.8 to 1.80.4 (cf. Fig. 4c and f), which means the visibility of the halo defects was dropped by about 75%. This demonstrated that the drastic increase of FFS is the main cause of the halo gloss transition defects, and adjusting the injection speed profile can eliminate the defect effectively. Application to a real-world commercial part

To demonstrate its practical effectiveness, the proposed multi-stage injection speed approach was applied to a commercial injection-molded part. The part was a home appliance component used for a refrigerator basket. The geometry and approximate dimensions of the part are presented in Fig. 7a. A constant injection speed of 120 mm/s was used for the original molding of the part. The part had a hot runner gate at the center, which resulted in a radial filling pattern in the early stages of filling. The flow front subsequently encountered the four rectangular cut-outs. Therefore, the flow front area suddenly dropped right after contact, causing a rapid increase in the FFS. This filling pattern and the change in FFS led to the halo defects, as shown in Fig. 7b.

Application of the multi-stage injection speed approach for a commercial home appliance part (refrigerator basket). a Part geometry. Note: Detailed part features and image of whole part are not shown here per the manufacturer’s request. b Halo defects under the constant injection speed profile condition (Δ=8.20.6). c Part free of halo defects using a multi-stage injection speed profile (Δ=1.90.5). Note: The mold surface was treated to matt, instead of being highly polished surface. Therefore, the light intensity of halo defects on the molded parts is reversed (darker than other area) as compared to highly polished surface (lighter than other area). This reversed surface gloss appearance due to the mold surface finish has been reported in37.

As an attempt to eliminate the halo defects, a multi-step injection speed approach was applied. The injection speed was set to decrease when the flow front contacted the cut-outs that reduced the flow front area. The position of the injection speed change was searched by short-shot tests on the shop floor. After the flow front passed the cut-outs, the injection speed was set to resume to the initial speed, i.e., 120 mm/s. The reduced injection speed was set as 20 mm/s after several tests of various speeds. As shown in Fig. 7c, the degree of halo defects was reduced by about 90% and the molded surface exhibited no visible halo defects.

Discussion

The proposed multi-step injection speed approach can effectively eliminate the halo gloss transition defects by mitigating the drastic increase in FFS that results in surface defects. The FFS can easily fluctuate depending on the part geometry and the position of the gate, even under a constant injection speed condition; this is particularly evident in products with complex geometries where the cross-sectional area changes abruptly, such as automotive interior and exterior parts. Therefore, a multi-step injection speed profile should be employed to reduce the fluctuation of FFS in order to produce injection-molded parts with high surface quality.

The proposed measurement method for surface defects is appropriate tfor quantifying subtle surface defects such as halo defects. This method measures the intensity of diffused reflected light from the surface. Surface defects easily recognizable outside of the specular direction to the light source are difficult to measure by typical gloss meters, which only measure specular reflection. This is because the relative variation of reflected light due to the surface defects outside of the specular direction is much larger than the variation of specular reflected light intensity. Therefore, the proposed measurement method can sensitively measure the halo gloss defects. Although direct comparison with other halo-specific quantitative methods is not possible due to the lack of prior literature, the proposed image processing method offers distinct advantages over conventional surface characterization tools. While microscopic methods such as confocal microscopy or scanning probe microscopy provide high-resolution sub-micron data, they are time-consuming and limited to small areas. In contrast, our methodology allows for a rapid, non-destructive, and macroscopic evaluation of the entire 230 mm flow length in a single capture, making it significantly more practical for shop-floor implementation.

According to the test results concerning the effect of process parameters on the halo defects, injection speed that is directly related to the FFS in the cavity has a considerable influence on the halo defects. This is in agreement with the previous research on gloss defects and surface quality2,6,7. Therefore, FFS is an influential factor on the surface defects, especially gloss-related defects, comparable to other machine parameters like mold temperature, and optimization of injection speed profile is important for high surface quality injection molding.

The fluctuation of FFS resulting in the halo defects in the tested specimen ranged around 50–200 mm/s (cf. Fig. 4e). According to systematic research on the gloss transition defects for ABS and PC materials7,8, this FFS range is outside of the stable gloss process window as determined by the replication factor (RF), an index of the effect of molding condition on surface gloss. This means that the fluctuation of FFS easily results in the gloss variation. The polymer material used was a blended polymer material of ABS and PC (PC/ABS). Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that PC/ABS should have a similar RF distribution to the molding condition presented in8. In addition to applying the proposed multi-step injection speed approach, two more approaches would be effective; namely, increasing injection speed to shift the FFS fluctuation range to within the stable gloss process window, and/or applying high mold temperature near the glass transition temperature of the material. However, an excessively high FFS can induce other surface defects such as tiger stripes and burn marks. A very high mold temperature decreases productivity due to longer cooling time. Therefore, the multi-step injection speed approach would be a more practical and effective approach to minimize halo surface defects.

Suppressing the drastic increase of FFS by reducing the injection speed extends the filling time and cycle time, yielding lower productivity. However, this approach only reduces the injection speed momentarily in responding to a specific filling pattern or screw position range to minimize increment of filling time. The benefit of the proposed approach by eliminating the halo defects outweights the reduction in productivity, especially for high surface quality injection-molded parts.

The proposed multi-step injection speed approach increases the filling time by approximately 0.78 s for the tested part because the overall flow front speed is lower than the tested constant molding condition. This longer cycle time reduces the productivity of the molding process. However, the elimination of surface defects such as halo defects is a higher priority than shorter cycle time because high productivity is meaningless for defective parts. Moreover, such increased filling time is much smaller than the total cycle time, and the influence of the overall productivity is insignificant.

Due to the slower filling of the cavity, the proposed approach could induce side effects related to the higher viscosity, and cooling effect. Slower filling reduces overall shear rate resulting in higher viscosity. Higher viscosity induces higher required injection pressure and it can generate flash or burrs near the gate or melt delivery system. Also, slower filling makes the cooling effect higher resulting in relatively faster solidification relative to the total filling time. Faster solidification reduces the effective thickness or radius of the melt flow channel resulting in higher flow resistance and higher injection pressure. Therefore, it could induce short-shot defects under insufficient maximum injection pressure limitation conditions.

Further application

The design of a hot runner system and the optimization of sequence valve gating (SVG) control should also consider the high influence of FFS on the halo defects. During the filling stage, a high hydrostatic pressure is developed in the manifold (back) side of initially closed hot runner gates48,49. Immediately after the opening of the gates, the compressed polymer melt under the high pressure could burst out from the hot runner gate(s) if the flow length from the flow front to the gate is not sufficient to prevent a large pressure difference between the front and back sides of the gate. This is because such a large pressure difference would cause a sudden increase in FFS and the occurence of halo defects in the SVG hot runner mold. Therefore, the sequence time for opening the gate should be adjusted to prevent a sudden excessive acceleration of FFS.

The optimum injection speed profile minimizing the deviation of FFS in a complicated part can bedetermined through iterative computer simulation. FFS is determined by the injection speed as the input flow rate into the cavity, flow front area as flow channel area, and volumetric compression of polymer melt due to the filling pressure. However, it is difficult to simply consider the variation in the flow front area according to the filling patterns of a complicated cavity and melt compression. The total injection volume to be delivered by a screw stroke can be divided into several sections. In each section, the overall flow front area can be found through the mold filling simulation results, and the proper injection speed that minimizes the fluctuation of FFS can be obtained. After determining the injection speed of a specific filling section, the next injection speed can be optimized iteratively.

Limitation and future directions

The multi-step injection speed conditions employed in this study were determined using either injection molding simulation or short shot test. Injection molding simulation presents detailed information on filling patterns and FFS fluctuation (cf. Fig. 4a, d, and e). However, conventional CAE simulations for injection molding cannot be directly used to predict surface quality such as surface gloss and the degree of halo defects as discussed in2. By short shot tests on the shop floor, actual surface quality and defects can be easily recognized in short time, even though detailed information on filling patterns and FFS cannot be gathered. A multi-step injection speed profile set by injection molding simulation should be fine-tuned by molding trials because filling pattern results predicted by CAE simulation could still be different from the actual molding results.

Application of a capacitive transducer that measures melt flow length has been proposed to tune the injection speed profile to maintain a constant FFS50–53. Unfortunately, such in-mold capacitor sensors have not been widely used and cannot be easily accessed in the in-mold sensor and monitoring system market. Instead, placing widely used in-mold sensors for detecting the flow front arrival can shorten the molding trial for fine-tuning of the injection speed profile. For example, mold surface temperature sensors can be located at the point where the melt front area is reduced rapidly, and the arrival of the flow front at the sensor location can be used as a trigger for the injection speed change. This approach can deal with variations of the filling patterns due to the batch-to-batch material property variation, introduction of recycled materials, and process disturbance induced by external influences.

Similar to the previous research40, the injection speed profile could be optimized by machine learning (ML) modeling as a future extension of this work. The response of the FFS deviation to the injection speed of each section could be modeled by an ML model and simulation results. After training an ML model with high prediction performance, an optimum injection speed profile could be determined without further simulations. The ML model could be reused for other polymer materials by a transfer learning approach to accelerate the optimization of injection speed profile and save computational cost for simulation data.

Conclusion

This paper presents a facile image processing method for halo defects detection, identifies the drastic increase in flow front speed (FFS) as the main cause for the surface defects, and proposes a practical multi-step injection speed approach to eliminate them. Halo defects on the injection-molded surface were analyzed by image processing and quantifying the diffused reflected light intensity from the surface defects. Influential process parameters on the halo defects were identified, and the detailed influence of those process parameters was investigated by comparing the injection molding simulation results and images of surface defects on tested specimens.

The contours of the filling patterns with the FFS acceleration corresponded to the location of the halo defects. This revealed that the main cause of the halo defects was the drastic increase in FFS during the filling stage. Therefore, the drastic increase in FFS should be minimized to significantly suppress the onset of halo defects. A multi-step injection speed profile taking into account the variation of flow front area and filling patterns was proposed to preventa drastic increase in FFS. The tested injection speed profile based on experimental short-shot tests successfully produced a commercial injection-molded home appliance part without any visible halo defects.

Even though multi-step injection speed is a well-known function of molding machines, its application for eliminating halo defects has not been proposed. The proposed approach can be applied effectively in the injection molding industry because the multi-step injection speed can be utilized for most injection molding machines without additional equipment and capital investment. For a more efficient implementation of the proposed approach, an optimum injection speed profile can be optimized by injection molding simulation considering the variation in the flow front area. The proposed multi-step injection speed approach can also be applied to more sophisticated optimization methods using iterative simulation or machine learning-based optimization. As a result, this study can contribute to reducing the time for setting up injection molding conditions that would produce parts with minimized halo visibility before mass production.
