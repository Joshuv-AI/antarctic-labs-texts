Transverse versus longitudinal skin incisions for totally implantable venous access port implantation: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Tomasz Drygalski, Aleksandra Żmuda, Paweł Maciejewski, Tomasz Składzień, Michał Cicio, Anna Kwinta, Wojciech Szpunar, Michał Terlecki, Jarosław Woroń, Tomasz Lonc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotally implantable venous access ports (TIVAPs) are widely used in oncologic patients requiring long-term vascular access. However, the impact of skin incision orientation on postprocedural pain, procedural efficiency, and early complications remains unclear. This study compared transverse and longitudinal incisions in terms of early postoperative pain, patient-reported discomfort, procedure duration, and complication rates.MethodsIn this randomized, single-center study, 170 patients undergoing TIVAP implantation were assigned to either a transverse or longitudinal incision group (85 patients each). All procedures followed a standardized protocol with ultrasound-guided venous access and fluoroscopic confirmation of catheter tip position. Pain intensity was assessed using the numeric rating scale (NRS) within 24 h, and patient-reported discomfort was evaluated on day 7. Procedural characteristics and early complications were recorded.ResultsPostprocedural pain was low in both groups, with most patients reporting NRS scores between 0 and 2. Analgesic requirements were minimal, with no significant between-group differences. Discomfort at day 7 was also low and comparable. Procedure duration showed a trend toward shorter times in the transverse incision group [20 (18; 30) vs. 25 (20; 35) minutes; p = 0.09]. Overall complication rates were low, with no major complications. Minor complications included local inflammatory reactions and hematomas. Bleeding within the port pocket was more frequent in the longitudinal incision group, although not statistically significant.ConclusionsTransverse and longitudinal incisions provide comparable outcomes in TIVAP implantation, with low pain, minimal discomfort, and low complication rates. A trend toward shorter procedure duration and less port pocket bleeding with transverse incisions suggests potential technical advantages.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT06766656 (Registered January 04, 2025).Keywords: Central venous access port, Chemotherapy, Complications, QoL, TIVAP

Introduction

The implantation of totally implantable venous access ports (TIVAPs) is indicated in patients requiring long-term venous access, such as those undergoing chemotherapy, prolonged drug administration, or frequent blood sampling [1]. Although traditionally performed using surgical techniques [2], ultrasound-guided cannulation of the internal jugular vein (IJV) has become the preferred approach due to its high success rate and favorable safety profile [3]. The procedure is typically performed under local anesthesia and involves percutaneous venous access, followed by creation of a subcutaneous pocket in the infraclavicular region for port chamber placement. Fluoroscopy is routinely used to confirm catheter positioning [4].

The port pocket is created through a small skin incision in the infraclavicular area, which can be made either transversely or longitudinally, depending on the physician’s preference. Both techniques are widely used in clinical practice and do not affect the further course of the procedure.

In recent years, there has been increasing recognition of the importance of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in both clinical research and routine care, particularly in oncology and procedures involving long-term implantable devices. While traditional endpoints such as complication rates and procedural success remain essential, they do not fully capture the patient’s perspective. PROs provide valuable insights into pain, physical discomfort, cosmetic outcomes, and overall quality of life, all of which may influence treatment adherence and long-term satisfaction [5]. Given that TIVAPs are often used for extended periods, even minor procedural differences may have a meaningful impact on patient experience.

In particular, cosmetic outcomes, including scar perception, have been shown to be relevant for patients with breast cancer [6]. Despite this, the impact of incision orientation on patient-reported outcomes has not been well established. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial comparing transverse and longitudinal skin incisions in TIVAP implantation.

Materials and methods

We conducted a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial at the University Hospital in Krakow, Poland, comparing two skin incision techniques for totally implantable venous access port (TIVAP) placement. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines.

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the transverse-incision group or the longitudinal-incision group for creation of the subcutaneous port pocket.

The primary outcome was postoperative pain within the first 24 h. Secondary outcomes included patient-reported discomfort at day 7, procedure duration, and the incidence of early peri- and postprocedural complications.

Pain intensity was assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10) at 1, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h after the procedure. Patient-reported discomfort related to the presence of the vascular port was assessed on postoperative day 7 using a simple 0–5 ordinal scale administered during a structured telephone interview, where 0 indicated no discomfort and 5 indicated the worst imaginable discomfort. Patients were asked specifically about discomfort attributable to the port device rather than to systemic symptoms of their underlying malignancy. Interviewers used standardized phrasing for all questions. This scale was used as an exploratory pragmatic measure rather than a formally validated patient-reported outcome instrument.

The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Jagiellonian University (No. 1072.6120.115.2024) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06766656). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Patient eligibility

Inclusion criteria: Adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing TIVAP implantation.

Exclusion criteria: Patients were excluded if they had impaired coagulation (platelet count < 50,000/µL or international normalized ratio [INR] > 1.5), ongoing dual antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulant therapy not discontinued according to recommended guidelines, a history of bilateral shoulder girdle or neck trauma or surgery, known central venous thrombosis, active infection, including sepsis or chronic opioid use.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the transverse-incision group or the longitudinal-incision group using a computer-generated random sequence with permuted block sizes to ensure balanced allocation. The randomization sequence was generated by an investigator not involved in outcome assessment.

Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened immediately prior to the procedure.

Due to the nature of the intervention, the anesthesiologist performing the procedure could not be blinded to group assignment. However, postoperative outcome assessors were blinded to allocation and were not involved in intraoperative care, ensuring independent assessment of pain and discomfort outcomes.

Venous port implantation protocol

All procedures were performed under local anesthesia using 1% lidocaine for skin and subcutaneous tissue infiltration. Venous access was obtained percutaneously under ultrasound guidance, with fluoroscopic confirmation of catheter position.

A subcutaneous pocket for the port chamber was created in the infraclavicular region through a small skin incision, oriented either transversely or longitudinally according to randomization. Apart from incision orientation, all procedural steps—including venous cannulation, catheter positioning, port fixation, and postoperative management—were identical between groups.

After connection of the port chamber to the catheter and verification of proper device function, the incision was closed and a sterile dressing was applied. All procedures were performed in a one-day hospitalization setting, and patients were discharged on the same day in the absence of complications.

Prophylactic antibiotic therapy was administered according to a local protocol. Patients with lymphocyte or neutrophil counts below 1,000/µL prior to port implantation received a single prophylactic dose of antibiotics.

Management of antithrombotic therapy and bleeding disorders was performed in accordance with the GAVeCeLT consensus statement [7].

Two types of totally implantable venous access ports were used: 6.5 Fr systems (B. Braun Celsite® Safety) and 7 Fr systems (Vygon Polysite® 3000).

Postoperative analgesia management

All patients received standardized postoperative analgesia recommendations, including oral paracetamol, metamizole, or ibuprofen administered every 8 h during the first 48 h after the procedure. The mean single dose was 500 mg for paracetamol, 500 mg for metamizole, and 400 mg for ibuprofen. Patients were instructed to initiate analgesic therapy when pain intensity exceeded 2 points on the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS). Analgesic selection and dosing were individualized according to the patient’s clinical status and relevant residual organ function parameters. When needed, analgesics were administered using a multimodal approach based on complementary mechanisms of action and routine clinical practice.

Pain intensity was assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was postoperative pain intensity assessed at 1, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10).

The secondary outcome was patient-reported discomfort related to the presence of the vascular port, assessed on day 7 using a 0–5 scale.

Additional outcomes included procedure duration and the incidence of peri- and postprocedural complications.

All outcome data were recorded prospectively. Pain assessments were performed according to a standardized protocol. Clinical data were collected using case report forms and subsequently entered into the study database by the research team.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1). A two-sided independent-samples t-test was selected for the primary analysis, assuming an effect size of d = 0.5 (corresponding to a clinically relevant 1-point difference on the NRS scale with an estimated standard deviation of 2), a significance level of α = 0.05, a power of 0.90, and an allocation ratio of 1:1. Under these assumptions, the calculated sample size was 86 patients per group (172 in total).

Enrollment was completed after reaching the planned sample size, with slight over-enrollment to account for potential loss to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. As none of the variables followed a normal distribution, results are presented as medians with first and third quartiles (Q1;Q3).

Between-group comparisons (transverse vs. longitudinal incision) were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. For 2 × 2 tables with expected cell counts < 5, Fisher’s exact test was used; for larger contingency tables, the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was applied.

To account for multiple comparisons of pain intensity (NRS) at five time points (1, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h), Bonferroni correction was applied, with a significance threshold set at α = 0.01 (0.05/5).

All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Data were collected in a research database (Excel 2021, Microsoft Corp) and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for macOS (version 29, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Between February and August 2025, 222 patients were assessed for eligibility. Thirty-two patients were excluded, including 30 due to chronic opioid use and 2 who declined to participate. A total of 190 patients were randomized, with 94 assigned to the longitudinal incision group and 96 to the transverse incision group (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of participants throughout the study

During follow-up, 20 patients were lost (9 in the longitudinal-incision group and 11 in the transverse-incision group), indicating balanced and non-differential attrition between study arms. Ultimately, 170 patients were included in the final analysis, with 85 patients in each group.

Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the groups, with no clinically meaningful differences (Table 1). The median age of the study population was 65 years, with comparable BMI values between groups. The cohort was predominantly female, reflecting the high proportion of breast cancer cases. The most common malignancies were breast, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers. Approximately 40% of patients had received chemotherapy prior to port implantation, and chronic anticoagulation therapy was present in a small minority of patients. Postoperative pain intensity (NRS) over time according to incision type

At 1 h after the procedure, median pain scores were minimal in both groups [1 (0 ; 1) in the transverse-incision group vs. 0 (0 ; 1) in the longitudinal-incision group]. Across the whole cohort, most patients reported only mild pain, and this pattern remained stable over time. At 24 h, 88.2% of patients still reported NRS scores of 0–2, whereas only 2.4% had pain scores of 5 or higher.

Analgesic requirements were low and similar between groups. Overall, 35.9% of patients required any analgesic within the first 24 h, whereas 64.1% did not require analgesic treatment at all. Among those who used analgesics, most required only one or two doses, and only a minority received three or more doses.

Patient-reported discomfort on day 7 was also low and did not differ between incision types (p = 0.79). The median discomfort score was 1 (1 ; 2) in both groups, and the distribution of scores was comparable, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Overall, 72.4% of patients reported minimal discomfort (score 0–1), while only 2.9% reported scores of 4–5.

Postprocedural complications were infrequent. The most common event was a local inflammatory skin reaction, observed in 4.7% of patients. Hematoma at the port site occurred in four patients, all in the longitudinal-incision group. Other complications were rare and included one case each of port site infection, persistent port site pain, and wound dehiscence. No severe systemic complications were observed.

Discussion

Principal findings

The present study demonstrates that the orientation of the skin incision (transverse vs. longitudinal) does not significantly influence postprocedural pain, patient discomfort, or early complication rates following totally implantable venous access port (TIVAP) implantation. Overall, both techniques were associated with low pain intensity, minimal discomfort, and a very low incidence of complications.

Procedural safety and standardized technique

Our findings confirm that TIVAP implantation is a safe procedure when performed using a standardized protocol. The absence of major complications, such as pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, or arterial puncture, highlights the high level of procedural safety.

In line with previous studies [8], the use of ultrasound guidance and fluoroscopic confirmation likely contributed to reduced complication rates and improved procedural efficiency. In our cohort, these techniques were routinely applied, which may explain the favorable outcomes observed.

Beyond procedural safety, TIVAPs are known to significantly reduce the burden of repeated venipuncture and improve the overall therapeutic experience for oncologic patients [9], supporting their widespread clinical use.

Procedure duration and technical implications

One of the most notable observations of our study is the consistent trend toward shorter procedure duration in the transverse incision group. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, the magnitude and consistency of this effect suggest potential clinical relevance.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies [10] evaluating incision orientation in vascular access procedures, where transverse incisions were associated with shorter operative times, likely due to easier and more intuitive port pocket creation. In our cohort, procedure duration was 20 (18; 30) minutes for transverse incisions and 25 (20; 35) minutes for longitudinal incisions (p = 0.09), supporting a similar trend.

From a practical perspective, this difference may reflect improved ergonomics and more anatomically aligned dissection with the transverse approach. Even modest reductions in procedure time may be meaningful in high-volume settings, particularly when combined with standardized workflows.

Bleeding and port pocket complications

An additional important observation concerns bleeding within the port pocket. Although overall complication rates were low and comparable between groups, excessive bleeding occurred more frequently in the longitudinal incision group.

While this difference was not statistically significant, it may indicate a clinically relevant tendency. One possible explanation is that transverse incisions allow for a more anatomically conforming and controlled pocket formation, potentially reducing tissue trauma and bleeding risk. This hypothesis, although not directly tested, is supported by our procedural experience and warrants further investigation.

Importantly, no increased bleeding risk was observed among patients receiving chronic anticoagulation therapy following appropriate perioperative management in accordance with current guidelines [11].

Pain and patient-reported outcomes

Postprocedural pain was low across all time points, with more than 95% of patients reporting NRS scores between 0 and 2 in the early postoperative period. These findings remained stable at 24 h, and analgesic requirements were minimal.

This is consistent with previous studies [12], which have demonstrated that port implantation under local anesthesia is generally well tolerated, with peak discomfort typically associated with venous puncture and device insertion stages.

Similarly, TIVAP implantation performed as an outpatient procedure has been shown to be safe and associated with a reduced risk of infection [13], further supporting its clinical utility.

Discomfort at day 7 was also low, with more than 70% of patients reporting minimal symptoms. This aligns with patient satisfaction data reported by Kunz-Virk and Krüger [14], where the vast majority of patients reported high satisfaction and willingness to undergo the procedure again.

Complications and comparison with previous studies

The overall complication rate in our study was low and limited to minor events, most commonly local inflammatory reactions and hematomas.

We did not confirm findings reported in earlier studies [15], where higher complication rates were associated with younger age and non-gastrointestinal malignancies. In contrast, our results suggest that complication rates remain low across patient subgroups when standardized implantation techniques are applied.

Periprocedural technical difficulties were infrequent, with guidewire-related issues and port pocket bleeding being the most common events. The absence of differences between groups in operator experience, vascular access, and device characteristics indicates that the observed outcomes are robust and reproducible.

Importantly, infection-related complications were rare in our cohort. This may be partially explained by the selective use of antibiotic prophylaxis in patients at increased risk, according to a local protocol (lymphocyte or neutrophil count < 1,000/µL). Although routine prophylaxis is not generally recommended [16], its use in selected high-risk populations has been described in large clinical cohorts [17].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with specific local protocols and operator experience, which may limit the generalizability of the findings; however, the use of a standardized implantation protocol and the inclusion of a heterogeneous oncologic population support their applicability to similar clinical settings. Second, follow-up was limited to the immediate postoperative period, with pain assessed only within the first 24 hours; therefore, longer-term outcomes, including persistent pain, long-term satisfaction, and device-related quality-of-life impact, were not evaluated. Third, cosmetic outcomes and scar perception were not assessed, although these may be particularly relevant in oncologic populations, especially among patients with breast cancer (Voci et al., 2018) [6]. Future studies should incorporate validated patient-reported measures of scar satisfaction and cosmetic outcomes. Fourth, discomfort at day 7 was assessed using a non-validated 0–5 ordinal scale. Although this approach provided a pragmatic early measure of port-related discomfort and was administered in a standardized manner, its psychometric properties have not been formally established, which may limit comparability with other studies and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should consider incorporating validated patient-reported outcome instruments, to allow comparability across studies. Finally, 20 randomized patients were lost to follow-up. Although attrition was balanced between groups (11 transverse vs. 9 longitudinal), and thus major differential attrition bias appears unlikely, some influence on outcome estimates cannot be completely excluded. In addition, blinding of the operator was not feasible due to the nature of the intervention, and the anesthesiologist performing the procedure was aware of group allocation.

Clinical implications and future directions

Given the comparable outcomes between incision types, the choice of skin incision for TIVAP implantation may be guided primarily by operator preference and technical considerations. However, the observed trend toward shorter procedure duration and a potentially lower risk of port pocket bleeding with transverse incisions suggests that this approach may offer practical advantages in routine clinical practice. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are warranted to determine whether these differences reach statistical significance and translate into clinically meaningful benefits. Such studies should also incorporate validated patient-reported outcome measures, including scar perception, cosmetic satisfaction, and longer-term quality-of-life outcomes, in addition to procedural efficiency, port function, and late complications.

Conclusions

In this randomized study, transverse and longitudinal skin incisions for TIVAP implantation were associated with similarly low levels of postprocedural pain, minimal discomfort, and a low rate of complications.

A consistent trend toward shorter procedure duration and a potentially lower incidence of port pocket bleeding with transverse incisions suggests possible technical advantages of this approach.

Overall, TIVAP implantation is a safe and well-tolerated procedure, and the choice of incision type may be individualized based on operator preference and procedural considerations.
