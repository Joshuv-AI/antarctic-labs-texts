Virus-like particle-based liquid phase blocking ELISA for evaluating the efficacy of O-type foot-and-mouth disease vaccines
Authors: Yun Zhang, Tian Wei, Mei Ren, Suyu Mu, Manyuan Bai, Shuanghui Yin, Shiqi Sun, Huichen Guo, Hu Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractVirus-like particles (VLPs), which closely mimic the structural and antigenic properties of native foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) virions while lacking infectious genetic material, provide a more economical solution by replacing inactivated viruses in liquid-phase blocking ELISA (LPBE) testing for FMD serodiagnosis and vaccine evaluation. In this study, a novel LPBE was developed by replacing inactivated antigens with serotype O foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) VLPs expressed in Escherichia coli. Based on evaluation of sensitivity and specificity, its correlation with virus neutralization test (VNT) results in immunized animal sera, as well as the feasibility and broad applicability for clinical serum sample detection, was further analyzed. Validation showed that VLPs-LPBE achieved high specificity (98.75%) and sensitivity (97.02%). Notably, in quantifying VLP-induced antibodies, the correlation with VNT results (R2 = 0.6809, P < 0.0001) was significantly stronger than that of commercial-LPBE. Blind evaluation of 853 clinical serum samples from vaccinated subjects demonstrated excellent agreement between VLPs-LPBE and conventional LPBE, with a kappa coefficient (κ) of 0.896, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.904, and the concordance rate of 95.8%. These results confirm the clinical reliability and applicability for serological monitoring. In conclusion, VLPs-LPBE provides a safe, economical, and reliable alternative method for the evaluation of FMD vaccines. It eliminates the biosafety risks associated with the handling of inactivated viruses while maintaining compatibility with existing monitoring workflows.Key points• VLPs-based ELISA safely replaces inactivated virus, reducing costs while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.• Stronger correlation with neutralization tests enhances VLPs-vaccine evaluation reliability vs. commercial kits.• High clinical concordance validates workflow compatibility for large-scale serological monitoring.Keywords: FMDV, Liquid-phase blocking ELISA, Virus-like particles, Antibody detection

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a highly contagious disease affecting cloven-hoofed animals, with significant implications for the global trade of live animals and livestock products (Grubman et al. 2004). Foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) belongs to the Aphthovirus genus within Picornaviridae (Logan et al. 2018). During morphogenesis, FMDV exhibits four structural variants with distinct antigenic properties: complete virions (146S antigen), empty capsids (75S antigen) lacking viral nucleic acid, pentamers (12S antigen), and assembled protomers (5S antigen) (Puckette et al. 2022). Among these, the 75S and 146S antigens share the closest structural and conformational similarities and serve as the primary components responsible for eliciting protective immune responses in vaccinated animals (Dong et al. 2021). FMDV virus-like particles (VLPs), composed of capsid proteins, mimic the antigenicity and immunogenicity of inactivated viruses while being non-infectious, offering safer vaccine and diagnostic potential (Guo et al. 2013).

Compared to the “stamping-out” strategy adopted by developed countries, vaccination combined with disease surveillance remains the most practical and effective control measure for developing countries. In China, mandatory immunization policies effectively controlled FMD, eliminating serotype Asia 1 and significantly reducing serotype A prevalence since 2019, according to the World Organisation for Animal Health data (WOAH 2021). Notwithstanding the marked decline in FMD incidence attributable to vaccination and surveillance programs, serotype O persists as the dominant circulating strain across China’s endemic regions (Cabezas et al. 2022; An et al. 2025). Sustained optimization of biosecurity protocols remains imperative to mitigate evolving threats from viral genetic drift and transmission dynamics. In recent years, FMD VLP vaccines have gradually emerged in the market, with the importance increasingly recognized. Due to their safety and high efficacy, VLP vaccines are now considered one of the most promising alternatives to traditional vaccines (Li et al. 2025a, b). The efficacy of newly introduced vaccines post-market launch has attracted substantial attention. Developing scientific and reliable evaluation methods is critical to vaccine research and application. Nowadays, we have witnessed remarkable advancements in FMD diagnostic technologies, with increasingly detection methods now capable of accurately measuring specific immunological parameters (Lu et al. 2007; Kashem et al. 2024; Cao et al. 2025). While novel techniques continue to emerge, the ELISA maintains its position as the benchmark for serological diagnosis in FMD surveillance. Current ELISA-based FMD antibody detection methods include specific antibody detection (Lu et al. 2007; Kashem et al. 2024), non-structural protein antibody testing (Lu et al. 2007; Hosamani et al. 2022), and neutralizing antibody assay (Cao et al. 2025), primarily utilizing indirect, competitive, or blocking formats. Among them, the liquid-phase blocking ELISA (LPBE) is regarded as the standard method for evaluating FMD vaccination efficacy (WOAH 2021). This technique has been consistently employed for evaluating the immunization effectiveness of all types of FMD vaccines. Because inactivated vaccines contain intact viral particles with multiple antigenic epitopes, ELISA can offer reliable reference values for evaluating different types of FMD vaccines. These methods are also simple and efficient for preliminary immune assessment (Ulziibat et al. 2020; Kharatyan et al. 2023). However, the natural conformation of antigenic epitopes in native viral particles may be partially lost due to denaturation during preparation. Likewise, the antigenic epitopes of VLPs may undergo subtle conformational changes due to differences in expression systems and assembly processes. These changes can impact the accuracy of antibody detection, leading to potential discrepancies between immune outcomes and diagnostic results.

In this study, an LPBE method tailored for VLP vaccines was developed. The coating antibody was derived from rabbit serum immunized with FMD VLPs, and the reaction antigen was FMD VLPs. This approach ensures high specificity and reliability in detecting antibody levels against VLPs-specific epitopes, better reflecting the immune efficacy of the vaccine while effectively reducing non-specific binding. In the development and optimization process of FMD VLP vaccines, this method provides critical immunological data, enabling accurate assessment of the vaccine’s immunogenicity and protective efficacy. Although inactivated vaccines currently dominate the veterinary market, the ongoing commercialization of VLP vaccines is steadily increasing their market presence. Market analysis indicates a clear user preference for diagnostic assays with validated performance against the corresponding vaccine platform. This preference ensures that the practical impact of our VLP-LPBE will grow substantially in parallel with the adoption of VLP vaccines. We further validated the consistency of this method with an O-LPB-ELISA Kit (Liquid-phase blocking ELISA kit for detecting foot-and-mouth disease virus O type antibodies, LSBio Co., Ltd, Lanzhou, China) and its correlation with VNT. The results confirm that the utility of this method extends beyond evaluating VLP vaccines, providing valuable reference data for assessing other vaccine platforms and indicating considerable potential for expanded impact.

Materials and methods

Serum samples

(i) Eighty negative serum samples were collected from a designated FMD-free farm in Gansu Province, China, with 40 samples each from healthy, unvaccinated Gansu local pigs (approximately 2–3 months old, mixed gender) and Gansu indigenous cattle (approximately 1-year-old, mixed gender), respectively. All samples were assured to be free of anti-FMDV antibodies by three commercial ELISA kits: the O‑LPB‑ELISA Kit and A‑LPB‑ELISA Kit (liquid-phase blocking ELISA kit for detecting foot-and-mouth disease virus A type antibodies, LSBio Co., Ltd, Lanzhou, China), as well as a blocking ELISA kit for in vitro detection of antibodies against foot-and-mouth disease virus nonstructural protein 3ABC (LSBio Co., Ltd, Lanzhou, China). (ii) A total of 168 serum samples (mixed gender) were obtained from Gansu local pigs (n = 98, 2–3 months old) and Gansu indigenous cattle (n = 70, 7–8 months old) experimentally infected with FMDV serotype O FMDV (O/MYA98/BY/2010) in the BSL-3 (biosafety level 3 laboratory) facility at the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (LVRI). Samples were collected at 7–14 days post-infection during the peak antibody response. (iii) A total of 280 serum samples were collected from pigs (n = 115; 1–2 months old) and cattle (n = 165; 5–6 months old) immunized with a single dose of FMDV type O VLP vaccines (HUΛYUBio Co., Ltd, Tengchong, China) at 0–28 days post-vaccination (dpv). All samples were Gansu local breeds and mixed gender. (iv) A total of 161 serum samples (134 from pigs and 27 from cattle of the same breeds and age as described in (iii)) were collected from animals immunized with inactivated FMDV vaccine (CAVETBio Co., Ltd, Lanzhou, China) at 0–28 dpv. (v) To assess assay specificity, serum samples were obtained from the LVRI biorepository, including the following: FMDV serotype Asia 1 (n = 10), FMDV serotype A (n = 25), bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV, n = 10), porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV, n = 10), classical swine fever virus (CSFV, n = 10), peste des petits ruminants virus (PPRV, n = 10), swine vesicular disease virus (SVDV, n = 10), and seneca valley virus A (SVA, n = 10). (vi) A total of 853 pigs and bovine serum samples with unknown vaccination status were randomly collected from Tianshui City (Gansu Province) and Luoyang City (Henan Province) for the evaluation of vaccine coverage and post-vaccination antibody responses. All samples were derived from local breeds, with pigs aged 3–4 months and cattle approximately 1 year old.

Preparation of serotype O FMDV VLPs

Positive recombinant bacterial strains containing plasmids encoding FMDV structural proteins (VP0, VP1, and VP3) were used for protein expression in a prokaryotic expression system (Guo et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2022). The harvested bacteria were lysed by sonication, and the lysate was centrifuged. The supernatant was purified using a Ni2⁺ affinity chromatography column. Proteins were assembled in assembly buffer at a defined volume ratio, followed by enzymatic cleavage to remove tags. The cleaved products were further purified using another round of Ni2⁺ affinity chromatography and then subjected to sucrose density gradient centrifugation (SDGC). FMDV VLPs were collected from the tube corresponding to the highest optical density (OD) peak. The purity and characteristics of the VLPs were evaluated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), western blot, dynamic light scattering (DLS), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The concentration of VLPs was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Preparation of enzyme-labeled rabbit antibody

Immunization of animals and purification of serum

Three adult rabbits were subcutaneously immunized at multiple sites with 500 µg of purified serotype O VLPs emulsified with Freund’s complete adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Rabbits were boosted with 500 µg VLPs antigen emulsified with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 14, 28, and 42 dpv. Sera were collected from rabbits at 56 dpv and stored at −80 °C.

Conjugation of rabbit immune serum with horseradish peroxidase

IgG antibodies were initially isolated from rabbit sera using the saturated ammonium sulfate precipitation method and then purified through Protein A-Sepharose™ (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA). The polyclonal anti-rabbit IgG was immediately labeled with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) using a commercially available FlyLink™ HRP Labeling Kit (DaKeWe, Shenzhen, China) according to the standard protocol.

Standardization of the LPBE

Briefly, 96-well ELISA plates (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) were coated with purified rabbit IgG (1.0 µg/well) diluted in carbonate buffer solution (pH 9.6) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Serum samples, serially diluted from 1:4 to 1:512, were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a predetermined concentration of VLPs diluted in phosphate-buffered saline with Tween (PBST) (0.1% Tween 20 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)). The next day, the mixtures were transferred to the wells immobilized in the preceding step. Four wells served as antigen controls and received VLPs only. Plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. In this step, unbound VLPs that did not react with serum antibodies were captured by rabbit IgG. Following washing with PBST, the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with HRP-conjugated rabbit antibodies. After washing the plates, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Surmodics, Inc., Eden Prairie, MN, USA) was added. After 10 min at 37 °C, 2 M H2SO4 was added to each well to stop the reaction. Finally, the OD value was measured at a wavelength of OD450nm.

To determine the critical value, four antigen control wells were analyzed. The highest and lowest OD450nm values of the antigen control wells were excluded, and 50% of the average of the remaining two OD450nm values was used as the critical value for calculating the antibody titer. Serum samples with OD450nm values above the threshold were considered negative. Samples with OD450nm values equal to or below the threshold were considered positive. The antibody titer was recorded as the highest dilution yielding a positive result. If the threshold fell between two dilutions, the geometric mean of the reciprocal dilution factors of the adjacent positive and negative wells was used to determine the titer. For example, if the critical threshold falls between 1:128 (2.1 log10) and 1:256 (2.4 log10), the antibody titer was assigned as 1:180 (2.25 log10).

Evaluation of LPBE

Determination of the LPBE cut-off value and analysis sensitivity and specificity

The cut-off value of the VLPs-based ELISA (designated as VLPs-LPBE) was established using 248 serum samples from diverse sources: 80 FMDV serotype O-negative sera for specificity (Sp) evaluation and 168 infected sera with varying antibody titers for sensitivity (Se) assessment. Antibody titers obtained by VLPs-LPBE were analyzed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the ROC curve (AUC) value distributions using GraphPad software (San Diego, CA, USA) to determine the optimal VLPs-LPBE cut-off value.

Repeatability test of LPBE

To evaluate the repeatability of the VLPs-LPBE method, intra-batch and inter-batch precision tests were conducted to measure the consistency of results under the same operating conditions. Three replicates of each control sample (n = 10) were analyzed within the same assay plate. The same control samples were analyzed in triplicate across three separate assay plates on different days. The variation coefficient (CV) was calculated using the following formula: CV (%) = (standard deviation/mean) × 100.

Cross-reactivity experiment

The specificity of the VLPs-LPBE was further confirmed by testing cross-reactivity with positive sera from serotype A FMD, serotype Asia 1 FMD, and other diseases, including bovine viral diarrhea (BVD), porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS), classical swine fever (CSF), peste des petits ruminants (PPR), swine vesicular disease (SVD), and seneca virus A (SVA).

Comparison of VLPs-LPBE, commercial-LPBE (O-LPB-ELISA Kit), and VNT

A total of 280 serum samples from animals immunized with VLP vaccines (with varying antibody titers) were analyzed using two LPBE methods. The results were compared to evaluate the accuracy and consistency of both methods under standardized conditions. Samples with inconsistent ELISA results were further analyzed by VNT.

A total of 253 serum samples (92 samples from animals immunized with VLP vaccines and 161 from those immunized with inactivated vaccines) were analyzed using the same two LPBE methods. Neutralizing antibody titers were used as a reference to assess the correlation between LPBE results and VNT outcomes. Particular emphasis should be warranted that VNT is inherently impractical for large-scale sero-surveillance due to its technical complexity, biosafety requirements for live virus, and substantial time and cost demands. Given our aim to develop a high-throughput monitoring assay, we limited VNT to a subset of samples for correlation analysis only.

Application of VLPs-LPBE for testing clinical serum samples

To evaluate the applicability of VLPs-LPBE in immune monitoring, 853 clinical serum samples from pigs and cattle were tested using both LPBE methods. A blinded assessment was conducted to evaluate the concordance between both detection methods under masked immunization conditions, so detailed information regarding the vaccine strains and valency (strains, monovalent/multivalent, etc.) may be unavailable. Nevertheless, the assay demonstrated broad applicability across vaccine products from different manufacturers, as the VLPs were engineered based on currently circulating serotype O FMDV strains. Statistical analysis of the results was performed using SAS software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The results were used to evaluate the utility of the VLPs-LPBE in routine immune monitoring.

Results

Calculation of the cut-off value, specificity, and sensitivity

The VLPs-LPBE was employed to determine antibody titers for 248 serum samples (Fig. 1A). The optimal cut-off value of 1.88 was calculated using the ROC curve analysis, with the sensitivity of 97.02% and specificity of 98.75%, respectively (Fig. 1B). The results were further supported by an AUC value of 0.9965. According to the cut-off criteria, samples exhibiting antibody titers ≥ 1.88 were classified as positive, while those below this threshold were designated as negative.

Cross-reactivity experiments were performed to evaluate the specificity of the VLPs-LPBE using positive sera against two distinct FMDV serotypes and six other pathogenic agents. Comparison of serum detection methods in VLP vaccine immune sera

A total of 280 post-vaccination serum samples with FMDV type O VLP vaccines (at different dpv) were analyzed by the VLPs-LPBE. The results were compared with those obtained from the commercial-LPBE. The qualitative comparison of antibody titers between the two methods revealed a high degree of concordance. As shown in Fig. 3 A, 119 serum samples were detected as negative and 113 were detected as negative by commercial-LPBE. Furthermore, the number of positive samples detected by the two methods was 161 and 167, respectively. Among six samples with discrepant results, five were consistent with the results of the VLPs-LPBE after VNT detection. The two methods identified 126 and 112 weakly positive samples, along with 35 and 55 strongly positive samples, respectively. These results further suggest that the majority of positive serum samples exhibited higher antibody titers when tested by the commercial-LPBE.

Correlation detection between VLPs-LPBE and VNT in VLP vaccine immune sera

A total of 92 sera (derived from 280 animals vaccinated with the VLP vaccines) were selected for further evaluation using the VNT. The antibody titers for each serum sample were statistically analyzed to assess the correlation between the two LPBE methods and the VNT. Statistical analysis revealed a higher correlation between the VLPs-LPBE and the VNT (R2 = 0.6809, P < 0.001) compared to the commercial-LPBE, indicating that the VLPs-LPBE more accurately reflects the neutralizing antibody levels detected by the VNT (Fig. 4A).

Applicability of VLPs-LPBE in immune antibody monitoring

A total of 853 field serum samples were analyzed using both the VLPs-LPBE and the commercial-LPBE. As shown in Fig. 5 and Table 3, the commercial-LPBE detected 659 positive and 194 negative sera, whereas the VLPs-LPBE detected 645 positive and 208 negative sera. The two methods showed a high level of agreement, with a positive concordance rate of 97.55% and a negative concordance rate of 92.04%. The kappa coefficient (κ) and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) were calculated to evaluate the level of agreement between the two methods. As shown in Tables 4 and 5, the κ value of 0.896 indicated almost perfect agreement between the commercial-LPBE and the VLPs-LPBE. Additionally, the ICC value of 0.904 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.867–0.928) demonstrated excellent reliability between the two methods in evaluating FMD immunization status.

Discussion

The commercial-LPBE based on inactivated antigens (conventional LPBE) remains the most widely adopted method for evaluating the immune efficacy of FMD vaccines. Given the demonstrated feasibility of VLPs as substitutes for inactivated FMDV antigens (Basagoudanavar et al. 2013; Ran et al. 2019) and recognizing the ongoing optimization of antigens and antibodies as a core objective in new-generation diagnostics, the use of non-infectious VLPs assay offers superior biosafety and production scalability over the conventional LPBE (Morioka et al. 2009; Lee et al. 2022). In this study, a VLPs-LPBE was developed and exhibited performance comparable to the commercial-LPBE. Moreover, it shows strong potential as a companion diagnostic for evaluating the immunological efficacy of serotype O FMD VLPs. Evaluation of our data indicates that the VLPs-LPBE assay exhibits high diagnostic performance, with sensitivity and specificity rates of 97.02% and 98.75%, respectively. Furthermore, it shows a statistically significant correlation with the VNT (R2 = 0.6809, P < 0.0001). Hence, the VLPs-LPBE provides a new option to assess serotype O FMD inactivated vaccine efficacy, while providing an evaluation criterion for their corresponding VLP vaccines.

To circumvent the biosafety issues inherent in the production of conventional inactivated FMDV antigens (Liu et al. 2017), the development of safer ELISA platforms has been a long-standing goal. Initial alternatives, such as recombinant protein-based ELISA (Ko et al. 2012), were constrained by inadequate conformational epitope integrity. The subsequent emergence of VLPs-based technologies has provided a superior solution, including A-type FMDV VLPs-based LPB ELISA (Basagoudanavar et al. 2013) and O-type FMDV VLPs-based competitive ELISA (Ran et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020). These methods were as applicable as the conventional ELISA for evaluating sera from inactivated-vaccine-immunized animals. Furthermore, the authors highlighted the need to develop specific assays for VLP vaccines, which, though not yet commercially available as FMD VLPs-vaccine at the time, were already demonstrating considerable promise. Compared with the competitive ELISA, the assay described herein generates specific antibody titers that provide a more nuanced dataset that surpasses simple positive/negative classification. In the evaluation of VLP vaccines-induced immune responses, the VLPs-LPBE was observed to correlate more closely with the VNT (R2 = 0.6809, P < 0.001) than the commercial-LPBE did (R2 = 0.6309, P < 0.001). This is further supported by our recent research (Li et al. 2025a, b), which demonstrated that a VLPs-based neutralizing antibody LPBE correlates more strongly with protection post-VLPs vaccination than the conventional LPBE. Therefore, the method developed in the present study effectively meets this need by providing a well-suited assay for the recently licensed VLP vaccines. The strong concordance between the VLPs-LPBE and commercial-LPBE underscores the high fidelity of VLPs antigenicity in mimicking native virions. However, minor quantitative discrepancies in titer—though not affecting qualitative outcomes—were noted specifically with VLP-vaccines sera (Fig. 3A), potentially attributable to differences in epitope conformation. Although a direct comparison between 75 and 146S particles is lacking, the marked reduction in immunogenicity upon the dissociation of 146S into 12S subunits (Li et al. 2024) underscores the critical importance of structural integrity. This implies that conformational sensitivity likely extends to serological assays, where any variation in VLPs assembly or stability could affect antigen-antibody binding during the LPBE’s pre-reaction step, leading to titer discrepancies. It is plausible that the observed divergence is rooted in fundamental differences between the vaccines during the immunization process (Doel 2003). This underscores why it is critical to use a well-matched diagnostic assay for accurate serological evaluation. Furthermore, as compared with the commercial-LPBE, our VLPs-LPBE demonstrated lower cross-reactivity. Among 25 FMD serotype A positive serum samples, nearly 50% showed cross-reactivity when tested with commercial-LPBE, whereas none reacted with the VLPs-LPBE, which substantially improves the accuracy of serotype-specific immune response assessment. The enhanced specificity likely stems from the defined composition of VLPs, which are self-assembled solely from structural proteins, yielding a pure and consistent antigenic (Xiao et al. 2016). In contrast, inactivated viruses involve more complex compositions and purification processes, which may introduce non-specific binding (Du et al. 2019; Hardham et al. 2020). This distinction is particularly critical for precise serological evaluations, as it reduces the risk of misinterpretation caused by cross-reactive antibodies. Nevertheless, a limitation of this study lies in its lack of cross-reactivity assessment against diverse serotype O topotypes, which is essential for evaluating the broad applicability of our assay. To address this, we are now collecting immunized sera against more prevalent strains to experimentally validate assay performance, while also developing VLPs representing other circulating serotype O strains. These VLPs will serve as key reagents for future vaccine-matched diagnostic applications.

In conclusion, a major advancement of this work is the concurrent evaluation of the immunogenicity of inactivated and VLP vaccines using our assay, revealing differences in their antibody detection profiles. The VLPs-LPBE described herein represents a significant advancement in FMD serology, combining high diagnostic accuracy with improved safety and production efficiency. Its economic advantage is driven by a low-cost E. coli expression system, coupled with the complete avoidance of high-cost BSL-3 facilities. Its performance characteristics support its adoption not only for evaluation of VLP vaccines but also as a versatile tool for broad serological monitoring in diverse epidemiological settings. Future studies should explore its applicability to other FMDV serotypes and potential integration into multiplex platforms for comprehensive immune profiling.
