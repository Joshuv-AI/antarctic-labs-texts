Intersection of Spinal Muscular Atrophy and Progressive Myoclonic Epilepsy Syndrome: ASAH1 Gene
Authors: Farsana Mustafa, Shariq Sha, Wiliam L Macken, Lindsay A Wilson, Ajay Garg, Neerja Gupta, Rohit Bhatia, Roopa Rajan, Anu Gupta, Mamta B Singh, Robert D S Pitceathly, Mary M Reilly, Michael G Hanna, Venugopalan V Vishnu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Acid ceramidase deficiency manifests in two distinct clinical forms: first, as a rare lysosomal storage disorder termed Farber’s disease (FD), and, second, as an epileptic disorder known as progressive myoclonic epilepsy and spinal muscular atrophy (PME-SMA). Both disorders are the result of mutations in the ASAH1 gene, which codes for a lysosomal hydrolase that can break down the bioactive lipid ceramide.[1] SMA is characterized by the progressive loss of lower motor neurons and diffuse muscle wasting.[2] PME-SMA syndromes are non-5q SMA syndromes with the age of onset ranging from 3–15 years.[3] The disease was first described clinically by Jankovic and Rivera in 1979.[4] PME-SMA is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by muscle weakness, wasting, and a combination of drug-resistant epilepsy and myoclonic jerks from childhood. We report one of the earliest genetically confirmed cases of PME–-SMA from India, presenting initially with symptoms of SMA at 10 years of age and later developing myoclonic jerks during the disease course.

A 37-year-old man of Indo-Aryan ethnicity was born to non-consanguineous parents and had normal development. He had insidious onset of gradually progressive difficulty climbing stairs and rising from squatting with onset at 10 years of age. The lower-limb weakness subsequently plateaued and has remained relatively static over the past 6 years. He has had difficulty in lifting heavy weights with his bilateral upper limbs for the past 6 years. He also reported a history of occasional myoclonic jerks during wakefulness but predominantly occurring at night for the past 2 years. There was no history of seizures, speech difficulties, gait imbalance, or tremors of the upper limbs. He had no history of similar illness in his family or history of similar symptoms in his family. On examination, he had intact higher mental functions with a single breath count of 45. Motor system examination showed tongue atrophy and proximal bilateral upper limb and lower limb weakness with a Medical Research Council Muscle Score of 4/5 with hyporeflexia and atrophy of proximal muscles more than distal muscles. Clinically, the patient had a lower motor neuron (LMN) type of proximal predominant symmetrical quadriparesis with myoclonic jerks. His complete blood count, renal function test, liver function test, and thyroid function tests were within normal limits. Creatine kinase was 746 IU/L (24–195 IU/L). Nerve conduction study and electromyography (EMG) showed pure motor neurogenic involvement in the cervical and lumbar segments with chronic reinnervation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain was normal and muscle MRI showed severe fatty infiltration in the gluteus medius, vastus lateralis, rectus femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps longus, and soleus muscles [Figure 1]. Electroencephalography (EEG) showed intermittent generalized spike/polyspike and wave discharges at 3–3.5 Hz, 220 microvolts with a bifrontal maximum [Figure 2]. Whole-exome sequencing revealed a homozygous ASAH1 variant (c.124A>G; p.Thr42Ala), classified as pathogenic based on American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria (PM3-strong, PM1, PM2, PM5-moderate, and PP2, PP3-supporting). Acid ceramidase activity could not be measured due to the non-availability of this test. The patient was initiated on levetiracetam 500 mg twice daily, and, after one month, there was a marked improvement in the myoclonic jerks.

PME-SMA typically presents by the age of 2 years with progressive LMN weakness, muscle atrophy, recurrent falls, tremors, and myoclonic jerks. Such patients with PME may exhibit slow and sharp bilateral waves of 3–4 Hz on an EEG around the age of 7 years.[5] Our patient had proximal predominant LMN quadriparesis starting at age 10, later developing myoclonic jerks with generalized 3–3.5 Hz spike and wave discharges. A marked reduction or complete absence of acid ceramidase activity is typically associated with FD, whereas a milder reduction of acid ceramidase is generally associated with the PME-SMA syndrome. The activity of the acid ceramidase enzyme in FD is typically below 10%. FD represents the severe end of the disease spectrum, with onset in infancy and a median survival of approximately 3 years. The classical triad of FD is characterized by the formation of subcutaneous nodules, painful and swollen joints, and the development of a hoarse voice or aphonia.[1] Dysphonia is a hallmark feature of FD and is uncommon in SMA phenotype ASAH1-related disorders.[6]

PME-SMA is an ultra-rare disease with a prevalence of less than 1 in 1,000,000.[7] We have reviewed the phenotypic spectrum of this neurological disorder as presented in previously published cases in Supplementary Table 1. Most reported patients have disease onset before 10 years of age and present with LMN quadriparesis, with myoclonic jerks appearing later in the disease course. In contrast, Yin et al.[8] reported a case with epilepsy and marked atrophy of the cerebral and cerebellar cortex, with weakness developing 4 years later. This suggests that CNS involvement preceded peripheral nervous system (PNS) involvement. Myoclonic seizure is the most prevalent seizure type in SMA-PME, manifesting as both positive and negative myoclonia, in addition to tremors or twitches that do not elicit corresponding EEG discharges. Death resulting from respiratory complications has been observed to occur within 5–15 years following the initial onset of the disease. In the present case, despite the onset of the disease at age ten, the disease has been slowly progressive. The manifestation of myoclonic jerks was notably delayed, with no evidence of respiratory involvement till date. Filosto et al.[5] reported the first cases of SMA in adults, characterized by no myoclonic epilepsy owing to an ASAH 1 c.124A>G p.Thr 42Ala homozygous mutation in exon 2, in both siblings born of a consanguineous parentage. In addition, Puma et al.[6] described a case in which one ASAH1 allele had a similar mutation in a compound heterozygous form and presented with a Farber-like multisystem involvement with LMN involvement [Supplementary Table 1]. All of these patients had a very slow progression of symptoms, as in our case.

SMA-PME due to ASAH1 variants should be suspected in patients with childhood-onset progressive proximal predominant weakness along with normal early milestones and neurogenic EMG changes especially when SMN1 testing is negative. The later emergence of myoclonic jerks with ictal EEG polyspike-wave correlates, with or without generalized seizures, in the absence of early features of classic Farber syndrome (FS) further supports this diagnosis. A mixed Farber syndrome/PME-SMA phenotype has been reported in a few cases and may present with polyarticular arthritis or hoarseness in addition to progressive motor neuronopathy and later-emerging myoclonus.[9] Recent case series have further delineated the natural history of SMA-PME, showing variability in the age of onset and progression of motor neuronopathy and epilepsy, highlighting the potential role of biomarkers such as C26-ceramide levels and in-cell ceramidase activity assays for disease monitoring.[10] PME-SMA is a rare disorder that affects both the CNS and PNS. More information on the phenotypic and genotypic spectrum of the disorder may assist in the future development of enzyme replacement therapy and gene therapy for ceramidase deficiency.
