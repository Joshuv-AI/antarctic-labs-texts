A large cohort analysis of metabolic signatures underlying the liver-eye axis
Authors: Mingxing Wu, Xueqin Li, Mingzhi Liu, Jun Zhang, Jingxin Zhou, Kai Jin, Zuyun Liu, Yumei Mao, Yuan Gao, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of the liver, a vital metabolic organ, in ocular health and the corresponding mechanisms remains unclear. This study aimed to examine the association between liver function and retinal thickness, and whether metabolic signatures (MS) of liver function mediate these associations.MethodsWe used data from 31019 participants in UK Biobank. Liver function was measured using seven serum-based circulating biomarkers: alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, gamma-glutamyltransferase, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin, total protein, and albumin levels. Retinal thickness was measured using optical coherence tomography, including the retinal nerve fiber layer, ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer, inner nuclear layer, inner nuclear layer-external limiting membrane, external limiting membrane-inner and outer photoreceptor segments, inner and outer photoreceptor segments-retinal pigment epithelium (ISOSRPE), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). The circulating metabolome was quantified using nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. A linear regression model and formal mediation analyses were performed.ResultsWe find that abnormal liver function is significantly associated with increased RPE thickness (β [SE]: 0.094(0.034); P = 0.021) and decreased ISOSRPE thickness (β [SE]: −0.172 (0.048); P < 0.001), after adjusting for demographic, lifestyle factors, best-corrected visual acuity, and intraocular pressures. Among the 249 metabolites, 23 are selected using lasso regression to construct MS for liver function. The mediation proportion of MS in association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness is 28.6% (P = 0.004). Among the 23 metabolites, six play a significant mediating role in the association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness, with mediation proportions ranging from 3.20% to 16.4%.ConclusionThis study demonstrates significant associations of liver function with retinal thickness and reveals potential underlying metabolomic mechanisms, providing insights into the liver-eye axis.Subject terms: Epidemiology, Predictive markers

Introduction

As of 2023, liver diseases, including cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, and liver cancer, caused over 2 million deaths annually, accounting for 4% of total global deaths, with 1 in 25 deaths attributed to liver diseases1. Eye diseases are related to visual impairment, affecting visual quality and function. More severe conditions, such as inflammatory eye diseases, including keratitis and uveitis, may be related to retinal nerve damage and even blindness2. Similar to the classic theory, “the liver governs the eyes”, in traditional Chinese medicine, liver-eye connections have received considerable attention from researchers. Studies have shown that the average intraocular pressure (IOP) level in Asian adults increases linearly with the grade of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease. Reduced retinal thickness/macular volume may be related to cirrhosis3. Genome-wide association studies have shown that hepatic lipase site variants are associated with late age-related macular degeneration (AMD)4. The regulation of retinal iron levels depends on liver-secreted hepcidin (Hepc)5. The liver is an important organ for synthesizing B vitamins, including folic acid6. Experimental studies have demonstrated that folic acid protects retinal ganglion cells from death and alleviates diabetic retinal microangiopathy by regulating DNA methylation/hydroxymethylation metabolism7–9. Some communication factors between the liver and eyes have been identified, such as the fibroblast growth factor (FGF)10. However, large epidemiological studies exploring the association between liver function and retinal thickness are lacking, which could enhance our understanding of the liver-eye connection.

Maintaining homeostasis is a complex process that involves almost all organs. The liver plays a central role in metabolism, and liver dysfunction is often accompanied by pathological phenotypes in distant organs, including the eye11. Previous studies have reported that multiple metabolomics markers (extremely large and very large lipoprotein particle concentrations, very low-density lipoprotein triglycerides, small high-density lipoprotein particles, glycoprotein acetyls, monounsaturated and saturated fatty acids, and amino acids) are positively associated with liver fat in the UK Biobank (UKB)12. Some studies have found that alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels are associated with liver inflammation13–15. In addition, recent evidence has highlighted more than 100 putatively causal relationships between plasma metabolites and advanced AMD, especially glycerophospholipid metabolism, lysophospholipids, triacylglycerols, and long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid pathways16. A small number of metabolites were associated with liver- or eye-related diseases, indicating the potential to explore the underlying mechanisms of the liver-eye relationship from the perspective of metabolites. Thus, the question of whether metabolomics partially mediates the association between the liver and eyes is of great interest but remains unexplored in previous literature. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is an imaging technique that provides accurate retinal thickness information noninvasively and cost-effectively, with special significance in chronic disease screening. OCT technology provides a new perspective for studying the specific conditions of the liver and eyes in the human body and further exploring the association between the liver and eye organs.

Therefore, using data from UKB, this study aims to explore the association between liver function and thickness of OCT-measured retinal layers. Furthermore, this study aims to investigate the role of metabolomics as a mediator in these associations. We find that abnormal liver function is significantly associated with decreased thickness of the retinal layer (i.e., inner and outer photoreceptor segments-retinal pigment epithelium, ISOSRPE), and 28.6% of this association is mediated by the metabolic signature (MS) of liver function constructed by 23 metabolites. This study provides new evidence for the intrinsic link between the liver and eye.

Methods

Study participants

The UKB is a large-scale, population-based study that recruited approximately 500,000 participants aged 40–70 years old in the UK17. During the baseline assessment from 2006 to 2010, multidimensional data were collected through interviews, physical measurements, questionnaires, and biological samples. Ophthalmological assessments, including OCT imaging, were conducted between 2009 and 2010. The UKB database was approved by the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the UKB, and researchers were allowed to use data without additional ethical clearance18,19. More comprehensive information about the UKB is available online (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/). The present study included two analyses: Analysis 1 aimed to explore the association between liver function and retinal thickness in analytic sample 1, and Analysis 2 aimed to investigate the role of MS of liver function as a mediator in the associations in analytic sample 2 (Fig. 1). First, among 502,235 participants in UKB, 465,763 participants were excluded according to the following criteria20: 1) without OCT imaging data (n = 435,124); 2) with poor image quality (n = 30,639): image quality score less than 45 (right eyes: n = 6105, left eyes: n = 5151), poor centration certainty, and segmentation certainty (poorest 20% of images excluded based on each of the segmentation) (right eyes: n = 19,448, left eyes: n = 19,686); 3) with poor refraction and visual acuity: high refractive error (> ± 6 diopters [D]) (right eyes: n = 1765, left eyes: n = 1957), visual acuity of worse than 0.1 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (right eyes: n = 8722, left eyes: n = 8353), and eyes with a Goldmann-corrected IOP > 21 mmHg (or if 0 mmHg) (right eyes: n = 1919, left eyes: n = 2242); 4) with self-reported glaucoma, retinal, or macular disease (right eyes: n = 2028, left eyes: n = 1669). If both eyes of one participant were eligible for inclusion in this analysis, one eye was randomly selected. Second, 5453 participants were excluded because of missing data on liver function and other covariates of interest. Finally, 31,019 participants were included in the analytic sample 1. After excluding 13,910 participants with missing data on NMR-based metabolic biomarkers, 17,109 participants were included in analytic sample 2.

OCT imaging and retinal thickness measurement

In the UKB, spectral-domain OCT was performed using the Topcon 3D OCT-1000 Mark II (Topcon, Inc, Japan) using a fovea-centered volume scan mode (512 horizontal A-scans per B-scan; 128 B-scans in a 6 × 6 mm2 raster pattern) in a dark room without pupil dilation. The average thickness across the nine subregions of the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid was used in our analysis21. The retinal layers were automatically segmented using the Topcon Advanced Boundary Segmentation (TABS) algorithm (version 1.6.1.1), and the retinal thickness was calculated after the segmentation of nine retinal boundaries. Seven retinal layer thicknesses were computed: external limiting membrane-inner and outer photoreceptor segments (ELMISOS), inner nuclear layer-external limiting membrane (INLELM), retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), ISOSRPE, ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GCIPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), and retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness. The following quality control measures were included during data collection: 1) image quality score, 2) internal limiting membrane indicator, 3) validity count, and 4) motion indicators22. These measures have been previously described and incorporated into the international consensus reporting guidelines for OCT metrics.

Liver function

According to previous studies23,24, liver function was measured using seven serum-based circulating biomarkers: ALT, Aspartate transaminase (AST), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin (TBIL), total protein (TP), and albumin (ALB). Each biomarker was classified as normal or abnormal based on the reference range determined using a Beckman Coulter AU5800 analyzer (https://www.beckmancoulter.com/support/tech-docs). The normal ranges of ALT, AST, TBIL, GGT, ALP, TP, and ALB were 7–52 U/L, 13–39 U/L, 5–17 μmol/L, 9–64 U/L, 34–104 U/L, 64–89 g/L, and 35–57 g/L, respectively25. If abnormal biomarkers were found, the participant was identified as having abnormal liver function.

Metabolomics measurement

Metabolic biomarkers were measured from approximately 270,000 UKB non-fasting EDTA plasma samples using a high-throughput 1H- nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics platform developed by Nightingale Health, Ltd. (Helsinki, Finland; nightingalehealth.com; biomarker quantification version 2021). The procedure and application of the NMR metabolomics platform have been described previously. This method allows for the simultaneous quantification of 249 metabolic biomarkers, including 168 directly measured and 81 derived biomarkers. Among them, 37 clinically validated metabolic biomarkers have been approved for diagnostic use. The measured metabolic biomarkers include amino acids, ketone bodies, lipids, fatty acids, lipoprotein subclass distribution, particle size, and composition26. In epidemiological analyses, NMR biomarker data in the UKB can generally be used without preprocessing and can, in principle, be analyzed in the same manner as the clinical chemistry data available in the UKB. During the quality control procedures, biomarker values that were heavily affected by interfering substances were removed27.

Covariates

In the present study, we adjusted for multiple covariates, including age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), Townsend deprivation index (TDI), smoking status, drinking status, regular exercise, current employment status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, liver disease, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and IOP. Ethnicity included white and non-white (Asian, Black, Chinese, and other ethnic backgrounds). TDI was calculated based on the participants’ area upon recruitment in the UKB study. TDI was a measure of socioeconomic status, with a higher score suggesting higher socioeconomic deprivation28. Smoking and drinking statuses were self-reported and classified as never, former, or current smokers or drinkers. Current employment status was categorized as employed or unemployed. Regular exercise was classified as yes if participants undertook 75 min of vigorous activity or 150 min of moderate activity or an equivalent combination thereof per week. Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and liver disease were collected from a self-reported touchscreen questionnaire and a verbal interview conducted by trained staff members. Considering the potential association of BCVA/IOP measurements with retinal thickness, we included these ocular measurements as covariates.

Statistics and reproducibility

The baseline characteristics of the participants were described as numbers and percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables. P values were generated using the χ2 and Kruskal-Wallis tests for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. In analytic sample 1, linear models were used to estimate the association between liver function and the retinal layer thickness. Beta (β) and Standard Error (SE) were documented in three models. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 was further adjusted for TDI, ethnicity, current employment status, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, and regular exercise based on model 1; Model 3 was further adjusted for BCVA, and IOP based on model 2. P-values adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure were used to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

In analytic sample 2, three steps were performed to examine the mediating role of metabolomics in the association between liver function and retinal layer thickness. All metabolites were standardized using the z-score, and KNN interpolation was performed before the analyses. First, we selected liver function-related metabolites and constructed an MS. It was constructed in two steps: 1) a linear regression model was used to initially select metabolites significantly associated with liver function (the model was adjusted for age and sex); 2) lasso regression was used to select metabolites. A two-step cross-validation approach was used to determine the regularization parameters: optimal α = 1 was first identified through 10-fold cross-validation, and subsequently, with α fixed at 1, λ was selected as the largest value within one standard error of the minimum cross-validation error, resulting in a final λ = 0.0047 (Figure S1a, b). The MS was constructed as a weighted sum of metabolites with non-zero coefficients and then standardized. The change in MS indicates a change in the aggregate effect of the weighted sum of the selected metabolites29. Second, we used linear regression to assess the association between liver function and retinal layer thickness, and the association between liver function and metabolites and MS, and the association between retinal layer thickness and metabolites and MS. Finally, mediation analysis was conducted to assess whether the selected metabolites and MS mediated the association between liver function and retinal layer thickness. Mediation analyses were conducted using the R “mediation” package, with 95% confidence intervals estimates obtained via percentile bootstrapping (500 resamples).

To assess the robustness of the above associations, several sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) we performed stratified analyses by age (age < 60 vs. age ≥ 60), sex (female vs. male), IOP ( > 18 mmHg vs. ≤ 18 mmHg), type 2 diabetes duration ( > 5 years vs. without type 2 diabetes), and BMI ( > 30 kg/m² vs. ≤ 30 kg/m²) to evaluate whether the associations differed by subgroup; (2) we repeated the analyses after excluding participants who developed liver disease; (3) we repeated the analyses with further adjustment for aspirin use, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and liver disease; (4) we further evaluated the association of liver function with the risk of diabetic retinopathy and the association of MS (conducted based on liver function) with the risk of diabetic retinopathy using Cox proportional hazard regression model. Participants were categorized as having a “high” MS status if their MS exceeded the threshold of the population median minus 0.0912, and “low” otherwise. Incident diabetic retinopathy was identified during follow-up through hospital inpatient records according to the International Classification of Diseases −10, code H36030. In these analyses, hazard ratios (HR) and 95%CI were documented in three models. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 was further adjusted for TDI, ethnicity, current employment status, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, and regular exercise based on model 1; Model 3 was further adjusted for aspirin use, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and liver disease based on model 2.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R software version 4.3.1. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The mean ages of the 31,019 and 17,109 recruited participants were 56.04 (SD: 8.23) years in analytic sample 1 and 56.18 (SD: 8.19) years in analytic sample 2, respectively. The majority were female (53.0% and 52.6%) and white (91.8% and 93.3%, respectively). A total of 10,893 and 6068 participants were assessed for abnormal liver function. Compared with participants with normal liver function, those with abnormal liver function were more likely to be older, male, have higher TDI scores, smoke, drink, exercise less regularly, be less employed, have hypertension, liver disease, diabetes mellitus, use cholesterol-lowering medication, and use aspirin. Participants with abnormal liver function were more likely to have decreased ELMISOS, INLELM, ISOSRPE, GCIPL, and RNFL. The detailed baseline characteristics of the participants are presented in Supplementary Data 1.

Associations of liver function with retinal layer thickness

Supplementary Data 2 shows the association between liver function and retinal layer thickness in analytic sample 1. After adjusting for demographic, lifestyle factors, BCVA, and IOP in Model 3, abnormal liver function was significantly associated with increased RPE thickness (β[SE]: 0.094 (0.034); P = 0.021) and decreased ISOSRPE thickness (β[SE]: −0.172 (0.048); P < 0.001). Similar results were observed in the analytic sample 2. As presented in Supplementary Data 3, abnormal liver function was significantly associated with increased PRE thickness (β[SE]: 0.102 (0.046), P = 0.065), and decreased ISOSRPE thickness (β[SE]: −0.167 (0.063); P = 0.042), and decreased RNFL thickness (β[SE]: −0.172 (0.068); P = 0.042) in Model 3. Retinal layer thickness was significantly associated with AST, GGT, and ALP levels. In Model 3, abnormal AST level was associated with decreased GCIPL thickness (β[SE]: −0.431 (0.136), P = 0.014); Abnormal GGT level was associated with decreased thickness of ELMISOS (β[SE]): −0.094 (0.029), P = 0.007), and GCIPL (β[SE]: −0.268 (0.106), P = 0.039); Abnormal ALP level was associated with decreased thickness of ISOSRPE (β[SE]: −0.226 (0.064), P < 0.001) (Supplementary Data 2, Fig. 2).

Mediation analysis of MS in the associations of liver function with retinal layer thickness

Among the 249 metabolites, 217 were significantly associated (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05) with liver function (Supplementary Data 4). A total of 23 metabolites were selected through the lasso regression to construct the MS of liver function (weights of the biomarkers included in the MS are shown in Supplementary Table S1). Among the 23 metabolites included in the score, eight (34.8%) ranked within the smallest 10% of P-values across all 249 metabolites, further indicating that the score is derived from a subset of metabolites with the strongest association signals (Fig. S2). In Model 3, abnormal liver function was associated with a higher MS (β[SE]: 0.372 (0.014); P < 0.001) (Supplementary Data 5). Among the 23 metabolites used to construct the MS, eight were significantly associated with ISOSRPE (all P < 0.05), and six were significantly associated with RPE (all P < 0.05). MS was significantly associated with ISOSRPE thickness, but the association of MS with RPE thickness was not significant (Fig. 3; Supplementary Data 6).

Sensitivity analysis

Supplementary Data 9 presents the associations between liver function and retinal layer thickness stratified by age and sex in Analysis 1. Interaction effect between age or sex and liver function on retinal layer thickness was not significant; Liver function was more significantly associated with retinal layer thickness in males and those younger than 60 years (all P < 0.05). When (1) excluding liver disease (Supplementary Data 10) and (2) further adjusted for aspirin use, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and liver disease (Supplementary Data 11), the associations of liver function with ISORPE and PPE thickness remained stable in analytic sample 1. Supplementary Table S2 presents the mediation proportion of MS and metabolites in the association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness, stratified by age and sex. The mediating role of MS was significant in males, and the mediating proportion of MS in the association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness was 25.5% (95% CI: 6.80%, 100.0%; P = 0.026). Supplementary Tables S3–S5 present the mediation proportion of MS and metabolites in the association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness, stratified by IOP ( > 18 mmHg vs. ≤ 18 mmHg), type 2 diabetes duration ( > 5 years vs. without type 2 diabetes), and BMI ( > 30 kg/m² vs. ≤ 30 kg/m²). The mediating role of MS was significant in participants with IOP ≤ 18 mmHg, without type 2 diabetes and BMI ≤ 30 kg/m². The mediating proportion of MS in the association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness was 27.5% (95% CI: 9.40%, 92.4%, P = 0.008), 25.5% (95% CI: 6.80%, 100.0%, P = 0.026) and 20.3% (95% CI: 7.00%, 46.1%, P < 0.001), respectively.

In analytic sample 2, abnormal liver function was associated with decreased RNFL thickness (β[SE]: −0.172(0.068); P = 0.042) (Supplementary Data 3). The mediation proportion of MS in the association between liver function and RNFL thickness was 22.8% (95% CI: 4.90%, 88.5%; P = 0.020) (Supplementary Table S6) after adjusting for all covariates in Model 3. There were six metabolites with significant mediating proportions in the association between liver function and RNFL thickness (Supplementary Data 12). The mediating role of MS was significant in females and participants younger than 60 years. The mediation proportion of MS in the association between liver function and RNFL thickness was 27.1% (95% CI: 5.20%, 100.0%; P = 0.028) in females and 18.8% (95% CI: 1.40%, 93.0%; P = 0.032) in participants aged < 60 years (Supplementary Table S7). In this study, metabolites with a selection probability ≥ 50% across 1000 bootstrap iterations were used to further validate the construction of the metabolite score from the 217 metabolites significantly associated with liver function. The results showed that 27 metabolites met the ≥ 50% threshold, including 22 of the 23 metabolites originally selected in our model, while the remaining metabolite had a selection probability of 45.0% (Figure S1c, d). This validated the stability of the metabolites selected in our initial model. The mediation proportion of the metabolite score (constructed from the 22 stable metabolites) in the association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness was 29.1% (95% CI: 9.30%, 59.4%; P = 0.004) (Supplementary Table S8). After adjusting for all covariates, compared with participants with normal liver function, those with abnormal liver function had a 27% higher risk of incident diabetic retinopathy (HR: 1.270, 95% CI: 1.180, 1.370; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables S9, S10, Figure S3). Compared with participants with low MS, those with high MS had a 126% higher risk of incident diabetic retinopathy (HR: 2.260, 95% CI: 1.800, 2.820; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S11, Figure S3).

Discussion

In this large population-based study utilizing UKB data, we found that abnormal liver function was significantly associated with increased RPE thickness and decreased ISOSRPE thickness. Moreover, MS of liver function mediated the association between liver function and ISOSRPE thickness. Our findings emphasize the importance of liver–eye connections and reveal the potential metabolic mechanisms involved.

The significant association between liver function and retinal layers thickness is consistent with previous research3,31–33. For example, a significant association between reduced retinal thickness and increased cirrhosis severity has been reported previously3. There are many possible explanations for the complex link between liver function and the eyes. OPN is a phosphoprotein associated with the eye and liver. Urtasun et al. found that OPN deficiency may be related to premature aging of the mouse retina, and OPN was also found to be a basal sediment component in the retina of patients with AMD, which is associated with AMD progression34. FGF-21, a hepato-ocular communication molecule, prevents retinal or choroidal neovascularization and local tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) expression by upregulating adiponectin in the blood circulation and retina in an AMD mouse model10. Baumann et al. found that the iron regulator Hepcidin secreted by the liver reduces the iron content in the blood, and abnormal liver function may be related to high blood iron levels, which in turn links to hypertrophy of the RPE and local degradation of photoreceptors. Conversely, retinal pigment epithelial-derived factor (PEDF) secreted by RPE can act on the liver, inhibit Wnt/β-catenin signaling in the liver and reduce steatohepatitis significantly. In this study, we found that abnormal liver function was positively correlated with RPE thickening, which may be related to the content of secreted “hepatocyte cytokines”, linking to damage to RPE’s operation and metabolic function, further thickening and hypertrophy of RPE, inability to phagocytose and digest the membranous disc shed by the outer segment of photoreceptors, apoptosis of photoreceptor cells, and finally leading to ISOS and RNFL layer thinning. Using large data from the UKB, we provided evidence of a significant association between liver function and retinal layer thickness.

As the largest solid metabolic organ, the liver regulates the secretion of metabolic markers that affect the biological activity of the ocular pathophysiology12. Our study found that metabolic biomarkers play an important mediating role in the association between liver function and the retinal layer thickness. Among the metabolites that played an important mediating role, the results suggested that abnormal liver function may be related to retinal thickness by affecting the levels of inflammation, essential amino acids, and fatty acids. Similarly, Han et al. studied the presumed causal relationship between 108 plasma metabolites and advanced AMD and found that these metabolites were rich in glycerophospholipid metabolism, haemolysin phospholipids, triglycerides, and long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid pathways. Most metabolites relevant to glycerophospholipid metabolism were inversely associated with AMD risk, suggesting that higher metabolite levels may protect against AMD. 29 serum metabolites were negatively correlated with ganglion cell inner plexiform layer thickness. These include high-density lipoprotein, apolipoprotein A1, choline, glucose, phospholipids in saturated fatty acids, total lipids, cholesterol, cholesterol esters, and the ratio of triglycerides to total lipids in small very low-density lipoproteins35. The serum metabolome plays a strong role in the characterization of metabolic disorders in AMD, indicating that plasma metabolites are correlated with the OCT structural features in patients with AMD. There is a significant correlation between plasma metabolites and three OCT features: high reflection lesions, atrophy, and ellipsoid zone destruction, among which most of the associations of metabolites are related to amino acids36. In addition, alterations in lipid metabolism, including reduced lipid synthesis in the retina and impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids, may serve as potential risk factors for worsening diabetic retinopathy. These studies suggest that abnormal liver function may be related to the eyes by altering the levels of metabolites in the blood. Visual analysis of OCT showed retinal thinning, and it is worth noting that ellipsoid destruction eventually may be related to irreversible loss of vision. Our analysis found that metabolites secreted by abnormal liver function were directly or indirectly associated with ISOSRPE thickness and negatively correlated with it. ISOSRPE is the outer layer of the retina, and advanced AMD or diabetic retinopathy may be related to the loss of structure and function in this layer. Abnormal liver function, related to metabolic biomarkers, directly or indirectly links to thinning of ISOSRPE, which may further be related to retinal diseases such as AMD or diabetic retinopathy, and ultimately related to visual impairment in patients. In this study, 87% of metabolites remained significantly associated with liver function after FDR correction, suggesting that abnormal liver function is linked to widespread, systemic metabolic dysregulation. Against this background, the 23‑metabolite score was constructed as a streamlined and representative summary measure of this dysregulated state employed for mediation modeling rather than a unique causal module. The subgroup analyses stratified by IOP, diabetes, and BMI indicated that the mediating effect of the metabolite score was not significant in high‑risk subgroups (e.g., elevated IOP, long‑standing diabetes, severe obesity). This suggests that in high-risk conditions, retinal changes may be predominantly driven by stronger local pathological factors (e.g., high IOP, chronic hyperglycemia, and severe obesity) or by broader systemic metabolic disturbances, thereby masking the independent contribution of liver dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation. Conversely, the significant mediation observed in low-risk populations indicates that liver metabolic dysregulation may contribute to retinal injury earlier in the disease course or during progression toward a high‑risk state, before the predominance of high-risk factors. These findings highlight the importance of stratifying and personalizing preventive strategies for retinal pathology based on an individual’s overall risk profile.

The association between liver function and the ocular health can be explained by multiple potential mechanisms. First, liver is the primary storage site for vitamin A, which is crucial for visual function. The liver synthesizes and secretes retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4), the principal carrier that transports stored vitamin A (retinol) to the retina37. In cases of severe liver dysfunction, particularly when synthetic function is impaired, RBP4 levels decrease, leading to a systemic reduction in vitamin A delivery to the RPE38,39. The RPE a central component of the visual cycle, and its enzymatic reactions rely on a continuous supply of 11-cis-retinal. Chronic substrate deficiency impairs regeneration of the visual pigment (rhodopsin), which is clinically manifested as prolonged dark adaptation and night blindness. Long-term functional impairment ultimately leads to photoreceptor degeneration and apoptosis40. The liver is the major production site for most complement factors, which are key components of the innate immune system41. Due to chronic liver disease, dysregulated complement activation may lead to excessive inflammation, contributing to retinal degeneration and other ocular pathologies41,42. Furthermore, liver dysfunction is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction in hepatocytes and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. In the retina, oxidative stress may accelerate photoreceptor degeneration43,44. These mechanisms may also overlap and synergize as liver function progressively declines, collectively contributing to RPE/OS damage.

These findings have several clinical implications. First, retinal status assessment may complement conventional liver function screening. The retina, particularly the OS layer structure measured by OCT, may serve as a sensitive “window” reflecting the long-term metabolic and nutritional status, as well as chronic inflammatory exposure in patients with liver disease. These changes may precede abnormalities in conventional liver function indicators. Deep learning (DL) algorithms based on artificial intelligence are rapidly advancing in medical image processing. Recent studies have utilized ResNet-101 deep neural networks to develop DL models trained on slit-lamp and fundus images, with slit-lamp model demonstrating good performance in detecting cirrhosis and cancer45. Second, for patients with liver disease who show signs of retinal pathology, treatment should integrate local ocular management with systemic treatment. To clarify temporal sequence and support causal inference, future research should prospectively examine the relationship between changes in liver function and the trajectories of OCT parameters. Population studies should measure serum RBP4, retinol levels and oxidative stress markers and evaluate their associations with OCT features. Furthermore, studies are needed to evaluate whether nutritional support or liver function-improving therapies can reverse or stabilize retinal structure deterioration.

This study provides valuable insights into the mechanisms linking liver function and the retina from a metabolomics perspective. Additionally, this study enhances the understanding of the classic theory in traditional Chinese medicine that “the liver governs the eyes”. Several potential limitations should be considered. First, owing to data limitations, our study was based on single‑time point measurements and is cross‑sectional in design. Thus, the findings do not support any causal or temporal inferences. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings. Second, assays measuring metabolic biomarkers and the OCT were performed within the same time period, which limited our ability to investigate the critical window effects of metabolic characteristics on retinal thickness. Third, UKB participants were mostly white and healthier than the general population. Therefore, our findings may not be applicable to other populations. Fourth, there may be residual confounding, even though we controlled for various covariates in the analysis. Future studies are needed to validate these findings in other populations.

Conclusion

In summary, our study found a significant association between liver function and retinal thickness, and metabolic markers played a significant mediating role in this association. This study provides crucial insights into the liver–eye axis and reveals the potential underlying metabolomic mechanisms.
