Beyond ejection fraction: Rethinking response and prognostication after cardiac resynchronization therapy
Authors: Anshul R Gupta, Christopher V Desimone, Abhishek Deshmukh
Published: 2026

Over the past 2 decades, cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) via biventricular pacing has revolutionized the treatment of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. It holds a Class I indication for patients with heart failure with New York Heart Association functional class II, III, or ambulatory IV, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 35% despite optimal pharmacotherapy, left bundle branch block, and QRS duration ≥ 150 ms, regardless of the etiology of cardiomyopathy. Despite significant efforts to optimize pacing strategies and identify variables predictive of response to refine patient selection, nearly one-third of those receiving CRT remain nonresponders.1

Demian et al present a prospective cohort study comparing LVEF with functional, frailty, and quality-of-life–based clinical scores obtained 6 months post-CRT implantation while predicting long-term outcomes post-CRT.2 Enrolling 528 patients with a mean follow-up of 3.73 years, the authors evaluated a primary composite end point of event-free survival, defined as freedom from all-cause death, heart transplantation, or left ventricular assist device implantation. The echocardiographic variable analyzed was LVEF, assessed at baseline and 6 months postimplantation. Clinical scores included the 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), gait speed, Timed Up and Go, Study of Osteoporotic Fractures frailty index (SOF), and the 5-level EuroQOL 5-dimension score. Notably, these clinical scores were evaluated only at the 6-month mark, precluding an assessment of longitudinal change from baseline. The incremental predictive value of these covariates was assessed by comparing the concordance indexes of nested Cox proportional hazards models with a standardized baseline clinical model. Consistent with established literature, 6-month LVEF was significantly associated with event-free survival (C-index 0.732). Interestingly, several multidimensional clinical scores demonstrated slightly numerically superior discrimination, led by the 6MWD (C-index 0.767) and SOF (C-index 0.754). When combined with LVEF, the clinical score/LVEF model provided incremental prognostic value. The prognostic value of some clinical scores (6MWD, Timed Up and Go, and SOF) persisted when patients were stratified by LVEF response.

Response to CRT has traditionally been classified according to echocardiographic and clinical criteria, although consensus remains poor.3 The main echocardiographic criterion for response is an increase in LVEF. Proposed clinical criteria include increases in 6MWD, New York Heart Association functional class, and quality of life scores. Known predictors of increased absolute long-term survival post-CRT implantation include baseline left bundle branch block, female sex, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, and a prolonged QRS duration, although there is some variability across studies. These are also shared as predictors of short-term echocardiographic response, including baseline echocardiographic parameters such as higher left ventricular end-diastolic volume and lower left atrial volume.1,4,5 In very long-term follow-up cohorts extending beyond a decade after implantation, survival trajectories between ischemic and nonischemic cardiomyopathies continue to diverge, highlighting the importance of myocardial substrate in shaping long-term outcomes.6

Demian et al provide an alternative perspective on the role of 6-month postimplantation clinical scores, framing them not merely as surrogates for therapeutic response but also as robust markers for long-term event-free survival. Although prior research has largely evaluated either the utility of baseline clinical scores as predictors of response post-CRT or longitudinal changes as markers of efficacy, the findings of Demian et al have some precedent. Vegh et al7 demonstrated that a higher 6MWD at 1 and 3 months post-CRT implantation was associated with higher event-free survival, with events defined as heart failure hospitalization, transplantation, left ventricular assist device implantation, or all-cause death at 3 years. Functional capacity metrics such as peak volume of oxygen and 6MWD are powerful independent predictors of mortality in heart failure.8,9 In addition, a lower baseline 6MWD is known to be an independent predictor of mortality post-CRT.10 As the authors acknowledge, a key limitation of the study is the absence of baseline clinical scores. Without change-over-time analyses, it is difficult to determine whether a high 6-month 6MWD reflects a successful response to CRT or simply identifies a cohort of “fitter” patients with heart failure who were destined for better outcomes from the start. This limits the ability to attribute post-CRT prognostic utility to these postimplantation clinical scores, especially considering the evidence cited above.

The concept explored in this study addresses an important limitation in the traditional framework used to evaluate CRT response. Historically, CRT outcomes have been categorized as “responder” or “nonresponder,” most often defined by improvement in LVEF or reduction in ventricular volumes. Although reverse remodeling is undoubtedly a central mechanism underlying the clinical benefits of CRT, it is increasingly recognized that LVEF alone provides an incomplete representation of patient trajectory after device therapy. In the broader clinical context, these findings by Demian et al suggest a paradigm shift in post-CRT monitoring. Moving beyond a solitary focus on echocardiographic function to incorporate multidimensional functional and frailty-based assessments may enable more precise risk stratification after device implantation. We have also suggested that latent biological risk may be detectable even before device implantation. For example, studies evaluating artificial intelligence (AI)–derived electrocardiographic markers have demonstrated that the discrepancy between AI-predicted ECG age and chronological age, often referred to as AI-ECG age, can serve as a marker of cardiovascular biological aging. In patients undergoing CRT implantation, preimplantation AI-ECG age has been shown to predict long-term survival, suggesting that electrical signatures of biological aging captured by the ECG may reflect underlying myocardial vulnerability, which is not fully captured by traditional clinical or echocardiographic metrics.11 Systematically evaluating these clinical metrics at baseline and longitudinally thereafter will provide more actionable data to refine patient selection and better differentiate inherent physiological reserve from the actual therapeutic response to resynchronization. Ultimately, the central question after CRT implantation may not simply be whether the ejection fraction improves but whether the patient’s overall clinical trajectory has meaningfully changed. LVEF tells us how the ventricle pumps; it does not fully tell us how the patient is living or will live. Integrating clinical scores, functional capacity, myocardial substrate, and emerging electrical biomarkers may therefore represent the next step toward a more comprehensive understanding of CRT outcomes.
