Could circulating tumor cells explain why breast conservation improves survival despite higher locoregional recurrence?
Authors: Muharrem Oner, Kefah Mokbel
Published: 2025

Introduction

A longstanding paradigm in breast cancer treatment is being fundamentally challenged by emerging evidence suggesting that breast-conserving surgery (BCS) followed by radiation therapy (RT) may offer superior overall survival (OS) compared with mastectomy. This counterintuitive finding—that preserving breast tissue can lead to better outcomes despite possible higher local recurrence rates—has prompted a novel hypothesis: the breast homing phenomenon (1). This commentary explores how the primary breast and regional lymph nodes may act as preferred “soil” for circulating tumor cells (CTCs) seeking a hospitable niche for colonization and growth.

Better OS with breast-conserving therapy (BCT) compared with mastectomy

The initial randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in the 1970s demonstrated comparable long-term survival between women treated with BCS followed by RT and those undergoing mastectomy (2,3). In the modern era, with advances in systemic therapy and radiation techniques, accumulating evidence from contemporary retrospective and prospective studies suggests that BCS with RT may confer superior OS compared with mastectomy in the management of early breast cancer. A recent meta-analysis involving over 1.8 million patients has demonstrated that BCS with RT is associated with a 36% reduction in overall mortality compared with mastectomy (4). In 1,802,128 patients (1,075,563 BCS; 744,565 mastectomy) with 4–20 years of follow-up, BCS was associated with superior OS versus mastectomy [risk ratio (RR): 0.64, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.55–0.74]. This effect was consistent across cohort and multi-institutional studies (RR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.49–0.67) and was more pronounced with <10 years of follow-up (RR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.46–0.64) (4). This apparent contradiction challenges longstanding assumptions about breast cancer treatment. This survival advantage extends across multiple subtypes. For example, in ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), patients treated with BCS plus radiation have shown superior survival, with an adjusted hazard ratio for death of 0.75 compared with mastectomy (5). Notably, among patients with invasive recurrence after DCIS, 88% of recurrences following mastectomy were regional or distant, whereas only 7% of recurrences were distant in the BCS group (6).

It should also be emphasized that findings regarding the incidence of locoregional recurrence (LRR) after BCS with RT are not entirely consistent, with some long term follow up studies reporting a higher incidence compared with mastectomy (7), while others demonstrate comparable rates between the two approaches (8,9).

A pooled analysis of six RCTs found that mastectomy significantly reduced the risk of LRR compared with BCT [odds ratio (OR): 1.561; 95% CI: 1.289–1.890; P<0.001] (7). Costeira et al. demonstrated no difference in LRR after mastectomy vs. BCS when adjusted for tumor stage (8), and Krug et al. reported that BCS was not associated with increased risk of LRR but was linked to superior disease-free survival (DFS) and OS in node-negative triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) (9).

The biological basis: early dissemination and dormancy

The breast homing hypothesis hinges on the understanding that breast cancer behaves systemically from its earliest stages. CTCs and disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) are detected in approximately 93% of patients with early breast cancer, including DCIS (10). These cells represent minimal residual disease that can persist undetected for years.

The journey of these cells is complex: after undergoing epithelial-mesenchymal transition, cancer cells detach from the primary tumor, some survive in the bloodstream as CTCs, and extravasate to become DTCs in distant organs (11). Dormancy occurs when there is an equilibrium between the number of newly formed cells and the number of dying cells among CTCs and DTCs (1). Remarkably, these CTCs and DTCs may remain dormant for over a decade; nearly half of patients with DTCs in the bone marrow remain recurrence-free at 10 years (12). However, their presence is predictive of OS (13).

The homing hypothesis: returning to familiar territory

The breast homing hypothesis proposes that reactivated CTCs and DTCs possess innate capabilities to return to their breast of origin, where a permissive tumor microenvironment (TME) is already established (1). Unlike colonization of distant organs, breast seeding requires little additional adaptation by CTCs to the local microenvironment in addition to engineering a pre-metastatic niche. This process is actively driven by the ability of CTCs to sense attraction signals from the primary breast of origin and to extravasate in response to such signals (1). This is largely mediated by the chemotactic CXCR4-CXCL12 axis (14). In addition to chemotaxis involving CXCR4/CXCL12, other factors play key roles in attracting reactivated CTCs to the pre-metastatic niche. Tumor cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) deliver bioactive molecules—such as miRNAs, circular RNAs (circRNAs), integrins, and metabolic enzymes—that modulate immune responses, remodel the extracellular matrix, and alter vascular permeability in distant organs, fostering organ-specific metastatic environments (15). Moreover, heterogeneous populations of tissue-specific macrophages interact dynamically with tumor cells and the local microenvironment, influencing both seeding and outgrowth through diverse polarization states and signaling pathways (16). Additional immune and non-immune cells—including bone marrow-derived cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and cancer stem cells—also contribute to the establishment of the pre-metastatic niche, supporting the attachment, survival, and proliferation of DTCs (17). Together, these multifaceted interactions near the site of origin and at distant sites underscore the complex landscape beyond the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis that governs metastatic progression in breast cancer.

The hypothesis postulated that if the permissive locoregional environment is surgically removed by mastectomy or substantially altered by RT, the activated CTCs may be stimulated to evolve and establish a new pre-metastatic niche in distant organs (1).

CXCR4 is highly expressed in breast cancer cells and stromal elements, while its ligand CXCL12 is abundant in organs prone to metastasis—and is overexpressed in histologically normal mammary tissue adjacent to tumors (19). Wu et al. reported that CXCL12 expression in breast cancer tissues did not differ significantly from adjacent normal tissues (P=0.156), creating a strong chemotactic gradient for CXCR4-positive CTCs (19). Papatheodorou et al. showed that CXCL12 membranous immunopositivity in normal breast epithelial cells is a risk factor for relapse, and its expression in fibroblasts is positively associated with tumor grade (20).

This molecular “guidance system” promotes adhesion, extravasation, and local growth. CTCs expressing epithelial markers show CXCR4 overexpression (1,19-21), priming them for homing back to the breast. The CXCL12-rich microenvironment may be more hospitable than distant organs, which require formation of a new niche—favoring breast seeding of aggressive CXCR4-positive CTCs (1).

Experimental evidence

Tumor self-homing has been demonstrated experimentally using magnetic particle imaging. Parkins et al. tracked iron-labeled CTCs in a mouse model, showing that these cells migrate preferentially to pre-existing breast tumor sites (22). Kim et al. also demonstrated that tumor masses can be readily seeded by highly aggressive CTCs derived from separate tumor masses, metastatic lesions, or direct inoculation mediated by IL-6 and IL-8 cytokines (23).

Clinical implications: revisiting the seed and soil theory

Evidence shows that 95% of ipsilateral breast tumor recurrences (IBTRs) occur in or near the index quadrant, even with clear margins (24). This supports the hypothesis that the local microenvironment—enriched with CXCL12—attracts reactivated CXCR4-positive CTCs and DTCs (1,18-23). This reframes IBTR not merely as residual local disease or a new primary tumor but as a form of metastasis. The significant mortality risk following isolated local recurrence—with 10-year survival dropping from 84% to 49%—reinforces this view (25).

Since the primary breast site may be more favorable “soil” than distant organs for CTCs with seeding potential, preserving the breast may increase local recurrence risk but lower distant recurrence and overall mortality risk. Long-term clinical data align with this concept. In addition to the study by De la Cruz Ku et al. (4), a more recent meta-analysis reported a pooled hazard ratio of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.68–0.75, P<0.001) in favor of BCS plus radiation (26). However, the OS benefit of BCS appears to extend to ipsilateral breast cancer recurrence, as repeat BCS (with or without repeat RT) has been associated with improved OS (RR =1.040; 95% CI: 1.003–1.079; P=0.032) (27). Gentile et al. reported that patients undergoing BCS for triple-negative or HER2-enriched IBTR had significantly better distant disease-free survival (DDFS), OS, and breast cancer-specific survival compared with mastectomy (P=0.009, P=0.002, and P=0.001, respectively) (28).

Regional lymph node homing

The homing phenomenon likely extends to regional lymph nodes. Emerging evidence suggests that the lymph node microenvironment also provides favorable “soil” for cancer cell seeding and proliferation. Resident immune and stromal cells express and secrete molecules that may support cancer cell survival in this setting (29). A study of primary breast tumors and corresponding lymph node metastases found that CXCL12 mRNA levels were significantly higher in lymph node metastases than in primary tumors, implicating CXCL12 in nodal metastasis (30). This may help explain why RCTs evaluating axillary surgery de-escalation have shown that complete axillary lymph node dissection does not improve OS compared with sentinel lymph node biopsy in node-positive patients (31-34). Notably, the KEYNOTE-355 and KEYNOTE-522 studies showed that patients who retained their regional lymph nodes derived greater survival benefits from immunotherapy compared to those who underwent lymph node removal. These findings suggest that preserving regional lymph nodes may be critical for the success of immunotherapy and highlight the need to reconsider the impact of surgical interventions on treatment outcomes (35). Subgroup analysis of the recently published NSABP B-51/RTOG 1304 trial (33) showed that TNBC patients achieving pathological complete response (pCR) experienced worse outcomes with regional nodal irradiation (RNI) (hazard ratio: 2.30; 95% CI: 1.00–5.25), supporting the hypothesis that RNI may disrupt immune surveillance in the axilla.

Therapeutic implications and future directions

Systemic therapies remain the cornerstone of improved outcomes, but unexplained residual differences motivate hypotheses like the breast homing hypothesis. The breast homing hypothesis has important implications for treatment de-escalation, improvement of survival and quality of life. More radical locoregional treatments targeting the breast and regional lymph nodes may reduce LRR but do not appear to improve OS, which is primarily driven by CTCs, DTCs and tumour biology. This paradigm shift suggests that BCT may reduce the risk of fatal distant metastasis and improve OS compared with mastectomy, while potentially increasing the likelihood of IBTR, consistent with recently published clinical data. It also supports de-escalation of axillary surgery in node-positive patients and highlights the emerging role of liquid biopsy tools (36) for monitoring disease progression, guiding therapy, and detecting recurrence before it becomes clinically evident—though liquid biopsy methods must reliably distinguish dormant from activated CTCs.

LRR is more easily detected and treated than systemic disease. Less radical locoregional treatment is also associated with better quality of life (1,31-34).

Targeting the CXCR4/CXCL12 pathway

Extensive expression of CXCR4 in cancer, including on activated CTCs, and the constitutive presence of CXCL12 in various organs activate this axis, which promotes angiogenesis, tumor progression, LRR, distant metastasis, tumor stemness, and drug resistance (1,20-23,37). Current therapeutic strategies targeting the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis aim to disrupt this signaling pathway and include small-molecule CXCR4 antagonists (e.g., plerixafor), monoclonal antibodies, and peptide inhibitors. These block the binding of CXCL12 to CXCR4, thereby inhibiting cancer cell migration and metastatic seeding. Preclinical and early-phase clinical studies suggest that combining CXCR4 inhibitors with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy may enhance treatment responses by sensitizing tumors and reducing residual disease. Despite promising results, further clinical trials are needed to confirm their safety, efficacy, and optimal integration into breast cancer treatment (38).

The role of adjuvant RT

Since most patients undergoing BCS also receive RT, the OS benefit seen with BCT is likely mediated largely by radiation. Radiation reduces IBTR by eradicating residual tumor cells, targeting CTCs circulating through the breast, and modifying the TME (1). Additionally, radiation may enhance anti-tumor immunity by releasing tumor antigens and eliciting local and systemic immune responses (39). Gomà and colleagues have shown that preoperative RT induces both early and late biological changes in breast cancer, with initial effects on proliferation reduction and DNA damage within days, followed by adaptive immune activation within weeks (39).

The immune response is particularly relevant in HER2-positive and TNBCs, where lumpectomy plus radiation is associated with improved 5-year DFS and OS compared with mastectomy (40,41).

Interestingly, Goodman et al., using a cohort of 1,697 patients with early-stage breast cancer from the National Cancer Database, found that treatment with radiation was associated with longer LRR-free survival, DFS, and OS in patients with detectable CTCs, but not in those without detectable CTCs (42). Supporting this finding, the SUCCESS trial showed that the risk of local recurrence was higher in CTC-positive women who did not receive radiotherapy. While CTC status was associated with a benefit from radiotherapy in patients who underwent BCS, it was not associated with a benefit in those who had a mastectomy (43). Taken together, these observations support the concept that CTCs contribute significantly to LRR through the homing phenomenon.

Surgical magnitude, anesthesia, and potential systemic effects on survival

An important distinction between BCS and total mastectomy lies in the magnitude of surgical intervention and the duration of anesthesia, particularly when mastectomy is combined with immediate reconstruction using autologous tissue, such as a free deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap. Theoretically, the more extensive tissue dissection and prolonged operative time associated with mastectomy could provoke a heightened systemic response, including increased release of stress-related hormones, growth factors, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, alongside anesthesia-related immunosuppression (44). Such physiological perturbations may, in turn, influence tumor biology and potentially contribute to differences in long-term outcomes, raising the possibility that patients undergoing total mastectomy could experience worse OS compared with those treated with lumpectomy.

Among patients undergoing mastectomy, some earlier studies suggested that reconstructive breast surgery might act as an independent stimulating event for the growth of micrometastases, potentially leading to accelerated relapse rates (45). In particular, more extensive procedures such as free DIEP flap reconstruction and bilateral surgeries were reported to be associated with a higher early peak of recurrence compared with unilateral implant-based reconstruction (45). However, more recent studies do not support this hypothesis, indicating that patients undergoing DIEP flap reconstruction do not experience higher rates of breast cancer recurrence compared with those treated with mastectomy alone (46).

Conclusion: a paradigm shift

The breast homing hypothesis reframes the breast not as a liability to remove but as an integral part of the metastatic landscape—a preferred “soil” for returning tumor cells. Mounting evidence supports prioritizing BCS with radiation over mastectomy when oncologically feasible, with a shift toward less extensive locoregional surgery combined with therapies that target tumor cell dissemination and homing. Future research should further elucidate pathways beyond CXCR4/CXCL12 and develop novel strategies targeting dormant and reactivated CTCs and DTCs, with the ultimate goal of improving long-term survival and quality of life for breast cancer patients worldwide. However, the “homing hypothesis” remains speculative, requires validation, and should be interpreted as a complementary framework rather than a definitive explanation.
