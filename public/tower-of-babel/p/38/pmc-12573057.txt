Debate: Social media in children and young people – time for a ban? Beyond bans: addressing the digital determinants of youth mental health and well‐being in the European region
Authors: Cassie Redlich, Ledia Lazeri, Jason Maurer, Jennifer Hall, Natasha Azzopardi‐Muscat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complex role that social media plays in shaping young people's mental health and well‐being requires a nuanced approach to regulation beyond simple bans. Social media presents demonstrated harms for young people, yet can also be a lifeline and provide a valuable resource for social support, learning, and expression. In this article, we draw on policy actions outlined in the WHO Europe policy brief Addressing the digital determinants of youth mental health and well‐being to emphasize the importance of evidence‐informed, multi‐faceted strategies aligned with the precautionary principle to mitigate risks while maximizing benefits. These strategies include regulating harmful platform features, enhancing digital literacy, empowering parents, increasing industry accountability, and promoting healthy offline activities.Keywords: Adolescence, mental health, social media, policy, digital literacy

Digital environments have become a mainstay of the lives of young people in Europe (Boniel‐Nissim et al., 2024), with social media playing a significant role in their social, emotional, and cognitive development. With observed declines in young people's mental health in many countries over the past decade (Cosma et al., 2023), there is growing debate about the potential harms these platforms pose to mental health, and how to address them.

Concerns about the harms of social media are valid. However, a simplistic binary response – either unrestricted access or outright bans – fails to account for the complex interplay of benefits and risks these platforms have for young people, the heterogeneity of young people's experiences and preferences, and the range of potential policy actions available.

In our recent policy brief, Addressing the digital determinants of youth mental health and well‐being (WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2025a), we advocate for a more nuanced, evidence‐informed approach. A comprehensive policy response that empowers young people, supports parents, and holds social media platforms accountable is necessary to protect mental health in our digitalized world. This response should be guided by the precautionary principle, informed by emerging evidence and established public health interventions, and focused on mitigating specific harms while preserving opportunities for positive social connection, creativity, and learning online (WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2025a).

The complexity of social media's role and effects

Social media use and how it affects youth mental health is complex, intertwining with a plethora of individual and contextual factors on and offline. For that reason, an outright ban or other generalized policy response is not warranted. The impacts differ markedly according to how young people use social media platforms (e.g., platforms used, content, usage time, and duration) and their own characteristics, motivations, and contexts (WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2025a).

For example, passive consumption, interaction with appearance‐related content, and cyberbullying exposure are all associated with adverse mental health outcomes such as depression, suicidality, and low well‐being (Choukas‐Bradley, Kilic, Stout, & Roberts, 2023). Conversely, some young people obtain social support through social media, particularly marginalized youth or those lacking access to real‐world communities (O'Reilly et al., 2023; Pottie, Ratnayake, Ahmed, Veronis, & Alghazali, 2020). LGBTQ+ adolescents, for instance, report using platforms to connect with peers, explore their identities, and access resources related to sexual health and mental well‐being (Coyne et al., 2023). Banning access might exacerbate feelings of isolation and marginalization, undermining the goal of promoting mental well‐being.

Social media platforms can also be powerful tools for learning, expression, and creativity, allowing young people to access information, participate in social movements, and advocate for social change (Choukas‐Bradley et al., 2023; Stoilova, Livingstone, & Khazbak, 2021). Restricting access to these platforms could limit their opportunities to develop critical thinking skills, express themselves, and contribute to their communities.

Beyond type of engagement, factors such as age, gender, pre‐existing mental health conditions, coping mechanisms, social support, and digital literacy can influence how young people respond to online experiences (Stoilova et al., 2021). For example, younger children may be more prone to harmful effects because of lower digital literacy or ability to recognize potentially harmful situations, while having a mental health condition may drive young people to engage with more harmful content (e.g., self‐harming content). Conversely, children with strong coping mechanisms, supportive offline relationships, and high digital literacy may be better able to manage online risks (Vissenberg, d'Haenens, & Livingstone, 2022). Restricting access may deprive these young people of key benefits (e.g., social connection) without providing any tangible benefit.

Other unintended consequences of bans

Banning social media for young people carries inherent risks and implementation challenges. As noted earlier, blanket bans deny the potential benefits of social media for young people and may harm well‐being (O'Reilly et al., 2023). Second, bans are difficult to enforce and can promote surreptitious access – young people may be less likely to discuss their social media use with adults, making it harder to monitor harmful uses. Enforcement may fall disproportionately on parents, introducing inequitable exposure to harms based on parental literacy or motivation. Third, without regulation of platform features considered to drive harmful engagement (e.g., infinite scrolling, push notifications) and improved restriction of harmful content, platforms might remain unsafe spaces for young people when they do reach the mandated age.

Complexity does not mean complacency: employing a public health approach and the precautionary principle

The precautionary principle provides a valuable framework for addressing the complexities of social media regulation and child protection. This principle suggests that when an activity has potential for harm, precautionary measures should be taken even in the absence of robust cause‐and‐effect relationships. This means shifting the burden of proof from demonstrating that social media and other digital technologies are harmful to proving they are safe, as is done for any other commercial product.

A comprehensive policy response requires a multi‐pronged approach that balances the need to mitigate harms with the importance of preserving benefits. Such an approach should encompass the following key elements, as outlined in our policy brief (WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2025a):

Regulation of harmful features

Policymakers should regulate specific features and practices that pose the greatest risks to mental health, including the following:

Limiting use of addictive design elements (e.g., infinite scrolling, autoplay, push notifications) that promote compulsive usage and displace other healthy activities. Measures might involve setting default time limits, providing users with greater control over notification settings, and requiring platforms to disclose the algorithms they use to personalize content and advertising.

Promoting digital and media literacy

Young people should be equipped with the necessary critical thinking skills to navigate the online world safely and responsibly. This includes providing digital literacy education in schools and communities and teaching children to evaluate information critically, identify misinformation, and protect their privacy online. It would also involve teaching how algorithms work and ways of limiting exposure to advertising generated by algorithms.

Empowering parents

Parents and caregivers can be provided with resources and knowledge to support healthy social media use in children and themselves. This includes developing and disseminating clear, actionable evidence‐based guidance on setting boundaries, monitoring activity, fostering open communication, and recognizing warning signs of problem use.

Fostering industry accountability

Social media platforms must be accountable for protecting users, particularly children, from harm. This may involve enacting legislation requiring platforms to implement stronger content moderation, protect user privacy, and be transparent about their algorithms and advertising practices. It may also involve establishing independent oversight bodies to monitor and enforce these requirements.

Promoting alternative activities

Finally, we must support alternative activities that promote young people's mental and physical well‐being, such as sports, arts, and community engagement. This may involve funding after‐school programs, community centers, and recreational facilities.

The EU and countries across the WHO European region are already moving to enact the above strategies through policy and legislation (WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2025b). The EU's Digital Services Act, Ireland's Online Safety Code, Germany's Youth Protection Act, and the UK's Online Safety Act target platform accountability through requirements for age‐verification systems, parental control options, and rapid responses to flagged content. Armenia, Austria, Poland, and Albania have also implemented initiatives to improve digital and media literacy through classroom and community‐based programmes. National guidelines for promoting online safety have been implemented in countries including Sweden, Croatia, and Denmark. Monitoring and evaluation of these actions will be critical in understanding how policy can be effectively implemented and for what impact.

Conclusion

The debate surrounding social media and children's mental health demands a move beyond binaries. While the potential harms of these platforms are undeniable and rapidly evolving, universal bans risk undermining potential benefits while being difficult to enforce. By embracing the precautionary principle and implementing a comprehensive, calibrated policy approach – combining targeted regulation, digital literacy initiatives, parental and youth empowerment, and industry accountability – policymakers can navigate this complex landscape to promote the mental well‐being of future generations.
