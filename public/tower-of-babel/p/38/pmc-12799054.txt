Music festivals, exclusive concerts and reward programmes: nicotine pouch promotion on social media
Authors: Page Dobbs, Charlotte McCormick, Deya Murthy, Paula Hayek, Grace Kong
Published: 2025

Nicotine and tobacco products, including e-cigarettes and nicotine pouches, have recently used music festivals to promote their products and brands globally.1–3 Historically, tobacco companies used brand sponsored events, such as music festivals, musical artists and other social or cultural events to attract vulnerable populations, including youth, young adults and minoritised populations (eg, Black or African Americans).4 5 This type of marketing was restricted in the US under the 2009 Tobacco Control Act for traditional tobacco products (eg, cigarettes, snuff, chewing tobacco);6 however, not all marketing restrictions under this act were extended to newer tobacco products (eg, e-cigarettes, nicotine pouches) under the 2016 Deeming Rule, in which the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) deemed all nicotine-containing products to be a tobacco product.7

In the USA, Zyn, the leading brand of nicotine pouches (owned by Philip Morris International (PMI)) has recently gained popularity among youth and young adults.8 9 An Instagram post made by Country Hub (an aggregator of country music-related news and content with over 1.2 million followers) advertised that on 19 April 2025, Zyn sponsored an exclusive concert for their reward members in Denver, Colorado with Noah Kahan, a prominent American singer-songwriter nominated for Best New Artist at the 66th Annual Grammys Awards (figure 1).10 This post received over 119 000 likes, generated over 600 comments and was shared over 152 000 times. In exchange for 3000 points, approximately 1000 Zyn rewards members (based on posted TikTok videos)11 received two tickets to the concert (one for the member(should be 21 years of age based on continuity programme eligibility requirements) and another for a guest who was 21 years of age or older) and two alcoholic drink tickets (one per person). Zyn’s continuity programme (eg, reward or loyalty programme that launched on 12 October 2020)12 gives users 15 points per can of Zyn, allowing them to redeem these points for prizes such as gift cards, grills2 3 13 and now—an exclusive concert that sold out quickly.14

While continuity programmes are not new for tobacco companies (eg, Marlboro Miles and Camel Cash), they were originally restricted under the Tobacco Control Act (2009).6 However, in 2012, the US Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit found there to be insufficient scientific evidence to support the claim that continuity programmes would substantially affect tobacco use of those under the minimum legal sales age, and therefore, this provision violated the tobacco companies’ First Amendment rights (Discount Tobacco City & Lottery, Inc. v United States of America).15 On appeal, only one 1992 Gallup poll and one cross-sectional survey from 1996 were cited as evidence that youth who engaged with tobacco promotional campaigns were more susceptible to tobacco use.15 16 Today, this area of research remains highly understudied.13 Thus, continuity programmes remain legal in the USA for all nicotine and tobacco products and recently have been heavily marketed for nicotine pouches on social media.13 If research were to find that continuity programmes increased susceptibility and use among those under the minimum legal sales age, the FDA could revisit this issue by seeking an injunction through their regulatory authority over tobacco marketing.

Alternatively, one provision of the Tobacco Control Act, upheld as constitutional by the Sixth Circuit in Discount Tobacco City & Lottery, prohibited sponsored events,15 including ‘y-restricted’ events due to the broad exposure of youth to branded event sponsorship. Therefore, this concert would have violated the Tobacco Control Act if it had been used to promote many of PMI’s other products that are made from tobacco leaf. Because nicotine pouches came under the FDA’s authority after this law was enacted, this marketing restriction of the Tobacco Control Act does not apply to nicotine pouches. However, Zyn’s ‘youth-restricted’ sponsored event was heavily advertised on social media platforms with a large youth following (eg, Instagram, TikTok).17 Therefore, research should explore if nicotine product continuity programmes increase susceptibility and use among those under the minimum legal sales age or if provisions of the Tobacco Control Act, including event sponsorship, should be expanded to newer nicotine products, including e-cigarettes and nicotine pouches.

Zyn has emerged in other musical promotions including sponsorship of the Rolling Stone UK’s Future of Music and advertising price promotions at music festivals. For instance, TikTok posts show people throwing Zyn cans to musicians during concerts and musicians tossing cans back into the crowd (which includes those under 21 years of age).18 Further, individual posts advertised with #zyn and #musicfestival also highlight promotions such as three cans of Zyn being sold for $1 each at music festivals in the US (figure 2).19

Therefore, nicotine pouch companies may be exploiting regulatory loopholes in laws by sponsoring events, as seen by Zyn’s recent Noah Kahan exclusive concert. Across different tobacco products and geographic locations, the tobacco industry seems to use music festivals to promote tobacco use. Such promotional activities directly target youth and young adults, including those under the minimum legal sales age who attend these events. These promotional activities are further disseminated on social media, which is another source frequented by young audiences. The FDA and tobacco regulatory agencies should be aware of marketing strategies employed by nicotine pouch companies that exploit regulatory loopholes and circumvent regulations.
