Identification of hub genes involved in early-onset schizophrenia: from genetic susceptibility to predicted regulated gene expression
Authors: Yawen Jen, Sung-Liang Yu, Po-Chang Hsiao, Po-Hsiu Kuo, Chih-Min Liu, Chen-Chung Liu, Tzung-Jeng Hwang, Ming H. Hsieh, Yi-Ling Chien, Yi-Ting Lin, Hailiang Huang, Yen-Chen Anne Feng, Chuhsing K. Hsiao, Yen-Feng Lin, Stephen V. Faraone, Benjamin Neale, Stephen J. Glatt, Ming T. Tsuang, Hai-Gwo Hwu, Wei J. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite high heritability, much of the genetic architecture of schizophrenia (SZ) remains unexplained, and early-onset SZ may represent a more genetically homogeneous subtype. We examined genetically regulated expression risk scores (GeRS) to identify gene networks associated with early-onset SZ and characterize their biological functions. Using genotype data from patients in multiplex families (223 early-onset and 372 late-onset) and simplex families (matched for sex and onset age), SZ-GeRS were estimated based on single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)–expression prediction models derived from two transcriptomic reference panels (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex tissue from individuals with psychiatric disorders and brain cortex tissue from the general population). Panel-specific GeRS were constructed using transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS)–derived effect sizes and integrated using a fixed-effects meta-analytic framework. Module-based SZ-GeRS were constructed by aggregating genes within empirically derived co-expression clusters, followed by network analysis to identify hub genes and functional mapping to infer biological context. Among 13 co-expression modules, meta-analysis identified a significant association between early-onset SZ and Module 10 GeRS (M10sub-GeRS: adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.22, 95%CI = 1.07–1.40). Hub genes prioritized by complementary network approaches also showed significant associations. Functional enrichment analyses indicated predominant involvement of excitatory neuronal processes and immune-related pathways. Sensitivity analyses using a variance-calibrated TWAS approach to account for polygenicity-driven inflation attenuated gene-level signals but did not materially alter module-level associations, with M10sub-GeRS remaining robustly associated with early-onset SZ. These findings highlight gene regulatory networks linking genetic susceptibility to molecular mechanisms in early-onset SZ and may inform risk stratification and targeted interventions.

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a leading cause of disability worldwide [1], with a median lifetime prevalence of 6.35 per 1000 people [2]. Despite an estimated heritability of 80% [3, 4], only limited variance in SZ was attributed to gene loci or the polygenic risk score (PRS) in genome-wide association studies (GWASs) [5–7]. SZ is a heterogeneous disorder characterized by psychotic symptoms (delusions and hallucinations) and cognitive impairments [8]. Because much of its heritability remains unexplained, variation in gene expression driven by gene-environment interactions is thought to further contribute to SZ. However, collecting postmortem brain tissue for gene expression analysis remains challenging [9, 10].

An alternative approach is transcriptome imputation, which integrates tissue-specific single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-expression prediction models with GWAS genotype data to estimate genetically regulated gene expression [11, 12]. Several transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) have applied this approach to SZ [12–15], yet similar to the findings in GWAS, only a few TWAS-identified genes remain significantly associated with SZ after Bonferroni correction, likely due to limited sample sizes [12–15].

To address this limitation, recent studies have developed predicted gene-expression risk scores (GeRS), analogous to PRS but based on TWAS-derived effect sizes. Compared with conventional PRS, which aggregate individual SNP effects regardless of their biological context, GeRS offer distinct advantages. First, they integrate expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data to infer genetically regulated expression in disease-relevant tissues (e.g., brain), linking genetic variation more directly to molecular function. Second, by operating at the gene expression level, GeRS reduce dimensionality and enhance interpretability. Empirically, GeRS often show stronger associations with disease-related traits than PRS. For example, GeRS have explained additional variance in psychiatric and substance use traits beyond PRS by incorporating co-expression network information [16], and brain tissue-derived GeRS correlate more strongly with cognition and symptom dimensions [12, 17]. These findings suggest that GeRS can bridge genetic susceptibility and functional regulation to improve biologic insight and prediction.

Parallel efforts have applied co-expression network analyses to derive genetically informed indices of SZ risk. Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), Pergola and colleagues showed that integrating polygenic risk with network-level gene expression improves biological interpretability, linking specific co-expression modules to imaging phenotypes and treatment response in SZ [18–20]. Building on this framework, the present study extends these concepts by constructing module-based GeRS to test whether genetically regulated expression converges within functionally coherent networks associated with early-onset SZ.

Although prior studies have linked GeRS to SZ-related traits such as clinical symptoms and cognition [12, 13, 21, 22], few genes with clear biological mechanisms have been identified, limiting clinical translation. Moreover, SZ heterogeneity complicates detection of genetic contributors. Dissecting SZ into more homogeneous subtypes may thus clarify its genetic architecture [13, 23].

Early-onset SZ, diagnosed before age 18 and accounting for about 8% of cases [24], represents a more homogeneous and severe subtype with greater genetic burden [25–28] and higher treatment resistance. GWAS of early-onset SZ have identified suggestive loci [29, 30], underscoring its potential as a phenotype for discovering early intervention targets, yet its genetic basis remains largely unknown [31].

Here, we examined GeRS in patients from multiplex and simplex families to identify hub genes implicated in early-onset SZ and explored their biological functions through enrichment analyses.

Materials and methods

Study workflow

The study workflow is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. To enhance the robustness and generalizability of GeRS estimates, we integrated genetically regulated expression information across multiple transcriptomic reference panels. Specifically, we first predicted genetically regulated gene expression levels in brain tissue using PrediXcan (https://github.com/hakyimlab/PrediXcan), based on GWAS genotype data from patients with SZ in multiplex families. SNP-expression prediction models were derived from two independent transcriptomic reference panels (https://predictdb.org/): the CommonMind Consortium (CMC; including 254 SZ, 52 bipolar disorder, and 279 controls) [12] and the Genotype-Tissue Expression Consortium v8 (GTEx; including 205 general donors).

To extract disease-related expression changes, we performed transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) using S-PrediXcan [32] with each panel-specific SNP-expression model and the latest summary statistics from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC; 76,755 SZ, 243,648 controls) [7]. For each gene, TWAS effect sizes were estimated separately within each panel.

Panel-specific GeRS were calculated by weighting predicted expression using the corresponding TWAS effect size and were subsequently standardized to z-scores to ensure scale comparability across panels. A composite GeRS was then derived by integrating standardized panel-specific GeRS using a fixed-effect meta-analytic framework, with weights proportional to sample size of each reference panel to account for the relative contribution of the CMC and GTEx training datasets.

Finally, module-based GeRS were constructed by summing the composite GeRS of genes belonging to published weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) modules (150 SZ, 94 bipolar disorder, 87 major depression, 50 autism, 17 alcoholism, and 293 controls) [33]. We evaluated the association between module-based GeRS and early-onset SZ in the multiplex sample and identified hub genes within significant modules using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA®, QIAGEN), STRING (https://string-db.org/), and Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1).

The same analyses were repeated in the simplex sample.

Participants

Two samples were analyzed: a multiplex and a simplex sample. The multiplex sample came from the Taiwan Schizophrenia Linkage Study (TSLS), which recruited Han Chinese families with ≥2 siblings affected by SZ and their first-degree relatives (1998–2002) [34, 35] (see Supplementary Methods). After quality control (QC), 595 patients and 570 unaffected relatives from 314 families remained, all with age at onset determined using the information for the first psychotic episode obtained via the Diagnostic Interview for Genetic Studies (DIGS) [36].

To validate findings in a lower-genetic-loading cohort, the simplex sample was drawn from the Schizophrenia Trio Genomic Research in Taiwan (S-TOGET), which included 2923 patients from simplex families [37, 38]. Among them, 1635 patients had both genotype and onset-age data.

Sex- and onset-age-matched subsamples were selected to minimize demographic differences between cohorts (Supplementary Table 1). The final simplex dataset included 595 patients and 1190 unaffected relatives. Both studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent (see Supplementary Methods).

Measurements

The age at onset and sociodemographic data were obtained from the DIGS. Early-onset SZ was defined as psychotic symptoms before age 18 [38]. Cognitive performance was assessed using the Continuous Performance Test (CPT) for sustained attention [39] and the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) for executive function [40]. The CPT sensitivity index (d′), WCST perseverative errors, and categories achieved were analyzed as cognitive indices (see Supplementary Methods).

Genotyping, quality control, and imputation

Genotyping was performed with the PsychChip array v1.1 (Illumina, San Diego, CA), containing 588,628 SNPs. Standard QC procedures were applied [41] (see Supplementary Methods).

For the multiplex sample, 1224 participants and 266,899 SNPs passed QC and were used for imputation; principal components (PCs) were computed for ancestry adjustment. The genomic inflation factor (λ = 1.07) and QQ plot are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Post-imputation QC excluded variants with INFO < 0.8 or minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.1, leaving 4,853,692 SNPs. For the simplex sample, the same QC pipeline yielded 293,643 SNPs and 5,154,665 imputed variants [37, 38].

Calculation of PRS

PRSs for schizophrenia (SZ-PRS) were generated using PRS-CS [42] with the latest PGC SZ summary statistics [7]. PRS-CS applies a Bayesian continuous-shrinkage prior accounting for linkage disequilibrium to estimate posterior SNP effect sizes and generate a genome-wide polygenic score without arbitrary p-value thresholds. All the SZ-PRS values were standardized to z scores (see Supplementary Methods).

Deriving effect sizes of predicted gene expression

Tissue-specific TWAS for SZ were conducted to obtain effect sizes of genetically predicted expression using S-PrediXcan [32]. To ensure that the effect-sizes estimation was based on the same SNP-expression model used for gene-expression prediction, TWAS analyses were performed separately for each transcriptomic reference panel. Specifically, SZ GWAS summary statistics from the PGC were integrated with SNP-expression prediction models derived from the CMC dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) dataset [12] and the GTEx Brain Cortex dataset. These panel-specific TWAS results were subsequently used for construction of panel-specific GeRS. Using updated PGC summary statistics improved statistical power and ancestry diversity, particularly among Asian participants. TWAS associations were considered significant at P < 5 × 10−8 (see Supplementary Methods).

Predicting gene expression

Genetically regulated expression for each gene was estimated using PrediXcan [11] based on individual genotypes that passed QC. Panel-specific gene expression prediction was performed separately using SNP-expression models derived from two resources: the CMC DLPFC model and the GTEx Brain Cortex model.

To evaluate whether CMC- and GTEx-based models captured partially shared genetically regulated expression components and imputed comparable expression signals, we conducted a cross-panel consistency analysis. For genes with predicted expression available in both panels, direction concordance was assessed using a binomial test, and cross-panel similarity was evaluated using Spearman correlation at the gene level.

Calculation of SZ-GeRS

For each gene, panel-specific SZ-GeRS was calculated by weighting its predicted expression by the corresponding TWAS effect size. Panel-specific SZ-GeRS were subsequently standardized to z-scores and integrated into a composite SZ-GeRS using a fixed-effect meta-analytic framework, with weights proportional to the sample size of each transcriptomic reference panel. All genes with available predicted expression were included without additional filtering. Hereafter, SZ-GeRS refers to the meta-analytic composite SZ-GeRS.

To identify biologically meaningful patterns, we constructed two aggregated SZ-GeRS measures:Module-based SZ-GeRS was defined as the sum of gene-level SZ-GeRS within 13 psychiatric disorder-related co-expression modules identified by WGCNA [33]. These modules were derived from postmortem brain tissue and represent transcriptionally coherent gene networks. The summed module-based SZ-GeRS aggregates genetically regulated expression across each network, capturing the cumulative impact of genetic risk mediated through coordinated gene expression. Because genes within a module are, by definition, correlated, the summed module-based GeRS does not assume statistical independence but instead provides an integrative and heuristic summary of genetically regulated risk within each network. This approach parallels network-based polygenic indices previously described by Pergola and colleagues [18–20], although our method extends this framework to imputed rather than directly observed gene expression.Hub gene-based SZ-GeRS was constructed by summing SZ-GeRS across hub genes, identified using two complementary network-based approaches. These included: (i) IPA-derived hubs, defined as genes directly connected to ≥4 genes within the top-ranking network [43–45]; and (ii) STRING protein-protein interactions (PPI) networks hubs prioritized based on the top 10 maximal clique centrality scores identified using CytoHubba [46, 47]. To corroborate hub status, we extracted module eigengene-based connectivity (kME) values from the WGCNA dataset for these genes. All SZ-GeRS were standardized to z scores.

To test specificity, we conducted null-model simulations using random gene sets matched in size to the identified module. Separately, to assess the reproducibility, we recalculated the module-based GeRS using updated WGCNA modules [48] (see Supplementary Methods). These modules, derived from the PsychENCODE dataset, provide robust, biologically meaningful co-expression patterns in the frontal cortex, spanning neuronal, glial, synaptic, and immune-related processes, and thus serve as functionally coherent gene sets to validate the biological relevance of our findings.

Sensitivity analysis

Because standard TWAS and related approaches may be susceptible to polygenicity-driven inflation in highly polygenic traits [49], we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a variance-calibrated TWAS approach. Using calibrated TWAS weights, we recomputed GeRS and repeated downstream association analyses, comparing calibrated and uncalibrated results at the gene, individual-score, and module levels. Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Enrichment analyses

Functional enrichment of hub genes was assessed with GENE2FUNC in the FUMA platform (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/) using gene-expression data across developmental stages. The Molecular Signatures Database (MsigDB) [50] was used to test enrichment in chemical and genetic perturbation sets. We further performed cell type-enrichment analysis using PsychSCREEN (https://psychscreen.beta.wenglab.org/psychscreen/gene), leveraging single-cell expression data from the PsychENCODE schizophrenia/bipolar multi-omics dataset. In parallel, complementary enrichment analyses were also conducted for the TWAS-significant genes, including tissue-level, cell-type, and literature-based enrichment, to evaluate broader biological convergence (see Supplementary Methods).

Statistical analyses

Group differences in SZ-PRS and SZ-GeRS among patients and relatives were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Associations between risk scores and early-onset SZ were tested via mixed-effects logistic regression (multiplex sample) with family index as a random effect and covariates including sex, education, and the first four ancestry PCs. For the simplex sample, logistic regression with identical covariates was used.

Explained variance was estimated as the change in Nagelkerke’s pseudo-R2 between null model (covariates only) and full model (covariates + risk scores) [51]. Meta-analyses combined both samples using the R package metafor [52] (restricted maximum likelihood, inverse-variance weighting. Pearson correlations with 95% CIs assessed collinearity between SZ-PRS and SZ-GeRS.

All analyses used PLINK [53] for PRS and PCs, and R packages lme4 and rcompanion (v4.2.2) for regression and pseudo-R2 calculations.

Association of PRS for SZ with early-onset SZ

To evaluate genetic predisposition for earlier onset, we tested SZ-PRS in models adjusted for sex, education level, and the first four ancestry PCs. In the multiplex sample, mixed-effects logistic regression (family index random effect) showed SZ-PRS associated with early-onset SZ (p = 0.04; variance explained = 1.02%). In the simplex sample, logistic regression with identical covariates also showed association (p = 0.03; variance explained = 1.04%). The significance threshold was p < 0.05.

Relative to unaffected relatives, patients with early-onset SZ had the highest SZ-PRS, followed by late-onset patients and then relatives in both multiplex (Fig. 1A) and simplex (Fig. 1B) samples. Predicted GeRS for SZ

Before constructing SZ-GeRS, we obtained panel-specific TWAS-based weights linking cis-genetic components of expression to SZ. Of the 10,358 genes included in the CMC DLPFC prediction model [12], S-PrediXcan generated TWAS results for 10,306 genes using PGC SZ GWAS summary statistics. Gene-level prediction performance ranged from 0.8 to 81.5% (mean = 8%, s.d. = 11.1%). Forty-six genes showed genome-wide significant TWAS associations (p < 5 × 10−8; Supplementary Data 1). Using these TWAS effect sizes as weights, we computed panel-specific SZ-GeRS for CMC in our cohorts. In the multiplex sample, 7834 genes had predicted expression; none reached genome-wide significance for association with early-onset SZ (Supplementary Fig. 5). In the simplex sample, 7987 genes were predicted; again, none reached genome-wide significance (Supplementary Fig. 6).

In parallel, using the GTEx Brain Cortex prediction model, S-PrediXcan generated TWAS results for all 5481 genes included in the model, of which 5345 genes had successfully predicted expression using PrediXcan. Gene-level prediction performance ranged from 2.1 to 73.0% (mean = 14.9%, s.d. = 13.1%). Fifty-two genes showed genome-wide significant TWAS associations (p < 5 × 10−8; Supplementary Data 2). Panel-specific SZ-GeRS derived from both transcriptomic reference panels were subsequently standardized and integrated into a composite SZ-GeRS.

Functional enrichment analysis of TWAS-significant genes revealed that CMC DLPFC-based genes showed predominant expression in cortical and basal ganglia tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3A), enrichment in glutamatergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 3B), and convergence with previously reported SZ-related gene sets (Supplementary Fig. 3C). In contrast, GTEx Brain Cortex-based TWAS-significant genes demonstrated enrichment across multiple peripheral tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4A), with gene set analyses indicating involvement in immune-related pathways and disease ontology categories (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C).

Module-based GeRS and early-onset SZ

To capture coordinated genetic effects, we evaluated 13 WGCNA-derived co-expression modules from postmortem brain tissue [33]. In the multiplex sample, module 10-based GeRS (M10-GeRS) explained the greatest variance in early-onset SZ (pseudo-R2 = 2.54%; Fig. 2A) and remained significant after false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction (p = 0.02; FDR threshold p < 0.05). Of the 161 genes in module 10, 99 had available SNP-expression weights, among which 58 were predicted to be expressed in the multiplex sample (Supplementary Table 2). In the simplex sample, the same 58 genes were predicted and completely overlapped with those identified in the multiplex sample. Early-onset patients had significantly higher standardized M10sub-GeRS than late-onset patients in both multiplex (Fig. 2B) and simplex (Fig. 2C) samples. When unaffected relatives were included, early-onset patients showed the highest M10sub-GeRS values, late-onset patients showed lower values, and relatives showed intermediate values (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). Hub genes for early-onset SZ

Network analysis of the 58 M10sub genes in multiplex data was performed using IPA, which identified four interaction networks. Among these, only Network 1 exceeded an IPA score of 40 (IPA score = 82; Supplementary Data 3 and 4). Robustness using alternative modules

We reconstructed module-based GeRS using an alternative set of modules derived from three diagnostic groups (SZ, bipolar disorder, autism) [48] (see Supplementary Results). Because none of the module-based GeRS achieved statistical significance under this framework, we subsequently focused on the two top-ranked modules with the greatest explanatory relevance. One of the nine hub genes identified using the top two modules in this alternative framework overlapped with a hub gene from the original early-onset-related Module 10.

Sensitivity analysis accounting for polygenicity-driven inflation in TWAS

In sensitivity analyses, we applied a variance-calibrated approach to account for polygenicity-driven inflation in TWAS. As expected, variance calibration attenuated gene-level association magnitudes; however, the relative ranking of hub genes was largely preserved, and the overall pattern of findings remained highly consistent with the original analysis. GeRS derived from calibrated and uncalibrated TWAS weights remained significantly correlated, although calibration shifted the absolute score distributions. Importantly, at the module level, the primary findings of the study were robust to this correction. The M10sub-GeRS remained the most informative module for distinguishing early-onset SZ in both samples, retained statistical significance after FDR correction, and showed comparable or slightly improved predictive performance. The meta-analytic summary effect for M10sub-GeRS was stable and directionally consistent with the main analysis. Similarly, STRING-derived M10hub-GeRS remained significantly associated, whereas the smaller IPA-defined hub subset showed attenuation and loss of significance after calibration. Additional results are provided in the Supplementary Results and Supplementary Figs. 15–18.

Discussion

Antipsychotics remain the cornerstone of SZ treatment, yet many individuals with early-onset SZ respond poorly or show resistance to therapy. Existing antipsychotic agents were developed largely through serendipitous observations of their therapeutic effects in unrelated contexts [54], rather than from mechanistic insights. Identifying novel drug targets through genomic approaches that clarify the molecular basis of early-onset SZ is therefore of critical importance.

In this study, we applied a composite GeRS framework integrating two transcriptomic reference panels to early-onset SZ and identified a co-expression module (module 10) whose genetically regulated expression was associated with earlier disease onset. Within this module, hub genes were identified using two complementary network-based approaches. IPA highlighted COPS6, MRPS7, PSMD2, PSMB5, RUVBL2, NDN, PTPRR, FEN1, and LRPPRC as highly connected candidates, whereas STRING network analysis identified COPS6, COPS7A, ERCC3, GARS, MRPS7, PARP2, PSMD2, SARS, RUVBL2, and STOML2. Notably, several genes (e.g., COPS6, MRPS7, RUVBL2, and PSMD2) were consistently prioritized across methods, supporting their central role within the module. Functional mapping and enrichment analyses suggested that Module 10 captured transcriptional programs relevant to neuronal, immune, and glial processes in the BA9 cortex, and that TWAS-significant genes were overrepresented in brain tissues and neuronal pathways, reinforcing the biological coherence of the GeRS-identified module.

At the molecular level, hub genes prioritized by IPA and STRING were enriched for processes including proteasomal protein degradation, DNA repair and chromatin remodeling, mitochondrial translation, and aminoacyl-tRNA synthesis, suggesting roles in cellular homeostasis and neurodevelopment. Temporal expression patterns further showed increasing expression from late prenatal development into postnatal stages, consistent with roles in cortical maturation. While these findings highlight coherent biological pathways and promising candidate genes, further work is needed to delineate their roles in schizophrenia pathophysiology and to evaluate their translational relevance.

Beyond the biological coherence of Module 10, it is important to assess the reproducibility of these findings. Because GeRS depend on transcriptomic reference models, confirming that associations reflect stable biological signals rather than panel-specific artifacts is essential. Prior work has shown that concordance may be present at intermediate levels, such as predicted expression or gene-level weights, even when phenotypic associations differ due to statistical power, sample composition, or model architecture (e.g., [19, 20]). In the present study, cross-panel concordance analyses revealed moderate agreement between CMC- and GTEx-derived predicted expression for overlapping genes, supporting partial sharing of cis-genetic regulatory signals while acknowledging biological heterogeneity across reference resources. Furthermore, the main association signals were robust to a variance-calibrated sensitivity analysis, suggesting that the observed Module 10 findings are unlikely to be explained solely by polygenicity-driven inflation in TWAS. Although the IPA-defined hub score was attenuated after calibration, this may reflect the greater sensitivity of smaller, more restricted gene sets to changes in TWAS weight estimation. In contrast, the broader Module 10 signal, particularly M10sub-GeRS, remained stable. Together, these findings strengthen the evidence for the reproducibility of the Module 10 association across diverse transcriptomic architectures and under more stringent statistical control.

Several methodological features support the internal consistency of our findings within a composite multi-panel GeRS framework. We evaluated module-based GeRS in two independent clinical cohorts and combined results through meta-analysis using identical SNP-expression weights and outcome definitions. Despite differences in familial loading between multiplex and simplex samples, the pattern of results was consistent. Across both samples, early-onset patients exhibited lower neurocognitive performance and higher SZ-PRS than late-onset SZ patients, consistent with previous reports [55–58] and supporting the rationale for pooling data in meta-analysis. Together, these findings demonstrate reproducibility across clinically distinct samples under a common GeRS construction framework. However, while integration of two transcriptomic reference panels enhances generalizability relative to single-panel approaches, it does not fully establish robustness across all possible transcriptomic architectures or tissue contexts. Future studies incorporating additional brain regions, ancestries, and larger expression reference panels will be necessary to further evaluate cross-resource stability.

Although no individual gene reached transcriptome-wide significance, consistent with prior TWAS reports [22, 59], aggregating genes into co-expression modules produced functionally meaningful results. Modules derived from postmortem brain tissues across five psychiatric disorders [33] showed significant association with early-onset SZ, whereas modules derived from three psychiatric disorders confined to two specific brain regions [48] did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy may reflect several factors. Psychiatric disorders across diagnostic boundaries share substantial genetic liability, and restricting analysis to limited brain regions may overlook contributions from other areas. Moreover, the gene list of module 10 partially overlaps with a neuronal mitochondrial gene-enriched module previously linked to essential neuronal processes and psychiatric diseases [33]. Module 10 has also been used to enrich co-expression profiles from mouse models carrying SZ- and autism-associated mutations, revealing associations with neuronal energetics and firing rate [60].

The module-based GeRS approach should therefore be interpreted as a biologically informed aggregation rather than a formal network model. Because genes within a co-expression module are inherently correlated, summing gene-level GeRS values represents a heuristic index of genetically regulated risk convergence rather than assuming independent effects. This conceptualization aligns with prior efforts using WGCNA-derived networks to construct genetically informed indices of SZ risk [18–20], but extends those efforts to predicted gene expression. Although future covariance-adjusted or Bayesian frameworks could further refine this strategy, the current approach offers a practical method to test whether genetically regulated expression converges within disease-relevant networks, thus providing insight into the molecular mechanisms of early-onset SZ.

The results also suggest that GeRS capture aspects of genetic risk that differ from those indexed by PRS. GeRS may have better cross-ancestry portability than PRS [61, 62], as they are derived from QTL linked to conserved biological mechanisms rather than population-specific allele frequencies. Adding module-based SZ-GeRS to models that already included SZ-PRS and key covariates substantially increased explained variance while maintaining the significance of main effects in both samples. The weak correlation between SZ-PRS and module-based GeRS indicates that they reflect largely distinct genetic dimensions. Accordingly, module-based SZ-GeRS may complement SZ-PRS by incorporating gene-level functional information, thereby enhancing differentiation between early- and late-onset SZ. These observations are consistent with prior evidence that GeRS and PRS quantify independent components of genetic risk [21, 61] and that integrating functionally informed GeRS can improve risk prediction for complex traits [62, 63].

While enrichment analysis of the full module 10 highlights coordinated neuronal-glial processes, we next focused on hub genes prioritized by network-based approaches to explore potential regulators within this network. Network analysis based on the 58 genes in M10 (the M10sub set) identified highly connected candidates using both IPA and STRING PPI frameworks. Several genes (including COPS6, MRPS7, RUVBL2, and PSMD2) were consistently prioritized across methods and exhibited high eigengene connectivity, supporting their central position within the network. Evidence from literature review, developmental expression profiling, and functional enrichment analyses suggests that these hub genes are involved in neurodevelopmental and immune-related pathways. Dysregulation of their coordinated expression could influence neural circuit maturation and immune signaling, processes long implicated in the pathophysiology of early-onset SZ [64–66]. The enrichment of gene sets related to perineuronal oligodendrocytes and B-lymphocyte regulation is consistent with earlier reports of glial alterations and immune dysregulation to SZ. Furthermore, the preferential expression of these genes in excitatory neurons aligns with recent single-cell transcriptomic studies showing that SZ risk variants converge on excitatory neuronal gene networks [67].

These hub genes may also have clinical relevance. Their involvement in regulatory networks rather than direct susceptibility loci raises the possibility that they act as genetic modifiers influencing disease course or treatment responsiveness. Although speculative, this interpretation highlights a potential mechanism underlying the limited therapeutic response observed in early-onset SZ, in which transcriptional regulation rather than receptor-level signaling may play a dominant role. Future studies integrating pharmacogenomic data and longitudinal clinical measures will be essential to determine whether these genes can serve as therapeutic entry points or biomarkers for early intervention.

This study nevertheless has several limitations. Because it employed a case-only design, the genes identified here may represent mixed loci that serve both susceptibility and modifier roles [31, 68], limiting the ability to detect purely modifier genes specific to early-onset SZ. The modest sample size also constrained statistical power, potentially explaining why individual gene-level associations did not reach transcriptome-wide significance despite strong aggregate effects. In addition, although we integrated two independent transcriptomic panels using a meta-analytic framework, this approach assumes homogeneous effects across panels. Moreover, variance calibration could be applied only to the GTEx panel because comparable panel-specific inflation parameters were not currently available for the CMC prediction models. Thus, inflation-related effects across all transcriptomic reference resources cannot be fully excluded. Nevertheless, the consistency of the Module 10 findings across calibrated and uncalibrated analyses provides some reassurance regarding the robustness of the reported associations. Residual heterogeneity related to tissue or prediction differences may not be fully captured, and more flexible modeling approaches could improve multi-panel GeRS estimation. Another limitation is that our eQTL and co-expression resources were derived from adult postmortem brain tissue; transcriptomic datasets based on fetal or developmental brain samples [69–72] may better capture early neurodevelopmental regulatory processes relevant to early-onset SZ. Finally, because GeRS reflect genetically predicted rather than directly observed expression, future in vitro and in vivo studies will be needed to validate their biological relevance and assess causal significance.

In summary, we demonstrated that a module-based GeRS approach integrating predicted gene expression can reveal additional genetic contributions to early-onset SZ. Network and enrichment analyses prioritized hub genes involved in neurodevelopmental, mitochondrial, and excitatory neuronal pathways. These findings deepen our understanding of SZ etiology and support further investigation of regulatory gene networks as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets.
