Isolation and identification of Klebsiella pneumoniae from Tibetan pigs and preparation of inactivated vaccine with Tibetan medicine adjuvant
Authors: Gang Wang, Yanjuan Zhao, Xiaoxia Ye, Runbo Luo, Mingbang Wei, Hongliang Zhang, Jian Zhang, Peng Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the prevalence of Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP) in Tibetan pigs. Nasal swabs and lung tissue samples were collected, from which hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKP) were isolated through culture, identification, serotyping, and virulence gene detection. These strains subsequently underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing and pathogenicity assessment. Using a hvKP as the antigen, three types of vaccines were prepared: an inactivated vaccine, an inactivated vaccine adjuvanted with Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide, and an inactivated vaccine with a conventional adjuvant. Mouse immunization experiments were conducted to evaluate immunogenic potency, survival rates post-challenge, and antibody production, thereby comparing the protective efficacy of the different vaccine formulations.The results of this experiment showed that 54 strains of KP were isolated from nasal swabs and lungs of Tibetan pigs; They were typed and found to contain two highly virulent serovars (K20 and K54);The virulence genes carried by the two serovars of KP were found to contain hvKP characteristic virulence genes in the virulence gene assay, which is consistent with the hvKP characteristic.Both serovars of KP exhibited limited drug resistance but still belonged to multidrug-resistant bacteria. In this study, mouse challenge experiments confirmed that these strains possess significant pathogenicity and lethality toward healthy mice, meeting the criteria for use as immunogenic antigens The results of sterility test and animal safety test proved that the final concentration of the inactivator was 0.3% and had no obvious adverse reaction to individual animals; the results of immune response test showed that the three kinds of inactivated vaccines had no adverse reaction to mice, and the antibody potency of inactivated vaccine with adjuvant of Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide was higher than that of inactivated vaccine of KP in the immune potency test, and the antibody potency of inactivated vaccine with conventional adjuvant was the highest, which proved that Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide could be used as a vaccine to increase the immune antibody potency. This proves that Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide can be used as a vaccine adjuvant to increase the antibody potency of immunity. In a murine model, the inactivated vaccine elicited complete protection (100% survival) against a lethal challenge with the homologous K20 and K54 strains under the tested conditions. Furthermore, bacterial re-isolation experiments confirmed that deceased mice in control groups were positive for KP, while all immunized mice survived and cleared the infection. In summary, the prevalent KP of porcine origin in the plateau Linzhi area is mainly prevalent in K20 and K54. These strains carry the virulence genes of hvKP, are pathogenic and lethal, and belong to the high-virulence KP. The inactivated vaccine prepared against these two serovars has been shown to have significant protective efficacy, and the incorporation of Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide as a vaccine adjuvant has been demonstrated to enhance the protective efficacy of the vaccine. This provides a valuable foundation for the development of subsequent preventive and control measures against Klebsiella pneumoniae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05392-0.Keywords: Tibetan pigs, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Inactivated vaccine, Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP) is a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen possessing O and K antigens. It is widely distributed in the environment and within animals, typically non-pathogenic but capable of causing infections when host immunity is compromised. The bacterium is categorized into two pathological types: classical Klebsiella pneumoniae (cKP) and hvKP. Both types exhibit multidrug resistance, posing an ongoing threat to global public health and the livestock industry [1–2]. Of particular concern is the emergence of hvKP, which can cause severe community-acquired infections in healthy people and has been associated with metastatic infections [3] such as liver abscess, meningitis, and endophthalmitis [4–5]. According to the literature, hvKP is an invasive pathogenic type distinct from cKP, characterized by hypervirulence and a hypermucoviscous phenotype, with serovars primarily including K1, K2, K5, K20, K54, and K57 [6]. The results of the string test were considered positive when a sticky string longer than 5 mm could be produced by touching and pulling individual colonies upwards with a standard inoculation ring. According to existing literature, iroB, iucA, peg344, rmpA, and rmpA2 are the five key virulence marker factors that define hvKp, and all of these markers have been shown to exist on virulence plasmids [6]. In recent years, with the emergence of hvKp on pig farms, infection rates have shown an increasing trend. These strains can not only cause pneumonia in pigs but also lead to sepsis, mastitis, and urinary tract infections [7–9]. Owing to the widespread transmission of KP between animals and humans (Hetland et al., 2025), in-depth research into the pathogenic mechanisms of pig-derived KP is of significant practical importance for safeguarding public health and enhancing biosecurity management in the swine industry. However, current research has predominantly focused on intensively farmed conventional pig breeds, while knowledge regarding the prevalence and evolutionary characteristics of hvKP in indigenous breeds inhabiting unique plateau ecosystems, such as Tibetan pigs, remains scarce. Tibet is in the southwestern part of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, with its geographical coordinates ranging approximately from 26°50ʹ to 36°53ʹ N and 78°25ʹ to 99°06ʹ E. Renowned for its unique geographical environment and climate conditions, this region serves as a habitat for various endemic species such as yaks and Tibetan pigs. Tibetan pigs are primarily distributed in Linzhi and Changdu. Among these, Linzhi is the most significant production region for Tibetan pigs. In this study, Tibetan pigs in Linzhi of Tibet were initially used as the research object. Tibetan pig are livestock breed that have been traditionally raised by farmers and herders on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau over an extended period, having undergone long-term evolution under extreme environmental conditions characterized by high altitude, low oxygen levels, and intense ultraviolet radiation. Consequently, investigating KP carried by Tibetan pigs not only addresses the knowledge gap in pathogen epidemiology within this region but, more importantly, provides an excellent natural model for exploring whether extreme environmental pressures have shaped the unique virulence, adaptability, or transmission traits of KP.

According to World Health Organization data, food is a significant route for transmitting antibiotic-resistant bacteria to humans, with foodborne Klebsiella pneumoniae drug resistance being particularly concerning [10]. Past inappropriate antibiotic use has led to KP evolving into a multidrug-resistant pathogen with continuously enhanced virulence and pathogenicity, constituting a global public health threat [10]. Therefore, monitoring the prevalence and drug resistance of KP in food is essential for developing effective prevention, control, and treatment strategies. In animal husbandry, increasing KP resistance causes significant economic losses, shifting the current focus toward prevention over treatment [11–12]. Although research into KP inactivated vaccines has shown some immunogenic effects, the protective scope of such vaccines remains limited due to KP’s numerous serovars. Currently, there are no specific therapeutic agents or commercial vaccines against KP available on the market. The primary control measures involve blocking transmission routes, including enhanced disinfection of breeding environments and personnel, regular monitoring, timely isolation and treatment of infected animals, and combining Chinese and Western medical therapies with antibiotic rotation in treatment to delay resistance development.

This study focuses on Tibetan pigs as subjects to isolate and identify porcine-origin KP, analyzing serovars, virulence genes, and drug resistance to identify prevalent hypervirulent strains in the plateau region. Pathogenicity experiments were conducted to evaluate the virulence of hvKP strains. Selected hypervirulent strains were used to prepare inactivated vaccines, with Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide employed as an adjuvant. The immunoprotective effects of this adjuvant were compared with those of conventional adjuvant vaccines to investigate the modulatory role of Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide on vaccine immune responses, thereby providing a basis for the prevention and control of. Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) investigate the prevalence, serovar distribution hvKP, and antimicrobial resistance of KP isolated from Tibetan pigs in the Linzhi region; (2) evaluate the pathogenicity of prevalent hypervirulent serovars (K20, K54) in a mouse model; and (3) for the first time, develop and evaluate an inactivated vaccine against these serovars, incorporating a novel adjuvant derived from local Tibetan medicine (Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide).

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Collection of Porcine Lung Tissues: In this study, lung tissue samples were collected from Tibetan pigs at a slaughterhouse in Linzhi, Tibet. A total of 109 samples were obtained, covering pigs of different genders and growth stages. The primary sampling subjects were healthy adult Tibetan pigs designated for slaughter.

Collection of Nasal Swabs: Nasal swab samples were primarily collected from a Tibetan pig breeding base in Linzhi, Tibet. The samples included swabs from both healthy Tibetan pigs (1–2 months old, of different genders) and those suffering from respiratory diseases. A total of 76 nasal swab samples were collected.

Collection of Pathological Specimens from Deceased Pigs: Pathological specimens were collected from deceased Tibetan pigs at the breeding base in Linzhi, Tibet. Samples included the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, with three specimens collected from each organ type, resulting in a total of 15 samples.

Isolation and Identification

Under sterile conditions, nasal swab or tissue samples were inoculated into MacConkey liquid medium and cultured at 37℃ with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h. Turbid cultures were streaked onto MacConkey solid medium and incubated at 37℃ for 24 h. Suspected pink colonies were selected for purification.

Identification of pure cultures was performed by assessing 11 biochemical indicators, including glucose, lactose, indole, Voges-Proskauer (VP), methyl red (MR), nitrate reduction, malonate utilization, urease, lysine decarboxylase, ornithine decarboxylase, and citrate utilization, following the Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology and related literature [13]. Each strain was tested in duplicate. After inoculation into the respective biochemical tubes, cultures were incubated at 37℃ for 18–48 h, and the reactions were observed and recorded.

Additionally, the final results were confirmed by combining PCR-based species identification. Using KP-specific primers designed according to the literature [14], the target gene was amplified to further verify the identity of the isolates.

Determination of capsular genotyping and hypermucoviscous phenotype

The isolates were PCR-amplified using capsular (K) serovar-specific primers targeting the seven major serotvars of hvKP. The primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

To identify the hypermucoviscous phenotype via the string test, all strains were inoculated onto agar plates containing 5% sheep blood as previously described [15] to assess whether they exhibited the hypermucoviscous phenotype. The string test was considered positive when a viscous filament longer than 5 mm could be formed by touching and lifting an individual colony with a standard inoculation loop.

Virulence gene Identification of hvKP

By referencing the virulence gene primers (Supplementary Table S2) for hvKP documented in the literature, 12 pairs of virulence gene primers were selected to detect the presence of virulence genes in hypervirulent strains [16–18]. The virulence genes included those associated with pili (ycfM, fimH, mrkD), capsular polysaccharide synthesis and its regulation (rmpA, magA, and rmpA2), lipopolysaccharide-related genes (wabG, uge), and siderophore-related genes (entB, IucA, iroN).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of hvKP

Referencing the literature and the 2020 Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (30th Edition) (Neuberger et al., 2008), a total of 11 antibiotic disks were selected for testing, including Amoxicillin (AMC), Azithromycin (AZM), Ceftazidime (CAZ), Cefotaxime (CTX), Ceftriaxone (CRO), Chloramphenicol (CHL), Ciprofloxacin (CIP), Doxycycline (DOX), Gentamicin (GEN), Meropenem (MEM), and Polymyxin B (PB). The antimicrobial resistance profile of hvKP was observed accordingly. Escherichia coli ATCC25922 was used as a control.

Animals and ethics statement

Kunming mice (Average weight: 150 g), aged 5–6 weeks, were sourced from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), ensuring they were specific pathogen-free (SPF). The ethical approval for this study was given to us (No. NY 202423) on 29/12/2024 by the the Ethics Committee for Experimental Animals of Xizang Agricultural and Animal Husbandry University to approve this trial.

Upon completion of the experiment, all mice were deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg). After confirming the absence of pain reflexes, euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation, followed immediately by the collection of required tissue samples. This procedure complies with the Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare in China and is designed to ensure that the animals experience no suffering throughout the process.

Pathogenicity testing of hvKP

Rationale for the murine infection model: To evaluate the pathogenic potential of the hvKP isolates and to establish a model for subsequent vaccine efficacy testing, a murine intraperitoneal (i.p.) challenge model was employed. The i.p. route was selected for the primary pathogenicity assessment because it provides a reliable and reproducible systemic infection model for K. pneumoniae, facilitating the determination of median lethal dose (LD50) and observation of characteristic systemic lesions (e.g., in liver and spleen) which are relevant to hvKP disease manifestations. The challenge doses for each serovar (K20 and K54) were determined based on preliminary range-finding experiments to establish a gradient that would yield mortality rates between 0% and 100%, allowing for accurate LD50 calculation. The primary purpose of this initial model was disease modeling and virulence quantification.

Five- to six-week-old mice were selected and divided into two major experimental groups and one control group based on serovar: Group A (blank control), Group B (K20), and Group C (K54). Groups B and C were further subdivided into 4–5 subgroups with different challenge dose gradients: Group A consisted of 1 blank control subgroup, Group B included 5 subgroups (1 × 109, 7 × 108, 4 × 108, 1 × 108,7 × 107; B1-B5) with varying challenge doses, and Group C comprised 4 subgroups (1 × 109, 1 × 108, 1 × 107, 1 × 106; C1-C4) with different challenge doses. In total, there were 10 subgroups, with 5 mice per subgroup, resulting in 50 mice in the experiment. The challenge was administered via intraperitoneal injection, and the mice were continuously observed for 7 days post-challenge. Clinical changes, time of death, number of deaths, and mortality rates were recorded and analyzed. On day 7, surviving mice were euthanized for pathological dissection. The median lethal dose (LD50) of the bacterial strains was calculated using the modified Karber’s formula based on the number of deaths in each subgroup. A semi-quantitative scoring system was employed in this study to evaluate gross pathological lesions. The severity of organ-specific alterations, such as congestion and enlargement (e.g., in the liver), was graded as absent, mild, moderate, or severe.

All mice designated for histopathological analysis were deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg). After confirming the absence of pain reflexes, euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation, followed immediately by the collection of required tissue samples. The collected tissue samples were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 24 h, then trimmed and washed under running water. Subsequently, dehydration was carried out through a graded ethanol series, followed by clearing in xylene and paraffin infiltration. The tissue blocks were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4–5 μm. Sections were floated in warm water, mounted onto slides, and dried on a slide warmer. The slides were then deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol gradient. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed as follows: nuclei were stained with hematoxylin, followed by differentiation and bluing, and the cytoplasm was then counterstained with eosin. After staining, the sections were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and finally mounted with neutral balsam for observation under a light microscope. The histopathological sections were independently examined and evaluated by two pathologists who were blinded to the group assignments, using an optical microscope.

Extraction and quantification of astragalus polysaccharide

Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide was extracted using a hot water decoction method. Briefly, astragalus roots were sliced, and 20 g of the slices were accurately weighed. After washing, the slices were placed in a beaker with 200 mL of purified water and decocted three times for 1.5 h, 1 h, and 40 min, respectively. The decoctions from each cycle were filtered separately, combined, and concentrated to a final ratio of 1:1 (crude drug : liquid). Residual impurities were removed by filtration and centrifugation. Three volumes of 95% ethanol were then added to the concentrate for alcohol precipitation. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration as the crude Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide, which was subsequently dried in a 40℃ oven. The dried polysaccharide extract obtained in this experiment weighed 0.72 g.

Subsequently, the total polysaccharide content of the dried extract was quantified using the phenol-sulfuric acid method. Using glucose as a standard, the total polysaccharide content of this batch of extract was determined to be 82.5 ± 1.8%.

Preparation of hvKP antigen suspension

Two hvKP strains of serovars K20 and K54 were selected and cultured in LB liquid medium at 37℃, 220 rpm for 12 h, followed by plate counting. The bacterial suspensions of both serovars were adjusted to a concentration of 1.0 × 10⁹ CFU/mL and divided into four groups. Formaldehyde solution was slowly added to each group for inactivation, with final concentrations of 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3%, and 0.4%, respectively (Table 1).

Screening of hvKP inactivation conditions

The bacterial suspensions containing the inactivating agent were incubated in a shaker at 37℃ and 220 rpm for 12 h, 24 h, and 36 h, respectively, followed by sterility testing. At each time point, 100 µL of the suspension was aspirated and evenly spread onto LB agar plates, with three replicates per group. The plates were then incubated in a 37℃ constant-temperature incubator for 72 h to check for bacterial growth. The absence of observable bacterial growth confirmed complete inactivation.

Formaldehyde solution was added to a hvKP suspension with a titer of 1.0 × 10⁹ CFU/mL to a final concentration of 0.3%. After gentle mixing, the suspension was inactivated by shaking at 37℃ and 220 rpm for 24 h. The inactivated bacterial suspension was then spread onto LB agar plates (100 µL per plate) for sterility testing. The plates were sealed with parafilm, appropriately labeled, and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h to check for bacterial growth. The absence of growth confirmed complete inactivation; any observed growth indicated either incomplete inactivation or contamination, necessitating repetition of the inactivation process. The fully inactivated suspensions of the two hvKP serovars (K20 and K54) were separately diluted with sterile PBS to a final concentration of 5.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. The inactivated K20 and K54 suspensions were then mixed at a 1:1 ratio to prepare the hvKP inactivated vaccine with a final concentration of 5.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, which was stored at 4 °C.

Preparation of inactivated vaccine of hvKP

The preparation process of the inactivated Klebsiella pneumoniae vaccine adjuvanted with Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide is as follows: 0.2 g Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide was weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of PBS. The polysaccharide solution was then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain a sterilized stock solution with a concentration of 20 mg/mL. The inactivated bacterial suspension was mixed with the astragalus polysaccharide adjuvant solution at a 1:1 ratio, yielding a final bacterial concentration of 5.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. The mixture was aseptically aliquoted into 15 mL centrifuge tubes and stored at 4℃.

The preparation procedure for the conventional adjuvant-based hvKP inactivated vaccine was as follows: Aluminum hydroxide adjuvant was selected as the conventional adjuvant, and the finished product was purchased from Beijing Nobleryder Technology Co., Ltd. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the inactivated KP antigen suspension was mixed thoroughly with the aluminum hydroxide adjuvant. The final concentrations were adjusted to 5.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for KP and 500 µg/mL for the aluminum hydroxide adjuvant.

Inactivated vaccine safety testing in hvKP

Twelve 5-6-week-old mice were randomly divided into 4 groups, with 3 mice in each group. Vaccines were administered via intraperitoneal injection. Group G1 served as the control group, with each mouse receiving 200 µL of sterile PBS. Group G2 was the immunization group, with each mouse receiving 200 µL of the hvKP inactivated vaccine. Group G3 was the immunization group, with each mouse receiving 200 µL of the hvKP inactivated vaccine supplemented with Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide adjuvant. Group G4 was the immunization group, with each mouse receiving 200 µL of the KP inactivated vaccine supplemented with aluminum hydroxide adjuvant. After vaccination, the mice were observed for mental status, signs of inflammation or other abnormal symptoms at the injection site, and survival conditions.

Evaluation of immunization efficacy in hvKP

To evaluate the immunogenicity of the vaccine, forty 5-6-week-old mice were selected and vaccinated according to the same grouping scheme used in the safety assessment, with 10 mice per group. The first immunization was designated as day 0, and a second immunization was administered after an interval of 14 days, using the same dosage and administration route as the first immunization.

Serum antibody titers were determined using a serum agglutination test. On day 14 after the first immunization and day 6 after the second immunization, blood was collected from the submandibular vein of the mice using sterile syringes. The blood samples were labeled and centrifuged at 4℃, 6000 rpm for 3 min. The upper serum layer was carefully aspirated using a pipette, avoiding red blood cells. Prepare a 96-well V-bottom microtiter plate and label it according to the aforementioned group designations (G1, G2, G3, G4) for later use. 20µL serum were collected and serially diluted twofold with sterile PBS before being added to the wells, resulting in final dilutions of 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1:16, 1:32, 1:64, and 1:128. 10µL sterile PBS were added to the last well as a control, with each sample tested in duplicate. 20µL KP suspensions, with concentrations of 4.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for the K20 and 1.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for the K54, were added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37℃ for 10 h. Agglutination was judged positive if sediment appeared at the bottom or edges of the V-shaped wells, and negative if the bottom remained clear with little or no sediment. Results were expressed as log₂ values of the dilution factor (N).

Rationale for the vaccine challenge model: To evaluate the protective efficacy of the inactivated vaccines, immunized mice were challenged via the intraperitoneal route. This model was chosen for vaccine evaluation because: (1) it allows for a controlled, severe systemic challenge that effectively differentiates between protected and non-protected animals within a short timeframe; (2) the challenge doses were directly derived from the LD50 values established in the prior pathogenicity study (4 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for K20 and 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for K54), ensuring a consistent and significant pathogenic insult; and (3) it aligns with established methods for screening the efficacy of bacterial vaccines, where survival and reduction of bacterial burden in systemic organs are key endpoints.

Seven days after the second immunization, Groups G1, G2, G3, and G4 were each subdivided into two subgroups: G11 and G12, G21 and G22, G31 and G32, and G41 and G42. An additional blank control group, Group G5, was included, resulting in a total of nine groups, each containing five mice. Mice in subgroups G11, G21, G31, and G41 were intraperitoneally injected with 0.2 mL of the K20 at a concentration of 4.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. Mice in subgroups G12, G22, G32, and G42 were intraperitoneally injected with 0.2 mL of the K54 at a concentration of 1.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. Group G5, the blank control, received 0.2 mL of sterile PBS via intraperitoneal injection. After challenge, the mice were monitored for mental status, dietary intake, clinical symptoms, and mortality, with detailed records kept.

Mice that died post-challenge were dissected, and small sections of visceral tissues (e.g., liver, spleen, and lungs) were collected, ground, and placed in MacConkey liquid medium. The samples were incubated at 37℃, 220 rpm for 12–16 h (with possible extension based on medium color changes). Turbid cultures were streaked onto MacConkey solid medium for purification. DNA was extracted from the purified cultures and subjected to PCR-based identification to detect the presence of KP by observing bands and their sizes.

Three days post-challenge, three mice from each group were randomly selected for dissection. Liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were collected, fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 24 h, and processed into pathological sections for histological examination. For mice that died within three days, dissection and pathological section preparation were performed immediately after death.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean±standard error (SEM) of triplicate samples per experimental condition from three independent experiments in GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, United States). One-way ANOVA followed by two independent samples t-test was conducted to detect significant differences between experimental groups. Statistical significance was determined at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

Prevalence and serovar of KP

A total of 54 suspected KP were isolated in the experiment, with an overall detection rate of 27%. As shown in Fig. 1A, the colonies appeared pink, mucoid, and moist on MacConkey agar. The isolation rates varied depending on the source and condition of the samples. As indicated in Table 2, the isolation rate from lung tissues of healthy Tibetan pigs was 5.5%, while nasal swabs from healthy piglets showed a detection rate of 51%. Samples from diseased Tibetan pigs (including nasal swabs and visceral tissues) exhibited a significantly higher detection rate of 62.5%, indicating a greater prevalence of KP in diseased animals compared to healthy ones.

Biochemical identification results were consistent with the characteristics of KP: positive for glucose, lactose, alanine, urease, lysine decarboxylase, and Voges-Proskauer (VP) tests, and negative for indole, nitrate reduction, ornithine decarboxylase, methyl red (MR), and Simmons citrate utilization tests. Sequencing and alignment of the obtained sequences through the GenBank database showed over 98% similarity, confirming that all detected strains were KP.

Identification results of virulence genes in hvKP

Ten virulence genes of 22 hvKP strains were amplified by PCR, and the positive amplification results of some strains are shown in Fig. 2B and C. The detection rates of the virulence genes varied: entB and iucA showed the highest rates at 91%, followed by ycfM, fimH, and mrkD at 77%, iroN and rmpA at 73%, while fimH, makD, and rmpA2 exhibited detection rates of 45%, 68%, and 41%, respectively (Fig. 2D).

Results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing in hvKP

Among the 22 hvKP strains tested for antibiotic susceptibility, the selected antibiotics and their corresponding resistance rates were as follows: CTR (4.55%), CAZ (0%), PB (0%), MEM (4.55%), CIP (31.82%), AZM (31.82%), AMC (95.45%), CHL (63.64%), CTX (13.64%), DOX (95.45%), and GEN (0%) (Table 3). Multidrug resistance analysis revealed that the highest level of resistance observed was six-drug resistance. Among the strains, 40.91% exhibited triple-drug resistance, 9.09% showed quadruple-drug resistance, 18.18% displayed quintuple-drug resistance, and 0.09% demonstrated sextuple-drug resistance. Strains with triple-drug resistance or higher accounted for 77.27% of the total.

Results of pathogenicity experiment

Based on the concentration gradients designed in the preliminary experiments, all mice in the PBS control group (Group A) survived. In Group B, K20 was administered at five concentration gradients: 1.0 × 10⁹, 7.0 × 10⁸, 4.0 × 10⁸, 1.0 × 10⁸, and 7.0 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, corresponding to subgroups B1, B2, B3, B4, and B5. The mortality rates observed over 7 days post-challenge were 100%, 100%, 80%, 20%, and 0%, respectively. In Group C, K54 was administered at four concentration gradients: 1.0 × 10⁹, 1.0 × 10⁸, 1.0 × 10⁷, and 1.0 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, corresponding to subgroups C1, C2, C3, and C4. The mortality rates observed over 7 days post-challenge were 100%, 80%, 40%, and 0%, respectively. Using the modified Karber’s formula, the LD50 for K20 was calculated as 10⁸ CFU/mL, and for K54 as 10⁷ CFU/mL.

In Group B (K20), all mice in subgroups B1 and B2 died within 12 to 48 h post-challenge. In subgroup B3, 80% of the mice died between 48 and 72 h post-challenge, while the surviving mice began to recover their mental state, appetite, and body weight after 4 days. In subgroup B4, 80% of the mice survived, with the deceased mice showing symptoms such as lack of appetite and thirst, rapid breathing, purulent discharge from the eyelids and nasal cavity, weight loss, and persistent emaciation, ultimately dying from severe emaciation and dehydration by day 6 post-challenge. Mice in subgroup B5 exhibited lethargy and reduced appetite post-challenge, with symptoms gradually alleviating after 3 days, and all survived. In summary, the main symptoms in mice challenged with K20 included loss of appetite, depression, lethargy, huddling, and rapid breathing. Most surviving mice developed purulent discharge from the eyelids and nasal cavity 24 h post-challenge. Deceased mice showed significant weight loss prior to death. Surviving mice in subgroup B5 had milder symptoms, with no obvious discharge from the nasal cavity or eyelids, but increased water intake.

In Group C (K54), all mice in subgroup C1 died within 8 to 24 h post-challenge. In subgroup C2, 80% of the mice died within 8 to 48 h post-challenge, while the surviving mice began to show symptom relief after 3 days. In subgroup C3, 40% of the mice died between 2 and 5 days post-challenge. Mice with chronic deaths exhibited obvious respiratory symptoms, including discharge from the nasal cavity and eyelids, rapid breathing, persistent emaciation, and weight loss, ultimately dying from severe emaciation and dehydration. Surviving mice began to show symptom relief after 3 days. Mice in subgroup C4 showed milder clinical symptoms, including reduced appetite, depression, and decreased water intake, and began to recover their appetite and body weight after 2 days. Overall, mice challenged with this serovar KP exhibited clinical symptoms such as depression, loss of appetite, lethargy, and rapid breathing. Deceased mice showed significant weight loss, poor thirst response, and obvious purulent discharge from the eyelids and nasal cavity. Surviving mice had relatively minor weight loss, moderate or increased thirst, and symptoms alleviated within 2–3 days, followed by recovery of appetite.

Necropsy of deceased mice in the experimental groups (Fig. 3) revealed that, compared with the control group, the liver in both K20 and K54 groups exhibited severe congestion. The kidneys showed congestion and swelling, the lungs displayed hyperemia and congestion, and the spleen exhibited congestion and significant swelling. No obvious lesions were observed in the heart.

Inactivation condition screening results

Experimental results showed that the addition of formaldehyde solution at a final concentration of 0.3% to K20 and K54 for 24 h achieved complete inactivation. Therefore, a formaldehyde solution at a final concentration of 0.3% was selected as the inactivating agent for the preparation of the hvKP inactivated vaccine.

Results of vaccine safety testing in hvKP

After 7 days of immunization observation, it was found that the mice immunized with the antigen solution only exhibited mild loss of appetite on the day of injection and returned to normal by the second day post-immunization. No deaths, significant adverse reactions, or abnormal behaviors were observed. The mice maintained good mental status, normal dietary intake, smooth fur, and showed no signs of inflammation (such as redness or swelling), tissue necrosis, or poor absorption at the injection site. These results indicat that all three antigen solutions meet the safety requirements for vaccines.

Discussion

Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP) is an opportunistic enterobacterium that can exist in various environments, including water and soil. The pathogenesis of this pathogen is associated with the complexity of clinical infections in humans and animals, and conventional treatments are often ineffective in most cases [19]. Studies have shown that livestock carrying KP may serve as a source of pathogenic and multidrug-resistant KP strains for humans [20]. KP has strong survivability, can persist and spread in the environment, and has a wide distribution range. Researchers have isolated KP strains from various livestock, wild animals, the environment, and food. Recent surveillance studies indicate that the transmission rate and detection rate of KP continue to rise [21]. According to previous literature, the isolation rate of KP in Polish swine herds was reported to be 76.9%, with isolates predominantly obtained from the lower respiratory tract (lung) [22]. Similarly, KP was also commonly present in swine herds in the United Kingdom, with isolates likewise originating mainly from the lower respiratory tract (lung) [7]. In the present study, the isolation rate of KP in Tibetan pigs was 27.0%, substantially lower than that reported in Poland, with isolates primarily derived from the upper respiratory tract. All affected pigs exhibited clinical signs of respiratory disease, including abdominal breathing, nasal discharge, and purulent nasal secretions in piglets with diarrhoea. It is hypothesized that concurrent infection with other pathogens may compromise host immune competence, thereby facilitating opportunistic infection or exacerbation of subclinical KP carriage-findings consistent with the epidemiological and pathogenic characteristics of KP. Furthermore, the isolation rate from the lower respiratory tract (lung) was 5.5%, whereas that from the upper respiratory tract reached 51%. These results suggest that KP is present in the rearing environment. However, due to the relatively robust immune status of healthy Tibetan pigs, bacterial invasion is limited; even when colonization occurs, it typically does not lead to clinical disease. In contrast, diseased and deceased pigs exhibited a substantially higher probability of KP carriage.

KP has numerous serovars. Studies from both domestic and international sources indicate regional differences in the prevalence of hypervirulent serovars [23–25]. According to previous literature, common capsular serovars such as K1, K2, K5, K54, and K57 have been isolated across 27 provinces in China, with the K54 serovar exhibiting an isolation rate of 10.7%. In the present study, however, only two serovars-K20 and K54-were identified, with detection rates of 28% and 13%, respectively, which are slightly higher than those reported in the existing literature [26]. HvKP possesses various virulence factors, including fimbriae, capsular polysaccharides, lipopolysaccharides, and siderophores, which are encoded by virulence genes such as fimH, rmpA, magA, uge, wabG, iucA, iroB, and others [27], endowing KP with virulence and pathogenicity. According to relevant literature, rmpA2 and iucA are the predominant virulence genes in hvKP strains, both with detection rates exceeding 75% [28]. In the present study, however, entB and iucA were identified as the most prevalent virulence genes in hvKP strains, both exhibiting detection rates above 90%, followed by ycfM, fimH, and mrkD with a detection rate of 77%, and iroN and rmpA with a detection rate of 73%. The detection rates of fimH, mrkD, and rmpA2 were 45%, 68%, and 41%, respectively. Notably, rmpA is involved in the regulation of the capsular hypermucoviscous phenotype. In this study, the detection rate of rmpA was 41%, slightly higher than that reported in the literature [28]. Interestingly, studies on KP isolates from other regions of China have identified the siderophore synthesis gene iutA as a predominant virulence factor [29], a finding consistent with the results of the present study in Tibetan pigs. Both indicate that siderophore synthesis genes serve as major virulence determinants. However, the prevalence of siderophore synthesis genes in Tibetan pig isolates was substantially higher than that reported in isolates from other regions of China. According to previous literature [30], hvKP strains are generally resistant to a wide range of commonly used antibiotics, yet remain highly susceptible to apramycin (0%), ceftiofur (0%), gentamicin (0%), neomycin (0%), and spectinomycin (0%). In the present study, Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates from Tibetan pigs also exhibited extensive resistance to multiple routinely used antimicrobial agents; however, they showed high susceptibility to ceftazidime (0%), polymyxin B (0%), and gentamicin (0%)-a finding that is not entirely consistent with earlier reports [30]. HvKP exhibits high pathogenicity and lethality. In this experiment, the pathogenicity and lethality of K20 and K54 were assessed by calculating the LD50, observing clinical symptoms in challenged mice, and examining pathological tissue sections using HE staining. The experimental results showed that mice experienced acute or chronic death after challenge. Literature reports indicate that some cKP can cause disease in immunocompromised humans or animals, whereas hvKP can infect both healthy and immunocompromised individuals [31], leading to illness or even death. Previous studies employed a low-dose challenge model (1 × 105 CFU) to accentuate differences in virulence, whereas the present study adopted a high-dose lethal challenge model (K20: 4 × 108 CFU; K54: 1 × 108 CFU) to rigorously evaluate vaccine protective efficacy [32].

In recent years, KP has acquired resistance to multiple antibiotics, including carbapenems. Douradinha et al. reported that emerging carbapenem-resistant KP strains harboring virulence genes characteristic of hypervirulent isolates have been designated Hv-CR-Kp or CR-Hv-Kp and classified as super-virulent strains [33]. The escalating prevalence of multidrug resistance (MDR), together with the heightened pathogenicity of hypervirulent KP variants, poses a critical threat to global public health. Accordingly, the development of effective chemotherapeutics and vaccines against KP has become a research priority. Although various vaccine platforms—such as whole-cell, live attenuated, inactivated, and subunit vaccines—are currently available [34], the formulation of broadly protective vaccines against KP is severely challenged by its extensive capsular serovar diversity. Moreover, multidrug resistance and genetic complexity further complicate therapeutic intervention. At present, KP management remains heavily reliant on prevention, with antimicrobial therapy used adjunctively under strict rotation and short-course regimens to minimize resistance selection. Astragalus polysaccharides have attracted considerable interest as vaccine adjuvants due to their potent immunostimulatory activity and ease of extraction [35]. Tibetan astragalus (Astragalus spp.), a medicinal herb native to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau renowned for its immunomodulatory properties, serves as the source of the polysaccharide adjuvant evaluated in this study. To assess its capacity to enhance the immunogenicity of an inactivated KP vaccine, three experimental groups were established: mice immunized with inactivated vaccine alone, with inactivated vaccine formulated with Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide adjuvant, or with inactivated vaccine formulated with a conventional adjuvant (aluminum hydroxide). Vaccine-induced protection was comprehensively evaluated through a hierarchical set of endpoints, including serum antibody titers (humoral immunity), survival rates following lethal challenge (protective efficacy), bacterial re-isolation from infected tissues (pathogen clearance), and histopathological examination of target organs via HE staining (tissue protection). Notably, while this study systematically evaluated the protective efficacy of the inactivated vaccine using a mouse model, it did not conduct an in-depth investigation into the specific functions of key virulence genes (e.g., iucC, rmpA) in Tibetan pig-derived hvKP. Chen et al. [32] demonstrated that knockout of the iucC gene significantly attenuated the virulence of porcine K2-type hvKP, suggesting that this gene represents a potential target for the development of live attenuated vaccines. Future studies should integrate gene knockout technologies to further optimize vaccine antigen design at the functional level of virulence genes.

It should be noted that the immunization experimental design in this study did not include a control group receiving “Tibetan astragalus polysaccharide adjuvant alone.” Therefore, while the protective efficacy of the vaccine is primarily attributed to antigen-specific immune responses, the possibility that the adjuvant may contribute minimally to the protective outcome through transient, non-specific immunomodulatory effects during the challenge cannot be entirely excluded.

Our study provides the first functional evidence that a polysaccharide extract from Tibetan Astragalus (TAP) can act as an effective adjuvant for an inactivated K. pneumoniae vaccine, as demonstrated by the significant enhancement of antigen-specific antibody titers and complete protection in mice. The immunopotentiating effect of astragalus polysaccharide is a well-documented phenomenon in vaccinology. As a class of biological response modifiers, polysaccharides are generally understood to enhance immune responses through interactions with the innate immune system. They can serve as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and may be recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on antigen-presenting cells, leading to enhanced antigen presentation and cytokine production that promotes adaptive immunity [36–39]. A primary limitation of the present work is that it remains exploratory in nature regarding the adjuvant’s mechanism of action. The study was designed as a proof-of-concept to evaluate the functional outcome (enhanced immunogenicity and protection) of incorporating a locally sourced medicinal plant extract into a veterinary vaccine formulation. Consequently, we did not investigate the cellular or molecular mechanisms underlying the observed adjuvant effect—such as which immune cell populations are activated, what cytokine profiles are induced, or which specific receptor pathways are engaged by TAP. This absence of mechanistic data is an acknowledged limitation that highlights an essential direction for future research. Detailed mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate how TAP exerts its effects, which would be crucial for optimizing its use, understanding its safety profile, and potentially standardizing its application as a novel vaccine adjuvant.
