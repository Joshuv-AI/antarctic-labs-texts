Performance of the Dutch Pediatric Early Warning Score: a nationwide study in academic, large teaching and general hospital contexts, using real-world data
Authors: Jikke Stevens, Janke de Groot, Janneke Hoogervorst-Schilp, Michèl A. Willemsen, Joris Fuijkschot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, there has been a global increase in largely unvalidated Pediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS) systems. In 2019, the Dutch PEWS was developed to standardise practices within the Netherlands. This system incorporates (1) a vital function score component comprising a minimal coreset of vital parameters, (2) a risk sign component covering worried and watcher signs and (3) risk stratification. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of the Dutch PEWS and its individual components in detecting deterioration in hospitalised children across and between various hospital contexts.MethodsThis study is part of a national study that implements and evaluates the Dutch PEWS in hospitals with diverse contexts. Mean detection rates were calculated to assess whether clinical deterioration (defined by the endpoints ‘unplanned paediatric intensive care unit transfer’, ‘resuscitation’, ‘paediatric medical emergency team support’ and ‘advanced paediatric life support intervention’) was alarmed timely by the Dutch PEWS. For children who did not reach an endpoint the number of non-case alarms was used to assess full impact on children and healthcare providers.ResultsData from 12 hospitals, 20 708 admissions and 342 achieved endpoints (events) were available. The mean detection rate for the Dutch PEWS was 83.9%. The vital function score component demonstrated a mean detection rate of 47.6%, highest in large teaching hospitals and the risk sign component reached 70.6%, highest in university medical centres. Non-case alarms occurred in 24.4% of the admissions, mainly related to the (standard) use of watcher signs (23.4%) and not to vital function score alarms (6.7%).ConclusionThe Dutch PEWS demonstrated good overall performance in detecting deterioration, but performance differed across and between hospital contexts. Importantly, the contribution of the vital function score component to performance seemed limited, whereas the risk sign component appeared to make a substantial contribution, especially in complex hospital environments.

Introduction

Recognising deterioration in hospitalised children is complex due to age-dependent physiological variations, compensatory mechanisms and limited capacities to express emotions. Nevertheless, children who experience unexpected deterioration often exhibit observable warning signs before their condition is formally identified.1 2

Pediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS) systems have been developed to support the early recognition of clinical deterioration in children, with the ultimate goal of reducing morbidity and mortality.3 4 These systems typically consist of two core components: (1) a scoring system derived from repeated clinical measurements and (2) a response algorithm that guides interventions when abnormal scores are detected.5 Scoring generally incorporates age-adjusted thresholds for vital parameters such as heart rate and respiratory rate, supplemented by assessment of consciousness and concerns raised by healthcare providers or parents.4 6 7 More recently, so-called ‘watcher signs’ have been identified as potential contributors to PEWS systems, serving as indicators for children at risk of a deviant clinical course even in the absence of immediate deterioration signs.6

Globally, PEWS systems are now widely being implemented in paediatric hospital care, many of which remain unvalidated.5 8 Moreover, the majority of validation studies have been conducted in university medical centres (UMCs), raising questions about their generalisability to other hospital contexts.4 9 Evidence demonstrating improvements in hard clinical outcomes, such as mortality, remains limited.10 Consequently, research has expanded to evaluate broader outcomes, including resuscitation events and unplanned transfers to paediatric intensive care units (PICUs).1 4 10

In 2019, the Dutch PEWS was developed through a national consensus process to standardise practices across the Netherlands. The Dutch PEWS is a uniform scoring system consisting of two main components and risk stratification. The vital function component includes a PEWS score based on a minimal coreset of vital parameters (ABC within ABCD (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability) approach) along with the AVPU score (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) (D within ABCD approach). The vital parameters to be measured include work of breathing, respiratory rate, oxygen therapy, heart rate and capillary refill time, with oxygen saturation and blood pressure added when age-specific cut-off values are exceeded. The risk sign component incorporates ‘worried signs’ reported by healthcare providers or parents, and ‘watcher signs’ tailored to specific treatments or local risk factors. This component was designed for adaptability, enabling implementation and validation across diverse hospital contexts. Furthermore, children were classified into risk categories based on risk stratification, which guides interventions to monitor their clinical course to prevent deterioration.11

The study aimed to validate the Dutch PEWS across and between different hospital contexts in the Netherlands. The primary objective was to evaluate the overall performance of the Dutch PEWS in detecting clinical deterioration. The second objective was to examine the contribution of its individual components—the vital function score component and the risk sign component—in recognising deterioration.

Methods

Study design and context

As part of a nationwide, practice-oriented project, this multicentre study prospectively evaluated the performance of the Dutch PEWS across 12 hospitals with diverse contexts in the Netherlands, using real-world clinical data. The study was conducted from December 2020 to June 2024, whereby each hospital was followed for 1 year. The detailed study protocol has previously been described.12

Setting and participants

The 12 hospitals participating in the study were selected through block randomisation and represent all hospital contexts in the Netherlands: UMCs including the National Pediatric Oncology Center (n=3), large teaching hospitals (n=5) and general hospitals (n=4). The population was heterogeneous, comprising all children aged from 0 to 18 years (including neonates with a gestational age >37 weeks) admitted to either a general paediatric ward (thus excluding (neonatal/paediatric) intensive/high care units) or a specialised paediatric oncology ward. The eligibility criteria are outlined in the study protocol.12

Implementation

In the Netherlands, the predominant electronic health record systems utilised within hospitals are Epic EHR (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, Wisconsin) and HiX (ChipSoft, Amsterdam, Netherlands). The Dutch PEWS was fully integrated into these health records prior to the start of this study. The participating hospitals used four different records: Epic EHR (n=2), HiX standard content (n=4), HiX non-standard content (n=4) and custom-developed records (n=2). Additionally, local adaptation within the Dutch PEWS led 10 hospitals to define context-specific risk treatments and local risk factors beyond the standard set, while two hospitals retained the standard configuration. The number of additional treatments and factors ranged from 3 to 11 per hospital, and a complete overview is presented in online supplemental appendix 1.13

Context of Dutch hospitals

Only UMCs are equipped with a PICU and a paediatric medical emergency team (PMET), thereby emphasising the disparities in hospital infrastructure. This necessitates the transfer of severely ill children requiring specialised care from other hospital contexts, with estimated ambulance travel times to the nearest PICU ranging from 15 minutes to 1 hour and 15 minutes, depending on the hospital’s location and prevailing traffic conditions.

Endpoints and outcome measurements

Four endpoints were defined and these are listed in table 1. Three of which correspond to traditional endpoints commonly used in PEWS validation studies, and one represents an additional endpoint. The additional endpoint concerns Advanced Pediatric Life Support (APLS) interventions and was added to gain a broader understanding of performance, especially in large teaching and general hospitals. These APLS interventions were expected to occur more frequently than other endpoints in these contexts, reflecting early detection of clinical deterioration, which allows more time to respond and stabilise the child’s condition.

Analyses were conducted for cases (admissions reaching an endpoint) and non-cases (admissions not reaching an endpoint). The case analyses were performed to determine whether deterioration was detected before reaching traditional and all endpoints combined. When an actual endpoint was reached, it was defined as an event. To help assess the full impact of the Dutch PEWS on paediatric hospital care, the occurrence of (false) alarms in non-cases was analysed. The study’s outcome measures are detailed in box 1.

Outcome measurements

Primary outcome measure

The overall performance of the Dutch Pediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS) was evaluated by examining cases and non-cases. Performance was assessed using two approaches: (1) the mean detection rates of clinical deterioration in children within the 8 to 1-hour timeframe prior to endpoint occurrence (traditional and combined endpoints) in case admissions and (2) the presence of (false) alarms in non-case admissions. Both approaches were analysed across and between different hospital contexts.

Secondary outcome measure

The contribution of the individual components in the Dutch PEWS—the vital function score component (vital parameters and AVPU score) and the risk sign component (worried and watcher signs)—was assessed by (1) calculating the mean detection rates of clinical deterioration in children within the 8 to 1-hour timeframe prior to endpoints (traditional and combined endpoints) in case admissions and (2) the presence of (false) alarms in non-case admissions. Both were analysed across and between different hospital contexts.

Data collection

Patient characteristics, including hospital name, admission duration, gender and Dutch PEWS age group, were recorded by the participating hospitals. For patients admitted and discharged on the same day, the admission duration was counted as 0.5 days. The following age groups were used: 0–3 months, ≥ 3–12 months, ≥ 1–4 years, ≥ 4–12 years and ≥12 years.

During the hospital’s assigned study period (12 months), nurses collected real-time vital function scores (vital parameters and AVPU scores) and corresponding risk stratification at least once per shift, with a minimum of three times per day, as part of routine care. Risk signs (worried and watcher signs) were recorded at least once daily.

At the end of the study period, each hospital submitted a mandatory baseline dataset and provided additional data where necessary. The baseline dataset included the following: (1) patient characteristics, (2) vital function score component, (3) risk sign component, (4) risk stratification into standard-risk, medium-risk or high-risk categories and (5) achieved endpoints. Variables 1 to 4 were automatically extracted from electronic health record systems. Endpoints (as shown in table 1) were collected using standardised registration forms and submitted manually. In cases of ambiguity or missing data, the research group requested additional data.12

The research group established a multicentre database in Castor, a cloud-based clinical data management platform, ensuring secure data capture and integration.14 Each hospital imported baseline and additional data into a dedicated Castor environment.

An algorithm, developed prior to the commencement of the study and detailed in the study protocol, was employed to ascertain which endpoints were eligible for inclusion in the analysis.12

Data analysis

A biostatistician contributed to the design of the datasets and advised on statistical analysis to ensure methodological rigour. Additionally, a central data manager was responsible for data storage and quality control of the analyses performed. Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Statistics V.29).15

Patient characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics. Normally distributed data were analysed with parametric tests and reported as means with SD. Non-normally distributed data were analysed with non-parametric tests and reported as medians with IQRs. Categorical data were summarised as counts and percentages.

A predetermined, although arbitrary, criterion was used to establish the clinical utility of the Dutch PEWS and its individual components. Based on validation studies, this criterion for clinical utility was met when the calculated mean detection rates exceeded 60%.1 4 16 17

Since this study was conducted in a real-world clinical context, we expected healthcare providers to intervene with children who had alarms. Therefore, alarms (particularly those related to vital parameters and worried signs) that did not result in an endpoint (non-cases) could not be registered as false positives. These alarms may represent true positives whose progression to an endpoint was prevented by timely and appropriate interventions by healthcare providers. Consequently, sensitivity and specificity were not calculated.

Cases

Mean detection rates were calculated and expressed as frequencies. For each case admission, we assessed whether an alarm was triggered by the Dutch PEWS (within 8 to 1 hour before the endpoint was reached) and identified the specific component responsible: the vital function score component, the risk sign component, or both. Normal values were defined as alert consciousness, PEWS <7 and the absence of worried and watcher signs. An alarm was characterised by impaired consciousness (verbal, pain, unresponsive), PEWS ≥7 or the presence of ≥1 worried or watcher sign. Detection rates were analysed in various hospital contexts. Results were weighted by the number of endpoints per hospital, with adjustments for missing or incomplete data. Mean detection rates were reported as percentages, classified as alarm before endpoint, no alarm before endpoint or missing data. For cases with an alarm, the time interval between alarm onset and the endpoint was also assessed. These intervals are presented as medians with IQR due to non-normally distributed data.

Non-cases

We calculated and expressed the number of non-case alarms as frequencies among all admissions for this subgroup. For each non-case admission, we assessed whether there were no alarms, one alarm or multiple alarms, and identified the responsible component. Non-case alarms were reported as percentages.

No sample size calculation was performed, as we anticipated that a sufficiently large number of children would be included.

Hypothesis

We proposed that the performance of the Dutch PEWS and the contribution of the individual components would differ between hospital contexts, with a more prominent role for risk signs in complex hospital contexts.

Patients and public involvement

Patients and the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans for this study.

Reporting guidelines

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines; the corresponding checklist is included as online supplemental supplement 1.

Traditional endpoints

Primary outcome measure

The overall performance of the Dutch PEWS showed a mean detection rate of 83.9% (range 33.3%–100%). Detection rates varied across hospital contexts, ranging from 58.8% to 87.4%. The median time to alarm initiation was 7:08:00 hours (IQR 3:45:45–8:00:00).

Secondary outcome measure

When comparing the individual components, the risk sign component was the leading contributor overall, with a mean detection rate of 70.6% (range 25%–100%), compared with the vital function score component, which had a rate of 47.6% (range 0%–100%). The risk sign component also triggered alarms earlier, with a median of 7:30:00 (IQR 3:40:00–8:00:00), compared with 4:30:00 hours (IQR 2:15:00–7:05:00) for the vital function score component.

Substantial variation was observed across hospital contexts. In general hospitals, the risk sign component (mean 47.1%) and the worried sign element (mean 35.3%) contributed most to alarm generation. In UMCs, the risk sign component (mean 85.1%) remained the highest contributor, but the watcher sign element made a larger contribution (mean 69%). In large teaching hospitals, the vital function score component (mean 76.9%) and the vital parameter element (mean 74.4%) had the greatest contribution.

All endpoints combined

Primary outcome measure

The overall performance of the Dutch PEWS demonstrated a mean detection rate of 68.1% (range 6.7%–92.9%). Detection rates differed between hospital contexts, ranging from 36.8% to 78.1%. The median time to alarm was 7:06:00 hours (IQR 3:50:30–8:00:00).

Secondary outcome measure

When comparing the individual components, the risk component had the highest mean detection rate at 58.2% (range 0%–92.9%) vers 31.3% (range 0%–65%) for the vital function score. The risk sign component also generated alarms earlier, with a median of 7:50:00 hours (IQR: 3:53:00–8:00:00), compared with 4:15:00 hours (IQR 2:00:00–6:45:00) for the vital function score component.

Substantial variation was again observed across hospital contexts for all endpoints combined. In general hospitals, the risk sign component (mean 28.9%) and the worried sign element (mean 21.1%) contributed most frequently to alarms. In UMCs, the risk sign component remained the strongest contributor (mean 75.6%), with the watcher signs most commonly responsible (mean 66.2%). In large teaching hospitals, the vital function score component (mean 44.7%) and specifically the vital parameter element (mean 40.8%) had the greatest impact.

Primary outcome measure

Overall, the Dutch PEWS showed no alarms in 75.6% of non-case admissions (range 29.1%–97%), one alarm in 8.8% (range 2.4%–21.9%) and multiple alarms in 15.7% (range 0.5%–61.7%). Variation in non-case alarms was observed between hospital contexts, with the greatest number of alarms triggered in more complex hospitals.

Secondary outcome measure

When comparing the individual components, the risk sign component generated the most alarms across all hospital contexts (7.7% for one alarm, 13.9% for multiple alarms), while the vital function score component generated fewer alarms (2.1% for one alarm, 4.6% for multiple alarms).

Of the individual elements, vital parameters, AVPU and worried signs triggered the fewest alarms. Watcher signs triggered the most. Within each element, differences across hospital contexts were generally minimal. However, an exception was observed for watcher signs, which triggered more alarms in complex hospitals.

Discussion

Interpretation of the results

This study demonstrates differences in the performance of the Dutch PEWS and its individual components in the context of alarm generation in hospitalised children. Overall, the findings provide evidence supporting the contribution of the Dutch PEWS, particularly in recognising more severely ill children. The highest mean detection rates and narrowest variabilities were observed for traditional endpoints.

Overall performance of the Dutch PEWS

For traditional endpoints, the total mean detection rate of the Dutch PEWS was 83.9% (range 33.3%–100%), surpassing the preset criterion of 60%, which indicates good practical usability. The median time to alarm initiation was approximately 7 hours before an endpoint (IQR ~4–8 hours). This suggests that the Dutch PEWS contributes to early detection of deterioration. Additionally, despite differences in study design, our mean detection rate was often more favourable than sensitivities reported in previous validation studies (Parshuram et al 2011: 64%; Lambert et al 2017: ~69%, range 37%–99%).1 16

However, table 4 clearly indicates that in two general hospitals (no. 10 and 11), the performance of the Dutch PEWS was much lower than average. When considering implementation issues within the general hospital context, our previous study depicted good implementation for hospital no. 11 and below-average implementation for hospitals no. 9 and 10. This weakens the case that the low performance is related to implementation problems. Contexts (case mix of patients, hospital infrastructure, staffing as well as distance to the PICU) showed no relevant differences. It therefore remains unclear at this point why performance was lower in these two hospitals.

Performance of the individual components

The individual components seemed to contribute differently to the performance of the Dutch PEWS, which is consistent with our hypothesis. The vital function score component appears to offer a small contribution across all endpoints. The AVPU (D in the ABCD approach) demonstrated no clinically relevant contribution, whereas the vital parameters (ABC in the ABCD approach) contributed to a limited extent. When focusing solely on vital parameters, large teaching hospitals exhibited a markedly higher mean detection rate for traditional endpoints than other hospital contexts at 76.9% (range 57.1%–100%). This finding is noteworthy, but its underlying explanation remains unclear. We hypothesise that this could point to transferring patients later to the PICU. General hospitals lack a PICU, which may result in timely transfers of higher risk patients, whereas UMCs have an on-site PICU, facilitating low-threshold transfers of these risk patients at an earlier stage. Although large teaching hospitals similarly lack a PICU, the greater availability of paediatric subspecialists and diagnostic and therapeutic resources may enable slightly longer treatment periods before PICU transfer compared with general hospitals.

Detection of deterioration via the risk sign component seems attributed to both worried signs and watcher signs. With a detection rate using traditional endpoints of 70.6% (range 25%–100%), the risk sign component appears to substantially contribute to the performance of the Dutch PEWS. When focusing on hospital contexts, data show that UMCs contributed to a large extent to this detection rate. This suggests that risk signs may be particularly valuable in complex hospital contexts with more severely ill children.

Non-case alarms

Non-case alarms occurred in 24.4% of the admissions, mainly related to the use of watcher signs (23.4%), especially in complex hospital contexts (UMCs), rather than vital function score component (6.7%). Watcher signs were used as standard operating procedures (eg, in transfers from PICU to ward) to improve situational awareness in some of the hospitals, mostly UMCs. This may explain the high share of watcher signs and increased non-case alarms. This could indicate a broader-than-intended use of the system rather than poorer performance in detecting clinical deterioration.

Taken together, the findings suggest that the Dutch PEWS achieves high mean detection rates without generating an excessive number of (false) positive non-case alarms, thereby avoiding a substantial increase in nurses’ workload and alarm fatigue. These results support the positive impact of the Dutch PEWS on paediatric care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has notable strengths and limitations. Initially, our study extended beyond UMCs and included large teaching and general hospital contexts. In addition, we included an additional endpoint alongside commonly used traditional endpoints to broaden insights into the performance of the Dutch PEWS in large teaching and general hospitals, as we expected this additional endpoint to occur more frequently in these contexts.9 16 17

Notably, we used real-world clinical data, thereby enhancing the external validity of the study.18 19 This enabled us to cover a comprehensive nationwide scope with data from 12 hospitals across diverse contexts, 20 708 admissions, over 130 000 Dutch PEWS forms, and more than 300 events. To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide multicentre study using real-world clinical data to examine the performance of a new PEWS system and its individual components across hospital contexts. Although the benefits of using real-world data are clear, it also raises some challenges that researchers need to acknowledge.

The first challenge we encountered with this type of data collection was that non-cases might have been captured less consistently than cases, which could create an imbalance and unconscious bias.

Second, we acknowledge the substantial number of excluded events (~56.8% of events), which occurred due to non-compliance with the inclusion criteria (phase I; ~77.3% of exclusions) and missing registrations (phase II; ~22.7% of exclusions). The exclusions in phase I were related to events outside the scope of the study, and we anticipate that these exclusions had minimal impact on the overall study results. Phase II exclusions mainly resulted from missing registrations in electronic health records, which is common in studies using real-world data. To mitigate this issue, we automated most of the data import process. However, events still had to be tracked and reported manually.20 Consequently, we expect that fewer events were recorded, which may have introduced reporting bias and underreporting. To minimise this, we provided standardised registration forms and requested missing data from the hospitals. Future technological developments in extracting (event) data from electronic health records could optimise this process and reduce the risk of bias.

Finally, statistical calculations were difficult to perform because a multivariable model could not be constructed using real-world clinical data. This resulted in an inability to assess the independent contributions of the components and achieve statistical rigour. Therefore, this study alone should not be considered as a statistical validation of the system.

Future directions

Despite the good overall performance of the Dutch PEWS, this study urges reflection on the differences found in performance of different components and across hospital contexts. It appears that performance relates to hospital complexity, and general hospitals seem to benefit from the system to a lesser extent. Adaptations to uniform usage of the system could be not only needed but also depend on healthcare providers’ perceptions of its added value, because systems such as the Dutch PEWS often have effects beyond detecting deterioration. Previous studies have reported perceived benefits in communication and situational awareness.21 22 Different perspectives are needed to fully understand how these systems actually contribute to high-quality hospital care. Additionally, following the Dutch principle of ‘appropriate care’, data from this study depict opportunities to minimise unnecessary vital parameter measurements (such as the AVPU) to further decrease impact on nurses’ workload and improve patient comfort.23

Before proposing modifications to the current Dutch PEWS, insights into its performance should also be considered alongside healthcare providers’ perceptions of its added value to assess full impact. These perceptions are currently being analysed and will be reported in a later phase.

Conclusion

Using a nationwide study approach, the Dutch PEWS demonstrated a good overall performance in detecting deterioration in hospitalised children, particularly in larger, high-complexity hospitals. The risk sign component was the primary contributor to performance in UMCs and the vital function score component in large teaching hospitals. Smaller general hospitals seem to benefit least from the Dutch PEWS. Different perspectives are needed to fully understand the ways these systems actually contribute to high-quality hospital care.
