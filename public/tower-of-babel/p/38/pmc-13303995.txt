Radiopharmaceutical Therapy in Oncology: Perspectives from Transatlantic Experts
Authors: Désirée Deandreis, Heather Jacene, Patrick Therasse, Fabrice André, Fabrice Barlesi, Kevin Haigis, Karim Fizazi, Toni K Choueiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT), the administration of a radioactive element coupled with a targeting vector, is one of the most promising innovations in oncology, supported by clinical trials. A theranostic approach—the pairing of diagnostic and therapeutic RPTs sharing the same target—facilitates selective delivery of therapeutic radiation to cancer cells. Its advantages and limitations are determined by the molecular targeting mechanisms employed, as well as the types of ionising radiation emitted. Therefore, understanding the biology of cell-surface targets and molecular mechanisms governing target expression in cancers is critical to the development and clinical use of these agents. This review provides an overview of the latest advances in the application of targeted RPTs within oncology, as discussed at the Fourth Transatlantic Exchange in Oncology, a hybrid conference held in March 2025 that brought together experts from Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (Boston, MA, USA) and Gustave Roussy (Paris, France). Key topics included the targeting of prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) for both imaging and therapy purposes in prostate cancer. In particular, PSMA-based imaging—encompassing both positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)—has become a fundamental tool for patient selection, evaluation of treatment response, and personalisation of therapeutic strategies. While theranostic approaches continue to pose challenges, they should increasingly enable improved treatment selection for patients with cancer, and more effective prediction of response and toxicity. Lessons learned from PSMA may apply to other emerging theranostic targets in prostate cancer and other tumour types, expanding the future potential of RPT applications. Beyond current achievements, new molecules and intensive translational research programs may optimise, potentiate and direct RPT. The impact of the expanding use of RPTs on healthcare systems was also addressed, defining strategies to overcome barriers and provide broader access to innovations in RPT in both clinical and research settings.Keywords: Alpha emitter, Beta emitter, Imaging, Oncology, Radiolabelled molecular therapy, Radiopharmaceutical therapy, Targeted therapy, Theranostic

Key Summary Points

Radiopharmaceutical therapy involves the administration of an unstable nuclide, coupled with a targeting vector, to selectively deliver therapeutic radiation to cancer cells, while theranostics refers to the pairing of diagnostic and therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals that have the same target.In patients with cancer, understanding the molecular heterogeneity and biology that underlies cell surface target expression and positron emission tomography (PET) findings has important therapeutic implications.Lessons learned from studying prostate-specific membrane antigen regulation and PET imaging may apply to other emerging theranostic targets in prostate cancer and beyond.A theranostic approach is expected to progressively allow better selection of treatment for patients with cancer and improve the prediction of response and toxicity, although challenges remain.While an enormous global opportunity exists to expand the number of patients with cancer who can benefit from radiopharmaceutical therapies, many healthcare systems will need to overcome major barriers to enable access to all patients.

Introduction

Radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT), based on the administration of a radioactive element coupled with a targeting vector, has emerged as one of the most promising innovations in oncology, enabling the selective delivery of therapeutic radiation to cancer cells through a theranostic approach [1, 2]. Theranostics is a concept that pairs diagnostic and therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals that have the same target. Imaging is performed first with a diagnostic radiopharmaceutical to determine if the target of interest is sufficiently present to proceed with the therapeutic radiopharmaceutical. A key advantage of this approach is the ability to perform a real-time, whole-body survey of the tumour to assess the presence or absence of a given target’s expression [2, 3]. The benefits and limitations of the therapeutic agents are nevertheless determined by many factors including the molecular targeting mechanisms and types of ionising radiation emitted by the selected radiopharmaceuticals [1, 2].

Structurally, the radiopharmaceuticals used in theranostic pairs all have the same general structure, incorporating a targeting vector, linker (or chelator) and radionuclide (Fig. 1) [4]. The targeting vector may be a peptide, small molecule or antibody that binds a specific molecular target, such as (more frequently) a cell-surface receptor expressed by a specific tumour type [3]. The diagnostic component of the theranostic pair includes in most cases positron-emitting radionuclide for imaging or gamma radionuclide for imaging with positron emission tomography (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), respectively. In contrast, the therapeutic component typically incorporates a beta (β)- or alpha (α)-emitter, which induces a cytotoxic effect upon the specific tumour cell (target) or nearby cells via a bystander effect. Of note, some therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals also emit gamma (γ) rays, enabling post-treatment confirmation of the drug’s biodistribution and determination of the radiation dose to the tumour and normal organs (referred to as dosimetry). Thus, the use of theranostics enables a personalized diagnostic and therapeutic approach for patients.

Although not a new concept in nuclear medicine [5, 6], theranostics has received significantly more attention and growth in recent years. This renewed interest has largely been driven by phase 3 clinical studies showing survival benefit compared with standard of care in neuroendocrine tumours [7, 8] and metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) [9, 10]. In particular, mCRPC affects a large population of patients and remains an incurable and fatal disease [11], for which additional treatment options are needed [12]. Given that mCRPC expresses high levels of the prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), RPT research has focused on PSMA as a target to improve survival outcomes first in heavily pre-treated patients and subsequently in earlier stages of the disease. Beyond PSMA and prostate cancer, numerous other roadmaps for the further development of RPTs are actively being explored. The Fourth Transatlantic Exchange in Oncology, a hybrid conference that brought together key experts from the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute in Boston, MA, USA and Gustave Roussy in Paris, France, took place on 19 March 2025. The aim of this conference was to discuss the state of the art and latest advances in the application of targeted RPTs within oncology, with a focus on PSMA and new directions for the future of theranostics. This paper provides an overview of the key topics discussed during the conference, merging expertise and perspectives from Europe and the USA, including the anticipated impact of the expanding use of radiopharmaceuticals on healthcare systems.

This paper is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Targeting PSMA in Prostate Cancer: Only the Beginning

Modulation of the PSMA regulatory network involves a PSMA transcription complex, an upstream enhancer that loops to the FOLH1 (PSMA) gene promoter, intergenic enhancers, and differentially methylated regions [12]. PSMA has become an important cell-surface imaging biomarker and therapeutic target in prostate cancer—its expression is dynamic, heterogeneous and differentially regulated across metastatic lesions, which may inform selection for PSMA-targeted therapies and development of complementary biomarkers [13]. The absence or presence of androgen receptor-positive disease also influences PSMA expression. Our knowledge of the mechanisms underlying PSMA expression and suppression continue to evolve [12].

Lutetium-177 (177Lu)-PSMA-617 (177Lu-PSMA) is a radiopharmaceutical therapy that delivers β-radiation to PSMA-expressing cells and the surrounding microenvironment [9]. 177Lu is a medium-energy, β-minus emitter with a physical half-life of 6.7 days, making it ideal for therapeutic applications requiring sustained radiation delivery. The approval of 177Lu-PSMA-617 in patients with mCRPC and PSMA-positive disease on PET who had previously received taxane chemotherapy and an androgen receptor inhibitor was based on the findings of the phase 3 VISION study. In this study, patients were randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to receive either 177Lu-PSMA (7.4 GBq every 6 weeks for four to six cycles) plus protocol-permitted standard care or standard care alone. The study showed that 177Lu-PSMA plus standard care was associated with prolonged overall survival (OS; median, 15.3 vs. 11.3 months; hazard ratio [HR] for death 0.62; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.52, 0.74; p < 0.001) and radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS; median, 8.7 vs. 3.4 months; HR 0.40; 99.2% CI 0.29, 0.57; p < 0.001) [9].

Other studies with different designs and/or in specific settings have also demonstrated the efficacy of 177Lu-PSMA in patients with mCRPC. The phase 2 TheraP study involved patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC who had previously received docetaxel. Patient were administered 177Lu-PSMA (every 6 weeks for a maximum of six cycles) or chemotherapy (cabazitaxel, 20 mg/m2 every 3 weeks for a maximum of ten cycles), respectively. The inclusion criteria for TheraP were more stringent than in VISION, requiring a higher level of overall PSMA expression and no discordant lesions on PET/computed tomography (CT) using 2-[18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose (FDG). Among the 291 patients screened, only 68.7% met the criteria for enrolment based on PSMA and FDG-PET/CT. TheraP showed improved prostate-specific antigen (PSA) responses and delayed disease progression with 177Lu-PSMA versus cabazitaxel, although OS was similar between the two cohorts [14]; after a median follow-up of 35.7 months, OS was 19.1 months (95% CI 16.9, 21.4) with 177Lu-PSMA versus 19.6 months (17.4, 21.8) with cabazitaxel.

Although the VISION and TheraP studies used different PET-based criteria for patient selection for treatment with 177Lu-PSMA, possible associations between the TheraP eligibility criteria and subgroups and the treatment outcomes of patients who were deemed suitable and treated based on VISION criteria have been evaluated [15]. In patients who were ‘VISION-eligible’ and treated with 177Lu-PSMA, but ineligible for TheraP with discordant findings had lower values for PSA decrease > 50% (PSA50), PSA-PFS and OS; this suggested that the shorter OS identified in those patients ineligible for TheraP was mainly driven by the presence of discordant disease.

Given its benefit in later-stage mCRPC, 177Lu-PSMA has been and is currently being evaluated in earlier lines of therapy. The phase 3 PSMAfore study demonstrated that in taxane-naïve patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC who had progressed once on a previous androgen receptor pathway inhibitor (ARPI), 177Lu-PSMA prolonged rPFS compared with the use of an alternative ARPI [10]; median rPFS was 11.60 months (95% CI 9.30, 14.19) with 177Lu-PSMA versus 5.59 months (4.21, 5.95) with ARPI change (HR 0.49; 95% CI 0.39, 0.61). Thus, 177Lu-PSMA is an effective treatment alternative for those patients who are being considered for a different treatment following progression on an ARPI and before taxanes. These results led to the approval of 177Lu-PSMA for mCRPC in the pre-chemotherapy setting in the USA. The phase 2 UpFrontPSMA study showed that the subsequential strategy of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 followed 6 weeks later by docetaxel achieved a better response compared with docetaxel alone for the treatment of chemotherapy-naïve patients with high PSMA-expressing mCRPC [16]. The primary endpoint was undetectable PSA (≤ 0.2 ng/mL) at 48 weeks; at 48 weeks, 41% (95% CI 30, 54) of patients receiving [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 versus 16% (95% CI 9, 28) receiving docetaxel alone had undetectable PSA (OR 3.88, 95% CI 1.61, 9.38; p = 0.0020).

Other studies are ongoing to evaluate the efficacy of 177Lu-PSMA in the metastatic hormone-sensitive disease setting. PSMAddition (NCT04720157) is an international, multicentre, open-label, randomised, prospective phase 3 study comparing 177Lu-PSMA in combination with standard of care versus standard of care alone in adult male patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC). In PSMAddition, combining 177Lu-PSMA with androgen deprivation therapy and ARPI significantly improved rPFS (HR 0.72; p = 0.002) [17]; safety findings were also consistent with the known profile and quality of life was not adversely affected. The PEACE6-Poor Responders study (NCT06496581) is an ongoing European international, multicentre, open-label, controlled, randomised phase 3 trial supported by the French Urogenital Tumor Study Group. It aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of 177Lu-PSMA when administered in addition to ongoing standard systemic treatment compared with standard systemic treatment alone. Enrolled patients have de novo mHSPC without a satisfactory response at 6–8 months following systemic treatment initiation for mHSPC (i.e. poor responders) in the absence of cancer progression. The PEACE6-Poor Responders study is being conducted at Gustave Roussy, while VISION, PSMAFORE, and PSMAddition studies were undertaken at both Gustave Roussy and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute.

While prospective data have demonstrated favourable efficacy and safety of radiopharmaceutical therapy with 177Lu-PSMA for up to six cycles in patients with mCRPC [9, 10, 14], retrospective analysis and real-life data have confirmed that extended therapy beyond this continues to be well tolerated and is not associated with an increased incidence of grade 3/4 toxicity [18]; preservation of efficacy was also demonstrated with 177Lu-PSMA rechallenge following a treatment break.

Seeing Beyond VISION: Improving Selection for a Better Survival Benefit with Radiopharmaceutical Therapy

While phase 3 studies of 177Lu-PSMA, such as VISION, demonstrate OS benefit in patients with mCRPC [9], there is ongoing debate regarding whether the design of these studies may limit achievable levels of survival with radiopharmaceutical therapy. An exploratory secondary analysis of the VISION study identified baseline 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT whole-body tumour mean standardised uptake value (SUVmean) as the best predictor of 177Lu-PSMA efficacy (based on rPFS and OS). This suggests that use of modified eligibility criteria could improve the efficiency of the primary survival endpoint in the study [19]; a 1-unit whole-body tumour SUVmean increase was associated with a 12% and 10% decrease in risk of rPFS and death, respectively.

Multivariable models of outcomes with 177Lu-PSMA have been developed using data from the VISION study and have demonstrated that a combination of pretreatment laboratory, clinical and imaging parameters influences survival outcomes [20]. Shorter-term outcomes (i.e. PSA50) may be primarily influenced by PSMA expression, indicated by measures such as SUVmean. As previously discussed, a biomarker study within TheraP showed higher total tumour SUVmean was predictive of higher likelihood of response to 177Lu-PSMA, but not cabazitaxel [21]; higher FDG metabolic tumour volume (MTV) was associated with a worse prognosis regardless of assigned treatment. Further investigation may enable MTV to be used to guide treatment intensification. Such information could be used to design future clinical trials to guide treatment optimization studies, enrich the study population with patients most likely to respond to treatment, and minimize drug exposure in those individuals deemed unlikely to benefit, improving efficiency of survival endpoints with 177Lu-PSMA.

Insights from the models developed by Herrmann et al. (2024) may also support clinical decision-making and physician–patient communications regarding treatment expectations with radiopharmaceutical therapies [20]. Monitoring Response with Radiopharmaceutical Therapy in CRPC

Recommendations from the Prostate Cancer Clinical Trials Working Group 3 for monitoring patients with CRPC focus on the assessment of blood-based markers, such as PSA every 3 or 4 weeks, along with imaging (bone scans and CT/magnetic resonance imaging) every 8 to 9 weeks for the first 24 weeks, then every 12 weeks [22]. However, there is a lack of consensus regarding how best to optimally monitor those individuals with CRPC who receive radiopharmaceutical therapies, with significant heterogeneity within clinical practice. Both registration studies for 177Lu-PSMA (VISION and PSMAfore) used CT/bone scans to evaluate treatment response, with no PSMA imaging performed during therapy [9, 10]; of note, the primary endpoint of PSMAfore was based on rPFS. However, conventional imaging has significant limitations for post-radiopharmaceutical therapy response evaluation. Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST) criteria, originally designed for chemotherapy assessment, rely on measuring soft tissue lesions and require a 30% decrease in the sum of target lesion diameters to define partial response. However, prostate cancer metastases are predominantly osteoblastic bone lesions that are classed as non-measurable disease by RECIST version 1.1. Additionally, radiopharmaceutical therapy can induce transient increases in bone turnover, potentially leading to apparent ‘progression’ on bone scans, rather than true disease progression, due to increased tracer uptake—a phenomenon known as the ‘flare response’ [23]. Conventional bone scintigraphy lacks specificity and cannot distinguish between treatment-induced bone remodelling, sclerosis and actual disease progression.

The role of PSMA-PET/CT and post-therapy SPECT/CT has been extensively discussed. Monitoring with PSMA-PET/CT is arguably the most ‘natural’ method to evaluate post-radiopharmaceutical therapy response given that all patients with mCRPC have baseline PSMA-PET/CT and any changes identified via PET are prognostic for survival. Furthermore, response on PMSA-PET/CT can precede morphological changes by weeks. Gafita et al. (2022) demonstrated that the use of PSMA-PET-specific criteria to evaluate early response in men with mCRPC treated with 177Lu-PSMA achieved higher prognostic values compared with conventional CT assessment or criteria adapted to PSMA-PET/CT from other imaging modalities [24]; PSMA-PET-specific criteria also achieved a high level of inter-reader reliability. The use of PSMA-PET/CT monitoring can also enable adaptive dosing [25]. While the open-label phase 2 ENZA-P study demonstrated that the addition of 177Lu-PSMA enhanced anticancer activity in patients with mCRPC and risk factors for early progression on enzalutamide, the use of monitoring with PSMA-PET/CT enabled adaptive dosing, leading to 11% of patients stopping after the second cycle of treatment [25]. However, there are several caveats that accompany the use of PSMA-PET/CT, including a lack of clear consensus on what constitutes progression or response, and the cost/availability of PET/CT scans. Several proposed criteria have been published, including Response Evaluation Criteria in PSMA-PET/CT (RECIP 1.0) [26], PSMA PET Progression Criteria [27] and PROMISE V2 [28]. Biomarker analysis from the TheraP study highlighted that PSMA-PET SUVmean values were predictive of a higher likelihood of favourable response to 177Lu-PSMA than cabazitaxel, potentially providing guidance for optimal 177Lu-PSMA use in patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC [21]. In addition, high MTV on FDG-PET/CT was associated with a worse prognosis regardless of assigned treatment; further investigation may enable MTV to be used to guide treatment intensification.

Increasing PSMA total tumour volume, evaluated via post-177Lu-PSMA therapy SPECT/CT, has been identified as a predictor of short-term PFS in patients with progressive mCRPC, suggesting it may have a potential future role as an imaging response biomarker [29]; physicians may also potentially be able to use it to identify when to cease or intensify 177Lu-PSMA therapy in individual patients. A retrospective study by Yadav et al. (2024) demonstrated that approximately half of all patients experienced changes in management based on post-177Lu-PSMA therapy SPECT/CT, typically at cycles 2 and 4, suggesting that post-treatment SPECT/CT can support appropriate patient management [30]. SPECT/CT also allows the development of both early and interim response criteria that can help optimally personalize treatments, given that it can be undertaken after each cycle of radiopharmaceutical therapy [31]. While undertaking multiple-time-point PSMA-PET/CT imaging in patients on treatment is typically not feasible at most institutions, SPECT/CT requires no additional radiopharmaceutical injections, may be performed on widely available SPECT/CT-capable γ-cameras, and may be undertaken at serial time points within a few hours after each theranostic dose [30, 31]. Furthermore, new-generation digital γ-cameras (cadmium-zinc-telluride-based technology) are becoming available with the possibility of further optimization and implementation of quantitative approaches.

While on- and post-treatment changes in SPECT-PET appear to be prognostic, there is currently no clear consensus on what constitutes response or progression, and optimal timing for the use of PSMA-SPECT following radiopharmaceutical therapy has also yet to be determined. The use of molecularly based biomarkers such as circulating tumour DNA has the potential to refine patient selection and optimize response evaluation when used in combination with imaging [32–34].

Primary Resistance to 177Lu-PSMA: Potential Causes and Solutions

It is important to recognise that while 177Lu-PSMA has added to the therapeutic armamentarium for mCRPC, a proportion of patients exhibit primary resistance despite having PSMA-avid disease on PET/CT [9]. Several resistance mechanisms to 177Lu-PSMA have been suggested, including the presence of PSMA-negative disease, defined as PSMA uptake equal or less than the liver for measurable lesions in the VISION study, the failure to deliver adequate radiation to metastatic disease, the underlying tumour microenvironment, and the presence of DNA damage-repair and checkpoint genes [35]. In the TheraP study, 28% of patients with PSMA-negative or discordant disease on FDG-PET/CT were excluded [14], while in the VISION study, patients underwent a PSMA-PET/CT and were eligible if they had PSMA-avid disease and no evidence of discordant PSMA-negative disease on the CT scan [9]. While dual PET imaging was used in the TheraP study to exclude most patients with low-PSMA expressing disease [14], small lesions or micrometastatic disease that cannot be visualized on PET/CT imaging could not be evaluated in this manner. One potential solution to overcome the presence of PSMA-negative disease could be to combine 177Lu-PSMA with an unselected treatment, such as chemotherapy.

As 177Lu emits β-radiation, with a long path length and low linear transfer, a single beam of this type of radiation is unlikely to induce cytotoxic double-stranded DNA breaks and micrometastatic disease may not receive a sufficient level of radiation to induce cell death [35]. In addition, macrotumours rely on crossfire radiation from neighbouring PSMA-positive cells to receive enough cumulative radiation to result in cell death [35]; areas of micrometastatic disease with single metastatic cells or small cell clusters may not therefore receive a lethal radiation dose due to a reduced crossfire effect. A possible solution to the mechanism of resistance could be the use of dosimetry with SPECT/CT to support dose optimization [36]. The relationship between response and dose can be stratified into three zones. Zone 1 (‘unpredictable response zone’) identifies the situation in which the delivered dose is below a threshold and response does not correlate with absorbed dose; some lesions may progress despite treatment. In zone 2 (‘effective response zone’), the delivered dose is above a defined threshold and there is a predictable response to treatment; most lesions respond favourably in zone 2. Finally, zone 3 (‘unnecessary dose zone’) describes the scenario in which very high absorbed doses can be delivered but response plateaus; this minimal additional therapeutic benefit has an increased toxicity risk.

Radiopharmaceutical therapy with α-emitting radionuclides has also emerged for the treatment of prostate cancer [37]. Alpha particles, such as those emitted by actinium-225 (225Ac) and lead-212 (212Pb), have shorter-range path lengths and higher linear energy transfer (LET) compared with β-radiation (Table 2) [38–41]. The ionisations from α-emitters result in an increased chance of inducing double-stranded DNA breaks and, therefore, cytotoxicity to the targeted cells; the shorter path length may also limit radiation to nearby cells. Alpha-emitting radionuclides, such as 225Ac, deposit larger amounts of energy compared with β-emitters, directly resulting in double-stranded DNA breaks and potentially overcoming resistance to 177Lu [37, 42–45]. Combination therapy with both α- and β-emitting radiopharmaceuticals is under investigation to take advantage of the physical properties of each type of radiation. Interim results from the phase 1/2 AlphaBet study, which is investigating 177Lu-PSMA-I&T plus radium-223 in patients with mCRPC, has demonstrated that the combination is feasible and well tolerated [35].

Another mechanism of resistance to 177Lu-PSMA may be related to the underlying tumour microenvironment, in which radiation possibly elicits mechanisms that hinder the development of effective anticancer immunity [46]. For example, an immunogenic form of cancer cell death is provided by the release of tumour antigens and induction of a tumour-specific systemic immune response; however, the impact of 177Lu-PSMA on the immune context of PSMA-expressing metastatic prostate cancer currently remains unknown. Differences in immunogenic response between α- and β-emitting radiation have been reported, with α-radiation from 225Ac-PSMA radiopharmaceutical therapy demonstrating a more substantial immunogenic effect compared with β-radiation and a higher antitumour effect in mCRPC [47]. Several other reports also suggest that α-emitting radiation elicits an immune response [48]. A potential solution to the limited immunogenic form of cancer cell death with 177Lu-PSMA could be the use of a single priming dose followed by maintenance treatment with immunotherapy, such as pembrolizumab, given that this approach has previously demonstrated encouraging preliminary activity in patients with mCRPC and was also well tolerated [49].

Finally, while α-radiopharmaceutical therapy targeting PSMA is an emerging treatment for mCRPC, there is also a subgroup of patients who experience a poor response to this therapeutic modality [50]; these individuals typically harbour mutations in DNA damage-repair and checkpoint genes, such as TP53. While the causal role of these alterations in patient outcomes remains to be determined, systematic molecular profiling of tumours and the development of patient-derived models of acquired resistance to targeted agents and immunotherapy appears feasible and can drive the selection of the next therapeutic strategy [51]. Thus, the use of a dedicated approach to enable molecular characterization of the tumour, such as the Unlock Program at Gustave Roussy, may prove useful.

Expansion of Radiopharmaceuticals to Other Solid Tumours: Breast Cancer as a Model

With the success of radiopharmaceuticals in prostate cancer and neuroendocrine tumours, there is rapid growth in research investigating radiopharmaceuticals for other solid tumours. Given the heterogenous array of molecular alterations, expression or upregulation of various molecular targets in breast cancer [3], this was discussed as an additional solid tumour model. Some of the potential molecular targets for radiopharmaceutical therapy in breast cancer include, but are not limited to, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), PSMA, somatostatin receptor (SSTR), gastrin-releasing peptide receptor 2 (GRPR2) and fibroblast activation protein (FAP) [30, 52–56]. Whole-body diagnostic imaging of HER2 targets combined with FDG-PET/CT has previously been used to discriminate between patients who will/will not benefit from treatments such as the antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) trastuzumab [52]. The multi-tracer diagnostic approach identifies intra- and inter-patient tumour heterogeneity, which can explain the suboptimal response in some cases. This is not dissimilar to the use of PSMA-PET/CT and FDG-PET/CT in the TheraP trial discussed in the previous prostate cancer section. 131I-GMIB anti-HER2 VHH1 is a targeted β-emitter directed at HER2-expressing cancers, with phase 1 data demonstrating uptake in HER2-positive lesions and a favourable toxicity profile in healthy individuals and patients with metastatic HER2-positive breast cancer [57]. While these data are only based on a small number of patients, this radiopharmaceutical may ultimately offer new therapeutic options to patients with breast cancer who have progressed on established treatments, such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab or trastuzumab emtansine, given its different mode of action. Along with prostate cancer, PSMA may also be a diagnostic and therapeutic target in triple-negative breast cancer, based on its expression on tumoural neovasculature [53].

Heterogeneous SSTR overexpression has been documented in breast cancer, particularly in oestrogen receptor-positive (ER+) and low Ki67 tumours, in both human breast cancer specimens and clinical studies [54]. Prospective clinical studies investigating SSTR-targeting radiopharmaceutical therapy in breast cancer, such as TRACY-1 (NCT06590857), are at an early stage. Expression of GRPR2 has been identified in various tumour types, appears to be associated with a variety of cancer proliferative processes and is overexpressed in a high percentage of ER+ tumours [56]. Thus, GRPR2 may be a suitable target for imaging and treatment in patients with ER-positive breast cancer, with radiolabelled GRPR ligands currently in development.

The upregulation of FAP expression has been observed in metastatic breast cancer, along with other various tumour types [30]. A retrospective study by Yadav et al. (2024) demonstrated promising efficacy and safety with the use of β-emitting 177Lu-DOTAGA.FAP inhibitor (FAPi) dimer radiopharmaceutical therapy in patients with advanced-stage breast cancer who had previously undergone 68 Ga-DOTA.SA.FAPi PET/CT scans to confirm the expression of FAP [30]; high disease control rates, favourable response outcomes and an acceptable safety profile were reported.

Beyond the availability of multiple targets, the challenge will be to confirm the efficacy of a growing number of radiopharmaceutical therapies in phase 1/2–3 studies and identify selection strategies for integrating these into the paradigm for breast cancer management.

Pharmaceutical Challenges of Developing New Radiopharmaceuticals

There are multiple factors to consider when designing new radiopharmaceuticals. Target selection needs to consider tumour-specific expression, with the target (e.g. receptor, antigen) being highly expressed on cancer cells with minimal normal tissue expression. The target should be accessible for binding (e.g. extracellular domain of a receptor), and some radioligands may work better when their target is internalized upon binding (e.g. PSMA, SSTR2).

The choice of radionuclide needs to consider the type of radiation (α-, β- or Auger-emitters) given that each type has a different range and purpose; of note, Auger-emitters have the shortest range and may be most effective if they can be internalized close to tumour-cell DNA. The half-life of the radionuclide should match the biological half-life of the ligand to ensure optimal tumour uptake prior to decay.

Theranostic pairs (e.g. 177Lu for therapy and 68Ga for PET imaging) should also be considered when assessing imaging capability. Selection of the targeting ligand can vary between antibodies, peptides/small molecules and aptamers/nanobodies: antibodies offer high specificity and long circulation times, peptides/small molecules are ideal for rapidly cycling targets due to their faster clearance, and aptamers/nanobodies offer high specificity and good tumour penetration due to their small size. Chelator selection must ensure stable radiolabelling, while serum stability helps to avoid premature degradation or off-target release of the radionuclide.

The pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of any radiopharmaceutical also need to be optimized, while clearance pathways need to be fully understood to avoid renal or liver toxicity. Radiation dosimetry and safety need to be considered, particularly with regards to tumour specificity, any potential for off-target accumulation and the possibility of patient-specific dosimetry. Potential interactions between radiopharmaceutical therapies and agents used in combination with them, such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy and radiosensitisers, also need to be fully understood.

Finally, there is a need to address radiochemical purity, with improved quality control methods needed to understand the impact of impurities and enable the development of higher-purity radiopharmaceutical therapies [58]; higher-purity radiopharmaceutical therapies may enable lower administered doses to achieve an equivalent efficacy or improved efficacy with an equivalent dose.

Diagnostic Radiolabelled Antibodies Allow Visualization of the Immune System

Imaging biomarkers are now able to assess in vivo cancer-related processes, for example, with PET using 2-deoxy-2-[18F]FDG being routinely used to evaluate tumour metabolism [59]; response to immunotherapy can also be predicted and monitored, although this approach can be rather unspecific. In contrast, ongoing research suggests that novel immuno-PET radiotracers can identify specific immune system targets and better highlight the mechanisms involved in immunotherapy, which may provide complementary predictive information on the therapeutic response in the context of precision oncology. The use of radiolabelled bispecific antibodies may also provide information on the biodistribution and target involvement of specific agents, thus helping to accelerate their development. Bouleau et al. (2022) reported the use of anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) PET radioligands with optimized pharmacokinetic properties to better predict and monitor responses to anti-programmed death-1/anti-PD-L1 immunotherapies using PD-L1 immuno-PET [60]. Such findings encourage further clinical translation of such radioligands for combination strategies.

Future Directions and Challenges for Theranostics

There are numerous radiopharmaceutical diagnostic and therapeutic agents currently under investigation [4]. Areas of research include new targets, new radionuclides, new platforms and the use of combination therapies (Fig. 2) [4]. The combination of targeted radiopharmaceuticals with currently established systemic therapies is an emerging, exciting approach with the potential to improve the outcomes of patients receiving radiopharmaceutical therapy [61]. For example, given that the antitumour activity of radiopharmaceutical therapy is based on the induction of DNA damage, this suggests potential synergistic therapeutic effects could be achieved via its combination with conventional chemotherapies, such as antimetabolites, alkylating agents or topoisomerase inhibitors. Given that activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) signalling pathways has been shown to induce radioresistance, the use of mTOR inhibitors might have a role in enhancing responsiveness to radiopharmaceutical therapy [61].

Many changes have been observed in the landscape of phase 1 studies since the era of cytotoxic chemotherapy. In addition to the typical complex challenges seen in phase 1 studies, the development of radiopharmaceutical therapies needs to consider the additional logistical requirements for the management of radiation, along with the need to undertake imaging in earlier clinical studies to evaluate biodistribution and dosimetry; study sites therefore need to be set up to manage these additional requirements. Phase 1 studies of radiopharmaceutical therapies are also made more difficult by their inability to identify a maximum tolerated treatment dose, thus leading to limitations in applying dose limits for external beam radiotherapy to radiopharmaceutical therapies; this may limit some therapies to remain at this early stage of development.

An enormous global opportunity exists to expand the number of patients who can benefit from radiopharmaceutical therapies, to address the urgent treatment needs of thousands of patients with cancer [61]. Promisingly, the number of clinical studies currently exploring radiopharmaceutical therapy approaches in patients with cancer continues to increase substantially [61]. However, demand for radiopharma-ceutical therapy has the potential to outstrip current healthcare service capacity in many countries over the next few years, given the projected growth of theranostics [62], particularly given that approximately 225 clinical studies are currently evaluating the use of radiopharmaceutical therapy in various cancers [63]. As radio-pharmaceutical therapy is expected to become the new essential ‘pillar’ in the management of cancer, there is an ongoing need to prepare healthcare services to ensure the availability and timely treatment for all eligible patients (Fig. 3).

Not all countries currently have the capacity to provide radiopharmaceutical therapy to eligible patients [63]. For example, while Germany and Spain appear to have a high capacity for providing radiopharmaceutical therapy (based on the estimated capacity of identified country-specific centres to treat patients with radiopharmaceutical therapy per 100,000 patients in 2023), this appears to be much lower in other countries, such as France and Italy. Disparities in the availability of radiotherapy machines between high-income countries and low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) also remains a major problem, with the lack of a suitably qualified workforce cited as a major limiting factor to the effective implementation of radiotherapy, particularly in LMICs [64]. Supply chains, the capacity for radioisotope production and regulatory issues have also been identified as factors potentially affecting access and availability. Multiple factors need to be acknowledged and proactively acted upon for eligible patients with cancer to be able to receive radiopharmaceutical therapy [61]. For example, manufacturing (central versus local) and logistics (such as delivery, application and waste management) need to be adjusted to compensate for the shorter shelf-lives of radiopharmaceuticals in contrast with conventional cancer therapies [61]. There is also the need to establish reliable distribution networks that are also capable of ensuring the safe and timely delivery of these agents, with the ability for ‘upscaling’ to meet the expected rapid increases in demand. Radio-pharmaceutical therapies must also be accessible in all countries with appropriate nuclear medicine facilities, thus ensuring that the benefits of these agents are available to patients globally, with the additional aim of overcoming inequalities in healthcare access. Both imaging and therapy with radiopharmaceuticals must be performed and overseen by a team of trained nuclear medicine professionals [61]. However, there appears to be a substantial worldwide shortage of nuclear medicine professionals with expertise in the use of advanced imaging equipment (such as PET/CT and SPECT/CT) needed for therapy planning, dosimetry and response assessment [65]. In addition, the size of the existing workforce and the number of sites capable of preparing and administering radiopharmaceutical therapy are not currently sufficient to meet the growing demand for these agents. Hence, there is an ongoing need for clinical training programmes and site upgrades with the goal of establishing multidisciplinary care centres capable of providing the full range of nuclear medicine professionals.

Barriers to referral for radiopharmaceutical therapy across countries appear to span four key areas: referral ecosystem and processes, nuclear medicine readiness, coordination of care within and between teams, and funding/reimbursement [66]. Potential barriers to referral for radiopharmaceutical therapy are shown in Fig. 4. A number of recommendations and actions have therefore been identified to support radiopharmaceutical referrals. For example, the development and implementation of standardized protocols, improved patient education provided and a more straightforward approach to day-to-day processes may enhance the overall referral process. In addition, an increased investment in PET scanners and nuclear medicine facilities is required, along with allowing direct access to imaging for eligibility tests. Collaboration between healthcare professionals (HCPs) could also be improved by conducting comprehensive HCP education programs and enhancing collaboration across specialists via the implementation of information-sharing systems.

Conclusion

Understanding the molecular heterogeneity and biology that underlies cell-surface target expression and PET findings has important therapeutic implications in patients with cancer. Lessons learned from studying PSMA regulation and PET imaging may apply to other emerging theranostic targets in prostate cancer and beyond. PSMA-PET/CT and/or SPECT-CT may ultimately become the preferred modalities for response evaluation for radiopharmaceutical therapy. A theranostic approach will progressively allow better selection of treatment for patients with cancer and improve the prediction of response and toxicity, although some challenges remain. Healthcare systems need to be prepared to give access to innovations in radiopharmaceutical therapy in both the clinical and research settings.
