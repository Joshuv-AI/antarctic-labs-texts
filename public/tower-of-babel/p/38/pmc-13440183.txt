Burnout and workload among clinical educators in physiotherapy teaching clinics: an observational study
Authors: Alberto Bermejo-Franco, Daniel Veiga-Pérez, Maxime Quentin Lejeune, Nuria Muñoz-Bermúdez, Ana Mallo-López, José Ángel Del-Blanco-Muñiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysiotherapy clinical educators play a key role in guiding students through practical training, but heavy teaching, managerial, and clinical demands increase their risk of burnout. Understanding the factor’s influencing burnout is essential to support educators’ wellbeing and maintain high-quality clinical education.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using an electronic survey distributed to 256 physiotherapy clinical educators from various accredited centers affiliated with the European University (Spain), with 143 completing the questionnaire. Burnout was measured using the Maslach Burnout Inventory. Descriptive statistics, correlations, and multiple linear regression were used to examine associations between sociodemographic and work-related variables and burnout dimensions, alongside a clinical interpretation of the scores.ResultsPhysiotherapy clinical educators exhibited moderate emotional exhaustion and moderate-to-high personal accomplishment and low-to-moderate depersonalization. No significant associations were found with age, sex, staff physiotherapist (no clinical educator) vs. clinical educator role, weekly tutoring hours, or resource availability. Descriptive analysis suggested slightly higher levels of burnout among managers/clinic directors, university internship coordinators, and clinical educators involved in schedule reorganization or reporting lack of resources, although these differences were not statistically significant. Regression analysis identified role satisfaction as the only significant predictor, being inversely associated with emotional exhaustion and positively associated with personal accomplishment.ConclusionsBurnout among physiotherapy clinical educators is generally moderate. Enhancing role satisfaction and providing organizational support may reduce burnout and improve professional fulfillment, highlighting the importance of institutional strategies to support physiotherapy clinical educators.

Background

Clinical educators play a central role in physiotherapy education, guiding students through the transition from theoretical learning to real clinical practice. Their supervision ensures that students develop the technical, interpersonal, and ethical competencies required for autonomous professional practice. Effective guidance and support depends not only on clinical expertise but also on motivation, workload balance, and adequate institutional support; factors that strongly influence the quality of the learning experience [1, 2]. However, educators often face demanding clinical, managerial, and teaching responsibilities, which may increase their vulnerability to stress and burnout.

Burnout, or occupational burnout syndrome, is a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged exposure to work-related stress when job demands exceed the available resources to cope with them [3]. In the workplace, it is characterized by persistent fatigue, detachment or cynicism toward work, and a reduced sense of professional efficacy and satisfaction, affecting both employee well-being and organizational performance and commitment [4].

It was first recognized among healthcare professionals, although it is now also documented in students of health sciences [5]. Contributing factors include excessive workload, limited control over tasks, insufficient support, and discrepancies between expectations and workplace realities [6]. One of the most robust theoretical frameworks in organizational psychology that explains professional burnout is the Job Demands-Resources Model, which proposes that burnout emerges when job demands chronically exceed the resources available to meet them [7, 8].

Workload refers to the set of physical, mental, and emotional demands involved in carrying out an activity within a given period. In clinical practice, it includes patient volume, case complexity, and decision-making responsibilities [9].

Role satisfaction is the degree to which individuals perceive their functions, responsibilities, and expectations as coherent, recognized, and rewarding; it is measured in relation to burnout dimensions, emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalization (DP), and personal accomplishment (PA), as well as organizational factors [10].

Motivation is defined as the set of internal and external processes that activate, guide, and sustain behaviour toward achieving goals, driving effort, persistence and commitment to tasks [11]. Satisfaction refers to the state of well-being arising when expectations or goals are met, generating a positive perception.

Institutional support is assessed through the specific training received by clinical educators, the information provided by clinical placement coordinators, and the resources available to carry out supervision [9].

In physiotherapy education, clinical educators face intense clinical, managerial, and teaching demands, which may predispose them to burnout [8]. Clinical placements are a pivotal component of training, during which students progressively assume responsibility in patient care under the supervision of clinical educators. Situations of heightened healthcare pressure, such as seasonal workload peaks or global events like the COVID-19 pandemic, may further increase stress exposure and burnout risk [12].

The development of burnout among physiotherapy educators is therefore multifactorial, influenced by academic, clinical, emotional, and institutional pressures. Evolving educational and clinical demands require continuous adaptation, adding to the stress experienced [13]. Neglect of self-care can heighten susceptibility to burnout [14]. Burnout is associated with reduced job satisfaction and may indirectly affect the quality of clinical education [15]. Evidence suggests that positive, relationship-oriented leadership styles can enhance job satisfaction and reduce emotional fatigue [16].

Given this context, the aim of this study was to examine the levels of burnout among physiotherapy clinical educators, which was assessed through its key dimensions (EE, DP, and PA) using a questionnaire specifically designed to capture these variables, in relation to workload, role satisfaction, motivation, and institutional support. By analyzing these variables, the study intended to better understand the conditions influencing educator functioning and to inform strategies that could optimize their well-being and indirectly support the learning environment for physiotherapy students.

Methods

Study design and setting, ethical considerations

A cross-sectional, observational study was conducted between September 1 and November 7, 2025, using an online questionnaire administered to physiotherapy clinical educators affiliated with internship centers collaborating with the European University (Spain). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies [17]. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the European University of Madrid (protocol code: 2025 − 684). All participants provided electronic informed consent prior to participation and authorized the use and dissemination of the anonymized data for research purposes.

Participants and sample

Eligible participants were physiotherapists holding an official degree, actively involved in supervising undergraduate students at affiliated clinical centers, and with sufficient Spanish proficiency to complete the questionnaire. Incomplete responses and professionals not currently engaged in clinical supervision were excluded.

The survey was distributed to 256 eligible physiotherapy clinical educators using purposive convenience sampling. Of the invited centers, 143 professionals voluntarily completed the online questionnaire (Google Forms, Menlo Park, CA, USA), yielding a response rate of 55.9%. The final sample was considered representative of the accessible population and comparable to previous research in similar educational healthcare contexts [18, 19]. Outcomes

Demographic features of the sample

The survey completed by participants in this study collected descriptive sociodemographic and professional information relevant to physiotherapists supervising students in clinical practice. Participants were asked to report their age using predefined ranges, allowing the analysis of potential associations between age and burnout outcomes. Sex was also recorded, providing insight into possible gender-related differences in experiences of supervision and workload. Additionally, respondents indicated their primary role within the clinical setting, such as staff physiotherapist or clinical coordinator, which may influence responsibilities and stress levels.

Professional engagement in student supervision was further quantified by recording the approximate number of weekly hours dedicated to teaching or supervising students. Participants were also asked to describe the impact of these teaching duties on their clinical workload, specifically whether supervision required them to reorganize their schedule or adjust other professional responsibilities. The availability of resources necessary for effective supervision, including materials, space, and time, was also assessed. Respondents who reported inadequate resources were asked to specify the materials or support they would need to improve supervision.

These sociodemographic and professional variables were collected following established survey methods in health professional research [20]. Similar approaches have been used in studies exploring physiotherapists’ roles and experiences in clinical education and burnout contexts [21, 22]. For example, Voges and Frantz examined clinical supervisors’ roles and responsibilities in physiotherapy education [21], while Carmona-Barrientos et al. analyzed sociodemographic and organizational factors associated with occupational stress and burnout among physiotherapists in Spain [22].

In addition, participants were asked to report their level of satisfaction with the tutoring role and whether they had received any previous management or coordination training related to supervising students. These variables were treated as self-reported work-related factors potentially associated with burnout outcomes and were included as predictors in the regression analyses.

Endpoints

Burnout outcomes were assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory - Educators Survey (MBI-ES) [23], in its validated Spanish version for university teachers [24], a widely used and psychometrically validated instrument for measuring occupational burnout among educators and healthcare professionals. The appropriate licenses to administer the instrument were obtained from Mind Garden, Inc., the official publisher and distributor of the Maslach Burnout Inventory. The MBI-ES used in this study is intended for research, clinical, and organizational applications, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the publisher’s licensing requirements.

The MBI-ES is a self-administered questionnaire composed of 22 items rated on a Likert-type scale, typically ranging from 0 (“never”) to 6 (“every day”), allowing respondents to indicate the frequency with which they experience specific feelings related to their professional role. The instrument evaluates three core dimensions of burnout: EE, DP and PA, each scored independently and interpreted according to established cut-off ranges that classify responses into low, medium, or high levels of burnout risk. The internal consistency of MBI-ES dimensions was assessed in the present sample using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients.

EE reflects feelings of being emotionally overextended and depleted by one’s work. It is measured through nine items, with scores interpreted as low (≤ 16), medium [16–25], and high (≥ 27). Higher scores indicate greater emotional fatigue and are considered a central marker of burnout.

DP captures an impersonal or detached response toward students or patients, often characterized by cynicism or emotional distancing. This dimension includes five items, with scores categorized as low (≤ 6), moderate [7, 8], and high (≥ 13). Elevated DP scores suggest a deteriorating interpersonal connection and increased burnout vulnerability.

PA assesses feelings of competence, effectiveness, and achievement in one’s professional role. It consists of eight items and is interpreted inversely, meaning that lower scores indicate a higher risk of burnout. Specifically, high scores (≥ 38) reflect strong professional efficacy and a low burnout risk, medium scores (32–37) indicate a moderate level of professional accomplishment, and low scores (≤ 31) suggest reduced professional efficacy and a greater likelihood of burnout.

Building on the detailed analysis of each subdimension of the Maslach Burnout Inventory, this multidimensional framework enables a nuanced understanding of burnout profiles among physiotherapists engaged in student supervision within clinical practice. By applying the established scoring thresholds for each burnout dimension, the study identifies varying levels of burnout severity and examines their associations with a range of sociodemographic and occupational factors. These contextual variables provide a comprehensive basis for analyzing how structural and personal factors may mitigate or exacerbate occupational stress in clinical teaching environments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all sociodemographic and work-related variables. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for burnout dimensions (EE, DP, PA) were performed using independent-samples t-tests (for dichotomous variables such as sex or availability of resources) and one-way ANOVA (for variables with more than two categories, such as age group or professional role), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test when appropriate.

The normality of continuous variables was confirmed using Shapiro-Wilk tests and visual inspection of histograms. Associations between ordinal or continuous predictors and burnout dimensions were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Nominal categorical variables (e.g., sex and professional role) were coded numerically to allow their inclusion in the correlation analyses.

Finally, multivariable linear regression models were built to identify independent predictors of each burnout dimension. Predictor variables included age category, sex, professional role, weekly hours of tutoring, satisfaction with the tutoring role, availability of institutional resources, and prior management training related to supervision or coordination roles. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Prior to regression analyses, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 5 considered acceptable. Model assumptions, including linearity, normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and independence of errors, were verified through residual analysis. Categorical predictors were coded numerically prior to inclusion in the regression models. Sex was coded as 0 = female and 1 = male. Professional role was treated as an ordinal variable reflecting increasing levels of supervisory responsibility within the internship program. Age group was also entered as an ordinal variable based on the predefined age categories used in the survey.

Questionnaires with incomplete responses were excluded from the analysis according to the predefined exclusion criteria. Therefore, no missing item-level data were included in the final dataset.

A total of 143 professionals from physiotherapy internship centers affiliated with the European University (Spain) participated in the study. All respondents met the inclusion criteria and completed the online questionnaire voluntarily and anonymously, representing a response rate of 55.9%.

Group comparisons across sociodemographic and professional variables

The analysis explored the potential associations between sociodemographic and professional variables and the three dimensions of burnout: EE, DP, and PA. The internal consistency of the MBI-ES dimensions in the present sample (n = 143) was good for EE (α = 0.89) and PA (α = 0.85). The DP subscale showed lower internal consistency (α = 0.31). Item–total correlation analysis indicated that items 15 and 22 contributed poorly to the internal consistency of this dimension.

Age did not show statistically significant differences in any of the burnout dimensions (EE: p = 0.270; DP: p = 0.169; PA: p = 0.300). Although professionals aged 41–50 years showed numerically higher mean scores for EE and DP, these differences were not statistically significant. Similarly, no statistically significant differences were found between men and women regarding burnout levels. Mean scores for EE, DP, and PA were almost identical across sexes (p > 0.60 in all cases).

When comparing professional roles, the highest mean values of EE were observed among university internship coordinators and clinic directors, whereas lower mean values were observed among clinical educators and physiotherapists without educational responsibilities; however, these differences were not statistically significant (EE p = 0.357; DP p = 0.662; PA p = 0.396).

No significant associations were found between weekly tutoring hours and burnout levels. Although those dedicating 11–15 h per week showed numerically higher mean values for EE and DP, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.70). Likewise, neither schedule reorganization nor availability of adequate resources were significantly related to any of the burnout dimensions (all p > 0.30).

Overall, no statistically significant differences in burnout dimensions were observed across demographic and professional variables.

Clinical interpretation

To facilitate the interpretation of burnout levels in the sample, mean scores for the three MBI dimensions were examined in relation to the established clinical cut-off ranges (EE ≥ 27 = high; DP ≥ 13 = high; PA ≤ 31 = low). Based on these cut-off criteria, the prevalence of each burnout dimension was calculated. High EE (≥ 27) was observed in 29 participants (20.3%), high DP (≥ 13) in 5 participants (3.5%), and low PA (≤ 31) in 14 participants (9.8%). When considering the three-dimensional burnout profile simultaneously (high EE, high DP, and low PA), only 2 participants (1.4%) met the criteria for burnout. Table 3 summarizes these results by sex, age group, professional role, and key work-related variables. Overall, mean scores fell within the moderate range for EE and PA and within the low-to-moderate range for DP according to the established cut-off ranges. Although no statistically significant differences were found, descriptive mean scores varied slightly across professional roles and work-related conditions such as schedule reorganization and perceived resource availability. Participants reporting adequate resources showed descriptively lower EE and DP mean scores and higher PA mean scores, although these differences were not statistically significant.

Bivariate correlations

Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were conducted to explore bivariate associations between predictor variables and the three burnout dimensions: EE, DP, and PA. As presented in Table 4, sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, professional role, and weekly tutoring hours showed weak and non-significant correlations with the burnout dimensions (|ρ| < 0.20; p > 0.05). In contrast, work-related variables included in the regression models, particularly role satisfaction, showed significant associations with burnout outcomes. Higher role satisfaction was associated with lower EE and DP and with higher PA. Availability of resources and management training showed weaker or inconsistent associations with burnout dimensions.

Discussion

The descriptive analysis revealed overall moderate levels of EE and DP and high levels of PA among physiotherapy professionals involved in internship programs. Moreover, only 1.4% of participants met the criteria for the full burnout profile (high EE, high DP, and low PA), indicating that severe burnout was uncommon in this sample. These findings are consistent with recent literature on burnout among clinical tutors in health sciences. Previous research has reported no significant differences in burnout dimensions according to gender, suggesting that sex may not be strongly associated with burnout development [25], which aligns with the results of the present study. However, Fierro Freixenet identified the academic year of supervised students as a relevant variable, with higher levels of EE, DP, and reduced PA when tutors were responsible for students in more advanced stages of training, likely due to increased clinical and educational demands [25].

Age has also been identified as an influential factor in burnout levels among clinical tutors. Higher stress and EE have been observed among professionals aged between 35 and 50 years, a period often associated with increased work–life demands, whereas lower levels of DP and EE have been reported in tutors over 50 years of age, potentially reflecting greater professional experience and more effective coping strategies [25].

Similar trends were observed in the present study, supporting the idea that professional maturity may be associated with lower burnout levels. Consistent with findings in university educators and clinical tutors, increased eEE has been linked to high clinical workloads outside student supervision [26].

In line with Flores et al., organizational support, workload, sustained occupational stress, professional role, and available resources emerge as relevant contextual factors [27]. This relationship became particularly evident during the COVID-19 pandemic, when increased patient volumes and insufficient institutional resources overwhelmed healthcare professionals, negatively affecting their mental health and exacerbating burnout symptoms [28]. Taken together, these findings suggest that burnout among clinical tutors may be influenced by a complex interaction of individual and institutional variables rather than by workload alone [27].

No statistically significant associations were found between burnout dimensions and variables such as age, sex, professional role, workload, agenda reorganization, or resource availability. The absence of significant differences across several sociodemographic and professional variables should be interpreted with caution. Some subgroups, such as older professionals or university internship coordinators, were relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle between-group differences. Therefore, the lack of significance does not necessarily indicate the absence of meaningful effects but rather reflects the complexity of burnout as a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by both individual and organizational factors.

The absence of significant correlations might also indicate that burnout in this population is more influenced by subjective experiences, such as perceived support or satisfaction, than by purely structural or demographic variables. Previous research has shown that organizational stressors, including multitasking demands and limited resources, play a relevant role in burnout development. Increased conversational multitasking has been associated with greater cognitive and workload demands, reduced professional satisfaction, and higher burnout risk, particularly affecting PA [29]. Similarly, working in low-resource healthcare settings has been linked to increased frustration and lower professional satisfaction, whereas improvements in available resources have been associated with better outcomes for both professionals and patients [30].

From a clinical standpoint, burnout levels remained generally within the moderate range for EE and DP, while PA scores were high across most groups. In addition, participants reporting adequate institutional resources tended to show lower EE and DP scores and higher PA. This interpretation is consistent with evidence suggesting that work organization and schedule flexibility contribute to psychological well-being. Greater autonomy over work schedules is associated with modest reductions in EE, fatigue, depressive symptoms, and burnout, although effects are generally moderate and context-dependent [31]. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of addressing organizational conditions and supporting emotional self-regulation and adaptive coping strategies to mitigate burnout among clinical tutors.

From a multivariable perspective, role satisfaction emerged as the only significant predictor of burnout outcomes, with higher satisfaction associated with lower EE and higher PA. This finding reinforces the relevance of psychosocial and motivational factors over structural or demographic variables in explaining burnout among clinical tutors. When tutors perceive their supervisory role as valued and recognized as an essential component of professional training, their sense of purpose, professional identity, and intrinsic motivation increase, fostering greater engagement and resilience [32]. Such recognition may be associated with lower EE and reduced PA but also enhances the quality of supervision and educational outcomes for students [33]. In contrast, variables such as age, sex, tutoring hours, schedule reorganization, or resource adequacy did not emerge as significant predictors, suggesting that objective workload or contextual conditions alone may not be sufficient to explain burnout risk. Instead, how tutors interpret and experience their role may play a more relevant role in burnout experiences. A clearly defined and institutionally recognized role reduces ambiguity, one of the main sources of organizational stress, and strengthens tutors’ ability to cope with demanding situations. Consistent with previous research, personal and motivational factors, particularly role clarity, recognition, and satisfaction, have been associated with lower burnout levels among health professionals and may contribute to lower perceived workload and reduced DP and EE [34].

These findings highlight the importance of fostering supportive work environments that enhance tutors’ sense of competence and purpose in their educational role.

Practical implications

The findings of this study have several practical implications for clinical education and healthcare management. The central importance of role satisfaction as a predictor of burnout underscores the need to foster supportive work environments that enhance tutors’ sense of competence, recognition, and purpose in their educational responsibilities. Interventions aimed at increasing job satisfaction, such as formal recognition of the tutoring role, greater autonomy in supervisory tasks, and opportunities for professional development, may help improve professional well-being and reduce EE.

In addition, ensuring adequate organizational support, including manageable workloads, flexible scheduling when feasible, and access to appropriate teaching resources, appears to buffer against burnout and facilitate more effective clinical supervision. Institutional strategies that explicitly value and support the tutoring function may therefore contribute to more sustainable educational environments.

Finally, regular monitoring of burnout levels among clinical educators could inform the implementation of targeted preventive measures, such as stress management programs, peer support initiatives, or training in time and resource management. Providing clinical educators with tools to enhance self-regulation and coping skills throughout the academic year may further reduce burnout risk and promote long-term well-being and educational quality.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between predictors and burnout dimensions. Second, the use of self-reported measures may be subject to recall or social desirability bias, which may be particularly relevant among clinical tutors due to the evaluative and responsibility-related nature of their role. Third, the non-probabilistic convenience sampling limits the generalizability of the results beyond physiotherapy tutors from the participating centers. In addition, voluntary participation may have introduced self-selection bias, as professionals with a greater interest in the topic or experiences related to burnout may have been more likely to participate.

Furthermore, although various work-related variables were included, key psychosocial factors identified in the literature, such as professional recognition and role clarity, were not directly assessed. Additionally, the internal consistency of the DP subscale of the Maslach Burnout Inventory was lower than expected in the present sample. Previous research has reported that this dimension often shows lower reliability compared with EE and PA when using the MBI-ES in academic or healthcare teaching populations [35]. One possible explanation is the reduced variability typically observed in DP responses, as many professionals report very low levels of this construct, which may attenuate internal consistency estimates.

Finally, the exclusive use of quantitative measures (Maslach Burnout Inventory) precluded in-depth exploration of tutors’ subjective experiences, suggesting that future studies incorporating qualitative or mixed-methods approaches may provide a more comprehensive understanding of burnout in this population.

Future lines of research

Future studies should consider longitudinal designs to examine causal relationships and changes in burnout over time, ideally incorporating qualitative approaches to explore tutors’ subjective experiences and the emotional and organizational factors influencing their well-being. Expanding samples to include multiple countries, healthcare disciplines, and types of institutions would improve generalizability. Research should also investigate interpersonal and organizational factors, such as quality of educational supervision, peer support, leadership style, role clarity, and job satisfaction, given their central role in burnout vulnerability. Finally, evaluating the effectiveness of interventions, such as resilience training or workload restructuring, may help identify evidence-based strategies to mitigate burnout in clinical education contexts.

Conclusions

In this study, physiotherapy clinical educators showed moderate EE, moderate DP, and high PA measured with the MBI. Although demographic and workload variables were not significantly associated with burnout, role satisfaction emerged as a key factor associated with lower EE and higher PA, highlighting the importance of intrinsic motivation and perceived professional efficacy. Organizational support, such as adequate resources, clear expectations, and manageable schedules, may contribute to lower burnout levels and enhance educators’ well-being. These findings underscore the need for institutional strategies that strengthen clinical educator support systems and help sustain high-quality clinical training environments in physiotherapy education.
