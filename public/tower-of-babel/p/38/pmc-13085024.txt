Screening of a quinonoid compounds library identifies decylubiquinone as an antioxidant and anti-apoptotic agent against glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis via CD39/CD73/adenosine axis
Authors: Yu Xu, Wenpeng Xu, Wencan Zhang, Shangzhi Li, Dingxin Zhang, Xiangyu Lin, Qian Sun, Yanguo Wang, Peijie Hu, Haipeng Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) is a prevalent and serious side effect of long-term glucocorticoid therapy, characterized by elevated oxidative stress and profound suppression of bone formation. There is a lack of therapies that effectively target the underlying oxidative stress and impaired osteogenesis simultaneously. In this study, we aimed to identify novel therapeutic agents from a library of quinonoid compounds. Using a high-throughput screen with Runx2 promoter-luciferase reporter cells, we identified decylubiquinone (DUB) as a potent candidate that counteracted dexamethasone (Dex)-induced oxidative stress and osteogenic suppression in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). In a murine GIOP model, DUB administration significantly alleviated bone loss, reduced oxidative stress, and inhibited osteoblast apoptosis. Mechanistically, DUB restored the glucocorticoid-impaired CD39/CD73/adenosine (ADO) axis, thereby elevating extracellular ADO levels. ADO subsequently exerted dual protective effects: it attenuated oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by glucocorticoids, and it activated the adenosine A2b receptor (A2bR), triggering the downstream cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling cascade to drive osteogenic gene expression. Genetic knockdown of CD39, CD73, or A2bR abolished the beneficial effects of both DUB and exogenous ADO. Furthermore, we developed bone-targeted DUB-loaded liposomes (DUB@TLip), which markedly enhanced the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of DUB. In summary, our study not only identifies DUB as a promising anabolic agent against GIOP but also reveals a novel mechanistic link between purinergic signaling and bone redox homeostasis, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the CD39/CD73/ADO axis for treating this and potentially other oxidative stress–associated skeletal disorders.

Introduction

Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) is the most prevalent form of secondary osteoporosis in clinical practice, and represents a major dose-limiting adverse effect of long-term glucocorticoid (GC) therapy [1]. With the widespread and increasing use of GCs in the management of autoimmune diseases, hematological malignancies, organ transplantation, and chronic inflammatory disorders, the global incidence of GIOP continues to rise annually [2]. Epidemiological data indicate that 30% to 50% of patients receiving long-term GC treatment develop osteoporosis, with a 2- to 3-fold increased risk of vertebral fractures, and the fracture risk is positively correlated with the duration and cumulative dose of GC exposure [3]. GCs induce rapid and progressive bone loss within weeks of treatment initiation, with an annualized bone loss rate of 5% to 15% in the early treatment phase, which is predominantly driven by two core pathological hallmarks: excessive oxidative stress in the bone microenvironment, and profound, sustained suppression of osteogenic differentiation and bone formation [4,5]. Despite the well-characterized pathological basis of GIOP, current clinical therapeutic strategies remain predominantly anti-resorptive, including bisphosphonates and denosumab. These agents fail to adequately restore impaired bone formation or reverse GC-induced oxidative damage to osteoblast lineage cells, resulting in limited long-term efficacy in halting or reversing disease progression. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to develop novel anabolic therapeutic agents that can simultaneously mitigate oxidative stress and rescue compromised osteogenesis, the two core pathological drivers of GIOP.

In recent years, the identification of novel therapeutic targets for osteoporosis based on the in-depth elucidation of its pathological mechanisms has emerged as a major focus of skeletal biology and translational medicine research [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The pathogenesis of GIOP involves a complex network of multi-pathway dysregulations, among which oxidative stress and osteoblast apoptosis are the initiating and central pathological links [10]. Supraphysiological levels of GCs disrupt the intracellular redox balance by elevating the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation products such as malondialdehyde (MDA), while simultaneously reducing the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes including superoxide dismutase (SOD) [11,12]. Excessive oxidative stress further activates mitochondria-dependent apoptotic cascades, upregulates pro-apoptotic proteins including Bax and cleaved Caspase-3, downregulates the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, and ultimately promotes massive apoptosis of osteoblasts and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [13,14]. Concurrently, GCs directly inhibit the osteogenic lineage commitment of BMSCs by antagonizing the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, while promoting their aberrant adipogenic differentiation, leading to a marked reduction in functional osteoblasts at bone remodeling sites [15]. GCs also disrupt the coupling between bone formation and resorption by enhancing the receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand/osteoprotegerin (RANKL/OPG) axis to drive excessive osteoclast activation [16]. Notably, there is a critical synergistic interaction between oxidative stress and impaired osteogenesis. GC-induced ROS overproduction further amplifies osteoblast apoptosis and inhibits osteogenic differentiation, forming a vicious cycle that drives progressive bone deterioration [17]. This pathological framework provides the core theoretical rationale for the urgent need to conduct drug screening, with the goal of identifying candidate agents capable of simultaneously targeting the two cardinal pathological hallmarks of GIOP: oxidative stress and impaired osteogenesis.

Quinonoids are a large class of natural organic compounds defined by a characteristic quinone core structure, which are widely distributed in plants, fungi, and microorganisms [18]. Their unique redox properties have garnered significant attention in pharmaceutical research. Structurally featuring a conjugated carbonyl system, these compounds exhibit excellent electron-accepting and donating capabilities, demonstrating remarkable advantages in modulating cellular redox status [19,20]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that multiple quinone derivatives, including ubiquinone, vitamin K family members, and curcumin quinone analogs, exhibit significant therapeutic potential in oxidative stress-related diseases via scavenging excess ROS, enhancing endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity, and regulating redox-sensitive signaling pathways [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Decylubiquinone (DUB), a member of the ubiquinone family, is a naturally occurring quinonoid compound with a well-characterized role as a redox carrier in the mitochondrial respiratory chain [25]. However, prior studies on DUB have been largely limited to its direct mitochondrial antioxidant effects, and its biological function in bone metabolism, as well as its therapeutic potential in GIOP, which remain completely uncharacterized. Furthermore, the non-antioxidant pharmacological mechanisms of DUB, particularly its regulatory effects on intercellular signaling pathways, have not been elucidated to date.

The CD39/CD73/adenosine (ADO) axis is the core rate-limiting system that governs extracellular ADO production in the tissue microenvironment [26]. In this cascade, the ectonucleotidase CD39 (encoded by Entpd1) sequentially hydrolyzes extracellular ATP to AMP, which is further dephosphorylated to ADO by CD73 (encoded by Nt5e), the rate-limiting enzyme in this process [27]. Extracellular ADO exerts its biological functions by binding to four G protein-coupled adenosine receptors (A1R, A2aR, A2bR, and A3R) on the cell surface [28]. Emerging evidence has indicated that the CD39/CD73/ADO axis plays a critical role in maintaining bone homeostasis. Dysregulation of CD39/CD73/ADO axis has been observed in postmenopausal osteoporosis, and ADO signaling has been shown to regulate the differentiation and function of both osteoblasts and osteoclasts [29]. However, whether the CD39/CD73/ADO axis is impaired in GIOP, and whether this axis can be targeted to mitigate GC-induced bone loss, remains completely unknown. Up to now, no prior studies have established a mechanistic link between quinonoid compounds, the CD39/CD73/ADO purinergic signaling axis, and bone redox homeostasis in the context of GIOP.

In this study, we conducted a screening of a 153-member quinonoid compound library and identified DUB as a promising therapeutic candidate against GIOP. We systematically evaluated the efficacy of DUB in mitigating glucocorticoid-induced bone loss and elucidated its underlying mechanism of action. We reveal that DUB exerts its protective effects by restoring the CD39/CD73/ADO signaling axis and activating the A2bR/cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway, providing new insights into the therapeutic potential of targeting the purinergic signaling pathway for GIOP treatment.

Methods and materials

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and ethics were approved by the Institutional Animal Use and Care Committee of Qilu Hospital (Qingdao) of Shandong University (Ethics Approval No. KYDWLL-202515). Male C57BL/6 mice were commercially obtained from the Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle and a constant temperature of 25 °C. No mice, samples, or data points were excluded from the analysis.

Primary BMSCs isolation and validation

Primary BMSCs were isolated from the femora of C57BL/6 mice using a previously described protocol [30]. Briefly, femora were dissected under sterile conditions and placed in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After removing the epiphyses, bone marrow cells were flushed out into a 10-cm culture dish using a syringe with a 25G needle and suspended in α-MEM (12571063, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, F101, Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (PS, 15140122, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 24 h, the medium was replaced to remove non-adherent cells. For validation, BMSCs were harvested and incubated with anti-CD29, anti-CD34, anti-CD45, and anti-CD90 antibodies (Table S1) at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by analysis on a FACS Celesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Osteogenesis and adipogenesis induction in BMSCs and quantification

The osteogenic induction was performed according to well-established protocols [31], primary BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM containing 10% FBS (F101, Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) and 1% PS (15140122, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), supplemented with 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid (A92902, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 10 mM β-glycerol phosphate (G9422, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). The osteogenic induction medium was renewed every two days over a period of up to 10 days. For osteogenesis assessment, cells were fixed with 70% ethanol and stained with 0.1% (w/v) Alizarin Red S (ARS, A5533, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA, pH 4.2) at room temperature for 10 min. Images were acquired using a scanner (V850 pro, EPSON, Nagano-ken, Japan) and a microscope (Ts2R-FL, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The relative mineralization area was quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.52a, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

For adipogenic induction, primary BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM containing 10% FBS and 1% PS, supplemented with 50 μM isobutylmethylxanthine (I5879, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), 0.5 μM dexamethasone (Dex, D2915, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), 50 μM indomethacin (I7378, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA), and 10 μg/mL insulin (I9278, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA). After 21 days, cells were fixed and stained using a Lipid (Oil Red O) Staining Kit (MAK194, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For quantification, stained Oil Red O was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ST038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and absorbance was measured at 492 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA).

Construction of Runx2-luciferase reporter BMSCs

Stable Mus musculus BMSCs expressing the Runx2-luciferase reporter gene were established via lentiviral transduction. Lentivirus harboring the Runx2 promoter-luciferase construct was purchased from QBiO Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Purified BMSCs were seeded into 10-cm culture dishes. Upon reaching 50% confluence, cells were transfected with an appropriate amount of lentiviral suspension in the presence of 5 μg/mL polybrene (C0351, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. At 48 h post-transfection, the medium was replaced with selection medium supplemented with 2 μg/mL puromycin (ST551, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to establish stably transfected cell pools.

Quinonoid compounds screening

A total of 153 quinonoid compounds (Table S1) were obtained from the Natural Product Library (L6000, TargetMol, Shanghai, China) via TargetMol Chemicals Inc. In the first round of screening, stable Runx2-luciferase reporter BMSCs were treated with each compound at 10 μM, or with an equivalent volume of DMSO (ST038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) as a vehicle control, in osteogenic induction medium supplemented with 10 μM Dex (D2915, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C for 72 h. Secreted luciferase activity in the supernatant was then measured using the Bio-Lumi™ Firefly Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (RG042M, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of Bio-Lumi™ substrate (provided with the kit) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Luminescence was quantified using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) and normalized to the total protein concentration, as determined by a BCA protein assay (23225, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA) from the corresponding cell lysates. Based on the results, the top 10 quinonoid compounds that enhanced Runx2 expression were selected for the second round of screening.

In the second screening round, BMSCs were incubated with 10 μM of each selected quinonoid compound or DMSO control in osteogenic induction medium with or without 10 μM Dex for 7 days. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was measured using a commercial ALP activity kit (P0322S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after drug treatment, primary BMSCs were lysed, centrifuged, and the supernatant was assessed at 405 nm using a spectrophotometer (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The top 3 compounds showing the highest ALP activity were subsequently evaluated for their effects on eliminating intracellular ROS levels using the DCFDA/H2DCFDA Cellular ROS Assay Kit (ab113851, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Cell proliferation assay

Primary BMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 0.5 × 104 cells per well and pre-cultured for 24 h. Cells were then treated with DUB (T27139, TargetMol, Shanghai, China) at a series of concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, and 50 μM) in the presence or absence of 10 μM Dex (D2915, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 2 or 10 days, respectively. To evaluate cell proliferation and DUB cytotoxicity, the MTT assay was performed by incubating cells with 0.5 mg/mL 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, M6494, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) in culture medium at 37 °C for 2 h. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL of DMSO (ST038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and the absorbance at 550 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA).

Western blot

Total proteins from cells or tissues were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (89901, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA) containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (78443, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA). Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were separated using Cytoplasm Abstraction Reagent and NE-PER™ Nuclear Extraction Reagents (P0028, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (23225, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA). 20 μg total protein of each sample was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore Sigma, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk (P0216, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S2) at 4 °C overnight, followed by secondary antibodies (Table S2) at room temperature for 1 h. Signals from target protein were visualized using a Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate Kit (32209, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA) and an Amersham Imager 600 system (General Electric Company, Boston, MA, USA). The intensity of bands was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.52a, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using a FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (RC112, Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg total RNA using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA). qPCR was performed on a StepOne Plus PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) using TB Green Premix (RR420A, TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) and primers listed in Table S3. Gapdh was used as the reference gene. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Primary BMMs isolation and osteoclastogenesis induction

Primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) were isolated from the bone marrow of bilateral mouse hindlimbs, and maintained in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS (F101, Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), 1% PS (15140122, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and 25 ng/mL recombinant macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 576406, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere until reaching 90% confluence. To induce osteoclast differentiation, primary BMMs were cultured in α-MEM containing 10% FBS and 1% PS, supplemented with 25 ng/mL M-CSF and 50 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL, 462TEC, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). After 6 days of culture, cells were fixed and subjected to tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining with a commercial TRAP Staining Kit (G1050-50T, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Mature multinucleated osteoclasts were identified as TRAP-positive cells with 5 or more nuclei under microscopic observation (Model Ts2R-FL, Nikon, Shinagawa-ku, Tokyo, Japan).

Establishment of GIOP model and administration of DUB or ADO

The establishment of GIOP animal model was performed according to well-established protocols [32]. Three-month-old male mice were randomly assigned to three experimental groups and administered daily for 5 consecutive weeks as follows: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as vehicle control; Dex (10 mg/kg, D2915, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was administrated to the mice via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection daily for 5 consecutive weeks. For intervention, DUB (10 mg/kg or 30 mg/kg, T27139, TargetMol, St. Louis, MO, USA) was given via intragastric gavage daily, and ADO (5 mg/kg, HY-B0228, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) via i.p. injection daily, both synchronized with Dex treatment for 5 weeks. At the end of the treatment period, all mice were euthanized, and femora and serum samples were collected for subsequent analysis.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and analysis

Femora were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, P1110, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 48 h. For micro-CT analysis, femora were scanned using a high-resolution micro-CT system (Skyscan 1172, Bruker Micro-CT, Kontich, Belgium) at 65 kV, 153 μA, and a resolution of 8.0 μm/pixel. Image reconstruction was performed using NRecon software (version 1.6, Bruker MicroCT). Trabecular bone parameters were analyzed using CTAn software (version 1.9, Bruker MicroCT), and 3D visualization was conducted with CTVol software (version 2.0, Bruker MicroCT). A region of interest (ROI) was selected starting 1 mm below the distal epiphyseal growth plate and extending 5 mm distally. Trabecular bone parameters including bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) were determined.

Histology, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Femora or non-bone tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, P1110, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 48 h. Femora tissue were then undergone decalcification process in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4, 10009617, Sinopharm, Shanghai, China) for one week. All tissues were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness using a microtome (HistoCore BIOCUT, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E, G1005, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) or Masson's trichrome (G1006, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for histological evaluation. For IHC detection of osteocalcin (Ocn), sections were incubated with a specific primary antibody (Table S2) overnight at 4 °C after dewaxing, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Table S2) for 1 h at room temperature. HRP activity was visualized using a DAB Substrate Kit (ab64238, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Nuclei were counterstained with Hematoxylin (G1004, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and images were acquired using a microscope (Ts2R-FL, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For immunofluorescence (IF) staining of tissue section slides, slides were processed with dewaxing, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, then incubated with specific primary antibodies (Table S2) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (Table S2) for 1 h at room temperature. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (36308ES20, Yeasen, Shanghai, China), and fluorescence signals were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Model Ts2R-FL, Nikon, Shinagawa-ku, Tokyo, Japan). The quantification of histological and IHC images were performed by using ImageJ (version 1.52a, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Bone formation histomorphometry analysis

C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally injected with calcein (10 mg/kg, C0875, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in PBS on day 10 and day 3 before sacrifice. Femora were fixed in 4% PFA (P1110, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and embedded in methyl methacrylate. Sections of 50 μm thickness were prepared using a hard tissue microtome (SP1600, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and polished. All sections were protected from light. Cortical endosteum surfaces were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (STELLARIS 5, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The mineral deposition rate (MAR) was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.52a, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Measurement of oxidative stress level

Levels of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), malondialdehyde (MDA), and advanced oxidation protein products (AOPP) in serum and intracellular samples were measured using commercial assay kits: Total SOD Assay Kit (A001-3-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), 8-OHdG Assay Kit (H165-1-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), MDA Assay Kit (A003-1-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), and AOPP Assay Kit (H596, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), respectively, following the manufacturers' instructions.

Metabolomics analysis

Serum samples derived from mice with different treatments were sent to QBiO Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. for metabolomic analysis. The analysis was conducted using an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with an AB Triple TOF mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Marlborough, MA, USA).

Measurement of ADO and ATP level and

ADO concentrations in serum, bone marrow (BM), and conditioned medium (CM) were quantified using a commercial ADO Assay Kit (ab211094, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Extracellular ATP levels in primary BMSCs were measured using an Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (S0027, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Luminescence signals were recorded using a luminescence microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and normalized to the total protein concentration, as determined by a BCA protein assay (23225, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA) from the corresponding cell lysates.

Genes knockdown/overexpression in vitro

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting Mus musculus genes Entpd1 (siEntpd1), Nt5e (siNt5e), Adora2a (siAdora2a), Adora2b (siAdora2b), and Adora3 (siAdora3) were purchased from QBiO Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (sequences in Table S4). A non-targeting siRNA (siNC) was used as a negative control. Primary BMSCs at 50–70% confluence were transfected with 100 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (L3000075, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Opti-MEM (11058021, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To overexpress Adora1 in cells in vitro, a total amount of 2 μg plasmid containing full coding sequence of mus musculus Adora1 gene (NCBI ref#: NM_001039510.2, PAdora1) or empty pcDNA3.1(+) vector (PVector) as negative control was transfected into cells by using the protocols as mentioned above when the cells on 6-well plate reached 70−90% confluency. After incubation of cells with the cocktails of transfection reagents for 6 h at 37 °C, the medium was replaced with a complete cell culture medium. RT-qPCR was used to validate gene knockdown or overexpression efficiency.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene knockdown in vivo

Recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors expressing short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting Mus musculus genes Entpd1 (shEntpd1, 5′-CCA AGG ACA TTC AGG TTT CAA CTC GAG TTG AAA CCT GAA TGT CCT TGG-3′), Nt5e (shNt5e, 5′-CAT TTG ACC TCG TCC AAT TAA CTC GAG TTA ATT GGA CGA GGT CAA ATG-3′), and Adora2b (shAdora2b, 5′-CGT GGC TAA CAA ATA CAT TTA CTC GAG TAA ATG TAT TTG TTA GCC ACG-3′), along with a non-targeting AAV vector as negative control (shNC), were produced by QBiO Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. A total of 1 × 1010 viral genomes (v.g.) of AAV were locally injected into the lateral femoral marrow cavity of 3-month-old male wild-type mice according to well-established protocols [33]. Five weeks post-injection and concurrent with the respective experimental treatments, femora were collected for micro-CT analysis. Knockdown efficiency was evaluated by qPCR.

Cell apoptosis detection

The proportion of early and late apoptotic primary BMSCs under different treatment conditions was determined using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (E-CK-A211, Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Briefly, harvested cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer. The cell suspension was then stained with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI), followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Cell populations were analyzed using a BD FACS Celesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Annexin V-FITC+/PI− cells were considered early apoptotic, while those Annexin V-FITC+/PI+ were classified as late apoptotic. Additionally, apoptosis was independently assessed by using a One-step TUNEL In Situ Apoptosis Kit (E-CK-A322, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The nuclei of apoptotic cells were labeled as red by the TUNEL assay. Images of cells were obtained using a confocal laser scanning microscope (STELLARIS 5, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Measurement of mitochondrial ROS levels

Mitochondrial ROS levels were detected using MitoSOX™ Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (M36007, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA) and Hoechst stain (C1011, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Images were acquired by confocal microscopy (STELLARIS 5, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and MitoSOX fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ (version 1.52a, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted from femoral bone tissue using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA purity, concentration, and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (701-058112, Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA sequencing and analysis were performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. Gene expression levels were estimated as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was conducted with significance thresholds set at p < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change)| > 0.58. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes were performed using the online tool (https://cloud.oebiotech.com/#/bio/tools).

Molecular docking analysis

The protein structures of mus musculus A1R (ID: Q60612), A2aR (ID: Q6TLI7), A2bR (ID: Q60614), A3R (ID: Q61618), CD39 (ID: P55772), and CD73 (ID: Q61503) were obtained from the Uniprot database. The structure of ADO (ID: 60961) and DUB (ID: 2971) was retrieved from the PubChem database. The molecular docking between each protein and chemical was performed by using the online cavity-detection guided blind docking tools (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2) [34]. The visualization of the docking results was done by using PyMOL (version 3.1.5.1, Schrödinger, Inc., New York, NY, USA) and Discovery Studio Visualizer (version 25.1.0.24284, BIOVIA, San Diego, CA, USA).

Measurement of cAMP level

Cells were grown at density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 prior to the experiments. The intracellular cAMP level was examined by using a cAMP ELISA Kit (E-EL-0056, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Preparation and characterization of DUB-loaded bone-targeted liposomes

The preparation of DUB-loaded bone-targeted liposomes was performed according to well-established protocols [35]. First, DSPE-PEG2000-ALN was synthesized via an amidation reaction between DSPE-PEG2000-NHS (HY-W441014, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) and alendronate (ALN, HY-108685, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China). Briefly, 1 g of DSPE-PEG2000-NHS was added to an ALN solution containing 307.5 mg of 4-(4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)-4-methylmorpholinium chloride hydrate (DMTMM, HY-Y1168, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China), and the mixture was stirred at 500 rpm for 12 h. The resulting product was dialyzed against deionized water for 2 days and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain DSPE-PEG2000-ALN. DUB@TLip was prepared using a thin-film dispersion method. In brief, DPPC (300 mg), Chol (100 mg), and DSPE-PEG2000-ALN (100 mg) were dissolved in 50 mL of chloroform in a 500 mL flask. DUB was added to the mixture to achieve a final concentration of 5 mg/mL. The organic solvent was evaporated to form a thin lipid film, followed by hydration with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The liposome suspension was then extruded through polycarbonate filters with a 100 nm pore size to obtain the final liposomal nanoparticles. To prepare non-targeted DUB-loaded liposomes, DSPE-PEG2000-ALN was substituted with an equimolar amount of DSPE-mPEG2000 (HY-142978, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) using the same preparation procedure. The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of the liposomes were measured using a Zetasizer ZS90 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK).

For DUB@Lip uptake assay in vitro, primary BMSCs were incubated with DUB@Lip labeled with IR-780 (HY-D1063, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) in culture medium. Cells were harvested at specific time points (0, 1h, 2h, 4h, and 6h post incubation) for the detection of fluorescence by using confocal microscopy (STELLARIS 5, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), or FACS Celesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). For the biodistribution assay in vivo, the DUB@Lip and DUB@TLip (in 100 μL PBS) were labeled with IR-780 dye and given to each mouse via tail vein injection, their bone-targeting efficiency was evaluated via ex vivo imaging strategy. The biodistribution of DUB@Lip and DUB@TLip in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and bone tissues was recoded and quantified at 2 h and 24 h post-injection.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m.), where n means the biological replicates, which represents the number of animals or independent repeats by using different biological samples for in vitro experiments. Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one- or two-way ANOVA. Significance is denoted as follows: not significant (ns) p > 0.05, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 11.0.0, Boston, MA, USA).

DUB is identified as a potential potent drug against GIOP

Given the well-known antioxidative properties of quinonoid compounds [20], which position them as promising therapeutic candidates for GIOP, we acquired a natural product library comprising 153 quinonoid compounds (Table S1) to systematically evaluate their therapeutic potential against GIOP. For primary screening, we selected Runx2, a well-established early marker of osteogenic differentiation, and generated BMSCs stably expressing a luciferase reporter gene driven by the Runx2 promoter (Fig. 1A). In this reporter system, stimulation that activates the Runx2 promoter drives luciferase expression in a manner proportional to endogenous Runx2 transcription. The amount of secreted luciferase in the culture medium can be quantified, thereby serving as an indicator of Runx2 transcriptional activity. Using this reporter cell system, we screened the 153 quinonoid compounds. It was found that 47 compounds significantly up-regulated Runx2 expression compared to the DMSO control, while 28 compounds down-regulated it under osteogenic induction conditions (Fig. 1B). For secondary screening, we extracted primary BMSCs from murine long bones. These primary BMSCs were characterized by flow cytometry as CD34-/CD45- and CD29+/CD90+ (Fig. S1A), which also exhibited both osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation potential (Fig. S1B–C), confirming their identity as healthy multipotent mesenchymal stem cells. We next assessed the effects of the 10 candidate compounds on the activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), another definitive functional marker of early osteogenic differentiation. It is found that dexamethasone (Dex) treatment markedly suppressed ALP activity in BMSCs; notably, several candidate compounds significantly reversed this Dex-induced inhibitory effect, highlighting their therapeutic potential for GIOP (Fig. 1C). The top 3 compounds with the most robust rescue effect on ALP activity were selected for the final round of screening.

For the tertiary screening, we focused on the antioxidant capacity of the 3 lead compounds, given the well-established causal role of oxidative stress in glucocorticoid-induced BMSC dysfunction [36]. As expected, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were significantly elevated in BMSCs following Dex exposure. Among the 3 lead candidates, DUB exhibited the most potent antioxidant effect, reducing Dex-induced ROS overproduction by 47.1% (Fig. 1D and E). This finding identifies DUB as the most promising therapeutic candidate from the initial 153 quinonoid compounds screened.

We further evaluated the cytotoxicity profile of DUB. Following incubation of primary BMSCs with DUB for 2 and 10 days, no significant cytotoxicity was observed across the tested concentrations (Fig. S1D). In addition, while Dex markedly inhibited BMSC proliferation, DUB restored cell proliferative capacity in a concentration-dependent manner, with the maximal rescue effect detected at 10 μM; no adverse effects on cell viability were noted even at higher concentrations (Fig. 1F). We next examined the effects of DUB on the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation potential of primary BMSCs in vitro. Dex treatment significantly suppressed osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and this inhibitory effect was dose-dependently reversed by DUB, with the most potent pro-osteogenic effect observed at 10 μM (Fig. 1G). Consistent with the phenotypic changes, Dex-induced downregulation of both mRNA and protein levels of key osteogenesis-related genes was significantly restored by DUB in primary BMSCs (Fig. 1H and S2A). Concurrently, DUB dose-dependently inhibited the aberrant adipogenic differentiation of primary BMSCs triggered by Dex exposure (Fig. 1I and S2B). Beyond the regulatory effects pf DUB on BMSC fate commitment, we further investigated the impact of DUB on osteoclastogenesis, another core pathological driver of GIOP. Using bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) as osteoclast precursors, we found that Dex treatment markedly enhanced osteoclast formation. Notably, DUB treatment suppressed Dex-augmented osteoclast differentiation (Fig. S2C–D). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that DUB protects BMSCs from glucocorticoid-induced dysfunction, alleviates oxidative stress, promotes osteogenesis, suppresses adipogenesis, and inhibits pathological osteoclast activation, supporting its potential as a novel therapeutic candidate for GIOP.

DUB alleviates glucocorticoid-induced bone loss and oxidative stress in vivo

Prior to in vivo therapeutic efficacy assessment, we first evaluated the in vivo biosafety profile of DUB. C57BL/6 mice were administered low-dose DUB (10 mg/kg), high-dose DUB (30 mg/kg), or corn oil as vehicle control via oral gavage for 5 consecutive weeks, with untreated mice included as blank control. At the end of the dosing cycle, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) showed no obvious organic abnormalities in either DUB dose group (Fig. S3A). Longitudinal body weight monitoring also revealed no significant abnormal fluctuations or growth inhibition in DUB-treated mice across the 5-week administration period (Fig. S3B). These results confirmed that DUB has excellent biocompatibility, supporting the dose selection for the therapeutic examination.

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of DUB on bone microstructure under GIOP conditions, mice receiving Dex for 5 weeks were co-administered either DUB (10 or 30 mg/kg) or an equivalent volume of vehicle control. As expected, Dex treatment induced a severe and significant reduction in both trabecular and cortical bone mass in the distal femora of mice. In contrast, DUB supplementation dose-dependently attenuated Dex-induced bone loss, with the 30 mg/kg high-dose group exhibiting the most robust protective effect. Specifically, DUB treatment significantly increased the key trabecular bone parameters, including trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), while markedly reducing trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and caused no significant change in cortical bone thickness (Ct.Th) in Dex-treated mice (Fig. 2A and B). Consistent with the micro-CT findings, histological staining revealed that DUB dose-dependently rescued the Dex-induced decline in trabecular bone area and collagen deposition fraction in the femoral metaphysis (Fig. 2C and D). We further assessed systemic oxidative stress levels via quantification of established serum biomarkers. Dex treatment significantly elevated the circulating levels of classic oxidative damage markers, including 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), malondialdehyde (MDA), and advanced oxidation protein products (AOPP). DUB elevates ADO level in serum and bone marrow microenvironment via CD39/CD73 axis in GIOP

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the protective effects of DUB against GIOP, we performed metabolomic sequencing on serum from control and GIOP mice, with or without DUB (30 mg/kg) treatment (Fig. S4A). Compared with control mice, Dex-treated mice exhibited 24 significantly upregulated and 12 significantly downregulated metabolites in the serum (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, in mice co-treated with Dex and DUB, 15 metabolites were significantly upregulated and 6 were downregulated relative to Dex-alone mice (Fig. 3A). Venn analysis of these differentially expressed metabolites identified an 8-metabolite overlap between the Dex vs. control and Dex + DUB vs. Dex comparisons (Fig. 3B). Notably, among these 8 overlapping metabolites, ADO showed the most pronounced reversal by DUB. Circulating ADO levels were significantly depleted in Dex-treated mice relative to controls, and this depletion was restored by DUB administration, with a 687% increase in serum ADO levels in the DUB group relative to the Dex-alone group (Fig. 3C and S4B). We further validated these metabolomic findings using ELISA, which confirmed the rescue effect of DUB on circulating ADO levels (Fig. 3D). Given that the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment is the primary niche for BMSC-mediated bone formation, we additionally quantified ADO levels in BM flush samples. Consistent with the serum findings, Dex treatment induced a marked reduction in local ADO levels within the BM cavity, which was also restored by DUB (Fig. 3D). Extracellular ADO is predominantly generated via the sequential dephosphorylation of extracellular ATP (eATP) by two rate-limiting ectonucleotidases: CD39, which converts eATP to adenosine monophosphate (AMP), and CD73, which catalyzes the final conversion of AMP to ADO. The CD39/CD73/ADO axis is highly expressed on BMSCs and acts as a central regulator of purinergic signaling in the bone marrow niche [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. We therefore hypothesized that DUB restores ADO levels in the GIOP bone microenvironment by rescuing glucocorticoid-induced impairment of the CD39/CD73 axis. To test this, we first quantified eATP levels in BM flush fluid, and found that Dex-induced eATP accumulation was dose-dependently reversed by DUB (Fig. 3E). We next investigated whether this altered exATP turnover was associated with CD39 and CD73 expression on BMSCs. Western blot analysis showed that Dex downregulated both CD39 and CD73 protein levels in primary BMSCs, and DUB treatment reversed this suppression (Fig. 3F). Consistently, mRNA levels of Entpd1 (encoding CD39) and Nt5e (encoding CD73) exhibited similar trends in response to Dex and DUB treatments (Fig. 3G).

We next sought to clarify the upstream mechanism through which DUB regulates Entpd1 and Nt5e transcription. We first ruled out direct binding between DUB and CD39/CD73 via molecular docking analysis, which showed extremely weak binding affinity (Fig. S4C). Given the core role of redox-sensitive pathways in GIOP pathogenesis, we further investigated the involvement of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), a well-established upstream transcriptional regulator of Entpd1 and Nt5e [42]. It is found that DUB significantly reversed Dex-induced HIF-1α downregulation, and the specific HIF-1α inhibitor PX-478 completely blocked the upregulatory effect of DUB on CD39 and CD73 expression (Fig. S4D). These findings suggested that DUB may regulate CD39/CD73 transcription indirectly via the redox-sensitive HIF-1α signaling pathway, rather than via direct transcriptional activation or protein targeting.

To further functionally validate that DUB modulates eATP-to-ADO conversion directly via BMSC-expressed CD39/CD73, we first quantified eATP and ADO levels in the conditioned medium (CM) of primary BMSCs. The results aligned with the bone marrow observations: Dex increased exATP and decreased ADO, while DUB counteracted these changes, supporting the role of BMSC-derived CD39/CD73 in mediating the DUB-driven ADO production (Fig. 3H). To validate this causal relationship between ADO and CD39/CD73 expression, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of CD39 and CD73 in primary BMSCs (Fig. 3I). Notably, knockdown of either CD39 or CD73 resulted in elevated ATP and reduced ADO levels in the conditioned medium in primary BMSCs co-treated with Dex and DUB, suggesting the beneficial effects of DUB on ADO production were abolished in CD39−or CD73-deficient cells (Fig. 3J). Taken together, these results demonstrated that DUB mitigates glucocorticoid-induced bone impairment primarily by rescuing the suppressed CD39/CD73 ectonucleotidase cascade, thereby restoring systemic and local bone marrow ADO levels, the core effector metabolite of its therapeutic effects.

Notably, when CD39 or CD73 was individually knocked down by siRNAs, the ability of DUB to mitigate Dex-induced oxidative stress was markedly impaired, demonstrating that DUB's antioxidative effect in primary BMSCs depends on both CD39 and CD73 (Fig. 4A). Increased intracellular oxidative stress often triggers apoptosis. We therefore evaluated apoptosis levels in primary BMSCs under different treatments. Dex treatment downregulated the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and upregulated the pro-apoptotic markers Bax, cleaved Caspase-3, and cytoplasmic Cytochrome C, indicating the induction of apoptosis in primary BMSCs (Fig. 4D). DUB treatment reversed these changes, suggesting a protective effect against Dex-induced apoptosis (Fig. 4D). This beneficial effect of DUB was significantly attenuated upon knockdown of CD39 or CD73 (Fig. 4D). Consistent with these findings, Annexin V/PI staining confirmed that the reduction in late apoptotic (Annexin V+PI+) primary BMSCs by DUB was dependent on CD39 and CD73 (Fig. 4E). Similarly, TUNEL assay showed that the ability of DUB to clear Dex-induced TUNEL+ cells was also abolished in CD39−or CD73-deficient primary BMSCs (Fig. 4F). Together, these results demonstrate that the anti-apoptotic effect of DUB in BMSCs requires the CD39/CD73 axis.

We next investigated whether CD39 and CD73 mediate the effects of DUB on osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation in primary BMSCs. Knockdown of CD39 or CD73 significantly impaired the ability of DUB to restore osteogenic differentiation compromised by Dex, as shown by Alizarin Red S staining, protein and mRNA expression levels of osteogenic related genes (Fig. 4G–H and S5A). Similarly, the suppressive effect of DUB on Dex-induced adipogenic differentiation was also attenuated, with increased lipid accumulation and elevated expression of adipogenic marker genes observed in CD39−or CD73-deficient cells even in the presence of DUB (Fig. S5B–C). In summary, these findings indicate that the multiple protective effects of DUB against Dex-induced damage in BMSCs, including antioxidative, anti-apoptotic, pro-osteogenic, and anti-adipogenic actions, are dependent on an intact CD39/CD73 axis.

Deficiency in CD39/CD73 impairs DUB's therapeutic effect on GIOP in vivo

Given the demonstrated dependence of DUB's multidirectional benefits in Dex-treated primary BMSCs on both CD39 and CD73, we next sought to validate the roles of these ectoenzymes in the therapeutic effect of DUB against GIOP in vivo. We performed knockdown of CD39 or CD73 in the bones of mice using adeno-associated virus (AAVs) during co-treatment with Dex and DUB. We first systematically validated the AAV silencing system. qPCR analysis confirmed highly efficient target gene silencing, with over 70% knockdown efficiency in whole femoral bone tissue and bone marrow cells (Fig. S6A–B). Fluorescence staining verified efficient AAV transduction in the bone marrow niche and specific silencing of CD39/CD73 in the local microenvironment (Fig. S6C–D). No significant off-target gene silencing was detected in major non-bone organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney), which may be attributed to the inherent bone tropism of the AAV9 serotype, the local intramedullary injection route, and the physical barrier of the dense bone matrix that limits systemic viral spread.

Using this validated system, we next evaluated the impact of CD39/CD73 silencing on the skeletal protective effect of DUB via micro-CT analysis. As expected, DUB treatment exerted a robust protective effect against Dex-induced trabecular and cortical bone loss in AAV-shNC-injected control femora. Strikingly, this protective effect was completely abolished in femora with AAV-mediated silencing of either Entpd1 or Nt5e, as evidenced by a significant reduction in key trabecular bone parameters, including Tb.BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, along with a marked increase in Tb.Sp, in CD39−or CD73-deficient femora compared with AAV-shNC control femora (Fig. S7A–B). Consistently, histological and functional assessments, including H&E staining for trabecular bone area, Masson staining for collagen deposition, immunohistochemistry for Ocn+ osteoblasts lining on the surface of trabeculae, and calcein double labeling for bone formation rate, confirmed that the capacity of DUB to counteract Dex-induced bone deterioration was compromised upon CD39 or CD73 knockdown (Fig. S7C–F). Taking together, these findings demonstrate that the therapeutic efficacy of DUB against GIOP in vivo depends on intact CD39/CD73 axis. The attenuated therapeutic effect of DUB on GIOP following CD39/CD73 deficiency is likely attributable to the impaired ADO production caused by the disruption of this core ectonucleotidase cascade.

Exogenous ADO supplement mimics DUB's multidirectional beneficial effects on Dex-treated primary BMSCs in vitro

Given the confirmed role of DUB in enhancing ADO level in serum, bone marrow in vivo, and conditional medium of primary BMSCs in vitro via CD39/CD73 pathway, we asked whether direct ADO supplementation could reproduce DUB's protective actions in glucocorticoid-stressed BMSCs. We first assessed the effect of ADO on the proliferation of primary BMSCs under Dex exposure. ADO enhanced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with maximal benefit observed at 5 μM and no adverse effects at higher concentrations (Fig. 5A). We next assessed the regulatory effect of ADO on the aberrant lineage commitment of Dex-treated BMSCs. ADO restored osteogenic potential in a dose-dependent manner, as indicated by Alizarin Red S staining (Fig. 5B and C). Consistent with this functional recovery, ADO significantly upregulated the expression of osteogenic related genes at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 5D and S8A). Furthermore, ADO effectively suppressed the Dex-induced promotion of adipogenic differentiation (Fig. S8B–C). We further investigated the antioxidative capacity of ADO. Similar to DUB, ADO attenuated Dex-induced oxidative stress by reducing intracellular levels of 8-OHdG, MDA, and AOPP and restoring T-SOD activity (Fig. 5E). A2bR is essential for the ADO-mediated activation of cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling cascade in primary BMSCs

To elucidate the downstream mechanism by which DUB restores Dex-impaired BMSC function through CD39/CD73-derived ADO, we performed RNA sequencing on primary BMSCs treated with Dex alone or in combination with ADO (Fig. 6A). Transcriptomic analysis revealed that ADO treatment under Dex exposure led to significant upregulation of 38 genes and downregulation of 56 genes compared to Dex treatment alone (Fig. 6B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that ADO influenced multiple biological processes related to oxidative stress, apoptosis, proliferation, and differentiation in BMSCs (Fig. 6C), consistent with our previous findings. Since ADO is known to transduce signal through four G protein-coupled receptors, A1R, A2aR, A2bR, and A3R [43], we sought to identify which receptor mediates its effects in primary BMSCs. Molecular docking analysis revealed that A2bR has the highest binding affinity for ADO (binding energy = −7.2 kcal/mol), suggesting it as the most likely functional receptor (Fig. 6D). Interestingly, Dex treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA level of Adora1 (encoding A1R) while downregulating Adora2a, Adora2b, and Adora3 (encoding A2aR, A2bR, and A3R, respectively), with Adora2b showing the most pronounced reduction (Fig. 6E). ADO supplementation did not alter the expression of any of these receptors under Dex conditions (Fig. 6E). These observations align with previous reports suggesting that A1R may inhibit osteogenesis, whereas A2aR, A2bR, and A3R likely serve as positive regulators of bone formation [43]. To functionally validate the role of each receptor, we overexpressed A1R or knockdown A2aR, A2bR, or A3R in primary BMSCs under Dex treatment and assessed the effect of ADO on the expression of osteogenesis related gene Runx2. Among all perturbations, only A2bR knockdown abolished the ability of ADO to restore Runx2 expression (Fig. 6F). To further investigate the mechanism underlying ADO's effects, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the cAMP signaling pathway, which was identified by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the differential expression genes (DEGs) between Dex alone and Dex + ADO groups. The results revealed that the cAMP pathway was restored by ADO in Dex-treated primary BMSCs (Fig. 6G). Given our finding that A2bR serves as the primary receptor mediating ADO's effects in Dex-stressed BMSCs, we examined intracellular cAMP levels following A2bR knockdown (Fig. 6H). Dex treatment significantly reduced intracellular cAMP levels in BMSCs, and this reduction was markedly reversed by ADO supplementation (Fig. 6I). Critically, the ability of ADO to restore cAMP levels was completely abrogated upon A2bR knockdown (Fig. 6I), demonstrating that ADO regulates intracellular cAMP production primarily via binding to A2bR.

The cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling cascade is a well-established master regulator of bone metabolism, and CREB activation has been shown to directly drive the transcription of key osteogenic genes [44,45]. We therefore investigated whether this cascade mediates the therapeutic actions of ADO in Dex-stressed BMSCs. We found that Dex treatment suppressed both PKA and CREB signaling pathways while ADO restored their activity. Importantly, this restorative effect was abolished upon A2bR knockdown (Fig. 6J), confirming that ADO activates PKA and CREB in an A2bR-dependent manner. To further validate this mechanism, we used Rp-cAMPS, a membrane-permeable agent that blocks PKA activation [46]. Treatment with Rp-cAMPS significantly attenuated ADO-induced CREB activation (Fig. 6J), confirming that ADO activates CREB through the cAMP/PKA axis.

Finally, we evaluated whether A2bR and PKA activation were required for ADO-mediated regulation of BMSC differentiation. Both A2bR knockdown and PKA inhibition by Rp-cAMPS abolished the ability of ADO to restore osteogenic differentiation in Dex-treated BMSCs (Fig. 6K and S9A-B). Concurrently, ADO's suppressive effect on Dex-induced adipogenic differentiation was also significantly compromised under these conditions (Fig. S9C–D). These findings collectively demonstrate that ADO redirects the differentiation fate of BMSCs toward osteogenesis and away from adipogenesis through the A2bR/cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling cascade.

Bone-targeted liposomal delivery system enhances the therapeutic efficacy of DUB against GIOP in vivo

Having established robust skeletal protective effects and detailed molecular mechanism of free DUB against GIOP, we next sought to address the clinical translational limitation of DUB: its intrinsic lipophilicity may lead to insufficient bioavailability and poor bone tissue targeting in vivo. To overcome this challenge, we designed and fabricated two liposome-based delivery systems, the non-bone-targeted DUB-loaded liposomes (DUB@Lip) and bone-targeted DUB-loaded liposomes (DUB@TLip) with bone-homing modification. The zeta potential of DUB@TLip was significantly lower than that of DUB@Lip due to the ALN modification (for the purpose of bone targeting), which has a negative charge (Fig. S11C). There were no significant changes in particle size or dispersion state observed after 7 days of storage in physiological buffer (Fig. S11D–E). These results confirmed the excellent colloidal stability and uniform physicochemical properties of both liposomal formulations, laying a solid foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo applications.

Discussion

GIOP remains the most common cause of secondary osteoporosis in clinical practice, with a pathological framework defined by two synergistic irreversible core defects: excessive oxidative stress and sustained suppression of osteogenic bone formation. Clinical longitudinal studies have shown that even with long-term low-dose glucocorticoid therapy, the risk of vertebral fractures increases by 2-3 times, and this risk is positively correlated with the duration of treatment [47]. GCs have several direct effects on osteoblast signalling pathways, such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ2 (PPARγ2), Kruppel-like factor 15 (KLF15), CCAAT/enhancer binding protein-α (C/EBPα), adipocyte protein 2 (aP2), and canonical Wnt signalling [[48], [49], [50]]. These effects result in impaired osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs while promoting adipogenesis, and this imbalance in cell fate determination is a key factor in the limited bone formation in GIOP. Current first-line anti-resorptive therapies, including bisphosphonates and denosumab, fail to address these two core pathological drivers, resulting in limited long-term efficacy in halting progressive bone deterioration in patients receiving long-term GC therapy. Therefore, there is an urgent unmet clinical need to develop mechanism-driven, dual-action anabolic agents that can simultaneously mitigate oxidative damage and restore impaired bone formation in GIOP. In this study, we conducted a systematic, multi-round high-throughput screening of a 153-member quinonoid compound library, and identified DUB as a potent, safe anabolic candidate against GIOP for the first time. We systematically demonstrated that DUB not only robustly reversed GC-induced suppression of osteogenic differentiation and aberrant adipogenic commitment of BMSCs, but also exerted powerful antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects in vitro and in vivo. Beyond its regulatory effects on osteoblast lineage cells, we further revealed that DUB significantly inhibited GC-augmented pathological osteoclastogenesis, achieving a dual regulatory effect on the uncoupled bone remodeling cycle in GIOP.

Oxidative stress and apoptosis are pivotal mechanisms in the development of GIOP [51]. Sustained elevation of glucocorticoid levels attenuates the interaction between glucocorticoid receptors (GRs)/B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) complexes and mitochondria, leading to electron leakage from the respiratory chain [52]. Most of these electrons react with oxygen within mitochondria to generate superoxide, thereby enhancing oxidative stress [53]. Concurrently, Dex can indirectly induce oxidative stress by depleting antioxidant molecules or suppressing antioxidant enzymes [54,55]. Research has reported that ROS-induced activation of the PKCβ/p66shc/JNK signaling cascade is a mechanism by which Dex promotes osteoblast apoptosis [56]. Consequently, GCs dual-induce osteoblast apoptosis via both the oxidative stress pathway and the mitochondrial pathway, further exacerbating impaired bone formation, an effect positively correlated with glucocorticoid dosage [57]. This study demonstrates that DUB effectively scavenges intracellular ROS and significantly reduces mitochondrial superoxide levels. Regarding anti-apoptotic regulation, DUB decreases the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins such as Bax, Cleaved-caspase-3, and Cytochrome c, as well as the proportion of apoptotic cells. This dual protective effect of DUB offers a novel perspective on the multi-target therapeutic mechanisms of natural compounds against GIOP.

The most groundbreaking finding of our study is the identification of the CD39/CD73/ADO axis as the core pharmacological target of DUB in GIOP, which fills a critical research gap in the field. The selection of this purinergic signaling cascade as the focus of our mechanistic exploration was evidence-based, driven by unbiased metabolomic findings, established pathological theory, and clinical translational relevance. First, untargeted serum metabolomic sequencing directly revealed that ADO was the most significantly depleted metabolite in the GIOP model, and the one most robustly restored by DUB treatment, which provided the initial, unbiased clue guiding our mechanistic investigation. Second, the CD39 and CD73 are the master rate-limiting system governing extracellular ADO production in the tissue microenvironment, and has been implicated in extracellular purine metabolism, controlling cellular homeostasis by integrating extracellular purinergic signaling with gene transcription, and are associated with cell proliferation, differentiation, and death [58]. When ATP is released from stressed or damaged cells into the extracellular space, it is rapidly hydrolyzed to ADO by CD39 and CD73 [59]. The CD39/CD73/ADO signaling pathway is altered in osteoporosis. Previous study had indicated that in ovariectomized animals, the expression of CD39 and CD73 in the bone marrow and extracellular ADO levels are significantly reduced [29]. Concurrently, research in apical periodontitis models shows that excessive caffeine intake (an ADO antagonist) leads to increased IL-1β and bone resorption [60]. Conversely, application of dipyridamole, which inhibits equilibrative nucleoside transporter (ENT) uptake into cells, thereby elevating extracellular ADO concentrations, reduces bone loss in ovariectomized animals [61]. In addition, we revealed that DUB does not directly bind to CD39 or CD73, but rather upregulates their transcription indirectly via restoring GC-suppressed HIF-1α signaling, a well-established upstream transcriptional regulator of the two ectonucleotidases [62]. This finding breaks through the conventional view that DUB and other ubiquinone derivatives act solely through direct mitochondrial free radical scavenging, revealing a previously unrecognized non-antioxidant pharmacological mechanism of DUB.

ADO is produced extracellularly through the hydrolysis of adenine nucleotides and exerts its physiological effects by activating specific cell surface G protein-coupled receptors (A1R, A2aR, A2bR, and A3R) [63]. Emerging evidence indicates that ADO attenuates oxidative stress and apoptosis across multiple tissue types [64,65]. The A2bR serves as the predominant receptor in osteoprogenitor cells [66]. Compared to wild-type (WT) mice, A2bR knockout mice exhibit reduced BMSCs differentiation into osteoblasts, decreased expression of osteoblast-related transcription factors, and diminished mineralized nodule formation [67]. When MC3T3-E1 cells overexpress CD73, the expression of osteocalcin and bone sialoprotein increases; however, this increase is abolished upon blockade of the A2bR [68]. In addition, previous research has reported that osteoblastic differentiation is mediated by a cAMP-dependent mechanism, as both activation of A2bR and the addition of a cAMP analog enhance osteoblastic differentiation [67]. Our research further reveals that ADO promotes the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by binding to A2bR and activating the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway. The therapeutic effect of ADO on GIOP was eliminated after the blockade of A2bR. Therefore, it is demonstrated that DUB treatment exerts a dual therapeutic effect on GIOP: on one hand, it upregulates CD39/CD73 and increases ADO levels to eliminate oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by glucocorticoids; on the other hand, the ADO binds to A2bR and activates the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway, which promote osteogenesis-related gene expression.

From a clinical translational perspective, our study provides multiple compelling advances for the management of GIOP. Unlike conventional single-target anti-resorptive agents, DUB simultaneously targets the three core pathological features of GIOP: excessive oxidative stress, suppressed osteogenesis, and augmented osteoclastogenesis, offering a more comprehensive therapeutic strategy than currently available treatments. To address the potential bioavailability limitation of DUB due to its intrinsic lipophilicity, we further developed and validated a bone-targeted liposomal delivery system (DUB@TLip) with alendronate modification, which significantly enhanced the bone-homing capacity and in vivo therapeutic efficacy of DUB. This biomaterial-based delivery strategy improves the pharmacokinetic profile of DUB, bridging the gap between basic pharmacological discovery and clinical translation. Notably, DUB is a naturally occurring ubiquinone derivative with a well-characterized safety profile in preclinical studies, and our comprehensive biosafety assessment confirmed no obvious systemic toxicity of DUB or its liposomal formulation in mice, further supporting its translational potential.

Although this study centers on the screening of quinonoid compounds for GIOP treatment and the mechanistic elucidation of DUB, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, the therapeutic potential of DUB may extend beyond GIOP to other types of osteoporosis (e.g., postmenopausal osteoporosis and age-related osteoporosis) and other glucocorticoid-induced musculoskeletal disorders (e.g., muscle atrophy and osteonecrosis), which warrant systematic evaluation in future studies. Second, our work focused on the regulatory effects of DUB on BMSCs and osteoblast lineage cells. While we have demonstrated the inhibitory effect of DUB on osteoclastogenesis, the detailed cell-autonomous and non-cell-autonomous mechanisms underlying this effect require further in-depth exploration. Third, the potential role of DUB in promoting the healing of GIOP-related fragility fractures needs further comprehensive evaluation. Finally, given the central role of A2bR in alleviating GIOP progression and directing BMSC lineage commitment, targeted interventions against A2bR represent a promising avenue for future skeletal disease research.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identifies DUB as a potent anabolic agent against GIOP, wherein DUB reverses dexamethasone-induced osteogenic suppression, adipogenic promotion, oxidative stress and apoptosis in BMSCs in vitro, mitigates bone loss in a murine GIOP model in vivo, and overcomes the intrinsic lipophilicity-related bioavailability limitation of DUB via a newly developed bone-targeted DUB-loaded liposomal delivery system that significantly enhances the bone-homing capacity and in vivo therapeutic efficacy of DUB. Mechanistically, DUB restores the GC-impaired CD39/CD73/ADO axis to elevate extracellular ADO levels, which in turn eliminates ROS, activates A2bR, and triggers the downstream cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling cascade to promote osteogenic differentiation and bone formation (Fig. 9). This study provides new insights into DUB's biological function and mechanism, establishes a basis for understanding purinergic signaling in bone homeostasis, and lays the groundwork for potential application of its bone-targeted DUB-loaded liposomal formulation.
