Rotation of microtextured anatomical breast implants: A retrospective series of 134 reconstructions
Authors: Tarik Ihrai, Alice Roosen, Dominique Lanvin, Philippe Giordano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnatomically shaped breast implants are widely used in implant-based breast reconstruction because of their ability to restore breast contour and projection. Following regulatory restrictions on macrotextured implants, microtextured anatomical implants have emerged as an alternative. However, concerns remain regarding their positional stability, particularly the risk of implant rotation.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of patients who underwent immediate or delayed implant-based breast reconstruction using microtextured anatomical breast implants between December 2020 and October 2025. All procedures were performed by a single experienced plastic surgeon. Implant rotation and postoperative complications were recorded. Outcomes were analysed on a per-patient basis.ResultsA total of 134 breast reconstructions were performed in 100 patients, with a mean follow-up of 15.5 months. Four cases of implant rotation occurred in four patients, resulting in an overall rotation rate of 4%. Implant rotation was observed exclusively in revision procedures or during second-stage reconstruction. Three cases were successfully managed by external manipulation, and one required surgical revision. Other complication rates were consistent with those reported in the literature.ConclusionIn this retrospective series, the microtextured anatomical breast implants used in the study, characterized by ultra-low surface roughness, were associated with a low rate of clinically detectable rotation in implant-based breast reconstruction. When combined with meticulous surgical technique and appropriate pocket management, satisfactory positional stability was observed in this cohort.

Introduction

Implant-based breast reconstruction is a standard option after mastectomy. Anatomically shaped implants have been widely adopted in this setting, owing to their ability to restore breast contour and projection. Their use in breast reconstruction is supported by clinical studies reporting well-defined indications, reproducible surgical techniques, and satisfactory aesthetic outcomes.1,2

Although the literature remains inconclusive regarding a clear morphological superiority of anatomically shaped implants in aesthetic breast augmentation, their use in breast reconstruction may offer specific advantages in selected patients. In particular, the different height-to-width ratios and implant footprints available with anatomical devices may help better reproduce the native contralateral breast morphology and improve breast symmetry, potentially reducing the need for contralateral symmetrization procedures. This interpretation is consistent with the findings reported by Khavanin et al. in a large reconstructive series involving >800 patients.3

Anatomically shaped breast implants emerged in the early 1990s with the introduction of highly cohesive, form-stable silicone gel devices. From their inception, these implants were predominantly coupled with a rough/textured outer shell surface, which was intended to promote tissue adherence and stabilize the implant within the pocket, thereby minimizing malposition and, in particular, rotational instability. Texturing had also been developed in response to earlier generations of implants that were associated with high rates of capsular contracture, with the goal of improving periprosthetic tissue integration and reducing fibrotic complication.4

However, the ability of macrotextured implant surfaces to reduce the risk of capsular contracture has been increasingly questioned. Long-term data from augmentation surgery report capsular contracture rates that may exceed 17% at 10 years with this kind of implant.5 In addition, the effectiveness of macrotextured surfaces in preventing implant rotation in the frontal plane appears debatable, with rotation rates reported in breast augmentation ranging from 3% to >16%, depending on the study.6, 7, 8 The double capsule phenomenon, classically associated with macrotextured breast implants, has been identified as a contributing factor in these high malrotation rates.9

Moreover, over the past decade, large-scale epidemiological studies have confirmed an association between certain macrotextured breast implant surfaces and the occurrence of breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma.10 In response to these findings, the French National Agency for the Safety of Medicines and Health Products implemented regulatory measures in 2019, leading to the withdrawal from the market of macrotextured and polyurethane-coated breast implants in France. Similar regulatory decisions were subsequently adopted in other countries, resulting in comparable restrictions on the use of these implant surfaces at an international level.11

In this context, the available alternatives in France for preserving the advantages of anatomically shaped implants consist of microtextured anatomical breast implants. Given the very low surface roughness of these implants, the question of their positional stability may legitimately be raised, particularly with regard to the risk of implant malrotation. We therefore report the results of a retrospective study designed to evaluate the risk of implant malrotation associated with microtextured anatomical implants in breast reconstruction.

Material and methods

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of all patients who underwent immediate or delayed implant-based breast reconstruction at our institution between December 2020 and October 2025.

All procedures were performed by a single experienced plastic surgeon, ensuring consistency of surgical technique. In all cases, implants were placed in a retropectoral position, with muscular coverage of the upper pole and subcutaneous coverage of the lower pole. The inferior edge of the muscle was fixed to the subcutaneous tissue to optimize muscular coverage, with care taken to avoid tension and excessive compression of the implant. No biological or synthetic matrices were used. Antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of intravenous cefazolin, as recommended by the French Society of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine (SFAR) ,12 without postoperative oral antibiotic prophylaxis.

The implants used were microtextured anatomical breast implants with a surface roughness (Sa) of 4 µm (Fig. 1) (SoftOne, Laboratoires ARION, Mougins, France). Depending on implant shape and projection, either monogel or bigel devices were used. Bigel implants featured a softer posterior gel, in contact with the chest wall, and a firmer anterior gel. Patients underwent regular postoperative follow-up and were specifically informed about the possibility of implant rotation, with instructions to return for consultation in case of any change in breast shape.

Data were collected from operative reports, follow-up visit records, and radiology reports. Demographic data were analysed using descriptive statistics. Complications, including implant rotation, were calculated on a per-patient basis using absolute rates. Only clinically significant seromas requiring puncture or surgical management were recorded as complications. Minor postoperative fluid collections that resolved spontaneously and did not require intervention were not recorded. Statistical analysis was performed using the software Medcalc (Version 23.4, Mariakerke, Belgium).

In accordance with French legislation on retrospective studies, formal approval from an ethics committee was not required, but patients were informed of their right to object to the use of their data. The study was registered on the national Health Data Hub platform (study No 25,941,942).

Results

We analysed outcomes from 134 breast reconstructions performed in 100 patients at our institution. The mean age at the time of surgery was 52.2 years (SD = 13.1). Prior chest wall irradiation before breast reconstruction was present in 19.4% of reconstructed breasts, and 18.7% of reconstructions were prophylactic.

The most frequently used mastectomy type was nipple-sparing mastectomy (41.8%), followed by skin-sparing mastectomy (25.4%). Most reconstructions were one-stage (83.6%) and immediate (86.6%). Revision procedures accounted for 32.1% of reconstructions. The indications for surgical revision were implant rupture, Baker grade III or IV capsular contracture, or unsatisfactory cosmetic results. Closed-suction postoperative drainage was used in 73.1% of reconstructions.

The mean implant volume was 365.4 mL (SD = 91.1 mL). Insertion funnels were used in 89.6% of cases. Concomitant lipofilling was performed in 12.7% of surgeries; however, it was never performed concomitantly with mastectomy, i.e., during immediate reconstruction. When secondary fat grafting was required to improve soft-tissue coverage or breast contour, it was performed at least 3 months after reconstruction. Fat was injected in the plane between the skin and the periprosthetic capsule. Adjuvant radiotherapy was administered to 19.4% of operated breasts.

The mean follow-up duration was 15.5 months. A total of four implant rotations occurred in four different patients, resulting in an overall rotation rate of 4% per patient at the latest follow-up. Implant rotations were observed at 3, 4, 5, and 26 months postoperatively. These events occurred in two cases of revision reconstruction and in two second-stage procedures of two-stage reconstructions. Three rotations were successfully corrected by external manipulation, while one case required surgical revision.

Other complications included capsular contracture (Baker grades II, III, and IV) in five patients, partial areolar necrosis in three patients, delayed wound healing in three patients, implant extrusion in two patients, as well as one implant rupture occurring at 22 months and one case of seroma. Four of the five cases of capsular contracture, both cases of implant extrusion, and two of the three cases of partial areolar necrosis occurred after adjuvant radiotherapy. Among the three cases of delayed wound healing, one occurred after adjuvant radiotherapy and one after prior irradiation. Discussion

Despite the well-established benefits of anatomically shaped implants in breast reconstruction,1,2 the issue of implant malrotation legitimately arises, as it represents a potential additional complication compared with those already encountered with round implants.

Historically, anatomical implants have been predominantly associated with textured surfaces, with the aim of improving positional stability and reducing the risk of implant malposition.4 However, the use of macrotextured surfaces has increasingly been called into question and has even been banned in several countries due to their association with breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma.11 In this evolving regulatory and clinical context, microtextured anatomical implants emerge as a logical alternative for breast reconstruction, allowing surgeons to retain the morphological advantages of anatomical devices while limiting exposure to higher-risk surface textures.

Within this framework, we report the results of a retrospective series of 134 breast reconstructions performed in 100 patients, specifically designed to evaluate the risk of implant rotation associated with microtextured anatomical implants.

The literature is relatively extensive regarding the risk of breast implant rotation. However, data specifically addressing anatomical microtextured implants with ultra-low surface roughness, such as those used in the present study, remain scarce. Even with macrotextured implants, which have since been withdrawn from the French market, most notably the Biocell surface (Allergan Inc., Irvine, CA, USA), clinically significant rotation rates have been reported in breast augmentation surgery. In this context, Schots et al. reported a particularly high rotation rate of 16.4% in a series of 73 primary breast augmentations.7 Montemurro et al., by contrast, observed a lower but still non-negligible rotation rate of 3.6% in a much larger cohort of 531 primary augmentations, predominantly using implants with the same Biocell macrotextured surface.8

Detailed data are also available regarding implant rotation in breast reconstruction. Lee et al. reported a rotation rate exceeding 14% in a cohort of 118 patients undergoing two-stage breast reconstruction.13 Their study involved implants with a Siltex surface (Mentor Worldwide LLC, Irvine, CA, USA), which, although classified as microtextured, exhibit a substantially higher surface roughness than the implants evaluated in the present study (30 to 40 µm14). Finally, Hammond et al. described rotation rates of 6.3% in primary reconstructions and 5.7% in revision procedures in a series of 259 patients, using the same anatomical implants.15

In light of the available literature, the rotation rate observed in our series (4%) appears particularly low. Surgical technique likely plays a major role in reducing the risk of implant malrotation. In particular, careful adjustment of the implant pocket to the device, whenever feasible, either through reduction of the skin envelope or by selective fixation of the skin to the underlying thoracic tissues, especially laterally, seems essential to achieving stable implant positioning.

Implant-related factors may also contribute to these findings. Although the surface used in the present study is characterized by very low roughness, it appears to provide adequate resistance to rotational displacement and to support maintenance of implant orientation. In addition, implant geometry itself may be less prone to rotation. For bi-gel devices, the presence of a softer posterior gel in contact with the chest wall may allow improved conformity to the thoracic contour and could further contribute to positional stability, although this remains speculative.

Notably, all four cases of implant rotation observed in this study occurred in settings where a capsule was already present, either in revision procedures or during second-stage reconstructions with placement of the definitive implant. This observation is consistent with the findings reported by Kullab et al., who, in a series of >1000 patients, identified a rotation rate of 5% in one-stage reconstructions compared with 11% in two-stage procedures.16 These findings suggest that the presence of a pre-existing capsule may contribute to the risk of implant rotation. In this context, because implant rotation has been associated with inadequate pocket control, it would be of interest to investigate whether capsule management during second-stage reconstruction or revision procedures, including total capsulectomy, could influence the risk of rotational instability. Further studies are needed to evaluate the potential impact of capsule management on implant positioning.

Aside from implant rotation, the rates of other complications, including reoperations, were moderate and consistent with those reported in the literature on implant-based breast reconstruction. Radiotherapy remains an important risk factor in implant-based breast reconstruction. In the present series, most complications were observed in patients who had received adjuvant radiotherapy, particularly capsular contracture, implant extrusion, and partial areolar necrosis. Although the limited sample size precluded a formal subgroup analysis, these findings are consistent with the well-established negative impact of radiotherapy on outcomes after implant-based reconstruction.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively limited, including 100 patients and 134 reconstructed breasts. Second, the mean follow-up duration of 15.5 months may be considered short. Nevertheless, in the series reported by Kullab et al.,16 the median time to implant rotation ranged from 7.4 to 14.6 months postoperatively, depending on the type of reconstruction, suggesting that the follow-up duration in the present study remains clinically relevant for the assessment of this complication.

In addition, implant rotation was assessed exclusively by clinical examination, without the use of imaging techniques. Sieber et al. reported that rotation rates identified by ultrasound could be up to five times higher than those detected clinically.17 Finally, animation deformity was not specifically assessed in this retrospective study. Although animation deformity may occur after retromuscular breast reconstruction, most patients did not spontaneously complain of this side effect during follow-up.

Conclusion

In this retrospective series, microtextured anatomical breast implants with ultra-low surface roughness demonstrated a low rate of clinically detectable rotation in implant-based breast reconstruction. When combined with meticulous surgical technique and appropriate pocket management, these implants appear to provide satisfactory positional stability while preserving the morphological advantages of anatomically shaped devices. Microtextured anatomical implants therefore represent a reliable option in breast reconstruction, particularly in regulatory environments where macrotextured surfaces are no longer available. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings.
