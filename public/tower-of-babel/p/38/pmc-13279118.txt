Risk‐Adjusted Excess Length of Stay for Patients With Heart Failure Across Facilities: A Large US Cohort Study
Authors: Laura H. Gunn, Chidimma Okoli, Esther C. Ekwonwa, Kimberly Mosley, Farzana Rahman, John Martin, Michael Korvink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHospital length of stay (LOS) among patients with heart failure (HF) is relevant for patients, payers, and hospitals. Risk adjustment of LOS supports fair and equitable reimbursement for facilities that may experience more complex cases, oftentimes serving marginalized populations. We aimed to assess factors contributing to HF LOS, building on commonly available information across a wide range of facilities.MethodsA Fine and Gray Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to assess hospital LOS using a large US cohort of 89 621 patients with HF hospitalized during the fourth quarter of 2023, controlling for censoring among patients leaving against medical advice and for competing risks of in‐hospital all‐cause mortality. In our primary aim, we risk‐adjusted HF LOS for patient‐level sociodemographic and clinical episode characteristics as well as facility‐level factors. Model performance was assessed via concordance statistics across derivation and validation cohorts, and risk adjustments were reported as subdistribution hazard ratios. As a secondary aim, we explored facility‐level risk‐adjusted idiosyncratic differences in LOS.ResultsSociodemographic, clinical episode, and facility‐level characteristics can explain differences in hospital LOS among patients with HF, with most variables being statistically significant. The model exhibited moderate performance with similar results across the derivation (C=0.686 [95% CI, 0.682–0.691]) and validation (C=0.691 [95% CI, 0.686–0.695]) cohorts.ConclusionsExcess LOS can be attributed to multiple characteristics at the sociodemographic, clinical episode, and facility levels. We demonstrate a HF LOS risk‐adjustment method that does not rely on, though can be expanded with, extensive patient clinical information, supporting more equitable assessments of facility performance and reimbursement.

Length of stay (LOS), which averaged 5.5 days across 36 million US hospitalizations in 2018, 1 is an important in‐hospital stay metric for patients, health care providers, and payers. 1 In addition to its economic impact, longer LOS is also associated with increased odds of developing hospital‐acquired infections 2 and worse mortality outcomes. 3 , 4 , 5 Excess LOS, defined as LOS not directly attributable to the medical episode, 6 has been linked to factors such as unnecessary waiting times, delays in discharge decisions, and organizational issues, among others. 1 Thus, there is a growing need to risk‐adjust clinical outcomes such as LOS and, relatedly, identify facilities or units with idiosyncratic characteristics resulting in risk‐adjusted excess LOS, as well as methods to address this excess LOS. 1 , 7

Heart failure (HF) has risen to epidemic levels, 8 , 9 and its related total cost of care is projected at >$160 billion in costs by 2030, 10 with room for enhancements across value‐based care metrics, including LOS. 11 , 12 Large disparities persist in both provision of treatment and LOS across social determinants of health. 1 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Thus, risk‐adjusting for factors associated with LOS variability 1 , 18 , 19 to facilitate the implementation of facility‐ or unit‐level strategies to reduce excess LOS is increasingly relevant, 1 , 7 particularly for facilities that treat individuals for HF.

Studies focusing on smaller groups, such as those based on single facilities, can benefit from homogeneous and more extensive access to patient clinical episode information for risk adjustment of HF excess LOS. However, such studies oftentimes suffer from low sample sizes, leading to unreliable estimates that can be difficult to extrapolate to other facilities. 20 , 21 Thus, they are not suitable for interfacility comparisons of HF LOS outcomes and associated equitable reimbursements. Conversely, more generalized cohort studies using clinical data across facilities can suffer selection bias if they are forced to exclude nonreporting facilities, 17 because nonreporting or underreporting may occur not at random (eg, at higher rates among facilities with fewer resources). Thus, novel approaches for risk adjustment that can encompass most facilities without the need for extensive patient clinical episode information, such as approaches built on administrative claims data, 22 are needed to risk‐adjust outcomes of patients with HF. Additionally, the American College of Physicians has recently called for new approaches for risk adjustment that take into account all factors associated with health outcomes and provide better estimates for equitable reimbursement, because unadjusted estimates can lead to unfair payments for services, particularly among marginalized populations. 23

Nonclinical administrative factors, such as payment type or discharge destination, have been found to be associated with LOS variability. 24 Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics can also explain inequities among patients with HF. 17 Additionally, hospital characteristics have been explored, though with modest results. 25 Risk‐adjusted geographical differences in LOS and in‐hospital mortality among patients with HF have further been found across census‐based regions. 26 However, although nonfederal health care regulations and standards of care are often defined by state and local agencies, 27 implementation of LOS enhancement strategies may also be useful at other cluster levels, such as the facility or physician level. 1

Although geographic disparities among individuals with HF have been identified by US state 28 and by country globally, 15 these studies remain difficult to extrapolate into risk adjustments applicable to the full US health care ecosystem. Many factors explored in the literature continue to be defined at the patient level, generally ignoring the potential clustering effects on measured associations between LOS and risk factors stemming from specific facilities or units, facility‐level characteristics, or geographical locations where treatment is provided. Zheng et al (2022) clustered at the facility level while incorporating HF clinical information, though with missing data limitations resulting in their exclusion of facilities that were non‐ or underreporting clinical information, thus potentially leading to selection bias. 17

Furthermore, studies that do not focus on overall LOS metrics across causes of termination of the in‐hospital stay or exclude nondischarge visits can be affected by both rate differences in discharges against medical advice (AMA) and severity mix differences across facilities leading to differences of in‐hospital mortality rates. This could bias LOS comparisons across patients, facilities, or health care systems. Facilities providing subsidized health care, or where patients are “fully” insured, are less likely to experience high rates of patients leaving AMA. 29 , 30 Prevalence of leaving AMA has been found to be more common among uninsured and Medicaid patients, resulting in 2.5 fewer hospitalization days. 31 Also, facilities attending patient mixes with more severe clinical episodes can experience higher in‐hospital mortality rates resulting in higher LOS, because patients who died in hospital have been found to experience 3.1 more days of in‐hospital time. 27

Excess LOS interventions, such as design of clinical pathways, 7 , 32 , 33 case management, 7 , 34 or more general logistical enhancements, 1 , 7 can be developed based on patient experiences across facilities. However, a prerequisite to their implementation is the identification of clusters of patients (eg, facilities) where risk‐adjusted HF LOS was substantially larger than anticipated. We have not found generalizable approaches in the literature that control for competing events and censoring to risk‐adjust HF LOS using a substantial number of variables and a nonexclusionary data set of facilities. Metrics to identify such patients with unexplained HF LOS could serve quality‐control personnel to compare operational standards with those from peers or aspirational, comparable facilities.

Our primary aim is to risk‐adjust LOS among patients with HF, using administrative data, through a combination of patient sociodemographic and facility‐level characteristics, as well as clinical episode information, while clustering at the facility level. Although some studies treated in‐hospital deaths separately, 17 we include all patient outcomes, including in‐hospital mortality and leaving AMA, during risk adjustment to reduce the potential for selection bias. A secondary study aim is to identify facilities with potential risk‐adjusted excess LOS. Thus, this study assesses whether HF LOS risk adjustments can be implemented with limited clinical episode factors, while keeping the potential to be enhanced when/if homogeneously recorded additional clinical information is widely available across facilities, such as in centralized health care delivery systems.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are the property of Premier, Inc. Requests to access the data set should be directed to Dr John Martin at Premier, Inc. at john_martin@premierinc.com.

Study Design

Administrative data were extracted from the Premier Healthcare Database, 35 including N=89 621 US inpatient hospitalizations with a HF principal diagnosis International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code listed in Table S1, 36 and terminating their in‐hospital stay for any cause during the last quarter of 2023. Where >1 visit was recorded per patient in that quarter, the first visit was used. The data included (1) patient sociodemographic information (age, sex, race, and 5 social vulnerability index [SVI] variables representing percentile ranks from 0 to 1, with larger values corresponding to greater social vulnerability 37 ; (2) visit‐related characteristics of the episode (primary payer, point of origin, ICD‐10 grouping of the primary diagnosis, counts of ICD‐10 procedures, total and HF‐related ICD‐10 secondary diagnoses, and a COVID‐19 indicator); and (3) facility‐specific information (teaching status, academic status, rural/urban, ownership type, bed count (ie, a proxy for facility size), case mix index (representing a facility’s average case complexity and resource allocation with higher values indicating lower adjusted costs per patient), and geographic location. The study was restricted to the 50 US states plus Washington, DC. LOS was defined as days until hospitalization termination. Causes for hospitalization termination were mapped to 3 statuses: (1) discharge event, (2) censoring due to leaving AMA, and (3) competing event of in‐hospital all‐cause mortality. Rate of discharge in this article refers to status (1). Because missingness in the data could have related to observations not belonging to any of the existing categories or not‐at‐random reporting status based on unobserved factors (eg, immigration status, clinical episode severity, misreporting, etc.), those were coded and estimated as their own “unknown” category, where needed, rather than imputed. This study was approved, and the requirement for informed consent was waived, by the Internal Review Board at University of North Carolina at Charlotte and received a notice of exemption (IRB‐24‐1051).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were tabulated for the full data set, and a cumulative incidence curve and risk table were calculated. The data were randomly split into an 80% derivation cohort (N=71 696) and a 20% validation cohort (N=17 925). The derivation cohort was used to fit Fine and Gray univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards (PH) models, 38 clustering by facility and adjusting for in‐hospital mortality competing risks. Subdistribution hazards for discharge events, representing the risk adjustment of HF LOS, were modeled to address the primary study aim. Time‐weighted average unadjusted and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs), 39 robust Huber–White 95% CIs, 40 and corresponding P values were computed. Using the derivation model coefficients, concordance statistics (Harrell’s C‐statistic) and 95% CIs were calculated for both the derivation and validation cohorts. Plots of HRs and corresponding 95% CIs were created for all categorical variables with at least 3 categories. A backward elimination variable selection approach using Akaike information criteria 41 explored whether a more parsimonious model provided additional predictive power in the validation cohort.

Although not necessary for risk adjustment, a secondary study aim was extracting risk‐adjusted facility‐specific excess LOS metrics. This was explored through 2 separate models with (1) facility‐specific fixed effects 42 and (2) facility random effects (with Gaussian frailty terms) in a mixed effects model. 43 A funnel plot was produced to demonstrate risk‐adjusted differences by facility. 44 The concept of “frailty” within the article relates to the biostatistical interpretation rather than the clinical interpretation. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 and above in a cloud environment.

Patients were predominantly 65 years or older (n=62 412; 69.64%) with the largest proportion of the cohort ages 85+ (n=17 842; 19.91%), male (n=47 591; 53.10%), and White (n=60 318; 67.30%). The overall SVI averaged 0.62 (SD=0.25). More patients had traditional Medicare as their primary payer (n=30 182; 33.68%) than any other type. Most admissions came from nonhealth care facilities (n=76 465; 85.32%). Nearly 43% (n=38 472) had a principal diagnosis of I13.0, representing hypertensive heart and chronic kidney disease (CKD) with heart failure and stage 1–4 CKD, or unspecified CKD. Patients had an average of 21.21 secondary diagnoses (SD=8.54) and 0.95 HF‐related secondary diagnoses (SD=0.25) and underwent 2.51 procedures (SD=1.83). Most patients did not have a documented COVID‐19 infection (n=88 212; 98.43%).

Facilities were mostly nonteaching (n=68 497; 76.43%), nonacademic (n=76 869; 85.77%), and urban (n=79 970; 89.23%). The majority were voluntary private nonprofits (n=54 864; 61.22%), and nearly 60% of facilities had >300 beds (n=51 970; 57.99%). The case mix index averaged 1.66 (SD=0.29). Florida had the highest number of facilities represented in the data (n=9630; 10.75%) followed by New York (n=7064; 7.88%).

Table 2 presents univariate and multivariate subdistribution average HR estimates, 95% Huber–White CIs, and corresponding P values for the derivation cohort using the Fine and Gray Cox PH model, accounting for in‐hospital all‐cause mortality, with clustering by facility for time to hospital discharge. After adjusting for all covariates, all younger age groups had significantly shorter LOS compared with individuals ages 85+ years old (P<0.001). Particularly, patients ages 45 to 54 and 55 to 59 years had at least 15% shorter stays than individuals 85+ (HR, 1.201 [95% CI, 1.153–1.251]; P<0.001 and HR, 1.202 [95% CI, 1.152–1.254]; P<0.001, respectively). Sex was nonsignificant (HR for women, 1.004 [95% CI, 0.987–1.021]; P=0.658). Asian patients experienced a 10.9% higher rate of discharge than White patients (HR, 1.109 [95% CI, 1.048–1.175]; P<0.001), whereas Black patients had a 4.4% lower rate (HR, 0.956 [95% CI, 0.931–0.981]; P<0.001), resulting in significantly longer adjusted LOS. Among SVIs, the household characteristics index showed a 22.3% increased rate of discharge (HR, 1.223 [95% CI, 1.042–1.435]; P<0. 014), whereas the racial and ethnic minority status index was associated with a 22.5% lower rate (HR, 0.775 [95% CI, 0.689–0.872]; P<0.001) of discharge.

DISCUSSION

The multivariate Fine and Gray model revealed important findings pertaining to LOS among patients HF while accounting for in‐hospital mortality as a competing event and censoring of clinical discharges due to patients leaving AMA. After adjusting for patient sociodemographic and clinical episode information, as well as facility characteristics, all younger age groups had significantly shorter LOS, with ∼20% higher hazards of discharge compared with patients ages 85+ years old. This finding aligns with a study that highlighted age‐based variations in HF outcomes. 45

Black patients had longer adjusted LOS and Asian patients had a shorter adjusted LOS compared with White patients. This is consistent with findings that reported racial disparities in HF outcomes. 46 Higher vulnerability, as measured by the SVI variables in the reported units, for the household characteristics, household type and transportation, and socioeconomic status domain indices of the broader SVI were associated with increased discharge hazards by between ∼13% and 22.3%, whereas areas with greater racial and ethnic minority populations and the overall index were associated with lower discharge hazards. This highlights the complex relationship between social determinants of health and hospital LOS. 47 In line with the existing literature, a greater burden of comorbidities was associated with extended stays, with each additional secondary diagnosis decreasing discharge hazards by 5%. 48

Our findings also revealed significant variations in LOS associated with facility characteristics, specifically academic and larger hospitals. Patients treated in academic facilities exhibited longer LOS compared with nonacademic settings, reflected by their lower hazard of discharge. This aligns with evidence suggesting that academic hospitals often serve as centers for research and clinical trials and also patients needing extended LOS being directed to academic hospitals, which may necessitate prolonged monitoring and treatment durations unrelated to the immediate clinical episode. 49

Larger hospitals were associated with longer LOS compared with smaller facilities, upon adjusting for all other individual and facility‐level factors. Operational inefficiencies, such as interdepartmental communication delays, resource limitations, and logistical challenges, may contribute to extended stays in larger hospitals, though the causality of the hypothesis is unclear and we found no literature against which to compare these results.

Our results align with studies emphasizing the need for targeted facility‐level interventions to address excess LOS, including improved discharge planning and streamlined care pathways. 50 These insights underscore the critical role of facility‐level strategies in mitigating LOS variability and optimizing resource allocation. Future research should explore the integration of tailored interventions across academic and larger hospitals to reduce excess LOS while maintaining high‐quality patient care.

The C‐statistic of our model was 0.691 for the validation cohort, indicating moderate predictive performance and discriminatory power. This is comparable to previous studies, which reported area under the curve values of at most 0.68, 51 ≤0.69, 52 and 0.70, 22 involving data on patients with HF. The moderate predictive power of our model may be attributed to limited clinical episode information as well as other unmeasured confounding factors. However, our model provides useful insight in predicting LOS, with in‐hospital mortality as a competing event, and serves as a foundation for future studies. It also provides a tool to risk‐adjust an important metric related to hospital payment, namely LOS among patients with HF, thus having direct implications in equitable hospital reimbursement.

In our secondary aim of addressing facility‐level idiosyncratic components affecting LOS variability, we extracted facility‐level estimates to identify facilities with large unexplained risk‐adjusted LOS (Figure S5). There is substantial LOS variability still present at the facility level. Facilities where patients experience large, unexplained risk‐adjusted LOS (high or low) can be identified as those outside confidence bands, and, among those, facilities with substantially large, positive unexplained LOS can undergo more in‐depth assessment of alternative potential causes of unexplained risk‐adjusted LOS, as well as the resources to enhance operating structures, processes, and practices, if needed. For example, some of the remaining variation could be due to differences in hospital operational protocols and staffing (eg, having a discharge nurse on staff). Identifying this variation is actionable information for hospitals and can be of interest to finance and quality administrators. This tractable approach to identifying outlying facilities where LOS enhancements are potentially needed can be more cost‐effective than universal, in‐depth analysis across hundreds of health care institutions.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

We estimate the risk‐adjusted subdistribution hazard rates of LOS, adjusting for AMA and in‐hospital competing mortality events, rather than modeling raw LOS, using a large US cohort of patients with HF. All P values reflect statistical tests, which are not related to clinical significance of the variables. That clinical significance is not assessed in this article. Moderate predictive results are successfully replicated in the validation cohort, demonstrating extrapolability of the risk adjustments. Although our approach is demonstrated using administrative data, more clinical episode information, if available, can be added as additional covariates. Although lacking clinical information is a limitation when modeling LOS, it is also a strength not to have to rely on its availability across facilities to perform excess LOS inferential analyses and risk adjustments, particularly in decentralized health care systems.

Premier’s database is not perfectly geographically representative by state, resulting in a geographical bias. There may be unaccounted seasonal components affecting LOS with uneven geographical impact. Our sample contains only Q4 data, which overlaps with some peak periods in hospitalizations for respiratory diseases. This may affect bed space and operating procedures differently by facility, based on local or regional prevalence of other diseases.

There is a high level of collinearity, which reduces the reliability of some coefficients. However, this does not affect the model predictive metrics, nor their use to risk‐adjust patient LOS or identify facilities with excess LOS. Also, a Fine and Gray Cox PH survival approach was used to enhance interpretability, though machine learning tools may also be viable.

Measurable or unmeasurable factors not considered in the study, including those affecting the case severity of the facility patient mix, could affect the primary aim’s risk‐adjustment estimates. The secondary aim’s results should be used only as broad signals of potential excess LOS, pending further, more in‐depth exploration of unaccounted factors that may explain excess risk‐adjusted LOS in specific facilities.

CONCLUSIONS

This study risk‐adjusts hospital LOS among patients admitted for HF as their principal diagnosis, across a wide range of administrative patient sociodemographic, clinical episode, and facility variables, while accounting for leaving AMA and all‐cause mortality. Several factors are identified as relevant to explain LOS variability and needed for risk adjustment. This is necessary to provide health care delivery facilities with equitable reimbursement for services. Some excess LOS can be explained by facility‐specific characteristics. Identification of facilities with large unexplained risk‐adjusted LOS is a necessary step to implement LOS reduction interventions among facilities treating patients with HF. The proposed approach does not rely on availability of substantial clinical episode information and achieves moderate results to explain LOS variability. The approach can be used across a wide variety of clustering variables and information sources.
