Quaternary palaeoecology meets deep-time palaeobiology
Authors: H. John B. Birks
Published: 2023

Studies of ecological dynamics fall into three contrasting temporal scales—real-time, Quaternary-time, and deep-time (1). Real-time can be weeks, months, years, decades, or, very occasionally, centuries and are studied by (neo-)ecologists. Quaternary time (Q-time) covers the last 2.7 My, particularly the past 50,000 y, the time span of radiocarbon dating, and is studied by palaeoecologists. Deep-time studies involve palaeontological methods to study dynamics across a wide range of time scales, typically >105 years, and involve palaeobiologists (1). These three contrasting time scales inevitably involve different spatial scales ranging from a quadrat to a continent or the entire planet. Real-time, Q-time, and deep-time studies have inevitably developed their own approaches, literature, and methodologies (1) including numerical analytical tools [e.g., real-time (2), Q-time (3), and deep-time (4)]. Although some Q-time palaeoecologists link their results to real-time ecological patterns and processes, there is little integration between Q-time and deep-time studies (1). Stegner and Spanbauer (5) analyse Q-time pollen assemblages covering the last 13,000 calibrated (cal) y B.P. (present is 1950 CE = 0 B.P.) using numerical approaches generally associated with deep-time studies. The last 13,000 cal y includes the Pleistocene–Holocene transition (PHT) at about 11,700 cal y B.P. and the entire Holocene. Stegner and Spanbauer (5) estimate six pollen-assemblage properties (sensu 6) or assemblage metrics. These are pollen taxonomic richness; short-term (1,000 y) taxon loss and gain; first (FAD) and last (LAD) taxon appearance datum; and abrupt pollen-assemblage change in 386 pollen sequences across North America (continental scale). They also study these metrics in four regions [Tundra/Taiga (61 sequences), Northern Forests (94), Western Forested Mountains (69), Eastern Temperate Forests (108)] within North America. All data are from the open-access Neotoma Paleoecology Database (7, 8).

Pollen taxa included are from trees, shrubs, and upland herbs and the assemblages are regarded as a proxy record of upland vegetation. All sequences have at least 20 samples, at least one sample per 250 y on average, and a temporal span of at least 2,000 y. Each sequence has an age-depth model estimated by the Bayesian bacon age-modelling procedure (8, 9). Age uncertainty is estimated by resampling (1,000 iterations) the Bayesian age-model posterior distribution for each sequence, and then quantifying the average level of the assemblage metrics across sequences in fixed 250-y time-bins. Each sample in each sequence is thus associated with a distribution of age estimates that are used to assign each sample into the time-bins (5). As the number of pollen sequences varies through time, particularly towards the present, and as time-bins with more pollen records may result in more abrupt changes being detected, a fixed number (N) of sequences (100 for the continental-scale analysis; 20 for the regional-scale analyses) is sampled.

Estimates of the six metrics in each time-bin across sequences are derived by randomly sampling one age-model iteration from each of the randomly selected N sequences and determining which sequences have samples that fall within each time-bin. The N sequences are then subsampled for each bin. Cross-sequence values of the six metrics are estimated from 50,000 iterations at the continental scale and 5,000 iterations at the regional scale, along with 95% CIs estimated across all iterations. As stratigraphic time-bins are temporally autocorrelated, nonoverlap of the CIs is used to establish whether there are statistically significant differences in a given metric among the time-bins (5).

Patterns

At the continental scale, taxonomic richness declines from ~11,500 y B.P. to about 6,000 y B.P. and then gradually increases, with a sharp rise near the present (Fig. 1). Short-term taxon loss changes little until the last 200 y B.P. Short-term gain also changes little but slightly declines from 6,000 y B.P. to the present. LADs are high between 11,500 and 9,500 y B.P. and then remain low until the present with a slight rise between 200 and ~150 y B.P. FADs are high at the PHT and then remain low until a slight rise at 200 y B.P. Rates of abrupt change are high soon after the PHT and then decline until ~1,000 y B.P. They increase sharply between then and the present (Fig. 1).

At the regional scale, high rates of abrupt change are seen at the PHT (except in Tundra/Taiga where sequences only extend to 8,000 y B.P.). There is an increase in abrupt changes in the last 1,000 y, especially in the Eastern Temperate Forests. LAD values increase in the last 1,500 y in all regions. Changes in other metrics are not consistent across regions. For example, FAD only increases in recent times in the Northern Forest and Eastern Temperate Forest regions.

Discussion

The results presented by Stegner and Spanbauer (5) support previous interpretations of pollen stratigraphies in North America with marked compositional change at or soon after the PHT (e.g., refs. 10–12) and at the onset of European settlement in the late 19th century in eastern and central areas (10, 13, 14) with logging, soil disturbance, and cultivation, as shown by the rise of Ambrosia (ragweed)-type pollen and the occurrence of pollen of other weed taxa such as Salsola kali (Russian thistle), and of cereals such as Zea mays (maize). In the Western Forested Mountains, logging in the late 19th century resulted in pollen declines of Pseudotsuga menziesii (Douglas fir), Thuja plicata (red cedar), and Tsuga heterophylla (western hemlock), and the rise of Alnus rubra (red alder) and Betula (birch) pollen (15, 16).

Stegner and Spanbauer’s study brings together Q-time pollen data and deep-time quantitative approaches to provide a novel analysis of pollen-assemblage properties at a continental scale and at regional scales within North America.

Stegner and Spanbauer (5) note that high rates of abrupt change and changes in biodiversity (FAD; LAD) are often used to define transitions in deep-time geological records (17). Increased numbers of LAD and abrupt compositional change in pollen assemblages at or near the PHT are paralleled by changes after 200 y B.P. This supports the proposal that the world is now in a new geological epoch, the Anthropocene (18). Current ideas about a “type” locality for the Holocene–Anthropocene transition centre on Crawford Lake, Ontario with the transition at 1950 CE (=0 y B.P.) as announced in July 2023 at the Max Planck Society Conference on a Sustainable Anthropocene held in Berlin.

A novel aspect of Stegner and Spanbauer’s contribution (5) is the application of numerical approaches primarily used in deep-time palaeobiology, for example, estimating taxon richness in samples with different count sizes. They use shareholder quorum subsampling (19, 20), a method in real-time ecology known as coverage-based rarefaction analysis (21). The first (FAD) and last (LAD) appearances of pollen taxa are assessed by counting numbers of taxa observed for the first or last time for each sample in each sequence excluding the three oldest and youngest samples (5). Taxon extirpations are estimated at a 1,000-y scale for each sequence by counting the number of taxa present in the 1,000 y prior to but absent in the target 1,000-y block. For taxon arrivals, the number of taxa present in the target 1,000-y block but not found in the prior 1,000-y block is counted. FAD, LAD, and taxon arrivals and extirpations in Q-time pollen data are rarely, if ever, quantified, only visually assessed qualitatively (e.g., ref. 22).

Abrupt changes are detected by using a Bayesian change-point procedure applied to principal curves of square-root transformed pollen proportions in each sequence (5). Principal curves are a powerful data-summarisation technique in real-time (e.g., ref. 23) and Q-time studies (e.g., refs. 24 and 25). Abrupt compositional change can alternatively be detected as “peak points” in the rate-of-change (RoC) approach of Mottl et al. (26).

It would be instructive to compare the results presented in Stegner and Spanbauer (5) with results derived from numerical techniques more commonly used in Q-time research such as rarefaction analysis to estimate pollen taxonomic richness (27) and rates of compositional change (26). The RoC results presented in Mottl et al. (28) for North America as a whole show peaks at 12,000 y B.P. and 1,000 y B.P. but contrasting RoC patterns are seen in seven regions within North America with peaks at 12,000 y B.P. in central and eastern areas and RoC peaks in the last 1,000 y present in all regions except north-eastern Canada.

Stegner and Spanbauer’s study (5) brings together Q-time pollen data and deep-time quantitative approaches to provide a novel analysis of pollen-assemblage properties at a continental scale and at regional scales within North America. It would be fascinating to see their approach applied to the rich and varied Q-time pollen data from Europe with its long history of human disturbance, settlement, clearance, and cultivation.
