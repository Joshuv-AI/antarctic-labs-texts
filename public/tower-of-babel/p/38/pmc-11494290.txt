Nuclear compensatory evolution driven by mito-nuclear incompatibilities
Authors: Debora Princepe, Marcus A. M. de Aguiar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding how nuclear and mitochondrial genomes coevolve is critical for elucidating organismal resilience and adaptation. This study explores how mismatches between these interacting genomes impact substitutions, shedding light on nuclear compensation—a process where deleterious mitochondrial mutations induce compensatory changes in corresponding nuclear genes. Using a speciation model with selection for mito-nuclear compatibility, we show that strong selection and increased mismatches facilitate nuclear compensation, as expected. Interestingly, high mitochondrial mutation rates can reduce the need for compensation, effect that may be overestimated by model backmutations, whereas substitutions in initially compatible nuclear genes increase. Furthermore, the presence of incompatibilities accelerates species radiation. These findings contribute to understanding population resilience despite genetic incompatibilities while linking mito-nuclear coevolution, substitution rates, and speciation.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is a remarkable genetic component for maintaining the myriad of mitochondrial functions in cellular metabolism, distinguished by its small size, rapid evolutionary rate, and general absence of recombination associated with uniparental inheritance. These characteristics render mtDNA susceptible to the irreversible accumulation of deleterious alleles (1–3). Evidence of multilevel purifying selection on mtDNA, spanning from cellular organization to individual and population levels, provides the most plausible explanation for the nonobservation of mutational erosion (4–6). Other manifestation of selection arises from the crucial interaction between proteins encoded by nuclear and mitochondrial DNAs for cellular respiration. These genomes must coevolve to maintain mito-nuclear genetic compatibility, ultimately impacting an organism’s phenotype (7–10). Resulting scenarios vary on how mitochondrial mutations impact fitness relative to the nuclear DNA background (11), with prevalence of purifying selection against incompatibilities (12).

Conversely, the nuclear compensation mechanism suggests that deleterious mitochondrial alleles drive compensatory changes in nuclear genes to preserve mito-nuclear coadaptation and functional mitochondria, leaving a positive signature (13–15). Detection of this process often relies on the ratio between nonsynonymous to synonymous nucleotide substitution rates (dN/dS), with compensation favoring the fixation of mutations while purifying processes should eliminate them. Nevertheless, inefficient removal or higher tolerance to slightly deleterious mutations can reduce the efficacy of purifying selection, or “relax” it, increasing dN/dS ratios (14, 16), challenging experimental tests of compensatory nuclear evolution (17, 18). Temporal patterns may differentiate them: Nuclear substitutions must arise after mtDNA substitutions if compensating for deleterious mtDNA alleles, whereas they occur concurrently in any other coevolutionary scenario (17), leaving the interpretation of selective forces dependent on hypothesis formulation and testing tools. Considering all this, empirical evidence both supports (19–21) and refutes (16, 17, 22) nuclear compensation, leaving the debate unresolved about its ubiquity and conditional factors.

Here, we study the impact of mito-nuclear incompatibilities on nuclear and mitochondrial substitutions in a model simulating species radiation. We aim to distinguish signatures of mito-nuclear coevolution across various scenarios, particularly identifying conditions that promote compensatory mutations in nuclear DNA (nDNA) to restore compatibility with the corresponding mtDNA part. To this end, we employ agent-based simulations where genetically explicit populations evolve from partially compatible mtDNA and nDNA. All individuals have a fraction f of mitochondrial genes incompatible with their respective interacting nDNA sites (first nDNA/mtDNA genes in the Upper section of Fig. 1). We assume no optimal nuclear or mitochondrial types, but their compatibility influences individual fecundity: Individual fitness decreases with mito-nuclear incompatibilities, exerting selective pressure toward matching genetic sequences.

Each individual carries two sequences representing its nuclear (nDNA) and mitochondrial (mtDNA) genomes. All nuclear genes initiate on zero. Nuclear DNA also governs mating compatibility, preventing reproduction between individuals with highly divergent nDNA. Reproduction is further constrained to spatially proximate individuals, leading to species emergence under the influence of mito-nuclear selection. We vary the strength of selection (S), the initial fraction of incompatible genes (f), and the mitochondrial mutation rate (μm), and track how substitutions vary over time for the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, distinguishing sections that start as incompatible (mismatched) or compatible (matched), and those unaffected by selection (non-interacting, Fig. 1).

In this simplified scheme, the process resembles the dynamics following mtDNA introgression, where foreign mtDNA invades the population and potentially introduces incompatibilities with the native nuclear genome (23). Numerous cases show that mtDNA transfers can occur without noticeable migration of nuclear genes, mainly due to sex-biased phenomena or selective sweeps (11, 24, 25). It can also happen when previously isolated populations come into secondary contact, followed by hybridization and backcrossing (24, 26, 27). In some cases, populations can benefit from mtDNA introgression; for example, if it occurs adaptively, providing a fitness advantage in a changing or novel environment, or when replacing a mitochondrial genome with a high mutational load (28, 29). We assume that any of these drivers are at play and explore the outcomes to address the seeming paradox between mitochondrial introgression and selection for mito-nuclear compatibility (28, 30). Our study can thus offer insights into the conditional factors for nuclear compensation and whether substitution rates might indicate the evolutionary stage of the population. It also reveals how mito-nuclear incompatibilities impact speciation, with the intensity of selection affecting the tempo and number of species formed.

Results

Model.

We investigated the impact of mito-nuclear incompatibilities during the radiation of spatial populations, employing an approach previously explored in refs. 31 and 32. The model incorporates sexual reproduction with separated sexes, dispersal, mutation, and genetic drift as processes that change allele frequencies. Together with conspecific mating restricted to nearby individuals, species emerge through isolation by distance (33). Our study involves individual-based simulations where initially identical individuals are characterized by haploid biallelic sequences of lengths B and Bm, with B>Bm, representing their nuclear and mitochondrial DNAs, respectively (Fig. 1; Materials and Methods). The mito-nuclear genetic interaction is modeled as a locus-by-locus coupling between mtDNA and the first Bm genes of the nuclear DNA, while the remaining (B−Bm) sites, designated “noninteracting genes,” do not participate in the interaction. Pairs of interacting loci that carry different allele values are considered incompatible and contribute to the mito-nuclear distance di, which in turn affects an individual’s fecundity. This scheme is a phenomenological approximation of the required, intricate biochemical and structural compatibility between proteins encoded by both genomes for cellular respiration (7, 22).

Populations evolve with nonoverlapping generations and in a homogeneous environment, maintaining a constant total number of individuals. During the reproduction phase, each individual has a probability of randomly choosing a mating partner within a spatial range of radius R. Genetic compatibility depends on the Hamming distance between their nuclear DNAs (dijn), which must be below the threshold G for successful mating. Offspring’s nuclear genome forms through parental free recombination with a mutation probability of μ per site, while mtDNA is inherited from the mother with a mutation probability μm per locus (Fig. 1). It is worth noting that mutations can reverse in our simulations. Species emerge as clusters of genetically compatible individuals with potential gene flow, delimited by the genetic mating restriction G, with no gene flow between clusters.

In the absence of mito-nuclear selection, all individuals have an equal probability of reproducing, and nDNA and mtDNA evolve independently. However, under selection for mito-nuclear compatibility, an individual’s fecundity is modified by their mito-nuclear distance. This effect is quantified by a fitness component wi=e−Sdi2, where S represents the strength of selection—high values indicate strong selection (see Materials and Methods for details). Individuals with higher fitness, normalized within the mating range, have a higher probability of reproduction, both as focal individual and drawn partner. We evaluated the impact of partially incompatible genomes, where the entire population initially possesses a fraction f of incompatible mtDNA genes, under various selection regimes and mitochondrial mutational rates. We sought signs of compensatory nuclear evolution by computing the substitutions along the dynamics, aiming to understand the specific conditions under which these phenomena may occur. This simplified description does not aim to replicate biological systems, but rather to capture the fundamental processes underlying species evolution under selection for mito-nuclear coordination. Parameter values, therefore, are chosen in order to reproduce observed patterns, although nominal values are not necessarily realistic. Prior work (31) demonstrated, for instance, that the phylogeny of copepods, a model organism for studying mito-nuclear coevolution, exhibit topological properties similar to the ones obtained in our simulations, thus supporting our modeling approach.

In this study, we use the term “substitutions” to denote mutations that become fixed in the population, changing from an initial state of 0 to a final state of 1 or vice versa (2). It is important to note that mutations in our model are neutral, meaning they have no direct impact on individual fitness and are equivalent among genes, conferring no evolutionary advantage or disadvantage. However, any mutation on matched genes will cause fitness loss if not followed by a change in the counterpart. Consequently, the dynamics on incompatible gene pairs resemble compensatory coevolution (3), with stabilizing selection acting toward minimizing mito-nuclear mismatches. We do not distinguish between synonymous and nonsynonymous substitutions; in a simplified manner, all substitutions in our model can be considered nonsynonymous.

In Fig. 3, we examine the trade-off between mitochondrial mutation rates and nuclear substitutions across these scenarios. We used noninteracting genes as a baseline to represent the absence of mito-nuclear selection in nDNA. Under mito-nuclear selection, the number of substitutions in initially incompatible nuclear genes consistently exceeded those observed in noninteracting genes, reflecting a compensatory effect. A lower μm was advantageous for this effect because, in our simulations, faster mtDNA mutations facilitated the rectification of incompatibilities. While this outcome might be influenced by our model’s limitations—only two alleles, with the possibility of switching back and forth—it suggests a potential scenario where the mitochondrial allele adjusts to a different state that coordinates with the nuclear gene. However, this effect might be overestimated. Conversely, under low μm/μ ratios, selection had a purifying action on initially compatible nDNA genes, reducing substitutions. However, as μm increases (μm/μ≥8), substitutions increased beyond the noninteracting level even in those genes. In summary, lower mitochondrial mutation rates facilitated the nuclear compensatory effect on incompatibilities, whereas higher rates increased substitutions in initially compatible genes due to the rapid accumulation of mutations in the mtDNA.

Strength of Selection.

The effect of selection strength on nuclear substitutions differed from that of the mitochondrial mutation rate. As depicted in the Top panel of Fig. 4, increasing selection strength led to more substitutions in mismatched genes but fewer substitutions in the compatible ones. Stronger selection resulted in a more intense compensatory response. For mitochondrial genes, substitutions in mismatched sites (Bottom Left in Fig. 4) exceeded the case with no selection (S=0) under weak and moderate selection (S=16 and S=32). However, under strong selection (S=90), substitutions initially increased before stabilizing at below the noninteracting level. In compatible mtDNA genes, the purifying effect was positively correlated with the strength of selection.

Mito-nuclear selection directly reduced substitutions in initially compatible sites (“Matched,” Right column) of both nuclear (nDNA) and mitochondrial (mtDNA) genes. In incompatible nuclear genes (Top Left), increasing selection directly promoted nuclear compensation. In the corresponding incompatible mtDNA genes (Bottom Left), the effect was nonlinear: Weak and moderate selection (S=16 and S=32) increased substitutions, but strong selection (S=90) suppressed them below the noninteracting level.

Initial Fraction of Incompatibilities.

Finally, we examined how substitutions were affected by the number of incompatible pairs of genes (Fig. 5). Despite loci being independent in our model, the number of substitutions per gene in mismatched nDNA sites increased with f while decreasing in the corresponding mtDNA genes. This observation provides direct evidence that incompatibilities promote nuclear compensation. Meanwhile, matched genes presented similar dynamic responses, roughly independent of the number of incompatibilities.

Mito-Nuclear Incompatibilities and Speciation.

The presence of incompatibilities influenced the radiation process. Increasing μm reduced the number of species in equilibrium but had a negligible effect on radiation time (Fig. 6A). It also increased the average mito-nuclear distance in the population ⟨d⟩ (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). This finding contradicts a previous suggestion that elevated mtDNA substitution rates compared to the nuclear genome might enhance the potential role of mito-nuclear incompatibilities in speciation (30). Strong selection similarly reduced richness (Fig. 6B) while decreasing the average mito-nuclear distance (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Thus, while variation in mitochondrial mutation rate and selection intensity had contrasting responses on nuclear substitutions (compare Figs. 2 and 4) and average number of incompatibilities (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B), they yielded similar outcomes on richness, indicating that the dynamics of specific nDNA segments alone may be insufficient for predicting species evolution. Regarding the initial level of incompatibilities, the number of species at equilibrium remained unchanged with varying f, as well as ⟨d⟩ (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).

(A) Increasing the mitochondrial mutation rate decreased the number of species at equilibrium without abrupt changes in radiation time (Inset: zoomed view). Fig. 6C shows that the presence of incompatibilities accelerated radiation when all other parameters were constant. A similar effect was observed with increasing selection strength, particularly pronounced with strong selection (Fig. 6B). Notably, prior research has shown that, in this model, selection delays the radiation process when starting from a population with fully compatible sets of genomes (31). However, when initiated with incompatible mitochondrial and nuclear DNAs, radiation under selection occurred earlier (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

We investigated the impact of mito-nuclear incompatibilities on nuclear and mitochondrial substitutions using a simplified model for species radiation, simulating the dynamics following mtDNA introgression under selection to minimize mito-nuclear mismatches. Our study qualitatively demonstrated nuclear compensation in incompatible nDNA genes, which exhibited consistently more substitutions than compatible and noninteracting genes. Parameters’ dependence on mismatched nDNA genes opposed the trends observed in corresponding mtDNA genes and compatible pairs, resembling another signature of compensation.

Mito-nuclear selection acts as a stabilizing mechanism, driving genomes toward minimal discordance (di→0), but its effect on substitutions depended on the initial genetic compatibility. Selection drove substitutions in incompatible nuclear genes above noninteracting level, indicating a positive response, i.e., nuclear compensation. In contrast, fixed mutations in initially compatible nDNA genes decreased with increasing selection (Fig. 4), reflecting a purifying effect. Interestingly, under high μm, compatible nDNA genes exhibited an increase in substitutions, while decreasing in incompatible genes (Fig. 3). This suggests that the purifying effect on matched genes becomes less effective with increasing μm, as nDNA have to keep pace with the rapid changes in the mtDNA. The increase of the population-averaged mito-nuclear distance ⟨d⟩ is another indicative of less efficient selection against incompatibilities (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The observation of a signature resembling nuclear compensation prompted by purifying selection highlights the complex interplay between selection and genetic adaptation conditioned by the mitochondrial mutation rate (15), challenging the interpretation of high dN/dS ratios as evidence of positive selection (14, 17).

Increasing μm hindered the compensatory effect, contradicting the expectation that higher rates of mtDNA mutation would drive compensatory changes in the nuclear genome (15, 23). In our simulations, this occurred because fast mtDNA mutation rates facilitated the rectification of incompatibilities, making mutations more likely in mitochondrial than in nuclear genes, and with the possibility of sequences of multiples mutations (e.g. 1→0→1→0). Such sequences are rare in nature, so the decline in nuclear compensation might be overestimated. Conversely, we suggest that our model, while limited to two allele values, could reflect a scenario where the mitochondrial allele adapts to coordinate with the nuclear gene. The decline was also followed by increasing substitution in incompatible mtDNA genes (Bottom LeftFig. 2), indicating interesting aspects worth further exploration. This mechanism may also be relevant during introgression, where highly mutable mtDNA invading an incompatible nDNA background could more readily facilitate the rescue of compatibility.

The response above the noninteracting level in compatible nuclear genes under high μm suggests that signatures of nuclear compensation may arise even in the absence of mtDNA introgression or preexisting incompatibilities (17), noting that substitutions accumulated more slowly in compatible than in incompatible genes (Fig. 3). In compensatory evolution, nDNA substitutions follow mtDNA deleterious substitutions, creating a temporal pattern (17), when in an usual coevolutionary process they occur interspersed in a similar pace. The distinct substitution trajectories observed in Fig. 3 for mismatched and matched nDNA genes illustrate the nuanced dynamics that may be challenging to detect empirically. A correlation between fast-evolving mtDNA and increased evolution of interacting nuclear genes was observed in angiosperms (12, 18), but not in mammals (17), among other examples. While metabolic or genetic structural differences may explain these contrasts, our findings suggest that prior mito-nuclear states also play a role, and motivate further research into mtDNA evolution rates across eukaryotes (12).

The observed trends in nuclear substitutions due to parameter variation did not correlate with resulting species richness: Although increasing selection and mitochondrial mutation rate had opposing effects on nDNA substitutions, they had the same impact on richness, suggesting that the dynamics of nDNA segments alone may not fully explain species evolution. Furthermore, incompatibilities accelerated radiation, as evidenced directly by increasing f (Fig. 6C). Increasing selection provided indirect evidence: Radiation occurred earlier when incompatibilities were present (f=0.5, Fig. 6B) but was delayed when starting from matched sequences (f=0 demonstrated in refs. 31 and 32).

The consistent equilibrium richness, independent of the initial number of incompatibilities (Fig. 6C), indicates that additional system conditions may influence population resilience. Here, we posit a connection to the constant resource availability represented by a fixed number of individuals. These results reconcile mito-nuclear introgression and speciation, illustrating the maintenance of mito-nuclear coevolution despite the expected selection against mtDNA introgression through simple mechanisms (29, 30) and how mito-nuclear incompatibilities can influence the emergence of species, albeit potentially masked by other ecological factors.

Our model’s predictions are limited by its simplified nature, offering a qualitative analysis of mitonuclear incompatibilities’ impact on populations’ evolution and speciation. Real genetic mechanisms are far more complex. Genes may not express independently, nor are they limited to two alleles as portrayed here, and their mutation rates are influenced by their physical structure. Additionally, mito-nuclear incompatibilities may have a polygenic genetic architecture that varies among populations (34). The model also does not account cointrogression, where mtDNA invasion is simultaneous or followed by the invasion of coadapted nuclear genes (23), thus minimizing mito-nuclear incompatibilities. Another limitation is that mutations are considered “neutral,” neglecting the pervasive purifying selection in mtDNA evolution, which primarily removes nonsynonymous mutations (4, 35). Furthermore, we consider only the “internal” environment, whereas mitochondrial genotypes can also be subject to selection from external factors (6, 36) and respond to environmental cues (37, 38). Future model iterations could explore testing the response with additional selection exclusively on mtDNA, and we posit that fitness gains from the adaptation of the mitochondrial genotype alone might enhance the observed effects.

Materials and Methods

Our model, adapted from refs. 32, 33, and 39, describes a spatially distributed population evolving under the influence of mito-nuclear selection. Individuals reproduce sexually with separate sexes (male and female). Each individual is described by its sex, its spatial location in a two-dimensional lattice with periodic boundary conditions, and by two haploid chromosomes representing the nuclear and mitochondrial DNAs. Chromosomes are biallelic and represented by binary strings: For individual i, the nDNA is given by (σ1i,σ2i,⋯,σBi) and the mtDNA by (ρ1i,ρ2i,⋯,ρBmi), with B>Bm and σki, ρki assuming the values 0 or 1 (Fig. 1).

Generations are discrete, with complete replacement of the parent population by their offspring. Each individual has a probability P of reproducing and a probability Q=1−P of dying without leaving offspring. During reproduction, individuals select a mate of the opposite sex from a pool of genetically compatible individuals within their designated mating neighborhood. Successful reproduction between individuals i and j depends on two criteria: 1) they must be within a maximum spatial distance R from each other, and 2) their nuclear genomes must be sufficiently similar, so that dijn=∑k=1B|σki−σkj|≤G (40). The genetic mating threshold G and the spatial mating neighborhood radius R are parameters of the model. When reproduction is successful, the offspring inherits the nuclear alleles of each parent with equal probability (free recombination) and the full mitochondrial genome from the mother. Alleles are then subjected to mutation with probabilities μ for nDNA and μm for mtDNA. Mutations can be reversed. The offspring is placed at the location of the focal individual with probability 1−D or around it with diffusion probability D, in one of the 20 closest sites, chosen at random. If the individual cannot find a compatible mate within R, it expands its search area to radius R+1 and then to R+2. If no compatible mate can be found in the enlarged area, or if the individual dies without reproducing by chance, another individual is randomly selected within its original mating neighborhood with radius R to reproduce in its place, keeping the total population size constant.

An individual’s chance of reproducing (fecundity) is modified by the fitness component wi=exp(−Sdi2) (referred to as a “fitness” function for simplicity). Here, di=(1/Bm)∑k=1Bm|σki−ρki| is the normalized mito-nuclear distance for individual i and S is the strength of selection, such that S=0 corresponds to the absence of mito-nuclear selection. Perfectly matched genomes, di=0, corresponds to maximum fitness. Only the first Bm nuclear alleles interact with the mitochondrial DNA. Initially all nuclear alleles of all individuals are set to 0. For the mitochondrial DNA, we set the first fBm alleles to 1 (“mismatched”) and the remaining (1−f)Bm alleles to zero (“matched”) (Fig. 1). Strength of selection can also be expressed as S=1/(2σw2), where σw measures how large the mito-nuclear distance can be without a substantial decrease in fitness—when di=2σw, wi=e−1≈0.37 (31, 32).

Selection over mito-nuclear compatibility affects an individual’s fecundity by adjusting the base probability of not reproducing Q. In the absence of selection, the probability that an individual is not selected to reproduce in N trials with replacement is Q=(1−1/N)N≈e−1 for large N. In the presence of selection, this probability is modified by the individual’s fitness relative to the population’s maximum (wmax) and minimum (wmin) fitness: Qw(i)=2Q(wmax−w(i))/(wmax−wmin). Individuals with the highest fitness have a guaranteed chance of reproducing (Qw(i)=0 and Pw(i)=1), while those with the lowest fitness still have a small chance (Qw(i)=2Q and Pw=1−2Q≈0.26). Notice that, as it was defined, w does not directly translate into the number of offspring. This approach assumes that a minimal level of compatibility remains in natural populations, allowing individuals to survive even if they do not reproduce optimally, thereby ensuring the survival of the initial population. However, as the dynamics stabilize around a value of dw≈1/S, individuals with di>dw are unlikely to persist in the long-term population. A mating partner is selected from a pool of compatible individuals within the mating neighborhood with a probability proportional to their fitness. To maintain a constant population size, when an individual dies without reproducing, another one is selected from its mating neighborhood, according to fitness, to reproduce in its place.

We performed simulations considering a standard set of parameters used in previous demonstrations of the model (31, 32), ensuring the initiation of radiation and the establishment of equilibrium (when the number of species becomes constant) within a few hundred generations. The parameters values employed were as follows: nuclear and mitochondrial genomes of sizes B=1,500 and Bm=500, respectively; genetic mating threshold of G=75, equivalent to 0.05 B; population of N=1,300 individuals randomly distributed in L×L=100×100 living area; nuclear mutation probability of μ=2.5×10−4 per gene; mating neighborhood radius of R=5; and a diffusion probability of D=0.02. We varied the ratio μm/μ across 3, 5, 8, and 10. The simulations spanned a total of T=2,000 generations. Previous studies investigated how variations in these parameters affect speciation. In general, increasing G or R hinders speciation, while an increase in B or μ facilitates it, reducing the time to reach equilibrium. Increasing N proportionally increases the number of species (33, 39). Recent work (41) has demonstrated this model’s capability to scale to realistic scenarios, validating it for biologically relevant cases. We ran 50 independent simulations for each parameter set and present ensemble-averaged measurements.

We define “substitutions” as mutations that become fixed in the population, changing from an initial state of 0 to a final state of 1 or vice versa. Mutations in our model are neutral, meaning they have no impact on individual fitness alone, i.e., all mutations are equivalent. Furthermore, we do not distinguish between synonymous and nonsynonymous substitutions; broadly speaking, all substitutions can be interpreted as nonsynonymous.
