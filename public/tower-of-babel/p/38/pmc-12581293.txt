A mixed-method scoping review examining parental perspectives on heavy metal exposure in children
Authors: Prispa Mwila, J. Anitha Menon, Elin Opheim, Mercy Wamunyima Monde, Knut Hestad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeavy metal exposure is a public health problem, especially in vulnerable populations such as children. A growing body of scholarly research sheds light on the issue of heavy metal exposure among children. However, there are still some gaps in the understanding of the full spectrum of factors that influence awareness and knowledge about the effects of heavy metals on children, especially in African countries. Children exposed to heavy metals primarily receive care from their parents. This scoping study aims to methodically map and synthesise the current literature on parental perspectives on children’s exposure to heavy metals.MethodsThe methodology employed in this scoping review was based on the techniques outlined by Arksey and O’Malley, as well as the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The review assessed and further processed articles published in English between 1970 and 2023. The reviews encompassed studies conducted globally with no significant geographical limitations or various methodologies. This review primarily focused on qualitative studies in the search strategy. It also included survey and questionnaire studies, in addition to other search terms. The authors synthesised the results via descriptive and narrative analysis.ResultsThe scoping review examined and included 30 publications out of the 424 records that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study identified four primary elements that contribute to parental experiences: knowledge regarding the health consequences of heavy metal exposure, factors that contribute to elevated levels of heavy metal exposure in children, approaches to reduce heavy metal exposure, and factors that hinder or support behavioural modifications in parents of children with heavy metal exposure.ConclusionParents’ experiences with children exposed to lead vary by area and remain inadequately understood, particularly in low-income countries. There is an increased need for parental knowledge and active prevention measures regarding heavy metal exposure. The consequences of exposure can be dire, underscoring the urgency and seriousness of this public health issue. Therefore, further study is necessary to clarify the fundamental concepts that inform practice and policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25079-9.

Introduction

Heavy metals occur naturally in the Earth’s crust and become concentrated on the surface due to human activities [1], posing a significant problem due to their prolonged persistence and non-biodegradability after consumption [2, 3]. The presence of heavy metal pollution is a significant public health concern, particularly for populations that are at risk and susceptible [4, 5].

Heavy metals are metals or semimetals that potentially cause human or environmental toxicity [6, 7]. These include copper (Cu), cobalt (Co), manganese (Mn), cadmium (Cd), nickel (Ni), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), tin (Sn), arsenic (As), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb) [8, 9]. The World Health Organisation (WHO) ranks heavy metals, such as lead (Pb), as the most hazardous chemicals for public health [10, 11]. Furthermore, Pb impacts numerous bodily systems, leading to alterations in the body’s physiological and biochemical processes and dysfunction [12–14]. Exposure to Cd and Hg impacts the liver, lungs, placenta, central nervous system (CNS), and bones. It can also result in hepatic, immunological, and other effects [9, 15]. Cu, Ni, and Sn can also cause disturbances and damage in the human body [16, 17].

Heavy metals affect all age groups and sexes [18]. Compared with adults, children are more vulnerable to their toxicity for the following reasons: they have a higher metabolic rate; they encounter several ways of vulnerability (hand-to-mouth activities, putting objects in their mouths, during breastfeeding, and prenatally); their surface area is larger than that of adults; their body organs are still developing and are more susceptible to any changes; they are more active; and they inhale more toxins because they are close to the ground [19–23].

Parents significantly influence their children’s environmental behaviours; their opinions are important for public health planning [24]. Children exposed to heavy metals develop comorbid conditions and require complex care. Parents bear the brunt of caring for children with complex needs. According to Woodgate, healthcare systems in several countries have transitioned from government-supported settings to home-supported settings, thereby intensifying parenting roles [24, 25]. Parents play a crucial role in their children’s development and influence their participation in various community activities [26]. Parents of children with exposure are skilled at identifying their families’ and children’s needs.

Parents employ various methods to enhance the quality of their children’s lives, with these activities being influenced by their understanding of social, health, and environmental issues. Gaining insight into parents’ behaviours and awareness concerning their health challenges empowers both the community and healthcare professionals to help reduce children’s exposure to stressors, ultimately promoting better child development [24].

However, the literature lacks clarity regarding the specific experiences, activities, and concerns of parents caring for children exposed to heavy metals. Consequently, this scoping review aimed to consolidate existing publications on parents’ perspectives regarding children affected by exposure to heavy metals. The review involved gathering and analysing data from various databases related to heavy metals from a parent’s perspective.

Scoping review research question

What is known in the literature about parental perspectives on children’s exposure to heavy metals?

Methodology

The scoping review aims to systematically delineate and integrate parents’ perspectives regarding children exposed to varying levels of heavy metals through a comprehensive examination of the existing literature. The scoping review employed the methodologies delineated by Arksey and O’Malley to execute and document the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The approach put out by Arksey, and O’Malley is extensively utilised in the domain of scoping reviews [27, 28]. The review encompassed five essential stages: formulating the research question, selecting pertinent papers, charting the data, synthesising the findings, discussing them, and deriving conclusions [29].

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

This scoping review employed the population, concept, and context (PCC) approach to standardise the screening approach and facilitate the formulation of eligibility criteria. PCC was used, as JBI recommends it for scoping reviews due to its broad nature, which ensures methodological rigour and consistency with international guidelines [30].

In accordance with the PCC framework, for the population component, we included studies on parents, guardians, or primary caregivers of children between 0 and 18. Studies that exclusively documented the subject matter from the viewpoint of other stakeholders in childhood heavy metal exposure, such as healthcare workers or policymakers, were also incorporated into the scoping review. We excluded papers that did not cover the subject matter or involved parents of children older than 18.

For the concept component, the review included studies published in English in peer-reviewed journals that explored parents’ perspectives on children exposed to heavy metals. These concepts encompassed knowledge, awareness, perception, attitudes, experiences, factors, practices, and perceptions related to heavy metal exposure in children. The scoping review excluded studies that focused on other forms of chemical exposure in children, such as exposure to agricultural pesticides. We excluded studies not published in English.

Heavy metal exposure is prevalent in most parts of the world; therefore, this review included studies from diverse geographical regions. It also included studies addressing any environment where children could be exposed—such as the home, community, classroom, clinic, and mining sites—regardless of the year of publication, which fall under the context component. The review excluded studies that had no connection to child exposure to heavy metals and solely concentrated on occupational exposure. Additionally, reviews, editorial comments, commentary articles, theses, and grey literature were excluded to preserve uniformity in quality assessment and reporting. There are no universal peer review processes for unpublished work in the grey literature, making it difficult to assess quality. Systematic searching, retrieving, and validating grey literature can be challenging, as it is not thoroughly indexed in many of the central databases. The literature search was limited to peer-reviewed articles and compared with comparable previous reviews by limiting it to bibliographic databases to maximise coverage and reproduction of the search.

Sources of information

The scoping study examined qualitative research designs, quantitative research designs, and mixed-methods studies from peer-reviewed journal publications published in English between 1970 and 2023, all of which are accessible in full. Articles failing to satisfy the inclusion criteria were omitted from the study. Co-authors EH and MWM retrieved studies using the following databases, as specified in the search strategy: Scopus, Global Index Medicus, MEDLINE, SciELO, and CINAHL.

Search strategy

From March to May 2023, Prospero, PubMed, Epistemonikos, and the Cochrane Library were explicitly searched for reviews on the research question to avoid duplication of existing reviews. An initial search was conducted in Medline (OVID), PsycINFO (OVID), Global Index Medicus, and CINAHL (Ebsco) to identify articles on the topic while analysing the key concepts and index terms used to describe them in their titles and abstracts. The selection of databases is performed to reduce bias associated with the representation of geographical regions. The search terms were combined with keywords such as ‘heavy metal exposure’, ‘lead poisoning’, ‘cadmium’, ‘children’, ‘parents’, and ‘caregivers’ and adjusted to fit each database. Experienced medical librarians developed and peer-reviewed a sensitive search strategy. The search strategy consisted of relevant subject terms and text words, along with their synonyms, for heavy metal poisoning, children, and parents, using Boolean and proximity operators (see Fig. 1). It was adapted to different databases. Finally, we searched the following databases: Medline (OVID), PsycINFO (OVID), CINAHL + with Full Text (EBSCO), Scielo.org, Global Index Medicus, and Scopus. The whole strategy is available in Additional File 1. The search started on June 7, 2023, and ended on June 9, 2023.

Selection of relevant studies

EO and MWM, responsible for data extraction, retrieved 424 studies and exported them to EndNote to manage references and remove duplicates easily; 89 studies were removed via EndNote (Clarivate Analytics). References were screened for their titles and abstracts.

The reference lists of all included studies were screened to capture those that could have been missed during database searching. Three independent reviewers (KH, JAM, and PM) screened the 335 studies. Three reviewers (KH, JAM, and PM) independently screened each study using a predefined criteria form for abstract and title reviews. Any remaining conflicts were resolved through a discussion. Any conflict that may have arisen from the discussion was resolved through a vote.

Data extraction

The author also charted the results of parents’ impressions of heavy metal exposure in children based on the data charting form. The form captured relevant information based on the following extracted variables: bibliographic information, study characteristics, exposure details, key findings, and outcomes. As familiarity with the data increased, this part of the data extraction form was iteratively tested and polished. All duplicates were identified and removed to ensure the comprehensive extraction of data. After eliminating duplicates, the remaining studies were evaluated based on their full text, and then the full-text papers were reviewed for eligibility by perusing the abstracts. All full-text articles that failed to meet the qualifying requirements were excluded. Moreover, the papers retrieved following the assessment were then reviewed by the three authors (PM, JAM, and KH) to verify that they met the inclusion criteria. PM, KH, and AJM consistently communicated and arranged these important discoveries under general themes, developing them into a deductive conceptual framework. Based on the aggregated data, the EH and MWM examined and commented on the final themes.

Synthesis and presentation of results

The purpose of a scoping review is to summarise the findings or results from the included sources. This review analysed the extracted data using basic descriptive statistics, such as frequency counts, populations, and study locations, and presented the results in tables and graphs [30]. In this scoping review, the authors used a narrative synthesis method to compile the results of all included studies. The authors chose this method because the study designs and outcomes differed, providing an opportunity to combine qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods evidence. This review adhered to the narrative synthesis methodology established by Popay et al. [31]. The results were then iteratively and inductively arranged into emergent thematic categories using a narrative synthesis. The first step involved compiling study data into structured tables that outline the study’s objectives, demographics, locations, and key findings. The second step involved using thematic analysis to identify patterns and groups within and between the studies. The process entailed classifying material sequentially based on its significance and formulating descriptive themes that encapsulated the patterns each author identified within the data, along with the review objective, giving rise to central themes. The third step involved examining relationships between studies by analysing their thematic findings across different regions of the world, subgroups of the population, and the study’s design. The synthesis was conducted with a clear focus on the quality of the studies, ensuring that the interpretation was based on the best available evidence. This synthesis method enabled the combination of different types of evidence, the identification of patterns that vary by situation, and the revelation of insights that can inform the design of interventions and the development of policies.

Summary and reporting of results

The lead author presented the results via a chart to indicate the articles’ time, location, methodology, and emerging themes.

Themes

Knowledge and awareness about heavy metal exposure

Thirteen research papers documented the awareness of parents and carers regarding heavy metal exposure, testing, sources, and health implications.

Parental awareness of heavy metals varies considerably depending on the context. In certain cities in the USA, including Pennsylvania and New York, some parents within these communities have a reasonable understanding of lead exposure and its related health hazards. However, their comprehension of the availability of remediation funding to address these issues remains limited [32–34]. Researchers, including Adachi-Mejia et al. from the USA, indicated minimal to negligible awareness of arsenic exposure and its health implications within the research population [35].

The study indicated that research in Bangladesh, South Africa, and Egypt identified minimal awareness of lead exposure among mothers and pregnant women residing in high-risk communities [36–38]. The participants occasionally failed to adhere to dietary and Pb protection programmes [38]. Furthermore, research has indicated that several participants in Australia and the USA have recognised household sources of heavy metal exposure and implemented preventive actions [39, 40]. Studies conducted in nations like South Africa, Nigeria, and certain regions of the USA, including North Carolina, possessed insufficient awareness regarding domestic sources, preventive strategies, and the impact of nutrition on lead exposure; consequently, communities fostered a negative disposition towards prevention and testing [37, 41–43]. Aziz et al. in Bangladesh observed that elements affecting awareness of heavy metal exposure, particularly arsenic (As), encompass awareness campaigns, educational attainment, and the presence of children, young women, and elderly individuals within homes [44].

Factors contributing to heavy metal exposure in children

Six research studies outlined some factors contributing to the high levels of heavy metals in children. Some of the factors associated with high blood lead levels (BLLs) in children include consuming paint chips and inhaling lead paint dust [01]. The environmental factors associated with high blood lead levels (BLL) in children include scratched paint Pb levels, interior window Pb levels, and house floor dust Pb concentrations [45]. In a study conducted in Nigeria, the prevalence of high Pb levels in children was high, requiring chelation therapy. The factors associated with BLLs in children included exposure to dust particles contaminated with mined lead [46].

Social factors can also be associated with high Pb levels in children, including socioeconomic status, water quality, fish consumption, cooking with an open fire, and proximity to chicken coops [46, 47]. Notably, the prevalence of Pb poisoning was higher in the Chinese study population than in other developed nations. The factors associated with high BLLs in children include the neglect of handwashing and the consumption of foods such as puffed grains and eggs [48]. Other risk factors associated with Pb exposure in South Indian children include low birth weight, living in houses painted with lead-containing paint, living in houses older than 10 years, and drinking lead-contaminated water [49]. High BLLs in South African children are associated with factors such as teenage motherhood, low education, male gender, and lack of mobile phone ownership [50].

Strategies for mitigating heavy metal exposure

Nine studies investigated the importance of interventions in reducing human exposure to heavy metals. This review drove home the need to adopt a multisectoral approach to preventing lead poisoning. Moreover, other countries could adapt and use the multisectoral approach if they considered social, cultural, and political factors [51]. In the USA, successful methods for educating the immigrant community include door-to-door campaigns, health fairs, workshops, and native-language public announcements [45]. Other scholars have emphasised the importance of media campaigns in addressing this issue. Moreover, the campaign successfully reached its target audience, influencing parents’ awareness of Pb prevention measures, increasing their knowledge of these measures, and encouraging parents to ensure that landlords comply with all relevant laws [52]. Reports indicate that some parents diligently carry out the recommended Pb prevention tasks because of the noted campaign [32]. Interventions related to Pb are important, but they must contain correct information. Online educational materials lack comprehensive information on the health effects of Pb exposure, have inconsistent strategies, lack empirical evidence, and fail to establish safe Pb thresholds [53].

Despite some inconsistencies in online information, well-established preventive measures are important for reducing BLL levels. These interventions reduced both indoor and outdoor exposure to Pb in the community, which was possible [39]. Correct programs to increase knowledge about the effects of Pb exposure and prevention methods can positively impact children’s neurodevelopment [54].

Community members in Bangladesh exhibited significant changes in their behaviour after being educated on interventions, as evidenced by a reduction in As levels and by moving away from contaminated drinking wells to safer ones [55]. This review also noted that adults’ satisfaction levels with Pb and other heavy metal elimination interventions varied significantly, with some being satisfied with the quality of work and others being dissatisfied [56].

Factors impeding and facilitating behavioural change

Six studies collectively addressed behaviours and their potential for change, identifying several elements that may hinder or support a parent’s ability to adopt behavioural change programs. Researchers looking into why parents in the USA do not get their children’s blood tested for Pb found several problems, including not knowing how dangerous it is and where it can come from, like dirt, old paint, and pencils; having to travel far to testing places; feeling uneasy about the testing; and using complicated brochures, television ads, and hard-to-understand videos [57].

The barriers to testing that were investigated may include institutional fears, the absence of visible symptoms of Pb exposure, the effort required for a prevention strategy, the presentation of familiar information, denial of the problem, and a misunderstanding of the information [45, 58]. Factors facilitating behavioural change include teaching simple strategies, making materials fun, demonstrating important concepts, and providing feedback after tasks are executed [58].

The scoping review identified community and family factors that affect caregivers’ decisions to screen children’s Pb levels. These factors include shame and blaming parents, the stigma attached to high Pb levels, outreach programs not addressing community needs, birth order, gender differences, diminished Pb levels, and reversible effects [59]. Stigma alluded to the “act of being exposed to lead contamination and the subsequent health effects caused by lead,” which participants described as “being leaded.” The term “leaded” was coined with various connotations, and despite its frequent use in jests, it was never a positive assessment. It generally denotes that an individual is clumsy, uncoordinated, stupid, or sluggish” [59].

Some scholars have also noted that barriers, such as reduced service capacity, community perceptions, organisational factors, and differences in socioeconomic status, may hinder children’s participation in screening services in South Wales. They also discovered that some enabling factors may be present, such as culturally acceptable models and routine health checkups associated with lead screening [60]. They also reported that a successful program facilitator involved quality training, community support, positive relationships, free toys and books, and nonmonetary rewards for providers. Some barriers included increased workload, complex group-based interventions, and logistics issues in providing toys and books [61].

The quality of the studies

The MMAT can evaluate the quality of empirical studies, namely, primary research derived from experimentation, observation, or simulation. The Mixed Method Quality Appraisal Tool (MMAT) Version 2018 [62] was employed to evaluate the quality of the articles. Authors PM, JAM, and KH assess the quality of the papers by considering the following components: the relevance of the study’s purpose, methodological rigour, study design, participant recruitment, data collection, data analysis, and the presentation of findings. The score varied from minimal to maximal. Supplementary File 3 contains the entire checklist.

Discussion

A scoping review of 30 studies published between 1997 and 2023 mapped the experiences and perceptions of parents or caregivers of children exposed to heavy metals. The review revealed that research has shifted from a North American and European perspective to a global one, reflecting an increase in research activity and a growing global awareness of the issue. Over half of the studies were conducted in high-income and upper-middle-income countries. Scholars from low-income countries, on the other hand, have begun to explore the experiences of parents of children exposed to heavy metals. The problem is multifaceted and impacts several elements. The low research output in low- to middle-income countries can be attributed to multiple factors, including cultural, policy, and government support, as well as the availability of research funding, motivation, community support, industry, development partners, and donor agencies [63].

This scoping review highlights the intricate interaction among awareness, perception, and action in how parents react to the hazards of heavy metal exposure in children. Particularly among LMICs, the narrative synthesis revealed a general lack of knowledge about parental perspectives and pointed to the value of culturally contextualised treatments [32, 34, 37, 38, 41, 43]. Compared to parents from high- and upper-middle-income nations, those from low-income nations were less aware of the health impacts of heavy metals on children [35, 36, 38, 39, 44]. Minimal knowledge and a lack of awareness should alarm all conscientious individuals in developing nations, as many mining activities occur in these countries [64]. It is also important for diligent workers to motivate parents, caregivers, and children to participate in health exams and environmental assessments [32]. Industrialised countries have long dealt with such problems and implemented significant measures to reduce the presence of heavy metals, such as Pb, in the environment. The significantly reduced BLL is evidence of the efficacy of these efforts in such a population [32].

This review revealed many factors contributing to high levels of heavy metals in children from the parents’ perspective. The review revealed four factors that may contribute to high levels of heavy metals, such as lead and arsenic, in the blood: environmental, societal, maternal, and household factors [40, 45–50]. The environmental factors included ingesting paint chips, inhaling lead paint dust, elevated Pb levels in scratched paint, high lead concentrations in interior windowsills and house floor dust, living in a house older than 10 years, and exposure to lead dust particles [40, 45]. Social factors included socioeconomic level, the water source for cooking, fish-eating, cooking near open fires and chicken coops, intake of puffed grains and eggs, hand hygiene practices, and consumption of Pb-contaminated water [46–49]. Maternal and household factors included low birth weight, adolescent motherhood, low educational attainment, the male gender of the child, and the absence of household ownership of a mobile phone. The authors emphasise the importance of educational campaigns and health promotion programs aimed at populations at high risk for health complications [49, 50].

This scoping review highlights the importance of interventions in preventing heavy metal exposure and the urgent need for a multisectoral approach. All stakeholders must be involved in addressing this issue to ensure effective prevention [51]. The approach can be tailored to suit different nations by considering social, cultural, and political factors. In industrialised nations, such as the United States, effective interventions include door-to-door campaigns, health fairs, parental education, workshops, and public announcements in native languages [33]. In the current technological era, we can employ additional strategies such as online education and media campaigns. The campaign effectively reached its target audience and increased parents’ awareness of Pb prevention measures [52, 53]. It is worth noting that online educational materials on heavy metal exposure should be sufficient, consistent, and evidence-based [53]. Through such interventions, parents must acknowledge that their children are vulnerable to heavy metal poisoning, such as Pb exposure. Parents must acknowledge that their children may be susceptible to Pb poisoning, even in the absence of overt symptoms. Parents will refrain from screenings and preventative behaviours if they lack awareness of the severity of the consequences. Parents need to understand the benefits associated with screening and preventive measures [40].

Although some regions have initiated successful educational programs, the findings suggest that awareness alone does not always translate into behaviour changes. More general contextual and institutional hurdles, including poverty, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, and mistrust in public institutions, affect parents’ ability to act. Other factors include diminished service capacity, community attitudes, organisational variables, and socioeconomic status disparities [33, 57, 59, 60]. Another obstacle to behavioural change is cultural beliefs. Parents believe that cultural barriers and stigma play a role in delaying their decision to seek screening services for their children. Parents often say that worries about negative opinions and blame, along with outreach programs not connecting with community views, the order of their children’s birth, differences between boys and girls, lower lead levels as kids get older, and the fact that Pb exposure can be fixed are some of the reasons that stop them from getting help. Public health research, which highlights the structural factors of health behaviour, supports this finding [40, 42, 54].

Some elements that could promote behavioural change include teaching basic tactics, making educational materials more engaging, demonstrating all key ideas, and giving feedback once tasks are completed, as reported by parents. Other potential facilitators may include regular health checkups linked to Pb screenings and incorporating culturally acceptable examples [60]. Research on experts’ and key informants’ perspectives suggests that one of the most beneficial aspects is that the program’s facilitators should have top-notch training and abilities. They should receive support from the community, family, and supervisors; foster strong relationships between participants and providers; offer toys and books to children at no cost; and provide nonmonetary benefits to providers. Some barriers that may hinder this process include a heightened workload, intricate group-based interventions, and logistical challenges in supplying toys and books [61].

According to the results, policymakers should implement a multilevel preventive strategy to decrease the risk of heavy metal exposure in children. They should implement culturally appropriate community education and outreach programs to help communities understand the source, the signs, and the effects of exposure to heavy metals. Such programs may vary depending on local beliefs, languages, and health literacy levels. Diverse populations, particularly those disadvantaged or marginalised, must find them practical and accessible. Outreach will involve trusted community figures, such as local health workers, teachers, and religious leaders, to establish trust and encourage participation. It is imperative to enhance the environmental health literacy of parents and caregivers. Teaching materials should be disseminated in various ways, such as schools, clinics, community centres, and digital media. These materials should cover more immediate interventions that parents can take to limit exposure, such as safe water usage, foods that help detoxify heavy metals (e.g., calcium- and iron-rich foods), and knowledge about household items containing toxic metals.

This scoping review offers interesting findings on parents’ perspectives on heavy metal exposure in children; however, limitations need to be considered. The review included only English-language studies, as non-English-speaking content was excluded, given that heavy metal exposure is prevalent in several parts of the world, including non-English-speaking nations. This inclusion reduced the number of studies in the review and created some bias toward English-speaking countries. The review also excluded grey literature, including proposals and reports, due to the large number of papers in the scientific literature. Although this choice improved the quality and comparability of included studies, it may have resulted in under-representation of relevant evidence from grey or community sources. Transition studies mainly depended on parents’ reports on knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours. Data may be limited due to recall bias or social desirability bias, which may inflate levels of awareness or protective behaviours. Many studies were cross-sectional, offering a momentary view of parental views at one time point. The review was therefore unable to ascertain trajectories of parental awareness and behaviour development over time, especially in response to interventions or policy change. Another limitation is the lack of direct parental interviews on the subject matter. The review primarily used a quantitative research design for most studies and included only one article.

Despite excluding some studies, the review encompassed a wide range of literature from all continents, offering a global perspective. A scoping review with a narrative summary was chosen; however, more precise methods, such as meta-synthesis and meta-analysis, may be more appropriate as the literature expands. Additionally, the review included all research without considering its quality.

Conclusion

A review of parents’ experiences regarding heavy metal exposure among children revealed disparities in knowledge, behaviour, concern, and attitudes across different societies. The scoping review revealed that studies conducted in LMICs have a limited understanding of heavy metal exposure, its effects, sources of exposure, and prevention measures. Delayed interventions may lead to its worsening, as heavy metals may cause cognitive decline, behavioural problems, and other health-related issues. Such effects may lead to a lack of achievement of personal, social, and national goals. There is a need for additional research to investigate parents’ experiences with children who have been exposed to heavy metals in LMICs to identify gaps that may require more interventions and measures that are culturally appropriate and do not cause strain on limited resources. Further studies are needed to evaluate education interventions, explore strategies for behaviour change among caregivers, and monitor the consequences of policy implementations aimed at reducing exposure. It is hoped that governments and non-governmental organisations (NGOs) will conduct awareness campaigns in at-risk areas and increase the availability of screenings and regulations to limit children’s exposure to heavy metals in the environment.
