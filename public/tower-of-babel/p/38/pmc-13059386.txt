Nutritional status and its determinants in obese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery
Authors: Ling Deng, Yuli Tao, Haiyan Wang, Yuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveObese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) face unique long-term nutritional risks following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) surgery. This study aims to evaluate postoperative nutritional status in this population, identify key preoperative predictive factors, and develop and validate a clinical scoring system for predicting the risk of postoperative malnutrition.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study consecutively included obese T2DM patients (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 32.5 kg/m²) who underwent RYGB between January 2020 and January 2023. The primary outcome was a composite endpoint of malnutrition at 12 months postoperatively, defined as moderate anemia (hemoglobin < 100 g/L) or hypoalbuminemia (albumin < 30 g/L). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors. Discrimination of the nomogram-based predictive model was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was evaluated using bootstrap calibration plots.ResultsA total of 195 patients were included in the final analysis, with a 12-month postoperative malnutrition incidence of 31.8% (62/195). Female gender (odds ratio [OR] = 2.702, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.034–7.058) and diabetes duration ≥ 5 years (OR = 4.890, 95% CI: 1.416–16.894) were independent risk factors for postoperative malnutrition, whereas higher preoperative levels of albumin, hemoglobin, ferritin, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D were protective factors. The joint predictive model incorporating these six factors demonstrated excellent discriminative ability for predicting postoperative malnutrition and good calibration.ConclusionThe preoperative clinical risk prediction model based on six readily available indicators shows robust predictive performance, guiding individualized nutritional management.Keywords: Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Malnutrition, Predictive model, Risk score

Introduction

The global prevalence of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has made metabolic surgery important therapeutic strategy. Particularly, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) is an approach to manage severe obesity with T2DM [1]. This surgery has been shown to achieve substantial and sustained weight loss, improve glycemic control, and even induce remission of diabetes [2]. However, the gastrointestinal anatomical and physiological alterations induced by RYGB also introduce unique long-term nutritional challenges [3]. These risks arise from the interplay of multiple factors [4]: (i) preexisting nutritional imbalances and chronic disease-related inflammatory states; (ii) surgical-induced intake restriction and bypass of critical absorptive sites such as the duodenum; and (iii) persistent postoperative influences of patient adherence, changes in gut hormones, and microbiota. Importantly, the inherent metabolic dysregulation in T2DM may further exacerbate these risks, rendering this patient population particularly vulnerable.

Current studies largely focus on isolated nutrient deficiencies or single influencing factors in the general bariatric surgery population, with limited systematic investigation into the risk of comprehensive postoperative malnutrition in high-risk patients with obesity and T2DM. Moreover, there is a paucity of predictive models based on commonly available preoperative clinical indicators. The absence of such predictive tools restricts clinicians’ ability to accurately identify high-risk individuals preoperatively and implement targeted nutritional interventions.

In this context, the present study aims to conduct a single-center retrospective cohort analysis to characterize postoperative nutritional status and its determinants in obese patients with T2DM undergoing RYGB. The core objectives are to identify and quantify key preoperative predictive factors and to develop a simple and practical clinical risk scoring system, so that we may provide evidence-based guidance for preoperative risk assessment and individualized nutritional monitoring strategies.

Participants and methods

Participants

This single-center retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (Approval No. 2024-201-02). We consecutively enrolled obese patients with T2DM who underwent RYGB surgery at our hospital between January 1, 2020, and January 1, 2023. Surgical indications strictly adhered to international and domestic metabolic surgery guidelines [5].

Inclusion criteria: (i) received RYGB at our hospital; (ii) confirmed preoperative diagnosis of T2DM with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 32.5 kg/m² [6, 7]; (iii) age ≥ 18 years; (iv) complete and traceable preoperative baseline data and regular postoperative follow-up records for at least 12 months.

Exclusion criteria: (i) concomitant severe medical conditions (e.g., congenital heart disease, chronic kidney disease stage 4 or higher); (ii) prior major gastrointestinal surgery other than RYGB; (iii) preoperative diagnosis of severe anemia (hemoglobin < 80 g/L) or severe hypoalbuminemia (albumin < 25 g/L); (iv) loss to follow-up or > 20% missing critical data during follow-up; (v) pregnancy or lactation.

Sample size calculation

After strict screening, 195 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. According to consensus on predictive model methodology, model stability depends on the number of events per variable (EPV). Assuming an expected 30% incidence of the outcome, this cohort would generate approximately 58 outcome events. Following the conservative rule of at least 10 outcome events per candidate predictor variable, this event number is sufficient to support robust construction and analysis of a model including 5–6 predictors [8, 9].

Data collection and quality control

To ensure accuracy, completeness, and consistency of retrospective data, a standardized data management process and quality control measures were implemented.

Extraction of standardized data

Two trained researchers independently extracted data from the hospital electronic medical record (EMR) system and laboratory information system using a predesigned standardized electronic data extraction form. The form covered all predefined variables, including demographic characteristics, clinical indicators, and follow-up outcomes.

Data entry and consistency check

A study database was established using EpiData 3.1 software with double independent entry. After entry, all discrepancies were checked using logical verification in the software and manual comparison, with reference to the original medical records for final adjudication. To further evaluate data consistency, 10% of entered variables were randomly rechecked, yielding an inter-rater Kappa value of 0.87, indicating excellent consistency.

Handling of missing and outlier values

For missing key variables (e.g., vitamin B12 levels at specific follow-up time points), data were supplemented through cross-checking paper medical records, outpatient records, and laboratory information systems. If data could not be retrieved, the value was treated as missing in the corresponding analysis. Physiologically impossible or highly suspicious extreme outliers were assessed in the context of the patient’s concurrent clinical status (e.g., severe dehydration, hemoconcentration). Values that could not be reasonably explained and were highly suggestive of laboratory or entry errors were treated as missing. This standardized procedure maximized the reliability of the retrospective data and provided a solid foundation for subsequent analyses.

Collection of variables

All baseline data were obtained preoperatively via the hospital EMR system. Demographic and clinical variables: age, gender, BMI, diabetes duration (Diabetes duration was categorized into a short-duration group (< 5 years) and a long-duration group (≥ 5 years) based on the time since diagnosis. This clinically intuitive cutoff was selected because it represents a well-established threshold for accelerated β-cell function decline and complication risk in T2DM [10]. While dichotomization may reduce statistical power compared to treating duration as a continuous variable, it provides a clear, actionable criterion for preoperative risk assessment, which aligns with the pragmatic intent of our prediction tool. We recognize that this approach may limit the detection of more nuanced, continuous relationships between diabetes duration and nutritional risk.(Duration was calculated from the date of confirmed diabetes diagnosis as documented in the medical records.), smoking history, alcohol history, body fat, visceral fat, waist-to-hip ratio (measured using InBody 720 body composition analyzer). Laboratory indicators: serum albumin, ferritin, vitamin B12, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, C-reactive protein (CRP), hemoglobin, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Complications: hypertension, hyperlipidemia.

Nutritional status assessment: The primary outcome was a 12-month postoperative composite endpoint of malnutrition (yes/no), defined as the occurrence of either: (i) moderate anemia (hemoglobin < 100 g/L), based on World Health Organization anemia diagnostic criteria and commonly used clinical thresholds; or (ii) hypoalbuminemia (albumin < 30 g/L), a recognized marker of disease-related malnutrition. This composite endpoint is based on its accessibility in clinical practice, strong reproducibility, and ability to reflect significant deterioration in protein-energy reserve, which is a core monitoring indicator for postoperative nutrition management [11, 12]. It should be explicitly noted that this endpoint primarily captures laboratory-based nutritional abnormalities and is not intended as a surrogate for comprehensive nutritional status (such as micronutrient deficiencies, sarcopenia, or functional status). In this study, it is defined as a clearly delineated, scope-defined outcome measure for risk prediction.

Statistical analysis

Data entry and analysis were performed using SPSS 27.0 software, with data processing and statistical analyses conducted in R 4.5.0. Continuous variables were first tested for normality. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-sample t-tests; non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate logistic regression to construct the predictive model. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to assess multicollinearity (VIF < 5 indicated no significant multicollinearity). Forward stepwise regression using the likelihood ratio test was applied to select independent predictors. We acknowledge that stepwise selection may increase the risk of model optimism; however, our adherence to the EPV ≥ 10 rule and the use of bootstrapping for internal validation aim to mitigate overfitting. A nomogram was then constructed based on the final model. Model performance was evaluated as follows:

Results

Incidence of postoperative malnutrition and distribution of related complications

Among the 195 patients included in the final analysis, 62 (31.8%) developed the composite endpoint of malnutrition at 12 months post-RYGB (malnutrition group), while the remaining 133 (68.2%) served as controls. The incidence of specific nutrition-related complications in the entire cohort was as follows: anemia 28.7% (56/195), hypoalbuminemia 15.9% (31/195), iron deficiency 32.8% (64/195), vitamin B12 deficiency 22.6% (44/195), and vitamin D deficiency/insufficiency 51.3% (100/195).

Comparison of baseline characteristics between malnutrition and control groups

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, preoperative BMI, preoperative weight, comorbid hypertension/hyperlipidemia, smoking/alcohol history, body fat percentage, visceral fat, or waist-to-hip ratio (all P > 0.05). The malnutrition group had a higher proportion of females and a significantly higher proportion of patients with diabetes duration ≥ 5 years compared to the control group (Table 1).

Analysis identified six independent predictors of malnutrition at 12 months post-RYGB (P < 0.05). Based on ORs and 95% CIs, female gender (OR = 2.702, 95% CI: 1.034–7.058) and diabetes duration ≥ 5 years (OR = 4.890, 95% CI: 1.416–16.894) were risk factors. Preoperative albumin (OR = 0.682, 95% CI: 0.557–0.834), preoperative hemoglobin (OR = 0.850, 95% CI: 0.800–0.904), preoperative ferritin (OR = 0.984, 95% CI: 0.974–0.994), and preoperative 25-hydroxyvitamin D (OR = 0.909, 95% CI: 0.845–0.978) were protective factors.

Discriminative performance of individual predictors and the joint model

The joint predictive model demonstrated optimal discrimination for postoperative malnutrition (AUC = 0.915, 95% CI: 0.875–0.955). Among individual predictors, preoperative hemoglobin had the strongest independent predictive value (AUC = 0.827, 95% CI: 0.759–0.894), followed by preoperative albumin and ferritin (both AUC = 0.751). Gender, diabetes duration, and preoperative 25-hydroxyvitamin D had relatively limited predictive ability (AUC: 0.574–0.624). DeLong tests confirmed that the AUC of the joint prediction model was significantly higher than that of any single predictor (all P < 0.05) (Table 4; Fig. 1).

Development and validation of the clinical risk scoring system

Based on multivariate logistic regression, the six independent predictors (gender, diabetes duration, preoperative albumin, hemoglobin, ferritin, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D) were incorporated into the model. Regression coefficients (β values) were converted into clinically practical integer scores using the following formula for each predictor: Score_i = (β_i / sum of all |β|) × 100. This proportional scaling resulted in a total risk score ranging from 0 to 100 points, where a higher score indicates a greater predicted risk of postoperative malnutrition. Using the final multivariate model, a visual nomogram was constructed for clinical risk prediction (Fig. 2A).

Calibration was assessed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). As shown in Fig. 2B, the calibration curve closely aligned with the ideal diagonal line (predicted probability = observed probability), indicating good agreement between predicted and actual outcomes.

Clinical net benefit was further evaluated using DCA. As shown in Fig. 2C, within a wide threshold probability range (~ 5%–65%), applying the risk scoring system for clinical decisions (e.g., intensified nutritional intervention for high-risk patients) yielded a higher net benefit than either “intervene in all patients” or “intervene in none” strategies, confirming its practical clinical utility.

Discussion

This study observed a postoperative malnutrition incidence of 31.8%, which falls at the upper-middle range of previously reported RYGB-related nutritional risk (20%–40%) [13], consistent with the risk spectrum in the general bariatric surgery population. However, the composition characteristics warrant particular attention. Unlike prior studies that primarily focused on single nutrient deficiencies (e.g., iron, vitamin B12) [14, 15], this phenomenon is closely related to the pathophysiological background of T2DM itself [16]. Chronic inflammation and insulin resistance may upregulate hepcidin, inhibiting iron absorption and mobilization, predisposing patients to “latent iron deficiency” preoperatively [17], which is further exacerbated by RYGB bypassing the duodenum, the primary site of iron absorption [18]. Similarly, obesity and T2DM are commonly associated with impaired vitamin D metabolism and low baseline levels. Postoperatively, fat malabsorption further reduces intake of fat-soluble vitamin D, compounded by potential behavioral changes such as reduced sun exposure, resulting in sustained high risk of vitamin D deficiency [19]. The relatively lower rate of vitamin B12 deficiency (22.6%) in this study may be attributable to routine postoperative supplementation strategies but underscores the need for ongoing monitoring [20]. Collectively, these findings highlight that postoperative nutritional management in obese T2DM patients should be approached as a comprehensive, long-term system encompassing macro- and micronutrients, requiring dynamic assessment and individualized intervention. Therefore, developing a predictive tool capable of identifying high-risk individuals preoperatively is crucial for proactive, systematic nutritional management.

This study confirmed that female gender is an independent risk factor for postoperative malnutrition (OR = 2.702), consistent with most studies on nutritional outcomes after bariatric surgery [21]. Mechanistically, females are more likely to have insufficient iron stores preoperatively due to physiological reasons, and differences in dietary habits and absorptive physiology may reduce their compensatory capacity against postoperative malabsorption [22]. This explains why gender, as a simple clinical demographic indicator, has repeatedly been validated as an effective risk stratification factor in both our model and prior studies. More importantly, a diabetes duration of ≥ 5 years (OR = 4.890) was identified as one of the strongest risk factors. This study classified duration with a 5-year cutoff because this time point typically represents a critical phase marked by significant decline in pancreatic β-cell function, consolidation of the “metabolic memory” effect of chronic hyperglycemia, and an elevated risk of microvascular complications [10]. This finding carries crucial clinical implications. Patients with long-standing T2DM are often characterized by more severe pancreatic islet failure, persistent metabolic memory effects, and a more extensive underlying foundation of microvascular complications. These factors may collectively contribute to more significant preoperative nutritional and metabolic disturbances and a chronic catabolic state in these patients, leading to slower postoperative gastrointestinal recovery and potentially impaired intestinal absorption. This study is the first to quantify a diabetes duration of ≥ 5 years as a potent independent risk factor, moving beyond the traditional binary view of diabetes as merely “present or absent” and providing a more discriminative new dimension for preoperative risk assessment. This suggests that patients with long-standing T2DM should be considered a “very high-risk” subgroup for postoperative nutritional complications, regardless of their preoperative glycemic control level, thereby warranting more intensive and proactive intervention.

Preoperative nutritional and metabolic reserve indicators serve as key windows for predicting postoperative malnutrition risk. In this study, lower preoperative albumin, hemoglobin, ferritin, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels were significantly associated with postoperative nutritional deterioration. Among these, preoperative hemoglobin demonstrated the strongest independent predictive value (AUC = 0.827) and, together with albumin, forms a core reflection of protein-energy reserve status [23]. Higher preoperative hemoglobin suggests adequate iron stores and robust erythropoietic function, potentially enhancing tolerance to postoperative iron deficiency due to blood loss and absorption impairment. Higher albumin levels indicate good protein synthesis capacity and nutritional reserve, buffering metabolic stress during the postoperative period of restricted intake and malabsorption [24]. Ferritin and 25-hydroxyvitamin D, as key micronutrient reserves, directly indicate “subclinical deficiency” when low preoperatively; such patients are more likely to progress to overt clinical deficiency postoperatively, such as iron deficiency anemia or vitamin D deficiency-related bone metabolism disorders [25, 26]. These findings underscore the proactive importance of preoperative nutritional reserve assessment and supplementation, which may be as critical as postoperative interventions. Notably, HbA1c and CRP levels showed no significant differences between groups, indicating limited predictive value; this suggests that, for nutritional outcomes, long-term metabolic burden (e.g., diabetes duration) may outweigh short-term glycemic fluctuations or acute inflammatory markers, though the role of chronic low-grade inflammation in nutritional metabolism warrants further investigation.

Based on these findings, the risk prediction model developed in this study provides a valuable clinical tool. Integrating easily obtainable indicators, the model demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.915) and good calibration, effectively identifying patients at high risk for postoperative malnutrition. Its clinical utility is multifaceted [27]. First, the model enables precise preoperative risk stratification, allowing clinicians to identify extremely high-risk patients, such as long-duration T2DM females with low preoperative hemoglobin. Second, it guides proactive and individualized interventions, including preoperative nutritional prehabilitation and intensive education, which go beyond traditional “one-size-fits-all” postoperative supplementation. Third, it helps optimize resource allocation by focusing limited postoperative monitoring, such as laboratory tests and follow-up, on high-risk individuals, thereby improving efficiency and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, the nomogram-based visual tool facilitates patient understanding, awareness, and adherence to long-term nutritional management.

Compared to existing predictive models in bariatric surgery, which predominantly focus on outcomes such as weight loss efficacy, diabetes remission, or surgical complications [28, 29], our study introduces a novel, nutrition-centric preoperative risk tool tailored for obese patients with T2DM. While previous scores have incorporated nutritional variables (e.g., albumin, vitamins), they often do so within general bariatric cohorts or for predicting single-nutrient deficiencies [30]. The key novelties of our model lie in its (1) specific targeting of the high-risk T2DM subpopulation; (2) intentional restriction to readily available preoperative variables to maximize clinical utility at the point of surgical decision-making; (3) integrative approach that combines a clinically meaningful demographic factor (prolonged diabetes duration ≥ 5 years) with a panel of core nutritional reserve indicators (albumin, hemoglobin, ferritin, vitamin D); and (4) prediction of a composite laboratory-based nutritional endpoint designed to signal significant protein-energy reserve deterioration.

First, the single-center, retrospective design inherently limits the generalizability of our findings. Selection bias may have been introduced, particularly through the exclusion of patients with incomplete follow-up. Therefore, external validation through multicenter, prospective studies is essential to confirm the model’s broader applicability. Second, our definition of postoperative malnutrition relied on a composite of anemia and hypoalbuminemia, which, while pragmatic and clinically accessible, does not encompass other important aspects of nutritional status such as specific micronutrient deficiencies or sarcopenia. Consequently, the endpoint reflects laboratory-based nutritional deficits rather than comprehensive nutritional status, and the true prevalence of malnutrition may be underestimated. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Furthermore, combining two distinct laboratory abnormalities with potentially different underlying pathophysiologies (e.g., iron metabolism vs. protein synthesis) into a single composite endpoint may introduce heterogeneity and affect model calibration in subgroups predominantly affected by one component over the other.

Third, as a preoperative prediction tool, our model intentionally focuses on baseline variables available before surgery. Consequently, it does not incorporate important postoperative determinants of nutritional status, such as dietary adherence, supplement compliance, or weight-loss trajectory. While this enhances its clinical utility for preoperative planning, it limits its ability to predict outcomes influenced by postoperative behaviors and interventions. Fourth, the 12-month follow-up duration represents a key limitation. As nutritional deficiencies after RYGB may emerge, evolve, or worsen beyond this period, our findings are constrained to early-to-mid-term risk prediction and do not support conclusions regarding long-term nutritional outcomes. Fifth, despite employing bootstrapping for internal validation and adhering to event-per-variable guidelines, the use of stepwise variable selection and the lack of an external validation cohort remain methodological limitations. Our model’s performance should therefore be interpreted with caution until validated in independent, prospective datasets. Sixth, our model did not incorporate specific surgical technical variables, such as Roux limb length, biliopancreatic limb length, operative time, or surgeon experience, which are known to influence nutrient absorption. The omission of these factors limits the mechanistic interpretation of nutritional risk. Finally, the dichotomization of diabetes duration at 5 years, while clinically intuitive, may reduce statistical power and obscure more nuanced dose–response relationships.

Future research should expand sample size through multicenter prospective designs, incorporate postoperative variables and more comprehensive nutritional indicators to optimize model accuracy and generalizability, and explore biological mechanisms underlying long-duration T2DM effects on postoperative nutritional status (e.g., gut microbiota, barrier function). Randomized controlled trials targeting high-risk populations identified by this model are warranted to validate the efficacy of preoperative nutritional interventions, advancing predictive modeling toward precision prevention.

Conclusion

In summary, postoperative malnutrition is common and multifactorial in obese T2DM patients undergoing RYGB, influenced by gender, diabetes duration, and multiple preoperative nutritional reserve indicators. Female gender and diabetes duration ≥ 5 years are independent risk factors, whereas higher preoperative albumin, hemoglobin, ferritin, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels are protective. The predictive model based on these six factors demonstrates excellent discrimination and clinical applicability, enabling preoperative identification of high-risk patients and guiding individualized nutritional management. External validation and model optimization through multicenter prospective studies, inclusion of postoperative variables, and extended follow-up are warranted to provide more precise evidence-based guidance for postoperative nutritional care in obese T2DM patients.
