Examining Attachment Styles and Internal Working Models in Relation to Physical and Mental Health in an Italian Sample
Authors: Carmen Trumello, Angela Ferrucci, Lucia Lombardi, Milena Mancini, Ilaria Basadonne, Silvio Mignogna, Camilla Milano, Tânia Brandão, Alessandra Babore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectivethis study, grounded in attachment theory and situated within the debate between categorical and dimensional approaches to attachment, aims to pursue two complementary objectives: firstly, to examine the differences in physical and mental health across different attachment styles; secondly, to analyze the specific contribution of internal working models (IWMs) in explaining these variations, allowing for an integrated interpretation of the results.Methoda total of 548 Italian adults participated in the study by completing self-report questionnaires assessing the aforementioned study variables. For the first aim, univariate analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted to test differences in physical and mental health according to different attachment styles; for the second aim, multiple linear regression analyses were performed by including physical and mental health as dependent variables and IWMs of the self and IWMs of others as potential explanatory variables.Resultsthe findings suggest that individuals with secure and dismissing attachment styles, both characterized by positive IWMs of the self, report better mental health when compared to those with preoccupied and fearful patterns, who are characterized by negative IWMs of the self. As for the second aim, IWMs of the self emerged as a substantially stronger predictor of mental health than IWMs of others. No associations were found with physical health.Conclusionsoverall, the findings highlight the central role of attachment in adult mental health from an integrative perspective. While the categorical approach reveals clear differences in mental health across attachment styles, the dimensional analyses indicate that IWMs of the self constitute the primary mechanism underlying these differences. These results support the value of combining categorical and dimensional approaches to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between attachment and health.

Introduction

Attachment theory, formulated by John Bowlby between the 1960s and 1980s (1969, 1973, 1980), encompasses a motivational system designed to maintain homeostasis between feelings of security and the exploration of the environment. This system drives individuals to seek proximity and contact with specific figures, such as a child with a caregiver or an adult with a partner, who function as sources of physical and emotional security.

In the literature, adult attachment is conceptualized and measured both as a set of categories defining different types of individuals (attachment styles) and as a combination of continuous dimensions along which individuals vary (e.g. internal working models of the self and others). This dual conceptualization of attachment has prompted a long-standing debate, also known as the “types versus dimensions question”, regarding which approach provides the most appropriate interpretation of attachment data (Crowell et al., 2016; Lubiewska & Van de Vijver, 2020; Scharfe, 2016; Tartaro et al., 2023).

The resolution of this debate remains elusive, as categorical and dimensional approaches offer distinct advantages and complementary perspectives in the study of attachment. The categorical approach is primarily descriptive and comparative, allowing complex patterns of individual differences to be synthetized into comprehensive profiles. This characteristic makes it particularly valuable for communicative, interpretative, and clinical purposes. However, it implicitly considers attachment styles as discrete and mutually exclusive groups, thereby assuming that differences within the same attachment category are irrelevant or negligible. Focusing exclusively on the variation between groups and conceptualizing attachment styles as mutually exclusive could result in an oversimplified view of attachment, potentially leading to a loss of meaningful information (Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994a; Morales-Vives et al., 2025; Raby et al., 2021; Ravitz et al., 2010). Conversely, dimensional approaches reduce information loss associated with categorization and allow for the detection of more fine-grained individual differences. They are also generally characterized by higher statistical power and measurement accuracy. Nonetheless, dimensional measures tend to have lower narrative value than qualitative measures, making them less clinically intuitive and less immediately informative. Additionally, they fail to capture emergent properties that arise from specific combinations of dimensions (i.e. attachment styles) (Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994a; Raby et al., 2021; Ravitz et al., 2010). In the context of this category-vs.-dimension controversy, some studies have adopted integrative perspectives, suggesting a reconciliation between them, rather than viewing the two approaches as mutually exclusive. These contributions suggest that attachment assessment should combine both approaches, in order to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the construct and its associations with theoretically relevant variables (Adamczyk & Bookwala, 2013; Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991; Meredith et al., 2006; Morales-Vives et al., 2021, 2025).

In line with this integrative perspective, the present study adopted both categorical and dimensional approaches to investigate the relationship between attachment and physical and mental health. To assess both categorical and dimensional attachment, we used the Relationship Questionnaire (RQ), a widely adopted measure developed by Bartholomew and colleagues in line with their two-dimensional, four-category theoretical model of adult attachment (Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991; Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994b). The two dimensions refer to individuals’ internal working models (IWMs) of the self and others, that is, internal representations that implicitly organize experiences of oneself and significant others. These models develop from early experiences with attachment figures and, although initially specific to individual relationships, progressively become generalized, functioning as cognitive–emotional filters that guide relational behavior (Bowlby, 1969). Each IWM can be either positive or negative. Positive IWMs of the self reflect a sense of self-worth and lovability, while negative ones are associated with self-doubt and unworthiness. Similarly, others may be viewed as trustworthy and supportive (positive IWMs of others), or as rejecting and unreliable (negative IWMs of others). The intersection of these two dimensions results in four attachment styles: secure, preoccupied, dismissing and fearful. These styles constitute systematic ways of relating to oneself and others, encompassing expectations, needs, emotional regulation, and social behavior (Shaver & Mikulincer, 2002). The secure style is characterized by positive IWMs of both the self and others, facilitating a healthy balancing between autonomy and dependence. The preoccupied style involves a negative IWM of the self and a positive IWM of others, often leading to excessive dependence and a strong desire for intimacy. The dismissing style reflects a positive IWM of the self and a negative IWM of others, typically associated with minimizing the importance of intimacy and compensating with extreme independence and self-sufficiency. The fearful style is characterized by negative IWMs of both the self and others, resulting in a pervasive distrust, fear of rejection and avoidance of intimacy to prevent emotional suffering.

While Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991) originally proposed this two-dimensional, four-category model, their empirical work focused only on the categorical classification of attachment styles. Subsequently, Griffin and Bartholomew (1994b) refined the measurement model by testing the validity and reliability of the two dimensions underlying the four attachment categories. Therefore, whereas Bartholomew and Horowitz operationalized the four attachment styles, Griffin and Bartholomew translated the IWMs into measurable dimensional constructs.

Attachment and health styles, internal working models

According to the World Health Organization, health is defined as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity” (WHO, 2023, p. VIII). Based on this definition, health encompasses multiple dimensions and cannot be understood solely as an individual condition, but also as a relational phenomenon that arises, is maintained and may be restored within attachment bonds. The quality of these relationships plays a crucial role in shaping emotional regulation processes, stress response and both psychological and physical well-being (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2012; Pietromonaco & Beck, 2019; Santoro et al., 2021; Spruit et al., 2020). Although several studies have documented differences in health outcomes across attachment styles, limited research has examined the unique contributions of IWMs of the self and others in explaining these differences. IWMs are a core element of attachment theory and are fundamental to understanding the correlates and outcomes of attachment, as well as the nature and effectiveness of clinical interventions. Despite their extensive citation in academic literature, these models are predominantly employed for interpretative purposes rather than being empirically evaluated (Thompson et al., 2022).

Compared to secure and dismissing individuals, preoccupied and fearful attachment styles have been consistently associated with poorer mental health (e.g., reduced general well-being, impaired social functioning, and emotional distress) as well as worse physical health outcomes, including greater bodily pain, lower general health perception, and physical limitations (Mayangsari et al., 2024; Platts et al., 2023; Santoro et al., 2021). Notably, the two attachment styles associated with poorer health outcomes are those characterized by negative IWMs of the self, suggesting that this dimension may exert a stronger influence on health than IWMs of others. To our knowledge, only one study, conducted in a clinical population, has partially addressed this issue by directly investigating the relationship between IWMs and mental health. As indicated by this research (Kovács et al., 2022), patients with a negative IWM of the self reported a greater psychological burden than those with a positive IWM.

Overall, these findings seem to underline that health differences between attachment styles are mainly attributable to IWMs of the self, rather than IWMs of others. Compared to categorical classifications, dimensional approaches allow researchers to capture more nuanced aspects of the associations between attachment and related variables and could provide a unifying conceptual framework that helps organize research on adult attachment (Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991; Raby et al., 2021). Nevertheless, exploring attachment styles beyond the two dimensions offers significant advantages and can add interpretative clarity. Indeed, the four styles exhibit emergent properties that cannot be fully inferred from the two underlying dimensions, as they are patterns derived from the combination of the dimensions of IWMs (Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994a; Scharfe, 2016).

Based on these different assumptions, the present study examined both categorical and dimensional attachment in relation to physical and mental health, in order to more deeply explore the relationship between attachment and health.

Aims

Although prior research has highlighted important links between attachment processes and health outcomes, several aspects of this relationship warrant further investigation, especially with regard to the contribution of IWMs in explaining health differences across attachment styles. To address these aspects, the present study assessed attachment using both categorical and dimensional approaches, with the aim of overcoming the theoretical and methodological limitations in each perspective and gaining a more integrated understanding of the link between attachment and health. More specifically, the categorical and dimensional approaches were adopted in a complementary manner: the former to assess how attachment styles differ in terms of physical and mental health, the latter to examine the specific contribution of the underlying dimensions (IWMs of the self and IWMs of others) in explaining these differences, allowing for an integrated interpretation of the results.

It was hypothesized that individuals with secure and dismissing attachment styles would report higher levels of physical and mental health compared to those with fearful and preoccupied attachment styles. In line with this hypothesis, it was further expected that IWMs of the self would account for a greater proportion of the association between attachment and health than IWMs of others.

By jointly considering both styles and dimensions, this study aims to provide a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of the interplay between attachment and health, allowing for the examination of both the impact of specific combinations of IWMs (attachment styles) on health and the independent contribution of the two dimensions.

Questionnaires

The study was designed as a comprehensive assessment using multiple questionnaires. For reasons of analytical focus and clarity, this article reports only on a predefined subset of measures relevant to the specific objectives of the present investigation.

First of all, a socio-demographic questionnaire was administered to collect information about gender, age, and education level.

To evaluate attachment, the Relationship Questionnaire (RQ) was used. The RQ, developed by Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991) and adapted to Italian by Carli (1995), consists of four brief paragraphs, each describing a prototypical attachment style as it applies to intimate relationships in adulthood: a) secure; b) preoccupied; c) fearful; d) dismissing (e.g. “I am comfortable without close emotional relationships. It is very important to me to feel independent and self-sufficient, and I prefer not to depend on others or have others depend on me”). The questionnaire comprises two parts, giving rise to both categorical (RQ-1) and continuous (RQ-2) attachment assessments. The first part (RQ-1) requires participants to choose from four paragraphs the one with which they most identify, without providing a numerical rating. Based on previous studies (Robin et al., 2023; Sechi et al., 2021; Timmerman & Emmelkamp, 2006), RQ-1 was used to identify each participant’s attachment style (secure, dismissing, preoccupied and fearful). In the second part (RQ-2), participants are asked to rate the extent to which each paragraph represents them on a 7-point Likert scale, resulting in four continuous scales. These scales were used to compute scores for the two dimensions underlying the four attachment styles: the IWMs of the self and the IWMs of others (Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994b). The score for the IWMs of the self is computed by subtracting the score of attachment styles characterized by negative internal working models of the self from the score of attachment styles associated with positive internal working models of the self. This calculation is represented as follows: (secure score + dismissing score) – (preoccupied score + fearful score). In contrast, the score for the IWMs of others is determined by subtracting the score of attachment styles defined by negative internal working models of others from the score of attachment styles defined by positive internal working models of others: (secure score + preoccupied score) – (dismissing score + fearful score). Scores on the dimensions of the IWMs of the self and others can range from −12 to +12. Positive scores indicate more positive IWMs of the self and others and negative scores indicate more negative IWMs of the self and IWMs of others.

Finally, mental and physical health were detected through the SF-12 Health Survey. The Italian form, developed by Apolone et al. (2005), evaluates physical and mental aspects of self-perceived health. It comprises 12 items and produces two summary indexes: the Physical Component Summary (SF-PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (SF-MCS). Four subscales, each with two items, and four single-item subscales assess various aspects of health and wellbeing (e.g. “During the past 4 weeks, how much did pain interfere with your normal work, including both work outside the home and housework?” 1 = Not at all; 5 = Extremely; “During the past 4 weeks, did you not do work or other regular daily activities as carefully as usual as a result of any emotional problems, such as feeling depressed or anxious?” Yes/No). Higher values of the indexes represent a better state of health. The specific spreadsheet for Microsoft Excel software developed by Ottoboni et al. (2017) was used to calculate the indexes; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.851.

Procedure

A computerized version of the questionnaires described above was prepared for online administration using Qualtrics software (https://www.qualtrics.com). Participants received a link to access the questionnaires, along with a brief study description that included requirements of legal age and a guarantee of anonymity. After acknowledging the informed consent, participants could proceed with the questionnaires. The survey was distributed online through popular social media platforms, including Facebook, WhatsApp, Twitter, Telegram, and LinkedIn. The study, including all questionnaires and procedures, was fully compliant with the Declaration of Helsinki and with the Ethics Code of the Italian Board of Psychology. It was also approved by the Institutional Review Board of Psychology of the Department of Psychological, Health and Territorial Sciences, University “G. d’Annunzio” of Chieti-Pescara (Italy) (Protocol Number: 24002).

Statistical Analyses

We conducted data analysis using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, version 22). Initially, we assessed the reliability of the measures, examined the normality of data distribution (skewness and kurtosis indices), and provided descriptive statistics. A significant level (alpha) of 0.05 was used for all statistical analyses. For the first aim of the study, we performed a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the mean scores of physical health (SFPCS) and mental health (SF-MCS) with respect to the “attachment style” factor (RQ-1). Subsequently, we conducted multiple comparison tests with Bonferroni correction to assess the variations in attachment styles concerning the investigated variables.

For the second aim, we employed multiple linear regression analyses using a stepwise approach. As independent variables, we included IWMs of the self and IWMs of others (RQ-2). The goal was to identify which of these independent variables best predicted participants’ physical and mental health outcomes, as measured by the Physical Component Summary (SFPCS) and Mental Component Summary (SF-MCS) of the SF-12. The use of regression models allowed us to assess the unique predictive power of each variable while controlling for the others, thus aligning with our objective to explore how individual differences in attachment relate to health outcomes in adulthood.

Discussion

The present study adopted an integrative approach to adult attachment by jointly examining categorical attachment styles and dimensional internal working models (IWMs) of the self and others in relation to physical and mental health. In the context of the “types versus dimensions question”, reconciling the two perspectives rather than considering them as mutually exclusive could enable a more accurate assessment of attachment (e.g. Adamczyk & Bookwala, 2013; Morales-Vives et al., 2021, 2025). By concurrently examining both categorical attachment styles and dimensional IWMs of the self and others, the present study aimed to provide a more detailed picture of the interplay between adult attachment and physical and mental health. To pursue this general objective, we considered two aims: the first was to examine the differences between attachment styles in terms of physical and mental health; the second was to analyze the specific contribution of the underlying dimensions in explaining these differences, allowing for an integrated interpretation of the results. Overall, the findings partially supported the study hypotheses and provided nuanced evidence that attachment-related differences are more strongly and consistently associated with mental health than with physical health, and that mental health is more related to the IWMs of the self than to the IWMs of others.

Attachment styles and health

Consistent with the study hypotheses and previous research (Mayangsari et al., 2024; Platts et al., 2023; Santoro et al., 2021), the findings of the first aim indicate that individuals with secure and dismissing attachment styles exhibit higher levels of mental health compared to preoccupied and fearful individuals. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were found among attachment styles in relation to physical health. This pattern aligns with a substantial body of literature indicating that attachment insecurity is more robustly associated with psychological distress, emotional dysregulation, and impaired well-being than with self-reported physical health, particularly in nonclinical adult samples (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2012; Pietromonaco & Beck, 2019; Santoro et al., 2021).

The post hoc analyses revealed a particularly meaningful pattern: secure and dismissing individuals did not differ significantly from each other in terms of mental health, nor did preoccupied and fearful individuals, whereas the former combined group (secure and dismissing) differed markedly from the latter (preoccupied and fearful). This finding is theoretically coherent within Bartholomew’s model (Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991) and highlights the central role of the IWMs of the self in shaping mental health outcomes. Secure and dismissing attachment styles share a positive IWM of the self, characterized by feelings of self-worth, competence, and relative confidence in managing distress independently. In contrast, both preoccupied and fearful styles are defined by a negative IWM of the self, involving chronic self-doubt, heightened vulnerability to rejection, and elevated attachment anxiety (Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994a; Shaver & Mikulincer, 2002). While dismissing individuals tend to minimize intimacy and rely on self-sufficiency, their positive IWMs of the self may protect them from some of the negative outcomes associated with insecure attachment (Costanzo et al., 2021; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2012). Nevertheless, it is important to consider the possibility that the similarity between dismissing and secure patterns is partly due to the difficulty dismissing individuals have in accurately evaluating themselves, given their tendency to downplay the emotional impact of events (Mikulincer et al., 2003). However, it is worth noting that the instrument used in our study to assess attachment (namely, the Relationship Questionnaire) did not show associations with self-deceptive bias, which refers to the unconscious tendency to provide positively distorted responses, unlike other measures of adult attachment (Leak & Parsons, 2001). Overall, the present findings seem to suggest that these shared negative IWMs of the self constitute a core psychological vulnerability underlying poorer mental health, regardless of differences in relational orientation toward others.

The absence of attachment-style differences in physical health may reflect several complementary factors. First, physical health outcomes are often influenced by a complex interplay of biological, behavioral, and environmental variables, which may attenuate the observable impact of attachment-related psychological processes in community samples (Spruit et al., 2020). Second, self-reported physical health measures, such as the SF-12 Physical Component Summary, may be less sensitive to attachment-related differences unless chronic illness, pain conditions, or long-term stress exposure are explicitly assessed (Pietromonaco et al., 2013). Third, attachment-related influences on physical health may operate indirectly through mental health, health behaviors, or physiological stress regulation over extended periods, effects that are not easily captured in cross-sectional designs (Maunder & Hunter, 2001; Pietromonaco et al., 2013; Pietromonaco & Beck, 2019).

Dimensional of the self findings: the primacy of the IWM

The regression analyses directly addressed the second aim of the study and provided convergent evidence for the central role of IWMs of the self. Neither IWMs of the self nor IWMs of others significantly predicted physical health, further supporting the notion that attachment-related processes may exert weaker or more indirect effects on perceived physical functioning in community samples. By contrast, IWMs of the self emerged as a strong and significant predictor of mental health, accounting for a substantial proportion of variance. The inclusion of IWMs of others yielded only a modest incremental increase in explained variance.

These findings offer empirical support for the hypothesis that IWMs of the self are the primary attachment-related mechanism linking attachment to mental health outcomes. A negative IWM of the self reflects internalized expectations of unworthiness and inadequacy, which are closely tied to maladaptive emotion regulation strategies, heightened stress reactivity, and vulnerability to psychopathology (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2012; Thompson et al., 2022). Individuals with negative IWMs of the self are more likely to interpret interpersonal experiences as confirming their perceived flaws, thereby reinforcing cycles of distress and impaired well-being.

Importantly, the present results are consistent with and extend previous findings by Kovács et al. (2022), who observed that patients with negative IWMs of the self experienced greater psychological burden than those with positive ones. By demonstrating similar patterns in a non-clinical adult sample, the present study seems to suggest that the associations of IWMs of the self with mental health are robust across different populations and levels of symptom severity.

Integrating categorical and dimensional perspectives

One of the central contributions of this study lies in its integrative use of categorical and dimensional approaches to attachment. Rather than treating these approaches as competing frameworks, the findings illustrate how they can be used complementarily to enhance theoretical clarity. The categorical analyses highlighted meaningful group differences in mental health that are clinically intuitive and easily interpretable, while the dimensional analyses identified the specific psychological mechanism—namely the IWMs of the self—that accounts for these differences.

This integrative interpretation supports previous arguments that attachment styles possess emergent properties that cannot be fully reduced to their underlying dimensions, while simultaneously acknowledging that dimensional representations provide greater explanatory precision (Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994a; Raby et al., 2021; Scharfe, 2016). In particular, the convergence between categorical groupings (secure/ dismissing vs. preoccupied/fearful) and dimensional IWMs strengthens the validity of both approaches and underscores the value of combining them within a single research design.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its contribution, this study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional nature precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the investigated variables. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how IWMs of the self and others prospectively influence mental and physical health across the lifespan. Furthermore, our reliance on self-report instruments places a degree of dependence on the honesty and introspection of the participants. Future research would benefit from incorporating objective health indicators or clinician-rated measures. Although we conducted inferential statistical analyses on the overall sample, we did not account for the potential influences of sociodemographic characteristics. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that a recent study examining differences in physical and mental health among the four attachment styles yielded similar results even when considering age, gender, and educational level (Platts et al., 2023). In the current research, the absence of any association with physical health can be attributed to the instrument used, the SF-12, and specifically to the procedure employed to generate the physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) health indices from the subscale scores. As previously noted by Taft et al. (2001), the methodology used to construct the PCS and MCS indices may not effectively synthetize the subscale scores, potentially leading to an oversight in detecting significant associations with physical health, mental health, or both (Farivar et al., 2007). Besides, it cannot be excluded that the use of a community sample may have limited variability in physical health outcomes, potentially obscuring attachment-related differences. Moreover, while the Relationship Questionnaire offers the advantage of assessing both categorical and dimensional attachment within a single instrument, future studies should replicate these findings using alternative attachment measures to further strengthen their generalizability. Finally, the integrative scope of the present study, while representing a strength in terms of theoretical inclusiveness, may also be considered a limitation with respect to the specificity of its implications. By jointly addressing the categorical versus dimensional debate and the association between attachment and health, the study prioritizes a broad conceptual perspective over the in-depth examination of highly specific psychological or clinical mechanisms. Future research could build on these findings by adopting a more focused approach, for instance by targeting specific attachment-related processes or clinical populations, thereby refining and extending the theoretical and applied implications of the present results.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the findings have meaningful clinical and theoretical implications. From a clinical point of view, they highlight the relevance of addressing attachment-related difficulties in therapeutic contexts. Attachment-informed interventions - particularly those focusing on strengthening positive IWMs of the self - may prove beneficial, especially for individuals with preoccupied and fearful attachment styles. The relatively weaker contribution of IWMs of others on mental health suggests that modifying relational expectations alone may be insufficient unless accompanied by changes in IWMs of the self. Parenting support programs and early interventions focused on promoting secure attachment relationships, emotional regulation, and the development of positive internal working models of the self may be effective in mitigating the risk of developing subsequent psychopathologies and interrupting the intergenerational transmission of psychological vulnerability.

From a theoretical point of view, the present study suggests that attachment-related differences in health are primarily evident in the domain of mental health and are largely driven by IWMs of the self. By integrating categorical and dimensional approaches, the findings clarify how attachment styles differ in mental health and identify the underlying psychological mechanism responsible for these differences.

A final reflection concerning the results of this study pertains to physical health. Contrary to expectations, both findings of the present study showed no significant associations between physical health and either categorical attachment styles or dimensional IWMs. These data suggest that while attachment is strongly associated with mental health, its effects on physical health may be more indirect, possibly mediated by long-term stress responses, health behaviors, or social support (Maunder & Hunter, 2001; Pietromonaco & Beck, 2019). Future studies with longitudinal designs could explore whether the impact of attachment styles on physical health becomes more pronounced over time. The incongruence in findings underscores the multifaceted nature of the relationship between attachment and physical health, suggesting that further research is needed to elucidate the nuances of these interactions.

Conclusions

Griffin and Bartholomew (1994a) drew an insightful analogy between romantic relationships and research in the field of attachment. Intimate relationships are characterized by alternating phases of excitement, novelty, and change, as well as periods of calm and apparent stability. Far from being unproductive, these quieter phases allow partners to integrate past experiences and new insights, ultimately fostering a deeper understanding of both the relationship and each other. A similar process can be observed in attachment research. After decades of prolific empirical work aimed at identifying new correlates and outcomes, periods of theoretical integration and reappraisal are equally necessary to consolidate knowledge and refine conceptual models.

The present study focused on two aspects that are well established in the literature yet remain insufficiently understood: the “types versus dimensions” question and the relationship between adult attachment and health. As repeatedly noted, the debate between categorical and dimensional approaches is far from being resolved, as each offers distinct and complementary advantages (e.g. Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994a; Morales-Vives et al., 2021, 2025; Raby et al., 2021). Rather than treating these approaches as mutually exclusive, the present study adopted an integrative perspective by concurrently examining attachment styles and internal working models of the self and others.

This dual approach allowed us to capitalize on the interpretative clarity of categorical classifications and the explanatory precision of dimensional representations. From a categorical perspective, the findings confirmed that individuals with secure and dismissing attachment styles—both characterized by a positive internal working model of the self—reported better mental health than individuals with preoccupied or fearful attachment styles. From a dimensional perspective, the results highlighted the central role of internal working models of the self, which emerged as a substantially stronger predictor of mental health than internal working models of others.

Taken together, these findings indicate that attachment-related differences in health are primarily evident in the domain of mental health and are largely driven by self-related attachment representations. By integrating categorical and dimensional approaches, the present study clarifies how attachment styles differ with respect to mental health and identifies the underlying psychological mechanism that accounts for these differences. This integrative framework contributes to ongoing theoretical debates in attachment research and underscores the clinical relevance of targeting internal working models of the self in interventions aimed at promoting psychological well-being.
