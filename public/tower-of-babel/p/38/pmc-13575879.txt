Proteomic Analysis of Extracellular Vesicles Reveals Vitronectin and Laminin Subunit Alpha-3 as Candidate Biomarkers for Gastric Cancer
Authors: Dong Chan Joo, Su Jin Park, Gwang Ha Kim, Moon Won Lee, Bong Eun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsClinically useful noninvasive biomarkers for gastric cancer remain limited. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) carry a molecular cargo reflective of their cells of origin and have emerged as promising candidates for blood-based cancer biomarkers. We aimed to identify EV-associated protein biomarkers for gastric cancer via a proteomic approach.MethodsProteomic profiling of EVs was performed using one normal gastric cell line (Hs738st/int) and two gastric cancer cell lines (AGS and NCI-N87). Selected proteins were validated in blood-derived EVs isolated from plasma samples of 10 healthy controls and 36 patients with gastric cancer.ResultsProteomic analysis identified 224 differentially expressed proteins whose expression was consistently altered in gastric cancer cell line-derived EVs. Among these, vitronectin (VTN) and laminin subunit alpha-3 (LAMA3) were selected based on their consistent upregulation. EV-associated LAMA3 levels were significantly higher in patients with gastric cancer than in healthy controls (p=0.003), with significant elevations observed from stage II onward (p=0.041, p=0.017, and p=0.004 for stages II, III, and IV, respectively). EV-associated VTN levels were not significantly different overall (p=0.089); however, stage-specific analysis demonstrated significant increases in VTN levels in patients with stage III (p=0.036) and stage IV (p=0.005) gastric cancer. Both EV-associated VTN and LAMA3 levels showed significant positive correlations with the cancer stage (ρ=0.564 and ρ=0.611, respectively; both p<0.001).ConclusionsThe levels of EV-associated VTN and LAMA3 appear to be more closely associated with disease progression than with early-stage detection of gastric cancer. These findings suggest that EV-based proteomic biomarkers may have clinical utility for monitoring tumor progression in patients with clinically advanced gastric cancer.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Extracellular vesicle, Proteomics, Vitronectin, Laminin subunit alpha-3

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer is the fifth most commonly diagnosed malignancy and fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 When diagnosis is delayed, gastric cancer is often detected at an advanced stage, resulting in a marked reduction in the 5-year survival rate. Because gastric cancer is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, patient outcomes remain poor, as early-stage disease is often asymptomatic and difficult to detect even with endoscopic screening.2-5 To overcome these limitations and develop less invasive diagnostic methods, various blood-based biomarkers have been explored. Among them, serum tumor markers, such as carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, and carbohydrate antigen 72-4, have been studied for their potential association with cancer stage and prognosis.6,7 However, these markers exhibit limited sensitivity and specificity. Beyond their well-known poor performance in early-stage screening, their detection rates often remain unsatisfactory even in advanced disease stages, which significantly restricts their use as effective diagnostic and monitoring tools.8,9

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) preserve their internal contents in a stable state by preventing enzymatic degradation through their lipid bilayer membranes and serve as critical mediators of intercellular communication.10 They are released by nearly all cell types and present in various body fluids, including blood.11 Moreover, unlike tissue samples, blood is a readily accessible specimen that can be easily obtained from patients. Consequently, there has been increasing interest in analyzing EV-associated contents, such as RNA and proteins, in blood samples to identify potential biomarkers for various cancers.12-17 Many studies have focused on RNA-based analyses, primarily investigating differential RNA expression. However, RNA levels do not necessarily correlate with the concentrations of their corresponding functional protein products.18

Proteomic analysis, an advanced technology, offers a powerful tool for systematically profiling the protein composition of biological samples, including EVs.19 Compared with RNA or DNA analyses, proteomic profiling provides a more direct and dynamic reflection of cellular physiology, as proteins act as the primary functional executors of cellular processes.19,20 Moreover, tools, such as functional annotation, enrichment analysis, pathway analysis, and network analysis enable the effective interpretation of complex proteomic datasets and identification of potential therapeutic targets.21 Therefore, EV-associated proteomic analysis is a particularly promising approach for biomarker discovery in cancer research.

Within this framework, extracellular matrix (ECM)–related proteins have been increasingly recognized as biologically relevant components in cancer progression and therefore represent a class of molecules with potential biomarker relevance. Proteins such as vitronectin (VTN) and laminin subunit alpha-3 (LAMA3) have been repeatedly reported in various malignancies, where they are known to play important roles in cell adhesion, migration, and tissue remodeling.22-26 Dysregulation of these processes has been implicated in tumor invasion and progression across multiple cancer types. In this study, we aimed to identify novel biomarkers for gastric cancer through proteomic analysis of EVs derived from gastric cancer cell lines and validate them using blood samples from patients with gastric cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Sample preparation

1) Cell culture

Two gastric cancer (AGS and NCI-N87, Korea Cell Line Bank, Seoul, Korea) and one normal gastric (control; Hs738st/int, ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA) cell line were used. Gastric cancer cell lines were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin. Normal gastric cell line cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin. All cell lines were grown at 37℃ with 5% CO2.

2) Patient blood sampling

Peripheral blood samples from 36 patients with gastric cancer and 10 healthy controls were retrospectively obtained from the Biobank of Pusan National University Hospital (Busan, Korea) between May 2019 and April 2021. Peripheral blood (3 mL) was centrifuged at 800 ×g for 10 minutes at 15℃ to separate the plasma. The samples were then stored at −80℃ until isolation of EVs. Approval for the study protocol was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Pusan National University Hospital (protocol code: H-2305-014-127). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the collection of blood samples.

2. Isolation of EVs

1) ExoDisc for cell lines

Until the cell density reached 90% to 100%, the cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 48 hours. Subsequently, 75 mL of the cell culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 300 ×g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 2,000 ×g for 10 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 30 minutes. The resulting supernatant was concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 (Millipore, MA, USA). Finally, EVs were isolated using centrifugation with ExoDisc (Labspinner, Ulsan, Korea).

2) ExoQuick for plasma

Plasma samples (300 µL) were transferred to clean tubes and 3 μL of thrombin (System Biosciences, Mountain View, CA, USA) was added, followed by incubation at 15℃ for 5 minutes. The samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 5 minutes and 250 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a new clean tube. ExoQuick solution (63 µL; System Biosciences) was added to the supernatant and the mixture was incubated at 4℃ for 30 minutes. The sample was then centrifuged at 1,500 ×g for 30 minutes, after which the supernatant was discarded. The EV pellet was resuspended in 300 µL of 1× phosphate-buffered saline.

3. Characterization of EVs

1) Western blotting

EV protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). EV samples, each containing 50 µg of protein, were separated using 4% to 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin solution for 1.5 hours at 15℃. The following primary antibodies were incubated with the membranes overnight at 4℃: anti-HSP70 (1:1,000) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-CD63 (1:1,000) (Abcam), anti-TSG101 (1:1,000) (Abcam), anti-CD9 (1:1,000) (Abcam), Vitronectin (Abcam), and LAMA3 (Atlas Antibodies, Bromma, Sweden). The next day, membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 for approximately 2 hours at room temperature. Secondary antibodies were then applied and incubated at room temperature. EV markers were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence Western blotting kit.

2) Transmission electron microscopy

EV samples were fixed by mixing 1:1 with 1% glutaraldehyde for 30 minutes. The fixed sample (6 µL) was pipetted onto a 100-mesh copper grid coated with a carbon-Formvar film (Hatfield, PA, USA) and incubated for 10 minutes. Excess liquid was blotted off and the grid was washed twice by brief immersion in 100 µL of distilled water, followed by blotting to remove residual moisture. The grid was stained with 1% uranyl acetate for 12 seconds. After the excess liquid was removed, the samples were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-1200EXII, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

3) Liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry

The prepared samples were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid in water and analyzed using a Q-Exactive Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptide separation was performed on C18 trap and analytical columns based on peptide hydrophobicity. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 90% acetonitrile), at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The gradient of solvent B was applied as follows: 4% for the first 14 minutes, increased to 15% over 61 minutes. It was then increased to 28% over 50 minutes, followed by an increase to 40% over 20 minutes. The solvent concentration reached 96% within 2 minutes and was maintained at 96% for 13 minutes. Subsequently, it was decreased to 4% within 1 minute and maintained at 4% for 24 minutes. A data-dependent acquisition method was used to select the top 10 precursor ion peaks for fragmentation. Ions were scanned at a high resolution (70,000 for MS1 and 17,500 for MS2 at m/z 400), with a scan range of 400–2,000 m/z for both MS1 and MS2. Precursor ions were fragmented with a normalized collision energy of 27% and dynamic exclusion was applied for 30 seconds to prevent repeated fragmentation of the same precursor ions.

4. Data analysis

Data were processed using ExDEGA (version 5.1.1, EBIOGEN Inc., Seoul, Korea) to generate visualizations including Venn diagrams, heat maps, and scatter plots. A standard fold-change threshold of 2 with a p-value <0.05 was applied for pathway enrichment analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID; available at https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov) platform. GO analysis results were categorized into three major categories: cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

1. EV characterization and identification

To verify the isolation of EVs from normal gastric (Hs738st/int) and gastric cancer (AGS and NCI-N87) cell lines, EV-specific markers were used following the isolation of EVs. Western blotting and TEM confirmed the successful isolation and identification of these EVs. Fig. 1 illustrates the captured EVs as confirmed using Western blotting and TEM. Western blotting revealed EV-associated markers (HSP70, CD63, TSG101, and CD9) in both normal gastric and gastric cancer cell lines (Fig. 1A). Additionally, the EV morphology was verified using TEM. TEM images confirmed characteristic cup-shaped EVs with diameters of 40–100 nm in cell lines (Fig. 1B).

2. Global protein profiling of gastric cancer cell EVs

Proteomic analysis using the ExDEGA (version 5.1.1) assay identified 1,437 proteins initially expressed in AGS and NCI-N87 cells. Using the criteria of |log2FC|>1 and p<0.05, we identified EV-associated proteins that were upregulated or downregulated in gastric cancer cell lines (AGS and NCI-N87) compared with those in the normal gastric cell line (Hs738st/int). In total, 224 differentially expressed EV-associated proteins (DEPs) were identified as the intersection between the two gastric cancer cell lines (Fig. 2A). Bioinformatic analysis identified two main clusters comprising 164 upregulated and 60 downregulated proteins. The expression patterns of these DEPs are summarized in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

3. Hierarchical clustering analysis

EV protein expression patterns in normal gastric and gastric cancer cell lines revealed distinct patterns of cancer-specific proteins. The proteomic analysis showed differential protein expression profiles between normal gastric and gastric cancer cell lines in the heatmap, with a clear segregation observed between the control and gastric cancer cell lines. These results demonstrate a marked difference in protein expression between normal gastric and gastric cancer cell lines (Fig. 3).

4. Identification of biological functions and pathways

We conducted a bioinformatic analysis to determine the functional significance of 224 DEPs. Using the DAVID tool, we conducted GO analysis for the DEPs and categorized these proteins into three main sections: cellular component, molecular function, and biological process. Within the biological process category, we identified significant enrichment of proteins related to protein localization to the telomere and cell adhesion (Fig. 4A). For cellular component, the proteins involving EV exhibited the highest significant enrichment (Fig. 4B). Analysis of molecular function revealed a significant enrichment in ECM structural constituents (Fig. 4C). Additionally, pathway analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) revealed that proteins involved in the ECM-receptor interaction pathway were the most significantly enriched (Fig. 4D).

5. Selection of specific proteins as candidate biomarkers for gastric cancer

Among 224 DEPs, we first screened those that repeatedly appeared across all three GO domains (biological process, cellular component, and molecular function) as well as in KEGG pathway enrichment, assuming that proteins consistently enriched across all annotation domains would have greater biological relevance. Through this process, a total of five proteins were initially identified (Table 1), two of which (VTN and LAMA3) showed marked upregulation, while the remaining three were downregulated. Because upregulated molecules have a higher likelihood of being detected in circulation and thus hold greater value as biomarker candidates, we focused on VTN and LAMA3 for further verification. Both proteins demonstrated strong enrichment across GO and KEGG analyses, particularly within cell adhesion and ECM–associated pathways linked to tumor progression. Considering these characteristics and their reproducible detection in our EV-based proteomic profiling, VTN and LAMA3 were selected as the final candidate biomarkers for gastric cancer.

Western blotting was performed to compare the expression levels of VTN and LAMA3 in the blood of healthy controls and patients with gastric cancer (Fig. 5A). EV-associated VTN was higher in patients with gastric cancer than in healthy controls, but the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.089) (Fig. 5B). When stratified by disease stage, EV-associated VTN levels were not significantly different between healthy controls and patients with stage I or II gastric cancer (p=0.810 and p=0.249, respectively). In contrast, a significant increase was observed in patients with stage III and IV gastric cancer compared with controls (p=0.036 and p=0.005, respectively). EV-associated LAMA3 levels were also significantly higher in patients with gastric cancer than in healthy controls (p=0.003) (Fig. 5C). Stage-specific analysis further demonstrated that EV-associated LAMA3 levels were significantly increased in patients with stage II, III, and IV gastric cancer compared with controls (p=0.041, p=0.017, and p=0.004, respectively), whereas no significant difference was observed in stage I gastric cancer (p=0.195). The correlation analysis demonstrated a positive relationship between EV-associated VTN levels and gastric cancer stage (ρ=0.564, p<0.001) (Fig. 5D). Similarly, a significant positive correlation was also observed between EV-associated LAMA3 levels and gastric cancer stage (ρ=0.611, p<0.001) (Fig. 5E).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we performed proteomic profiling of EVs derived from a normal gastric cell line and two gastric cancer cell lines as the initial discovery step. From this analysis, VTN and LAMA3 were identified as consistently upregulated candidates in EVs of gastric cancer cell lines. Then, stage-stratified analyses in patients with gastric cancer revealed distinct expression patterns for the two proteins. EV-associated LAMA3 levels were significantly elevated in patients with stage II–IV gastric cancer compared with healthy controls, whereas no significant increase was observed in stage I disease. In contrast, EV-associated VTN showed a statistically significant elevation only in patients with stage III and IV gastric cancer. Consistent with these findings, correlation analyses demonstrated significant positive associations between EV-associated protein levels and tumor stage for both markers, supporting a stage-dependent increase in circulating EV cargo. Taken together, these results suggest that LAMA3 may reflect disease progression beginning at stage II, which is generally regarded as a clinically advanced stage, whereas VTN predominantly mirrors tumor burden in more advanced gastric cancer. Despite these differences, both proteins demonstrated stage-dependent associations with gastric cancer progression. These findings indicate that EV-associated VTN and LAMA3 may be more suitable as biomarkers reflecting disease progression in clinically advanced gastric cancer rather than as markers for early-stage detection.

Proteomic analysis of EVs offers a biologically relevant approach for biomarker discovery, as proteins reflect cellular function more directly than RNA,27,28 and prior studies in various cancers support the clinical applicability of EV protein biomarkers.29,30 However, EVs released from cancer cells in body fluids, such as blood, are mixed with those derived from non-cancer cells, making the detection of cancer-specific EV-associated biomarkers challenging.18 To address this limitation, we conducted proteomic profiling using a normal gastric cell line and two gastric cancer cell lines to identify DEPs that were consistently expressed in two different gastric cancer cell lines. Thus, we were able to minimize potential confounding factors arising from EV-associated heterogeneity in plasma. These identified proteins were then validated using blood-derived EVs from healthy controls and patients with gastric cancer, thereby bridging in vitro findings with clinical relevance.

VTN is a multifunctional glycoprotein abundant in the serum, ECM, and bone, known for facilitating cell adhesion, migration, and spreading through interactions with integrins, such as αvβ3.22,23 Previous studies have implicated VTN in the development and progression of various malignancies including breast, cervical, prostate, and ovarian cancers.23,31-34 Regarding gastric cancer, Joshi et al.35 identified an association between increased VTN expression and gastric cancer through proteomic analysis of urine samples from patients with gastric cancer. However, they did not investigate VTN expression levels according to cancer stage. Gong et al.34 demonstrated that VTN expression, assessed using immunohistochemistry, was significantly upregulated in gastric cancer tissues and served as an independent predictor of both overall and disease-free survival. Although the VTN expression was increased even in tissue from patients with stage I gastric cancer in their study, significant EV-associated VTN elevation was observed in patients with stage III–IV gastric cancer in our present study. This discrepancy might stem from methodological differences, as Gong et al.34 analyzed tumor tissues via immunohistochemistry, whereas we assessed blood samples via circulating EVs. Given that EV-associated cargo may reflect systemic disease burden rather than localized tumor presence, early-stage tumors might release fewer EVs, explaining the lack of significant elevation in patients at stage I.

LAMA3, one of the subunits of laminin-332 (comprising the α3, β3, and γ2 chains), plays an important role in mediating cell-matrix interactions and modulating cell adhesion and migration.24 LAMA3 expression is upregulated in various cancers, suggesting its potential role in promoting cell proliferation, regulating apoptosis, and driving tumor progression through alterations in signal transduction pathways.25,26 Huang and Chen36 further demonstrated that elevated LAMA3 expression promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells. LAMA3 expression was significantly higher in gastric cancer tumor tissues than in normal gastric tissues.37 In this study, we observed a stage-dependent increase in LAMA3 expression in blood samples from patients with gastric cancer. This finding suggests that circulating EV-associated LAMA3 not only serves as a diagnostic biomarker but may also have prognostic implications related to tumor invasiveness and metastasis.

This study is the first to demonstrate elevated levels of VTN and LAMA3 in blood-derived EVs from patients with gastric cancer, reinforcing their potential as minimally invasive biomarkers for gastric cancer. Previous studies investigating the associations of VTN and LAMA3 with gastric cancer were primarily based on tissue specimen analyses.34,37,38 Another strength of this study is the strategy used to address the heterogeneity of plasma-derived EVs. By initially identifying consistently upregulated proteins through proteomic profiling of a normal gastric cell line and two gastric cancer cell lines, we minimized potential confounding factors. Subsequent validation using blood-derived EVs from healthy controls and patients with gastric cancer further strengthened the clinical relevance of our findings. Moreover, although inter-patient heterogeneity is a well-recognized challenge in cancer biomarker research, the consistent upregulation of VTN and LAMA3 across samples from patients with gastric cancer strengthens the robustness of our findings. Furthermore, in contrast to previous studies that examined the association of VTN and LAMA3 with gastric cancer without assessing stage-specific expression patterns, the present study validated these biomarkers in blood samples from patients across all stages of gastric cancer. This comprehensive validation indicates that VTN and LAMA3 are not only overexpressed in gastric cancer but also associated with disease stage and prognosis.

This study had several limitations. First, although the use of cell lines minimized contamination from non–tumor-derived EVs, it does not fully capture the biological heterogeneity of primary tumors, which exhibit substantial genetic, epigenetic, and phenotypic diversity. Therefore, findings based on cell line models may not entirely reflect the complex biological behavior of gastric cancer in clinical settings. Nevertheless, in this study, the candidate biomarkers identified from cell line-derived EVs were successfully validated in blood-derived EVs from patients with gastric cancer. This concordance between in vitro and clinical samples supports the reliability and clinical relevance of our findings despite the inherent limitations of cell line-based approaches. Second, although validation was performed using Western blotting rather than a fully quantitative platform, both VTN and LAMA3 demonstrated stepwise increases across cancer stages, indicating that the findings extend beyond qualitative detection alone. This consistent stage-dependent trend in plasma-derived EVs supports their biological relevance and suggests potential prognostic implications. However, the absence of survival data in this cohort precluded direct evaluation of prognostic performance, and future studies incorporating quantitative assays and longitudinal follow-up are warranted. Third, the sample size was limited, particularly after stage stratification, and all samples were derived from a single institution. Because plasma samples were retrospectively obtained from a biobank, age and sex matching could not be controlled, resulting in a younger, all-female healthy control group. In addition, although EV characterization was not performed for every plasma-derived sample, identical isolation protocols were consistently applied, and EV properties were rigorously validated at the cell line-based discovery stage, supporting the reliability of the downstream analyses. Larger, prospectively recruited, multi-center studies with appropriate demographic matching will be required to enhance the generalizability of these findings.

In conclusion, EV-associated VTN and LAMA3 are promising biomarkers reflecting disease progression in gastric cancer. Their progressive elevation with advancing cancer stage suggests that these proteins are more closely associated with tumor burden and disease advancement rather than early-stage detection. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the mechanistic roles of VTN and LAMA3 in gastric cancer and to evaluate their clinical utility for monitoring disease progression and therapeutic response in patients with clinically advanced gastric cancer.
