Type-I and -II collagens from bone and cartilage colocalize at the osteochondral cement line
Authors: Khizar Hayat, Neil Marr, Kingston K L Mak, Michael Doube
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe osteochondral cement line (OCL) plays a key role in joint integrity by attaching articular calcified cartilage (ACC) to underlying subchondral bone (SCB), whose predominant collagens are type 2 (Col-II) and type 1 (Col-I), respectively. Previous studies report contrasting evidence of the presence of collagen fibrils in the OCL, albeit in different species and joints. If present, collagen fibrils might provide a basis for osteochondral bonding in the organic phase. We aimed to study the morphological variations of the osteochondral cement line, to observe whether cartilage and bone collagen fibrils are present in the OCL, and whether they colocalize in a manner that could help explain how ACC attaches to SCB.MethodsWe used immunofluorescence, confocal microscopy, and deconvolution to image Col-I and Col-II collagen fibrils and measure their overlap and colocalization, in OCL harvested from equine and bovine femoral head, patella, and proximal and distal metatarsal condyles. Large mammalian species were chosen to have size and pathobiology relevant to human anatomy. Thousands to millions of Col-I/-II colocalizing complexes were observed per mm² of OCL over a tissue depth of 1 to 5 µm. Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted for intra- and interspecies statistical analysis.ResultsThe areal volume (µm³/mm²) of Col-I/Col-II complexes was up to ten times greater in equine than bovine OCL (p = 0.016 to 0.029). Similarly, the number of Col-I/-II complexes and mean volume per complex differed significantly (p < 0.001 to 0.032 and p < 0.001 to 0.029, respectively) among anatomical sites between equine and bovine OCL. Gaps or tears near OCL were present uniquely in the bovine patella.ConclusionCol-I/Col-II overlap and colocalize at OCL, which could be a critical source of bond strength between cartilage and bone that should be considered when cartilage repair is attempted in clinical settings.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(8):735–744.Keywords: Osteochondral cement line, Collagen type-I/-II colocalization, Osteochondral bonding, Bone remodelling, Osseointegration, cartilage, collagens, patellae, condyles, femoral head, Mann-Whitney U tests, subchondral bone, cartilage repair, statistical analysis, confocal microscopy

Introduction

The soft-hard tissue junction between cartilage and bone is integral to joint function.1 The osteochondral cement line (OCL) is an interface located between subchondral bone (SCB) and articular calcified cartilage (ACC) that may play a role in attaching these two skeletal tissues.2,3 However, its bonding mechanisms and composition are not well understood.4 The predominant collagen in SCB is type-I (Col-I), and type-II collagen (Col-II) in ACC. Shear tests demonstrate that mature cartilage fails at the mineralizing front (‘tidemark’) between soft hyaline cartilage and ACC, or within the calcified cartilage just beneath it,5 while young adult cartilage tears more chaotically in ACC.6 These studies illustrate that loading stress can cause a tear at the osteochondral junction, which can cause the failure of the entire joint. Understanding the bonding mechanisms at the ACC-SCB interface is crucial because it maintains integrity under high and repetitive loads.

Inorganic and geometric contributions to osteochondral bonding have been explored in a small number of prior studies.7-10 Some studies, using small numbers of specimens, identified fibrillar structures in the OCL but did not characterize them further.11-13 The contribution of fibrillar bonding at the OCL to joint integrity has not been thoroughly investigated and has sometimes been dismissed altogether.14 This lack of understanding might hamper the efficiency of clinical cartilage repair strategies and the use of collagen-based hydrogels to treat osteochondral defects.15 A recent scanning electron microscopy study demonstrated a gap between ACC and SCB in the decalcified OCL of the bovine patella, which was interpreted as a lack of continuity in the organic phase and an absence of bonding interaction between cartilage and bone collagens.9 This observation contradicted an earlier multiphoton light microscopy finding of fibrillar proteins crossing the OCL in the equine distal third metacarpal bone.12 The apparent disagreement between these observations could be due to different species, different bones with distinctive developmental histories (patella/sesamoid versus metacarpal/long bone), or the various imaging and analytical methods used.

OCL formation is similar, although not identical, to the formation of osteonal cement line between secondary osteons and the adjacent bone matrix.16-18 Collagen fibrils have been observed in the osteonal cement line; however, neither the fibrils’ tissue origin (whether from osteonal or extra-osteonal matrix) nor bonding between the fibrils was reported.19 The similar formation mechanisms of osteonal and osteochondral cement lines support investigation of the OCL for the presence of collagen fibrils.20-22 On the other hand, finding collagen fibrils in the osteonal cement line would be inconsistent with the observation of osteoblast-like cells cleaning osteoclastic resorption pits of organic components after osteoclastic resorption and prior to osteoid deposition. 23,24

Previous contradictions might have arisen because of species differences; therefore, more than one species needs to be investigated with a uniform methodology for confirmation of results. In addition to the contrasting reports of fibrillar presence in the OCL, no studies have been conducted that characterize collagen fibrils at the OCL. The objective of this study was to analyze OCL at several anatomical sites using a uniform methodology to investigate its morphological variations. Additionally, we aimed to investigate if Col-I and Col-II fibrils from SCB and ACC, respectively, are present in the OCL, and whether they colocalize with sufficient proximity to contribute to bone-cartilage binding.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

Equine and bovine osteochondral samples were collected immediately post-mortem from skeletally mature healthy adult animals. The anatomical sites (femoral head, metatarsal condyles, and patella) were selected to provide joint material from various biomechanical environments and developmental origins (Table I). Large mammalian species may be more representative of the human situation than smaller species due to secondary osteonal remodelling being absent from small mammals, and due to the more similar tissue and organ scales. The epiphyseal region was cleaned of soft-tissue to expose the joint, and to ensure that there was no gross orthopaedic disease such as osteophytosis or cartilage eburnation. An electric bone saw, set to its lowest speed to avoid excessive heat generation, was used to cut 5 × 5 × 5 cm rough-cut blocks of articular cartilage and underlying bone. These samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in phosphate-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C for 24 hours, then transferred to 70% ethanol for storage. A low-speed water-cooled diamond saw (Isomet Low-Speed Saw; Buehler, USA) was used to cut 5 × 5 × 5 mm blocks from the samples, which were then demineralized in 10% formic acid (pH: 7.4, Sigma-Alrich) and embedded in paraffin for sectioning (Supplementary Tables i and ii).

Four consecutive sections (4 µm thick) from each block were mounted per glass slide: three controls (no label, Col-I only, Col-II only) and one complete experiment (Col-I + Col II) (Figure 1). Sections were cleared using xylene (Honeywell, USA) and rehydrated through serial ethanol concentrations (Supplementary Table ii). Antigen retrieval was performed using 0.02 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) (Honeywell) and pepsin (TCI, China) at 30 mg/ml. Non-specific binding was prevented by incubation in 1% bovine serum albumin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sections were labelled with unconjugated primary antibodies against rabbit Col-I (PA126204, 1:200; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and goat Col-II (PA1-86055, 1:200, Invitrogen), coupled with fluorescent donkey anti-rabbit (A32790:A488, 1:500, abcam, USA) and chicken anti-goat secondary antibodies (A21468:A594, 1:500, Invitrogen). After each labelling, sections were washed with 1× tris-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich) in three five-minute changes to remove unbound labels. Finally, precision coverslips (Carl Zeiss, Germany) were mounted using glycerol-based antifade mountant (P36970, Thermo Fisher) (Supplementary Table iii).

Image acquisition and analysis

A point-scanning confocal microscope (Nikon A1-R HD25 Confocal Microscope with Nikon Eclipse Ti2-E; Software NIS-Elements C version 5.30, Nikon, Japan) was used for z-stack imaging of immunolabelled sections (Ex/Em: 405/425-475, 488/500-550, 561/620-670 for DAPI, A488, and A594 respectively). A 100×/1.45 NA objective lens (CFI Plan Apochromat DM Lambda 100× oil; Nikon) was used for image acquisition (Supplementary Table iv). The instrument’s channel alignment and deconvolution algorithms were validated using a multicolour bead slide (T14792, Invitrogen). These images were used to assess potential misalignment in the fluorescent channels, to confirm that the deconvolution algorithm was functioning as intended, and to characterize any residual artefacts. A standardized imaging protocol was employed to maintain consistency and minimize variability in the acquired images. Furthermore, experimental images were uniformly denoised and deconvolved before further analysis. In immunofluorescence studies, we observe fluorophores and not the epitopes themselves; thus, the approximate maximum distance between Col-I/-II epitopes and fluorophores was estimated (~20 nm) to account for inaccuracy in epitope localization. All the specimen preparation, image acquisition, analysis, and data interpretation was performed by the same person (KH) to minimize any individual variation.

Each section was virtually divided into five parts, and one microscopic field was imaged for each part (Figure 1). Samples and fields viewed in each section are listed in Table I. For sectioning, samples were prepared such that the osteochondral interface was perpendicular to the direction of cutting. Special care was taken to avoid oblique sectioning to reduce false overlap/colocalization between Col-I and Col-II. The entire OCL within each field of view (FOV) was analyzed so that geometrical variation within the Z-plane would not impact the final degree of overlap and colocalization. The Nyquist theorem was used for appropriate sampling (xyz resolution) during imaging setup.

Image analysis

Bone cement lines range in thickness from 0.04 to 5.0 µm.13,25-27 Therefore, a region of interest (ROI) about 5 to 10 µm wide from each image set was cropped manually at the osteochondral interface between ACC and SCB.

Images were segmented using DiAna plugin of Fiji.28,29 In DiAna, colocalization is analyzed using an object-based thresholding mechanism through spatial information to quantify the degree of colocalization. Firstly, images were segmented by computing local maxima belonging to different objects. Individual channels were labelled as separate segmented output images. These images were then superimposed to identify the colocalization of Col-I and Col-II labelled channels. These colocalization complexes were then counted and their volumes measured (Supplementary Figures a and b). The OCL in each dataset was also normalized. The length of the OCL in the xy plane in each dataset was measured manually. This value was then multiplied by the depth of the image stack in the z-direction to get the total area analyzed per section.

It is important to note here that one fibril can have several antibody complexes attached; therefore, we were counting Col-I/-II colocalizing complexes and not fibrils. Hence, multiple complexes can be formed between the same Col-I/-II fibrils. The diameter of the collagen fibrils in adult cartilage is 25 to 100 nm while it has been reported up to ~500 nm in bone.30,31 Fibrils were observed to have both transverse and longitudinal profiles at the OCL compared to the imaging plane. During segmentation, these appeared as ‘dots’ and ‘lines’, respectively. The colocalization algorithm was identifying the maxima, therefore the lines might have been further broken down into dots, all corresponding to the same fibril. Thus, the colocalization was measured in terms of colocalization complexes and not fibrillar complexes.

Statistical analysis

Colocalization data had a γ distribution, so non-parametric statistical analysis was performed. A Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to compare the colocalization extent at different anatomical sites (intraspecies) with Dunn’s post-hoc test. All data points were included to allow for a holistic observation of colocalization variability at different anatomical sites. For interspecies differences in colocalization parameters (volume, number per mm2 of OCL measured, and mean volume of Col-I/Col-II colocalization per complex), normality of data obtained from all anatomical sites was assessed. Datasets with normal Gaussian distribution were tested with an independent-samples t-test, while non-normal datasets were tested with Mann-Whitney U test.

Results

Composite images showed non-uniform colocalization and overlap of Col-I and Col-II fibrils at the OCL in both equine and bovine samples (Figure 2). Here, we use ‘colocalization’ to mean the presence of Col-I and Col-II in the same resolution-limited space (submicron, pixel scale), while ‘overlap’ means the presence of Col-I and Col-II in the same tissue region without occupying the same spatial position at pixel level (Figures 2i and ii d, j). Thus, fibrillar overlap allows us to differentiate between adjacent collagen fibrils of different types. Col-I/Col-II overlap and depth

Significant overlap of Col-I and Col-II was observed in both species, with equine samples showing a relatively higher degree of fibrillar overlap. Similarly, the mean depth of Col-I/ Col-II fibrillar overlap was higher in equine than in bovine (1.5 vs 3.2 µm, respectively) (Figure 2). A bright band of Col-II immunoreactivity was observed consistently in bovine but never in equine OCL, sometimes associated with a band of Col-I (Figure 2 and Figure 3).

More intertwining of Col-I and Col-II fibrils is visible in equine at OCL compared to bovine where a distinct boundary is being formed by Col-II fibrils present in ACC.

We observed that OCL morphology varies along its length, changing character within a span of tens of microns. Within the same field of view (42 × 42 × 4 µm), the OCL appeared in some places as a distinct boundary with minimal to no apparent Col-I/Col-II colocalization or overlap (Figure 2). In other places within the same FOV, significant Col-I/Col-II overlap and colocalization were observed (Figure 2). Collagen fibrils frequently but variably overlapped and crossed the OCL both from ACC to SCB and from SCB to ACC (Figure 2), and the extent of colocalization differed among different anatomical sites (Supplementary Table v).

Col-I/Col-II number of colocalizing instances

Proximal metatarsal condyle had the highest areal volume of colocalization complexes in equine OCL (up to 5.3 × 106/mm2), while in bovines, the highest number (up to 2.4 × 106/mm2) was observed in the dorsomedial femoral head. The number of colocalization complexes was significantly higher in equine compared to bovine (p < 0.001 to 0.032, Mann-Whitney U test), except in the dorsomedial femoral condyle (Figure 4). Further, the number of colocalization complexes also varied to a higher degree in bovine compared to equine except at the metatarsal distal condyle, where a much tighter grouping was observed in bovine compared to equine (Supplementary Figure a). Also, object-based colocalization analysis showed that one type of fibril could overlap and colocalize with multiple adjacent fibrils of the other type if the direction of fibrillar approach is orthogonal to the section plane (Figure 2).

The interspecies analysis showed that the volume of Col-I/-II complexes differed significantly at all anatomical sites (p < 0.05, Mann-Whitney U test), with the metatarsal proximal condyle having the highest Col-I/Col-II colocalization in both species (Supplementary Table v). The lowest colocalization volume was found in the equine patella and bovine metatarsal distal condyle (3.62 × 105 (SE 5.56 × 104) µm3/mm2 and 1.15 × 104 (SE 3.80 × 102) µm3/mm2). Similar to the number of colocalization complexes, the volume of these complexes also showed a higher degree of variation at different anatomical sites compared to the equine OCL (Supplementary Figure c).

Mean Col-I/Col-II volume per complex was calculated by dividing total colocalization volume by the number of complexes for each anatomical site, and normalizing by the area of OCL at each site. Equine OCL had a significantly higher mean colocalization complex volume than bovine OCL (p < 0.001 to 0.0286, Mann-Whitney U test) at all anatomical sites (Figure 5b).

Osteochondral debonding

The bovine patella exhibited striking morphological variation, including a distinct gap between ACC and SCB, in addition to Col-I/Col-II overlap and colocalization (Figure 3a). Some microscopic fields imaged showed a bigger gap compared to others, while others did not show any debonding. Where there was a gap observed at the osteochondral junction, remnants of both Col-I and Col-II were observed on both sides of the debonded area, indicating a tear through previously overlapping ACC and SCB. The gap between SCB and ACC at the OCL was unique to bovine patella, being absent from all other sites in our sample, including the equine patella (Figure 3b).

Discussion

Our study revealed the presence of Col-I and Col-II fibrils in the OCL that form a dual feltwork, which may be acting to hold articular cartilage to SCB. Confocal imaging revealed that collagen fibrils originated from both ACC and SCB and overlapped (occupying the same tissue region but not necessarily the same pixel) to a depth of up to 5 µm. Additionally, Col-I/Col-II colocalization (signal from both collagens found in the same pixel) was observed, and its extent (volume, number, and size per complex) was significantly higher per unit area of OCL in equine than in bovine OCL. The extent of Col-I/Col-II colocalization varies among anatomical sites and between species. Our findings shed light on the potential variability in the formation of cement line based on the type of substrate involved, such that cement lines formed within bone might not be identical to the cement lines that bone forms on other types of naturally mineralized material such as ACC.

Previous studies have focused on the role of minerals, non-fibrillar organic matrix, and geometrical interdigitation as osteochondral bonding mechanisms.8,9,27 However, these studies were not able to observe fibrillar content in OCL, and some have described a ‘gap’ interface in demineralized samples. OCL is a highly crenellated 3D structure where colocalization between Col-I/Col-II labelled areas might be due to overlap that has resulted from the cutting angle of the section relative to the plane of the OCL, i.e. a superimposition artefact produced from projecting in z through an oblique interface. This potential artefact was addressed by performing colocalization analysis on deconvolved confocal optical section data rather than maximum intensity or widefield projections.

We observed that both Col-I/-II fibrillar overlap and colocalization were greater in equine than bovine samples. While the degree of this difference (and significance of results) varied across the different sites, the trend was found to be uniform across all anatomical locations. This might relate to the higher stress and loading that equine joints are under which causes an increased overlap of Col-I/-II fibrils, or it could be unrelated to biomechanics and rather represent a phenotypic variation of the species. Similarly, the bright band of immunoreactivity seen in bovine OCL might relate to a species-level variation in the way osteoclasts and osteoblasts phagocytose and process collagen proteolysis products. The colocalization parameters (colocalization volume, number, and volume per complex) of equine OCL were also comparatively higher than in bovine samples. This might be a result of the higher overlap where more fibrillar overlap led to an increased possibility for the fibrils to intertwine and make complexes expressing both Col-I and -II specific epitopes. However, as this investigation was resolution-limited due to the use of light microscopy, further studies of the OCL using higher-resolution techniques are warranted to measure the distance between adjacent Col-I/-II epitopes.

This analysis supports previous findings of fibril-like structures in the OCL observed through multiphoton imaging.12,13 It also aligns with earlier research on osteonal cement line, where relatively disorganized collagen fibrils (compared to osteonal and interstitial bone) were observed using focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy.19 Furthermore, this evidence corroborates previous data indicating that fibrils in the OCL express similar epitopes as those in ACC (Col-II) and SCB (Col-I).11 These data help to resolve the apparent conflict in the literature by showing that the bovine patella uniquely contains tears or gaps in its osteochondral junction.9 The presence of the tear zones only in bovine patella suggests that the stress type might play a role in osteochondral debonding. However, the absence of debonding in equine patella suggests a site-species interaction. A finite elements model might be able to help us understand the impact of different loading patterns in the equine and bovine patellae. Osteochondral debonding is currently not a part of the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) histopathology grading scheme.32 The addition of this phenomenon might be a useful component of the grading scheme. The use of bovine patella as a model to understand osteochondral dynamics may also need to be reviewed, since the junction could be behaving idiosyncratically, making it an unreliable model. Alternatively, the debonding we found in the bovine patella could be an excellent model for human joint injury, however this will not be known until human OCL is investigated in detail. Investigation of undecalcified samples is needed to determine whether the patellar tears contain mineralized infill, which would have been removed during tissue decalcification, potentially creating the gap observed in our, and previous, images.33

Colocalization of Col-I and Col-II fibrils raises the possibility of fibrillar interaction and bonding between ACC- and SCB-derived Col-II and Col-I fibrils. This study observed that Col-I and Col-II fibrils were colocalizing within the resolution limits of deconvolved confocal microscopy (lateral resolution: 0.071 µm, axial resolution: 0.533 µm). Previous studies have reported that collagen fibrils, when present in close proximity, tend to form heterofibrils that express antigenic properties of both contributing fibrillar types.34,35 Colocalization of Col-I and Col-II fibrils in this study hints towards the formation of heterofibrils that would bind both Col-I and Col-II specific antibodies. We speculate that heterofibrils could contribute to strengthening the osteochondral interface between ACC and SCB by creating covalent bonds between cartilage and bone extracellular matrices. This study places bone and cartilage collagens within tens to hundreds of nanometres of each other; higher-resolution imaging and proteomics studies are required to determine whether covalent bonds form between Col-I and Col-II.

SCB advancement into ACC is similar if not identical to secondary bone turnover.22 In conventional bone turnover, after osteoclasts have removed mineralized bone, osteoblast-like cells clean the resorption pits before osteoblasts can start depositing osteoid, the first step in new bone formation.23,24,36 The presence of Col-I and Col-II fibrils in the osteochondral cement line challenges this understanding, because if the ACC resorption pits were being fully and universally cleaned of residual cartilage matrix components, Col-II fibrils should be completely absent from the interface, as osteoblasts depositing new bone do not produce type-II collagen. It is possible that ACC osteoclastic pits might not be cleaned by osteoblast-like cells and, instead, contain ragged ends of Col-II fibrils that are subsequently covered with Col-I fibrils. Our observation of colocalizing Col-I/Col-II fibrils in the osteochondral cement line suggests that while the major steps in bone remodelling might occur, the type of substrate could affect osteoclastic resorption. Therefore, to recapitulate in vitro the bone remodelling mechanism during endochondral ossification of ACC, mineralized cartilage must be used as a substrate.

This variability in colocalization extent demonstrates that the degree of collagen interaction at the OCL may appear much lower or higher if only a limited number of fields and samples are observed. There are surprisingly few detailed images of OCL available in the published literature. Despite their rarity, it is possible to observe variability in OCL morphology in relation to the extent of mineralization and the presence of a ‘gap’ interface in bovine patella.7,9 Prior studies of both osteochondral and osteonal cement line have tended to examine a single anatomical location in one species and at high magnification, which may have led to a failure to detect important morphological variations among species and sites.

It was also observed that equine samples demonstrated more frequent Col-I reactivity in ACC than bovine samples. This observation is in line with previous studies where it was noted that Col-I can be observed in articular cartilage and is more prevalent in high-loading conditions.37,38 However, this widespread sparse immunoreactivity of Col-I in equine ACC differed from that noticed at the OCL. Firstly, Col-I expression at the OCL was more fibrillar in nature with fibrils extending into the SCB. Secondly, Col-I, present away from the OCL in the ACC, did not show any colocalization with Col-II fibrils. Rather, Col-I appeared to be located in the space between adjacent Col-II fibrils.

Osteochondral debonding can result in partial or complete detachment of SCB from ACC, thereby causing considerable damage to joint integrity. Of all the samples in the present study, only the bovine patella showed osteochondral debonding that created gaps between ACC and SCB. In this case, Col-I and Col-II were observed on both sides of the gap interface, suggesting a tear in the osteochondral interface that leaves fragments of both fibril types on each side. Its presence in only the bovine patella samples, despite the simultaneous and identical processing of other specimens, makes the possibility of these tears being tissue-processing artefacts less likely. This finding further supports our hypothesis that the OCL has non-uniform morphology, highlighting the need to study it as a distinct structure rather than solely a demarcating interface, and to explore its diversity across sites and species. If the collagen fibrils were simply overlapping each other with minimal to no physical and/or chemical interaction, at the time of this osteochondral debonding, there should not have been any leftover remnants of collagen fibril type-I and type-II attached to ACC and SCB, respectively. Therefore, we suggest that these fibrils might be interacting with each other through physical and/or chemical bonding between the polypeptide chains of the adjacent fibrils. The bovine OCL often displayed an oversaturated band of Col-II reactivity. This feature was consistent across all the bovine samples, but absent from the equine samples. This strong Col-II signal might come from the deposition of Col-II fibrillar residues produced during the SCB (cutting-cone) advancement or from the exposure of Col-II epitopes during osteoclastic demineralization and proteolysis.

Further research is needed to propose a unifying model for OCL formation and morphology. Electron microscopy could supplement our understanding of the role of fibrils in osteochondral bonding. Moreover, the possibility of Col-I/Col-II heterofibril formation needs to be investigated to further elucidate whether colocalization was purely physical in nature (fibrillar entanglement) or if fibrils form covalent bonds with each other, chemically bonding cartilage to bone. In vitro osteoclast and osteoblast activity should be studied using ACC as a substrate to determine how the OCL forms during the resorption of ACC and its replacement with bone.

Bone and cartilage collagens form an interwoven dual feltwork at the OCL, which may be an important source of interface binding strength holding articular cartilage to bone. We revealed the presence of both Col-I and Col-II fibrils in the OCL, with varying degrees of colocalization at different anatomical sites within the same individual, and even within the same joint. Equine osteochondral junction had a significantly higher degree of colocalization and fibrillar overlap depth between Col-I and Col-II than found in bovine joints. This finding indicates that OCL formation varies among species and sites, such that generalization from one species (or site) to another is not straightforward unless specific comparative studies have been performed. Close attention should be paid to the critical cartilage-bone interface between ACC and SCB and how it is formed in order to improve cartilage repair strategies.
