Virtual memory CD8⁺ T cells in aging: heterogeneity, bystander protection, inflammatory potential, and inhibitory regulation
Authors: Archit Kumar, Raymond Yung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging reshapes the CD8⁺ T cell compartment through contraction of the naïve pool and expansion of memory-phenotype populations, including the progressive accumulation of antigen-inexperienced virtual memory (VM) CD8⁺ T cells. VM CD8⁺ T cells arise in the absence of foreign antigen priming and acquire a memory-like phenotype. In aged hosts, VM CD8⁺ T cells constitute a substantial fraction of the antigen-inexperienced memory-phenotype CD8⁺ T cell pool, and their differentiation is shaped by self-reactivity, homeostatic cytokines, and transcriptional programs linked to IL-15 and EOMES. During aging, VM CD8⁺ T cells accumulate numerically, display altered proliferative responses, retain sensitivity to homeostatic and inflammatory cytokines, and can preserve effector function during infection. These features suggest that VM CD8⁺ T cells may partially compensate for the age-associated loss of naïve CD8⁺ T cells by providing rapid effector-like responses. At the same time, aged VM CD8⁺ T cells appear to comprise heterogeneous subsets, including populations with cytotoxic and inflammatory potential. Emerging evidence suggests that inhibitory receptors may mark distinct VM states during aging, although their functional significance and potential regulatory role remain to be established.Keywords: Aging, CD8+ T cells, Memory T cells, Virtual memory T cells, Granzymes, Inflammation

Background

Aging is associated with a loss of physiological function and dysregulation of the immune system, which increases susceptibility to infections [1–3]. CD8+ T cells play an important role in immune surveillance and release IFN-γ and granzymes to mediate killing of the infected cells [4–6]. Aging is accompanied by remodeling of the CD8⁺ T cell compartment, characterized by contraction of the naïve pool and accumulation of memory-phenotype T cells, resulting in a reduced T cell repertoire associated with impaired response to infections [7–12]. Moreover, CD8+ T cells have been implicated as one of the factors contributing to the chronic low-grade inflammation termed inflammaging [3, 13–15].

Conventional memory CD8+ T cells, which arise following antigen exposure, are broadly divided into effector memory, central memory and tissue resident subsets, based on their function, localization, longevity, and phenotypic markers [16–24]. However, in antigen-unprimed hosts, a significant pool of CD8⁺ T cells bearing a memory phenotype exists alongside the naïve compartment [16, 25–27]. These antigen-inexperienced memory CD8+ T cells are categorized into innate memory (IM) and virtual memory (VM) cells, which differ by their developmental origin, localization and cytokine dependence [16, 25–27]. IM CD8⁺ T cells arise predominantly in the thymus and are shaped by IL-4 produced by PLZF-expressing thymocytes [26, 28–34]. By contrast, VM CD8⁺ T cells arise mainly in the periphery from naïve CD8⁺ T cells with stronger tonic self-peptide–MHC recognition, often reflected by high CD5 expression, and are maintained through IL-15/CD122-dependent homeostatic signaling [26, 35–38]. Although both IM and VM CD8⁺ T cells can express CD44, CD122, and EOMES and generally lack CD49d, they are best distinguished from each other by thymic IL-4-dependent programming versus peripheral IL-15-dependent homeostatic differentiation [25–27, 37–39]. Although both populations depend on IL-15 in the periphery, their anatomical distribution differs. IM cells are predominantly found in secondary lymphoid organs such as the spleen following thymic egress, whereas VM cells recirculate through blood and secondary lymphoid organs and additionally accumulate in non-lymphoid tissues including the liver, lung, and adipose tissue [26, 27, 36, 40–43]. In the periphery, VM cells constitute 5–20% of CD8+ T cells and express high levels of CD44, CD62L, CD122/IL-15Rβ, EOMES, and rely on cytokines for their survival, maintenance, and proliferation [16, 25–27]. While VM CD8⁺ T cells in young mice exhibit effector functions and mediate bystander protection, they exhibit proliferation defects during aging [36, 39, 44, 45]. Moreover, emerging data suggest that VM CD8+ T cells constitute heterogeneous subsets, express inhibitory receptors, and can mediate inflammatory diseases, with similar characteristics identified in aged hosts through single-cell-based analyses [42, 46–48].

In this review, we discuss the development, function, heterogeneity, bystander protection, and regulatory potential of VM CD8⁺ T cells, with particular emphasis on their remodeling during aging and their emerging role in age-associated inflammation (Table 1). We also propose a working model in which the B-cell-associated inhibitory receptor Fcgr2b may contribute to the regulation of the aged VM compartment (Fig. 1).

Aging-driven remodeling and regulatory heterogeneity of VM CD8+T cells. VM CD8+ T cells arise from antigen-inexperienced naïve CD8 + T cells under the influence of tonic self-pMHC/TCR signaling, self-reactivity, IL-15, context-dependent IL-4, and EOMES-dependent programming. During aging, contraction of the naïve CD8 + T cell pool is accompanied by accumulation of VM CD8+ T cells, which retain cytokine responsiveness but exhibit impaired TCR-driven proliferation together with survival and metabolic fitness. Emerging evidence suggests that the aged VM compartment is heterogeneous and includes at least two broad states: a memory-like/homeostatic VM state characterized by Eomes, Il2rb (CD122), Bcl2 survival-associated features, and rapid responder potential (Il12rb2, Il18r1), and a cytotoxic/inflammatory VM state enriched for Fcgr2b, Gzmm, Ccr2, Cxcr3, Stat1, and Runx3. A proposed working model is that FcγRIIb may contribute to differentiation or stabilization of the cytotoxic VM state, whereas age-associated IgG may engage FcγRIIb and promote apoptosis or restrain persistence of the Fcgr2b+ subset. Functionally, aged VM CD8+ T cells may provide beneficial early effector-like responses and partial compensation for naïve CD8+ T cell loss, but may also contribute to chronic tissue inflammation, adipose dysfunction, and age-associated inflammatory disease. Further mechanistic studies are required to define the role of FcγRIIb in VM cell fate and function. Defining VM CD8+ T cells

Conventional antigen-driven CD8+ memory cells are mainly described as effector memory (EM) and central memory (CM) T cells [16, 49, 50]. Both populations express the activation-associated receptor CD44, but EM CD8+ T cells are CD62L− CCR7−, whereas CM CD8+ T cells express homing receptors CD62L and CCR7, which direct their localization in lymphoid tissues [16, 23, 51]. Functionally, EM CD8+ T cells are more immediately poised for effector function, whereas CM CD8+ T cells are long-lived and exhibit greater self-renewal and proliferative capacity. Both EM and CM CD8+ T cells depend on common γc cytokines, IL-7 and IL-15, for homeostatic maintenance; however, CM CD8⁺ T cells rely more heavily on IL-7, owing to their higher IL-7Rα (CD127) expression and CCR7/CD62L-dependent localization to secondary lymphoid organs, which support memory T cell survival and homeostatic proliferation via IL-7 and IL-15 [16, 52–54].VM CD8+ T cells share several surface and transcriptional features with conventional antigen-experienced memory CD8⁺ T cells, including expression of CD44, CD122/ IL-15Rβ, and EOMES [25–27]. These shared features reflect a common dependence on IL-15 signaling and common memory-like transcriptional programming under homeostatic proliferation. However, unlike true antigen-experienced memory cells, VM CD8+ T cells are defined by the lack of CD49d (encoded by Itga4), a marker associated with prior antigen encounter [39]. Accordingly, VM CD8+ T cells are most precisely described by the surface phenotype CD44+ CD62L+ CD122+ CD49d− [25–27, 39, 55]. VM CD8+ T cells can also display rapid functional responsiveness to inflammatory cytokines, including IL-12 and IL-18, with induction of IFN-γ and expression of cytokine-responsive genes such as Il18r1, Il12rb1, and Il12rb2 [27, 39]. VM CD8+ T cells share several features with thymus-derived IM CD8+ T cells in circulation, however, IM cells are generally less associated with effector-linked programs, and can be distinguished from VM cells by lower surface NKG2D expression, consistent with the greater cytotoxic bias often attributed to VM cells [25, 26, 56, 57]. Notably, while thymic IM cells lack T-bet expression, peripheral IM cells upregulate T-bet following IL-15 exposure, rendering T-bet unreliable as a distinguishing marker between circulating IM and VM cells [26]. Although phenotypic definitions remain useful, VM CD8+ T cells should be interpreted with caution in the setting of infection, because antigen-specific CD8⁺ T cells can lose CD49d expression over time, complicating discrimination from bona fide VM CD8+ T cells [58].

Developmental origins of antigen-inexperienced memory-like CD8+ T cells

Conventional CD8+ T cell development

CD8+ T cells develop in the thymus, where immature thymocytes undergo sequential differentiation and selection steps to generate a functional and self-tolerant T cell receptor repertoire [59, 60]. During this process, double-negative thymocytes undergo β-selection, followed by positive and negative selection based on strength of recognition of self-peptide-MHC complexes [59, 60]. These signals not only ensure self-tolerance but also guide lineage commitment toward either the CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T cell lineage [59, 61]. As CD8 single positive (SP) thymocytes mature, they upregulate CCR7 and CD62L, egress from the thymus, and enter the periphery as naïve CD8⁺ T cells [62–65]. Upon cognate antigen encounter, these naïve CD8+ T cells become activated, differentiate into effector cells, and eventually give rise to conventional memory CD8+ T cell phenotypes known as EM and CM T cells [16, 18].

Conventional memory arises after antigen exposure and is typically maintained in secondary lymphoid organs and peripheral tissues, and is associated with expression of CD44, CD62L, and the transcription factor EOMES [16, 18, 66, 67]. However, it is now clear that not all CD8+ T cells with a memory phenotype are generated through classical antigen-driven priming [26]. In addition to conventional memory cells, there are antigen-inexperienced memory-like CD8+ T cell populations, including IM CD8+ T cells that develop in the thymus and VM CD8⁺ T cells that arise in the periphery [25, 26, 68, 69].

Thymic antigen-inexperienced IM CD8+ T cells

Innate memory CD8⁺ T cells were first described in the thymus of mice lacking the TEC kinase IL-2–inducible T cell kinase (ITK), where they were identified by the expression of markers such as CD44 and CD122 [31–34]. Later studies showed that the accumulation of these cells is not simply due to a CD8⁺ T cell–intrinsic program but is strongly influenced by IL-4 produced largely by PLZF-expressing cells, including natural killer T (NKT) cells, and CD4⁺ T cells [26, 28, 30, 56, 57]. Their accumulation is also strongly influenced by background strain: BALB/c mice generate substantially more IM CD8+ T cells than C57BL/6 mice [26, 29, 34, 70]. However, it remains unclear whether the conversion of single positive CD8⁺ T cell into IM in the thymus is stochastic or depends on specific stimuli.

Peripheral VM CD8+ T cells

In contrast to thymic IM CD8+ T cells, VM CD8⁺ T cells develop mainly outside the thymus and are maintained in the periphery [26, 37, 38, 68]. They are highly responsive to IL-4 and IL-15 for their development and proliferation [37, 71]. Unlike thymic IM CD8+ T cells, which rely heavily on IL-4, VM CD8⁺ T cells can arise in the absence of IL-4 in C57BL/6 mice whereas in BALB/c mice VM development is IL-4-dependent [26, 37, 38]. VM CD8⁺ T cells are strongly dependent on IL-15 and EOMES for their expansion and maintenance and are therefore absent in IL-15- and CD122-deficient mice [26, 37, 38]. Under steady-state, VM CD8⁺ T cells rely on basic leucine zipper transcription factor ATF-like 3 (BATF3)-dependent CD8α+ dendritic cells (DCs) as the major source of IL-15 [37, 72]. Although IL-15 plays a crucial role in the proliferation and maintenance of VM CD8+ T cells, IL-7 may contribute by sustaining the naïve CD8+ T cell pool from which VM CD8+ T cells arise. Homeostatic proliferation of naïve CD8+ T cells is driven by IL-7 together with tonic TCR-self-pMHC signals [73]. However, naïve CD8+ T cells can further be supported by elevated levels of γc cytokines including IL-7, memory CD8+ T cells rely mainly on IL-7 or IL-15 [73–77]. Together, VM CD8+ T cell development reflects the intersection of naïve CD8 + T cells homeostasis, self-pMHC-driven fitness and IL-15-depedendent peripheral expansion, rather than a single cytokine-driven pathway [36–38].

Transcriptional regulation

Transcriptional regulation mediated by T-box transcription factors plays a crucial role in CD8+ T cell memory development. The balance between T-bet and EOMES determines effector and memory fate: T-bet favors differentiation of terminal effectors [78, 79], while EOMES promotes the development of long-lived central memory CD8⁺ T cells and their survival following homeostatic proliferation [40, 66, 80, 81]. IL-4-mediated expression of EOMES is essential for the development of IM CD8⁺ T cells from CD8 SP thymocytes [29, 82, 83], a process involving EOMES interaction with RUNX3-bound enhancers in a BRG1-dependent manner [84]. In VM CD8⁺ T cells, EOMES is required for effective IL-15-mediated homeostatic proliferation and upregulates the expression of CD122/IL-15Rβ [37]. IL-4 and type I interferons further regulate EOMES-mediated expansion of VM CD8⁺ T cells [71, 72], while Aiolos (Ikzf3)-mediated repression of EOMES constrains VM CD8⁺ T cell generation [85].

Tonic TCR signaling and self-reactivity

T cell receptor (TCR) signaling is a key determinant of cell fate for VM CD8+ T cell differentiation [35], while thymic IM CD8 + T cells development depends primarily on IL-4 produced by PLZF-expressing iNKT and/or CD4+ cells [26]. Naïve CD8⁺ T cells with stronger tonic TCR signaling in response to self-peptide–MHC are more likely to differentiate into VM CD8+ T cells [35]. Consistent with this, naïve CD8+ T cells with high CD5 expression are more likely to convert into VM CD8+ T cells than those with low CD5 expression [35, 36]. CD5 is used as a marker of self-reactivity, and its expression reflects the strength of signaling upon engagement of TCR with self-peptide-MHC. VM CD8⁺ T cells are enriched among naïve CD8⁺ T cell clones with stronger tonic recognition of self-peptide–MHC, suggesting that basal TCR signal strength may influence their entry into a VM-like state [35, 36, 86]. In support of this idea, CD8–Lck coupling has been shown to affect tonic TCR signaling and VM CD8⁺ T cell formation, although this mechanism should be interpreted as contributing to VM-cell generation or enrichment rather than peripheral expansion [35, 36, 86].

Functional programming of VM CD8+ T cells

Innate-like effector capacity and bystander protection

Homeostatic proliferation (HP) can generate memory-phenotype CD8⁺ T cells in the unprimed host [87–91], and these HP memory cells display greater proliferative, cytotoxic, cytokine-producing capacity than naïve T cells, including protection following infection [89–93]. Together, these observations suggest that antigen-inexperienced memory CD8⁺ T cells are functionally poised for rapid innate-like effector responses. Consistent with this, Haluszczak et al. identified antigen-specific CD44hi CD8⁺ T cells in unprimed mice that exhibited enhanced proliferative responses to antigen relative to naïve precursors and rapidly produced IFN-γ following stimulation with IL-12 and IL-18 [39]. Lee et al. subsequently demonstrated that naturally occurring polyclonal VM CD8⁺ T cells constitute a functionally distinct antigen-inexperienced population [45]. Although these cells respond more rapidly than naïve precursors and can mediate protection against Listeria monocytogenes (LM), they are not functionally equivalent to bona fide antigen-experienced memory CD8⁺ T cells, as they exhibit limited TCR-driven IFN-γ production [45]. Later studies further strengthened this framework by showing innate-like effector function of VM CD8+ T cells, which are poised for bystander protection and express IL-12R, IL-18R, IFN-γ, NKG2D, and granzyme B, consistent with inflammatory responsiveness [36, 44]. Bystander protection mediated by VM CD8+ T cells against LM is strongly IL-15 dependent, as loss of IL-15 markedly reduced expression of IFN-γ, NKG2D, and granzyme B in VM CD8+ T cells [36]. The same study also showed that their distinct trafficking properties help determine where this protection can occur by constraining tissue access and localization [36]. Further dissecting the heterogeneity within VM CD8+ T cells, Hou et al. identified a CCR2⁺ subset within VM CD8+ T cells that is preferentially poised for effector function, expresses cytotoxicity-associated genes including Gzmm and Ctla2a, rapidly infiltrates the lung through CXCR3-mediated recruitment during primary influenza infection, and promotes early viral control [47].

Cytokine- and infection-driven plasticity

Beyond steady-state homeostatic maintenance, cytokine sensitivity and inflammatory stimuli further shape the effector potential of antigen-inexperienced memory-phenotype CD8⁺ T cells. Renkema et al. showed that IL-4 responsiveness influences both the size and functional composition of the peripheral memory-phenotype CD8⁺ T cell pool and contributes to a robust response following LCMV infection [94]. In a related but distinct setting, Baez et al. showed that Th1-biased infection, as well as IL-12 plus IL-18 administration induces thymic IL-4 and IL-15 and promotes the emergence of single-positive CD8 thymocytes with innate-like features; adoptive transfer of these cells improved control of Trypanosoma cruzi infection, indicating that inflammatory conditions can imprint innate effector properties during thymic CD8⁺ T cell development [95].

Helminth infection provides one of the clearest examples of cytokine-driven VM plasticity. Helminth-induced IL-4 response in BALB/c mice has been shown to expand the VM CD8+ T cells compartment and enhance protection against murid gammaherpesvirus-4 [96]. This expansion was associated with increased expression of cytotoxic genes (Gzma, Gzmm), chemokine receptor genes (Ccr2 and Ccr5), and type I interferon-related gene Mx1. Following viral challenge, helminth-infected mice also exhibited enhanced expansion of virus-specific CD8⁺ T cells and increased frequencies of TNF-α- and IFN-γ-producing CD8⁺ T cells [96]. Extending this framework, Yang et al. used single-cell RNA sequencing to identify a helminth-induced, IL-4-responsive CD22⁺ VM CD8+ T cell subset. Notably, this population was enriched for effector-associated genes, including Ifng, Gzma, Gzmm, Ccr2, Ccr5, and Cd39 [46].

Mechanistically, antigen-independent bystander activation of VM CD8⁺ T cells is likely to draw on signaling pathways in which IL-12 and IL-18 promote IFN-γ transcription through STAT4- and AP-1-associated programs, whereas IL-15 supports survival, expansion, and granzyme B expression [36, 97–99]. Constitutive expression of IL-12R, IL-18R, and CD122 on VM CD8+ T cells along with increased chromatin accessibility at effector genes in these cells [100] poise them for rapid responses during the early phase of infection, particularly when the frequency of naïve precursors is low. Together, these studies indicate that the effector potential of VM CD8+ T cells is not fixed but is dynamically tuned by cytokine exposure, inflammation and developmental timing.

VM CD8+ T cells in aging

Age-associated accumulation of VM CD8+ T cells

VM CD8+ T cells emerge during the neonatal period of physiological lymphopenia and persist throughout adulthood [38]. Timestamping analyses have demonstrated that neonatal CD8+ T cells are more likely than adult CD8+ T cells to undergo homeostatic proliferation and adopt a VM phenotype [100]. During aging, the naïve CD8⁺ T cell compartment undergoes nonrandom attrition, leaving behind an antigen-inexperienced pool enriched for precursors with higher TCR:pMHC avidity [101]. In unprimed aged hosts, these naïve CD8+ T cells undergo enhanced homeostatic turnover and acquire a memory-like phenotype, consistent with preferential preservation of clones with superior self-pMHC-driven homeostatic fitness [101]. Following thymic involution, maintenance and proliferation of the antigen-inexperienced CD8+ T cell pool become increasingly dependent on peripheral homeostatic signals, including IL-7-dependent survival signals. During aging, increased circulating levels of IL-7 has been reported [102]. While IL-7 may preserve antigen-inexperienced CD8⁺ T cells with sufficient homeostatic fitness, whereas IL-15 could promote proliferation and acquisition of memory- or VM-phenotype during aging [36, 38]. Although age-related accumulation of antigen-inexperienced CD44hi CD8⁺ cells had been reported previously [103], key evidence came from Chiu et al. (2013), who showed that the CM CD8+ T cells population that accumulated with age is composed mainly of antigen-inexperienced VM CD8+ T cells. Importantly, this age-associated accumulation occurs independently of CD4+ T cells, CCR5, and CXCR3 [55] and is accompanied by heightened responsiveness to homeostatic cytokines, driven by upregulated CD122 expression, as well as to type I interferons [72, 104, 105]. Moreover, VM CD8+ T cells have also been identified in extralymphoid tissues such as lungs, liver, and aged adipose tissue, suggesting that local tissue environments may redirect them toward pathogen-responsive or metabolically inflammatory states [36, 42, 43]. The tissue distribution of VM CD8⁺ T cells reflect a combination of local survival signals and trafficking-associated programs. In IL-15-rich tissues such as the liver, and potentially in adipose tissue, IL-15 may support the maintenance or accumulation of VM CD8⁺ T cells [26, 36, 106]. During inflammation, VM CD8⁺ T cell entry into peripheral tissues may be supported by chemokine receptor expression and type I interferon-associated inflammatory cues. For example, in young mice, CCR2⁺ VM CD8⁺ T cells rapidly infiltrate the lung during influenza infection and express CXCR3-associated trafficking programs; however, additional studies are needed to determine how these pathways regulate VM CD8⁺ T cell accumulation in aged hosts after infection [47]. High expression of CD44 may further support tissue retention, while LFA-1 (CD11a/CD18) supports adhesion to vascular endothelium and tissue entry [21, 26, 36]. Together, these trafficking-associated properties may direct VM CD8⁺ T cells toward specific compartments such as the liver, lung, and adipose tissue, shaping where they contribute to early immune defense or, in aged hosts, to tissue inflammation.

Functional remodeling of aged VM CD8+ T cells

Age-associated accumulation of VM CD8+ T cells is accompanied by selective functional remodeling rather than uniform loss of responsiveness. As the naïve CD8+ T cell pool contracts with age, clones with self-MHC-driven homeostatic fitness are preferentially retained and can remain functionally competent during infection. Rudd et al. demonstrated that high-avidity CD44hi Vβ10⁺ gB-8p-precursors remained enriched during aging and preserved efficient recruitment together with effector differentiation, including KLRG1 upregulation, CD62L downregulation, and maintenance of polyfunctional IFN-γ, TNF-α, and granzyme B production [101]. By contrast, Lanzer et al. directly examined bona fide VM CD8+ T cells and showed that, in aged influenza-naïve mice, VM CD8+ T cells make a major contribution to the antiviral response, differentiate into granzyme-producing effectors, and support viral clearance [43]. Although aged VM CD8+ T cells retain effector responsiveness following TCR-driven stimulation [105, 107], this response in aged VM CD8+ T cells becomes increasingly dominated by monofunctional rather than polyfunctional cytokine-producing cells [107]. In parallel, aged VM CD8+ T cells exhibit impaired TCR-driven proliferation, linked to increased apoptosis and acquisition of a senescence-like state marked by increased γ-H2AX and phosphorylation of p38, ERK1/2 and MAPK [105, 107]. Notably, this proliferation impairment does not necessarily indicate a complete loss of cell division programs, as aged VM CD8+ T cells retain higher frequency of asymmetric cell division than aged naïve CD8+ T cells [108]. Although aged VM CD8 + T cells proliferate poorly after TCR engagement, they retain the capacity to proliferate in response to homeostatic cytokines and maintain high expression of CD122/IL-15Rβ [105, 107].

Metabolic and survival adaptations of aged VM CD8+ T cells

The persistence of aged VM CD8+ T cells is supported by intrinsic survival and metabolic programs. Increased expression of anti-apoptotic genes Bcl2, Bcl2l2, and Bcl2l1 in aged VM CD8+ T cells is associated with their accumulation with age [107]. Metabolically, aged VM CD8+ T cells display high spare respiratory capacity, preserved glycolytic capacity and heightened IL-15 responsiveness, consistent with enhanced survival capacity [108, 109]. Yet these adaptations do not rescue their antigen-driven in vitro cytotoxicity and TCR-driven proliferative response [109]. Consistent with this, Borsa et al. showed that aged VM CD8+ T cells preserve asymmetric cell division, and that their daughter cells exhibit greater memory potential, improved expansion, and enhanced cytokine production following viral challenge [108].

Infection history reshapes aged VM CD8+ T cells

As discussed in the previous section ‘Cytokine- and infection-driven plasticity’, helminth infection expands the VM CD8+ subset in a cytokine-dependent manner. However, Hussain et al. showed that VM CD8+ T cells from aged mice exhibit both intrinsic and extrinsic defects and fail to expand following helminth infection [110]. Extending this line of work, the same group examined how different infections reshape the VM CD8+ T cell pool, they further showed that lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) causes a significant loss of VM CD8+ T cells, leaving behind a residual VM CD8+ T cell pool with higher TCR sensitivity. Notably, this heightened TCR sensitivity persisted into old age and was accompanied by attenuation of senescent VM CD8+ T cells [111]. The finding that viral infection can attenuate senescence in aged CD8+ T cells further questions the relationship between lifelong infections such as cytomegalovirus (CMV) acquired during aging and VM CD8+ T cells. In humans, however, establishing the relationship between persistent viral infection and VM-like CD8⁺ T cells requires caution. Evidence suggests an association between CMV infection and expansion of IL-15-responsive CD57⁺ VM-like CD8⁺ T cells [112–114]. Persistent viral infections, especially CMV, can generate large antigen-specific clonal expansions within the CD8⁺ T cell compartment in elderly individuals [115]. CMV pp65-specific CD8⁺ T cells frequently acquire a terminal or terminally differentiated effector memory (TEMRA)-like phenotype characterized by CD45RA re-expression, CD57 expression, and loss of CD27 and CD28, markers that overlap substantially with proposed human VM-like CD8⁺ T cell phenotypes [112, 113, 115–119]. Moreover, CMV lysate has been shown to induce IL-15 production from leukocytes and stimulation of IL-15 induces expression of CD45RA in CD45RA− CD45RO+ CMV-specific CD8+ T cells [120]. Therefore, associations between CMV seropositivity and expansion of CD57⁺ and/or CD45RA⁺ cells within human VM-like CD8⁺ T cell populations must be interpreted with caution, because these cells could represent cytokine-expanded antigen-inexperienced cells, antigen-experienced CMV-specific clones, or a mixture of both. Distinguishing these possibilities will require antigen-specific analyses, such as CMV peptide-MHC multimer staining, together with TCR repertoire assessment and CD57 expression as a marker of replicative senescence [121, 122]. Together, these findings suggest that aging does not produce a uniform VM CD8+ T cell compartment, rather, it leaves behind a larger but functionally heterogeneous pool whose survival, proliferative fitness, and effector quality can be reshaped by infection history.

Heterogeneity, inflammation and regulatory potential of aged VM CD8+ T cells

Heterogeneity and inflammatory potential of VM CD8+ T cells

The VM CD8+ T cell compartment is composed of heterogeneous pools that display properties of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. In young mice, VM CD8+ T cells differentiate into short-lived effector cells during infection and are subsequently enriched for a CM phenotype during the memory phase [45]. This effector vs. memory divergence is further reflected by CCR2 expression, where CCR2+ cells are better poised toward an effector fate, while CCR2− cells serve as memory precursors which further could acquire the tissue-residency phenotype following infection [47]. Consistent with this framework, our single-cell-based analysis suggests that heterogeneity also exists within aged VM CD8+ T cell subsets and that Ccr2 expression may be associated with cytotoxic VM CD8+ T cells during aging (Figs. 1 and 2). However, further validation experiments are required to define the extent and functional significance of heterogeneity within aged VM subsets.

Adipose tissue is considered one of the key niches for understanding age-related immune remodeling [123], functioning both as a reservoir for memory T cells [124–126] and as a site of age-associated immune cell accumulation [42, 127–129]. Importantly, CD8+ T cells have been associated with aging and inflammation [13, 14, 42, 128, 130, 131], making it pertinent to dissect the heterogeneity within VM pools and identify the subsets that drive inflammation during aging. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing combined with RNA velocity and trajectory analyses of CD8⁺ T cells from aged adipose tissue revealed that VM CD8⁺ T cells in this compartment are transcriptionally heterogeneous [42] (Figs. 1 and 2). Trajectory analyses identified a differentiation continuum in which cytotoxic-like VM CD8+ T cells, expressing granzyme M, arise from the broader VM pool. These effectors subsequently transition into a quiescent state with reduced translational and metabolic activity but sustained Bcl2 expression and cytokine receptor gene expression, consistent with the survival adaptations as described earlier.

While the effector function of VM CD8+ T cells could be beneficial to the aged host during infection and provide protection against infections [43], their chronic accumulation could be pathological. CD8⁺ T cells, including cytotoxic VM pools have been implicated in granzyme-mediated tissue inflammation in aged hosts [14, 42, 132, 133]. This pathological potential is further supported by the observation that granzyme B-expressing innate cytotoxic VM CD8+ T cell subset, differentiated from VM pools, drive pathological condition alopecia areata in an NKG2D-dependent manner, and NKG2D blockade is sufficient to attenuate disease [48]. These findings underscore the need to identify other regulatory proteins expressed on VM CD8+ T cells during aging that could have functional relevance.

Inhibitory receptors on VM CD8+ T cells

Trajectory analyses of CD8+ T cells from aged adipose tissue identified the inhibitory receptor Fcgr2b as one of the notable genes expressed in cytotoxic-like VM CD8+ T cell subset [42] (Fig. 2). FcγRIIb is an inhibitory receptor expressed on B cells that limits the BCR signaling [134]. Interestingly, the presence of B cell inhibitory receptors on VM CD8+ T cells is not confined to FcγRIIb. Elevated expression of CD22, another inhibitory receptor expressed on B cells [135], has been reported on IL-4-driven VM CD8⁺ T cell subsets with an activated phenotype expressing granzyme A, granzyme M, and IFN-γ [46]. CD22 functions as an intrinsic negative regulator of the IL-4-induced effector program, limiting the expansion and responsiveness of VM CD8+ T cells during Heligmosomoides polygyrus infection [46]. Together, these suggest that the functional output of VM CD8+ T cells may be influenced by inhibitory receptors classically known to express on B cells.

Earlier, FcγRIIb has been shown to be expressed on memory CD8+ T cells and on activated CD8+ T cells in tumor and infection settings, where it regulates memory cell expansion and mediates apoptosis in an Fgl2-dependent manner [136–140]. During aging, elevated Fcgr2b expression has also been reported on regulatory CD8+ T cells, however, its functional relevance has not been determined [141]. Interestingly, IgG, a ligand for FcγRIIb, accumulates with age and has been implicated as driver of aging [142, 143]. Considered together with trajectory analyses of aged VM CD8+ T cells, these observations suggest that Fcgr2b activation may regulate the innate-like cytotoxic VM CD8+ T cell pool via IgG-mediated apoptosis (Fig. 1). Notably, these Fcgr2b-expressing innate-like cytotoxic VM CD8+ T cells were found to express Avcr2a, Klra3, Klrc1, Klrk1, Ctla2a, Stat1, Runx3, suggesting a possible regulatory role of these cells during aging [42] (Figs. 1 and 2).

Supporting this, evidence suggests that elevated Fcgr2b expression is part of a distinct age-associated VM CD8+ T cell signature and that FcγRIIB-expressing aged VM cells may be prone to apoptosis [144]. These observations are compatible with the idea that Fcgr2b may influence the emergence or regulation of a cytotoxic/inflammatory VM state. At the same time, blockade of FcγRIIB has been suggested to enhance CD8+ T cell responses [144], further raising the possibility that FcγRIIb contributes to age-associated dysfunction in VM CD8+ T cells. However, further mechanistic studies are required to determine whether FcγRIIb directly drives cytotoxic differentiation, mediates IgG-dependent apoptotic control, or instead marks a terminal or regulatory state within the aged VM compartment.

VM-like CD8+ T cells in human aging and inflammation

The VM phenotype has been most clearly defined in mice, whereas defining an equivalent antigen-inexperienced VM CD8⁺ T cell population in humans remains more challenging, cause of phenotypic changes induced by infection history. A human VM-like CD8+ T cells has been described within a CD45RA+ CD27− compartment [48, 107, 145, 146]. However, the phenotypic markers used to define naïve, memory, TEMRA, and VM CD8+ T cells are highly overlapping [117, 147, 148]. While CD45RA was historically considered an exclusive marker of naïve T cells, its expression can also be induced on memory CD8⁺ T cells during CMV infection [147]. As discussed earlier, infections such as CMV induce phenotypic changes within the CD8⁺ T cell compartment, including loss of CD27 [115, 118, 119], and therefore require additional caution when defining human VM CD8⁺ T cells.

However, these putative human VM cells express low levels of CD5 [36] and express CD122, Nur77, and EOMES, consistent with IL-15 responsiveness and elevated basal TCR signaling, two central features of murine VM CD8⁺ T cells [25–27, 36]. They also express the inhibitory receptors KIR (killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptor) and NKG2A, show loss of CCR7 and CD27, and have therefore been proposed as a close human VM-like counterpart [25–27, 36, 52, 115, 117–119, 146, 149]. Functionally, these human CD45RA+ Eomes+ KIR / NKG2A+ T cells resemble murine VM CD8+ T cells in their ability to mount effector-like responses and cytotoxicity following IL-12 and IL-18 stimulation [36, 146]. Interestingly, these cells accumulate with age in blood and spleen, and exhibited localization in liver and skin, like murine VM CD8+ T cells [36, 48, 107, 145]. Like murine VM cells, human VM-like CD8+ T cells exhibit proliferation defects following TCR stimulation and display high spare respiratory capacity as discussed previously, and have also been linked to inflammatory disease, including alopecia areata [48, 107, 109].

Interestingly, human CD62L⁺CD45RO⁺CD25⁺ memory-like CD8⁺ T cells with a highly diverse TCR repertoire have been shown to provide protection against influenza virus [150, 151] and may compensate for the loss of naïve CD8⁺ T cells in aged individuals [152]. Similar findings were reported for VM CD8⁺ T cells in aged mice by Lanzer et al. [43], although it remains unclear whether this CD62L⁺CD45RO⁺CD25⁺ subset truly represents a population of VM-like CD8⁺ T cells in humans. That said, whether human VM-like CD8+ T cells simply accumulate with age or are shaped by infection history and whether they compensate for naïve CD8⁺ T cell loss or contribute to inflammation in humans requires further investigation.

Finally, the contribution of VM-like CD8⁺ T cells to inflammaging must be considered in the context of multiple inflammatory drivers, including senescent cells with a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), aged innate immune cells, adipose tissue inflammation, persistent infection, and cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α.

Conclusion and open questions

Functional and phenotypic heterogeneity has emerged as a defining feature of the VM CD8⁺ T cell compartment during aging. Current evidence supports a model in which one fraction of aged VM CD8⁺ T cells retain memory-like properties, whereas another acquires innate-like cytotoxic characteristics. Although aged VM CD8⁺ T cells show impaired proliferation following TCR stimulation, they remain highly responsive to homeostatic cytokines and can mount rapid effector-like responses. In the context of age-associated loss of naïve CD8⁺ T cells, these properties may help preserve early immune protection. At the same time, persistent accumulation of cytotoxic VM subsets may also have pathological consequences, particularly if sustained granzyme expression contributes to chronic tissue inflammation in aged hosts.

A major unresolved issue is therefore whether cytotoxic VM CD8⁺ T cells are primarily protective, pathogenic, or context-dependent. Addressing this question will require a better understanding of the heterogeneity of VM CD8⁺ T cell subsets across tissues, the signals that govern their differentiation, and their fate following infection in aged hosts (Fig. 1). It will also be important to determine whether distinct tissue environments selectively promote either memory-like or cytotoxic VM states.

A second set of open questions concerns the regulation of aged VM CD8⁺ T cells by inhibitory receptors, particularly Fcgr2b. Although Fcgr2b expression has been observed in aged VM-associated cytotoxic subsets, the timing, anatomical distribution, and functional significance of this expression remain unclear. It is not yet known which host-derived signals induce Fcgr2b expression in VM CD8⁺ T cells, whether FcγRIIb⁺ and FcγRIIb⁻ VM subsets differ in survival or effector capacity, or whether FcγRIIb preferentially marks a terminal, regulatory, or apoptosis-prone cytotoxic state. These questions are especially intriguing considering age-associated IgG accumulation, which raises the possibility that IgG- FcγRIIb interactions may shape the fate, cytotoxicity, or inflammatory potential of VM CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 1). However, this possibility requires direct functional testing.

Finally, the relevance of VM CD8⁺ T cells to human aging remains insufficiently defined. Human VM-like CD8⁺ T cell populations share several phenotypic and functional features with murine VM cells, but whether they play comparable protective or inflammatory roles during aging is still unclear. Lifelong infection history, including persistent infections such as CMV, as well as repeated acute inflammatory exposures, may substantially remodel this compartment in humans. Resolving these questions will require integrated single-cell, spatial, and functional studies in mice together with careful phenotypic characterization in human aging cohorts. Such work should clarify whether VM CD8⁺ T cells act mainly as compensatory effectors in the aging immune system or whether they also represent a driver of age-associated inflammatory disease.
