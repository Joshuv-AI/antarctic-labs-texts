Unraveling the Deposition and Dissolution Behavior of the Ag-Modified Li Surface Based on Electrochemical Atomic Force Microscopy
Authors: Weerawat To-A-Ran, Naila Riaz Mastoi, Young Jae Song, Seong Heon Kim, Young-Jun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium is a promising anode material for advanced batteries because of its high capacity and low redox potential. However, its practical use is hindered by nonuniform Li deposition and dendrite formation, leading to safety concerns in Li metal batteries. Our study shows that Ag-based materials enhance the uniformity of Li deposition on Ag-modified Li (AgLi) surfaces, thereby addressing these key challenges. This improvement is due to the strong affinity of Ag for Li, which promotes uniform deposition and dissolution. Additionally, the AgLi surface demonstrated an improved cycling stability, which is crucial for long-term battery reliability. Emphasizing our analytical approach, we utilized comprehensive techniques such as Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) and electrochemical atomic force microscopy (EC-AFM) to locally analyze the electrical properties and unravel the Li deposition/dissolution mechanisms. KPFM analysis provided crucial insights into surface potential variations, while EC-AFM highlighted topographical changes during the Li deposition and dissolution processes, contributing significantly to the development of safer and more efficient Li metal batteries.Keywords: Li deposition, nucleation sites, EC-AFM, Li metal anodes, surface potential

Introduction

With the rapid increase in global electric vehicle (EV) usage, there is an accompanying increase in the demand for advanced energy storage solutions that are both high-performance and cost-effective.1,2 Currently, Li-ion batteries (LIBs) are the predominant energy sources for EVs.1,3 However, the current LIBs, comprising graphite anodes and layered oxide cathodes, offer a practical energy density of approximately 250 Wh kg–1,4 and this falls significantly short of the future requirement, which is required to be 350–500 Wh kg–1.5,6 Li metal is a key factor in achieving the required energy density, offering an extremely large theoretical specific capacity (3860 mAh g–1) and a very low standard electrochemical redox potential (−3.040 V vs standard hydrogen electrode), which can provide an energy density between 440 and 2600 Wh kg–1 depending on its configuration.2,4,6,7 Despite its impressive energy density, Li metal presents safety concerns and a low Coulombic efficiency, which are generally because of nonuniform Li deposition/dissolution and associated side reactions. These factors lead to dendrite formation and significant volume expansion, which are critical issues that need to be overcome.7−9

In recent decades, numerous efforts have been made to address safety concerns and improve the Coulombic efficiency of Li metal batteries. These efforts include the development of an artificial solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) and protective coating layers,10 optimization of electrolyte additives,11 modification of current collectors such as ZnO and Al2O3 thin films on copper current collectors,29 application of interfacial pressure on the Li metal surface,30 and use of lithiophilic host materials.12−14 One of the most promising materials for hosting Li metal is Ag-based materials, which offer several advantages including excellent electronic conductivity, superior lithiophilicity, and the high affinity of Ag for Li, which in turn reduces the Li nucleation barrier. They promote uniform Li deposition/dissolution and exhibit a good cycling stability.15−17

This study presents a detailed analysis of Ag-modified Li (AgLi) surfaces using comprehensive analytical techniques such as Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) and electrochemical atomic force microscopy (EC-AFM) to measure the local electrical properties and unravel Li deposition/dissolution. KPFM analysis offers crucial insights into variations in the surface potential, while EC-AFM demonstrates the topographical changes occurring during the Li deposition and dissolution processes. The key feature of our work is the identification of the surface potential as a crucial factor in controlling Li deposition and dissolution in lithium metal batteries (LMBs). By modification of the surface potential, we can create more favorable electrochemical sites at the electrode surface. Our research aims to investigate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced performance of the AgLi surface compared to that of bare Li, with a particular focus on the initial deposition on Ag sites and the induction of uniformity in later cycles. Using subtractive AFM imaging and the numerical integration method, we quantitatively evaluated morphological evolution and surface roughness. The findings of this study are expected to significantly advance our understanding of Li deposition and dissolution on Li metal surfaces, contributing to the development of more efficient and safer LMBs.

Experimental Section

Ag-Modified Li Electrode

Commercial lithium (Li) foil from Honjo Metal was cut into a disc with a diameter of 10 mm and then transferred to a PTFE holder. Then, 5 mg of silver (Ag) nanoparticle powder, with an average size of 100 nm, from Sigma-Aldrich, was added to the Li surface. The holder was gently oscillated with a vortex stirrer (KMC-1300 V, Vision Scientific, South Korea) at 3000 rpm for 2 min to distribute the Ag powder across the Li surface, resulting in a Ag loading of 6.35 mg cm–2. Subsequently, the surface was gently pressed to secure the Ag powder onto the Li surface. All activities were performed under dry atmospheric conditions (dew point −70 °C and temperature 23 °C).

Surface Potential Analysis

Surface potential mapping was performed using KPFM with a Cr/Au-coated Si tip (Multi 75-EG, BudgetSensors, Bulgaria) using AFM (NX-10, Park Systems, South Korea). The KPFM controller was set to an AC signal frequency of 17 kHz and an amplitude of 1 V. All KPFM scans were conducted at a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels over a 25 × 25 μm2 area. The morphological features within the same area analyzed by KPFM scanning and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were examined by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi, Japan).

Electrochemical Surface Evolution

Further analysis was conducted using EC-AFM scanning in a custom-made polyether ether ketone (PEEK) liquid cell, as detailed in Figure S1, filled with 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide in tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (LiTFSI in TEGDME) electrolyte solution. This setup utilized a two-electrode configuration comprising a AgLi or bare Li electrode disk as the working electrode and a Li electrode ring as the counter electrode. Li electrodeposition was facilitated by applying a current density of 2 mA cm–2 for 3 min to the working electrode using an SP-300 potentiostat (Bio-Logic, France). Topological changes were observed in the tapping mode using an AFM tip (NCHR, NanoSensors, Switzerland). All EC-AFM scans were conducted at a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels over a 25 × 25 μm2 area. All experimental procedures, including sample preparation, were performed in a dry-atmosphere room maintained at approximately 23 °C with a dew point of approximately −60 °C.18 After the EC-AFM images were acquired, image subtraction, numerical integration, and roughness calculations were performed using MATLAB 2018a.

Results and Discussion

The topological changes on the Li surface during the galvanostatic deposition and dissolution were monitored using EC-AFM. The procedure involved a current density of 2 mA cm–2 for 3 min, alternating between a negative current for deposition and a positive current for dissolution for each cycle, as depicted in Figure 1. Before each deposition cycle, the initial Li surface was scanned (Figure 1a–d), followed by Li deposition, with the extent of deposition shown in Figure 1e–h. The subsequent dissolution process involving the removal of Li from the surface is shown in Figure 1i–l. The Li metal surface exhibits a noticeable change before (Figure 1a) and after applying the deposition current in the first cycle (Figure 1e). A significant portion of Li was deposited after the current was applied and granules formed on the Li metal surface, as depicted in Figure 1e. Following dissolution, Li was mainly removed from the initial deposition area, leaving remnants along the edges and leading to pit formation on the surface. This phenomenon occurs during driving Li deposition beneath the SEI layer, generating significant local stresses that induce local cracks.2,19 Consequently, this promotes new Li extraction during dissolution,20 thus leaving inactive Li at the edges and resulting in pit formation.

In the fifth cycle, the initial surface, as shown in Figure 1b, was rougher than that in the first cycle with numerous pits formed in the previous cycles. These pits, visible on the initial surface of the fifth cycle, resulted from repeated deposition and dissolution processes, highlighting the cumulative impact on the Li surface morphology. Upon reapplying the deposition current (Figure 1f), these pits served as nucleation sites for Li deposition. This nucleation within the pits, a phenomenon previously reported in the literature,19,21 can be attributed to the insufficient physical strength of the SEI during the deposition and dissolution of the Li metal surface. This leads to the breaking and repair of the SEI in the pits. Additionally, the insufficient physical strength of the SEI promotes Li diffusion and kinetically enhances Li deposition within the pits.21,22 The repeated occurrence of pit formation and nucleation within these pits through cycles 10–15, as shown in Figure 1c,d,g,h,k,l, led to significant surface distortion. This distortion is readily apparent when the initial surface topography, as shown in Figure 1a, is compared with the final topography, as shown in Figure 1l. The resulting surface distortion provides evidence of nonuniform deposition and dissolution on the Li metal surface.

Moving forward to the AgLi surface, the surface electrical properties of AgLi were evaluated by KPFM, as depicted in Figure 2a,d for topography and the corresponding surface potential, respectively. The areas containing Ag on the Li surface exhibit surface potentials lower than those of the surrounding areas, indicating relatively higher negative charges. Indeed, it has been reported that the humidity of ambient air can alter surface electronic properties, such as the surface charge density or charge trapped on the surface.31,32 To address this issue, we ensured that all KPFM scanning steps in our study were conducted in a controlled, dry-atmosphere room environment. This setup is commonly accepted in battery research18 and offers the advantage of maintaining a low relative humidity level below 0.1%.33 By operating under these conditions, we significantly minimize the risk of any alterations in surface electronic properties due to humidity. Following with EC-AFM scanning, these areas showed the topography before (Figure 2b) and after (Figure 2e) applying a current density to drive Li deposition. It is evident that the areas with Ag, which display a lower surface potential than the surrounding areas, lead to a reduction in the Li nucleation barrier,23 thereby making them favorable for Li deposition. This is indicated by the main features of topographical evolution occurring in these areas, as illustrated in Figure 2e. The topographic evolution was further confirmed by image subtraction, subtracting Figure 2b from Figure 2e to emphasize the changes; the results are depicted in Figure 2c. As shown in Figure 2c, the topological changes in the Ag-containing area are on the order of 1000–2000 nm, as indicated by the green color, whereas the surrounding bare Li area exhibits changes within 100 nm, as indicated by blue. Further confirmation is provided by the line profiles shown in Figure 2f extracted from the KPFM and EC-AFM images along the dashed red lines. The orange and green lines extracted from Figure 2a,b represent the KPFM and EC-AFM topography before Li deposition, respectively, which are nearly the same. The protrusions shown in the two topography lines correspond to the lower surface potential feature in the distance range from 5 to 10 μm. In addition, large changes were observed in the EC-AFM topography after Li deposition (the blue line in Figure 2f).

To confirm that Ag exhibits a lower surface potential than Li, we conducted KPFM scanning and SEM/EDS analysis on the same area, as shown in Figure 3. Figure 3a displays the topography from the KPFM scan, where six distinct locations were identified, corresponding to lower surface potential areas, as illustrated in Figure 3b. Figure 3c,d shows SEM/EDS analyses of regions 1 and 2, respectively. As shown in Figure 3c, the three low-potential points within region 1 are analyzed, whereas Figure 3d shows the analysis of the remaining three points in region 2. The SEM/EDS analysis in both regions confirmed a lower surface potential at the locations where Ag was present compared to Li. This observation was consistent across both regions and supported the conclusion that Ag acts as an electron sink. This tendency of electrons to preferentially accumulate at the Ag sites makes Ag highly attractive for Li+ accumulation during Li deposition.

The morphological evolution of AgLi during galvanostatic Li deposition and dissolution was observed using EC-AFM, employing the same current density and duration as those previously used for bare Li. Negative and positive currents were applied for the deposition and dissolution, respectively, in each cycle, as shown in Figure 4. Initially, we conducted scanning of the AgLi surface to establish its baseline image for each cycle, as depicted in Figure 4a–d. This step provided a reference for the surface’s topology prior to the beginning of any electrochemical activity. Following this, the deposition process was initiated by applying a negative current, with the resulting morphology depicted in Figure 4e–h. Finally, a positive current was applied for Li removal during dissolution, and the corresponding morphological changes are depicted in Figure 4i–l. Unlike bare Li, after applying the deposition current in the first cycle, Li+ ions were preferentially deposited in specific regions identified as Ag. This observation is consistent with the results shown in Figure 2, where Ag exhibits a lower surface potential than Li, thereby providing favorable sites for Li+ ions during the Li deposition process. The role of Ag as an electron sink could facilitate additional electrical interactions during Li deposition owing to the attractive forces between the negative charges (i.e., electrons) and the positive charges (i.e., Li+ ions). Upon switching to the dissolution process by applying a positive current, Li previously deposited on Ag was largely, but not completely, removed. Consequently, some Li on the surrounding Li surface dissolved to compensate for this nonremovable portion at the Ag sites. Moreover, the presence of Ag during the Li deposition/dissolution process inhibited the formation of pits on the surface of AgLi, leading to a smaller surface roughness after the deposition/dissolution process. However, Ag appeared to be less active in the later deposition cycles, as demonstrated in the 5th, 10th, and 15th cycles (Figure 4f–h). Consequently, the Li surface becomes more active during deposition and dissolution. Interestingly, during the deposition in these later cycles (Figure 4j–l), even though Li deposition and dissolution mainly occurred on the Li surface, there was significantly less pit formation compared with the bare Li case. This effect can be attributed to the presence of Ag on the surface during the initial cycles, where it initially served as the primary site for Li deposition and dissolution. This early interaction with Ag allowed the formation of a more stable SEI layer with sufficient physical strength to prevent cracking during Li deposition and dissolution while simultaneously facilitating ionic conductivity on the Li surface. This promoted uniform Li deposition and dissolution in subsequent cycles. A similar phenomenon was reported by Peng et al.,24 who observed that treating a Li metal surface with AgTSFI led to Ag serving as a nucleation site for Li deposition. Furthermore, the reaction of Li with F contributed to the formation of a LiF-rich SEI layer, which facilitated the uniformity of the Li deposition and dissolution.

To highlight the changes in surface evolution during Li deposition and dissolution, we employed subtractive AFM imaging. In this technique, the initial image serves as a reference, with subsequent images after deposition or dissolution. The resulting images, depicting the differences across various cycles, are presented in Figure 5a–d, which show the subtractive images of Li deposition on AgLi. In the first cycle (Figure 5a), Li+ is mainly deposited at the Ag sites, suggesting that Ag’s intrinsic property of lowering the surface potential plays a crucial role in Li deposition during this initial stage. This property makes Ag act as an effective electron sink. The activity of Ag in the first cycle contributes to the development of a stable SEI layer. Consequently, in subsequent cycles, Li deposition becomes more uniform across the surrounding Li surface, as evidenced in later images such as Figure 5b (the 5th cycle), where deposition occurs both at the Ag sites and on the Li surface. By the 10th cycle (Figure 5c), deposition mainly occurs on the Li surface, with reduced activity at the Ag sites. By the 15th cycle (Figure 5d), the Ag sites no longer participate significantly in Li deposition, with the process occurring exclusively on the Li surface. This trend toward uniform Li deposition in the later cycles is because all initial depositions occur only at the Ag sites, leading to the formation of a stable SEI layer. The presence of Ag in the early stages plays a critical role in facilitating this uniform deposition pattern. Compared with Li deposition on bare Li, Figure 5e presents the subtractive image of Li deposition on bare Li in the first cycle. The morphological evolution of Li deposition on bare Li was characterized by granular deposition, which later dissolved and formed pits, as previously described. These pits then act as nucleation sites, leading to a more pronounced granular morphology in later cycles (Figure 5f–h) and contributing to an increase in the surface roughness of bare Li. Notably, the nucleation of Li+ on the Li surface was associated with voltage spikes, as observed in the voltage profile (Figure 5j). These spikes are typical characteristics of nucleation barriers.25 By contrast, such voltage spikes are nearly absent for AgLi (Figure 5i), indicating that the presence of Ag can reduce the Li nucleation barrier.23

Conceptually similar to the deposition process, subtracted images of the dissolution process are shown in Figure 6. As previously observed, AgLi exhibited preferential deposition sites. Upon proceeding to dissolution, Li dissolution primarily occurred at the Ag sites, although it was not complete (Figure 6a). This partial dissolution at the Ag sites can be attributed to two factors. The first possibility is the formation of an irreversible phase during Li deposition that does not fully dissolve during the dissolution process.26 The second possibility is a side reaction of the Ag surface with the electrolyte, which may provide an inactive species on the Ag surface. Moving to the later cycles, as shown in Figure 6b–d, Ag predominantly participated in dissolution during the early cycles and then became less active, similar to the deposition process. Notably, despite this reduced activity, Ag contributed to uniform dissolution and inhibited pit formation. By contrast, the subtractive images of the bare Li dissolution (Figure 6e–h) clearly reveal the pit formation. Thus, the presence of Ag on the Li surface not only prevented pit formation during the dissolution process but also promoted uniform Li dissolution. The voltage profile of the bare Li dissolution typically exhibits a voltage spike, as shown in Figure 5i. However, this spike was absent for AgLi (Figure 5j), indicating that the presence of Ag facilitated Li dissolution and reduced the Li stripping barrier. Notably, the voltage response observed in our study using EC-AFM was larger than that of a typical Li/Li symmetric cell configuration. We attribute this larger voltage response primarily to the unique geometry of our experimental setup. Specifically, the significant distance between the working and counter electrodes results in an increased intrinsic resistance within the electrolyte pathway. A similar phenomenon was reported by Sanchez et al.,27 where Li dendrite formation was observed using operando video microscopy in a plan-view cell configuration. In our setup, however, the fixed distance between the working and counter electrodes is an inherent design feature, ensuring consistent experimental conditions throughout the study.

In addition to highlighting the surface evolution, we utilized image subtraction data for numerical integration, employing the Riemann double-sum method,28 as described in eq 1. In this method, Xij and Yij represent the constant X- and Y-axis distances of a pixel, respectively. These constants were determined by using a controlled image size and pixel count. Zij represents the subtractive topographical height of each pixel (i and j are indices). Note that Xij and Yij are constants determined by dividing the total constant distance by the number of pixels in the X and Y directions. Consequently, the area of each pixel, denoted as A, remains constant. The volume calculated using this numerical integration, represented as V, was determined using MATLAB software. The final equation for this calculation is shown in eq 2.

Because the subtractive deposition image was obtained by subtracting the initial image from the deposition image, the volume numerically calculated from this subtractive deposition image represented the volume of the deposition (Vdep). Similarly, the subtractive dissolution image, derived by subtracting the initial image from the dissolution image, allowed us to calculate the remaining volume after dissolution (Vdis). The results of this numerical integration for deposition and dissolution based on the subtractive images are shown in Figure 7a,b. Figure 7a presents the calculated volumes of bare Li, while Figure 7b shows the calculated volume of AgLi. The Vdep values for both bare Li and AgLi are approximately 300 μm3, indicating similar changes in the volume following the application of the deposition current. However, a notable difference was observed after the dissolution. Upon applying the dissolution current, AgLi showed a significantly lower Vdis than bare Li. This observation suggests that the presence of Ag on the Li surface facilitates Li dissolution, leading to a decrease in the amount of inactive Li remaining on the surface.

In addition to representing the surface roughness from the EC-AFM scanning, we employed root-mean-squared (RMS) roughness calculations in MATLAB. The evolution of the RMS values across various cycles, covering the initial, deposition, and dissolution stages for bare Li, is depicted in Figure 7c, while the corresponding data for AgLi are shown in Figure 7d. The results revealed that the RMS roughness of bare Li consistently increased in all cases (initial, deposition, and dissolution stages), indicating nonuniform deposition and dissolution, which is a pattern typically observed in the evolution of bare Li surfaces during the Li deposition and dissolution processes. By contrast, AgLi exhibited a significantly high RMS value in the first cycle of deposition because of the uneven distribution of Ag on the Li surface and the preferential deposition of Li only at the Ag sites. However, the RMS roughness for AgLi deposition drastically decreased with an increasing cycle number, suggesting that the presence of Ag on the Li surface promoted uniform Li deposition.

Even though the RMS values of the AgLi surface for deposition and dissolution appear to be larger than those of the Li surface in cycles 1, 5, 10, and 15, a detailed analysis of the roughness values from the subtractive images, which reflect the net morphological changes after each deposition and dissolution cycle, provides additional insights. As shown in Figure S2, the initial roughness of the AgLi surface is indeed higher. However, the roughness of AgLi decreases significantly over the cycles, indicating a stabilization in the morphology. Specifically, the roughness of AgLi shows a significant decrease from cycle 1 to cycle 15, suggesting that the Ag coating leads to a more uniform and stable deposition and dissolution process over time. In contrast, the roughness of the bare Li surface remains relatively high and fluctuates more throughout the cycles. These findings suggest that although AgLi initially exhibits a higher roughness, it promotes a more consistent and uniform deposition and dissolution process over longer cycling periods. This behavior indicates that AgLi may ultimately lead to a more stable and reliable performance in the Li metal anode.

Conclusions

Our study employing various analytical techniques, such as EC-AFM, KPFM, and SEM/EDS, provided in-depth insights into the morphological and electrical properties of AgLi surfaces compared to those of bare Li surfaces. By employing subtractive AFM imaging and the Riemann double-sum method for numerical integration, we evaluated the dynamic changes in the Li deposition and dissolution as a function of the cycle number. In the initial cycles, AgLi exhibited preferential sites for Li deposition, primarily because of the lower surface potential of Ag compared with that of the Li surface, which acts as an electron sink. This led to the Li+ ion deposition/dissolution being primarily localized at the Ag sites, thereby inhibiting pit formation during the initial cycle. However, as the number of cycles increased, the activity of Ag decreased, and a transition toward a more uniform Li deposition across the Li surface was observed. This evolution suggests that the presence of Ag in the early stages played a critical role in preventing pit formation and in the development of a stable SEI layer, which facilitated uniform deposition and dissolution in subsequent cycles. Moreover, RMS calculations revealed that the bare Li surface consistently exhibited increasing roughness across all stages (initial, deposition, and dissolution), indicating nonuniform deposition and dissolution. By contrast, the AgLi surface showed a high RMS roughness in the first cycle, owing to the uneven Ag distribution and preferential Li deposition at the Ag sites. Remarkably, the RMS roughness of AgLi decreased significantly in the subsequent cycles, indicating the promotion of uniform Li deposition by Ag. In the dissolution process, AgLi also demonstrated an excellent performance, with significantly lower remaining volumes postdissolution compared to bare Li, underscoring the role of Ag in facilitating Li dissolution and reducing inactive Li on the surface. Our findings highlight the beneficial effect of Ag in enhancing the uniformity of Li deposition/dissolution. This contributes significantly to our understanding of the surface interactions in Li metal anodes and opens new avenues for the electrode design and engineering of Li metal systems with improved cycle life and safety.
