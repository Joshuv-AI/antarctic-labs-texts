Mechanism of atrazine metabolism by the microbial Y9 community and related soil–plant remediation effects
Authors: Ming Liu, Tong Yu, Han Gao, Yuling Gao, Yuzhu Zhang, Yongxia Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A bacterial community with atrazine-degrading capability, designated Y9, was enriched from cool-temperate farmland soil having a long-term atrazine (ATZ) application history. Under optimized culture conditions, Y9 degraded 98.62% of 50 mg/L ATZ within 8 h, with a half-life of only 2.02 h. A metabolomic analysis revealed that Y9 metabolizes ATZ to cyanuric acid via the synergistic cooperation of dechlorination-hydrolysis and oxidative dealkylation pathways. In soil remediation experiments, Y9 degraded 70% of 50 mg/kg ATZ within 1 day and achieved complete degradation within 14 days. Pot experiments demonstrated that soil remediated by Y9 alleviated the phytotoxic effects of ATZ on soybean, with plant growth indices and physiological functions recovering to normal levels. This study provides a bacterial community resource and theoretical basis for the bioremediation of ATZ-contaminated soil.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04938-4.

Environmental implications

In soybean-producing regions of Northeast China, atrazine (ATZ) residues from corn-soybean rotation systems threaten subsequent soybean growth and soil health. The degrading Y9 community, obtained in this study, effectively removed ATZ residues in laboratory experiments, thereby reducing the phytotoxicity risk to sensitive crops, such as soybean, and ensuring food safety. This revealed a synergistic degradation mechanism, providing a potential microbial resource and theoretical support for developing novel bioremediation agents. This technology is environmentally friendly and easy to operate, showing potential for rehabilitating pesticide-contaminated farmland such as the black soil region in Northeast China, and promoting sustainable agricultural development.

Introduction

Atrazine (ATZ), a widely used triazine-selective herbicide applied on corn and other crop fields [1], exerts its herbicidal effect by inhibiting photosystem II (PSII) [2]. While effective for weed control, its strong environmental persistence and long half-life (ranging from 13 to 261 days) have raised considerable ecological concerns. In particular, in agricultural areas having long-term application histories, residual ATZ can accumulate in the soil, causing phytotoxicity to subsequent sensitive crops (e.g., soybean) [3, 4] and disrupting soil microbial community structures [5]. Furthermore, it inhibits key elemental cycling functions, thereby compromising soil ecological health and sustainable agricultural production [6]. The black soil region of Northeast China, a vital grain production base [7–9], faces a prominent issue in which ATZ residues within the corn–soybean rotation system exert significant toxic effects on soybean. This has emerged as a critical factor threatening regional agricultural safety and productivity [10].

Microbial remediation is widely recognized as an effective strategy for mitigating pesticide residues due to its environmental friendliness, low cost, and minimal risk of secondary pollution [11–13]. To date, most reported ATZ-degrading microorganisms are single strains, such as Streptomyces sp. and Rhodococcus sp [14]. Although these isolates can degrade ATZ via pathways, including dealkylation, hydrolysis, or ring cleavage [15], they often suffer from limitations, such as suboptimal degradation rates, intermediate metabolite accumulation, and poor environmental adaptability, which collectively hinder their practical application. In contrast, microbial consortia, leveraging interspecific synergism and functional complementation, often demonstrate superior degradation efficiency and enhanced ecological stability levels [16, 17]. Recent studies have highlighted the significant advantages of using mixed cultures in degrading chlorinated organic compounds and pesticides [18]. For instance, a co-culture consortium has achieved an ultra-high degradation rate for sulfamethoxazole [19], and the composite system YM2 effectively facilitates the bioremediation of the pollutant nicosulfuron [20].

However, research on mixed consortia for ATZ degradation is not extensive. Existing studies have predominantly focused on improving the degradation rate; for example, a 3:2 mixture of Klebsiella variicola FH-1 and Arthrobacter sp. NJ-1 achieves a 97.4% degradation of 50 mg/L ATZ under optimized conditions, representing an 11.8% increase over non-optimized conditions [21]. Remediating microbial consortia play dual roles in contaminated soil; they directly degrade pollutants and indirectly promote the functional recovery of the indigenous microbiome by alleviating environmental stress. These processes involve multiple mechanisms, including the reduction of pollutant toxicity, improvement of environmental conditions, and optimization of microbial interactions.

Nonetheless, current ATZ microbial remediation technologies still face several bottlenecks. On the one hand, there is a scarcity of reported efficient microbial resources, particularly consortia capable of adapting to complex field environments for in-situ remediation [22]. On the other hand, available degrading strains often exhibit low ecological adaptability, poor colonization capacity, and unstable functionality in real soil, severely limiting their practical utility [23]. While microbial consortia are theoretically poised to overcome these issues through interspecific cooperation, a systematic understanding of their degradation pathways, ecological effects, and mechanisms in alleviating phytotoxicity to subsequent crops in authentic soil environments is currently lacking [24, 25]. Studies regarding consortium dynamics, functional gene expression, and soil micro-ecological responses remain insufficient, making it difficult to comprehensively evaluate application potential [26, 27]. Therefore, developing composite consortia with high degrading capacities and ecological adaptability levels, and elucidating their degradation mechanisms and soil remediation efficacies, is of great significance for advancing the practice of bioremediating ATZ-contaminated soil [28].

To address the phytotoxicity issue caused by ATZ residues in black soil regions, this study isolated a bacterial community with ATZ-degrading capability, designated Y9, from cornfield soil with a long-term ATZ application history through selective enrichment using ATZ as the sole carbon and nitrogen source. The aim of this study was to elucidate the metabolic mechanisms and functions of this laboratory-enriched degrading community. Using conducting condition optimization, kinetic analyses, and multi-omics investigations, we preliminarily characterized its metabolic pathways. Furthermore, soil remediation and soybean pot experiments were performed to validate the ATZ removal capacity of the Y9 community and its mitigating effects on soybean phytotoxicity. This work provides a potential microbial resource and theoretical support for the bioremediation of ATZ-contaminated soil, and it offers scientific insights to promote the application of microbial communities in agricultural pollution management.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and media

Atrazine powder (purity > 97%) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The ATZ standard solution (100 µg/mL in methanol) was obtained from the National Institute of Metrology (Beijing, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade and supplied by Liaoning Quanrui Reagent Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China).

The minimal salt medium (MSM) contained the following components per liter: 0.2 g of MgSO4·7H2O, 0.007 g of CaCl2, 0.001 g of FeSO4·7H2O, 1.5 g of Na2HPO4·12H2O, and 1.5 g of KH2PO4. The pH of the MSM was adjusted to 7.0 prior to sterilization by autoclaving at 121 ℃ for 20 min. Atrazine-amended MSM (AMSM), used for degradation experiments, was prepared by supplementing the sterile MSM with a specific concentration of filter-sterilized ATZ.

Soil samples were collected in 2023 from a cornfield in the Greater Khingan Mountains area having a long-term history of ATZ application. A five-point sampling method was used, with the five sampling points were randomly selected within the field, and topsoil (0–20 cm) was collected. Soils from the five points were thoroughly mixed, and plant residues were removed.The composite sample was air-dried at room temperature, passed through a 2-mm sieve, packaged in sealed polyethylene bags, and stored at 4 ℃ for subsequent experiments. Soil used in subsequent bioremediation studies was black soil free of ATZ.

The soybean cultivar used in this study was Heihe 43. The original seeds of this cultivar were provided by the Heihe Branch of the Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The seeds actually used in our experiments were subsequently propagated and harvested at the experimental station of Heilongjiang Bayi Agricultural University in Anda City, Heilongjiang Province.

Enrichment of the ATZ-degrading community

To obtain a bacterial community capable of degrading ATZ, a selective enrichment method using ATZ as the sole carbon and nitrogen source was employed. The enrichment process was conducted under conventional bacterial culture conditions. Briefly, 5 g of soil sample was used as the inoculum and transferred into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of AMSM with an initial ATZ concentration of 50 mg/L. The culture was incubated in the dark at 30 ℃ with shaking at 150 rpm. After 7 days, 3 mL of the culture (3% v/v) was transferred to fresh AMSM with the ATZ concentration increased to 100 mg/L. This subculturing was repeated every 7 days, with the ATZ concentration sequentially increased by 50 mg/L per transfer until a final concentration of 200 mg/L was reached. Through this serial passage, a bacterial community having the degradation activity was maintained and stably subcultured in the laboratory. This community, designated as Y9, served as the subject of this study. The subsequent degradation characterization, omics analysis, and remediation application experiments were performed using this community.

Determination of the enriched community’s ATZ degradation ability in liquid medium

The Y9 was pre-cultured in AMSM for 24 h until its OD600 reached 1.5. Then, a 3% (v/v) inoculation amount was transferred to AMSM medium containing 50 mg/L ATZ. Sterile AMSM medium containing the same concentration of ATZ was used as the uninoculated control. All the treatments were incubated in the dark at 30 ℃ with 150 rpm shaking. Samples were taken every 2 h to measure both the absorbance value at 600 nm and the residual ATZ concentration, and to plot the degradation kinetic curve.

Atrazine extraction from liquid medium was performed. Briefly, 2 mL of culture solution was placed into a centrifuge tube, 4 mL of chloroform was then added. The tubes were shaken at room temperature for 20 min and then centrifuged at 8 000 rpm for 8 min. After centrifugation, each upper aqueous phase was carefully discarded, and the organic phase was dried using a nitrogen blowdown instrument. Each residue was redissolved in 2 mL of methanol. Each sample was then passed through a 0.22 μm organic phase filter membrane and transferred to a brown injection vial for UPLC analysis.

The Waters ACQUITY UPLC system was used to quantitatively analyze the ATZ residue in each sample. The UPLC analysis conditions were as follows: ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 chromatographic column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA); column temperature, 30 ℃; mobile phase, methanol–water (70:30, v/v); isocratic elution; flow rate, 0.2 mL/min; injection volume, 0.5 µL; detection wavelength, 220 nm; run time per sample, 1.5 min.

Optimization of degradation conditions using response surface methodology (RSM)

The parameter ranges for RSM optimization were determined through single-factor experiments. While maintaining other factors at standard conditions (pH 7, 30 ℃, 3% inoculum size), the effects of pH (4–10), temperature (10–40 ℃), and inoculum size (1–11%, v/v) on ATZ degradation were investigated separately. All the cultures were incubated at 30 ℃ in the dark with 150 rpm shaking. Based on the degradation curve results from "Determination of the enriched community’s ATZ degradation ability in liquid medium" section, residual ATZ was measured at 10 h. Using these results, pH, temperature, and inoculum size were selected as independent variables A, B, and C, respectively, with ATZ degradation rate as the response Y. A three-factor, three-level Box–Behnken design with 17 experimental runs was generated using Design-Expert 13 software. Residual ATZ was measured at 10 h for each experimental run. Degradation experiments were subsequently conducted under the model-predicted optimal conditions for validation, with sampling every 2 h to determine residual concentration. The results were compared with the degradation efficiency under pre-optimized conditions.

Degradation kinetics with different substrate concentrations

To study the degradation ability of the degrading community at different initial ATZ concentrations, under the optimized culture conditions, samples were taken from each AMSM culture containing 50, 100, 200, and 500 mg/L ATZ every 2, 3, and 12 h to determine the residual ATZ amount.

Analysis of community composition and ATZ metabolism of the Y9 community

To investigate the structural changes in the microbial composition of community Y9 and elucidate the metabolic pathways of ATZ degradation, a sterile control group (CK) and a Y9 group were established under the optimized conditions specified in "Optimization of degradation conditions using Response Surface Methodology (RSM)" section, with three replicates per group. Samples (2 mL) were aseptically collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h, respectively. After collection, samples were immediately stored at -80 ℃. All the frozen samples were transported on dry ice to Hangzhou Kaitai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for analysis. For each time point, the same sample was used for two independent analyses: microbial diversity and non-targeted metabolomics.

Analysis of the bioremediation effect of community Y9 on ATZ-contaminated soil

Non-sterile soil free of ATZ was portioned into 700 g aliquots in culture pots (2 L), and three treatments were established: no ATZ (CK), 50 mg/kg ATZ (AT), and 50 mg/kg ATZ of Y9 bacterial liquid (Y9). After uniformly mixing with the ATZ, the soil was aerated for 36 h to volatilize the methanol introduced during soil contamination. After methanol volatilization, the Y9 group received 6% (42 mL, v/w) of bacterial liquid with an OD600 of 1.5. This inoculation amount was determined by rounding the optimal inoculum size of 5.53% obtained from the response surface optimization in liquid culture (see "Optimization of degradation conditions using response surface methodology (RSM)" section), which aimed to ensure efficient degradation while facilitating practical operation. The other culture pots received an equal amount of sterile water. After thorough content mixing, the pots were incubated in the dark at 25 ℃. Samples were collected every 24 h to determine the residual ATZ amount in the soil. On Days 1, 3, 7, and 14, samples were aseptically collected from each replicate, stored at -80 ℃, and subsequently sent to Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. for a microbial diversity analysis. After 21 days of cultivation, the soil was used for the subsequent soybean phytotoxicity assessment experiment.

Atrazine was extracted from the soil as follows: 5 g of soil sample was weighed and placed into a centrifuge tube. Then, 15 mL of methanol was added and each sample underwent ultrasonic extraction for 2 h. After standing in the dark for 12 h, each sample underwent another 1 h of ultrasonication. The extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 8 min. The supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic phase filter membrane. They were then transferred to brown injection vials, and ATZ was detected in accordance with the method described in "Determination of the enriched community’s ATZ degradation ability in liquid medium" section.

Assessment of the community’s ability to alleviate soybean phytotoxicity

A pot experiment using soybean, variety Heihe 43, was conducted with the treated soils (CK, AT, and Y9) from "Analysis of the bioremediation effect of community Y9 on ATZ-contaminated soil" section to evaluate the mitigating effect of community Y9 on plant toxicity under ATZ stress. Soybean seeds were surface-disinfected by soaking in a 5% NaClO solution for 3 min, then rinsed with clean water until odor-free. This disinfection step was performed to control experimental variables, ensuring that plant physiological responses could be directly attributed to soil treatments by eliminating potential interference from seed-borne microorganisms. The soybean seeds were sown in black plastic pots (9 × 9 cm) and cultivated in a greenhouse set at 25 ℃ with a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod. When the plants reached the V1 stage, indicators of soybean cotyledons and roots were measured. The chlorophyll content was quantified using the ethanol extraction method [29]. Photochemical efficiency was determined using a large-scale photosynthetic fluorescence imaging system. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD), as well as root activity, were measured using appropriate commercial assay kits (SOD: BC5160; POD: BC0090; Root Activity: BC5270; Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.).

Data analysis

All the experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as means ± standard deviations. The statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software. Differences between treatment groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data visualization and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software. The optimization experiments for degradation conditions were designed and analyzed using Design-Expert 13 software.

Results

Degradation characteristics and optimization of the Y9 community

Validation of the degradation efficiency of the Y9 community

To maintain the stability of the degrading Y9, it was subcultured at a 3% inoculation ratio in AMSM containing 200 mg/L ATZ and incubated in the dark at 30 ℃ with shaking at 150 rpm for 7 days. Subsequent experiments were conducted after 50 consecutive subcultures. The degradation efficiency of Y9 for ATZ was verified in MSM having 50 mg/L ATZ as the sole carbon and nitrogen source (Fig. 1). At the beginning of the incubation period, Y9 experienced a brief lag phase of 2 h before entering the exponential growth phase. The cell density (represented by OD600) increased continuously from 0.075 ± 0.002 at 0 h, reaching a peak of 0.121 ± 0.005 at 10 h, after which growth plateaued as the substrate was depleted. This growth trend correlated synergistically with the ATZ degradation rate. The degradation rate increased rapidly from 7.08 ± 1.04% at the initial 2 h to 96.19 ± 0.73% at 10 h. No significant difference (p > 0.05) was shown thereafter compared to the 10-h level. In the CK, the ATZ degradation rate remained below 10% (6.25 ± 1.32%) throughout, and no significant removal was observed. The coupling between microbial growth and ATZ degradation confirmed that ATZ removal was mediated by the metabolic activity of the Y9 community, rather than by abiotic factors.

Optimization and validation of degradation conditions for the Y9 community

To determine the optimal conditions for ATZ degradation by Y9, key environmental factors were first evaluated through single-factor experiments to identify their appropriate ranges. Subsequently, RSM was employed to precisely model and optimize the interactions among these factors. The results indicated that pH, temperature, and inoculation size all significantly influenced the degradation efficiency of the Y9 community (Fig. 2a–c). The community demonstrated a degradation capability across a broad pH range, achieving degradation rates exceeding 90% within pH 5–10. Temperature significantly affected the degradation performance, with the highest degradation rate observed at 30 ℃. Notably slower metabolic activities at both low and high temperatures led to reduced degradation rates. Inoculation size tests revealed that even a low inoculation size of 1% could achieve a 50% degradation rate, while degradation rates reached over 90% when the inoculation size ranged from 3% to 11%.

Fig. 2Single-factor effects on atrazine degradation by the Y9 community. Effects of (a) pH, (b) temperature, and(c) inoculation amount on the atrazine degradation rate

Single-factor effects on atrazine degradation by the Y9 community. Effects of (a) pH, (b) temperature, and(c) inoculation amount on the atrazine degradation rate

Based on the above results, the investigation levels for each factor in the subsequent RSM experiments were determined as follows: pH 4–10, temperature 10–40℃, and inoculation size 1%–11%. Using pH, temperature, and inoculation size as independent variables A, B, and C, respectively, and the degradation rate as the response Y, optimization was performed via a Box–Behnken design (Table S1). An analysis of the results established a highly significant quadratic regression model: Y (%) = -127.067 + 23.9752A + 5.7555B + 12.9248C + 0.237337AB − 0.215223AC + 0.19198BC − 1.91156A2 − 0.155082B2 − 0.91296C2 (p < 0.0001), and the lack-of-fit term was not significant (p = 0.1197 > 0.05), indicating a good model fit. The model’s coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9852) and adjusted coefficient of determination (Adj-R2 = 0.9662) were both high, demonstrating that the model could explain over 96.62% of the response variation and could be used for theoretical predictions and analyses. An analysis of variance showed (Table S2) that the effects of all three factors on the degradation rate reached significant levels (p < 0.05), with the order of influence being: inoculation size > temperature > pH. Furthermore, the quadratic terms (A2, B2, and C2) and the interaction terms AB and BC also had extremely significant effects on the degradation rate (p < 0.01), indicating a complex non-linear relationship between the factors and the response value. Among them, the interaction between temperature and inoculation size, BC, was the most significant, with a steep response surface and elliptical contour lines (Fig. 3a, b), suggesting that synergistic optimization of these two factors could efficiently enhance degradation performance (see Figure S1 for other factor interactions). By solving the model, the theoretically optimal conditions for ATZ degradation by Y9 were predicted as: pH 7.4, temperature 27 ℃, and inoculation size 5.53%, under which the predicted maximum degradation rate was 98.86%. To verify the model’s reliability, validation experiments were conducted under these optimal conditions. The actual degradation rate reached 98.62%, showing excellent agreement with the predicted value (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the degradation rate under optimized conditions was significantly enhanced, with a significant change observed as early as 2 h, and the maximum degradation rate was achieved within 8 h of cultivation, demonstrating higher efficiency compared to the pre-optimization conditions. These results fully confirm the validity and practicality of the model, providing precise process parameters for the further application of the Y9 community.

Degradation kinetics of the Y9 community at different substrate concentrations

To evaluate the application potential of Y9, its degradation capacity for treating different ATZ concentrations ranging from 50 to 500 mg/L was determined under optimized conditions. As shown in Fig. 5a–d, Y9 exhibited a greater degradation efficiency across all tested concentrations, but its degradation rate showed a clear concentration dependence. Atrazine at 50 mg/L and 100 mg/L was rapidly degraded by over 98%, with half-lives of 2.02 h and 4.84 h, respectively. As the initial concentration increased to 200 mg/L and 500 mg/L, the time required to achieve a similar degradation level was correspondingly prolonged, and the half-life gradually extended from 2.02 h to 35.36 h. Despite this, Y9 still achieved a final degradation rate of 84.8% even at the high concentration of 500 mg/L, demonstrating considerable tolerance and degradation capability. The degradation process was fitted using a first-order kinetic model, which showed excellent fitting (R² > 0.97) for all concentrations, indicating that this model satisfactorily describes the degradation pattern of Y9 across different ATZ concentrations (Table S3). The degradation rate constant (k) derived from the fitting equations decreased significantly with increasing initial concentration, dropping from 0.3424 /h at 50 mg/L to 0.0196 /h at 500 mg/L. These results suggest that although high ATZ concentrations might reduce the initial degradation rate of Y9, potentially due to substrate inhibition effects, the community can ultimately achieve efficient ATZ degradation through its robust metabolic activity.

Analysis of community dynamics and ATZ metabolic pathways during degradation

Microbial community structure diversity

An alpha diversity analysis is important for assessing the structural characteristics of microbial communities. To comprehensively evaluate the dynamic changes in the microbial community during the entire ATZ degradation process following the inoculation of Y9, we analyzed the microbial diversity throughout the process. As shown in Table 1, compared to the CK, the introduction of Y9 significantly reshaped the community structure. The species richness of the Y9 group, as assessed by the Chao1 index, was significantly lower than that of the CK group (p < 0.001), with the mean value decreasing to 22.4% of that in the CK group. Additionally, the species diversity of the Y9 group, as assessed by the (Shannon index) was significantly higher than that of the CK group (p < 0.05). These results indicate that Y9 successfully occupied the core ecological niche, effectively suppressed the growth of most background microorganisms in the system, and formed a community having Y9 functional strains at its core. The community was characterized by a uniform species distribution and a compact structure.

To further investigate the specific succession patterns within the community, changes in species composition at the genus level were analyzed to identify the key microorganisms driving the degradation. By screening for genera showing significant changes in relative abundances or having high absolute abundance levels during the degradation process, the dynamics of the following five key groups were focused on: Paenarthrobacter sp., Variovorax sp., Pelomonas sp., Sphingomonas sp., and Methylovorus sp. (Fig. 6). Their relative abundances exhibited significant synergistic succession patterns over time. Paenarthrobacter sp. and Variovorax sp. were the core populations within the community. Specifically, the relative abundance of Paenarthrobacter sp. increased continuously, peaking at 56.4% at 6 h, coinciding with the time when the ATZ degradation rate exceeded 90%. In contrast, Variovorax sp. showed a ‘V’-shaped change pattern, with its abundance decreasing from an initial 52.7% (0 h) to 23.7% at 6 h, followed by a partial recovery. The abundance of Sphingomonas sp. increased steadily and peaked at 6.9% at 6 h, whereas the Methylovorus sp. accumulated continuously throughout the incubation period, reaching its highest level (8.6%) at the endpoint (10 h). Notably, although the initial abundance of Pelomonas sp. was very low, it exhibited nearly a 100-fold explosive growth between 4 and 8 h, demonstrating a strong functional response. The aforementioned community succession suggests that different microorganisms within Y9 play distinct roles. Genera like Pelomonas sp. might be directly involved in the initial degradation of ATZ, Paenarthrobacter sp., as the dominant genus, supports the core of the degradation process, whereas Methylovorus sp. likely utilizes degradation products for growth in the later stages. This synergistic interaction collectively contributes to the highly efficient degradation function of the community.

Y9 metabolite identification and a proposed ATZ degradation pathway

To elucidate Y9’smetabolic ATZ degradation pathway, the degradation process was analyzed using non-targeted metabolomics. Atrazine and the following five key metabolic intermediates [30] were successfully identified: deethylatrazine (DEA), hydroxyatrazine (HA), N-isopropylammelide (N-IpA), ammelide, and cyanuric acid. The dynamic changes of these substances provided direct evidence for deciphering the ATZ degradation pathway of Y9. Signals corresponding to HA, N-IpA, and cyanuric acid were also detected in the CK, with their relative abundances fluctuating to some extent across different time points. These signals may originate from synthesis impurities in the commercial ATZ product, trace non-biological degradation during medium sterilization or storage, or activity from minimal background microorganisms in the system. As shown in Fig. 7a–f, the metabolite profile after inoculating with Y9 differed markedly from the CK group, with flux magnitudes far exceeding those in the CK. First, ATZ (Fig. 7a), as the substrate, was efficiently and rapidly degraded in the Y9 group; its relative abundance plummeted from an initial (3.13 ± 0.14) × 108 to (2.37 ± 0.01) × 105 within 6 h (degradation rate > 99.9%), whereas its concentration remained high in the CK group throughout. Second, concomitant with this consumption was the substantial accumulation of the final degradation product, cyanuric acid (Fig. 7f). As a known terminal product, the abundance of cyanuric acid in the Y9 group peaked as early as 4 h, with a signal intensity significantly higher than in the CK. However, by 10 h, it decreased to a level comparable with the CK group, suggesting the community might possess the potential to degrade cyanuric acid. The key intermediate N-IpA (Fig. 7d) was present at relatively high levels in the Y9 group during the initial degradation phase but was rapidly converted thereafter, leading to a decrease in its abundance in the later stage. This clear consumption trend, contrasting sharply with the high flux state of this compound in the CK group, confirms that it is likely an important intermediate utilized by Y9 in the degradation pathway. The dynamic trends of other intermediates provided supplementary evidence for pathway inferences. Both metabolite HA (Fig. 7c) and DEA (Fig. 7b) showed declining trends, indicating that they might be initial degradation products. In contrast, the signal for ammelide (Fig. 7e), produced via the dealkylation pathway, showed little fluctuation throughout the process, suggesting the dealkylation pathway might be a slower, secondary auxiliary route.

In summary, the metabolomics data indicated that the Y9 community likely employs a dual-pathway synergistic strategy, dominated by the dechlorination-hydrolysis pathway, for degrading ATZ (Fig. 8). The dechlorination-hydrolysis pathway constitutes the following high-flux core metabolic route: ATZ → HA → N-IpA → cyanuric acid. Concurrently, a low-flux oxidative dealkylation pathway, ATZ → DEA → ammelide → cyanuric acid, likely operates as a functional supplement. This demonstrates the diversity and high efficiency of the Y9 degradation mechanism, providing a metabolic pathway basis for understanding its molecular foundation in the sustained degradation of ATZ in complex environments.

KEGG functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of ATZ degradation by Y9, functional potential and metabolic activity analyses were conducted. A prediction analysis based on PICRUSt2 of the 16S data showed that, compared to the CK, inoculation with Y9 significantly enhanced the predicted abundance of the ATZ degradation pathway (Fig. 9). In contrast, there was no significant difference in the nitrogen metabolism pathway between the two groups, and its level remained relatively stable throughout the process, indicating a highly specific metabolic regulation in Y9. The predicted abundances of genes encoding cyanuric acid amidohydrolase, the enzyme responsible for catalyzing the ring cleavage and mineralization of the end product cyanuric acid, was also specifically enriched in the Y9 group (Fig. 10). This suggests that Y9 possesses the potential for the complete mineralization of ATZ. A non-targeted metabolomics analysis confirmed the occurrence of degradation activity at the metabolite level. A KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differential metabolites between the Y9 and CK groups at the endpoint revealed that the ATZ degradation pathway was the only metabolic route that was extremely significantly enriched (Fig. 11). This result mutually corroborates the degradation kinetics and intermediate identification, confirming the efficient and specific degradation capability of Y9 from a functional execution perspective. Notably, although both functional prediction and metabolic activity confirmed the degradation process, significant signals for all known classic ATZ-degrading enzyme genes (e.g., atzA/trzN encoding dechlorinase/hydrolase) were not predicted. Collectively, the multi-omics evidence indicates that Y9 efficiently degrades ATZ via a complete metabolic pathway, which may involve novel degradation genes.

Bioaugmentation remediation of ATZ-contaminated soil

Enhanced removal of ATZ from soil

To evaluate the remediation effect of Y9 in a soil environment, its enhanced removal capability for ATZ in contaminated soil was investigated through a laboratory-scale simulation using artificially contaminated soil. The results, shown in Fig. 12, indicated significant differences in the residual ATZ concentrations among the different treatment groups. No ATZ was detected in the CK throughout the incubation period. The residual concentration in the AT group remained stable, within the range of 43.91–49.16 mg/kg, during the entire incubation period (21 d), with minor fluctuations. This revealed that the natural degradation of ATZ in the soil, without the introduction of exogenous degrading community, was extremely slow and that the residue itself was highly stable. The Y9 treatment group exhibited a highly efficient and rapid degradation capability. On the first day after inoculation, the ATZ concentration in the soil decreased sharply from the initial 50 mg/kg to approximately 15.18 ± 0.48 mg/kg, achieving a removal rate as high as 70%. Thereafter, the degradation process continued, and by Day 7, the residual concentration had dropped to approximately 5.0 mg/kg (90% removal rate). Ultimately, by Day 14, the residual concentration of ATZ in the soil had decreased to undetectable levels and remained stable during the subsequent incubation period (until Day 21). This result directly demonstrates that the exogenous inoculation of the Y9 community can effectively remediate ATZ-contaminated soil.

Soil bacterial community diversity

Atrazine contamination and the remediation by Y9 exerted significant dynamic effects on the alpha diversity of the soil bacterial community (Table 2). Compared to the CK group, ATZ contamination consistently suppressed both the richness and diversity, as assessed by the Chao1 and Shannon indices, respectively, of the soil bacteria. The restorative effect of Y9 became gradually apparent over time. By Day 14 of incubation, the community diversity, as assessed by the Shannon index, in the Y9 group had recovered to a level significantly higher than that of the AT group (p < 0.05), and the richness, as assessed by the Chao1 index, was also numerically higher than that of the AT group. To investigate the overall changes in community structure, a beta diversity analysis was further conducted. A PCoA further illustrated the dynamic response process of the soil microbial community to ATZ stress and Y9 remediation (Fig. 13). In the early stage of remediation (Days 1–3), the community structure of the Y9 treatment group was highly clustered, indicating that the intervention effect of the exogenous community was consistent and significant. In contrast, the AT treatment group showed a dispersed state, suggesting that ATZ stress led to an unstable community structure. By the middle stage of remediation (Day 7), the internal community structure of the Y9 treatment group began to differentiate. At the end of the incubation period (Day 14), the community structures of some Y9 and AT treatment samples showed convergence, potentially due to more complex microbial interactions and ecological succession following the removal of ATZ.

Changes in microbial community composition and a functional prediction analysis

To identify the specific taxonomic units driving the aforementioned diversity changes, the community composition at the phylum level at the remediation endpoint was further analyzed (Day 14). The focus was on the four most abundant phyla (Fig. 14), Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota, that constituted over 90% of the total sequences. The abundance of Proteobacteria was significantly compromised under ATZ stress (p < 0.05), while Y9 remediation restored its level to that of the CK (Fig. 15). This is likely because Y9’s degradation of ATZ reduced the toxic stress, thereby creating favorable ecological niches for indigenous microorganisms having organic pollutant degradation functions. Bacteroidota also exhibited sensitivity to ATZ, with its abundance significantly decreasing in both the AT and Y9 groups (p < 0.05), and it did not recover with Y9 remediation. Furthermore, some low-abundance phyla, such as Chloroflexi and Gemmatimonadota, also showed sensitivity to ATZ stress, with no significant recovery observed after Y9 remediation, further indicating the broad inhibitory effect of ATZ on the soil microbial community.

Soybean plant status in soil remediated by the Y9 community

Plant growth and biomass accumulation

To verify the protective effect of Y9 on subsequent crops, indicators of soybean plant growth in soils from different treatments were analyzed (Fig. 17). Soybean grown in AT soil exhibited significant growth inhibition; their heights (Fig.17a), root lengths (Fig.17a), and plant part biomasses (Fig.17b,c) were significantly lower than those in the CK group (p < 0.05). In the AT group, the fresh and dry weights of the shoots decreased by 36.2% and 34.6%, respectively, compared to the CK group, while the fresh and dry weights of the roots decreased by 42.7% and 40.0%, respectively, and the root length was shortened by 32.1%. Conversely, soybean in the Y9 treatment group, in which ATZ had been pre-efficiently degraded, showed no signs of stress from the early growth stages. None of the growth indicators in the Y9 treatment group showed statistically significant difference from the CK group (p > 0.05). These results demonstrated that Y9, through its soil pre-remediation function, completely eliminated the phytotoxicity risk from ATZ residues, thereby effectively ensuring normal growth, development, and biomass accumulation in soybean.

Discussion

The screening and application of degrading microbial consortia represent a bioremediation strategy for pesticide-contaminated soil. This study successfully enriched and screened a ATZ-degrading microbial community Y9, which demonstrated not only an degradation capability in an aqueous culture but also effectiveness in soil bioremediation and alleviating ATZ phytotoxicity in soybean. Under optimized conditions (pH 7.4, 27 ℃, 5.53% inoculation size), it degraded 98.62% of 50 mg/L ATZ within 8 h, with a half-life of only 2.02 h. This performance is significantly superior to many reported pure cultures; for instance, Citricoccus sp. TT3 required 54 h to degrade 100 mg/L ATZ [31], and Agrobacterium rhizogenes AT13 required 16 h to degrade 50 mg/L ATZ [32]. The high efficiency, coupled with their broad environmental adaptability to pH (4–10) and temperature (10–40 ℃), highlights the advantages of microbial consortia over single strains. This likely stems from functional redundancy, division of labor, and synergistic interactions among member microorganisms, thereby broadening the range of environmental tolerance. As reported by Zhang et al. [33], the degrading DNC5 achieved faster growth and degradation rates than single strains through combined metabolism and nutritional interactions. Furthermore, the degradation rate of Y9 is higher than other mixed cultures; for example, a mixture of FH-1 and NJ-1 achieved 98.23% degradation of 50 mg/L ATZ in 9 days at pH 9 and 30 ℃ [34].

A multi-omics analysis provided a basis for an in-depth understanding of the efficient degradation mechanism of Y9. The rapid degradation process was coordinated by community succession [35]. The explosive growth of Pelomonas in the early stage suggests it may be a primary degrader, initiating the catabolism of ATZ. Subsequently, Paenarthrobacter became the dominant genus, and its abundance dynamics highly coincided with the degradation curve, indicating its core role in the degradation process. The accumulation of Methylovorus in the later stages implies its specialization in utilizing downstream metabolites, such as cyanuric acid. This niche differentiation and temporal succession are crucial for the stable and efficient degradation of pollutants by microbial communities, because they encompass initial degradation and functional reinforcement [36–38]. However, despite the extremely high degradation efficiency, a PICRUSt2 functional prediction failed to detect significant signals for known classic ATZ degradation genes (e.g., atzA, atzB, and trzN) [30, 39]. This discrepancy suggests that Y9 possesses novel degradation genes with low homology to known ones or employs a unique enzyme complex mechanism for initial dechlorination. This speculation provides a theoretical basis for future research utilizing metagenomic and functional genomic techniques to mine novel degradation genes. The conclusions are similar to those drawn by other researchers, suggesting the potential presence of new genes [40, 41].

The metabolomics analysis indicated that Y9 likely employs synergistic dual pathways to achieve ATZ mineralization, with the dechlorination-hydrolysis pathway [42, 43] (ATZ → HA → N-IpA → cyanuric acid) being the dominant, high-flux route, supplemented by a low-flux oxidative dealkylation [44, 45] pathway. This is consistent with pathways reported in other efficient degraders. For instance, in bacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas), the degradation pathway involves dechlorination, dealkylation, and ring cleavage reactions, which produce less toxic intermediates like cyanuric acid, ultimately leading to mineralization [46]. Chen et al. [30] found that a consortium enriched with Bradyrhizobiaceae etc., simultaneously increased the abundance of dechlorination (atzA) and dealkylation (atzB and atzC) genes, promoting dual metabolic pathways and achieving a degradation rate of 85.13%. Furthermore, the significant enrichment of the ATZ degradation pathway in the KEGG analysis and the increased predicted abundance of cyanuric acid amidohydrolase (CAH) [47] suggest that Y9 has the potential for ATZ mineralization, thereby avoiding the accumulation of persistent intermediate products that could pose secondary environmental risks. For example, genomic analyses have shown that over 90% of bacteria (e.g., Herbaspirillum genus) utilize the biuret pathway, in which cyanuric acid is hydrolyzed by CAH, then carboxybiuret is converted to biuret by a decarboxylase, and finally mineralized by biuret hydrolase. This pathway is common in natural environments, and the distribution of bacteria carrying CAH genes is widespread, from soil to wastewater systems, indicating its adaptability in global carbon and nitrogen cycles [46].

During the remediation of contaminated soil, the inoculated microbial Y9 community exhibited notable remediation capability. It removed 70% of the ATZ (50 mg/kg) within 1 day and achieved complete removal within 14 days. In contrast, the ATZ concentration in the control group without Y9 inoculation (AT group) remained at the initial level throughout the incubation period. This result indicates that the rapid removal of ATZ in this experimental system was driven by the inoculation of Y9. Moreover, the natural attenuation capacity of the indigenous soil microbial community during this period was extremely limited, showing no detectable degradation activity. Additionally, functional prediction analysis based on community structure revealed that the introduction of Y9 rapidly increased the predicted gene abundance of the ATZ degradation pathway in soil samples, which primarily originated from the metabolic functions of the Y9 community itself. By rapidly eliminating the pollutant, Y9 fundamentally alleviated the widespread stress exerted by ATZ on soil microorganisms, thereby indirectly mitigating its suppression of key ecological functions such as soil nitrogen metabolism. This drove the overall functional recovery of the soil microbial community toward a healthier state, which is consistent with previous findings that microbial functions can recover after the removal of pollutant stress [48]. Therefore, the degrading Y9 community not only directly removes pollutants but also creates favorable conditions for the functional recovery of the indigenous soil microbiome by eliminating the stress source [49].

Subsequent pot experiments demonstrated that pre-remediation of soil with Y9 completely eliminated the phytotoxicity of ATZ to subsequent soybean crops. This result is consistent with findings from other studies on remediation using mixed microbial communities. For instance, an immobilized consortium (FH-1/NJ-1) shortened the half-life of 20 mg/kg ATZ in soil to 7.96 days and alleviated phytotoxicity in soybean and wheat, increasing plant height by 64.74% and 14.99%, respectively [34]. Similarly, a bio-organic fertilizer containing DNS10 (DNBF10) removed 95.05% of soil ATZ (15.26 mg/kg) within 10 days and promoted soybean growth [50]. In Y9-treated soil, all the measured growth, physiological, and biochemical indicators (plant height, biomass, chlorophyll content, root activity, and Fv/Fm) recovered to levels statistically indistinguishable from the CK group. The current study revealed the organ-specific regulation of the antioxidant system by Y9, providing insights into the underlying mitigation mechanism. In leaves, Y9 restored the elevated SOD and POD activities induced by ATZ stress to normal levels, indicating the effective alleviation of oxidative damage. This is consistent with the findings of Jiang et al. [51], in which co-inoculation of soybean with Arthrobacter sp. DNS10 and Enterobacter sp. P1 alleviated phytotoxicity by accelerating soil ATZ removal and phosphorus uptake, reducing oxidative stress markers, and upregulating photosynthesis and metabolism-related genes. However, a different antioxidant system response was observed in the roots: ATZ stress significantly inhibited root SOD activity while further elevating POD activity. After Y9 remediation, SOD activity recovered, but POD activity remained at a relatively high level even though the pollutant had been removed. This suggests that the intense oxidative attack may have directly compromised the root’s first line of defense (SOD), forcing the plant to upregulate its second line (POD) to scavenge hydrogen peroxide. The restoration of root SOD activity to control levels after Y9 remediation directly confirms the alleviation of ATZ toxicity. Nevertheless, the persistently higher POD activity in the Y9 group, in which the pollutant had been eliminated, may indicate an interaction between the Y9 community and the plant, maintaining a heightened state of ‘alertness’. We hypothesize that Y9, as an exogenous beneficial microbial community, may have actively triggered induced systemic resistance in the plant through its colonization in the rhizosphere. Many plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria can activate the jasmonic acid/ethylene signaling pathway in plants via microbial-associated molecular patterns, leading to a basal upregulation of defense-related genes, including those encoding certain antioxidant enzymes, thereby establishing a systemically enhanced stress-resistant state [52, 53]. It is noteworthy that Y9 is a functional community in which core members, such as Paenarthrobacter sp. and Sphingomonas sp., possess the potential to promote plant growth through pollutant degradation or direct interactions, forming a foundation for beneficial plant-microbe interactions [54–56]. Therefore, we propose that Y9 alleviates soybean phytotoxicity directly by removing the pollutant, and we further speculate that, as a beneficial microbial community, it may induce the soybean root system to maintain a high level of defensive preparedness as a whole. This aligns with the stress-mitigating effects of beneficial microorganisms on soybean reported by other researchers, further confirming the application potential of microbial remediation in agricultural ecosystems [57].

In summary, this study systematically elucidated the metabolic pathways and synergistic mechanisms of ATZ degradation by Y9 under laboratory conditions and validated its potential for remediating contaminated soil and alleviating soybean phytotoxicity through pot experiments. The soil remediation experiments were conducted using non-sterilized soil, which better mimics field conditions and helps evaluate the survival and functional performance of Y9 in realistic microbial contexts. However, there remains a gap between controlled laboratory conditions and the complexity of field environments. The long-term colonization stability, degradation performance of Y9 in field soil, and its competitive interactions with complex indigenous microbial communities require further validation through field trials. Additionally, this study focused on the degradation function of the bacterial community and cannot exclude the potential auxiliary role of fungi in the original soil or during the degradation process. Therefore, future research should employ fungal community sequencing or targeted isolation and cultivation to further investigate the potential auxiliary functions of fungi in the degradation process, thereby comprehensively revealing the microbial network of ATZ degradation in soil. These efforts will provide critical support for the practical application of Y9 in agricultural environments and facilitate its translation into field-ready remediation technologies.

Conclusion

This study successfully enriched a microbial community having an ATZ-degrading capability, designated Y9, from agricultural soil with a long-term ATZ application history. In AMSM under optimized conditions (pH 7.4, 27 ℃, and 5.53% inoculation size), Y9 achieved a remarkable degradation rate of 98.62% for 50 mg/L ATZ within 8 h, with a half-life of only 2.02 h, and demonstrated broad adaptability to environmental conditions and substrate concentrations. A multi-omics analysis revealed that Y9, through ecological niche occupation, reshaped the community into an ecological structure in which functional genera, like Pelomonas, Paenarthrobacter, and Methylovorus, acted synergistically. Efficient degradation was accomplished via coordinated microbial succession and a dual-pathway metabolic strategy dominated by dechlorination-hydrolysis. Importantly, the functional prediction did not detect known classic degradation genes (e.g., atzA and trzN), suggesting the potential existence of novel genetic degradation mechanisms. Soil pot experiments confirmed that Y9 rapidly removed 70% of 50 mg/kg ATZ from soil within 1 day and achieved complete removal within 14 days. By eliminating the source of phytotoxicity, Y9 effectively alleviated the herbicide damage to subsequent soybean crops, restoring their various physiological indicators to normal levels. This study provides a valuable microbial resource for the bioremediation of ATZ-contaminated soil, and the revealed degradation mechanisms hold both theoretical significance and application potential.
