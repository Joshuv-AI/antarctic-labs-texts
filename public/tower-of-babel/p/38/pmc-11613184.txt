A Comparison of the 2022 Versus 2011 National Institute of Child Health and Human Development Web-Based Risk Estimator for Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia
Authors: Mitchell Kinkor, Jake Schneider, Farhath Sulthana, Janelle Noel-Macdonnell, Alain Cuna
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the predictive accuracy of the 2022 vs 2011 National Institute of Child Health and Human Development estimator for identifying infants at high risk for bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD).MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective study of infants ≤28 weeks' gestation. Demographic and respiratory support data were used to calculate risk for BPD or death by the BPD estimators. Our outcomes of interest included (1) treatment with systemic steroids for BPD and (2) composite outcome of death or highest severity of BPD at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age. We used c-statistics and area under receiver operator characteristic curves (AUC) to compare accuracy of the BPD estimators.ResultsA total of 159 infants (mean gestational age, 26 weeks; mean birth weight, 837 g) were included. Steroid treatment for BPD occurred in 61 infants. We found that predictive accuracy was similar for both 2022 and 2011 BPD estimators, with c-statistics ranging from 0.77 to 0.89, indicating good-to-excellent accuracy for predicting steroid treatment. Twenty-one infants had death or grade 3 BPD based on definitions used in the 2022 estimator, and 68 infants had death or severe BPD based on definitions used in the 2011 estimator. Overall, we found both BPD estimators had poor AUC for predicting infants at high risk for death or highest severity of BPD, with the exception of the 2022 estimator, which had an acceptable AUC of 0.773 on postnatal day of life 28.ConclusionsThe 2022 and 2011 BPD estimators both demonstrate similar good-to-excellent accuracy for identifying infants at high risk for steroid treatment, but poor-to-fair accuracy for predicting death or highest severity of BPD at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age.Keywords: prematurity, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, postnatal corticosteroids, risk

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a type of chronic lung disease that afflicts many infants born preterm.1 Although the diagnosis of BPD is not made until 36 weeks’ corrected gestation, early prediction of the infants who will develop BPD and who will not is of value. In 2011, the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) developed a web-based estimator that predicts the risk of BPD (no, mild, moderate, or severe) or the competing outcome of death.2 Beside providing prognostic information to families and care teams, this tool has also been used in clinical research to assist with characterizing risk factors for BPD and estimating treatment effect of interventions for BPD in randomized controlled trials.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Additionally, our team demonstrated that the 2011 BPD estimator could predict the use of systemic steroid treatment in high-risk preterm infants.8 We also demonstrated that the 2011 BPD estimator can be used in clinical practice to objectively stratify infants at highest risk for BPD who would likely benefit from steroid treatment.9

In 2022, the NICHD updated the BPD estimator to reflect changes in the respiratory care for premature infants as well as changes in how BPD is defined.10,11 Similar to the original tool, the 2022 tool estimates the premature infant's probability of BPD severity or death based on risk factors present on prespecified time points in the first 28 days of life (DOLs). New to the 2022 BPD estimator is the inclusion of antenatal steroid treatment for predictions on DOL 1 and surgical necrotizing enterocolitis for predictions on DOLs 14 and 28. Additionally, noninvasive positive pressure ventilation was added as a form of respiratory support in the 2022 edition, and output for DOL 21 was excluded. The impact of these changes to the updated BPD estimator on its ability to identify infants at high risk for BPD remains unknown.

In this study, we compared the predictive performance of the 2022 BPD estimator with the 2011 BPD estimator in identifying high-risk infants who were treated with postnatal steroids for BPD, and who developed the combined outcome of death or highest severity of BPD at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age. We hypothesized that the revised 2022 NICHD BPD estimator would offer improved accuracy for predicting both postnatal steroid treatment and severe outcomes when compared with its 2011 counterpart.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study included preterm infants admitted to the regional level IV neonatal intensive care unit at Children's Mercy Kansas City in Kansas City, Missouri, between January 2010 and December 2016. The data for this study were provided by the institutional review board-approved data repository at the Center for Infant Pulmonary Disorders at Children's Mercy. The same cohort of infants used in our prior study was used for this study comparing the 2022 BPD estimator with the 2011 BPD estimator.

Study Population

We included very preterm infants with sufficient data to calculate BPD risk using the NICHD BPD estimators for 2011 and 2022. This cohort included infants with a gestational age between 23 and 28 weeks and birth weight between 501 and 1250 g. Infants were also required to have respiratory support data on ≥1 of the time points evaluated by the estimator (postnatal day 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, or 28). Infants with congenital heart disease or multiple congenital anomalies as well as infants with incomplete data were excluded.

Use of Steroids During the Study Period

During the study period, no formal guidelines existed for identifying which infants were high risk for BPD to be considered eligible for steroid treatment. Such decisions were made by the treating neonatologist based on individualized risk-benefit assessment. This determination was largely subjective and typically based on the infant's clinical course, such as preterm infants who remain intubated beyond 2-4 weeks of life, with variability in the threshold of ventilatory support and oxygen need to consider treatment. Incorporation of the NICHD BPD risk estimators into consensus guidelines for clinical decision-making regarding steroid treatment was not introduced in our unit until 2018.

Data Collection

We collected demographic and respiratory support data to calculate risk estimates for BPD or death using the 2011 and 2022 NICHD BPD estimators. Demographic data collected included gestational age, birth weight, race, sex, and maternal antenatal steroid exposure. Respiratory support data collected included the average oxygen levels and the highest ventilator support corresponding with each day of interest in the BPD estimator. We also collected data on surgical necrotizing enterocolitis, as this variable was included for DOLs 14 and 28 calculation in the 2022 BPD estimator. Infants with criteria outside the range of the BPD estimator (such as gestational age or birth weight) were excluded. Infants with unavailable BPD risk estimates from earlier time points owing to a later admission were still included provided that ≥1 time point was available to estimate BPD risk. Infants who received steroid treatment within the time points of the BPD estimators were also included in the analysis. Demographic and respiratory support data were then inputted to the BPD risk calculators to obtain risk estimates for death, no BPD, or BPD of varying severity at different postnatal days.

Outcome Variable

We compared the ability of 2011 and 2022 NICHD BPD outcome estimator in predicting 2 outcome variables of interest. The first outcome variable was treatment with postnatal steroids for BPD on DOLs 1, 3, 7, 14, and 28. DOL 21 was also investigated for the 2011 estimator. The second outcome variable was a composite outcome of death or highest severity of BPD at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age. The highest severity of BPD was defined based on the definition of BPD used in each version of the BPD estimator. For the 2011 BPD estimator, the highest severity of BPD, termed severe BPD, was defined as the need for any positive pressure ventilation and/or oxygen level of ≥30% at 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age. For the 2022 BPD estimator, highest severity of BPD, termed grade 3 BPD, was defined as the need for invasive positive-pressure ventilation regardless of oxygen level.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic data collected were summarized descriptively. To determine performance in identifying infants treated with postnatal steroids, we first performed univariate logistic regression at each time point for each risk estimate output of the 2011 and 2022 estimator. We then used stepwise multiple logistic regression analysis to select risk estimates from the BPD estimator with predictive effects and remove risk estimates without predictive effects. The c-statistic was used to compare overall predictive accuracy of the various predictive models between the 2011 and 2022 estimators. To determine performance in predictive severe outcomes, we first calculated the predicted risk for death or severe BPD/grade 3 BPD for each of the different time points in the 2011 and 2022 BPD estimators. We then performed receiver operator characteristic (ROC) analysis and compared the area under ROC (AUC) to compare the accuracy of the 2011 and 2022 BPD estimators for distinguishing infants with and without death or severe BPD/grade 3 BPD. Statistical analysis was performed with SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Sixty-one preterm infants (approximately 38%) were treated with postnatal steroids for BPD at any time during their hospital stay. To compare the ability of each calculator for predicting postnatal steroid treatment, we first used univariate logistic regression to identify the variable with the highest C-statistic for each time point (Table III). Overall, C statistics were similar between the 2 calculators, with a median absolute difference of 0.009 (range, 0-0.037). We then used stepwise logistic regression analysis to identify the best candidate model for predicting steroid treatment (Table IV). Overall, the models for 2022 were simpler to use because for each time point, the best model reflects the original output of the calculator, with the exception of DOL 28 time point, which combines grade 2 or grade 3 BPD. In comparison, 3 of the 6 models for the 2011 calculator comprised composite outcomes that require combining 2 outputs of the calculator as variables selected for by stepwise regression. Differences in C statistic were again modest, with a median difference of 0.016 (range, 0.003-0.048).

To compare the ability of each calculator for predicting severe outcomes, we first dichotomized infants into 2 categories. High-risk outcomes comprised the composite of death or highest severity of BPD (severe BPD for 2011 calculator, grade 3 BPD for 2022 calculator). Low-risk outcome comprised the following: no BPD + mild BPD/grade 1 BPD + moderate BPD/grade 2 BPD. We then used ROC analysis to determine the accuracy of the BPD estimators in distinguishing infants with high-risk outcome and those with low-risk outcome. The incidence of highest severity of BPD or death was 43% (68/159) when using the old BPD definition from the 2011 BPD estimator and 13% (21/159) when using the new BPD definition from the 2022 BPD estimator. Overall, we found that the 2022 estimator had higher AUC values in identifying high-risk outcome compared with the 2011 estimator (Table V). For both BPD estimators, accuracy improved with increasing postnatal age. By DOL 28, the 2022 BPD estimator had an acceptable AUC of 0.773 (95% CI, 0.658-0.888), whereas the 2011 BPD estimator had a fair AUC value of 0.690 (95% CI, 0.608-0.773).

Discussion

In this study, we compared the predictive performance of the 2022 and 2011 BPD estimators developed by the NICHD Neonatal Research Network. Overall, we found that both versions of the BPD estimators were comparable in identifying high-risk infants for postnatal steroid treatment, with reliable prediction achieved as early as DOL 14. In contrast, both BPD estimators demonstrated poor predictive power for identifying the combined outcome of death or highest severity of BPD at all time points, except for the 2022 BPD estimator, which offered fair prediction at DOL 28.

Several BPD prediction models have been developed over the years. In a meta-analysis that included 65 studies, Romijn et al12 demonstrated that, although many of these prediction models performed adequately, every model was also at high risk of bias owing to small sample size, mishandling of missing data, lack of internal or external validity, and absence of calibration measures. In another study, Kwok et al13 conducted a meta-analysis of 64 BPD prediction model studies followed by external validation using data from >62 000 preterm infants included in the UK National Neonatal Research Database. Of the various BPD prediction models evaluated, the authors identified the 2011 BPD estimator as the most promising for predicting BPD and death. However, because the authors conducted their search of the literature in August 2021, the 2022 BPD estimator was not included in their analysis. To help bridge this gap, our study sought to compare the performance of the updated 2022 BPD estimator with the original 2011 version.

We previously demonstrated in our single-center study that the 2011 BPD estimator could be used to identify preterm infants at high risk for BPD who had eventual treatment with postnatal steroids.8 Based on updates to the 2022 BPD estimator (larger sample size, more contemporary cohort, use of evidence-based BPD definition), we hypothesized that this updated version would offer improved accuracy in predicting postnatal steroid treatment. Surprisingly, using the same cohort of infants as our prior study, we found that the predictive performance of 2022 BPD estimator was essentially the same as the 2011 BPD estimator for predicting eventual steroid treatment. One potential benefit of the 2022 BPD estimator is that the most reliable models for predicting steroid treatment, as identified by stepwise multiple logistic regression, had cutoff thresholds based on a single variable (eg, ≤11.3% risk of no BPD on DOL 14), whereas models based on the 2011 BPD estimator required combining 2 variables to generate a similar C statistic (eg, >37% risk of severe BPD or death on DOL 14).

A major change with the updated 2022 BPD estimator was the lack of BPD risk prediction at DOL 21. The impact of this exclusion on the 2022 BPD estimator's performance for identifying infants for early treatment with postnatal steroids is unknown. Our findings demonstrate that both estimators achieved reliable prediction for identifying eventual need for steroid treatment as early as DOL14, with no additional increase in performance when compared with later time points, suggesting that the exclusion of DOL 21 output would not hamper the usefulness of the 2022 BPD estimator for identifying high-risk infants for steroid treatment.

One potential impact of using the NICHD BPD estimator for assessing risk for BPD is that it may facilitate earlier treatment with steroids. Based on our findings, the NICHD BPD estimators provide early and reliable prediction of high-risk infants for steroid treatment by DOL 14, and that waiting for DOL 21 or DOL 28 does not provide any additional benefit. Initiating treatment around DOL 14 coincides with the moderate early initiation of postnatal steroids (between 8 and 14 days) recommended in the network meta-analysis by Ramaswamy et al14 as the most beneficial approach for decreasing BPD or death. Other studies have also demonstrated that earlier initiation of postnatal steroids, ranging between 8 and 21 days, was associated with decreased BPD compared with later initiation of treatment.15, 16, 17 These studies, together with our results, suggest that initiating steroid treatment based on DOL14 output from the BPD estimator would be acceptable, and may also be beneficial to decrease BPD compared with later treatment.

In contrast with our findings, Srivatsa et al18 found that the 2022 BPD estimator did not perform as well in predicting steroid treatment (73% sensitivity and 78% specificity in their study, compared with 80% sensitivity and 80% specificity in our study). One possible explanation for this difference is that their study used a BPD risk threshold based on Doyle et al19 for determining the 2022 BPD estimator's prediction for identifying steroid treatment. In our study, we did not have a prespecified threshold value of BPD risk, but rather used ROC analysis to determine the optimal cut-off risk value that offered the best combination of sensitivity and specificity. Another potential explanation is the disconnect between how BPD was defined in the studies that comprised Doyle et al’s meta-regression analysis (based on the 2001 National Institutes of Health consensus definition20) and how it was defined in the 2022 BPD estimator (based on the 2019 Neonatal Research Network definition10).

Another potential use of the BPD estimator is to identify patients at high risk for poor outcomes for family counseling and for recruitment to focused research protocols. Although the 2022 BPD estimator did have better performance than the 2011 BPD estimator at all time points, both BPD estimators overall had poor AUC for predicting infants at high risk for death or highest severity of BPD. Performance was particularly poor at early time points, with gradual improvement over time. Only by DOL 28 did the 2022 BPD estimator achieve an AUC of >0.75, whereas the 2011 BPD estimator did not reach that threshold. Ideally, earlier prediction is preferred to allow for timely and efficient recruitment into clinical trials that evaluate interventions to prevent or alter the course of BPD. However, the later predictions using the BPD estimator could still be valuable for family counseling, particularly the 2022 BPD estimator on DOL 28.

Distinct from our results, Baker and Davis21 described a good AUC of 0.81-0.84 for the 2011 BPD predictor in identifying infants at highest risk for severe BPD/death.10 Our study's different results potentially could be attributed to differences in baseline demographics and care practices that could impact how well the BPD estimators perform. Baker and Davis's study was a dual site study conducted in Australia, had more Asian representation, and had higher use of antenatal corticosteroids (91%), while our study was conducted in the United States, had more Black and Hispanic representation, and had lower antenatal steroid use (75%).

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to offer a direct comparison between the 2011 BPD estimator and its updated 2022 version. Using the same cohort of infants to assess both estimators increases the validity of our results, and ROC analysis to determine the optimum risk threshold instead of prespecified threshold values decreases bias in our study. One limitation of our study is that we did not account for the timing of steroid treatment initiation relative to the time points of the BPD estimator. As such, it is possible that some of the risk estimates from the BPD estimator used for modeling may have been influenced by preceding administration of steroids. However, most infants in our cohort did not start steroid treatment until after DOL 28 (mean age of steroid initiation was DOL 33 [IQR, 26-38 days]), suggesting minimal impact on overall analysis. Our study is also limited by the single-center study design and small sample size, which decrease generalizability.

In conclusion, we found that the 2022 BPD estimator demonstrates similar good-to-excellent accuracy compared with its 2011 predecessor for identifying infants at high risk for steroid treatment as early as DOL 14. In contrast, both the 2022 and 2011 BPD estimator performed poorly at all time points for predicting death or highest severity of BPD, except for the 2022 BPD estimator, which had acceptable accuracy on DOL 28. These findings suggest that the 2022 BPD estimator can be used to help clinicians identify high-risk infants for targeted postnatal steroid treatment, and may be of value for family counseling, especially at DOL 28. The use of BPD estimators for identifying high-risk infants for potential recruitment into clinical trials needs further study.
