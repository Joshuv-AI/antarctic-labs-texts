Superb Microvascular Imaging–Enhanced Lymphatic Ultrasound for Preoperative Mapping in Lymphedema-related Lymphaticovenular Anastomosis
Authors: Makoto Mihara, Hisako Hara, Yohei Iwanaga, Hiroki Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Accurate preoperative identification of functional lymphatic vessels and veins is essential for successful lymphaticovenular anastomosis (LVA). Conventional Doppler-based ultrasonography (D-CUPS [Doppler, cross, uncollapsible, parallel, superficial fascia]) may produce motion-related artifacts that complicate lymphatic–venous discrimination. This study compares examination time and examiner burden between a superb microvascular imaging–based method (S-CUPS) and D-CUPS for preoperative LVA mapping.Methods:Twenty patients with primary or secondary lymphedema underwent both D-CUPS and S-CUPS in randomized order before LVA. In 36 limbs, 88 predefined scanning sites were evaluated. Per-site examination time, vessel depth and diameter, intraoperative NECST classification (normal, ectasis, contraction, sclerosis), and examiner fatigue using a 1–10 visual analog scale (VAS) were recorded. Linear mixed-effects models accounted for clustering of multiple sites within patients, and Spearman correlation analyses were performed for continuous variables. All ultrasound-identified veins and lymphatic vessels were confirmed intraoperatively.Results:S-CUPS shortened per-site examination time compared with D-CUPS (estimated marginal means, 51.7 versus 75.0 s; P = 0.0193). Mental VAS scores were lower with S-CUPS (1.24 ± 0.64 versus 1.86 ± 0.69; P < 0.001), whereas physical VAS scores did not differ significantly (P = 0.19). Larger lymphatic and venous diameters correlated negatively with examination time, and larger lymphatic diameter was associated with lower physical fatigue. BMI correlated positively with lymphatic depth but not with examination time or VAS scores. Ectasis-type lymphatic vessels predominated intraoperatively (79.5%).Conclusions:S-CUPS was associated with shorter per-site examination time and lower examiner-reported mental fatigue than D-CUPS in preoperative mapping for lymphedema-related LVA, although the modest magnitude of these differences warrants further validation in clinical practice.

Takeaways

Question: Can superb microvascular imaging–enhanced ultrasound improve preoperative mapping for lymphaticovenular anastomosis compared with conventional Doppler ultrasound?

Findings: In 20 patients (36 limbs, 88 sites), S-CUPS shortened per-site examination time and lowered examiner mental fatigue while maintaining intraoperative concordance of identified lymphatic vessels and veins. Vessel diameter, not body mass index or vessel depth, was associated with scanning difficulty.

Meaning: S-CUPS may improve efficiency and workflow for preoperative lymphatic mapping in lymphaticovenular anastomosis, although the absolute benefit was modest and should be confirmed in larger, multioperator studies.

INTRODUCTION

Lymphedema is a chronic condition that frequently develops after cancer treatment and markedly impairs patients’ quality of life.1,2 Lymphaticovenular anastomosis (LVA) is a minimally invasive microsurgical procedure that bypasses lymphatic fluid into the venous system and has been established as an effective treatment, especially for early-stage lymphedema.3,4 The success of LVA depends critically on the accurate preoperative identification of functional lymphatic vessels and suitable recipient veins.5

Superb microvascular imaging (SMI) is an advanced Doppler-based imaging mode designed to visualize low-velocity microvascular flow while suppressing motion-related clutter artifacts. Unlike conventional color Doppler ultrasound, which relies on wall filtering and may eliminate low-flow signals together with noise, SMI uses adaptive clutter suppression algorithms that separate true blood flow signals from tissue motion artifacts. By preserving low-velocity flow components and reducing background noise, SMI may enable clearer visualization of small-caliber vessels without contrast enhancement. In clinical practice, SMI is implemented as an imaging mode integrated into compatible ultrasound platforms rather than as an external device. In the present study, SMI was used as a functional imaging option within the ultrasound system to enhance the conventional CUPS workflow. In this study, we define S-CUPS as a lymphatic ultrasound protocol combining conventional ultrasound evaluation with the SMI mode to optimize vessel detection and mapping.

Conventionally, lymphoscintigraphy and indocyanine green lymphography have been the primary imaging modalities used before LVA.6–8 However, these methods visualize only superficial lymphatic flow and provide limited information on the deeper lymphatic system. In contrast, ultrasound examination offers a noninvasive and repeatable approach that enables real-time visualization of both lymphatic vessels and veins, making it a useful tool for preoperative evaluation.9–12

Our group previously developed the D-CUPS method (Doppler, cross, uncollapsible, parallel, superficial fascia), a Doppler-based technique that differentiates lymphatic vessels from veins using 5 ultrasonographic features.13 Although D-CUPS has proven to be clinically useful, conventional Doppler imaging often displays flow signals from the dermis and superficial fascia, generating artifacts that may complicate discrimination between veins and lymphatic vessels in some cases.

To address this limitation, we introduced the S-CUPS method, which incorporates SMI technology. SMI selectively enhances blood flow signals while suppressing motion-related artifacts from nonvascular structures such as the dermis or fascia.14,15 Consequently, S-CUPS may facilitate lymphatic–venous differentiation by reducing background noise during vessel assessment.

In addition, the NECST classification (normal, ectasis, contraction, sclerosis) is used to assess the morphological changes of lymphatic vessels.16 Among these, Ectasis-type (E-type) lymphatic vessels, which remain dilated and functional, are considered favorable for LVA and have been associated with better surgical outcomes. Therefore, identifying the proportion of E-type lymphatic vessels before surgery may have clinical relevance.

The primary objective of this study was to compare S-CUPS and D-CUPS in terms of per-site examination time and examiner-reported physical and mental burden (VAS). Secondary analyses explored anatomical factors associated with examination difficulty and the concordance between preoperative ultrasound findings and intraoperative observations. Rather than presuming superiority, we sought to evaluate whether incorporation of SMI was associated with measurable differences in efficiency and examiner workload under routine clinical conditions.

This was a retrospective study based on a prospectively maintained cohort. All examinations were performed by the same experienced lymphatic surgeon at Lymphedema Clinic Tokyo (Tokyo, Japan). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Aichi Medical University (approval no. 2024-303). Data collection was standardized and prospectively recorded in a predefined database before analysis.

To avoid order effects, the sequence of examinations (S-CUPS first versus D-CUPS first) was randomized 1:1. All scans were performed by a single lymphatic surgeon (M.M.) with experience exceeding 3000 LVA procedures and 1000 lymphatic ultrasound examinations.

All examinations were performed using a high-frequency linear probe (Aplio i800, Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 24–33 MHz. Examination time was recorded per predefined scanning site in seconds and defined as the interval from probe-to-skin contact to the examiner’s confirmation of lymphatic–venous differentiation at that site.

For S-CUPS examinations, the SMI mode was activated during vessel assessment to enhance visualization of low-flow lymphatic structures and adjacent venules. Imaging parameters—including gain, depth, focus position, and dynamic range—were adjusted at each predefined scanning site to optimize vessel visualization in accordance with routine clinical practice. Both S-CUPS and D-CUPS examinations were performed under these optimized conditions.

Vessel depth was measured on B-mode images as the distance from the skin surface to the center of the lymphatic or venous lumen. Both lymphatic and venous diameters were measured ultrasonographically as external diameters. During LVA, the presence of lymphatic vessels and subcutaneous veins at the same anatomical sites corresponding to the preoperative ultrasound findings was directly confirmed under the operating microscope.

Immediately after each scan, the examiner rated mental and physical fatigue using a visual analog scale (VAS) from 1 (none) to 10 (extreme). These scores reflected examiner—not patient—subjective burden and were used to compare the ergonomic load of S-CUPS and D-CUPS.

During LVA, exposed lymphatic vessels were classified macroscopically under high magnification according to the NECST system. Associations between preoperative ultrasonographic findings (method, depth, ultrasonographically measured diameter) and intraoperative NECST classification were evaluated.

Continuous variables (body mass index [BMI], examination time, VAS scores, vessel depth, vessel diameter) were analyzed retrospectively. To compare S-CUPS and D-CUPS, we applied a linear mixed-effects model to account for hierarchical dependency due to multiple sites nested within the same patient. Patient ID and limb/site were included as nested random effects. Unadjusted models included imaging method as a fixed effect, whereas adjusted models further incorporated BMI as a covariate to control for patient body habitus. Estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals were derived from the linear mixed-effects model.

Correlations between continuous variables (BMI, vessel depth/diameter, VAS scores, examination time) were assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients. Categorical variables (International Society of Lymphology [ISL] stage, lymphoscintigraphy type, NECST classification) were compared with the Kruskal–Wallis test. A 2-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Python v3.11 and R version 4.2.3. Because this was an exploratory study, no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons.

RESULTS

A total of 20 patients were included, yielding 36 limbs and 88 predefined scanning sites. Ultrasonographic examinations using both S-CUPS and D-CUPS were successfully completed at all sites (Table 1). At every scanning site, the subcutaneous veins and collecting lymphatic vessels visualized preoperatively were directly identified intraoperatively at the same anatomical locations under microscopic observation.

Linear mixed-effects modeling demonstrated that examination time per site in seconds was significantly shorter with S-CUPS than with D-CUPS in the BMI-adjusted model (estimated marginal means: 51.7 versus 75.0 s; P = 0.0193) (Table 2). Although the absolute time difference per site was modest, it reached statistical significance. Because multiple predefined sites were evaluated per limb, cumulative examination time per extremity may increase depending on the number of sites assessed. Mental VAS scores were significantly lower with S-CUPS (1.24 ± 0.64) than with D-CUPS (1.86 ± 0.69; P < 0.001), whereas physical VAS scores did not differ significantly between the 2 methods (P = 0.19) (Fig. 2).

Representative Cases

Two representative cases are presented to illustrate the differences in visualization between S-CUPS and D-CUPS and the concordance between preoperative ultrasonographic findings and intraoperative anatomy.

Case 1

Case 1 involved lower limb lymphedema in a 65-year-old woman with bilateral lower limb lymphedema following surgery for endometrial cancer. Scanning of the lateral lower leg (ISL stage II) revealed that D-CUPS produced motion-related artifacts within the superficial fascia, making discrimination between the collecting lymphatic vessel and adjacent subcutaneous vein difficult.

Using S-CUPS, the subcutaneous vein was clearly visualized as a flow-positive structure, whereas the collecting lymphatic vessel appeared as a non-enhancing tubular structure (Fig. 4). (See Video 1 [online], which displays B-mode, D-CUPS, and S-CUPS imaging in case 1 at the lateral lower leg.) The venous diameter measured 0.8 mm at a depth of 7.3 mm, and the lymphatic vessel measured 0.7 mm in diameter at a depth of 7.8 mm. During LVA, both vessels were directly identified at the same anatomical site, and the lymphatic vessel was classified as E-type according to the NECST classification (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the performance of SMI-based lymphatic ultrasound (S-CUPS),14,15 compared with the conventional Doppler-based method (D-CUPS),9–13 and examined anatomical factors associated with examination difficulty and examiner burden. Across 88 scanning sites, all ultrasonographic findings—including vessel presence, depth, and diameter—were confirmed intraoperatively at the corresponding anatomical locations. This complete concordance indicates that both S-CUPS and D-CUPS accurately delineated true lymphatic vessels and subcutaneous veins. However, the 2 methods differed in examination efficiency and examiner-reported mental fatigue.

Although VAS scores were statistically lower with S-CUPS, the absolute difference was modest within the 1–10 scale, and the baseline fatigue levels were already low in this study population. Therefore, the clinical significance of the observed VAS difference should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, even small reductions in examiner burden and per-site examination time may accumulate in high-volume clinical practice where multiple scanning sites are evaluated per limb. Future multioperator and multicenter studies will be important to determine whether these differences translate into meaningful improvements in workflow efficiency and operator endurance.

Because the imaging order was randomized and BMI-adjusted linear mixed-effects modeling was applied, the observed differences are unlikely to be solely explained by patient-related confounding factors. Rather, they may be attributable to the intrinsic imaging characteristics of SMI. SMI suppresses motion artifacts arising from the dermis and superficial fascia while selectively enhancing low-velocity blood flow signals in small veins and depicting lymphatic vessels as nonflow structures.14,15 This capability may reduce ambiguity during lymphatic–venous differentiation and facilitate identification of the target vessels.2–4

Correlation analysis provided additional insight into factors influencing scanning difficulty. Vessel diameter showed significant negative correlations with examination time, and lymphatic diameter was also negatively correlated with physical fatigue (Table 3). These findings indicate that larger caliber vessels, including veins and collecting lymphatic vessels, are easier to detect ultrasonographically and require less scanning effort. In contrast, vessel depth did not correlate with examiner burden, even in patients with higher BMI, despite BMI showing a positive correlation with lymphatic depth. Thus, although higher BMI leads to deeper lymphatic vessels anatomically, this did not translate into increased scanning difficulty in this cohort, suggesting that S-CUPS performance remained consistent across a range of body habitus. However, because both S-CUPS and D-CUPS achieved complete intraoperative confirmation, the observed differences should be interpreted primarily in terms of examination efficiency rather than diagnostic superiority. Because correlations were calculated using per-site measurements, potential within-patient clustering should be considered when interpreting these exploratory associations. The representative cases shown in Figures 4–6 illustrate typical examples of lymphatic–venous differentiation and are not intended to represent the statistical correlations summarized in Table 3.

Neither ISL stage nor lymphoscintigraphy classification was significantly associated with examination time or VAS scores.5–8 These findings suggest that local anatomical characteristics—particularly vessel diameter—may have a greater influence on scanning difficulty than global indices of lymphatic dysfunction.

Intraoperative NECST classification demonstrated that E-type vessels accounted for 79.5% of all exposed lymphatic vessels (Table 4).16 These vessels also exhibited the largest external diameters. The ability of S-CUPS to identify such dilated lymphatic vessels preoperatively, with subsequent intraoperative confirmation, supports the feasibility of S-CUPS as a tool for selecting physiologically favorable lymphatic vessels for LVA. Because E-type lymphatic vessels maintain lumen patency and pliability, they represent ideal targets for anastomosis, and their reliable visualization may assist surgical planning.

Taken together, these findings suggest that S-CUPS is associated with shorter per-site examination time and lower examiner-reported mental fatigue while maintaining accurate anatomical mapping. Although the magnitude of these differences was modest, S-CUPS may represent a practical addition to preoperative lymphatic ultrasound assessment. Further studies with larger cohorts and multi-institutional designs are needed to validate these findings and determine how S-CUPS can best be integrated into standardized lymphatic imaging protocols.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and all examinations were performed by a single experienced lymphatic surgeon, which may limit generalizability to operators with different levels of experience. Second, the study was conducted at a single institution, and multicenter validation will be necessary to confirm the reproducibility of these findings. Third, lymphatic and venous diameters were assessed ultrasonographically; although intraoperative confirmation was achieved at all sites, minor discrepancies between ultrasound measurements and direct microscopic measurements cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, this study represents a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained cohort and therefore inherits the inherent limitations of retrospective study designs. In addition, imaging parameters were adjusted at each scanning site according to operator judgment to optimize vessel visualization. Although both S-CUPS and D-CUPS were evaluated under these optimized conditions, such adjustments may introduce a degree of operator-dependent variability. Finally, because this was an exploratory analysis, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed, and the results should be interpreted accordingly.

CONCLUSIONS

S-CUPS, an SMI-based lymphatic ultrasound technique, was associated with statistically shorter per-site examination time and lower examiner-reported mental fatigue compared with the conventional D-CUPS method. Intraoperative confirmation supported the accuracy of preoperative vessel mapping, and vessel diameter was identified as a key determinant of examination difficulty.

Although these differences reached statistical significance, their magnitude was modest, and their clinical relevance warrants further validation in larger, multioperator settings. S-CUPS may represent a practical addition to preoperative lymphatic ultrasound assessment, with potential implications for workflow efficiency and surgical planning in the management of lymphedema.
