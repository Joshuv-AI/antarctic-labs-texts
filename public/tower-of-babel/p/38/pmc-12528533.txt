Strategies to reduce intestinal toxicity in neoadjuvant management of locally advanced rectal cancer
Authors: Hoda Mahdavi, Sahar Dashti, Shima Jafari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The management of locally advanced rectal cancer has progressively evolved to improve both survival outcomes and quality of life. Intestinal toxicity is a prevalent side-effect of pelvic radiotherapy affecting most patients. The pursuit of neoadjuvant intensified treatment strategies to enhance response rates or facilitate organ preservation may lead to an increased likelihood of late toxicity, depending on radiation technique and chemotherapy intensity. This narrative review synthesizes current knowledge on radiation-induced intestinal toxicity, refines effective strategies, with an emphasis on studies evaluating techniques to reduce both unintended and intended radiation exposure to the small intestine in locally advanced rectal cancer patients, and highlights their clinical significance.

Introduction

Cancer of the rectum is currently ranked the eighth most common cancer worldwide, with an incidence and mortality age-standardized rate of 7.1 and 3.1, respectively [1]. The standard multimodal management of those staged as locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) charectarized by tumoral invasion beyond muscularis propria (T3, T4), or node positive diseases, typically includes neoadjuvant (presurgical) chemoradiotherapy followed by proctectomy, delivered in a staged sequence rather than upfront surgery. Chemotherapy might be delivered either neoadjuvant or adjuvant, however, more contemporary strategies emphasize a holistic approach of optimizing local control, reducing toxicity, and enhancing overall patient outcomes [2]. Explicitly, since the number of cancer survivors has increased, an auxiliary objective of LARC management to enhance patients’ quality of life has become an increasingly important priority [3]. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of rectal cancer, encompassing variations in tumor biology and patient-specific factors, highlights the necessity for personalized treatment approaches.

Of all the aspects of management, surgery has remained the cornerstone of the curative treatment, especially as the introduction of the total mesorectal excision (TME) has shown marked impact in local control by itself [4]. Historically, a Dutch study showed that preoperative radiotherapy enhanced local control even when TME was performed [5]. As a result, decades of trials have shifted the role of radiotherapy from the adjuvant setting to the neoadjuvant. Using neoadjuvant radiotherapy, clear resection margins have been more frequently fulfilled, and local relapse rates have been further reduced. The long course 45–50.4 Gy in fractions of 1.8 to 2 Gy fluoropyrimidine-based neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) has brought improved local control compared to adjuvant chemoradiotherapy [6], or conventional radiotherapy alone [7] Thus, concurrent CRT has become the standard neoadjuvant strategy. However, this approach is accompanied by intestinal toxicity, which stems from the obligatory inclusion of intestinal segments within 3D conformal radiation fields due to anatomical constraints and the high radiosensitivity of intestinal mucosa.

Importantly, the manifestation of acute radiation-induced symptoms has been consistently correlated with subsequent development of chronic complications that profoundly impact patient quality of life metrics. The short course regimen (5 × 5 Gy) is another approach for neoadjuvant, in which the local effects of tumor shrinkage are less substantial, yet the acute toxicity is also less prominent [8]. More recently, the traditional surgical paradigms are challenged, since there has been a growing interest in non-operative management options for LARC. The potential benefits of avoiding surgery, including reduced morbidity and improved quality of life, have spurred extensive research in this area. Now, carefully selected compliant patients with complete clinical responses to neoadjuvant treatments, including the elderly whom the surgical procedure is debilitating, are offered this option [9–11].

Studies exploring intensified neoadjuvant treatments have been refined to maximize the efficacy of combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy [12–14]. In response, researchers have explored total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT), integrating chemotherapy and radiotherapy in distinct neoadjuvant sequences before proctectomy, and it is being increasingly recognized as a primary treatment for LARC [15, 16]. TNT may enhance pathological complete response (pCR) rates, which is associated with improved rates of disease-free survival and the potential of achieving organ preservation [17]. pCR almost doubled with TNT, when the interval from short course radiotherapy to surgery increased, in the RAPIDO trial, or when combination chemotherapy was intensified in the PRODIGE 23 trial [18]. As well, the long-term results of the PRODIGE 23 protocol which included 3 months of neoadjuvant mFOLFIRINOX demonstrated a possible overall survival advantage as the secondary end point for the first time of any TNT trial [19]. In particular, The calculated Number Needed to Treat of 7.5 individuals managed with long course chemoradiation and consolidation chemotherapy to achieve one additional pCR, makes this treatment selection sound justifiable [20]. Although achieving a pCR has been a critical outcome in LARC management, it does not generally translate into survival benefit [20, 21]. While the initiation of TNT with long-course chemoradiotherapy provided the highest response rates in the CAO/ARO/AIO-12 trial, concerns about long-term toxicity remain, since half of patients experienced grade 1–2 late toxicity at 3 years, though severe toxicity was uncommon [22]. By contrast, the RAPIDO trial found no significant differences in late toxicity or quality of life between TNT and standard neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [16], These findings highlight the importance of tailoring TNT strategies to individual patient goals [18].

Intestinal radiation toxicity, more specifically referred to as radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIID), a type of mucositis that cause enteropathy, is a prevalent and often debilitating complication of LARC treatment, though estimates of the prevalence is uncertain and variable, up to a fifth of rectal cancer patients experience high grade (Grade ≥ 3) late GI toxicity [23]. The most frequent manifestations including fecal and gas incontinence, diarrhea, and less commonly, pain, bleeding, obstruction [15, 24] Importantly, with a high incidence, reaching up to 90%, of long-term bowel dysfunction arises in pelvic malignancies, which may affect quality of life [25]. The management of these side effects are challenging and largely symptom directed, ranged from life-style modifications, to endoscopic or radio-interventional treatments [25–27].

Currently, three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT) is the standard neoadjuvant radiotherapy technique, while more sophisticated means have not shown a survival advantage [28, 29]. Still, balancing aggressive treatment aimed at disease eradication with preserving patients’ quality of life has become a delicate endeavor. Hence, there has been a shift toward adopting more advanced treatment delivery designed to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce toxicity [28]. Notedly, a Cochrane review on studied interventions demonstrated that the benefit of advanced radiotherapy for reduction of gastrointestinal symptoms is uncertain. Also any other intervention, either pharmacological or non-pharmacological, except for a shift from two-dimensional (2D) to three-dimensional conformal (3DCRT) radiotherapy planning, have uncertain, low, or very low value of supporting evidence for benefit. This may be due to methodological limitations and heterogeneities [30]. In general, positive findings of studies specific to rectal cancer were insignificant and the strength of evidence may not be elevated unless more randomized studies are conducted.

Hereby, this current review focuses on potential strategies and their evidence to reduce radiation-induced intestinal toxicities with a focus on LARC patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.

Pathophysiology

Ionized radiation causes direct or indirect injury to the DNA and other subcellular structures of the tumoral tissue and normal tissue as well, by creating free radicals when photons interact with water. Rapidly proliferating structures, including the intestinal mucosa, are vulnerable to this injury , most which are affected in the G2 and M phases of the cell cycle. Damage to intestinal stem cells leads to subsequent enterocyte loss and disruption of the normal intestinal barrier. This interaction initiates a complex cascade of molecular events extending to inflammatory processes [25]. The gut microbiome can also modulate oxidative stress, the inflammatory processes, and affect the mucosal composition and permeability. The toxic effects are accompanied by microbiome dysregulation, including two interrelated mechanisms: translocation and dysbiosis of the gut microbiome. Translocation denotes the movement of bacteria from the intestinal lumen into the bloodstream, which can incite systemic inflammation. Concurrently, dysbiosis, characterized by an imbalance in gut microbiota composition, compromises the protective barrier function of the intestinal epithelium, further exacerbating radiation-induced injury [31]. Clinically it manifests by the last fractions of RT delivery, but by definition the acute injury occurs within the first 90 days following treatment initiation [23]. Severe stem cell impairment results in chronic toxicity characterized by histological alterations such as vascular damage, sclerosis, and fibrosis, which generally lack the inflammatory responses of the acute phase. chronic intestinal toxicity can extend beyond months and may last for years which contribute to the development of irreversible symptoms, including pain, cramping, diarrhea, and malabsorption [32, 33].

Preventive measures

Given that the rectum serves as the primary radiotherapy target in LARCs, effective prevention of radiation toxicity may prove challenging. Various patient-related risk factors have been associated with the development of symptom severity, including smoking, diabetes, and some vascular conditions. Most of which are not reversible before treatment initiation. As well, no interventions have been identified to effectively control established late intestinal toxicity of pelvic radiotherapy [30]. Individualized nutrition support with high protein intake has demonstrated benefits in improving quality of life and survival [34]. A higher skeletal muscle index, as an indicator of well-nourishment, has been associated with improved local control of rectal cancer [35]. Accordingly, acute and late toxicity decreased when nutritional counseling, education, and monitoring of colorectal cancer patients were implemented, compared to controls with no dietary intervention or those who received a fixed dose supplement [36]. Particularly, low BMI < 18.5 has risen the risk of RIID to 4-fold [25], emphasizing the necessity of maintaining optimal nutritional status throughout radiation therapy. The administration of different probiotic strains has shown potential in free-radical scavenging, bacterial translocation modulation, enhancing intestinal immune responses, and suppression of pathogenic growth. Prebiotics, defined as non-digestible food components, serve as substrates for beneficial gut bacteria, promote overall microbiome health, and potentially reduce RIID [37, 38]. Innovative therapeutic interventions targeting the microbiome, termed as Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), offer a promising approach by restoring a healthy microbial community. This is a medical procedure in which processed stool from a healthy donor is transferred into the gastrointestinal tract of a patient to restore microbiome balance [39]. An ecologic study on a mouse model showed that how FMT keeps enteric bacterial mixture, preserved gut function, and gene expression profiles of irradiated cells, reduced radiation related death without promoting cancer cells [40, 41]. Human studies of the gut microbiome or microbial metabolites, are warranted due to in inconclusive outcomes of available studies [42].

Set-up and contouring considerations

An initial step in radiation therapy is patient positioning, which has been incorporated since the 2D era. Simple techniques have been used to displace the mobile intestinal loops away from high-dose radiation regions and reduce low-dose gradients affecting normal tissues, including the intestine. Prone positioning, or compression devices such as belly boards, or both have been used for this aim. Prone positioning compared to supine set-ups had fewer random errors, especially in the anterior-posterior direction [43]. however, higher systematic anterior-posterior set-up errors limits reproducibility. The use of belly-board improves reproducibility measures, but usual set-up verification and correction procedures may not detect some of the displacements unless controlled by real-time adjustments. Also, prone position is limited in the elderly or frail patients, or post-surgical cases with ostomy [44, 45]. Although prone positioning is generally accepted in 3D-CRT but it is not a popular option in IMRT as it has not been shown to make a substantial improvement in its planning quality indicators [43, 46, 47].

The influence of contouring on intestinal toxicity can sometimes be substantial, given that the PTV of LARC encompasses lateral lymph nodes, mesorectum, and presacral regions. It is suggested that target volumes may be tailored according to recurrence patterns [48]. For instance, in patients with negative circumferential resection margin (CRM) and node-negative disease, it is suggested that lower cranial margins of the radiotherapy field to the true pelvis does not compromise outcomes while reduces intestinal toxicity [49]. Additionally, anterior or caudal limit modulation can be utilized based on lateral lymph node involvement and the status of the involved lymph nodes of the internal iliac or obturator region [50]. Although less emphasized in 3D-CRT, it is suggested that contouring the whole bowel bag instead of the bowel loops can be a more acceptable representation of the at-risk organ since the intestine is mobile [51]. There is a wide variety in the suggested margins for CTV to PTV expansion. Some advocate anisotropic margins for CTV to PTV expansion, others declare scalar margins according to the traditional Van Herk et al. formula is acceptable but where available, institutional analyses of rectal motion should be taken into account. Since margin requirements varies depending on technique, there is currently no universally accepted standard [, 52].

Technique selection

Several factors contributing to the incidence of RIID stem from techniques and dose distributions. The evolution of radiotherapy has markedly advanced the protection of intestinal tissues. Firstly, in the standard neoadjuvant setting, compared to the historical adjuvant approach, the volume of intestine adjacent to the target area in the pelvis is reduced. Moreover, the traditional 2D radiotherapy of the pelvic area offered limited precision, often resulting in the unintended irradiation of normal tissues [30]. The development of 3DCRT improved target volume coverage compared to 2D plans of LARC patients, especially in T4 staged cancers in which external iliac nodes are targeted [53]. However, when using the traditional four-field box technique with 3DCRT, the portion of the bowel inside the pelvis is unintentionally irradiated.

Intensity Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT) enhances treatment planning flexibility, enabling more precise dose distributions that conform closely to the target volume while minimizing radiation exposure to surrounding tissues, especially the intestine. The dose distribution of the horseshoe-shaped PTV structure of LARC can be tightly confined using more conformal dose delivery through IMRT, so the bowel bag or loops may be spared more effectively [54]. Whereas, the overlap between the PTV and the organs at risk (OARs), including the bowel loops, is deliberately exposed to radiation to account for uncertainties in patient positioning and shifts in internal organs. In pelvic malignancies, set-up errors are typically minimal, staying under 5 mm, which reduces the necessity for fixation. However, even when these are meticulously managed through image guidance, the challenge of internal organ motion persists [44]. Dosimetric studies have demonstrated that a typical 5-field IMRT can reduce the volume of 45–50 Gy dose to bowel loops, but it can increase the volume of bowel receiving low dose gradients [55, 56]. The dosimetric advantages of IMRT within the neoadjuvant context for LARC are well documented [2, 57, 58], but the evidence for the clinical benefits of IMRT over 3DCRT in LARC remains mixed. There is inconsistent evidence for dose between gradients (V10-40) delivered to the intestine and acute intestinal measures, possibly because most clinically significant high-grade toxicities arise when dose over 50–55 Gy is delivered to the small intestine and large bowel [59, 60], still the significance of maintaining smaller volumes of low-dose gradients becomes more pronounced in short-course neoadjuvant regimens [55, 56] or contemporary intensified protocols.

Dosimetric constraints derived from Emami’s seminal work established that limiting the volume of small bowel receiving ≥ 50 Gy to < 5% significantly reduces the risk of late intestinal obstruction to 5% [61]. More recent models have refined the classical constraints in which volumes of bowel receiving 15 Gy (V15) were observed as a threshold to predict intestinal toxicity in chemoradiation with 5-FU [62, 63], or when 5-FU was intensified by oxaliplatin [64]. Other models on neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy of LARC revealed that volumes of dose levels as small as 5 Gy significantly determined acute toxicity presenting as diarrhea, where it can be concluded that there is no safe dose to the intestine [65, 66], Justifying optimal technique selection.

The main advantage of IMRT in the setting of rectal cancer has been the observed higher rates of pCR and tumor downstaging compared to 3DCRT. Furthermore, some studies suggest an improved dose to the anal sphincter and an increased chance of anal preservation in patients with low rectal cancer with the cost of lower rates of R0 resection [29, 67, 68]. IMRT technique was further enhanced by Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT), which incorporates refinements such as reduced treatment time, decreased number of monitor units, and lower integral dose, making it an efficient option for planning [44, 66].

A Cochrane review on pelvic malignancies indicated that IMRT versus 3DCRT may reduce acute (risk ratio 0.48, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.88) and grade 2 and higher late toxicities (risk ratio 0.37, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.65). However, evidence of IMRT superiority compared to 3DCRT regarding intestinal toxicity is of low certainty [30]. These findings may not be generalizable to rectal cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. A more focused meta-analysis compared the rates of acute toxicity associated with IMRT and 3DCRT in patients with LARC undergoing neoadjuvant chemoradiation demonstrated that IMRT was associated with lower rates of overall intestinal toxicity compared to 3DCRT. These findings suggest that IMRT may be a more tolerable treatment option for patients with LARC; however, long-term effects and cost-effectiveness were not assessed [29].

The optimal choice of IMRT technique is less established, and the choice depends on factors such as tumor location, patient anatomy, and availability of technology. In usual photon therapy reports on plans designed for LARC had superior target homogeneity and conformity and decreased OAR doses for both tomotherapy and VMAT compared to 3D-CRT. However, VMAT outstanded other methods in terms of causing lower integral dose [69], some reported superior dose distribution and plan quality for tomotherapy in terms of OARs [70], but these advantages was mostly reported for bladder [70, 71], and head of femor [71], rather than the bowel dose. With the addition of IGRT using daily MVCT in tomotherapy, the CTV-PTV margin extension was effectively reduced, hence, the overlap between PTV and small bowel was minimized. This significantly improved V15 and NTCP of the small bowel compared to 3D plans [54].

Proton beam therapy can precisely deliver an equivalent radiation dose or escalate the dose to the tumor target while potentially reducing the dose to OARs, resulting in a markedly improved dosimetric profile compared to IMRT. The key advantage is the Bragg peak effect: protons deposit most of their energy at a specific depth, allowing for precise targeting and normal tissue sparing. Therefore, it offers a compelling advantage in minimizing the dose to the small bowel when compared to conventional 3DCRT. Proton therapy does not generally differ from IMRT regarding small intestine exposure at higher dose thresholds (30–40 Gy), but outperforms 3DCRT at lower doses (10–20 Gy). Due to the lack of widespread availability, and possibly high costs of treatment, the advantage may be offered to rare incidences high risk for bowel toxicity, such as some cases of recurrent disease [72].

Dose escalation

The ability to safely escalate the radiation dose from the standard 45–50.4 Gy in 25–28 fractions to higher delivered dose while preserving surrounding healthy tissues has expanded treatment options and improved patient outcomes. Data suggest a positive correlation between increased equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) and tumor regression by escalated dose to the tumor ranging from 50.4 to 70 Gy. The specific dose-response relationship varies across studies, influenced by treatment protocols, patient characteristics, and techniques [73]. High-dose IMRT was tested in a study in which 55.8 Gy was delivered to the PTV of low rectal cancers. The response rates were reportedly high, and 40% experienced pCR, allowing for full-thickness local excision. Moreover, DFS and OS correlated with tumor response [74]. Simultaneous integrated boost (SIB) in IMRT allows for dose escalation to the tumor without extending the overall treatment time. SIB plans are less sensitive to daily variations in patient positioning. In a phase 2 study using tomotherapy, patients were simultaneously boosted to 55.2 Gy when CRM involvement was < 2 mm. This was one of the first studies incorporating IGRT for this purpose. High-grade intestinal and genitourinary toxicities were acceptable, and metabolic responses were higher in the boost group [75]. Almost similar encouraging results were reported by [76–79]. Conversely, some other published articles report on higher acute toxicities by using IMRT-SIB. In one study, patients were boosted to 55.7 Gy, while oxaliplatin biweekly 130 mg/m2, was added to concurrent capecitabine. Despite the advantage of high pathologic responses, over a fourth of patients experienced grade 3 and higher acute intestinal toxicities [80].

Brachytherapy has been employed for neoadjuvant, definitive, or palliative treatment of rectal cancer. High cCR rates of the primary tumor in most reports have made it a choice in patients who were candidates for non-operative management, regardless of stage. In the early stage disease, predictive models have been developed to identify patients who may benefit most from definitive brachytherapy [81]. In later stages when pelvic lymph nodes are involved, a nodal target volume by external RT is planned, followed by a brachytherapy boost. Most studies have reported promising results, showing high complete response rates. High-grade late toxicities were low in almost all studies, making brachytherapy a good choice for even frail patients [82].

A meta-analysis examined the impact of dose escalation in neoadjuvant radiotherapy for LARC since the advent of modern inverse-planning techniques, including studies utilizing radiation doses exceeding 54 Gy with 3DCRT or IMRT [83]. The results were encouraging, demonstrating significantly higher rates of pCR at 24.1% compared to 15% of historical controls, with rates of R0 resection at 90.7% and low rates of acute grade 3 toxicity at 11.2%.

Collectively, these findings suggest that dose escalation in neoadjuvant radiotherapy for LARC is feasible, yielding improved treatment responses. Side effects were acceptable when combined with optimized plans, supporting the broader adoption of advanced techniques.

Adaptive strategies and automated systems

The significance of image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) in LARC treatment is not very substantial unless reduced set-up margins are aimed, and conducted through rigorous verification and correction protocols. Here, factors such as soft-tissue deformation, tumor mobility, and the hollow structure of the rectum and the adjacent bladder contribute to uncertainties, especially at the upper-anterior rectum [44].

The implementation of IGRT has further refined treatment accuracy by enabling verification of target location and adaptive treatment planning. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) is an advanced imaging method that allows for the quantification of organ motion and setup errors. MVCT, MRI, and PET-based systems are also available with different merits [84, 85]. Unlike traditional EPID technology, these modalities provide insights into internal anatomy, organ motion, and changes in their shape and volume. These advancements enhance our understanding of setup errors and organ displacement throughout treatment, and whether corrections are needed [44]. The volume of rectum carries less variability when empty, promoting bowel preparations, and protocols for bladder filling are encouraged. Changes in bladder filling during the radiotherapy course have the most effect on bowel displacement and a smaller compression effect on mesorectal volume. However, in contrast to genitourinary malignancies, the application of IGRT in LARC has not been thoroughly investigated. Additionally, systematic reviews have not adequately addressed bowel toxicity in this context [52].

The current evidence supports the idea that IGRT may not sufficiently reduce treatment toxicity without incorporating adaptive strategies. The choice can be one of many strategies, such as frequent re-scan and re-plan at one or more set timepoints or live tracking to account for intra-fraction target motion [86]. Online and offline adaptive RT involve multiple stages based on the imaging modality and available technology. The process begins with image acquisition, deformable registration, and organ motion tracking, supported by certain systems. This is followed by a decision-making phase to determine if adaptation is necessary, leading to rapid replanning. Among several approaches, the 'library of plans' approach seems to be the most evidence-based strategy for LARC treatment [85]. This method involves creating multiple treatment plans based on different PTV margins, allowing for flexibility in adapting the treatment to daily changes in the patient’s anatomy. By reducing PTV margins and minimizing radiation exposure to healthy tissues, this approach may lead to reduced side effects and improved local tumor control [87]. However, adaptive methods can be time-consuming and resource-intensive, furthermore, they require advanced equipment and skilled technologists. The following studies were conducted in the setting of LARC.

A plan selection strategy was clinically feasible in LARC, with an acceptable interobserver consistency, which could cause smaller plans [88]. A study on short and long course RT for LARC using VMAT with dual arcs showed a reduced V15 of the bowel bag with online strategies while the target coverage remained the same [89]. The practicality of implementing adaptive strategies through an online CBCT-based approach was assessed for LARC patients undergoing short-course radiotherapy. Despite the extended treatment durations and labor-intensive processes for daily adaptation, patients were compliant with the workflow. The reference PTV margins were adjusted to reduce replanning frequency and to balance between workflow complexity and speed, but this might lose the merits of online adaptation concerning dose to the OARs. Further evaluation is necessary to determine its potential clinical significance in minimizing toxicity [90]. The library of plans strategy for rectal cancer, based on population statistics, was viable and lead to a significantly lower average volume of rectum PTV compared to traditional radiotherapy. A CBCT-guided strategy for plan selection in LARC patients was proposed, demonstrating that the planning target volume (PTV) decreased by an average of 15.5%. Additionally, the upper-anterior margin of the mesorectum showed potential for displacement by up to 50% posteriorly [87].

Adaptive Radiotherapy, while beneficial in tailoring treatment to individual patient responses, significantly increases workload and planning time. Plan optimization during this process is both time-intensive and crucial, and despite standardized protocols, its outcomes often rely on the planner’s expertise. To fully capture the benefits of adaptive therapy, it may be advantageous to integrate it with other advanced technologies, such as automated systems.

The ability of MR-linacs to provide real-time imaging of the tumor during treatment allows for daily adjustments to the treatment plan. MR allows for the visualization of the mesorectum and organs at risk but also enables the primary tumor and pathological lymph nodes to be observed during each treatment session [91]. A study designed for daily full online recontouring and replanning demonstrated that MR-linac helped with interfraction motion adaptation. It reduced the PTV volume to an average of 70%, lowered dose to OARs, though the amount of dose reduction was not measured. However, the time on the treatment couch per fraction was high with a mean 48 min, besides training the technologists for online recontouring was disadvantageous for the workflow [92]. To address these challenges, automatic planning systems have been extensively researched and developed as benchmarking tools, but the robustness of these methods depends on the quality of the accumulated input data [93]. For instance, knowledge-based optimization is based on data from successful plans. It applies advanced algorithms or machine learning techniques to analyze and extract patterns, trained for adaptive strategies [93, 94]. This method has been shown to improve planning efficiency in LARC patients [95], and resulted in lower V30-40 to the bladder and bowel in VMAT plans [93]. Protocol-based planning is another method that uses clinical goals set by experienced planners as input to generate plans [94]. This was incorporated as a rectal planning optimizer, and showed to efficiently reduce the planning process by < 1 min. Although a third of the automated plans were deemed inferior to manual planning, all were generally acceptable and caused significantly lower V30-40-35 to the bowel [96]. Multi-criteria optimization is another automated procedure to overcome unmet plan objectives by providing single or multiple plans with clinically desired trade-offs between all objectives [93]. This was developed and tested in LARC patients and was shown to reduce the MR- LINAC IMRT planning time from 4 to 6 h to about an hour, leading to lower mean doses for the bladder and rectum with similar target coverage [97].

Additionally, advanced imaging pattern recognition artificial intelligence algorithms enable identification and contouring of anatomical structures, leading to more accurate target volume definition [98–100]. The potential benefits of using an automated template-based treatment planning system for radiotherapy of anal, rectal, and prostate cancers were explored. Patients were replanned for both IMRT and VMAT so the automated plan quality was compared to corresponding clinically accepted and manually generated plans. Based on their data, the authors changed their practice to withhold manual VMAT planning in their institution in favor of automated IMRT plans. In particular, dosimetric values of achieved target coverage, as well as OAR sparing were not significantly different among different plans [101]. Therefore, the cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes of online adaptive strategies should be carefully evaluated in each context to determine their feasibility for implementation, as well as their effect on reducing radiation toxicity. Moreover, further research is needed to fully evaluate long-term benefits and to identify the patient selection criteria.

Conclusion

This review evaluates alternative rectal cancer strategies that enhance treatment efficacy. The current evidence indicates that in the context of neoadjuvant treatment of LARC, intensified therapies are increasingly adopted when higher oncologic outcomes are aimed. In this setting acute and late toxicity is high while effective interventions to prevent intestinal side effects may be limited. Nutritional support, particularly maintaining BMI, and prebiotic and probiotic formulations, may mitigate both local inflammatory responses and systemic metabolic consequences of radiation enteropathy. Furthermore, given the presumed absent safe dose threshold for the intestine, the application of advanced radiotherapy techniques are reasonable. IMRT offers dosimetric advantages that better conform to target geometries while sparing critical intestinal structures. Moreover, the integration of motion management strategies can lead to further PTV margin adjustments, which reduce volumes receiving high doses. This can help to decrease the intentional dose to the intestine, which is directly related to how strictly aiming for management of set-up errors and organ motion. These advantages may be translated into improved quality of life. Emerging solutions incorporating artificial intelligence-driven auto-segmentation, deformable image registration, and automated adaptive replanning algorithms show significant promise for optimizing treatment efficacy and normal tissue sparing. This review is limited by its narrative nature. In fact, no certain conclusions can be brought to address practical challenges. Availability of advanced techniques may be currently restricted. Therefore, focused studies on quality of life in rectal cancer survivors would possibly lead to future recommendations regarding selection for contemporary radiotherapy paradigms provided that practical constraints are effectively managed.
