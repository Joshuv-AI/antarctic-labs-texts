Maternal Use of Physical Discipline and Children's Externalizing Problems Across Childhood in Singapore
Authors: Yena Kyeong, Yuna Koyama, Mioko Sudo, Michelle Zhi Ling Kee, Helen Chen, Johan Gunnar Eriksson, Fabian Kok Peng Yap, Yap Seng Chong, Henning Tiemeier, Peipei Setoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study explored the reciprocal associations between maternal physical discipline and children's externalizing problems across childhood, distinguishing between trait‐like (i.e., between‐person) and state‐like (i.e., within‐person) differences. We used data from 604 dyads in Singapore, a context where physical discipline is commonly accepted. We analyzed maternal physical discipline and children's externalizing problems at three timepoints between ages 4 and 10.5 using a random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel model, adjusting for sociodemographic factors. At the between‐person level, maternal physical discipline covaried positively with more externalizing problems in children, supporting extensive evidence in the literature. However, at the within‐person level, maternal physical discipline at age 4.5 predicted children's lower externalizing problems at age 7. These findings underscore the value of disentangling between‐ and within‐person effects and highlight the complex associations between physical discipline and externalizing problems in a setting where physical discipline is common.

Introduction

Physical discipline involves using physical force to inflict pain, but not injury, on a child to modify or control their behavior (Straus and Donnelly 2017). Research consistently shows its detrimental effect on children's psychological, physical, and cognitive development (for a review, see Avezum et al. 2023; Gershoff and Grogan‐Kaylor 2016). Children's externalizing problems (i.e., aggressive, delinquent, or hyperactive behaviors) in particular, have consistently been associated with caregivers' use of physical discipline in cross‐sectional and longitudinal studies (e.g., Pinquart 2021), with some evidence indicating bidirectionality in these associations (Heilmann et al. 2021). Despite declining use in many regions, physical discipline persists globally (Fleckman et al. 2023; Global Initiative to End All Corporal Punishment of Children 2023). In this study, we aimed to refine our understanding of the reciprocal associations between maternal physical discipline and externalizing problems. Using data from a prospective birth cohort study, we employed a statistical approach that distinguishes different sources of variance (i.e., at between‐ and within‐levels). We addressed our research question in Singapore, where parental physical discipline is traditionally accepted and used (Sudo et al. 2023).

Bidirectional Associations Between Physical Discipline and Externalizing Problems

Extensive research has documented a robust link between harsh parenting practices, including physical discipline, and children's externalizing problems. Several theoretical frameworks offer explanations for this association (for a review, see Gershoff 2002). From a social learning perspective, the use of physical discipline can legitimize violence as a viable problem‐solving method, thereby transmitting aggressive tendencies to children (Gershoff 2002). Relatedly, parental physical discipline may model poor regulating behaviors, dampening children's self‐regulatory skills (Li et al. 2023), a key precursor to externalizing problems (Robson et al. 2020). Furthermore, socialization frameworks suggest that power assertive methods may undermine children's internalization of moral values by externally imposing desired behaviors (Grusec and Goodnow 1994). Collectively, these theories elucidate the parent‐driven effects underlying the link between physical discipline and children's externalizing behaviors.

In parallel, children are not passive recipients of parental influence; rather, children can actively shape their social environments by eliciting responses from caregivers (i.e., evocative effects), exerting child‐driven effects (Scarr and McCartney 1983). Hence, children's behavioral problems (e.g., aggressive behaviors, defiance) and difficult temperament may elicit parents' harsh parenting, such as physical discipline, as parents attempt to manage children's challenging behaviors (Ferguson 2013; Larzelere et al. 2018). This recursive nature of these parent–child dynamics is illustrated by Patterson's (2002) coercion model, which highlights how harsh parenting and child externalizing behaviors may mutually reinforce one another in escalating feedback loop. For example, Yan et al.'s (2019) longitudinal study found that throughout childhood, children's externalizing behaviors predict increases in intrusive parenting, which subsequently predict heightened externalizing behaviors into adolescence. This cascading pattern indicates the long‐lasting developmental implications of child‐driven processes in parenting context.

However, empirical findings on the bidirectional associations between physical discipline and externalizing problems remain somewhat inconclusive. While some studies (e.g., Lansford et al. 2012; Speyer et al. 2022; Wang and Liu 2018) found the bidirectional associations between parental physical discipline and externalizing problems during childhood, others only revealed child‐driven effects (e.g., Verhoeven et al. 2010) or found no significant effects in either direction (e.g., Besemer et al. 2016). These mixed findings may stem, in part, from limitations in the analytical approaches traditionally used to model parent–child associations. To model reciprocal associations between physical discipline and child outcomes, the cross‐lagged panel model (CLPM) has been widely used. However, its ability to support causal interpretations has been increasingly questioned, mainly due to its conflation of between‐ and within‐person effects (Berry and Willoughby 2017; Hamaker et al. 2015; Usami et al. 2019). Between‐level effects reflect stable, trait‐like differences across families, representing covariation that may be underpinned by underlying genetic predispositions or shared environmental factors affecting both parents and children. In contrast, within‐person associations denote how temporal dynamics unfold over time within individual families: whether changes in parental physical discipline within one family predict subsequent changes in their child's externalizing behaviors, and vice versa (Berry and Willoughby 2017). When these two levels are not decoupled, as in the CLPM, bidirectional parent‐ and child‐driven effects within families are confounded with stable dyadic characteristics, precluding accurate interpretation of dynamic associations (Berry and Willoughby 2017).

Unlike the CLPM, its alternative, the random‐intercept cross‐lagged panel model (RI‐CLPM), accounts for stable trait‐like variabilities at the between‐person level (i.e., random intercepts; Usami et al. 2019), and cross‐lagged paths reflect associations between individual score fluctuations around their person‐specific means over time (Hamaker et al. 2015; Mulder and Hamaker 2021). Utilizing this modeling, Berry and Willoughby (2017) found that cross‐lagged paths between spanking and externalizing problems became nonsignificant at the within‐person level after controlling for between‐person variability. Similarly, Pritsker (2021b) demonstrated that the positive association between these variables found in traditional CLPM became nonsignificant or reversed in RI‐CLPM. These findings have led some researchers to argue that the negative effects of limited physical discipline may be overstated (Larzelere et al. 2024). Using generalized estimating equations that also can account for between‐person confounds, Besemer et al. (2016) found no significant within‐person association between parental physical discipline and boys' externalizing problems. However, Speyer et al. (2022) reported that harsh parenting, including physical discipline, was associated with hyperactivity and inattention at the within‐person level, but not with conduct problems. While substantial evidence from traditional approaches suggests adverse effects of physical discipline, limited evidence from newer analytic techniques indicates a more nuanced picture.

Contextual Considerations

Much research on physical discipline and child adjustment has focused on European or North American countries (Heilmann et al. 2021). To address this gap, international research efforts by Lansford and colleagues (e.g., Lansford et al. 2005; see also Lansford et al. 2016 for a review) have investigated how culture shapes parenting and child outcomes. Cultural normativeness theory posits that in societies where physical discipline is normative, parents may use it in a more regulated manner, which may not connote hostility or rejection (Deater‐Deckard and Dodge 1997; Lansford et al. 2005). Supporting this view, Lansford et al. (2012) found that maternal spanking predicted externalizing problems among European American but not African American children. However, other studies report no differences in the longitudinal associations between physical discipline and externalizing problems across multiple countries (Alampay et al. 2017; Cuartas 2024) or diverse ethnic groups in the United States (e.g., Ward et al. 2021).

Recent research has explored the longitudinal effects of physical discipline in understudied regions, such as low‐ to middle‐income countries, where its use is more normative than in high‐income countries (Cuartas 2024). For example, Cuartas (2024) examined the effects of spanking in four low‐ to middle‐income countries (e.g., Cambodia, Ethiopia), using between‐person and within‐person analyzes. Despite its limitation of only two timepoints, this research showed that spanking was linked to poorer child outcomes, including socioemotional adjustment.

In Asian cultures, particularly those influenced by Confucianism that highlights parental authority (e.g., China, Korea), strict parental control, including physical discipline, is viewed as more normative than in many European or North American countries (e.g., Son et al. 2017). While scant research has investigated reciprocal associations between physical discipline and child behavioral problems in Asian contexts, one study from mainland China found both parent‐driven and child‐driven effects using an autoregressive latent trajectory model (Wang and Liu 2018). In contrast, using the RI‐CLPM, Zhou et al. (2023) found no within‐person level effects of harsh discipline (e.g., slapping) on externalizing problem among Chinese adolescents, but only a child‐driven effect. Collectively, evidence remains inconclusive when concerning the reciprocal associations between physical discipline and externalizing problems, underscoring the need for further investigation across cultures.

Physical Discipline in Singapore

Singapore provides an interesting stage for examining physical discipline. As a multiracial Asian country—ethnic Chinese (74%), Malays (14%) and Indians (9%; Singapore Department of Statistics 2023)—it shares similarities with high‐income nations where physical discipline has been widely studied (e.g., the United States, Canada). However, unlike in these nations where its use is declining and increasingly viewed less favorably (Fleckman et al. 2023; Mehus and Patrick 2021), Singapore has no bans on physical discipline, including within families (Global Initiative to End All Corporal Punishment of Children 2023). Public attitudes toward its potential abusiveness remain uncertain (Lui et al. 2019). For example, caning (i.e., hitting with a thin stick, mostly on the buttocks or limbs) has long been accepted, with the majority (71%) of the respondents surveyed in 1994 considering it “sometimes” or “always” acceptable (Elliott et al. 1997). Moreover, Lui et al. (2019) suggest that public attitudes toward caning became more tentative in 2010. While fewer respondents considered caning as definitely “not abuse” (decreasing from 29% to 21%), fewer also perceived it as definitely “abuse” (from 28% to 19%). In a recent study, Won, Sudo, et al. (2025) found that despite recalling negative childhood experiences with physical discipline, many young adult respondents (71%) did not support a ban on its use in Singapore, reflecting the enduring cultural acceptance toward disciplinary practices.

In a study by Sudo et al. (2023) using the same cohort as the present study, 94% of Singaporean mothers reported using some form of physical discipline (e.g., spanking, slapping) at least once when child was age 4.5. Although prevalence declined as children aged, over 80% of mothers still reported using physical discipline when the child was age 11. Cheung and Lim (2022) suggest in their scoping review that power‐assertive (e.g., strict, authoritarian parenting) parenting practices, typically viewed suboptimal in Western cultures (e.g., European or North American countries), may not always result in negative outcomes among Singaporean children. This trend aligns with perspectives in some Asian cultures, such as Chinese, where harsh parenting is sometimes interpreted as care and concern (Chao and Tseng 2002). However, Singapore's strong Asian heritage coexists with Western influences (Cheung and Lim 2022). Sudo et al. (2023) found that maternal physical discipline was not linked to children's perception of parental care, whereas paternal physical discipline was associated with lower paternal care ratings. These findings challenge the traditional notion that physical discipline connotes care, highlighting its nuanced implications.

Nonetheless, there is a dearth of research directly investigating the role of physical discipline in child outcomes in Singapore. For example, Sim and Ping Ong (2005) examined the relations between parental physical discipline and children's aggression in ethnic Chinese families but focused on hypothetical parental intentions rather than actual practices. Notably, Won, Kyeong, and Setoh (2025) found that minor physical discipline (e.g., caning) during childhood was not associated with young adults' externalizing problems after accounting for other parenting behaviors (e.g., psychological aggression) and sociodemographic factors. Other studies were restricted to youth offenders (Li et al. 2021) or children with clinical levels of externalizing problems (Sng et al. 2018). Taken together, further research is needed to explore the implications of physical discipline within its cultural normativeness, enhancing our understanding of how the cultural landscape shapes the impact of parenting.

Overview of the Current Study

Our study explored the longitudinal reciprocal associations between maternal physical discipline and externalizing problems in early (age 4), middle (age 7–8), and late childhood (age 10.5). While parents generally reduce physical discipline as children grow, considerable variability exists in how families adjust this practice (Lansford et al. 2009). We focused on maternal physical discipline, as mothers are typically the primary caregiver in Singapore (Cheung and Hawkins 2014). To capture reciprocal dynamics, we used RI‐CLPM to disentangle the stable trait‐like variance from within‐person transactional associations. Drawing upon prior research (Berry and Willoughby 2017; Pritsker 2021b), we expected the random intercepts to covary positively at the between‐person level. However, given mixed findings at the within‐person level, we did not have an a priori hypothesis regarding the cross‐lagged paths. Situating our research question in Singapore, where physical discipline is normative and prevalent, the current study aimed to illuminate its effect on children's externalizing problems across childhood.

Method

Participants and Procedures

This study utilized data from an ongoing longitudinal birth cohort, the Growing Up in Singapore Toward healthy Outcomes (GUSTO). Specifically, we used data from 604 participants (M age = 30.68, SDage = 5.04 for mothers at recruitment) who had completed questionnaires on physical discipline (at child's age of 4.5, 8.5, and 10.5 years old) or child behavioral problems (at child's age of 4, 7, and 10.5 years old) for at least one timepoint. Further details on recruitment, eligibility criteria, and participant flow chart (Figure S1) are provided in the Supporting Information. Table 1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of the final analytic sample. The protocol of the GUSTO study was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board and the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. Data used in this study can be made available upon request pending approval from the GUSTO study executive committee.

Measures

Maternal Use of Physical Discipline

Mothers self‐reported their use of physical discipline when their child was age 4.5, 8.5, and 10.5 by using the Parenting Style and Dimensions Questionnaire—Short Version (PSDQ; Robinson et al. 2001). The PSDQ is composed of 32 items that evaluate three global dimensions of parenting practices (i.e., authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive), each comprising several lower‐order subscales. In this study, four items of the physical coercion subscale (e.g., “I spank when our child is disobedient”) under the authoritarian parenting style were used. Mothers reported the frequency of each behavior they employed using a five‐point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always). In the current sample, the physical coercion subscale demonstrated slightly suboptimal to acceptable reliability at each timepoint (Cronbach's αs = 0.67, 0.70, and 0.75, respectively). Given that the scale contains a small number of items, which can constrain Cronbach's α, we also examined the average inter‐item correlation following Clark and Watson (2019). These values at each timepoint (0.33, 0.38, and 0.44, respectively) fall within the acceptable (0.15–0.50), indicating sufficient internal consistency.

Child Externalizing Problem

Caregivers, primarily mothers (94%–99% across timepoints), reported on children's externalizing behavioral problems at ages 4, 7, and 10.5 using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach 1991; Achenbach and Rescorla 2001). The CBCL assesses socioemotional and behavioral problems, including global internalizing and externalizing problems. In the GUSTO study, the CBCL/1.5–5 (99 items) was administered at age 4, while the CBCL/6–18 (113 items) was used at ages 7 and 10.5. Caregivers rated each behavior (e.g., “gets in fights”, “screams”) on a three‐point Likert scale (0 = not true to 2 = very true). Sum scores were computed for externalizing problems: attention problems (five items) and aggressive behaviors (19 items) at age 4; rule‐breaking (17 items) and aggressive behaviors (18 items) at ages 7 and 10.5. Given the use of different versions across timepoints, we used age‐ and gender‐specific T scores to account for heterotypic continuity. These scores were based on the norms in the United States, which falls under Group 2 societies (i.e., intermediate problem scores), consistent with Singapore (Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment 2023). The externalizing problems subscale demonstrated good reliability across timepoints (Cronbach's αs = 0.91, 0.89, and 0.88).

Analytic Plan

Main Analyzes

To examine the longitudinal reciprocal associations between physical discipline and externalizing problems, we utilized a RI‐CLPM (Hamaker et al. 2015). Children's biological sex, maternal ethnicity, maternal educational attainment, and maternal age at recruitment, as well as monthly household income at recruitment were included as time‐invariant covariates at the between‐person level. Zero‐order correlations between these covariates and the main study variables are shown in Table S1. In RI‐CLPM, all autoregressive and cross‐lagged paths, as well as within‐time correlations were freely estimated without any time‐invariant constraints (Mulder and Hamaker 2021). For comparison purposes, we also conducted an analysis using traditional CLPM, which does not distinguish effects at between‐ and within‐person levels. This modeling contrast allows critical evaluation of how different analytic frameworks may influence observed developmental associations and helps situate our findings within the broader literature that has predominantly used traditional CLPM.

RI‐CLPM and CLPM were analyzed in Mplus 8 (Muthén and Muthén 1998–2017). We conducted attrition analyzes comparing participants with complete and incomplete data across key sociodemographic characteristics using independent samples t‐tests and χ2 tests. Mothers with incomplete data on physical discipline did not differ significantly from those with complete data on any sociodemographic factors (ps > 0.086). In contrast, mothers with lower educational levels were more likely to have incomplete data on externalizing problems (p = 0.019). Ethnicity was significantly associated with completion rates on externalizing problems as well (p < 0.001), such that Chinese mothers showed the highest completion rate (63.7%), followed by Malay (50.6%) and Indian (44.8%). Missingness in focal variables was handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation under the assumption of missing at random.

In evaluating model fit, following Hu and Bentler (1999), comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI) > 0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08 were considered acceptable. Missing values for covariates (i.e., n = 34 for household income, n = 8 for maternal educational attainment; n = 1 for maternal age) were imputed. Since Mplus uses listwise deletion for missing data on exogenous variables, multiple imputation was conducted for sociodemographic covariates to retain a larger analytic sample and circumvent sampling bias that can be introduced by case exclusion (e.g., Sterba 2014). In Mplus, Bayesian analysis based on the Markov Chain Monte Carlo estimation is used for multiple imputation (Asparouhov and Muthén 2010). The imputation model included harsh parenting dimensions (i.e., physical discipline and verbal hostility) and behavioral problems (i.e., externalizing and internalizing problems), as well as covariates, generating 50 imputed datasets. To circumvent the convergence issue, we increased the number of iterations to 10,000, so that all imputed datasets can be used for estimation.

Robustness Check

To address potential limitations of RI‐CLPM, which assumes an equilibrium state (Andersen 2022), we conducted a supplementary analysis using DPM (Allison et al. 2017). Additionally, we conducted sensitivity analyzes, which are detailed in the Supporting Information. Specifically, we applied the RI‐CLPM under three conditions: (a) without imputing missing values for sociodemographic information (see Figure S2), (b) including only maternal reports of externalizing problems (see Table S2 for a description of this subsample; see Figure S3 for results), and (c) including only participants who had data for at least two timepoints for either or both physical discipline or externalizing problems (see Figures S4 and S5).

Discussion

This study explored the transactional associations between maternal physical discipline and children's externalizing problems from early to late childhood. While much prior research has used statistical models that confound two different sources of variance, the current study aimed to decompose between‐ and within‐person variance using RI‐CLPM. Moving beyond the Western cultures, we leveraged prospective cohort data from Singapore, where parental physical discipline is normative (Sudo et al. 2023).

Our findings demonstrated a positive covariation between physical discipline and externalizing problems at the between‐person level, suggesting that children who experienced more physical discipline than their peers were more likely to show greater externalizing problems. This aligns with extensive prior research indicating the relation between physical discipline and behavioral problems (e.g., Gershoff and Grogan‐Kaylor 2016), though the causal direction remains undetermined. As this result represents stable trait‐like differences, the covariation may be driven by underlying genetic predispositions or shared environmental factors affecting both parents and children (e.g., family stress; Klahr and Burt 2014). Alternatively, the link between physical discipline and externalizing problems may have been established and stabilized before the timeframe captured in our study (i.e., before age 4.5).

After accounting for between‐person differences, most cross‐lagged paths were nonsignificant, except for one significant parent‐driven effect: higher use of physical discipline (compared to their person‐specific mean) at age 4.5 was associated with decreased externalizing problems in children at age 7. This inverse association was largely replicated in our robustness checks, including the DPM approach, contrasting with prior research suggesting an adverse role of physical discipline. Our finding, however, aligns with Pritsker (2021b), who found a dampening effect of infrequent, non‐object spanking on externalizing problems from ages 6 to 7 and 7 to 8 in children in the United States. Despite differences in time window and sociocultural contexts, our study similarly suggests that an increase in physical discipline from the person‐specific mean is associated with a decrease in children's externalizing problems at the subsequent timepoint.

Of note, this observed inverse within‐person association emerged during early to middle childhood, a developmental window when children may be particularly attuned to parental authority and less likely to exhibit defiant responses. During this period, physical discipline is more frequently used and perceived as normative (Gershoff 2002), with children potentially viewing such discipline as legitimate parental authority, given their still‐developing interpretive capacities for understanding parenting behaviors. This intersection of developmental susceptibility and perceived normativeness may contribute to temporary behavioral suppression, aligning with the immediate compliance effects documented in Gershoff and Grogan‐Kaylor's (2016) meta‐analysis. However, caution is needed in interpreting this finding. While our design captures snapshots of ongoing developmental processes across time (Dormann and Griffin 2015), our 3‐year measurement intervals are not suited to detect the moment‐to‐moment dynamics of compliance (Pritsker 2021a), which limits our ability to infer immediate behavioral responses. Crucially, even if temporary compliance contributes to the observed patterns, it should not be equated with developmental adjustment, given the unclear long‐term implications for internalized regulation and its broader association with negative developmental outcomes (Gershoff and Grogan‐Kaylor 2016). Indeed, while parents' use of physical discipline typically declines with children's age (e.g., Lansford et al. 2009; Sudo et al. 2023), its negative effects may increase over time (Ferguson 2013), suggesting longer‐term adverse effects. Overall, our findings underscore the need for longitudinal research that can disentangle transient behavioral compliance from enduring self‐regulatory trajectories.

More broadly, our findings substantiate a central tenet of cultural psychology: universalism without uniformity. At the between‐level, we observe universal processes—physical discipline consistently covaries with externalizing problems across cultural contexts, reflecting common parent–child dynamics around behavioral regulation and conflict. At the within‐level, the direction and magnitude of these associations could be nuanced across cultural contexts. The cultural normativeness hypothesis further sheds light on the unexpected inverse association between physical discipline and externalizing problems. In Singapore, where the majority of parents report using physical discipline (Sudo et al. 2023), and where it is often widely accepted as a form of corrective care, evaluating the impact of physical discipline on child outcomes may be challenging (Cheung and Lim 2022; Sim and Ping Ong 2005). For example, children in Singapore may not perceive maternal physical discipline as neglect or autonomy‐undermining (Sudo et al. 2023), and less severe forms of physical discipline may not confer the same adverse effects observed in European or North American contexts (Won, Kyeong, and Setoh 2025). Hence, in some Asian cultural settings, maternal physical discipline may be perceived as a more neutral disciplinary strategy for behavior regulation. This nuanced pattern might also stem from the tendency for harsh and supportive parenting behaviors to coexist in Singaporean mothers (Cheung and Hawkins 2014; Seow et al. 2026).

Our findings indicate that cultural acceptance and the developmental normativeness of physical discipline converge during earlier developmental phases, yielding temporary behavioral suppression. From children's perspectives, their early compliance following a disciplinary act may be internalized as evidence of its effectiveness, potentially contributing to its intergenerational transmission. This pattern may help explain why Singaporean young adults who experienced physical discipline during childhood tend to oppose its legal bans (Won, Sudo, et al. 2025). For parents, observing temporary behavioral suppression may reinforce beliefs about disciplinary effectiveness, likewise perpetuating its use. These individual experiences might be collectively reflected in broad public attitudes toward physical discipline, sustaining favorable or ambivalent attitudes toward it (Elliott et al. 1997; Lui et al. 2019). Nonetheless, caution is warranted, as adult children in Singapore retrospectively reported emotional and physical harms associated with these childhood experiences (Won, Sudo, et al. 2025). Taken together, our findings highlight the importance of considering both cultural and developmental normativeness in shaping the impact of disciplinary practices.

To contextualize our findings within the broader literature that has predominantly used traditional approaches, we also conducted analyzes using a conventional CLPM, which does not separate between‐ and within‐person effects. These results were largely consistent with existing literature, showing significant concurrent associations at all time points and a positive association between physical discipline at age 8.5 and externalizing problems at age 10.5. These findings suggest that the positive associations between physical discipline and children's externalizing problems may primarily reflect between‐person associations, consistent with previous research indicating minimal or negative relations once between‐level variance is accounted for (Berry and Willoughby 2017; Pritsker 2021b; Zhou et al. 2023). While cautious causal inferences can be made at the within‐person level, confounding by time‐invariant variables in CLPM limits causal conclusions (Usami et al. 2019). Together, these disparate findings add a methodological caveat to this literature, showing that isolating between‐person variation can alter the direction of within‐person associations typically observed in traditional models. This nuanced finding supports ongoing calls for longitudinal methods that disentangle between‐ and within‐person processes (Cuartas 2024).

Our findings should not be interpreted as advocating for physical discipline. Notably, mirroring Pritsker (2021a), the standardized coefficients for the associations between physical discipline at age 4.5 on externalizing problems at age 7 (β = −0.25, p = 0.01) had a wide 95% confidence interval [−0.45, −0.05]. This uncertainty warrants caution in interpretation, as it may reflect a chance finding. While we found no evidence of long‐term adverse effect, other studies have reported such effects at both between‐ and within‐levels (e.g., Cuartas 2024). Acknowledging this, we echo Hamaker (2023) in emphasizing the importance of critically evaluating findings, particularly given challenges in interpreting cross‐lagged effects. Altogether, our research demonstrates that associations between physical discipline and child outcomes can vary depending on the statistical approach. In addition, interpretations should consider both levels of analyzes. That is, although our findings suggest a temporary reduction in externalizing behaviors at the within‐person level, this should be understood in the context that, at the between‐person level, elevated levels of physical discipline covaried with more externalizing problems. Therefore, while isolating effects in analytic models is crucial without merging them as CLPM does (Lucas 2023), it is also important to consider both within‐ and between‐person levels in interpretation for a more comprehensive understanding of the implications of physical discipline.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be noted. First, we relied on maternal self‐reports of physical discipline and their children's problems, which may have introduced response bias. However, prior research found no evidence of under‐reporting in this sample (Sudo et al. 2023). We did not include fathers' parenting behaviors, despite evidence of sex‐specific associations with externalizing problems (e.g., Sim and Ping Ong 2005). Future research should incorporate multiple informants, including other caregivers and the child, to mitigate shared informant bias and offer a more comprehensive perspective.

Second, our measure aggregated various forms of physical discipline (e.g., spanking, slapping) without assessing severity or implementation details. While some studies suggest severity does not alter its adverse effect (Alampay et al. 2017), others indicate differential effects based on type and severity (Larzelere and Kuhn 2005; Won, Kyeong, and Setoh 2025). Additionally, the perceived normativeness of specific forms may vary by cultural context. For example, caning as a form of parental physical discipline is widely acceptable in Singapore (Elliott et al. 1997; Lui et al. 2019) but was not assessed in our data (Sudo et al. 2023). The exclusion of culturally salient behaviors may have led to underestimation of both its frequency and psychological implications. Future research should employ culturally sensitive and behaviorally specific instruments to better capture the full spectrum of disciplinary practices and examine their nuanced effects, as they may vary in frequency, severity, and cultural normativeness.

Third, the demographic characteristics of the GUSTO cohort are not representative of the broader Singaporean population. Specifically, as noted by Soh et al. (2014), the GUSTO study was designed to overrepresent families from Malay and Indian ethnic backgrounds (28% Malay and 14% Indian in our analytic sample, compared to 13.4% Malay and 9.2% Indian in the resident population at the time of recruitment; Singapore Department of Statistics 2011a). Participants were also less likely to hold a university degree compared to the resident population in a similar age range (25–34 years of age; Singapore Department of Statistics 2011a), and the cohort included a relatively larger proportion of middle‐income households (i.e., $2000–5999) and a smaller proportion of households at both the lower ($0–1999) and higher ($6000 and above) ends of the income distribution (Singapore Department of Statistics 2011b). As such, our findings may not be generalizable to the general population.

Lastly, RI‐CLPM is not without potential limitations. Although we supplemented our analyzes with DPM to address equilibrium assumptions, other modeling assumptions preclude a perfect causal estimation (Mund and Nestler 2019). Both RI‐CLPM and DPM remain susceptible to bias from unobserved time‐varying covariates (Rohrer and Murayama 2023; Usami et al. 2019). For example, stressful family life events (e.g., financial insecurity; Coe et al. 2020) or interparental conflict (Coln et al. 2013), which can change between timepoints but were not measured in the study, could contribute to residual confounding. Our design was also limited in that the timing of data collection between the two constructs was not perfectly concurrent, especially at the second timepoint. Although these assessments fall within a similar developmental window and provide a developmentally meaningful snapshot within broader phases (i.e., early, middle, and late childhood), caution is warranted in interpreting within‐person level associations, and more tightly synchronized assessments would help address this limitation. A longer timeframe and additional measurement occasions would allow for alternative modeling approaches (e.g., autoregressive latent trajectory modeling with structural residuals; Berry and Willoughby 2017), enabling further robustness checks across developmental stages.

Conclusions

This study is among the few to explore reciprocal associations between physical discipline and children's externalizing problems in an Asian context, where harsh parenting is relatively normative (vs. European or North American countries). Findings from a traditional CLPM showed a positive covariation between physical discipline and externalizing problems, while RI‐CLPM results revealed a reversed within‐person association, with no support for adverse effects. Caution is needed in generalizing this finding in light of the ecological setting, given that the normative context of harsh discipline may have shaped these results. More rigorous, evolving approaches and evidence from diverse sociocultural contexts would help refine our understanding of its role in child development.
