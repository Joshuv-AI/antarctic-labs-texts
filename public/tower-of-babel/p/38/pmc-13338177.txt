Antagonism shapes social maps in the human brain
Authors: Isato Chikazawa, Ryo Ishibashi, Tamami Nakano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman relationships form a complex web of affiliative and antagonistic ties. Yet how the brain represents their affective structures remains unclear. Here, using a television drama depicting intertwined friendships and rivalries, we examined how the brain encodes the valence of interpersonal relationships. Participants underwent fMRI scanning before and after watching the drama while viewing the faces of its central characters. They then rated each character pair for relationship strength and valence, and whole-brain representational similarity analysis (RSA) identified brain regions representing these relational structures. Relationship valence effects were most prominent for antagonistic (negative) relationships in the left anterior supramarginal gyrus and right medial prefrontal cortex. Univariate analyses revealed increased activation in the precuneus after drama viewing, suggesting enhanced retrieval of narrative-related person knowledge, though this region did not show statistically significant representational similarity patterns reflecting interpersonal relationships. These findings indicate that the human brain constructs a multidimensional social map from narrative experience, with antagonistic ties playing important role in shaping social relationship representations.Subject terms: Cognitive neuroscience, Human behaviour

Introduction

To behave adaptively within a group, humans must not only recognize individual others but also understand the relationships among them, thereby grasping the complex structure of social networks. Social interactions encompass both affiliative ties such as friendship and trust, and antagonistic ties such as competition and hostility. Comprehending this intricate web of relations forms the foundation of social judgment and behavior.

A long tradition of research has sought to elucidate how such social relationships are represented in the brain. The hippocampal–entorhinal system is known to encode relations among entities in the environment by forming a cognitive map, including both spatial and more abstract forms of organizations1–3. Similar neural mechanisms are thought to extend to the social domain, enabling humans to organize social knowledge in a map-like structure. Indeed, neural activity in the hippocampus has been shown to represent others’ relative positions within a two-dimensional social space defined by power and affiliation4. Grid-like coding patterns in the hippocampal–entorhinal system have been reported to support navigation through abstract social hierarchies5. Moreover, the amygdala contributes to learning and representing social hierarchies and dominance relationships6.

While these studies have revealed how abstract social attributes such as hierarchy or personality traits can be embedded in neural representational space, real-world social relationships are far more dynamic and context-dependent. Recent work has therefore begun to examine how the brain represents naturally occurring social networks. When participants’ actual classroom friendship networks were mapped, neural activity in the prefrontal and parietal cortices reflected the structure of those networks, including each person’s centrality within the group7. Beyond everyday social experience, humans also acquire rich knowledge about interpersonal relationships through narratives—such as novels, films, and television dramas—that portray evolving social connections and conflicts. Behavioral evidence shows that people tend to organize their knowledge and memories of others according to narrative relationships rather than personality traits8, and an electroencephalography (EEG) study indicates that neural responses to faces reflect relational information from very early stages of perception9. Together, these findings suggest that the human brain spontaneously constructs internal representations of social networks, primarily around connections among people rather than individual attributes.

Importantly, such connections are not characterized merely by their strength but also by their valence—whether they are affiliative or antagonistic. A recent large-scale behavioral study has identified relationship valence as core dimension underlying social relationship representations10. In particular, negative ties such as rivalry and conflict systematically shape network structure and behavior: in geopolitics, enduring rivalries and oppositional alliances organize international networks11, and in organizations, negative ties sharpen differentiation and affect performance12. Psychological evidence likewise demonstrates that negative social information exerts a disproportionately stronger impact than positive information13. These findings collectively indicate that negative connections—rivalries, conflicts, and hostilities—form a crucial dimension of social structure. Nevertheless, most neuroscience research on social cognitive mapping has focused primarily on affiliative ties, such as friendship and cooperation. It remains unclear whether the brain represents relationship valence – affiliation versus antagonism- alongside strength within a shared representation space.

To address this question, we examined how the human brain constructs a multidimensional space representing not only the strength but also the valence of social relationships. Using the television drama Suits (6 episodes from Season1, USA Network, 2011)—set in a law firm where friendship, rivalry, and romance are intertwined—we investigated how narrative-driven learning shapes neural representations of social connections. Participants underwent fMRI scanning before and after viewing the drama while observing the main characters’ faces (Fig. 1A, B). By comparing representational similarity across characters between pre- and post-viewing sessions and relating these changes to narrative-derived ratings of relationship strength and valence, we identified brain regions where neural patterns became more aligned with the drama’s interpersonal relationship structure, capturing the multidimensional nature of social ties.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-one college students participated in this study (mean age: 21.3 years, range: 19–23 years). The sample included 12 males and 9 females, based on participant self-report. Information on gender identity, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status was not collected. None had a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, and all had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. To quantify statistical sensitivity, we conducted a sensitivity analysis for paired-sample effects, indicating that with 21 participants the study has 80% power (two-sided α = 0.05) to detect standardized within-participant effects of approximately Cohen’s d ≈ 0.62 or larger. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Institute of Information and Communications Technology (NICT; approval number: 2310240020). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the experiment. Participants received monetary compensation of 16,000 JPY for their participation in the study. This study was not preregistered.

Stimuli and experimental procedure

Participants watched the first six episodes (~5 h in total) of the American television drama SUITS (Season 1, USA Network, 2011). Functional MRI (fMRI) scans were conducted twice—once before and once after viewing the drama. Only individuals who had not previously seen the drama were recruited. The average interval between the two scanning sessions was 14.5 days (range: 12–21 days), during which participants viewed the episodes at home on their personal computers over several days. Regarding the timing of the post-viewing scan, 14 participants underwent scanning on the same day they finished viewing the final episode, 5 participants were scanned one day later, and 3 participants were scanned two days later. To confirm that participants had attentively watched the drama, they completed two three-choice comprehension questions after each episode. The mean accuracy rate was 90.0% (range: 83.3–100%), confirming that all participants adequately understood the content.

During both the pre- and post-viewing MRI scans, participants performed an identical visual task. They lay supine in the MRI scanner with their heads stabilized using foam cushions and wore earplugs to attenuate scanner noise. Visual stimuli were projected onto a screen and viewed through a mirror mounted on the head coil (visual angle: 23°). The stimuli consisted of face images of eight main characters from the drama. For each character, six distinct facial images were extracted from the drama footage. Each face image was presented for 3 s (visual angle: 14° × 18°) at the center of the screen, followed by an interstimulus interval of 4–7 s (randomized). Stimulus presentation was controlled using PsychoPy (https://www.psychopy.org/).

Each participant completed three sessions per scan. In each session, faces of the eight characters were presented three times in random order, using different images of the same character across repetitions, yielding 18 trials per character per session. To maintain attention during scanning, 10% of the trials contained immediate repetitions of the same image, and participants were instructed to press a button with their right index finger whenever they detected a repetition.

After the second fMRI session, participants completed a behavioral rating task assessing interpersonal relationships among the eight main characters. On each trial, two character faces were presented side by side on a PC monitor, and participants rated their perceived relationship on two semantic differential scales: (1) Strength (Strong–Weak) and (2) Valence (Antagonistic–Affiliative). Ratings were made on a continuous 7-point slider scale (0–6). All 28 possible character pairs were evaluated, and the order of presentation was randomized across participants. The rating task was also implemented using PsychoPy.

Data acquisition

Functional images were acquired using multiband T2∗-weighted gradient echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequences, which were obtained using a 3-Tesla MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma) with a 64-channel array coil. We collected 480 scans per session (slice number = 66, slice thickness = 2.5 mm, repetition time [TR] = 1000 ms, echo time [TE] = 30 ms, flip angle = 60°, field of view [FOV] = 200 × 200 mm, voxel size = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm, and multiband factor = 6). To generate anatomical reference images, we acquired a T1-weighted structural image for each subject (rapid gradient echo sequence, slice thickness = 1 mm, TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.22 ms, flip angle = 8°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, and voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm). To correct the geometric distortion in the EPI, we also acquired field maps for each participant (Siemens standard double echo gradient echo field map sequence, slice thickness = 2.5 mm, TR = 656 ms, TE = 4.92 ms, flip angle = 60°, FOV = 210 × 210 mm, and voxel size = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm).

Imaging data analysis

The acquired MRI data was processed using SPM12 and MATLAB. To correct the image distortion caused by field inhomogeneity, we conducted field map correction using the SPM field map toolbox. We confirmed that the head movements were <3 mm in all participants, and the EPI images were realigned and unwarped. Each participant’s structural image was coregistered to the mean of the motion-corrected functional images. Subsequently, the EPI images were normalized to the standard brain template (Montréal Neurological Institute template) and smoothed using a Gaussian kernel filter with an 8-mm full-width-at-half-maximum. After preprocessing, we conducted voxel-wise regression analyses of the expected hemodynamic responses for nine conditions (eight character faces plus button-press events) using a general linear model (GLM) at the single-subject level. The six realignment parameters were included as nuisance regressors to account for motion-related variance. To identify brain regions showing changes in activity in response to character faces before versus after viewing the drama, we performed a group-level t-test. Whole-brain analyses were thresholded using a family-wise error (FWE)–corrected cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05, with a cluster-defining threshold of p < 0.001.

Whole-brain Searchlight RSA

Whole-brain representational similarity analysis (RSA) was performed using the beta maps for the eight characters obtained from the GLM14. The GLM for RSA was estimated using unsmoothed functional images (i.e., without applying the 8-mm spatial filter) to preserve fine-grained multivoxel patterns. A spherical searchlight with a radius of 8 mm was systematically moved across the entire brain. Within each sphere, neural representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) were constructed by computing cross-validated Mahalanobis distances (crossnobis) among activation patterns evoked by the eight characters, with cross-validation performed across the three runs to reduce noise-related bias. This metric was chosen because it accounts for the covariance structure of the data and is well suited for capturing multivariate pattern differences in fMRI signals15. As explanatory model RDMs, we constructed a continuous relationship valence RDM derived from the behavioral ratings, and facial feature dissimilarity. Behavioral valence ratings (0–6) were recoded into a centered scale ranging from −3 (antagonistic) to +3 (affiliative), and pairwise dissimilarities were computed based on these values. Facial feature dissimilarity was computed by extracting 512-dimensional embedding vectors for each character using a face recognition neural network (ArcFace)16 and calculating pairwise distances between these vectors. To examine how neural representations of interpersonal relationships changed with drama viewing, interaction terms with Time (Pre- vs. Post-viewing) were included for the valence predictors (Fig. 2A). Here, Time represents the factor distinguishing pre- and post-viewing sessions. Random effects were modeled for participants and for the interaction between participant and Time. Finally, for each searchlight, two-tailed t-tests were conducted on the interaction coefficients of Valence × Time and the main effect of Facial Feature to identify regions showing significant effects. Results were thresholded at p < 0.001 (voxel-level) and p < 0.05 (cluster-extent corrected). Data distributions and model assumptions were inspected prior to statistical testing, and no substantial violations of assumptions were observed.

Results

To characterize how participants perceived the web of relationships among the drama characters, we conducted a semantic differential (SD) evaluation after the fMRI experiment (Fig. 1C). Participants rated every pair of characters along two dimensions: relationship strength (strong–weak) and valence (affiliative-antagonistic). As shown in Fig. 1D, plotting the mean scores across participants revealed a clear multidimensional structure of interpersonal relationships. For example, the relationship between the protagonist and his friend was rated as both strong and affiliative, locating it at the positive end of the valence axis. In contrast, the relationship between the protagonist and his rival was equally strong in terms of connection strength, yet it occupied the opposite, most antagonistic end of the valence spectrum. Meanwhile, relationships rated as weak in strength tended to cluster around neutral valence values. This pattern indicates that pairs with strong relationships tend to occupy both affiliative and antagonistic extremes, suggesting a systematic relationship between strength and the magnitude of valence. Consistent with this, a significant correlation was observed between relationship strength and the absolute value of valence (r (26) = 0.74, p < 0.00001), between strength and affiliative valence (r (26) = 0.80, p < 0.001), and between strength and antagonistic valence (r (26) = 0.94, p < 0.000001). Accordingly, behavioral ratings of interpersonal relationships are better characterized by two components, affiliative and antagonistic, rather than by a two-dimensional framework separating strength and valence. Therefore, in the subsequent RSA, we modeled relationship valence along a continuous antagonistic-to-affiliative dimension, separately from relationship strength.

To identify where in the brain interpersonal relationships were represented, we conducted a whole-brain searchlight RSA using the neural patterns elicited by each character’s face. For each searchlight, the neural RDM was compared with model RDMs derived from behavioral ratings of affiliative (positive) and antagonistic (negative) relationship valence (Fig. 2A). A linear mixed-effects model was used to estimate the interaction between these predictors and session (Pre- vs. Post-viewing), while controlling for facial feature similarity. The analysis revealed that neural representations became more closely aligned with relational valence after drama viewing, with effects being most prominent for antagonistic (negative) relationship in the left anterior supramarginal gyrus (SMG), left superior parietal gyrus and right medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) (Fig. 2B, Table1). Effects related to affiliative (positive) valence did not survive the reporting threshold. Neural representations also showed significant correspondence with the facial feature similarity model in regions associated with face perception, including the bilateral fusiform gyrus, anterior temporal lobe and occipital cortex (Fig.2C, Table 1). To further visualize the significant Time × Valence interaction, we plotted the relationship between neural representational dissimilarity and behavioral relationship valence separately for the pre- and post-viewing sessions in the left anterior SMG and right mPFC clusters (Fig. 2D). These plots illustrate that neural representations of antagonistic character pairs became more similar after drama viewing.

Visual inspection of the scatter plots further suggested that overall neural representational distances between character pairs increased after drama viewing. To quantify this effect, we compared the mean pairwise neural representational distance (crossnobis distance) within each ROI between the pre- and post-viewing sessions. Mean representational distance increased in both regions, reaching significance in the right mPFC (t(20) = 2.49, p = 0.022, d = 0.54, 95% CI [0.086 1.00]) and showing a similar tendency in the left anterior SMG (t(20) = 1.92, p = 0.069, d = 0.42, 95% CI [−0.027 0.87]) (Fig. 2E). To determine whether this effect extended beyond the RSA-defined ROIs, we performed a whole-brain analysis comparing mean neural representational distance between the pre- and post-viewing sessions. This analysis revealed widespread increases in representational distance after drama viewing, including frontal, temporal, and precuneus regions (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings suggest that drama viewing promoted greater segregation of neural representations among characters, resulting in a more differentiated social representational space.

Next, to examine how drama viewing altered the overall level of neural responses to the characters’ faces, we conducted whole-brain univariate analyses comparing activity between the pre- and post-viewing sessions (Fig. 3, Table 2). A significant increase in activation after viewing (Pre < Post) was observed in the left precuneus, a region often implicated in autobiographical reminiscence17. Conversely, greater activation before viewing (Pre > Post) was found in the bilateral fusiform gyrus and the posterior occipital gyrus. These results suggest that repeated exposure to the drama enhanced higher-order, socially reflective processing of character information while reducing early perceptual responses to faces.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that interpersonal networks spontaneously acquired through narrative experience are represented in the brain as a multidimensional space defined by both affiliative and antagonistic relationship valence. RSA revealed that antagonistic (negative) relationship valence was represented in the left SMG and right mPFC, whereas affiliative relationship valence effects did not survive the reporting threshold. Univariate analyses revealed increased activation in the precuneus, suggesting engagement in episodic retrieval of narrative information associated with individual characters after drama viewing. These findings suggest that the human brain represents interpersonal relationships in a multidimensional social cognitive map derived from narrative experience, in which antagonistic ties serve as key anchors.

Negative ties as anchors in social cognitive maps

Recent work has identified relationship strength and valence as core dimensions underlying social relationship representations10. Similar dimensions have been reported in non-human primates, where affiliative and antagonistic relationships jointly structure social networks18. Traditional social network theories have primarily centered on positive ties as the foundation of network organization. Such models emphasize how being connected to others enhances access to information, social support, and influence, often linking centrality in social networks to greater success and well-being19,20. Neuroimaging studies have similarly shown that social network size (the number of friends) correlates with the volume of brain regions involved in social cognition, including the amygdala and the temporoparietal cortex21,22. Moreover, the brain represents the centrality of others in real-life social groups7.

However, real-world social networks are not composed solely of positive relationships. Rather, as Simmel23 argued, competition, rivalry, and conflict are not simply breakdowns of social ties but constitutive forces that actively shape group structure and drive social organization. Correspondingly, psychological research has shown that negative social information has greater salience and impact on learning and memory than positive information13, and organizational studies suggest that disliking ties are essential for completing the full social ledger, offering greater explanatory power for social dynamics than positive ties alone12.

In line with these perspectives, our findings suggest that antagonistic valence is preferentially represented in the mPFC and SMG. The mPFC has been implicated in social and affective evaluation, including mentalizing and self/other representation24,25, suggesting that it may contribute to representing antagonistic aspects of interpersonal relationships. The SMG has been implicated in perspective-taking26, intention attribution27, and social norm processing28, indicating a role in the social-cognitive interpretation of such relationships. Together, these regions may support complementary affective and cognitive processes underlying the representation of antagonistic social ties. These findings highlight a prominent role of antagonistic ties in shaping neural representations of social relationships, suggesting that negative relationships may carry greater cognitive and neural weight in modeling social networks based on narrative experience. Beyond the representation of antagonistic ties themselves, drama viewing was also associated with an overall increase in neural representational distance among characters, particularly in the mPFC and, to a lesser extent, the SMG. Similar effects were observed across widespread cortical regions. These findings suggest that narrative experience promotes the differentiation of character representations, resulting in a more segregated social representational space. Antagonistic relationship structure may therefore emerge within a broader process of social representational reorganization driven by narrative learning.

Episodic representation of individual agents in the precuneus

Of particular note, the precuneus was the only region to show increased activation in response to individual characters face after drama viewing. As a major hub of the default mode network (DMN), the precuneus is densely connected with the hippocampus and has been implicated in self-referential thought, autobiographical memory retrieval, and theory of mind17,24,25,29,30. The enhanced activity in this region may reflect the formation of richer, episode-based mental representations for each character as a result of narrative experience. In the present study, significant representational similarity effects related to interpersonal relationship structure were primarily observed in the mPFC and inferior parietal regions. Together, these findings suggest that the precuneus may be more strongly engaged in narrative-related person representations grounded in episodic memory, whereas relationship-related representations were more prominent in prefrontal and parietal regions.

Implicit social structure encoding versus hippocampal relational navigation

Narrative comprehension—the integration of who, what, where, and when into a coherent storyline—has been closely linked to episodic memory formation. The hippocampus, a core structure for episodic encoding, has been shown to represent narrative elements such as people, locations, and contextual features of events in humans31. Beyond narrative processing, the hippocampal–entorhinal system is known to support spatial navigation through place and grid cells1,32 and to form cognitive maps for abstract domains, including object categories33 and social dimensions such as power and affiliation4,5. These studies suggest that the hippocampus constructs navigable cognitive spaces that guide behavior in both physical and social contexts. However, in the present study, significant effects related to interpersonal relationship structure were not observed in the hippocampal or entorhinal regions at the reporting threshold. One possible explanation lies in task demands: previous studies explicitly required participants to compare personal attributes or navigate within an abstract social space, thereby engaging hippocampal relational mapping. In contrast, our study examined how social structure is implicitly represented after narrative learning, without requiring explicit relational judgments or navigation. Under these conditions, the prefrontal and inferior parietal regions may support the representation of abstract, semantic aspects of social relationship structure. Nonetheless, it remains possible that the hippocampus contributes to social structure processing when relational information must be explicitly recalled, compared, or navigated—particularly during memory-based reasoning about interpersonal dynamics within a narrative.

Limitations

The present paradigm did not explicitly require participants to evaluate or infer social relationships during scanning. Instead, our design allowed us to examine whether social relationship structure is implicitly encoded in neural representations of individual characters. Future studies that explicitly probe relationship representations, for example be presenting character pairs or requiring relational judgments, may further clarify the neural mechanisms underlying explicit social relationship processing.

Conclusion

“A man’s stature is determined by his enemies, not his friends,” says the politician character played by Al Pacino in City Hall (1996). Echoing this perspective, the present study revealed that the human brain preferentially encodes antagonistic aspects of interpersonal relationships. Using a narrative-based learning paradigm, we examined how the brain represents the structure of interpersonal relationships after participants watched social dramas. RSA revealed that antagonistic valence—rather than affiliative—was specifically represented in the prefrontal and inferior parietal lobe. These results suggest that the human brain constructs a multidimensional social map, with negative social connections serving as salient anchors. Such internal representations may support adaptive social understanding by enabling individuals to track complex interpersonal dynamics within group contexts.
