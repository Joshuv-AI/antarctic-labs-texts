Cardiac POCUS Use and Barriers Among Hospitalists at an Academic Level I Trauma Center

POCUS = point-of-care ultrasound.

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) refers to the use of ultrasonographic imaging performed at the patient's bedside by a treating physician for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes.1 POCUS is performed by the physician to evaluate multiple organ systems without the formal participation of ancillary staff or specialist physicians. As such, POCUS allows rapid diagnosis of patients, especially in an acute care setting. In addition, POCUS can be used to guide diagnosis and management of numerous cardiovascular presentations. POCUS also restores physician time at the bedside, and it has been increasingly recognized as a tool that allows physicians to make a clinical impression on the patient.

Project Rationale

Given a series of technological breakthroughs, the implementation of POCUS has become increasingly possible in terms of cost and clinical utility. One commonly appreciated barrier to POCUS adoption is a widespread lack of training, for which efforts have been escalating to make POCUS a part of the standard academic curricula.2 Nevertheless, challenges remain. One landmark survey found significant barriers in POCUS use, with obstacles relating to limitations in formal training, infrastructure, equipment, and clinician perceptions of its applicability.3 Another important consideration is that the improper use or interpretation of POCUS can lead to diagnostic insufficiencies and patient harm. The present study aims to evaluate the extent of POCUS use and to identify key challenges among hospitalists at the University of New Mexico Hospital, an academic level I trauma center.

Project Description

A survey to ascertain current practice patterns was conducted within the institution between November and December of 2025. The primary goals were to evaluate current POCUS practice patterns and identify barriers to POCUS use.

QuestionPro (QuestionPro Inc), a web-based survey tool, was used to generate an online questionnaire. The survey was then disseminated to adult hospitalists within the department of internal medicine and distributed via department-wide email, with recipients given 6 weeks to voluntarily participate. The emailed invitation included a description of the project and a link to the questionnaire.

The questionnaire included 27 questions, with careful emphasis on cardiac POCUS. Since cardiac applications of POCUS are the most widely practiced within the department of internal medicine at our institution, the questions were limited to cardiac POCUS specifically. The questions were designed to query individual knowledge and confidence, institutional support, attitudes toward the usefulness of cardiac POCUS, and barriers to its use. Possible responses were stratified by level of knowledge (not at all knowledgeable, somewhat knowledgeable, moderately knowledgeable, and extremely knowledgeable).

Inclusion criteria for participants were physicians employed through the academic department of internal medicine who practice strictly in the inpatient setting. Exclusion criteria were advanced practice practitioners, outpatient physicians within the department of internal medicine, hospitalists trained in family medicine, and nonacademic contract physicians practicing in internal medicine.

Project Deliverables

Outcome

The survey was distributed to 59 eligible participants in the department of internal medicine, of whom 27 responded, for a participation rate of 45.8% (Figure 1). Self-assessed knowledge level of cardiac POCUS varied across multiple domains, but in every case, only a minority of respondents reported feeling moderately or highly knowledgeable (Figure 2). Recognition of right ventricular strain or dysfunction showed the greatest knowledge deficit, with 63.0% of respondents reporting they were not at all knowledgeable. Additionally, a significant number of participants reported no knowledge of other POCUS skills including basic POCUS principles (37.0%), obtaining standard cardiac views (38.0%), evaluating left ventricular systolic function (41.0%), identifying pericardial effusion (41.0%), and assessing the inferior vena cava for volume status (33.0%). Among the domains assessed, respondents felt relatively more knowledgeable in evaluating the inferior vena cava for volume status and the basic principles of cardiac POCUS, with 33.0% and 38.0% participants reporting feeling moderately and extremely knowledgeable, respectively. In every domain, a majority (63.0%-74.0%) of the surveyed hospitalists reported being not at all to only somewhat knowledgeable.

A majority of respondents cited lack of formal training (52.0%), while several participants also cited clinical time constraints (22.0%) and limited access to ultrasound equipment (15.0%) as barriers (Figure 3). Most respondents (81.5%) agreed that formal training is necessary for safe and effective use of POCUS. A majority of participants (66.0%) stated they would be more likely to use POCUS if offered a pathway for formal credentialing or certification.

Beyond knowledge and barriers, participants also were asked about their perceptions of institution-wide POCUS use and their beliefs about its clinical utility. The vast majority of respondents (93.0%) believed that POCUS was underused within the institution as a whole, and only a minority (18.5%) felt that the institution provided adequate support for its use in daily practice. Most respondents (93.0%) believed that POCUS is a valuable tool for hospitalist practice, and many participants (59.3%) felt comfortable with the idea of incorporating it in management decisions. Participants believed that POCUS could increase diagnostic accuracy beyond physical examination alone (80.8%) and that POCUS can meaningfully improve patient outcomes (76.8%).

Impact

Our findings highlighted a significant gap between the recognized value of cardiac POCUS and its practical implementation among hospitalists, with limited formal training, restricted knowledge, and lack of equipment emerging as major barriers to routine use.

Future directions

These findings underscore the need for structured educational pathways, in particular curricula with an emphasis in cardiac applications. Furthermore, institutional investment in equipment, credentialing programs, and workflow integration are necessary interventions to support robust implementation of cardiac POCUS in inpatient medicine, predominantly in high-acuity and resource-limited settings such as our institution.

Discussion

Since the 1990s, POCUS has been increasingly implemented in acute care hospitals, with widely touted benefits. A number of clinical POCUS protocols have been developed and validated for use in the acute care setting.4,5 Although evidence remains mixed, several protocols have clearly demonstrated superior outcomes compared with standard of care such as physical examination alone, especially in resource-limited settings.6,7 For example, there are multiple studies in which the addition of POCUS to routine physical examination resulted in improvements in identifying cardiac dysfunction, valvular pathology, and unstable patients.3 Mozzini et al8 likewise demonstrated that performing serial POCUS examinations to guide diuretic management in patients with heart failure resulted in shorter hospitalizations. Meanwhile, other systemic reviews reveal that bedside ultrasound can change management plans in an estimated one-third of cases, though a majority of studies involve settings outside of internal medicine.6

Although this evidence exists, widescale implementation of this tool is accompanied by some concerns. One major worry has been user competence, with concern that improper POCUS use and interpretation can lead to false physician reassurance or patient harm.9 In 2019, in an effort to improve current practices and increase access to this important tool, the Society of Hospital Medicine issued a position statement calling for hospitalists to continue building POCUS capabilities with organizations, including an effort from the society and the American College of Physicians to provide introductory education opportunities.4

In this study, we found that the majority of respondents (77.8%) believe that POCUS can meaningfully improve patient care. Moreover, 96.3% viewed bedside ultrasound as an important tool, with 42.3% agreeing and 38.5% strongly agreeing that POCUS improves diagnostic accuracy compared with physical examination alone. Despite these positive beliefs, our findings showed that a majority of respondents (92.6%) thought POCUS was underused at our institution, with lack of formal training as the most important barrier (51.8%). When asked to reflect on POCUS skills among the group, 66.7% felt that peer hospitalists could not reliably perform or interpret cardiac POCUS. This finding is consistent with the Society of Hospital Medicine guidance that hospitalist POCUS competency requires both formal certification and longitudinal training, a sentiment shared by 81.5% of our participants.

A multicenter survey involving hospitalists found that the lack of training was a major barrier in 79% of respondents, with many expressing concerns that lack of training can lead to patient harm.6 Meanwhile, 78% reported that they would use POCUS more regularly if equipment and handheld devices were readily available,6 barriers which were confirmed by this study. Based on 51.8% of our respondents mentioning lack of training as a major challenge, it is unsurprising that a significant majority did not feel knowledgeable across multiple areas of cardiac POCUS (Figure 3).

The findings of this survey are lastly relevant to cardiologists because hospitalists' attitudes and knowledge of POCUS influence cardiology consultations and use of formal echocardiography, and they improve overall triage to appropriate levels of care.

Conclusions

Among adult hospitalists at an academic level I trauma center, lack of formal training and limited knowledge emerged as the greatest barriers to the use of cardiac POCUS. These findings underscore an important gap between the perceived value of POCUS and its practical implementation. The results clearly indicate that targeted educational opportunities, leadership engagement, and infrastructure support represent essential pillars to promote safe, effective, and sustainable practices of cardiac POCUS among hospitalists.
