The efficacy and safety of MSCs in GVHD prevention and the treatment of SR-aGVHD: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Shuzhou Wu, Wenming Lu, An Xie, Jiabing Wang, Wenhui Zeng, Cixiang Chen, Nan Wu, Junsong Ye, Zhengwei Zou, Lincai Li, Lin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is a cornerstone in the treatment of hematological disorders. However, its application is frequently complicated by acute and chronic graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD/cGVHD), pathological conditions in which donor-derived immune cells attack host tissues. With suboptimal survival rates and limited therapeutic options, GVHD remains a major clinical challenge. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic modality due to their immunomodulatory capabilities, yet standardized protocols for their use in preventing or treating GVHD have not been established.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library up to 10 February 2025 to identify eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Study selection was based on the PICOS framework, and the risk of bias was assessed using appropriate quality appraisal tools. Outcome data were systematically extracted and synthesized via meta-analysis.ResultsA total of 15 RCTs were included. The meta-analysis revealed that MSC administration significantly reduced the incidence of aGVHD (OR: 0.47; 95% CI 0.32–0.71; p = 0.00003) and cGVHD (OR: 0.50; 95% CI 0.34–0.74; p = 0.0005) compared with controls. MSC therapy was also associated with improved response rates in steroid-refractory aGVHD (SR-aGVHD) (OR: 1.50; 95% CI 1.04–2.17; p = 0.03).ConclusionMSCs demonstrate efficacy in preventing both aGVHD and cGVHD following HSCT, particularly in moderate to severe forms. A dose range of 1 × 10⁶ to < 4 × 10⁶ cells/kg was associated with optimal prophylactic outcomes. For SR-aGVHD, MSC infusion resulted in significantly higher remission rates compared to conventional treatments, especially in severe cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04955-0.

Introduction

Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is a common and serious complication of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) [1], which is characterized by the attack of donor immune cells on the recipient’s healthy tissues, leading to multisystemic damage [2, 3]. Allo-HSCT is an effective treatment for a wide range of hematological disorders [4, 5]; however, the prevalence of GVHD remains high, with rates of 13%-47% for aGVHD [6], and 30%-70% for cGVHD [3, 7]. Current prophylactic strategies predominantly involve pharmacological T-cell modulation, including anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) and anti-lymphocyte globulin (ALG) [8]. However, these interventions fail to substantially improve long-term survival outcomes [9]. In therapeutic contexts, systemic corticosteroids constitute the first-line standard of care for aGVHD, yet achieve response rates approximating 50%, with a substantial proportion of patients developing steroid-refractory disease associated with dismal prognoses [10, 11]. Second-line therapeutic agents demonstrate constrained efficacy (approximately 30% response rates) and lack standardized protocols [12]. In addition, less than half of the patients treated with steroids achieve complete remission, and more patients become steroid-refractory [10]. Therefore, exploring new therapeutic strategies to improve the survival and quality of life of GVHD patients is an important direction of current research.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), recognized for their immunomodulatory properties and low immunogenicity [13, 14], hold significant therapeutic potential in GVHD management. Derived from diverse tissue sources including bone marrow, umbilical cord, and placenta [15], MSCs exhibit robust self-renewal capacity, multipotent differentiation potential, and tissue-reparative functions [16, 17]. Clinical trials have demonstrated that MSCs infusion significantly reduces GVHD incidence, mitigates associated complications [18, 19], and maintains favorable safety profiles. A landmark advancement occurred on December 18, 2024, when the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Ryoncil® (remestemcel-L), developed by Mesoblast Limited, for treating steroid-refractory acute GVHD (SR-aGVHD) in pediatric patients aged ≥ 2 months [20]. However, most of the current studies are non-randomized controlled trials and lack the support of large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs). And issues regarding the optimal dose, infusion frequency and treatment timing of MSCs still need to be further explored. This study employs systematic review and meta-analysis methodologies to rigorously evaluate the efficacy and safety of MSCs infusion in GVHD management, with the dual objectives of identifying optimal clinical protocols and establishing evidence-based guidelines for GVHD prophylaxis and treatment.

Methods

The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO under registration number CRD420251059633 (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251059633). The authors declare that they have not use AI-generated work in this manuscript.

Search strategies

A comprehensive literature search was performed using the electronic databases Web of Science, PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library, covering publications in both Chinese and English. Relevant studies published from database inception to February 2025 were identified using the search terms “Mesenchymal Stem Cells” and “Graft-versus-Host Disease,” along with their corresponding MeSH terms, with the search syntax adapted using appropriate Boolean operators (AND/OR) for each database. The full search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Additional File 1. To minimize the risk of omitting relevant studies, manual searches of reference lists from included articles were also conducted.

Study selection

Two investigators independently screened titles, abstracts, and keywords to identify studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a consensus.

Inclusion criteria

Eligibility criteria were structured according to the PICOS framework:

Population (P)

Patients with hematological disorders undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), or those who developed grade II–IV steroid-refractory acute GVHD post-HSCT, regardless of geographic, ethnic, or demographic background.

Intervention (I)

Administration of Mesenchymal stem cells (MSC) infusion for prophylaxis or treatment.

Comparison (C)

Conventional pharmacotherapy or placebo.

Outcomes (O)

Prophylaxis

Primary: Incidence of acute and chronic GVHD (aGVHD/cGVHD).

Secondary: Overall survival (OS), neutrophil engraftment, platelet engraftment, disease-free survival (DFS)/failure-free survival (FFS), relapse rates, cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, relapse-associated mortality.

Treatment

Primary: Overall response rate (ORR).

Secondary: Day 28 complete response (CR), Day 28 disease control rate (DCR), OS.

Study Design (S): Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included.

Study Design (S)

This systematic review exclusively incorporates randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Exclusion criteria

Excluded were conference abstracts, duplicate publications, case reports, meta-analyses, commentaries, studies in languages other than Chinese or English, and articles with incomplete or inaccessible data. Studies not relevant to the research focus—such as those using animal models or non-MSC interventions—were also excluded.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted data into a standardized spreadsheet. The following information was collected: patient demographics (cohort size, age, sex, clinical diagnosis), MSC intervention details (dose, tissue source, frequency of administration), outcome measures, and study characteristics (authors, publication year, country, study design, follow-up period). Disagreements were resolved through consensus.

Assessment of the risk of Bias in the included studies

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool was applied using Review Manager (v5.4). Two reviewers independently assessed the following domains: (1) random sequence generation; (2) allocation concealment; (3) blinding of participants and personnel; (4) blinding of outcome assessment; (5) incomplete outcome data; (6) selective reporting; (7) other biases. Each item was rated as “low”, “high”, or “unclear” risk. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

Date analysis

Data were analyzed using Review Manager 5.4. Effect measures included odds ratios (OR) or mean differences (MD), each presented with 95% confidence intervals [21–23].Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. A random-effects model was applied if I² > 50% or p < 0.05; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used [24]. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted to explore sources of high heterogeneity. If heterogeneity remained unexplained, descriptive synthesis was performed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Forest plot

The forest plot presents a meta-analysis comparing MSC groups with non-MSC control groups for the prevention or treatment of GVHD. Each horizontal line represents the 95% confidence interval of an individual study. The wider the line, the greater the uncertainty of the result, and the arrowheads on the lines indicate the direction in which the interval extends when it exceeds the plotted area. The squares represent the OR or MD for each study, with their size proportional to the study’s weight in the meta-analysis. Blue squares denote outcomes when the effect measure is OR, while green squares indicate MD. The diamond symbols represent the pooled OR or MD for subgroup and overall effects, with the center indicating the point estimate and the width corresponding to the 95% confidence interval. The vertical solid line (OR = 1 or MD = 0) represents the line of no effect; if a confidence interval crosses this line, the result is not statistically significant. In this study, for the incidence of acute and chronic GVHD, an OR < 1 indicates a significant preventive benefit of MSCs, whereas for the overall response rate, an OR > 1 suggests superior therapeutic efficacy of MSCs.

Results

Results of the search

A systematic literature search across four electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library) identified 9,023 records: 1,486 from PubMed, 3,195 from Embase, 4,215 from Web of Science, and 127 from Cochrane Library. After removing 3,452 duplicates, 5,571 unique articles were screened based on title and abstract. Among these, 5,077 were excluded according to predefined criteria: 1,696 utilized animal models, 1,426 were reviews, commentaries, or previous meta-analyses, and 1,955 were irrelevant to the research focus. This left 494 articles for full-text assessment. After detailed evaluation, 424 studies were excluded for not being randomized controlled trials (RCTs), 32 were conference abstracts, 15 were clinical trial registrations without outcome data, and 8 were excluded due to unavailability of full text or insufficient data. Ultimately, 15 RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. The study selection process is summarized in a PRISMA-compliant flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the studies

Fifteen studies were included in the meta-analysis. Eleven evaluated Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) for graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis, and four investigated MSCs for the treatment of steroid-refractory acute GVHD. Studies were categorized into two groups: GVHD prophylaxis (11 studies) and steroid-refractory acute GVHD (SR-aGVHD) treatment (4 studies). Meta-analysis

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 eligible studies was performed, including 11 investigations on GVHD prophylaxis and 4 studies on steroid-refractory acute GVHD treatment, using fixed- or random-effects models and standard meta-analytic techniques.

Prophylaxis

Primary indicators

AGVHD

This meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of MSCs in preventing aGVHD across different severity grades. Analysis of nine studies (n = 499) showed a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 0.47 (95% CI 0.32–0.71, p = 0.00003) for reducing grades I-IV aGVHD, with low heterogeneity (I² = 8%). The protective effect was more pronounced for moderate-to-severe aGVHD (grades II-IV: OR = 0.42, p < 0.00001; grades III-IV: OR = 0.23, p = 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analyses confirmed efficacy regardless of MSC source, with significant results for both umbilical cord (UC-MSCs: OR = 0.43, p = 0.003) and bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs: OR = 0.53, p = 0.03) (Figure S3). Dose-response analysis revealed that medium (≥ 1.0 × 10⁶ to < 4.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg) and high-dose (≥ 4.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg) infusions were effective (OR = 0.48, p = 0.01 and OR = 0.38, p = 0.01, respectively), whereas low-dose (< 1.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg) regimens showed no significant benefit (OR = 1.09, p = 0.87) (Figure S4). All analyses exhibited minimal heterogeneity.

CGVHD

This meta-analysis demonstrated a significant protective effect of MSCs against chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD). The overall analysis (9 studies, n = 551) showed a significant reduction in cGVHD incidence (OR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.34–0.74, p = 0.0005, I² = 0%), with stronger efficacy for moderate-to-severe (OR = 0.43, p = 0.02) and severe (OR = 0.32, p = 0.004) manifestations (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analyses by source indicated significant efficacy for umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs: OR = 0.43, p = 0.0006), whereas bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) did not show a statistically significant effect (OR = 0.67, p = 0.26) (Figure S5). Dose-subgroup analysis revealed a significant benefit only for medium-dose infusions (≥ 1.0 × 10⁶ to < 4.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg; OR = 0.49, p = 0.007). Low-dose and high-dose regimens did not reach statistical significance. All subgroups exhibited low heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Figure S6).

Secondary indicators

OS

No significant improvement in overall survival was observed with MSC therapy. The pooled analysis showed non-significant effects for overall survival (OR = 1.29, p = 0.19), one-year survival (OR = 1.18, p = 0.70), and two-year survival (OR = 1.25, p = 0.38), with negligible heterogeneity across all analyses (I² = 0%) (Fig. 5).

Relapse

No significant increase in relapse incidence was observed with MSCs therapy (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.72–1.57, p = 0.77) (Fig. 6a).

CMV Infection

MSC infusion was not associated with an increased risk of CMV infection (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.52–1.26, p = 0.35) (Fig. 6b).

Relapse-associated mortality

No significant increase in relapse-associated mortality was observed with MSC infusion (OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 0.85–4.74, p = 0.11), although the point estimate suggested a potential trend toward increased risk (Fig. 6c).

Platelet engraftment

No significant difference in platelet engraftment was observed between groups (MD = -2.87, 95% CI: -6.55 to 0.82, p = 0.13), with substantial heterogeneity detected across studies (I² = 88%) (Fig. 7a). Sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Wu et al. showed complete resolution of heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 7b).

Neutrophil engraftment

No significant difference in neutrophil engraftment was observed between the MSC and control groups (MD = -3.71, 95% CI -4.65 to -2.77, p = 0.07), with significant heterogeneity detected across studies (I² = 96%) (Fig. 7c).

DFS/PFS

No significant improvement was observed in disease-free survival (OR = 0.97, p = 0.92) or progression-free survival (OR = 1.06, p = 0.90) (FigureS7).

Descriptive analysis

Immunological Reconstitution

In the investigation conducted by Xiang et al.., patients receiving Mesenchymal stem cells infusion demonstrated significant elevations in serum immunoglobulin levels (IgA, IgG, IgM, IgE) and peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets - including T-cell populations (CD4+, CD3 + CD4+, CD4 + CD8+) and B-cell counts - at 1, 3, and 6 months post-transplantation [25]. Contrastingly, Gao et al.. observed distinct immunomodulatory effects following MSCs administration, characterized by an expansion of memory B lymphocytes and regulatory T cells (Tregs), a heightened Th1:Th2 cell ratio, and a concomitant reduction in natural killer (NK) cell numbers. Adjustment of the Th1:Th2 cell ratio has been identified as a potential mechanism through which MSCs administration mitigates cGVHD [26].

Adverse Events

Multiple clinical investigations have consistently demonstrated that MSCs infusion is well-tolerated, with no immediate or long-term toxicity or treatment-related adverse events reported across trials [26–33]. Notably, in the study by Shipounova et al.., the majority of recipients exhibited transient pyrexia and rigors within 24 h post-infusion, though no severe complications were observed [34].

Treatment

Primary indicators

ORR

MSCs therapy demonstrated a significant improvement in overall response rate (ORR) compared to non-MSCs therapies, with a pooled OR of 1.50 (95% CI: 1.04–2.17, p = 0.03) and no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 8a). Subgroup analyses revealed greater efficacy in patients aged < 18 years (OR = 3.42, p = 0.01) compared to adults (OR = 1.18, p = 0.47) (Figure S8), and significant benefits were observed in those with higher disease severity (grades III-IV OR = 1.74, p = 0.02) (Figure S9) and multi-organ involvement (OR = 2.16, p = 0.003) (Figure S10). A significant response was also associated with cumulative doses < 16.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg (OR = 1.80, p = 0.03) (Figure S11). No significant improvements were found in patients with grade II aGVHD, single-organ involvement, or specific organ involvement subgroups (Figure S12). BM-MSCs showed a marginally significant effect (OR = 1.50, p = 0.03), while UC-MSCs did not reach significance (Figure S13).

Secondary indicators

OS

No significant improvement in overall survival was observed with MSC therapy (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 0.69–2.41, p = 0.43), with moderate heterogeneity detected across studies (I² = 59%) (Fig. 8b).

CR

MSC therapy was associated with significantly higher complete remission rates compared to controls (OR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.31–3.37, p = 0.002), with no heterogeneity observed across studies (I² = 0%) (Fig. 8c).

DCR

MSC therapy showed no significant improvement in disease control rate compared to controls (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 0.88–2.21, p = 0.15), with no heterogeneity across studies (I² = 0%). (Fig. 8d).

Descriptive analysis

FFS

Zhao et al.. demonstrated significantly prolonged FFS in the Mesenchymal stem cells cohort compared to the control group (11.3 months vs. 6.0 months) [35]. Furthermore, Jiang et al.. reported marginally higher 2-year FFS rates in the MSCs -treated cohort within their per-protocol analysis framework, though statistical significance thresholds were not fully attained [36].

Adverse events

No instances of MSCs infusion-related toxicity or treatment-associated ectopic tissue formation have been reported across clinical evaluations [37, 38]. Commonly observed adverse events during therapeutic interventions included peripheral edema, abdominal pain, cytopenias (thrombocytopenia, leukopenia), cytomegalovirus viremia, anemia, and pyrexia [35–37]. Notably, Kebriaei et al.. documented comparably low rates of treatment-emergent adverse events potentially related to remestemcel-L administration versus placebo (1.2% vs. 3.7%, respectively) [37]. In contrast, Zhao et al.. reported grade 3–4 infections in 65 patients (65.7%) within the MSCs cohort versus 78 patients (78.8%) receiving conventional therapy, alongside a significantly lower incidence of hematological toxicity in MSCs recipients (37 cases, 37.4%) compared to controls (53 cases, 53.5%; p < 0.05) [35]. The incidence of serious adverse events was similar between the MSCs and control groups, and there were no serious adverse events related to the investigational agent [35–37].

Discussion

Over the past decades, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation has remained a critical therapeutic approach for various malignant and non-malignant hematological disorders [39, 40]. However, the clinical efficacy of this procedure may be compromised by several severe complications, particularly graft-versus-host disease, a potentially fatal complication unique to allo-HSCT [1, 8]. In clinical practice, patients receive radiotherapy/chemotherapy-based preconditioning regimens to prevent or manage GVHD. Nevertheless, current therapeutic strategies have shown limited efficacy in reducing GVHD incidence or mortality rates. Mesenchymal stem cells have demonstrated considerable potential in both GVHD prevention and treatment due to their immunomodulatory properties.

Our study exclusively included RCTs and incorporated five newly published studies from 2020 onwards for analysis. Additionally, there has been no prior meta-analysis specifically focusing on RCTs of MSCs for treating SR-aGVHD. While existing meta-analyses support the safety of MSCs application in GVHD and indicate their potential in preventing cGVHD, the optimal MSC dosage and source remain undefined [41–44]. Our findings demonstrated that MSCs prevent not only cGVHD but also aGVHD. This meta-analysis specifically investigated the impact of MSC dosage and source on their efficacy in preventing GVHD and treating SR-aGVHD. A dosage range of ≥ 1.0 × 10⁶ to < 4.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg appeared to be more effective for GVHD prevention. Furthermore, BM-MSCs were associated with a more significant overall response rate in the treatment of SR-aGVHD. These findings provide valuable guidance for the design of future clinical trials.

The preconditioning regimens involving chemotherapy and radiotherapy prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation inflict substantial damage to the bone marrow hematopoietic microenvironment [45, 46], resulting in delayed post-transplant hematopoietic reconstitution, elevated risks of infection and hemorrhage, and potential graft failure [47, 48]. Mesenchymal stem cells MSCs have been shown to promote hematopoietic reconstitution through the modulation of hematopoietic cell physiological activity [49]. Accumulating evidence indicates that MSCs secrete diverse hematopoietic growth factors [50–52], including granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) [53], which collectively enhance the proliferation and differentiation of hematopoietic cells, thereby accelerating the restoration of hematopoietic function. Furthermore, MSCs facilitate hematopoietic recovery via cytokine-mediated mechanisms, such as the secretion of stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), which induces homing of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) to the bone marrow niche [54, 55]. This mechanism contributes to the restoration of physiological SDF-1 levels in the bone marrow microenvironment, effectively mitigating chemotherapy-induced toxicity and shortening hematopoietic recovery time. Clinical investigations corroborate these findings, as exemplified by a study by Wang et al.., in which 35 pediatric patients with severe aplastic anemia (SAA) underwent haploidentical HSCT combined with donor-derived bone marrow MSCs co-transplantation, achieving successful hematopoietic reconstitution in all cases [30]. These collective observations underscore the significant therapeutic potential of MSCs in enhancing hematopoietic recovery. Nevertheless, our meta-analysis using a random-effects model showed no significant difference in median time to platelet or neutrophil engraftment, and this is likely due to the limited number of studies and small sample sizes that underpowered the analysis.

Mesenchymal stem cells exhibit remarkable immunomodulatory potential, primarily mediated through the secretion of diverse cytokines and chemokines that suppress the proliferation and activation of T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, thereby exerting immunosuppressive effects [56, 57]. Furthermore, MSCs regulate immune cell functionality via direct cell-cell interactions and paracrine mechanisms, such as exosome secretion, which collectively promote tissue repair and regeneration [58, 59]. Mesenchymal stem cells exert potent inhibitory effects on T cell proliferation and activation by secreting anti-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) and immunosuppressive molecules (e.g., IDO, PD-L1) [60], thereby suppressing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, MSCs modulate immune responses through the secretion of extracellular vesicles (EVs), which are enriched with diverse bioactive molecules, including miRNAs and proteins [61, 62]. These molecular components orchestrate immune cell functionality, thereby attenuating inflammatory cascades and facilitating tissue regeneration. Additionally, MSCs foster immune tolerance by promoting the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and regulatory B cells (Bregs), further amplifying their immunoregulatory efficacy [63, 64].Notably, functional heterogeneity exists among MSCs derived from different tissue sources. Comparative studies demonstrate that bone marrow-derived MSCs and adipose tissue-derived MSCs (AT-MSCs) exhibit superior capacity to inhibit lymphocyte proliferation compared to umbilical cord blood-derived MSCs, weathers UC-MSCs and AT-MSCs induce a higher Treg/Th17 cell ratio [30]. The polyclonal nature of MSCs introduces additional complexity, as distinct clonal subpopulations display variability in morphological features [57, 65], proliferative capacity, and functional properties - differences that may critically influence their therapeutic outcomes in disease-specific contexts. Consequently, the systematic identification of MSCs subpopulations that optimally synergize hematopoietic support with immunomodulatory activity remains a pivotal unresolved challenge in translational research.

This meta-analysis further substantiates that mesenchymal stem cell infusion significantly reduces the incidence of chronic graft-versus-host disease following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, while also demonstrating efficacy in mitigating acute GVHD. Subgroup analyses indicated that UC-MSCs demonstrated a superior trend in preventing cGVHD, while MSCs in general exhibited protective effects against severe manifestations of both aGVHD and cGVHD. Notably, despite the marked reduction in GVHD risk associated with MSCs administration, no corresponding improvement in long-term survival rates was observed. The absence of survival benefit may be attributed to multiple factors including patient clinical characteristics, pre-transplant disease status, post-transplant complications, disease relapse, and infection-related variables [66, 67]. A 2020 meta-analysis indicated that prophylactic administration of allogeneic MSCs in patients undergoing HSCT improved survival probability, which contrasts with our findings [41]. However, this conclusion might be compromised by limited research, and larger multi-center randomized controlled trials are required in the future to further clarify the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs.

Critical determinants of OS, beyond GVHD, prominently include infectious complications and disease recurrence [68, 69]. Our analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between MSCs groups and control groups in terms of relapse rates or cytomegalovirus infection incidence, indicating that MSCs administration does not exacerbate these risks.

Among included studies, the 1.0–4.0 × 10^6 cells/kg range represented the predominant administration regimen [28, 30, 32, 34, 70]. Furthermore, while only one study employed doses below this range [27], it was nevertheless categorized into a separate subgroup for analytical completeness. Our analysis revealed that low-dose MSCs infusions (< 1.0 × 10^6 cells/kg) demonstrated insufficient efficacy in preventing both aGVHD and cGVHD. Furthermore, in the high-dose subgroup (≥ 4.0 × 10^6 cells/kg), MSCs administration showed no statistically significant preventive effect against chronic GVHD. Notably, medium-dose MSCs administration (≥ 1.0 × 10^6 cells/kg to < 4.0 × 10^6 cells/kg) demonstrated potential efficacy in preventing both acute and chronic GVHD, suggesting this range may represent an optimal therapeutic window. However, these preliminary observations necessitate rigorous validation through large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials to establish definitive clinical recommendations.

Although only four studies investigating MSCs therapy for steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease were included [35–38], the pooled analysis encompassed 548 participants, lending substantial statistical power to the meta-analysis. Our primary endpoint focused on the overall response rate, defined as a composite metric incorporating complete response and partial response (PR). Given the rapid progression and high lethality of SR-aGVHD, therapeutic efficacy must be evaluated within a clinically actionable timeframe. Consequently, ORR was assessed at 28 days post-treatment - a standardized interval enabling timely evaluation of target organ improvements (e.g., intestinal, cutaneous, hepatic involvement) and preventing delays in therapeutic adjustments [20, 71]. Meta-analytic results demonstrated statistically significant differences in CR and ORR rates between the MSC-treated and control groups. However, no significant improvement in OS was observed. This discrepancy may be attributable to post-response mortality from infections or disease relapse in patients achieving short-term ORR [72], despite transient clinical improvements. Notably, existing evidence suggests a correlation between ORR attainment and prolonged median survival [38], though data addressing this association remain limited, warranting further investigation in future studies.

The therapeutic efficacy of Mesenchymal stem cells exhibits considerable heterogeneity. We conducted subgroup analyses stratified by total MSC dose, tissue origin, patient age, steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease severity grade, involved organ systems, and number of affected organs. In the BM-MSCs subgroup, the odds ratio was 1.50 with a p-value of 0.05, indicating a potential therapeutic advantage for the MSCs group. However, the generalizability of these findings requires further validation through large-scale randomized controlled trials in clinical settings. Subgroup analysis stratified by dose showed that a cumulative dose of < 16.0 × 10^6 cells/kg was associated with a significantly higher clinical response rate, whereas the cohort receiving ≥ 16.0 × 10^6 cells/kg exhibited no statistically significant therapeutic advantage, suggesting a potential plateau effect in MSC dose-response relationships beyond a critical threshold. Notably, pediatric patients (< 18 years) exhibited statistically significant improvements in treatment outcomes, potentially attributable to heightened immune plasticity in this demographic, which may enhance MSCs-mediated induction of immune tolerance. Furthermore, MSC therapy conferred greater clinical benefits in patients with severe SR-aGVHD, with emerging evidence suggesting prophylactic efficacy against progression to high-grade GVHD - a mechanistic basis warranting further elucidation. Intriguingly, subgroup analysis of affected organ systems revealed a trend toward enhanced efficacy in intestinal involvement, approaching statistical significance. Preliminary data from a global multicenter Phase III trial corroborate these findings, demonstrating pronounced gastrointestinal symptom alleviation, potentially mediated through MSCs immunomodulatory effects on gut microbiota homeostasis or paracrine-mediated enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity [37].

Mesenchymal stem cells have demonstrated significant efficacy in preventing graft-versus-host disease and have achieved notable advancements in the management of steroid-refractory acute GVHD. However, the clinical application of MSCs continues to face standardization challenges, particularly regarding the impact of critical variables such as tissue source, dosing regimens, timing and frequency of administration, and recipient-specific factors on therapeutic outcomes [73, 74]. Current heterogeneity in MSCs sources and dosing protocols across clinical studies has hindered cross-trial comparability and limited the generalizability of findings [75, 76]. Furthermore, unresolved issues persist in MSCs manufacturing protocols and quality control metrics [77], necessitating rigorous standardization to ensure batch-to-batch consistency and functional reproducibility. While short-term clinical benefits of MSC therapy are well-documented in multiple trials, long-term safety profiles and sustained efficacy remain inadequately characterized [78], necessitating systematic evaluation through extended longitudinal follow-up studies.

Currently, in vitro priming, bioengineering, or genetic engineering of MSCs represents a feasible strategy to enhance their clinical efficacy. Transforming MSCs from simple functional cell products into functionally enhanced or tailored cellular preparations has become a key point for next-generation MSC-based therapies. Priming with active peptides or pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ has been shown to improve MSC survival, targeting persistence, and cytokine secretion [79, 80]. Overexpression of CXCR4 has been demonstrated to significantly enhance MSC migration to inflammatory and bone marrow sites, thereby prolonging their therapeutic activity [81]. Similarly, NurOwn - a small molecule “programmed” MSC product - has been shown to deliver multiple neurotrophic factors (NTFs) while concurrently secreting immunomodulatory components typically produced by MSCs [82]. Through refined preconditioning or precise genetic modification, it is expected that multi-potency, controllable, and clinically adaptable “next-generation MSCs” can be developed. These advancements are expected to serve as a key driver in establishing more effective MSC therapy in treatment for GVHD, ultimately extending its benefits to a broader patient population.

This meta-analysis systematically evaluated the efficacy and safety profile of mesenchymal stem cells in both preventing graft-versus-host disease and treating steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease, providing evidence-based recommendations for clinical application. However, several methodological limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the findings. Primarily, heterogeneity existed among the included studies - evident of variations disease subtypes and stages, sources of hematopoietic stem cells and mesenchymal stem cells, administration regimens, follow-up duration, and study design - which may affect the generalizability of the pooled results. Furthermore, differences in reported outcome measures and incomplete data from the included studies may lead to discrepancies between specific outcome data and the overall results. Additionally, the limited number of studies on mesenchymal stem cell therapy for steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease, combined with variations in sample sizes across trials, constrained the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Finally, the lack of standardized outcome measures may also limit the strength of the conclusions, and the use of overall response rate as the primary efficacy endpoint for steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease has inherent limitations, as it failed to account for long-term treatment-related toxicities or relapse risk [83]. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal biomarker analyses and extended follow-up to establish a comprehensive framework that captures both short- and long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

Mesenchymal stem cells infusion demonstrates significant efficacy in preventing both acute and chronic GVHD in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation recipients, particularly for moderate-to-severe manifestations. Subgroup analyses suggest UC-MSCs may offer superior prevention of chronic GVHD compared to other sources. A medium-dose range (≥ 1.0 × 10⁶ to < 4.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg) appears optimal for GVHD prophylaxis. In steroid-refractory acute GVHD, MSC therapy significantly improves treatment response, with enhanced efficacy observed in younger patients (< 18 years), those with severe (grades III-IV) disease, and cases involving the gastrointestinal tract or multiple organs. However, higher cumulative doses (≥ 16.0 × 10⁶ cells/kg) did not yield additional benefit. While MSC prophylaxis was not associated with increased relapse or CMV infection, a non-significant trend toward elevated relapse-associated mortality warrants careful monitoring. These findings support the therapeutic potential of MSCs in GVHD management but highlight the need for standardized protocols validated in large-scale randomized trials.
