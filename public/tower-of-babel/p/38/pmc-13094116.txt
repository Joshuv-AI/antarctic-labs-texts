Molecular mechanisms and therapeutic potential of tryptophan metabolism in gut-brain signaling transduction: a narrative review
Authors: Jiuba Zhang, Yanan Zhou, Xi Mei, Shuai Yan, Jing Mao, Yu Li, Zhenhua Bian, Lin Li, De Ji, Tulin Lu, Juxiang Chen, Lianlin Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs an essential amino acid, tryptophan (Trp) serves as a pivotal mediator in gut-brain axis (GBA) communication through three primary metabolic pathways: kynurenine (Kyn), indole, and serotonin (5-HT), which together regulate neuroimmune and neuroendocrine homeostasis via the vagus and spinal afferent nerves, circulatory system, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. This review systematically examines Trp metabolism’s critical roles in GBA, emphasizing molecular pathways, rate-limiting enzymes, and receptor-mediated signaling. We discuss the bidirectional interplay between gut microbiota and host Trp metabolism, encompassing microbial modulation of host enzyme activities such as indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase and direct production of bioactive indole derivatives like indole-3-propionic acid. Characteristic disruptions in Trp metabolism patterns are identified across GBA-associated disorders including irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, depression, Alzheimer’s disease, schizophrenia and Parkinson’s disease, marked by aberrant neurotoxic to neuroprotective metabolite ratios and enzymatic dysregulation. The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) emerges as a molecular hub connecting Trp metabolites to GBA functions, with distinct metabolites eliciting opposing effects through AhR activation. Therapeutic strategies targeting Trp metabolism are critically evaluated, including fecal microbiota transplantation, probiotic supplementation, metabolite administration, and enzyme inhibitors. Future research directions address mechanistic gaps and translational challenges in restoring GBA homeostasis via Trp pathway modulation.Keywords: Tryptophan metabolism, Gut-brain axis, Aryl hydrocarbon receptor, Vagus nerve, Gut microbiota

Background

Contemporary life sciences no longer regard the brain as an isolated organ but rather as an integral component of host–microbe ecosystem that is functionally coupled to the gut microbiota through bidirectional signaling. This bidirectional communication network, termed the gut-brain axis (GBA), integrates neural, endocrine, immune, inflammatory, and metabolic pathways to connect the gastrointestinal tract with the central nervous system (CNS) [1]. Over the past decade, research has progressed from establishing the existence of this connection to deciphering its molecular language. In this context, tryptophan (Trp)—an essential amino acid and a precursor for multiple bioactive metabolites—has emerged as a key messenger in GBA communication [2].

Trp exerts its influence through a complex and highly compartmentalized metabolic network shared between the host and commensal microbes. This network is classically divided into three major pathways: the kynurenine (Kyn) pathway, the indole pathway, and the serotonin (5-HT) pathway [3]. Central to understanding these routes are their rate-limiting enzymes and tightly coordinated catalytic control. More than 95% of dietary Trp is metabolized via the Kyn pathway, initiated by tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO, primarily hepatic) and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO, broadly expressed in immune cells). These heme-dependent dioxygenases catalyze the first step to generate metabolites with “double-edged sword” properties, such as the neuroexcitotoxic quinolinic acid (QA) and neuroprotective kynurenic acid (KYNA) [4, 5]. Importantly, subsequent branching of the Kyn pathway is shaped by competitive enzymatic flux, particularly between kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO) and kynurenine aminotransferase (KAT). Their tissue- and cell-type-specific expression patterns govern the balance between neurotoxic and neuroprotective metabolite pools, thereby influencing neuroinflammation and susceptibility to psychiatric and neurological disorders [6]. However, it remains unclear whether targeting this competitive enzymatic flux could lead to clinically relevant therapeutic outcomes, as shifting the balance in either direction may have unintended consequences. In parallel, the indole pathway represents a microbiota-dependent route that is largely restricted to the intestinal lumen. Microbial enzymes such as tryptophanase, aromatic amino acid transaminase (ArAT), and tryptophan decarboxylase convert Trp into a diverse array of indole derivatives, including indole−3-propionic acid (IPA) and indole−3-aldehyde (IAld). These molecules serve as signaling ligands for host receptors, most notably the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), thereby regulating epithelial barrier integrity, mucosal immune responses, and microglial function, and enabling microbial metabolic states to be transduced into neuroimmune outputs [7, 8]. By contrast, only 1–2% of Trp is routed toward 5-HT biosynthesis, a critical monoaminergic neurotransmitter in the brain and a signaling molecule in the gut. 5-HT production is controlled by tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), which exists as two isoforms: TPH1 in peripheral tissues and TPH2 in the CNS. Both isoforms require tetrahydrobiopterin and Fe2+ as obligate cofactors for catalytic activity [9]. 5-HT clearance and catabolism are further constrained by monoamine oxidase (MAO). The strict compartmentalization and regulatory control of these enzymes dictate the synthesis and metabolism of 5-HT, thereby fine-tuning mood, cognition, and gastrointestinal motility.

Notably, these three metabolic pathways do not operate in isolation. Instead, they engage in dynamic competition and crosstalk shaped by host genetics, immune tone, environmental stressors, and—critically—the gut microbiota. Microbes not only consume Trp directly but also regulate host rate-limiting enzymes like IDO1 via their metabolites or cellular components, remodeling the Trp metabolism [10, 11]. Collectively, these interactions position Trp metabolism as an enzyme-driven and highly plastic biochemical interface for gut-brain signaling. Dysregulation of this orchestrated enzymatic network has been implicated across a broad disease spectrum. Disorders including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), depression, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), schizophrenia, and Parkinson’s disease (PD) frequently exhibit characteristic Trp metabolic disturbances, often rooted in abnormal expression of rate-limiting enzymes that disrupt metabolite ratios [2, 3, 12–15]. These alterations are not mere epiphenomena but central drivers of pathogenesis [16, 12]. Nevertheless, the understanding of how enzymes are contextually regulated in distinct cells and pathologies—and how their catalytic products precisely modulate neuroimmune circuits—remains fragmentary. This context dependence remains a major barrier to translation.

This review systematically examines the role of Trp metabolism in GBA regulation, with an emphasis on molecular pathways, rate-limiting enzymes, and receptor-mediated signaling mechanisms underlying its three metabolic routes. We further discuss the interplay between the gut microbiota and host Trp metabolism and summarize observed metabolic alterations in neurological and gastrointestinal disorders. Potential therapeutic interventions, such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), probiotic supplementation, exogenous metabolite administration, and enzyme inhibitors, are also discussed. Finally, we outline future research priorities to address critical gaps in mechanistic understanding and challenges in clinical translation.

Tryptophan and its metabolic network

Kynurenine pathway

The three metabolic pathways of Trp are illustrated in Fig. 1. The Kyn pathway primarily operates in the liver, CNS, and immune cells, including macrophages and dendritic cells. This pathway converts Trp into neurotoxic and neuroprotective metabolites through a series of enzymatic reactions, while the balance between these metabolites is crucial for maintaining GBA homeostasis. Trp is initially catalyzed by IDO or TDO, producing N-formylkynurenine (NFK), which is subsequently deformylated to Kyn by kynurenine formamidase (AFMID). As a key metabolite with blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, Kyn links peripheral and central metabolic regulation. In the CNS, Kyn metabolism occurs in a cell-type-dependent manner. In astrocytes (ASTs), Kyn is converted into KYNA, while in microglial cells, it is metabolized into 3-hydroxykynurenine (3-HK) and QA [17]. Although Kyn itself does not exhibit direct neurotoxicity, its accumulation shifts the metabolic pathway toward the production of neurotoxic metabolites (3-HK and QA), which are considered biomarkers of depression and schizophrenia [18]. From Kyn, the pathway diverges into three branches: 3-HK, KYNA, and anthranilic acid (AA). 3-HK, which crosses the BBB, induces neurotoxicity by promoting free radical generation and neuronal apoptosis [19, 20]. KYNA, though unable to penetrate the BBB, exerts neuroprotective effects by antagonizing N-Methyl-D-aspartic acid (NMDA) receptors, counteracting excitotoxic metabolites like QA [21]. In the gut, KYNA activates GPR35 receptor to support mucosal integrity and immune regulation [9]. AA exhibits dual neurobiological roles: lower AA levels are associated with an increased risk of stroke, whereas elevated levels are found in schizophrenia and PD [22, 23]. The 3-HK branch generates a complex metabolic network, producing metabolites such as 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3-HAA), alanine, pyruvate, xanthurenic acid (XA), α-amino-β-carboxymuconic-ε-semialdehyde (ACMS), QA, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), and picolinic acid (PICA). 3-HK is metabolized to 3-HAA, XA, and alanine. 3-HAA is known for its broad bioactivities, including antitumor, anti-aging, and anti-atherosclerotic effects [24–27], while XA activates the AhR to modulate inflammation [28, 29]. ACMS, the downstream product of 3-HAA, can either be processed into QA (a precursor in NAD+ biosynthesis) or α-aminomuconic semialdehyde (AMS), which enters the tricarboxylic acid cycle. QA induces excitotoxic neuronal death through NMDA receptor activation, while NAD+ plays a crucial role in energy metabolism, redox balance, and DNA repair [30, 31]. Other Kyn pathway branches are relatively simpler: AA re-enters the 3-HK branch via 3-HAA, and KYNA is metabolized to kynuramine or 5-hydroxykynurenamine (5-HKYNU).

Three metabolic pathways of Trp: the Kyn pathway, indole pathway, and 5-HT pathway

Indole pathway

In contrast to the host-dominated Kyn pathway, the indole pathway is driven by the gut microbiota, which directly catabolizes Trp into indole and its derivatives, competing with host pathways for Trp availability. Core metabolites of this pathway include indole, indole−3-pyruvate (IPYA), indole−3-lactic acid (ILA), indole−3-acetaldehyde (IAAld), indole−3-acetic acid (IAA), IAld, indole acrylic acid (IACA), IPA, indoxyl sulfate (IS), indoxyl, and skatole. These metabolites contribute to gastrointestinal homeostasis and influence central neuromodulation. The first-tier metabolites derived from microbial Trp metabolism branch into four routes: IPYA, indole, indole−3-acetamide (IAM), and tryptamine (TRY). IPYA serves as an intermediate that can be decarboxylated by indole−3-pyruvate decarboxylase (IPYAD) to form IAAld or reduced by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)-dependent indole lactate dehydrogenase to generate ILA. The secondary metabolites, comprising IAAld (from TRY or IPYA), ILA (from IPYA), IAA (from IAM or IAAld), and indoxyl (from indole) form a largely linear network. Subsequent transformations yield tertiary metabolites, including IS (derived from indoxyl sulfation), IACA (from ILA decarboxylation), skatole and IAld. IS, a uremic toxin, contributes to chronic kidney disease progression by inducing platelet hyperactivation and thrombosis, while suppressing angiogenesis via hypoxia-inducible factor/IL−10/vascular endothelial growth factor signaling [32, 33]. The fourth tier yields IPA, which derived from IACA. These metabolic steps highlight the multi-stage nature of microbiota-derived indole metabolism and its complex crosstalk within the host–microbe interface.

Serotonin pathway

5-HT is predominantly synthesized in enterochromaffin cells of the gastrointestinal tract and CNS neurons, with the highest concentrations in the gut, followed by platelets, and the lowest levels in the CNS. Peripheral 5-HT cannot cross the BBB and is primarily synthesized in peripheral tissues, whereas CNS 5-HT is locally produced from Trp [34]. Although peripheral 5-HT does not directly influence CNS 5-HT levels due to BBB limitations, it can exert indirect effects on the CNS [35]. In the gut, Trp is converted to 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP), the immediate precursor of 5-HT, primarily by TPH1 expressed in enterochromaffin cells. Additionally, a subpopulation of neurons within the enteric nervous system expresses TPH2 and contributes to intestinal 5-HT synthesis [36, 37]. In the CNS, 5-HT is predominantly synthesized in the raphe nuclei, where TPH2 plays a central role. Both gut and neuronal 5-HTP are decarboxylated to 5-HT by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC) and pyridoxal−5’-phosphate (vitamin B6) serving as a cofactor. Intestinal 5-HT is stored in secretory vesicles via vesicular monoamine transporter 1 and released through exocytosis [36, 37], with approximately 10% of 5-HT originating from enteric neurons. Notably, the serotonin transporter (SERT) plays a critical role in regulating 5-HT homeostasis. In the gut, SERT is expressed in both enteric neurons and epithelial cells, where it regulates local 5-HT levels by clearing excess 5-HT released from enterochromaffin cells [38]. In the CNS, SERT mediates the reuptake of 5-HT from the synaptic cleft into presynaptic neurons, a process crucial for terminating serotonergic signaling and maintaining 5-HT levels in the synaptic cleft [39]. 5-HT is not an endpoint metabolite. It is oxidized by MAO-A to form 5-hydroxyindoleacetaldehyde (5-HIAL), which is further metabolized to 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) by aldehyde dehydrogenase [40]. Alternatively, arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AANAT) acetylates 5-HT to N-acetyl−5-HT, which is subsequently methylated by hydroxyindole-O-methyltransferase (HIOMT) to yield melatonin [41].

While the three metabolic pathways of Trp are largely independent, cross-talk exists between 5-HT and Kyn pathways, with IDO1 converting 5-HT and 5-HTP into 5-HKYNU and 5-hydroxykynurenine, linking these two pathways [40–42].

Enzymes and catalytic mechanisms in tryptophan metabolism

The precise catalytic action of key enzymes underpins the functional execution of each Trp metabolic pathway. Below, we provide an in-depth overview of the structural features, cofactor dependencies, and catalytic mechanisms of the major enzymes involved in the Kyn, indole, and 5-HT pathways.

Rate-limiting enzymes and reactions in the kynurenine pathway

Both IDO and TDO are heme-dependent dioxygenases that require heme as a crucial cofactor. TDO is predominantly expressed in the liver, while IDO is distributed across a wide range of tissues. Both enzymes recognize the indole ring and carboxyl group of Trp through their substrate-binding domains, but they differ in substrate specificity: IDO acts on free Trp and short Trp-containing peptides, whereas TDO exclusively targets free Trp [43]. Mechanistically, IDO and TDO catalyze oxygen insertion into the indole ring of Trp, forming an unstable epoxide intermediate, which spontaneously opens to yield NFK [44]. NFK is subsequently hydrolyzed by AFMID, an oamidase family member characterized by α-helical and β-sheet structural elements AFMID employs a catalytic triad (Ser157, His276, Asp244) to activate water molecules, cleaving the amide bond to produce Kyn and formic acid [45]. Kynureninase, a PLP-dependent enzyme within the transaminase superfamily, cleaves Cβ–Cγ bond in Kyn or 3-HK through proton abstraction and water activation, generating AA or 3-HAA and L-alanine [46, 47]. KAT, another PLP-dependent enzyme, coordinates PLP in its active site via a lysine residue. It transfers the substrate’s α-amino group to PLP, forming an α-keto acid intermediate that condenses to yield KYNA or XA [48]. KMO, a flavoprotein monooxygenase, utilizes flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and NADPH to activate molecular oxygen into a reactive oxygen intermediate (O2-) that hydroxylates Kyn at the C3 position to produce 3-HK [49]. 3-Hydroxyanthranilic acid oxidase (3-HAO) is a non-heme iron-dependent dioxygenase, acts on 3-HAA by recognizing its hydroxyl and amino groups. It utilizes Fe²⁺-activated oxygen to generate a superoxide intermediate, which cleaves the aromatic ring between C3 and C4 positions, yielding ACMS [50]. Alanine transaminase, a PLP-dependent transaminase, transfers the α-amino group of alanine to PLP to generate pyridoxamine phosphate and pyruvate [51]. ACMS decarboxylase (ACMSD), a zinc-dependent metalloenzyme, facilitates ACMS decarboxylation through Zn²⁺-mediated polarization of the carboxyl group, yielding AMS [52]. Quinolinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase (QPRT), a phosphoribosyltransferase family member, converts QA and 5-phosphoribosyl−1-pyrophosphate (PRPP) to nicotinic acid mononucleotide, a pivotal intermediate in NAD+ biosynthesis [53].

Rate-limiting enzymes and reactions in the indole pathway

Tryptophan decarboxylase, a PLP-dependent decarboxylase, catalyzes Trp decarboxylation by forming an external aldimine intermediate, followed by α-carboxyl group decarboxylation and Tyr348-mediated protonation to yield TRY [54]. ArAT, another PLP-dependent enzyme, transfers the Trp amino group to PLP, generating pyridoxamine phosphate and IPYA through external aldimine formation [55]. Tryptophanase, also PLP-dependent, eliminates Trp’s β-substituent after external aldimine formation, producing indole [56]. IPYAD, a thiamine pyrophosphate-dependent α-keto acid decarboxylase, forms an enamine intermediate upon thiamine pyrophosphate-carbonyl interaction. This leads to CO2 release and proton transfer, producing hydroxyethyl-thiamine pyrophosphate and ultimately yielding IAAld [57]. Indole lactate dehydrogenase, an NADH-dependent enzyme, reduces IPYA’s carbonyl to hydroxyl via NADH-mediated hydride transfer, producing ILA [58], which undergoes further dehydrogenation to IACA. CYPZE1, a heme- and NADH-dependent cytochrome P450 monooxygenase, hydroxylates indole at the C3 position. This process involves NADH-driven heme iron reduction, O2 activation into a reactive Fe4+=O intermediate, and radical-mediated substrate oxidation to indoxyl [59]. Indole−3-acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (IAADH), an NAD+-dependent aldehyde dehydrogenase, oxidizes IAAld through nucleophilic attack by a catalytic cysteine thiol, forming a thioester intermediate that hydrolyzes to IAA [60]. Indole−3-acetic acid decarboxylase (IAAD), a glycine radical enzyme, decarboxylates IAA to skatole by transferring a proton, generating an anionic radical intermediate [61]. Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, an FAD-dependent flavoprotein in mitochondria and peroxisomes, likely converts IACA to IPA through dehydrogenation, hydration, re-dehydrogenation, and thioester cleavage [62].

Rate-limiting enzymes and reactions in the serotonin pathway

TPH, a enzyme in the aromatic amino acid hydroxylase family, exists as two isoforms: TPH1 and TPH2. These isoforms catalyze hydroxylation at the C5 position of Trp’s indole ring to generate 5-HTP, a reaction that requires tetrahydrobiopterin and Fe²⁺ as cofactors [63]. AADC, a PLP-dependent enzyme, specifically produces dopamine and 5-HT by catalyzing the α-carboxyl group decarboxylation of 5-HTP with PLP as a cofactor [64]. MAO, a flavoprotein containing FAD-binding and substrate-binding domains, has two subtypes: MAO-A and MAO-B. MAO-A predominantly metabolizes 5-HT, norepinephrine, and dopamine, while MAO-B mainly oxidizes benzylamine, dopamine, and phenethylamine [65, 66]. During 5-HT metabolism by MAO-A, binding to the active site reduces FAD to FADH₂, converting 5-HT into an imine intermediate that spontaneously hydrolyzes to 5-HIAL upon enzyme release [67]. AANAT, a member of the GCN5-related N-acetyltransferase superfamily, binds acetyl-CoA and 5-HT at distinct sites to form a ternary complex. It transfers the acetyl group to 5-HT’s amine group to generate N-acetyl−5-HT and coenzyme A [68]. Aldehyde dehydrogenase, an NAD+-dependent oxidoreductase, activates its catalytic cysteine through a glutamate residue, allowing nucleophilic attack on 5-HIAL’s aldehyde group. This forms a thiohemiacetal intermediate coupled with hydride transfer to NAD⁺, ultimately releasing 5-HIAA and NADH after intermediate hydrolysis [69]. HIOMT, a methyltransferase, specifically recognizes N-acetyl−5-HT’s hydroxylated indole ring. Its active site coordinates N-acetyl−5-HT and S-adenosylmethionine to transfer a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine to the substrate via cysteine-mediated interactions, thereby synthesizing melatonin [70]. Dynamic interactions between gut microbiota and tryptophan metabolism

Gut microbiota-mediated tryptophan metabolism

The gut microbiota dynamically interacts with Trp metabolism through two primary mechanisms. First, specific gut microbes directly catabolize Trp via chromosomally encoded enzymatic systems. For instance, Escherichia and Bacteroides (e.g., bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, bacteroides ovatus) express tryptophanase, which catalyzes Trp deamination, resulting in the production of indole. This indole then activates the AhR to enhance intestinal barrier function by upregulating tight junction proteins [11, 71–74]. Additionally, Bacteroides, Clostridium, and Butyrivibrio produce skatole via dehydrogenases and methyltransferases-mediated degradation and methylation [75–77]. Furthermore, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, and Clostridium strains produce IAA using transaminases and decarboxylases, which similarly activate AhR signaling to maintain epithelial integrity and modulate T cell differentiation [71, 73, 75, 78]. Clostridium sporogenes and Peptostreptococcus produce IACA and IPA through aromatic lactate dehydrogenase-catalyzed reduction, exhibiting antioxidant/anti-inflammatory effects via AhR activation [11, 73, 79–82]. Lactobacillus acidophilus and Lactobacillus reuteri generate IAld via oxidoreductases, acting as AhR agonists to fortify mucosal immunity [83–85]. Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Clostridium convert Trp to ILA via lactate dehydrogenases, suppressing proinflammatory cytokines production while promoting regulatory T cell differentiation [71, 75, 79, 84–87]. Second, gut microbiota modulate Trp metabolite levels by regulating host rate-limiting enzymes. For example, Lactobacillus downregulate the Kyn pathway by inhibiting IDO1 through hydrogen peroxide or short-chain fatty acid (SCFAs)-mediated suppression, thus reducing the conversion of Trp to Kyn [21]. Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to inhibit IDO1 activity to attenuate neurotoxic QA production [88]. In contrast, lipopolysaccharides (LPS) derived from Proteobacteria enhance IDO1 activity through immune stimulation, driving systemic Trp depletion and Kyn accumulation [89]. In addition, the gut microbiota can regulate 5-HT synthesis in the colon. Certain bacteria, such as Bacteroides and Clostridium, produce SCFAs that promote the expression of TPH1 in enterochromaffin cells, thereby enhancing 5-HT production. This microbiota-mediated regulation of 5-HT is essential for maintaining gastrointestinal motility and platelet function [90, 91]. While the influence of gut microbiota on Trp metabolism is widely acknowledged, it is important to critically examine the variability in microbial contributions. Not all microbiota possess the same capacity to influence Trp metabolic pathways. The mechanisms through which specific microbial species modulate IDO1 expression remain poorly understood, warranting further investigation into the diversity of microbial impacts on Trp metabolism. Gut microbiota in Trp metabolic pathways

Tryptophan metabolism modulates gut microbiota composition

Trp-derived microbial metabolites play a pivotal role in reshaping the gut microbial ecosystem through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms, exerting both inhibitory and stimulatory effects on producer bacteria as well as neighboring strains. Among the three Trp metabolic pathways, the indole pathway exerts the most extensive regulatory influence on gut microbiota composition. As an intercellular signaling molecule, indole modulates bacterial antibiotic resistance, sporulation, virulence, and biofilm formation in species, particularly in species such as Clostridium difficile and Bacteroides strains [92]. IPA, synthesized by Clostridium sporogenes and Peptostreptococcus spp., demonstrates significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. IPA activats the AhR to strengthen intestinal barrier integrity, thereby establishing a survival advantage for bacteria via positive feedback loops [11]. Notably, IPA may also serves as a metabolic substrate for Akkermansia muciniphila, potentially enhancing its proliferation and indirectly improving intestinal barrier function [79]. Lactobacillus-derived IAld suppresses the hyphal morphogenesis of Candida albicans through AhR activation. This action constrains fungal overgrowth to its nonpathogenic yeast morphology and maintaining mycobiota homeostasis [83]. AhR signaling further fosters the enrichment of beneficial commensals, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Bifidobacterium, and Akkermansia muciniphila, while concurrently suppressing pathogen expansion such as Enterobacteriaceae [9, 93]. Moreover, 5-HT signaling also modulates gut microbiota composition, influencing intestinal immune responses, and susceptibility to colitis. 5-HT can be taken up by specific bacteria, such as Turicibacter, via their expressed SERT-like transporters, thereby inhibiting bacterial sporulation [94]. Additionally, 5-HT indirectly affects the gut microbiota by suppressing the production of antimicrobial peptides like β-defensins. This modulation impacts the intestinal immune environment, including the regulation of inflammatory cytokines and barrier function, ultimately increasing the host’s susceptibility to colitis [95]. Tryptophan metabolism as a key pathway in gut-brain axis signaling

Trp and its derivatives serve as signaling molecules that transduce signals along the GBA primarily through three pathways: the afferent nerve pathways, the circulatory system pathway, and the HPA axis pathway, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Bidirectional communication between the gut and the brain: the roles of the vagal and spinal afferent pathways, the circulatory system, and the HPA axis. In pathological conditions, both intestinal and BBB permeability are increased, allowing gut-derived molecules such as Trp derivatives, LPS, cytokines, and immune cells to translocate into systemic circulation through the compromised intestinal barrier. These molecules subsequently reach cerebral regions via blood flow. Simultaneously, certain molecules traverse the BBB to access the CNS, where they modulate neuronal function and synaptic plasticity. In the HPA axis pathway, gut microbiota dysbiosis and disrupted Trp metabolism trigger HPA axis hyperactivity, accompanied by aberrant elevation of corticosterone/cortisol levels that interfere with neuronal homeostasis. Through the vagal and spinal afferent pathways, Trp metabolites binds to receptors on vagal and spinal afferent neurons, transmitting signals to central neural circuits and exerting regulatory effects on brain function

Tryptophan metabolism stimulate intestinal afferent nerve signaling

Trp metabolism serves as a critical mediator in gut-brain communication through vagus nerve-dependent mechanisms in vertebrates. Vagal afferent terminals in the intestinal mucosa and muscular layers are categorized into mucosal and muscular subtypes. Mucosal vagal fibers detect epithelial-derived stimuli, including cytokines, nutrients, gut peptides, and hormones [96]. Direct synaptic connections between enteroendocrine cells and sensory neurons form a neuroepithelial circuit for immediate gut-to-brain signal transmission [97–100]. Microbial modulation of these luminal factors alters vagus nerve activity, as evidenced by studies demonstrating vagal afferent sensitivity to microbial metabolites including SCFAs. These signals are relayed to brainstem nuclei (nucleus tractus solitarius) and further propagated via polysynaptic pathways to sympathetic premotor neurons, regulating gastrointestinal motility and sympathetic outflow. Perturbations in gut microbiota composition or specific bacterial depletion can disrupt vagal signaling fidelity [101]. Trp-derived microbial metabolites critically engage this gut-brain neural axis, with 5-HT being central to this process. Enterochromaffin cells, the primary source of gastrointestinal 5-HT, release this monoamine to activate 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferents through paracrine diffusion, initiating signal transmission via nodose ganglion synapses [102, 103]. Other 5-HT receptor subtypes, such as 5-HT1A, 5-HT2C, and 5-HT7, are also indispensable for mediating 5-HT-regulated vagal neurotransmission [104, 105]. Indole derivatives can also stimulate intestinal vagal nerves via 5-HT. These metabolites are sensed by the transient receptor potential ankyrin A1 receptor on enteroendocrine cells, leading to the secretion of 5-HT from enteroendocrine cells, which subsequently stimulates enteric neurons and vagal sensory ganglia [99].

In addition to vagal afferents, the gastrointestinal tract is innervated by spinal primary afferent neurons whose cell bodies reside in the dorsal root ganglia and central terminals synapse in the spinal dorsal horn. These neurons project to the gut via splanchnic and pelvic nerves. They represent a dominant sensory route for visceral nociception, mechanosensation, and inflammation-evoked hypersensitivity, with particularly prominent contributions in the distal colon and rectum [106]. 5-HT signaling is a key interface between the mucosal environment and spinal afferent excitability. 5-HT can activate or sensitize visceral afferent endings through multiple 5-HT receptor subtypes, prominently including 5-HT3 receptors expressed on primary afferents. Pharmacological evidence supports a spinal nociceptive role for 5-HT3 signaling: the selective 5-HT3 antagonist alosetron suppresses colorectal distension-evoked visceral nociceptive indices and reduces stimulus-induced spinal Fos activation [107]. Importantly, recent work provides direct links between microbiota-derived metabolites, 5-HT release, and spinal afferent-driven GBA outcomes. Microbial metabolites such as isovalerate can trigger 5-HT release, which in turn activates 5-HT3 receptors on mucosal afferents to promote mechanical hypersensitivity [108]. Beyond 5-HT, whether other Trp-derived ligands (including selected indole and Kyn pathway metabolites) can directly modulate spinal afferent excitability remains an active area of investigation. Notably, AhR signaling has been implicated in pain-related pathways and is detectable in peripheral sensory neuron contexts, raising the possibility that the Trp–AhR axis could influence gut-evoked sensory states through neuron-intrinsic programs [109].

Circulatory-mediated gut-brain signaling through tryptophan metabolism

Trp metabolism facilitates gut-brain communication via the systemic circulation, crossing two anatomical barriers: the intestinal barrier and the BBB. The integrity of these barriers is influenced by various factors, including gut microbiota, SCFAs, mechanical stress, and inflammation, which dynamically regulate the flow of information between the gut and brain [110]. Following intestinal absorption, approximately 10–20% of dietary Trp remains unbound in plasma. Trp and its derivatives—including Kyn, 3-HK, 5-HTP, N-acetyl−5-HT, melatonin, TRY, IAA, and IPA—cross both the intestinal barrier and the BBB to regulate neuroinflammation and neuroimmune responses, thereby modulating the GBA [111, 112]. For instance, in chronic restraint stress (CRS)-induced depressive mice, compromised intestinal barrier integrity (particularly in the colon) facilitates systemic leakage of gut metabolites. Concurrently, Trp metabolic dysregulation occurs in the colon, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and serum, characterized by reduced 5-HT and elevated Kyn through preferential activation of the Kyn pathway [60]. Pharmacological inhibition of IDO1 attenuates hippocampal Kyn pathway activation, effectively reversing CRS-induced depressive behaviors [60]. FMT from healthy adolescents to depressed mice significantly reduces brain and colonic Kyn levels, as well as neurotoxic metabolites like QA and 3-HK, highlighting circulatory Kyn pathway overactivation as a key driver of depression [113]. Similarly, the indole pathway modulates gut-brain crosstalk. Indole, IAA, and IPA restore intestinal barrier integrity in APP/PS1 mice, blocking systemic proinflammatory cytokine transfer to the brain while rescuing synaptic plasticity proteins and alleviating cognitive deficits related to AD [114]. Trp metabolites also regulate BBB integrity via AhR-dependent mechanisms. IS, a microbiota-derived uremic toxin, accumulates in chronic kidney disease models, activating AhR to induce BBB endothelial dysfunction and cognitive impairment [115]. Chronic inflammation further enhances BBB permeability through AhR-driven CCL2 upregulation in endothelial cells, promoting neuroinflammatory monocyte infiltration and amplifying cerebral Kyn levels [116, 117]. Conversely, BBB disruption facilitates proinflammatory cytokine influx, creating feedback loops that sustain neuroinflammation and Kyn pathway hyperactivity [116].

A critical aspect of Trp metabolism in gut-brain signaling is the activation of platelets associated with 5-HT, which directly impacts BBB permeability. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota increases intestinal permeability, allowing inflammatory factors to enter the bloodstream and activate platelets to release 5-HT [118]. Elevated circulating 5-HT levels then bind to receptors in the cerebral vasculature, stimulating prostaglandin production. Excessive prostaglandin stimulation enhances vesicular transport in endothelial cells, resulting in BBB disruption and triggering neuroinflammation and associated disorders such as depression and anxiety [118]. Platelet-derived 5-HT also affects fear behavior in mice by modulating inhibitory neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus. Natural killer cells regulate the expression of SERT on platelet surfaces by releasing IL−13, thereby controlling platelet uptake and storage of 5-HT. When platelets are depleted or their activity inhibited, hippocampal 5-HT levels decline, leading to enhanced fear memory [119]. This platelet-centered pathway introduces an additional layer of complexity to gut-brain signaling. It suggests that the influence of gut-derived 5-HT on the brain is not solely dependent on its direct neural effects, but also on its storage and release dynamics from platelets. This raises important questions about how systemic inflammation, often originating from the gut, can leverage this pathway to exert remote control over BBB integrity and, consequently, brain function.

Bidirectional regulation between tryptophan metabolism and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis

The HPA axis is acentral component of the neuroendocrine system, coordinating adaptive stress responses through hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal interactions. Emerging evidence highlights bidirectional communication between the neuroendocrine system and gut microbiota. Gut microbiota modulates CNS function, stress-related neuroendocrine responses, and HPA axis activity, while HPA activation conversely influences microbial composition and gut permeability [119–122]. Notably, gut microbiota in specific pathogen free chronically stressed mice regulate emotional behaviors during social conflict by altering HPA axis activity. This is primarily achieved by suppressing pituitary Fkbp5 expression, a gene governing glucocorticoid (GC) receptor sensitivity [123]. 5-HT serves as a crucial mediator in the reciprocal regulation between Trp metabolism and the HPA axis. Acute administration of selective 5-HT reuptake inhibitors stimulates the HPA axis in rats, whereas chronic use induces desensitization. This phenomenon is associated with altered postsynaptic 5-HT receptor dynamics on corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) neurons [124]. Among 5-HT receptors, 5-HT1A and 5-HT2A receptors synergistically enhance adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and corticosterone release. Agonists of 5-HT1A receptor, such as ipsapirone, elevate plasma ACTH and corticosterone/cortisol levels in both animals and humans [125–127]. Similarly, m-chlorophenylpiperazine, a 5-HT2C receptor agonist, elevates ACTH and corticosterone/cortisol secretion, while 5-HT2C antagonists suppress stress-induced ACTH release more effectively than 5-HT1A antagonists [128, 129]. Thus, both agonism and antagonism of 5-HT receptors profoundly modulate HPA axis activity.

Reciprocally, the HPA axis exerts inhibitory effects on the 5-HT system, primarily through GCs. Cortisol regulates 5-HT1A receptor gene transcription, as demonstrated by rapid upregulation of hippocampal and septal 5-HT1A mRNA synthesis, total mRNA levels, and receptor binding sites following adrenalectomy in rats. These effects are reversed by low-dose corticosterone treatment [130, 131]. Chronic immobilization stress elevates circulating corticosterone levels, which reduces hippocampal 5-HT1A receptor binding. Electrophysiological studies in adrenalectomized rats reveal that mineralocorticoid receptor activation by corticosterone attenuates 5-HT1A receptor-mediated hyperpolarization in hippocampal CA1 neurons, a phenomenon replicated in mice with chronic corticosterone exposure [132, 133]. Similarly, corticosterone influences 5-HT2C receptor function, with 5-HT2C mRNA editing emerging as a key mechanism for serotoninergic adaptation to HPA activity [134]. The interplay between 5-HT and the HPA axis is particularly relevant in pathologies such as depression, where HPA dysregulation and Trp metabolism imbalance are core etiological factors. Clinical studies link hyperactive HPA function (elevated nocturnal cortisol) with altered Trp metabolism (reduced Kyn/Trp ratio) in major depression, independent of antidepressant treatment [135].

The Kyn pathway and HPA axis engage in mutually reinforcing interactions that drive disease progression. Chronic stress shifts central Kyn metabolism toward KYNA by suppressing KMO overexpression in mice. As an endogenous α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor antagonist, KYNA disrupts receptor-mediated HPA axis negative feedback, leading to elevated serum corticosterone levels. This finding has been confirmed in KMO-knockout mice [136]. Conversely, HPA hyperactivation amplifies GC-driven induction of IDO, diverting Trp from 5-HT synthesis to neurotoxic metabolites like 3-HK and QA. This imbalance results in neuronal apoptosis, hippocampal/prefrontal atrophy, and depressive behaviors [137]. Immune pathways further integrate Trp metabolism with HPA regulation. For instance, IFN-γ induces IDO expression in hypothalamic GT1−7 and pituitary AtT−20 cells, generating neurotoxic metabolites that exacerbate neuroinflammation and impair HPA function—effects that can be suppressed by IL−10 [138]. Collectively, dysregulation in both 5-HT and Kyn pathways exacerbates HPA hyperactivity and hypercortisolism, while elevated GCs further disrupt Trp metabolism, establishing a pathogenic vicious cycle.

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor: a molecular nexus linking tryptophan metabolism to the gut-brain axis

Although multiple receptor systems have been implicated in sensing signals from Trp metabolism and shaping GBA homeostasis, the AhR has emerged as the most prominent signaling hub (Fig. 4). To date, a diverse spectrum of physiological and dietary AhR ligands has been identified, including Trp metabolites, curcumin, carotenoids, and gallic acid [139–141]. Within the intestinal compartment, AhR is widely recognized as a pivotal regulator of immune responses at barrier sites, reinforcing mucosal integrity while modulating the activity of multiple immune cell populations [142]. Recent evidence indicates that AhR activation constrains pathological angiogenesis by suppressing the proliferation and migration of intestinal vascular endothelial cells, thereby preserving vascular homeostasis [143]. Consistently, in murine models of enteric infection, AhR deficiency markedly exacerbates host susceptibility to pathogens, whereas dietary supplementation with AhR ligands such as indole−3-carbinol significantly improves survival rates [143]. Moreover, AhR activation promotes the survival and functionality of intestinal type 3 innate lymphoid cells and Th17 cells, driving IL−22-dependent antimicrobial defense mechanisms [144]. AhR signaling further orchestrates intestinal stem cell dynamics by upregulating E3 ubiquitin ligases that degrade WNT receptors, thereby suppressing excessive stem cell proliferation and fostering differentiation [145]. Importantly, AhR function extends beyond epithelial and immune compartments into neuro-regulatory circuits. In the enteric nervous system, AhR acts as a direct neuronal sensor of luminal microbial cues. Obata et al. demonstrated that microbial colonization induces AhR expression in enteric neurons of the distal gut, enabling these neurons to couple luminal cues, such as indole metabolites, to neuron-specific transcriptional effector programs [146]. Neuron-specific deletion of Ahr attenuates colonic peristalsis, whereas supplementation with the AhR ligand precursor I3C partially restores gut motility [146]. In the CNS, AhR signaling has been linked to neuroendocrine regulation, neurodevelopment, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and behavior. For example, the xenobiotic ligand 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin perturbs estrogenic feedback by reducing estrogen receptor expression and estrogen levels in the brain, and it alters CRH and vasopressin expression within the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) [147, 148]. Intriguingly, AhR exhibits context-dependent effects on BBB regulation. Certain exogenous ligands like β-naphthoflavone or 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin enhance BBB detoxification by inducing cytochrome P450 enzymes and transporters in cerebral microvascular endothelial cells [149, 150]. In contrast, pathological accumulation of IS under renal dysfunction activates AhR, increases BBB permeability and induces cognitive impairment in mice [115].

The AhR, upon activation by Trp metabolites, mediates regulatory functions in both the gastrointestinal tract and the CNS. In the intestinal system, AhR is activated upon binding to ligands such as KYNA, XA, and indole derivatives. It exerts multiple regulatory functions, including suppression of NF-қB-mediated inflammatory cytokine release, inhibition of intestinal stem cell (SC) hyperproliferation via WNT signaling, maintenance of vascular integrity through vascular endothelial cell (VEC) regulation, and promotion of IL−22 secretion via Th17 cell differentiation. Within the CNS, AhR activation enhances BBB function by upregulating transporter protein expression. It also ameliorates neuronal apoptosis and synaptic impairment, inhibits excessive activation of the HPA axis, and modulates both AST activity and mitochondrial function to support neural homeostasis

Owing to its pleiotropic functions across both gut and brain, AhR has gained increasing attention as a regulatory node of the GBA, particularly through the Trp-AhR axis. However, this pleiotropy presents a critical challenge for therapeutic targeting. The outcome of AhR activation is highly dependent on the specific ligand involved. In general, the indole-AhR pathway predominantly exerts positive regulatory effects, promoting balanced gut-brain signaling and tissue homeostasis [151–153]. Dysbiosis-induced disruption of IPA/AhR signaling has been associated with hippocampal microglial hyperactivation and excessive synaptic pruning in intrauterine growth-restricted rats. Postnatal IPA supplementation alleviates autism spectrum disorder-like behaviors and rescues hippocampal injury [154]. In neurodegeneration models, Lactobacillus reuteri elevate circulating ILA, reduces soluble Aβ burden in 5xFAD AD mice, and improves cognitive performance. In vitro studies confirm that ILA potentiates Aβ phagocytosis by microglia and astrocytes in an AhR-dependent manner [155]. IAld also functions as a gut-brain signaling metabolite that links microbial Trp metabolism to stress vulnerability, and either dietary I3C or Lactobacillus reuteri supplementation suppresses neuroinflammation while promoting hippocampal neurogenesis via AhR signaling [156]. Moreover, IACA ameliorates intestinal inflammation and mitigates psychological burden in DSS-induced zebrafish colitis models and in human HT−29 colonic epithelial cells [157]. In contrast, the Kyn pathway generates comparatively fewer AhR-activating metabolites, with Kyn itself being the most consistently reported ligand. Accumulating evidence suggests that the Kyn-AhR axis often disrupts gut-brain communication and counteracts the protective effects associated with indole-AhR signaling. For instance, TDO2 overexpression in human glioma cells correlates with enhanced Kyn production and AhR activation, thereby facilitating tumor progression [158]. Kyn can also amplify neuroinflammatory cascades by augmenting monocyte responsiveness to CCL2 chemokines via AhR, promoting CNS infiltration and proinflammatory interactions with astrocytes; pharmacological AhR antagonism reverses these effects and alleviates neuroinflammation and depressive-like behaviors [117]. Following intracerebral hemorrhage, elevated brain Kyn activates AhR and exacerbates neuronal apoptosis via RhoA/Bax-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction. This increases oxidative stress markers and pro-apoptotic proteins (Bax, caspase−3) while downregulating the anti-apoptotic Bcl−2 [159]. Collectively, growing evidence implicates the Kyn-AhR axis in the pathogenesis of AD, PD, migraine, and IBS, supporting its disease-promoting role and highlighting AhR antagonism as a potential therapeutic strategy for attenuating these conditions.

Tryptophan metabolism plays a critical role in disease pathogenesis

The precise regulation of Trp metabolism is a crucial foundation for maintaining GBA homeostasis. Once its metabolic balance is disrupted, it can initiate or exacerbate a wide spectrum of GBA-related disorders. In the following sections, we systematically summarize disease-specific Trp metabolic signatures across IBS, IBD, depression, AD, schizophrenia, and PD, highlighting its importance as a common metabolic hub in cross-disease mechanisms.

Irritable bowel syndrome

IBS is the most prevalent functional gastrointestinal disorder and is clinically characterized by abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits. Although its etiology remains incompletely defined, accumulating evidence supports a central pathogenic contribution of gut microbiota dysbiosis and Trp metabolic perturbations [160, 161]. Among Trp-derived mediators, 5-HT represents a key driver of IBS pathophysiology, contributing to both gastrointestinal and extraintestinal manifestations through aberrant peripheral 5-HT elevation. In humans, peripheral 5-HT levels show strong correlations with symptom severity and IBS clinical phenotypes [162–164]. Mechanistically, Ruminococcus gnavus can metabolize dietary amino acids to produce trace amines, including phenethylamine and TRY. These amines stimulate TPH1 and AADC activities, thereby promoting 5-HT synthesis and aggravating intestinal dysmotility and diarrhea in IBS with diarrhea (IBS-D) murine models [165]. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies targeting 5-HT receptors have emerged as primary interventions for IBS symptom management. Although 5-HT4 receptor antagonists such as piboserod are not currently used clinically for IBS, this class of agents remains of interest because inhibiting 5-HT4 signaling may slow intestinal transit and counteract diarrhea-related dysmotility [166]. However, clinical translation has been limited by insufficient evidence in IBS-specific endpoints and the lack of approved indications. Additional therapeutic advantages are observed with antagonists targeting 5-HT1, 5-HT3, and 5-HT7 receptors. Importantly, the magnitude and duration of 5-HT-mediated biological effects are constrained by the SERT, and altered 5-HT signaling via SERT dysfunction impacts gastrointestinal function in IBS. Clinically, IBS patients often display elevated 5-HT levels concomitant with reduced SERT expression, with studies reporting a robust association between SERT insertion/deletion polymorphisms and IBS susceptibility. Rectal mucosal biopsies from IBS patients, particularly those with prominent abdominal pain in IBS-D, further demonstrate higher 5-HT concentrations compared to healthy controls [167–169]. Consistent with these observations, female SERT-knockout rats display lower pain pressure thresholds in response to colorectal distension, accompanied by increased colonic enterochromaffin cell numbers and 5-HT concentrations relative to wild-type counterparts. These phenotypes are accompanied by upregulated expression of 5-HT1B, 5-HT2C, 5-HT3A, 5-HT3B, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 receptors [168].

Beyond serotonergic disruption, Kyn pathway abnormalities represent another hallmark of IBS, particularly in IBS-D. Patients exhibit elevated serum Kyn, accompanied by an imbalance between QA and KYNA production, which is linked to N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor 2B upregulation. This neuroimmune remodeling likely contributes to both gastrointestinal symptoms and affective disturbances, potentially driven by increased peripheral IDO1 activity [170, 171]. Notably, acute Trp depletion and dietary Trp restriction significantly reduce peripheral Trp and Kyn levels and can ameliorate cerebral cortex-mediated cognitive impairments in IBS cohorts [172, 173]. Perturbations of indole metabolites have also been consistently reported. In IBS-D patients, fecal concentrations of IPA, IAld, IAA, and ILA are reduced, whereas fecal Trp levels may not differ significantly from controls [160]. A reduction of urinary IS has been reported in related inflammatory cohorts [161]. In murine IBS models, suppression of indole pathway activity reduces the availability of AhR ligands, leading to decreased IL−22 expression, increased intestinal permeability, and barrier dysfunction—phenotypes that parallel the altered indole metabolite profiles observed clinically [174].

Inflammatory bowel disease

In IBD, intestinal inflammation can propagate along the GBA to impair brain function and contribute to psychological comorbidities such as anxiety and depression. In parallel, chronic pain, especially persistent abdominal pain, is highly prevalent in IBD and may be mechanistically coupled to GBA dysregulation. Trp metabolism has been implicated as a key contributor to these pain-related circuits. Dysregulated Kyn pathway activation enhances neuroactive metabolites (e.g., QA and KYNA) capable of modulating NMDA receptor signaling, neuroinflammation, and central sensitization processes linked to chronic pain [175]. Conversely, psychological stress and chronic pain can further aggravate intestinal inflammation through reciprocal GBA feedback. Trp metabolism critically modulates this interplay, with pathway alterations profoundly impacting IBD progression. At the systemic level, large-scale serum profiling of over 500 IBD patients revealed markedly reduced Trp levels, which were inversely correlated with disease activity. This was accompanied by elevated peripheral IDO1 activity and Kyn pathway overactivation, particularly increased QA and 3-HAA [12, 176]. However, the functional role of IDO signaling remains context dependent. In murine models, IDO inhibition or genetic deletion has been reported to exacerbate colitis, whereas XA and KYNA levels inversely correlated with intestinal inflammation severity in both mice and humans [28, 177, 178]. Moreover, Kyn has been shown to attenuate 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid-induced colitis in KMO+/+ mice and to stimulate IL−10R expression in dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-treated colitis models, supporting an inflammation-resolving dimension of selected Kyn metabolites under specific conditions [179, 180].

Within the 5-HT pathway, converging evidence indicates that elevated 5-HT generally exacerbates colitis, consistent with its proinflammatory role in IBS. Khan’s group provided mechanistic evidence that 5-HT stimulation enhances dendritic cell cytokine output and promoted CD4⁺ T-cell responses characterized by increased IL−17; importantly, adoptive transfer of 5-HT-pulsed dendritic cells aggravates colitis [181]. Clinically, active Crohn’s disease patients exhibit significantly higher serum 5-HT levels compared to those in remission or with refractory disease [182]. In experimental colitis, TPH1-deficient mice, with suppressed 5-HT synthesis, displayed diminished macrophage infiltration and lower proinflammatory cytokine production, resulting in attenuated DSS-induced pathology. In contrast, 5-HTP supplementation restores 5-HT levels and aggravates disease severity [183]. Similarly, combined exposure to 1% DSS and exogenous 5-HT exacerbates murine colitis [184]. 5-HT also modulates intestinal inflammation in IBD through receptor interactions. Inflamed colonic mucosa from Crohn’s disease patients shows increased expression of several 5-HT receptors (including 5-HT3, 5-HT4, and 5-HT7), together with downregulated SERT [185]. Pharmacological studies further support receptor-specific complexity: 5-HT4 receptor antagonism can induce mild colitis in otherwise healthy mice, whereas 5-HT7 receptor inhibition worsenes colitis severity, contrasting with agonist-mediated improvement [186, 187]. Conversely, 5-HT3 receptor antagonists ameliorate 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid-induced rat colitis by reducing IL−1β, IL−3, and TNF-α levels [188]. Additionally, murine colitis models demonstrated downregulated SERT expression, with SERT knockout exacerbating colitis severity [188, 189].

Beyond Kyn and 5-HT disturbances, microbiota-derived indole metabolites represent a third major axis in IBD pathophysiology. Key indole metabolites, including IPA and IACA, are significantly reduced in fecal and serum samples of IBD patients, contributing to intestinal inflammation and dysbiosis [190, 191]. These metabolites are widely viewed as protective through AhR-dependent regulation of epithelial integrity and immune tone [160, 192, 193]. Nevertheless, inconsistencies remain across studies regarding TRY, IAA, and Indole−3-methanol concentrations, likely reflecting biological matrix differences [191]. Collectively, dysregulated indole metabolism, coupled with Kyn and 5-HT overactivation, may amplify inflammatory feedback loops and perpetuate mucosal injury in IBD.

Depression

Depression, a psychiatric disorder characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, and cognitive dysfunction, is increasingly linked to gut microbiota-mediated modulation of Trp metabolism and GBA functionality. Epidemiological and nutritional observations suggest that individuals with low dietary Trp intake exhibit greater vulnerability to irritability and anxiety-related phenotypes [194]. As the biosynthetic precursor for 5-HT, Trp influences depressive pathology through metabolic competition between serotonergic biosynthesis and diversion toward the Kyn pathway. Clinically, reduced plasma Trp levels and elevated Kyn/Trp ratios have been directly associated with depressive symptoms and diagnostic status [195, 196]. Although plasma Trp levels do not differ significantly between major depressive disorder patients and healthy controls, patients with major depressive disorder show lower 5-HT and 5-HTP levels. This is likely due to suppressed TPH1/2 expression in the brain during depressive states [197, 198]. Impaired BBB transport of 5-HTP and diminished cerebral 5-HT synthesis further support the rationale that 5-HTP supplementation may elevate 5-HT levels and alleviate depressive symptoms [199]. In parallel, dysregulated serotonin catabolism contributes to depression. Elevated MAO-A and MAO-B activities reported in both human subjects and animal models, while MAO inhibitors demonstrate therapeutic efficacy [200, 201]. Depressive phenotypes are also associated with reduced melatonin and 5-HIAA levels [202, 203], and abnormal 5-HT receptor signaling underpins the mechanism of many antidepressants targeting serotonin reuptake.

The Kyn pathway antagonizes 5-HT synthesis, and its hyperactivity exacerbates depression. Increased IDO and TDO expression during depression highlights these enzymes as potential therapeutic targets [204]. Depressed patients frequently exhibit an imbalance between neuroprotective and neurodegenerative Kyn metabolites, marked by increased 3-HK branch activity, reduced KYNA production, and elevated QA and 3-HK. Correspondingly, plasma KYNA concentrations and KYNA/QA ratios are often decreased relative to controls [205–207]. FMT from healthy adolescent donors to depressive mice increases colonic 5-HT levels while reduce QA and 3-HK [113]. Mechanistically, KMO critically regulates branch pathway shifts, with astrocytes predominantly generating KYNA and microglia/macrophages favoring QA production via the 3-HK pathway [208, 209]. Preferential microglial Kyn degradation over astrocytic pathways in depression, coupled with astrocyte dysfunction promoting 3-HK dominance, creates a vicious cycle of astrocyte apoptosis and pathway hyperactivation [210, 211]. Disruption of indole metabolism is also directly associated with depressive behaviors. Plasma IS levels are positively correlated with the severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms, whereas the IPA/IS ratio is negatively correlated [212]. In individuals with obesity, reduced Trp and indole derivatives, particularly IAld, are significantly associated with more severe depressive symptoms, and low IAld further strengthens the predictive effect of the inflammatory marker C-reactive protein on depressive symptoms, suggesting a synergistic role of inflammation and disrupted indole metabolism in driving depression [213].

Alzheimer’s disease

AD, a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cognitive impairment and memory decline, is pathologically defined by β-amyloid deposition and neurofibrillary tangles. Gut microbiota and Trp metabolites influence neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative changes via the GBA, playing a pivotal role in AD pathogenesis. Dysregulation of the serotonergic neurotransmitter system is a prominent feature of AD. Early clinical studies have identified significant reductions in 5-HT and 5-HIAA levels in the brains of AD patients, particularly in the temporal and frontal cortices [214]. Further evidence demonstrates markedly decreased concentrations of 5-HT, Trp, 5-HTP, and melatonin in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of AD patients compared to healthy controls [215]. Notably, deficiencies in 5-HT receptors (5-HT1, 5-HT2, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7) critically contribute to AD development [216–219]. AD patients exhibit a metabolic shift from 5-HT synthesis toward the neuroinflammatory Kyn pathway, characterized by reduced serum Trp levels, elevated Kyn/Trp ratios, and increased IDO1 and TDO activities. This shift is accompanied by elevated QA and 3-HK concentrations alongside decreased KYNA levels in serum [2, 220–222]. Concurrently, CSF Kyn, QA, and KYNA levels are significantly upregulated in AD [221, 223]. Elevated Kyn/Trp ratios strongly correlate with inflammatory biomarkers, reduced functional independence, and impaired memory scores, while increased 3-HK/Kyn ratios associate with elevated total tau levels in the CSF of AD patients [224]. Indole derivatives produced by gut microbiota represent a third key axis influencing AD pathogenesis. Multi-omics analyses show that IPA, IAA, and ILA are markedly reduced in the feces and plasma of patients with AD. These indole derivatives, mainly produced by indole-generating taxa such as Clostridium sporogenes and Peptostreptococcus spp., serve as key signaling molecules for maintaining GBA homeostasis [225]. In addition, decreased serum IAA is accompanied by accelerated diversion of Trp into the Kyn pathway via IDO and TDO, further suppressing the neuroprotective indole branch [226]. This metabolic reprogramming not only weakens AhR-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling but also promotes M1-like microglial activation, establishing a feed-forward neuroinflammatory loop that facilitates Aβ aggregation and tau hyperphosphorylation [227].

Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia patients exhibit peripheral Kyn pathway dysregulation, marked by an increased neurotoxic-to-neuroprotective metabolite ratio [228]. Studies demonstrate elevated serum QA/KYNA ratios and urinary Kyn/Trp ratios in schizophrenia patients, alongside reduced neuroprotective metabolites including serum KYNA and XA, as well as urinary PICA levels and decreased urinary PICA/QA ratios [228]. Furthermore, CSF Kyn and KYNA concentrations are significantly upregulated in schizophrenia [229]. A meta-analysis corroborates increased IDO activity and diminished KMO activity in both the CNS and serum of schizophrenia patients [15]. TPH2, the rate-limiting enzyme for CNS 5-HT synthesis, exhibits reduced activity in schizophrenia, contributing to decreased 5-HT production [230]. Additionally, schizophrenia patients show elevated CSF 5-HIAA concentrations, indicative of altered 5-HT metabolic pathways [231]. These findings collectively highlight the potential of Kyn and 5-HT pathway metabolites as peripheral biomarkers for cerebral dysfunction in schizophrenia. Quantitative studies on indole metabolites in schizophrenia patients remain limited. Early research has observed a significant increase in the excretion of serotonin and IAA in the urine during symptom exacerbation in schizophrenia patients [232]. Modern studies further reveal that in schizophrenia patients who experience overweight or obesity following long-term antipsychotic treatment, dysbiosis is associated with a significant decrease in the fecal levels of beneficial metabolites such as indole−3-carboxylic acid [233].

Parkinson’s disease

PD, a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by dopaminergic neuron degeneration in the substantia nigra, is modulated by disruptions in Trp metabolism. Recent studies analyzing urinary Trp metabolites in PD patients identified significantly elevated levels of IAA, IPA, and Kyn/KYNA ratios [234, 235]. Serum concentrations of Trp, Kyn, and KYNA are notably reduced in PD patients compared to healthy individuals. Additionally, increased Kyn/Trp ratios and 3-HK levels in the putamen, frontal cortex, and hippocampus suggest heightened IDO and TDO activities within the CNS [236, 237]. PD patients exhibit reduced KYNA levels in the striatum and CSF, coupled with elevated QA concentrations in the striatum and cortex [238]. Furthermore, evaluations of Kyn pathway metabolites in PD plasma and CSF reveal a fourfold elevation in the 3-HK/KYNA ratio alongside decreased AA levels [239].

By summarizing the alterations in Trp metabolism in the aforementioned diseases, we propose that overactivation of the Kyn pathway and reduction of most indole metabolites serve as a common hallmark in the pathogenesis of several disorders (Table 3).

Administration of Trp and its metabolites modulates gut microbial dysbiosis while alleviating systemic pathology through GBA signaling. Therapeutic inhibition of rate-limiting enzymes in Trp metabolism reprograms metabolic fluxes, reinstating both homeostatic Trp catabolism and gut-brain communication pathways

Fecal microbiota transplantation and probiotic supplementation

Gut microbiota, central regulators of host physiology and pathology, are critical mediators of Trp metabolism. FMT and probiotic supplementation are emerging as therapeutic strategies to modulate this pathway. FMT from healthy adolescent volunteers into CRS-induced adolescent depressive mice elevated 5-HT levels in the brain and colon while reducing neurotoxic metabolites QA and 3-HK, significantly ameliorating depressive behaviors. Concurrently, Roseburia intestinalis colonization upregulated TPH1/TPH2 expression and suppressed IDO1 and 3-HAO activity, replicating FMT effects [113]. Paradoxically, FMT from schizophrenia patients into antibiotic-treated mice induced behavioral abnormalities, impaired learning and memory, and elevated Kyn pathway activity in both peripheral and brain tissues. It also raised hippocampal 5-HT and IDO1 mRNA levels, suggesting that restoring Trp homeostasis via optimized FMT may represent a viable therapeutic approach [242]. FMT from Fu brick tea theabrownin-treated mice alleviated colitis by enhancing IAA and IAld production, activating the AhR to upregulate IL−22 expression [243]. Clinically, FMT improved depressive and anxiety symptoms in functional constipation patients, correlating with reduced serum 5-HT [244]. Intriguingly, FMT increased colonic 5-HIAA concentrations in formula-fed piglets, activating AhR signaling to mitigate diarrhea [245].

Probiotic supplementation modulates gut microbiota less invasively than FMT. Bifidobacterium administration elevated ILA in the hippocampus of depressive mice, activating AhR to suppress microglial hyperactivity and proinflammatory cytokines (IL−1β and IL−6) [246]. Lactobacillus reuteri reversed Trp dysmetabolism by increasing colonic and prefrontal cortex TPH1 expression and systemic 5-HT levels, ameliorating social avoidance in depressive mice [247]. A mixed probiotic (Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus and Enterococcus faecium) accelerated Trp metabolism, restored 5-HT and 5-HTP levels, and reduced depressive-like behaviors in macaques [248]. Lactobacillus rhamnosus UA260 and Lactobacillus plantarum YU28 mitigated hyperuricemia in mice by generating anti-inflammatory metabolites (IPA and IAA), suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation [249]. Akkermansia muciniphila prevented colitis by elevating serum IACA [250]. Clinically, Bifidobacterium breve CCFM1025 demonstrated antidepressant potential by enhancing Trp, 5-HTP, 5-HT, 5-HIAA, and IPA production [251].

Tryptophan and its derivatives supplementation

Supplementation with Trp and its derivatives modulates GBA communication by enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, restoring immune homeostasis, and regulating neuroendocrine pathways. This demonstrates therapeutic promise for IBD and neurodegenerative disorders. Indole pathway metabolites, mostly beneficial, improve GBA dysregulation. IPA enhances hippocampal inhibitory synaptic transmission in autism spectrum disorder models by regulating γ-aminobutyric acid levels and glutamate decarboxylase 65/67 expression, while rescuing social and cognitive deficits [252]. IPA supplementation also enriches gut microbial diversity and reduces cerebral infarct volume in ischemic brain injury [253]. I3C and Lactobacillus reuteri (an indole−3-carbinol-producing strain) attenuate stress susceptibility via AhR/SOCS2/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling, suppressing neuroinflammation and promoting hippocampal neurogenesis [156]. TRY, IAA, and ILA enhance neuronal viability by inhibiting Aβ aggregation, thereby mitigating AD pathology [254–257]. Although Kyn pathway hyperactivity often exacerbates disease, select metabolites exhibit protective effects. XA administration normalizes dopaminergic neuron activity in the ventral tegmental area and substantia nigra, improving dopamine dysfunction [258]. KYNA, on the other hand, counteracts QA-mediated excitotoxicity and alleviates binge-eating disorder by suppressing hyperactivity of the vagus nucleus tractus solitarius-paraventricular thalamus circuitry [259]. 5-HT pathway metabolites further modulate gut-brain interactions. 5-HIAA activates PPARγ to inhibit MAPK and NF-κB pathways, thereby reducing colitis-associated inflammation [260]. 5-HTP can improve sleep disturbances in elderly individuals by enhancing gut microbial diversity, enriching SCFA-producing taxa, and suppressing IL−6 secretion linked to sleep deprivation [261].

Enzyme-targeted interventions in tryptophan metabolism

Emerging evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of antagonizing rate-limiting enzymes in Trp metabolism to precisely modulate pathway fluxes, offering novel strategies for treating GBA-associated disorders. Inhibition of Kyn pathway enzymes mitigates neurotoxic metabolite accumulation. 1-methyltryptophan-mediated suppression of IDO activity reduces Kyn pathway flux, diminishes neurotoxic metabolite production, enhances 5-HT synthesis, and alleviates CRS-induced depressive-like behaviors in mice [262]. Similarly, TDO inhibition with 680C91 in drosophila shifts Trp metabolism from 3-HK and QA toward KYNA, ameliorating neurodegenerative phenotypes through KYNA-mediated antagonism of NMDA and α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors [263]. KMO governs the conversion of Kyn to 3-HK and QA. In neuropathic pain models, IL−1 signaling upregulates KMO in hippocampal neurons. Intracerebroventricular KMO inhibitors block QA and 3-HK generation and specifically improve depressive-like behaviors in these mice [264]. Conversely, KAT inhibition, which reduces KYNA synthesis, shows promise in managing sleep disorders, memory deficits, and schizophrenia [265–267]. Within the 5-HT pathway, TPH and MAO—but not AADC—are primary targets. TPH inhibition reduces 5-HT synthesis, attenuating postsynaptic receptor overstimulation, while concurrently modulating neuronal excitability via K+ channel regulation to improve depressive symptoms [268]. Clinically used MAO-A/MAO-B inhibitors (moclobemide and selegiline) treat psychiatric disorders, with phenelzine demonstrating additional neuroprotective, anticonvulsant, and anxiolytic effects [269–271]. These findings underscore enzyme-targeted modulation as a pivotal approach to rebalance Trp metabolism in multisystem diseases.

Although FMT and probiotic supplementation have shown promising results in some clinical contexts, these interventions are not without limitations. The variability in patient responses and the lack of standardized protocols for microbiota restoration pose significant challenges to their widespread clinical use [272–274]. Furthermore, enzyme inhibitors targeting Trp metabolism, while therapeutically promising, often suffer from a lack of specificity, leading to potential off-target effects that could exacerbate conditions like neuroinflammation [275]. A more refined approach is needed, perhaps focusing on personalized medicine or the development of targeted therapies.

Outlook and challenge

This review systematically delineates the central hub role of Trp metabolism in GBA communication. As an essential dietary amino acid, Trp generates a spectrum of neuroactive metabolites through three principal metabolic pathways—Kyn, indole, and 5-HT—establishing a critical biochemical bridge connecting gut microbiota and brain function. We emphasize the pivotal regulatory role of rate-limiting enzymes in this network, encompassing host-derived enzymes such as IDO, TDO, TPH, and KMO, alongside microbial enzymes including tryptophanase and ArAT. Together, they form an exquisitely tuned regulatory system that responds to both host and microbial signals. The gut microbiota directly participates in Trp metabolism, producing bioactive metabolites such as IPA and IAld, while also remodelling the global metabolic landscape upstream by modulating host enzyme activities like IDO1. AhR emerges as a pivotal molecular hub integrating signals across distinct metabolic pathways, playing central roles in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, regulating immune homeostasis, and influencing cerebral functions. Under pathological conditions, Trp metabolic network dysfunction results in abnormal rate-limiting enzyme activities and imbalanced ratios of neurotoxic-to-neuroprotective metabolites. This dysfunction represents a unifying pathological hallmark across GBA-associated disorders ranging from IBS and IBD to depression and AD. Building on these insights, Trp metabolism-targeted interventions, including FMT, specific metabolite supplementation, and enzyme inhibitor, demonstrate promising therapeutic potential for restoring metabolic balance and improving GBA function.

While this review synthesizes the established centrality of Trp metabolism in the GBA, translating this knowledge into mechanistic understanding and clinical therapies faces multifaceted challenges. A major limitation is the lack of mechanistic depth. Our understanding of how specific metabolites regulate neuroimmune circuits in complex physiological contexts remains fragmented [276]. This gap highlights the need to clarify their cell-type-specific and context-dependent effects. The intricate crosstalk between microbial and host enzymatic networks remains underexplored, with limited clarity on how metabolic cross-regulation establishes dynamic equilibrium [277]. Translational gaps between experimental models and human pathophysiology persist, as species-specific variations in enzyme expression and microbial composition complicate extrapolation from animal studies [278, 279]. Personalized approaches are imperative given the heterogeneity arising from genetic background, dietary patterns, and baseline microbiota profiles [277]. Therapeutic optimization is crucial, requiring improved standardization and safety of FMT, enhanced probiotic colonization efficiency and strain specificity, and refined targeting of enzyme inhibitors to minimize off-tissue effects [272–275]. Beyond the acknowledged gaps, several emerging challenges critically impede progress. A primary hurdle is the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the Trp metabolic network. Metabolite concentrations and enzyme activities fluctuate dynamically across different gut regions, cell types, and circadian rhythms, creating a complex, non-static system that is poorly captured by bulk tissue analyses [208, 280]. Furthermore, establishing definitive causality in microbiota-host interactions remains difficult. Correlations between specific bacteria and metabolite levels are abundant. However, proving that a particular strain directly shifts host Trp metabolism—and then alters brain function—requires more sophisticated tools than conventional germ-free or antibiotic-treated models can provide [281]. The functional redundancy within the gut microbiome also poses a significant challenge. Inhibiting one bacterial pathway may be compensated for by another, blunting the effect of targeted interventions [282]. Finally, the GBA is bidirectional and involves multiple organs. Therefore, targeting gut Trp metabolism may cause unintended effects in the liver, immune system, OR brain [283]. The reverse is also possible. As a result, systemic side effects remain a major concern for drug development.

These approaches can map the Trp metabolome and its regulatory enzymes to specific cell types within tissue architectures, such as the intestinal crypt-villus axis or specific brain nuclei. Ultimately, transformative progress will necessitate the interdisciplinary integration of these emerging technologies. Trp metabolism is a validated central node in GBA research. However, translating this knowledge into mechanistically informed clinical interventions will require deeper fundamental insights using advanced tools. It will also require more physiologically relevant human models and innovative methodologies. Together, these efforts may open new therapeutic avenues for interconnected gut–brain disorders.

Conclusion

Trp metabolism transcends its canonical role as a biosynthetic precursor to emerge as a fundamental signaling system and homeostatic regulator of the GBA. This review establishes that the three Trp pathways—Kyn, indole, and 5-HT—do not operate in isolation but form an integrated, competitive, and highly plastic metabolic network. The critical insight is that the functional output of this network is determined not merely by the presence of metabolites, but by the dynamic balance between them, a balance meticulously governed by a symphony of host and microbial enzymes. The AhR acts as a master interpreter within this system, translating the complex language of Trp metabolites into coherent transcriptional programs that coordinate barrier function, immunity, and neuroinflammation. Consequently, a paradigm shift is underway. A diverse array of gastrointestinal and neuropsychiatric disorders can be viewed as sharing a common pathophysiological thread—a breakdown in the enzymatic control of Trp metabolism. This disruption can drive a characteristic shift from neuroprotective toward neurotoxic signaling outputs. This unifying perspective reframes these disorders, at least in part, as metabolic diseases of the GBA. It is this reconceptualization that validates the targeting of the Trp metabolism—through microbial, nutritional, or enzymatic interventions—as a rational and promising therapeutic strategy for restoring communication and health along the gut-brain continuum. Importantly, despite rapid progress, major knowledge gaps remain regarding causal mechanisms, cohort heterogeneity, and translational validity, highlighting the need for longitudinal, multi-omics, and intervention-based studies to enable precision targeting of Trp metabolic pathways.
