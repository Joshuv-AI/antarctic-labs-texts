Community-based health insurance beneficiaries’ satisfaction with health service provision and its predictors at outpatient department of public health facilities in Ethiopia: findings from a cross-sectional study
Authors: Teklu Debebe, Adamu Wondimtekahu, Bereket Habte, Kidist Dereje, Alemayehu Bedassa, Wudalew Meselu Tesfaye, Hilina Faye, Dereje Mengistu, Yamerot Andualem, Tesfaye Worku, Geremew Tesgera, Tigist Yoseph, Frehiwot Abebe, Muluken Argaw, Adamtachew Beyene, Mekonnen Ararso, Fikru Teshome, Wodaje Gietaneh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEthiopia’s Community-based health insurance (CBHI) is currently providing financial protection to more than 54 million beneficiaries (64% of the target population). Beneficiaries’ satisfaction with health service provision is among the important factors that influence the decision to enroll and renew. Despite the importance of satisfaction in influencing such important decisions, there is limited evidence on systematic studies conducted at a national level on CBHI beneficiaries’ satisfaction with health care service provision. Hence, this study aimed to measure the level of CBHI beneficiaries’ satisfaction with health service provision and identify its determinants in Ethiopia.MethodsThis study employed a health facility-based cross-sectional study design. A total of 1,836 patients who made OPD visits were sampled, yielding a 99.9% response rate. The mean value of a composite variable derived from satisfaction items, was used to measure the level of satisfaction. Factors determining patient satisfaction were measured using binary logistic regression model, and variables with p-values < 0.05 were considered the final predictors of satisfaction.ResultsOverall, 58% [(95% CI: 55.8, 60.4)] of the patients were satisfied with the health service they received. Factors associated with higher satisfaction include fully receiving prescribed medicines [AOR = 5.31, 95% CI; 3.74, 8.12], partially receiving prescribed medicines [AOR = 1.92, 95% CI; 1.27, 2.9], being informed about health problem [AOR = 1.84, 95% CI; 1.47, 2.31], being informed about recovery methods [AOR = 1.61, 95% CI; 1.28, 2.03], patient’s perception about conformity of service with benefit package [AOR = 1.4, 95% CI; 1.12, 1.73], visiting urban health facility [AOR = 1.34, 95% CI; 1.07, 1.67], and being in older age group. The study revealed a lower satisfaction at hospitals (53%) compared with health centers (61%).ConclusionNotably, a substantial proportion of patients (42%) were not satisfied, a rate higher than reported in comparable studies. Dissatisfaction was predominantly associated with shortages of prescribed medicines, inadequate laboratory services, and exposure to out-of-pocket payments. These findings highlight the need for coordinated efforts among health authorities and service providers to ensure uninterrupted availability of prescribed medicines and diagnostic services in alignment with the CBHI benefit package. Addressing these gaps is critical to fostering trust and improving satisfaction among CBHI beneficiaries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13490-2.Keywords: Community-based health insurance, Satisfaction, Health service provision, Beneficiary

Background

Community-based health insurance (CBHI) programs have become important mechanisms for improving access to healthcare services and providing financial protection, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. In the African context, including Ethiopia, CBHI programs have gained importance as a means to achieve universal health coverage (UHC) goals [2, 3]. Many studies have highlighted the role of CBHI in expanding health coverage and reducing catastrophic health expenditures among vulnerable populations.

The Ethiopian government has been implementing CBHI programs since 2011, with the main objectives of improving access to modern healthcare services and providing financial protection to citizens. The CBHI was first piloted between 2011 and 2013 in 13 selected districts of the four agrarian regions of Ethiopia [4]. CBHI implementation has now been scaled up to all regions and city administrations in the country. As of 2023, the total population coverage of CBHI reached more than 54 million beneficiaries (which is 64% of the target population) and 81% of the districts in the country [5].

The CBHI benefit package includes a range of health care services that are provided in outpatient and inpatient departments of public health facilities, including medicines listed under the CBHI benefit package. Health insurance beneficiaries can access health services with contracted health facilities at all levels of the healthcare tier system, i.e., from health centers to tertiary level hospitals, following the referral system.

To ensure CBHI beneficiaries are accessing the full range of health services included in the benefit package, it is important to conduct regular monitoring and follow-up in the program implementation. One way of doing this is through assessing the satisfaction of CBHI beneficiaries with the health care service they received.

Patient satisfaction represents the gap in expectation of the patient about the health service and the actual experience and often it is also viewed as an established indicator of the quality of care [6]. Assessing patient satisfaction helps to identify and address gaps in service delivery. Several studies show that a satisfied patient contributes to improved health care outcomes as it facilitates patients to adhere to the treatment and advocate about the health care service [6]. In the case of CBHI beneficiary, in addition to the direct health outcome, satisfaction with health service is an important factor that influences decision to renewal of CBHI membership. If CBHI beneficiaries do not access health care services listed under the CBHI benefit package, they would be exposed to out-of-pocket payments. This would lead them to loose trust in the program and influence their decision to drop out from membership [7]. Research conducted [8] to explore the factors affecting uptake of members to CBHI shows that negative perceptions related to service quality, administrative inefficiencies, and the affordability of premiums have been reported, influencing member satisfaction and retention. Similarly, studies conducted in Ethiopia to assess the satisfaction of CBHI members’ shows that adequate health service provision is significant determinant of satisfaction [9–11]. Therefore, the satisfaction of beneficiaries is a very important factor that determines their renewal decision and hence is crucial for the sustainability of the health insurance program.

Despite the importance of beneficiaries’ satisfaction in influencing important decisions such as renewal, which, in turn is crucial for the sustainability of the program, there is limited evidence on systematic measurement conducted on the level and determinants of satisfaction of CBHI beneficiaries with health service provisions at the national level. Hence, this study is carried out to measure CBHI beneficiaries’ satisfaction with health service provisions and their determining factors. The evidence from the study can be translated into policy and program designs aimed at improving the quality of the CBHI program, and ultimately achieve its objectives of enhancing access, affordability, and quality of health care for the target population.

Methods

Study area and setting

The study was carried out in seven regions (Amhara, Oromia, Southern Nations Nationalities and Peoples Region, Sidama, Southwest Ethiopia, Harari, and Gambella) and two city administrations (Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa) of Ethiopia. In total, there were 973 CBHI schemes in these regions and city administrations at the end of 2023 with about 10 million CBHI members (75% enrollment rate) and more than 50 million CBHI beneficiaries [5].

Sample selection technique and procedures: The sample selection followed a three-stage stratified sampling procedure to select study participants specified in the sample size. Data collection tools and procedures

A structured questionnaire was developed for the patient exit interview. The questionnaire was prepared after reviewing relevant studies and adapted to fit specific context of this study [13–15]. It included socio-demographic characteristics of the participants, satisfaction items on a Likert scale, and health care provision related questions. The questionnaire was translated into the Amharic, Oromoiffa, and Sidama languages. The questionnaire was developed using ODK collect to enable web based data collection and transfer.

Data quality control measures

The questionnaire was tested at selected health facilities in the Addis Ababa city administration and in the Oromia region. Experienced and trained data collectors and supervisors with at least a first degree education were deployed for data collection in the study. Three-day training was provided to the data collectors and supervisors on the data collection tools and the sampling procedures. Close follow-up and support were provided by supervisors throughout the data collection process to ensure the quality of the data. The researchers coordinated the data collection process and checked its completeness and consistency during this period.

Data processing and analysis

The data collected using a web based “ODK collect tool” was downloaded from the server in excel format and exported to SPSS version 21 and cleaned to check for its completeness and missing value. The internal consistency and reliability of the satisfaction items were checked using Cronbach’s alpha, which was found to be 0.871, confirming the internal consistency of reliability of the measurement [16]. For descriptive statistics part, frequencies, proportion, and mean were calculated and for empirical analysis binary logistic regression model was applied. Results of the analysis were presented in text, tables, and graphs as deemed appropriate.

Measuring level of satisfaction: Patient satisfaction was measured using twelve satisfaction items administered on a five-point Likert scale (strongly disagree = 1, disagree = 2, indifferent = 3, agree = 4, strongly agree = 5) which, together gives a maximum score of 60 and a minimum of 12. A composite variable was created by summing the responses from the twelve satisfaction items for each respondent. Finally, the study participants were labeled “satisfied” if they scored a value equal or above the mean value of the composite variable and “not satisfied” if their score was below the mean of the composite variable [17].

Identifying determinants of satisfaction: Factors that determine the level of satisfaction of CBHI beneficiaries were measured using binary logistic regression model. The dependent variable was a dichotomous variable with a value of 1 if the respondent is “satisfied” and a value of 0 if the respondent is “not satisfied”. The independent variables were socio-demographic characteristics and health provider-related variables. The final predictor of satisfaction was identified using a multivariate logistic regression model, and variables with p-values for AOs (adjusted odd ratios) less than 0.05 were considered predictors. Hosmer and Lomeshow test was used to check for goodness-of-fit of the model and the test was found to be insignificant (p = 0.21), which indicates that the model was adequate. Similarly, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to check for multicollineartiy in the independent variables. The VIF for all independent variables was found to be less than 2, confirming the absence of multicollineartiy.

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

Out of the total sample of 1836 participants, the study covered 1,834, with a response rate of 99.9%. Among the total participants included in the study, 52.6% were female and 47.4% male. About 38.4% of the participants were between the ages of 31 and 50 years, whereas only 11.5% were above 61 years. The majorities (80.6%) of the respondents were married (Table 1).

Factors associated with patient satisfaction

The multivariate logistic regression model revealed that four variables related to health service provision and health facilities appeared to have strong association with satisfaction (Table 3). The variables include being explained about health problem, being informed about methods of recovery, medicine availability, and perceptions about the conformity of the services received with the CBHI benefit package. Patients whose health problems were explained to them by the physician were 1.8 times more likely to be satisfied than those who were not informed (95% CI: 1.47–2.31, p < 0.0001). Similarly, patients who reported that they received adequate information from the physician on methods that help recovery were 1.6 times more likely to be satisfied than patients who did not receive adequate information (95% CI: 1.28–2.03, p < 0.0001).

Results of the multivariate regression model (predictors of patient satisfaction)

The Availability of prescribed medicines at health facilities was found to be a strong predictor of patient satisfaction. Patients who received the medicines prescribed to them in full amount were 5.3 times more likely to be satisfied compared to their counterparts who did not receive any medicine (95% CI: 3.47–8.12, p < 0.000). Likewise, patients who partially received prescribed medicines were 1.9 times more likely to be satisfied than their counterparts who did not (95% CI: 1.27–2.91, p < 0.0001). Similarly, the patients’ perceptions of the service they received regarding its conformity with the CBHI benefit package had a significant effect on their satisfaction. Accordingly, those patients who answered that the service they received was as per the CBHI benefit package were 1.4 times more likely to be satisfied than those patients who felt that the service was not in conformity with the CBHI benefit package (95% CI: 1.12–1.73, p = 0.003) (Table 3).

Regarding factors related to socio-demographic characteristics, three variables (age, type of health facility, and location of health facility) appeared to be strongly associated with patient satisfaction (Table 3). Patients above the age of 61 years were 1.67 times more likely to be satisfied than patients younger than 30 years (95% CI: 1.16–2.39, p = 0.006). Compared with patients who visited health facilities located in rural areas, those who visited urban health facilities were 1.34 times more likely to be satisfied (95% CI: 1.07–1.67, p = 0.012). Patients who visited higher-level health facilities experienced lower levels of satisfaction. The odds of being satisfied were 31% lower for patients who visited general hospitals than for those who visited health centers (95% CI: 0.50–0.95, p = 0.02). Similarly, patients who visited tertiary hospitals had 50% lower satisfaction than their counterparts who visited health centers (95% CI: 0.35–0.73, p = 0.000). This finding is somewhat contrary to expectations since improved service is expected at higher levels.

Discussion

Overall patient satisfaction

The findings revealed that over half of the patients (58%) [(95% CI: 55.8, 60.4)] were satisfied with the health service provision. The level of satisfaction reported in this study is lower than satisfaction level of 65.8% [(95% CI: 60.7, 71.2%] reported from a study conducted in southern Ethiopia among CBHI beneficiaries. [18, 19]. Another study conducted on patient satisfaction in South Wollo, Ethiopia [20, 21] reported an overall satisfaction level of 64. % [(95% CI: 59.3, 67.6)], which is again higher than the satisfaction level of the current study. Interestingly, no significant variation in the level of satisfaction was found based on CBHI membership (between paying and indigent) and sex (men and women), implying that the program is likely to meet the needs and expectations of both groups in a fairly equitable manner. These are very important equity dimensions that need to be regularly monitored. Generally, patients reported lower satisfaction scores regarding the availability of medicine, laboratory service, waiting time, and protection from out-of-pocket payments, indicating a need for strong attention in this area.

Factors associated with patient satisfaction

The study found that both health providers’ related and socio-demographic factors predict patient satisfaction with varying levels of significance. Among the health providers’ related factors, adequately explaining health problems to patients and providing adequate information to patients, which helps recovery, were found to have strong association with satisfaction. The results of the current study are supported by findings from a study conducted at health centers in central Ethiopia, which revealed that good dialogue including clearly explaining about the name and cause of the illness to patients by the physician were significantly associated with satisfaction [22]. Another study [23] made in Ethiopia also revealed that the presence of good dialogue with service providers and the maintenance of privacy during care have strong positive association with satisfaction. A study [24] made on patient satisfaction in Asian countries indicated that patient-physician communication was the most powerful predictor of satisfaction. The consistently observed positive association between satisfaction and physician treatment across multiple studies suggests that enhancing this relationship could play a key role in improving overall satisfaction.

The other variable related to the health provider side that was strongly associated with satisfaction was the proportion of prescribed medicine being available to patients. Patients who secured all prescribed medicines at the health facility were five times more likely to be satisfied. This finding is not surprising, as a shortage of medicines at health facilities is the most challenging issue in the health system of the country in general and in the implementation of CBHI in particular. A finding from a study conducted in Ethiopia strongly supports this result. Patients who secured prescribed medicines were more than twice as likely to be satisfied as those who did not. Similarly, respondents who did not receive all the required services from the hospital were 22% less satisfied than their counterparts who received the service (AOR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.41, 0.96) [24].

In this study, the critical role of medicine availability in patient satisfaction was evident from participants’ responses regarding priority areas for improvement. When asked which aspects of care they would like to see improved, the majority of patients (90%) identified the availability of medicines as their top priority. Additionally, among those who said they are not willing to revisit the health facility (6%) or to renew their CBHI membership (5%), medicine shortages were cited as the primary reason for their decision. These findings indicate that interventions aimed at improving the quality of health services and patient satisfaction should prioritize addressing gaps in medicine availability.

Similarly, those patients who reported that the health care service they received was as per the CBHI benefit package were about 1.38 times more likely to be satisfied compared with those who didn’t feel the service was as per the benefit package. This is in line with a finding from a study conducted in China, which reported that fulfillment of patient’s expectations of insurance benefits during service delivery positively influenced their satisfaction [25].

Another finding in this study, and potentially debatable was the relatively higher satisfaction observed at health centers than at hospitals. The satisfaction of visitors at general and tertiary hospitals decreased by 29 and 46% respectively, compared with those of health center visitors. This finding is supported by the findings of a study conducted in Ethiopia [18], which revealed that health center visitors were 2.7 times more likely to be satisfied than hospital visitors (95% CI = 1.79, 2.85). Similarly, another patient satisfaction study made in Ethiopia also reported that patients who visited health centers were 2.3 times more likely to be satisfied compared to patients who attended health care at hospitals. Physical accessibility might be one reason for the high level of satisfaction at health centers; as most health centers are located near to beneficiaries, they are likely to reduce travel time and cost, easing additional burdens for beneficiaries. The higher satisfaction at health centers might also be attributed to the shorter waiting time at health centers than at hospitals, which might increase the degree of comfort for health center visitors. In the present study, the mean waiting time for hospitals was significantly higher than the waiting time at health centers. Moreover, supply-side factors might also be another possible reason for low levels of satisfaction at hospitals. For example, the current study shows that proportion of patients who received all the medicine prescribed to them at health centers was 11% higher than the proportion of patients who received all the medicine at the hospitals. The proportion of patients at hospital who expressed positive response about the protection they received from out-of-pocket payment during the exit interview was 7% lower than that of health center visitors. These are very serious issues that are likely to reduce patient satisfaction. Hence, it is imperative to fill such gaps observed at hospitals and improve financial protection for beneficiaries.

Among socio- demographic characteristics, the age of the study participants and the location of the health facilities were found to have strong association with patient satisfaction. Patients above the age of 60 were 1.6 times more likely to be satisfied than patients less than 30 years old. Similarly, the odds of being satisfied increased by 30% for patients between the ages of 46 and 60 years compared with their younger counterparts. This result is in line with the findings from other studies in Ethiopia, which finds that for patients over 34 years old the likelihood of being satisfied was increased by more than twofold (AOR = 2.07; 95% CI = 1.28–2.85) [18]. This finding could be attributed to various reasons; older individuals often tend to have large family sizes and more dependents, which increase the economic burden on the household. In this regard, the financial protection they receive through CBHI is expected to ease some portion of the economic burden they experience; hence, they might easily become satisfied with the health services they receive through their membership, which they cannot afford easily otherwise. This might imply the importance of age-specific interventions in our approach to improve the satisfaction of CBHI beneficiaries.

Regarding the location of the health facility, those patients who attended their health care at a health facility in urban area were 1.3 times more likely to be satisfied. This might be related to availability of better health work force and medical supplies in urban areas relative to rural areas. Health facilities in rural areas of Ethiopia are often disadvantaged compared to their urban counterparts due to their remoteness from the center. Comparing the finding with others, a study made in Ghana found no statistically significant difference in patient satisfaction between urban and rural health facilities [26].

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations worth considering. The study focused primarily on investigating the demand side, i.e., the perspectives and satisfaction levels of CBHI beneficiaries. However, a comprehensive understanding of the challenges and opportunities of the CBHI program necessitates an examination of the supply side such as health care providers and CBHI schemes. Hence, the limitation of not examining the supply sides might result in missing some opportunities that would enable us to identify systemic issues and areas for improvement that directly impact beneficiary satisfaction.

Another potential limitation of the study relates to social desirability bias, meaning that participants might feel the urge to answer positive responses, believing that it would be viewed favorably by the CBHI system and health care providers. To reduce such biases, the interviewers explained the objective of the study and the importance of truthful responses to realize these objectives. Moreover, it was clearly explained to them that their identity would not be revealed at any stage of the analysis and report. Despite these efforts, some bias may still exist.

Conclusions

The finding revealed that a considerable proportion of patients (42%) were not satisfied with the health care provided at health facilities - a rate notably higher than that reported in comparable studies – indicating the need for strong effort to make improvements. Interestingly, no significant differences in the level of satisfaction were observed between paying and indigent CBHI members as well as men and women, implying that the program is likely meeting the needs and expectations of both groups in an equitable manner. These are very important equity dimensions that need to be regularly monitored.

Ultimately, the availability of prescribed medicines, adequacy of explanations provided to patients regarding their health status, the quality of advice received on recovery methods were pivotal in determining the satisfaction of patients. Among the satisfaction dimensions used, patients reported relatively higher positive ratings for their consultations with health workers, particularly with regard to the respect and attention received, as well as the perceived adequacy of the diagnostic process. Hence, strengthening these aspects of care represent a critical opportunity to improve patient satisfaction. On the other hand, the patients reported relatively lower satisfaction with respect to availability of prescribed medicines, laboratory services, and protection from out-of-pocket payments. A substantial proportion (30%) of patients expressed their dissatisfaction with the financial protection provided. Without prompt interventions, these issues may lead to member’s dropout from the CBHI scheme and could undermine the overall sustainability of the program.

Overall, the findings highlight the critical need for coordinated efforts by the Ministry of Health, regional health bureaus, Health Insurance Service, Pharmaceutical Supply Service, health care providers and other relevant bodies to ensure the consistent availability of medicines, laboratory services and other essential diagnostic services. Addressing these gaps will contribute to the delivery of improved quality of healthcare in alignment with the CBHI benefit package and foster greater trust and enhance satisfaction among beneficiaries.
