Healthcare providers’ knowledge, perceptions and practices of malaria control strategies in the Republic of Guinea: insights from a qualitative study
Authors: Elhadj Marouf Diallo, Alice Langlet, Letitia A Onyango, Mory 1 Kourouma, Banavona Melaine Kambou, Fatoumata Battouly Diallo, Alixia Bufferne, Anastasia Petrova, Maeliane Deyra, Chloe Gay, Alioune Camara, Laurent Gerbaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite ongoing malaria control efforts, barriers such as delayed diagnosis, poor quality treatment and out-of-pocket expenditure persist. This study explores healthcare providers’ knowledge, perceptions and practice of malaria control interventions to optimise strategies.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted between December 2022 and March 2023. Data were collected in nine health districts through 63 individual interviews and 18 focus groups involving healthcare providers. The Clark and Braun thematic approach was used to categorise key ideas into subthemes and themes, including prevention, case management, provider perceptions and financing, and then to analyse the transcripts according to the six steps. The final themes and subthemes were clearly defined and illustrated by quotes to ensure interpretative transparency and coherence. The COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) checklist was used to verify the quality of the results report. Qualitative results were complemented by descriptive analysis of the codes, subthemes and themes using R software.Results264 modalities, grouped into 45 subthemes and 9 themes were identified. A high level of knowledge about malaria and its risks, particularly for vulnerable groups, was observed. Healthcare providers highlighted gaps in integrated vector control related to insecticide-treated net (ITN) distribution, use and larval destruction. Furthermore, the participants considered that seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) started with delays in covered districts. Non-compliant practices with national case management guidelines were reported by providers. These mainly related to overcharging for services linked to malaria, provider complacency, poor reception of patients and over-reliance on clinical experience for diagnosis. Additionally, some healthcare providers often viewed malaria patients as poor, neglectful or unwilling to follow medical advice. It was noted that many only sought appropriate care after failing to self-medicate or developing complications. The healthcare providers emphasised the importance of strengthening community-based control initiatives, distributing resources to health centres for managing complicated cases and improving the remuneration of healthcare providers.ConclusionsThis study identifies persistent operational barriers to malaria control in Guinea, including weaknesses in free case management, SMC implementation and ITN design. These challenges require sustained improvements, and future research will focus on community perceptions and use of malaria prevention measures.

Introduction

The Republic of Guinea is characterised as a stable malaria-endemic country, with peaks during the rainy season and a national prevalence of 17% in 2021, and asymptomatic infection rates reaching up to 60% in certain districts.1 2 In 2023, malaria accounted for 47% of outpatient consultations and 21% of hospitalisations,3 making it one of the most common infections encountered by healthcare providers, particularly in primary healthcare settings. Given its magnitude and economic impact, the country has intensified efforts to control malaria transmission and reduce the associated economic burden. Since 2007, Guinea has provided free insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy (IPTp) and, starting in 2010, free malaria diagnosis and treatment.4 5 Under the new National Strategic Plan for Malaria Control 2023–2027, the country aims to achieve an 80% reduction in malaria incidence and mortality by intensifying primary and secondary prevention interventions.6 Achieving this objective depends on mobilising adequate human and financial resources, investing in research and ensuring the early identification and management of malaria cases. The operationalisation of this plan requires the active involvement of healthcare providers and community health workers (CHWs), who are in direct contact with the population. Healthcare provider adherence to national recommendations for primary and secondary prevention will be a critical factor in the effective deployment of these interventions. For instance, in its certification of Azerbaijan and Tajikistan as malaria-free, the WHO emphasised the role of healthcare providers in testing, diagnosing and promptly treating cases with appropriate antimalarial drugs, alongside monitoring environmental, entomological and epidemiological risk factors by malaria control actors.7

The latest Malaria Indicator Survey in Guinea showed that 33% of the population had used an ITN, with differences between regions and urban and rural areas. In terms of malaria prevention among pregnant women, 50% had received at least three doses of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine as part of IPTp. In terms of malaria case management among children aged under 5 years, 32% sought care within 24–48 hours of symptom onset, and of these, 28% underwent blood testing for malaria diagnosis. Of those who received treatment, 38% were treated with artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT).8 However, the national malaria case management guidelines require systematic testing of all febrile cases, with confirmed cases treated with ACTs or related derivatives. Additionally, seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC), implemented for children aged 3–59 months during the high transmission season (July–October) since 2015, currently covers 17 of the country’s 33 districts. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that malaria patients often navigate multiple care options before receiving a proper diagnosis and treatment.9 This leads to out-of-pocket expenses for patients or their families, sometimes reaching up to US$ 38, and delays in appropriate care.10 11 The epidemiological data on malaria for communities is available, but there is a lack of information on the practices and perceptions of healthcare providers regarding the prevention measures currently implemented. These operational stakeholders are the fundamental link in the deployment of control measures. A nuanced understanding of healthcare providers and users’ perceptions and perspectives of primary and secondary malaria prevention measures is essential for adjusting or reorienting interventions to optimise their use. This study aims to conduct an in-depth analysis of healthcare providers’ knowledge, perceptions and practices of malaria control interventions implemented in the Republic of Guinea.

Working definitions

Karamoko

Muslim religious healers who use their knowledge of religion and medicinal plants to offer potential remedies for drinking or washing. Communities call on their services when the cause of a health problem is considered supernatural (devil, witch, evil eye or curse).

Clinics

Private care facilities, commonly referred to as clinics by communities and care providers. This includes private facilities at different levels, such as private practices (primary healthcare), private dispensaries corresponding to the health centre level and private polyclinics equivalent to referral hospital level with hospitalisation options.

Healthcare providers

Healthcare professionals, including physicians, nurses, midwives and technical health workers; CHWs, including community relays and their supervisors, and community health agents; laboratory technicians and their support staff.

Malaria chemoprevention

Mass or routine interventions using specific drugs to prevent malaria episodes in targeted groups. These include: (1) SMC targeting children aged under 5 or 10 years according to the national malaria control policy during peak transmission periods; (2) perennial chemoprevention for children aged under 1 or 2 years in high-transmission areas throughout the year; (3) chemoprevention for pregnant women known as IPTp; and (4) chemoprevention for travellers or high-risk groups such as non-immune individuals or those in at-risk professions (eg, forestry or mining workers).12 This study focuses on SMC and IPTp.

Malaria prevention

This refers to the various means used in the fight against malaria in the Republic of Guinea. Primary prevention involves vector control, such as ITNs, larval and adult vector control and chemoprevention. Chemoprevention is important for preventing the clinical forms of the disease, especially its severe forms. Secondary control measures include early diagnosis and treatment at the community level and in public and private health facilities.6

Methods

Study setting

In Guinea, the entire population is at risk of contracting malaria. The public healthcare sector is organised in a pyramidal structure: the primary level consists of CHWs, health posts and health centres. In 2021, there were 2051 health posts and 423 health centres. The secondary level consists of referral hospitals, including 26 districts, 8 regional hospitals and 8 communal medical centres. At the tertiary level, three national hospitals make up the Conakry University Hospital Centre.13 The private healthcare sector is present at all three levels of the health pyramid and is less regulated. There are more than 606 private health facilities, of which 110 are integrated into the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP) through regular supply of commodities and routine data reporting.

Site selection

One health district was randomly selected from each of the eight administrative regions. Due to the variation in malaria prevalence between the regions (less than 1% in Conakry and 30% in Nzerekore), one district was sampled from each region to ensure representativeness across different areas. However, two districts were sampled in the Nzerekore region due to its size (six health districts) and malaria prevalence (30%). A total of nine health districts were sampled. In each district, the referral hospital (district, regional or national level) was selected. Then, one urban health centre, two rural health centres, two health posts and a private facility integrated into the NMCP were randomly sampled (online supplemental figure 1: Maps of district selection). At the hospital level, the emergency, paediatrics and internal medicine departments were selected. In total, 9 hospitals, 9 urban health centres and 18 rural health centres and health posts were sampled.

Study design

This is a qualitative cross-sectional study based on in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs). Data were collected between December 2022 and March 2023 at health facilities and community health centres. This study is part of the ‘Determinants of the therapeutic itinerary of malaria patients in The Republic of Guinea’ research project. The theoretical model used for this research project is Axel Kroeger’s conceptual framework developed in 1983.14 This framework has been adapted to the Guinean context and enriched with concepts from the Self Determination Theory (SDT) and the delayed care-seeking model.15 16

Study population

Care providers and CHWs were selected in each health facility sampled. They included physicians, nurses, midwives, technical health workers, laboratory assistants and CHWs, including the community relays, as government servants, contract workers, interns or community volunteers. With a mission order from the NMCP, approval was obtained from the district health authorities and the heads of health facilities. The NMCP shared an email requesting support from technical assistants. Healthcare providers were recruited for individual interviews and focus groups based on their involvement in malaria management activities.

In each health district, 10 IDIs were planned in rural areas. This number was considered sufficient to reach data saturation on a national level, as no new information was emerging beyond this point. The distribution of interviews included a maximum of two per rural health centre, one per health post, four with CHWs (two per rural commune) and two per private facility. All interviewees were active healthcare providers directly involved in malaria case management at the time of the study. The choice to conduct individual interviews in rural areas was justified by the limited number of available healthcare providers. In addition, two FGDs were planned in each referral hospital and urban health centre. The unit managers (paediatrics, general medicine, emergency) or urban health centre managers provided approval and identified focus group participants. The minimum required for each focus group was 5, and the maximum was 12. A total of 153 people, divided across 18 FGDs, took part in the discussion groups (online supplemental table 1). Overall, 10 IDIs and 2 FGDs with healthcare providers were carried out in each selected health district.

Data collection

Two semi-structured interview guides were developed and tested with healthcare providers in the Conakry region. Both guides included questions on knowledge of malaria, its means of transmission, perceptions of severity and patients’ healthcare-seeking behaviour. Other questions covered the management of malaria cases in health facilities and at the community level. For individual interviews, additional elements such as gender, age, socioprofessional category and level of education were included. For focus groups, the additional characteristics recorded included total number of participants, gender, age, years of experience and socioprofessional categories. The data collectors were undergraduate students at the end of their medical training cycle and one master’s degree student. They were trained with the support of the NMCP and the Public Health Chair of the Faculty of Health Sciences and Techniques at the University of Conakry. Three interviewers were deployed in each selected health district, working in pairs to conduct interviews or focus groups.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants for individual interviews and the focus groups. The participants were informed of the project objectives and how the data would be used. The confidentiality of personal information and the freedom to participate, refuse or interrupt the interviews at any time were respected. For focus groups, a number was assigned to each participant. All audio files were recorded and uploaded with KoboCollect. Regarding data saturation, predefined objectives and monitoring of audio files uploaded to KoboToolbox by data collectors made it possible to track new information as it emerged. As the predefined analysis approach was inductive, tracking was based solely on the questions included in the interview guides. This approach ensured sufficient data richness and diversity of perspectives across all districts.

Transcription and analysis

Transcription

Audio files were stored in KoboToolbox Cloud and transcribed in French. Of the 81 interviews, 14 were in Guinean local languages (Fulani, Mandinka and Sussu), and 3 were lost due to download errors. Of the 19 focus groups, one audio file was lost. Thus, three different contributors (a master’s student in health evaluation and two medical interns) transcribed the 63 interviews and 18 focus groups in French, and another contributor transcribed the local language interviews, which were then translated into French. For specific words or expressions related to the local context, team discussions were held to find the appropriate expressions or words for transcription. Each interview or focus group was transcribed in full, and keywords and expressions were annotated during this phase.

Categorisation and codification

An inductive method based on Clark and Braun’s thematic approach was employed.17 This method analyses data flexibly from a wide range of research questions and theoretical perspectives. This enables researchers to: (1) explore people’s experiences, understandings and representations of phenomena; (2) analyse various types of data (eg, interviews, media sources, focus groups); (3) manage small and large datasets; and (4) produce data-driven or theory-informed analyses. In this study, this approach was used to construct themes through which the perceptions, practices and attitudes of service providers were analysed for individual and group discussions. Discussions, notes, nodes and themes were iteratively developed, tested and refined. All transcripts were proofread by study contributors and the principal investigator to ensure that transcription errors, including comprehension or typing errors, were corrected.

Keywords and sentences, documented during transcription and proofreading, were classified as nodes (appropriate subthemes). These nodes led to broader themes covering the different areas of malaria control in Guinea. To ensure the quality of the categorisation approach used, another expert was consulted. The coding grid was iterated through a preliminary test phase involving 12 individual interviews and 3 focus groups. A total of 264 fine modalities, grouped into 45 subthemes, structured around 9 themes (online supplemental table 2) were consolidated. An Excel file was used, and a unique identification code was assigned to each interview and focus group. All interviews and focus groups were coded by a team of three contributors, including the main investigator.

Analysis

R V.4.4.0 (RStudio 2023.12.0+369) was used to perform descriptive analyses through word clouds, frequency tables and graphical representations. These analyses supported the qualitative phase of the study by quantifying the occurrence and distribution of codes and subthemes across interviews and FGDs. During the reporting step of the thematic analysis, the final themes, subthemes and codes were clearly defined, named and supported by illustrative quotes to ensure transparency and coherence between the data and the analytical interpretation. A conceptual framework of the dimensions of healthcare providers’ choices of the therapeutic approaches to malaria cases was developed according to the results obtained from the analysis of the various themes, which were not predefined.

Malaria knowledge

According to healthcare providers’ narratives, 10 subthemes were identified, including modes of transmission, perception of severity, malaria symptoms, differential diagnosis, the parasite cycle and the difference between complicated and uncomplicated cases (addressed in focus groups only) and the vulnerable groups. Overall, these findings demonstrate a relatively comprehensive understanding of malaria, shaped by both formal training and practical experience. However, misconceptions or incomplete explanations suggest the persistence of contextual and experiential knowledge alongside biomedical understanding.

Four perceived specific codes were identified for the malaria transmission modes: mosquito bites, transplacental transmission, blood transfusion and dirty water (transmission source). The predominance of mosquito bites in participants’ narratives reflects a strong understanding of the primary vector-borne nature of malaria transmission. Mentions of blood transfusion and mother-to-child (transplacental) transmission demonstrate awareness of less common but clinically recognised transmission routes. These pieces of information reflect a good understanding of the mechanisms of malaria transmission, with links between the lack of environmental sanitation and the prevalence of mosquitoes responsible for transmission.

Malaria is transmitted by the bite of a female mosquito, already infected, which transmits the disease from a sick man to a healthy man. (nurse, male, rural health centre)

You know, in the gutters, where the mosquito settles in the dirt or in stagnant water, it will come to bite people in their homes and give them malaria. (community relay, male, rural area).

Care providers also discussed the parasite development stages, particularly its evolution in the liver and blood. The female Anopheles mosquito and the blood stage of the parasite were the most addressed codes, with the parasitic nature of Plasmodium raised in 15 of the 18 focus groups. This reflects a solid conceptual understanding of the biological cycle of malaria, especially among those with laboratory or clinical responsibilities.

There are still cases where you can think that the schizonts may remain in the liver and with microscopy, you won’t see parasites. The density is a little low and we neglect it, but the cycle continues. (focus group, Fg15)

For recognition of malaria symptoms, 17 elements were identified, ranging from uncomplicated manifestations (fever, headache, chills) to complications, such as cardiorespiratory collapse and confusion. Children and pregnant women were consistently identified as the most vulnerable groups, indicating awareness of risk stratification in malaria morbidity. These insights underline healthcare providers’ clinical vigilance and their emphasis on early management to prevent complications, reflecting both professional experience and the effect of continuous training.

Malaria is a serious disease because not only does it cause neurological disorders, but it also leads to collapse and respiratory distress. Complications can even lead to death if not adequately treated. (focus group, Fg18)

Malaria kills if not treated in time. It’s a very fearsome disease, especially in the very young. Children aged under five can’t complain, and they can’t know exactly what’s hurting them. It’s only their behaviour that warns you that something’s wrong. If left untreated, it can kill in its most serious form. (nurse, male, rural health centre).

In terms of severity perception, a total of 24 care providers perceived malaria as a potentially fatal disease. Providers cited several complications and consequences of malaria, including abortion in pregnant women, maternal–fetal complications and high mortality if treatment is delayed.

As a midwife, I’m going to say that this is a serious disease because if a pregnant woman develops severe malaria, malaria can cause anaemia in the woman, abortion, miscarriage and premature delivery and even stillbirth. (focus group, Fg9).

The least predominant subthemes concerned community beliefs, differential diagnosis and biological signs. A few healthcare providers reported that some patients still attributed severe malaria symptoms to a ‘bird disease’, reflecting the persistence of traditional explanatory models within communities. While these beliefs were less frequently mentioned, they remain clinically relevant, as they can delay care-seeking and influence treatment adherence. This underscores the importance of strengthening community health education alongside provider training to ensure coherent messaging on malaria causation and prevention.

Malaria prevention

Care providers discussed malaria chemoprevention, ITNs and environmental sanitation as primary forms of malaria prevention. The use of ITNs was the most frequently cited, in 32 interviews and 14 focus groups. The narratives on malaria chemoprevention included knowledge of drug use, timing and target groups for chemoprevention, while IPTp was mainly discussed by midwives and antenatal consultation practitioners. For SMC, as one of the most impactful malaria prevention interventions currently implemented, the healthcare providers reported the substantial reductions in malaria incidence in districts where it was implemented. However, several participants highlighted delays in campaign initiation, particularly the exclusion of June, a month marking both the onset of agricultural activities and heightened exposure to mosquito bites due to extended outdoor activity.

With SMC during the rainy season, cases have dropped significantly. Malaria used to be much more prevalent in July, August, and September. When they took the initiative of organizing these campaigns at the end of each month of July, August, September, and October, we reduced the number of malaria cases to very few compared with before. (nurse, male, rural health centre)

I find that SMC is late, whereas it’s more a question of prevention. For example, at Mali, the campaign sometimes starts in July or August. We need to start at least in June and continue until October, as most cases of malaria occur during this period at Mali district. (focus group, Fg2).

Providers discussed strengths and weaknesses of ITN distribution mechanisms and ITN use among community members. Although respondents indicated that ITNs were effective, they also reported challenges with proper use. The discussions emphasise that the most important factor is the use of ITNs, not their coverage.

The donation of ITNs is one of the strengths of the prevention system, but the lack of follow-up after distribution is a weakness. People need to be made aware of the importance of using mosquito nets. If they know the importance, they’ll use them. They give them away for free, but we don’t know how important they are because of a lack of awareness. If I had the power, we would punish those who use mosquito nets in other ways, in their own garden, because it’s true that they give them away for free, but it’s clear that behind it, it’s money that’s spent on us. (focus group, Fg6)

There are some square nets, but we need to increase the number of circular nets, because most people want circular nets. (focus group, Fg2).

Some healthcare providers detailed their use of awareness-raising and community education to challenge ITN misuse and address the limitations of use. In these cases, healthcare providers focus on raising awareness among community members about how to address environmental factors that contribute to mosquito abundance.

Some patients refuse to use impregnated mosquito nets, or they turn them into fences, and we tell them time and time again, use your mosquito nets. The mosquito nets we have given you are not for use in the garde; they are to protect you against malaria. During the rainy season, we insist on eliminating stagnant water. Where the water stagnates, put earth on it so that it doesn’t persist, because that’s where mosquitoes multiply. Sometimes we also tell them that if you have fountains where you can keep water for more than 1 or 2 weeks, you need to close them to prevent mosquitoes from developing in them. (focus group, Fg15)

Malaria means mosquitoes, so let’s tackle mosquitoes by spraying instead of spending millions of dollars on ITNs. Mosquito nets are for sleeping, but during the time people spend outside, in lounges, in bars, they can catch malaria. We know where the larval breeding grounds are; you just have to go and pump where the water is held back, it’s very easy. If we can’t reduce the number of mosquitoes, we’ll always be talking about malaria. Let’s step up our fight against mosquitoes first, otherwise we’ll be bringing in tons of medicines, we’ll be bringing in bales of mosquito nets, and it’ll go on and on. (focus group, Fg10)

Regarding the use of rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), providers reported multiple challenges, including: (1) stock-outs, (2) false-negative results (often linked to self-medication or the limited specificity of RDTs for Plasmodium falciparum), (3) patient resistance, (4) lack of technical skills of healthcare providers, (5) variable quality of RDTs and (6) in some cases, service fees charged to patients. These narratives reveal that while RDTs have become an essential component of malaria case management, their effective use remains constrained by logistical, technical and behavioural barriers. In addition, providers are aware of cases of residual infection masked during RDTs. The persistence of user fees in some settings also reflects systemic gaps between national policy and practice, potentially undermining equitable access to diagnosis. Healthcare providers also highlighted challenges with microscopy, including the need for laboratory equipment at health centres, the lack of commodities to perform thick smears tests, and the technical skills to take blood samples. These structural limitations, coupled with variable provider capacity, highlight a critical implementation gap in the diagnosis of malaria cases.

The RDT allows us to screen for malaria, because there is almost no infectious disease that is not characterized by fever. But the RDT alone isn’t enough. For example, someone can have malaria, and you yourself can see clinically that it’s malaria. It’s recommended that as soon as the RDT is positive, we make the diagnosis, but if it’s negative, even if clinically it’s a case of malaria according to our experience, we rule out malaria. In our monthly reports, when the RDT is negative, even if clinically you see that it’s positive, we’re not empowered to confirm that it’s malaria. At our level, this is one of our weaknesses, particularly with the lack of commodities to do microscopy, such as the thick smear test, which really gives us the right result, the parasite count, and even the haemoglobin level, but we don’t have this equipment. (technical health agent, male, rural health post)

With the RDT, it’s mainly Plasmodium falciparum that we’re looking for. But you need a certain number of trophozoites to be able to signal to the strip; otherwise, the test will be negative, but the malaria is there. Only the thick smear test can detect it in this case. And here, when you request the RDT, you are told not to perform microscopy. The RDT only gives you one piece of information, but microscopy gives the details, quantifies the trophozoites, and provides the information you need to make decisions. (focus group, Fg8)

For malaria treatment, most healthcare providers discussed the use of artemether-lumefantrine or Coartem and quinine (for oral treatment) to treat uncomplicated cases. For complicated cases, they highlighted injectable artesunate, injectable artemether, injectable quinine and rectal artesunate (as prereferral treatments). The narrative indicated that adherence to national malaria management guidelines is often hampered by the availability of commodities, especially in rural areas.

It takes more time and trust for us to be able to use certain drugs that others are allowed to use, like artesunate. It’s effective and it’s a first-line treatment. We are not authorized to use artesunate and quinine injectable in health posts; these are weaknesses that affect us. You can do it, but the guidelines don’t allow you to do it, and you must refer the patient. For the referral, there is the economic burden on patients. You refer to Siguiri hospital, which is 50 km away; a patient who has a fever can’t even walk. This is one of the reasons why private facilities are frequented more than health posts, because they do it and it works. (technical health agent, male, rural health post)

Healthcare providers also discussed the need for supportive treatments to improve the patient’s general condition. These additional treatments range from analgesics to blood transfusion, depending on the stage of malaria. In addition, healthcare professionals addressed the effectiveness of ACTs and their derivatives in treating malaria cases and noted that when they are available and used correctly, recovery is rapid.

With the availability of artesunate 60 mg, glucose serum, and quinine, ie, the treatment of severe malaria, which they use, there has always been success. (nurse, male, rural health centre)

Malaria case management also encompassed practices such as traditional and self-treatment, reflecting persistent barriers in access to formal health services. Traditional treatment, often based on medicinal plants or ritual substances, was reported to be widespread in remote areas lacking community health relays. Healthcare providers highlighted recurrent challenges including stock-outs of antimalarial drugs, delayed care-seeking and poor adherence to treatment. Inadequate supply chains and the cost of supportive treatments further constrained case management. Moreover, patients’ dissatisfaction with artemether-lumefantrine for uncomplicated malaria led some providers to prescribe injectable forms intended for severe cases. This trend illustrates both the persistence of therapeutic misconceptions among patients and the adaptation of providers to community expectations in contexts of limited diagnostic and therapeutic options.

People who live in the countryside start with the healer or the karamoko for any illness, because they think that the devil is the source of any illness. (nurse, male, rural health centre).

Some health posts have stock-outs of medicines. Not all health posts receive all necessary medicines, especially at present. There was a health post that was obliged to go to pharmacies to buy medicines, but now many pharmacies are closed. (community relay, male, rural health centre)

There’s a point about malaria that we have noticed here. For simple malaria, when we give lumefantrine artemether, there are cases that improve, but for the most part, they come back to say that it’s not going well after three days. That is why, even if it’s simple malaria, we prefer to give injectable artemether, even if it’s only one day’s treatment. We have found that in 80% of cases, artemether-lumefantrine alone isn’t enough, and we don’t know why. (focus group, Fg7)

Provider perceptions of care-seeking behaviour

Healthcare providers’ narratives addressed the reasons for self-medication and non-compliance with treatment plans, as well as awareness messages. They attributed these behaviours to factors such as negligence, reluctance or illiteracy. Providers also attributed care seeking behaviour to a breakdown in trust between healthcare providers and healthcare users. According to providers, the Ebola and COVID-19 crises, and the lack of communication between communities and the healthcare system, have affected adherence to awareness-raising messages and created reluctance among communities. These perceptions emphasise that past health crises and ongoing communication issues have undermined community confidence in the formal healthcare system, leading to the reinforcement of informal and self-directed care practices.

Once you’re admitted to a health facility, there’s a protocol to follow. For example, here we always start with malaria screening and biological exams, then we start the treatment. But you will see people who say no, I don’t want to do this, I don’t want to do that. For example, we meet a man with anaemia, who needs a transfusion, but they say no, using arguments such as: Since I was born, no one close to me has had a transfusion. So, there’s a total refusal or resistance on the part of some patients or their families. (focus group, Fg16)

Well, firstly, there’s a bad memory that our parents have of the two epidemics that we had. I think, especially at the time of the Ebola epidemic, when you have hyperthermia, you come to the hospital, they just put the thermoflash on you, and now, it’s over, you don’t go home anymore. So, they say to themselves, isn’t it the same principle that’s continuing? So, there’s this bad memory, that’s still there, that’s still in the heads of those in rural areas. (focus group, Fg4)

In the community, people don’t have much faith in health workers, as is said at the community level, because of previous actions during the Ebola epidemic or the Coronavirus pandemic. (community health worker, male, rural health centre)

Self-medication

A total of 19 reasons were identified for self-medication, with the most frequently mentioned reasons being the lack of financial resources, followed by insufficient awareness of free malaria treatment at public and private integrated health facilities (online supplemental figure 2: reasons for self-medication). According to healthcare providers, patients self-medicate because of financial constraints and prefer to prioritise essential household needs rather than seek care in health facilities as a first response. Although malaria treatment is officially free of charge, providers noted that patients often face indirect costs due to frequent shortages of essential drugs and commodities. The discourses also revealed that individuals resort to various forms of self-treatment before consulting a health professional, including the use of analgesics such as paracetamol, antimalarial drugs, antibiotics and herbal or bath-based remedies to relieve fever. This tendency reflects a pragmatic adaptation to economic vulnerability and a perceived inconsistency between policy promises of free treatment and the actual experience of care, which undermines trust in the health system and reinforces informal treatment practices.

Well, I think patients resort to self-medication, because prescription drugs have become excessively expensive. People think they must start by self-medicating with decoctions and inexpensive drugs. If they have one or two dollars, they can look for tablets, especially quinine, to see if it can really calm the situation. People’s income isn’t high. Let’s say you have $15, with that money you prefer to use it for family needs, food or whatever, rather than to go to the hospital. And even if that isn’t enough, because you must go for a consultation, you must go to the lab, you must buy medication, so the $15 won’t be enough. So, the first option is to self-medicate, and when that doesn’t work, then, you must go to the hospital. (focus group, Fg4)

With the unavailability of drugs at health facilities, the parents or patients must dig deep into their pockets to treat malaria, which is contrary to the information disseminated in the media. In communication spots, it is said that malaria treatment in health facilities is free. (physician, male, university hospital)

They self-medicate before they get there, and sometimes they take artemether, paracetamol, and it’s when there’s no improvement that they try to come to the hospital. Others have understood that artemether-lumefantrine or quinine treats malaria. (focus group, Fg15)

Non-adherence to malaria treatment

Six focus groups and seven individual interviews addressed various reasons for non-adherence to antimalarial treatment. These included a lack of confidence in the prescribed medication, a persistent preference for injectable forms, the unpleasant taste or smell of oral drugs, slow remission of symptoms such as fever or pain, poor understanding of dosage instructions and the influence of personal or cultural beliefs. According to several providers, injectable or transfusion-based treatments remain widely perceived by patients as more effective, regardless of malaria severity. Providers also highlighted how community norms and misconceptions about medication contribute to inconsistent adherence, particularly for children. This suggests that non-adherence reflects both structural and sociocultural dimensions, where perceptions of treatment efficacy and entrenched therapeutic traditions intersect with weak communication between providers and patients, undermining effective malaria case management.

When they take the anti-malarial drugs, they find them so bitter, that some of them vomit. Then they categorically refuse the rest of the medication, saying they can’t take it because it’s bitter. (focus group, Fg2)

When you give coartem, you necessarily need an intravenous infusion. If you don’t use an infusion, patients won’t trust your treatment. Coartem and paracetamol aren’t enough as a prescription, you necessarily need an infusion with quinine, because even if you give 1000 tablets, he won’t take them. (technical health agent, male, rural health post)

The communities have the information, but there are people who, if you send them medicines, such as those intended for SMC, they say my child won’t take those medicines, because if he does, he’ll be sick. Medicines do things to our children, and that’s not good. (technical health agent, female, rural health centre).

Healthcare facility use

Care providers discussed patient use of health facilities from three angles, including the timing of healthcare facility use, the reason for using private facilities and recent improvements in the use of public healthcare facilities. The timing of healthcare facility use was the most frequently discussed topic during individual interviews with 58 providers and focus groups with 16 providers.

Timing of health facility use

In FGDs and interviews, healthcare providers discussed patients’ care-seeking behaviours. Four main patterns emerged regarding the timing of care: seeking care in cases of severe illness or complications, when symptoms persisted, at the onset of the first symptoms or after attempting other treatment approaches (figure 2). Providers widely mentioned the use of healthcare facilities as a last resort (complications or symptom persistence). The recourse to healthcare facilities is often preceded by the use of herbal medicines or antipyretics.

Frequency of the modalities ‘time of use of healthcare facilities’ subtheme.

Where we are, there is a lot of self-medication: when there’s a fever, whether it’s malaria or not, people go to the pharmacy to buy paracetamol, which wipes out the fever. Apyrexia sets in, and the patient’s parents believe that everything is back to normal, whereas it’s only apyrexia that’s there. After a few days, there’s a relapse. When the patient starts having convulsive attacks, we refer him to the hospital, especially where we are, it’s too much. That’s why I consider any case of malaria as a complicated case, when you see a patient here, you mustn’t minimize the dose with which malaria is treated. Children come here already intoxicated. Any patient you see here has finished with the pharmacopoeia, the products from the pharmacies, and when things don’t go well, the hospital becomes an option. (physician, male, rural health centre)

Providers described patients’ tendency to delay care-seeking until the illness became complicated. They noted that delaying recourse to healthcare facilities for appropriate treatment jeopardises the vital prognosis of the most vulnerable, particularly in rural areas. Other providers observed that adults tended to seek immediate care for their own health problems but often delayed doing so for their children.

Sometimes the child dies even before the consultation. Cases of abortion and anaemia, like that morning, the child who was sent was a stillborn. There is a recommendation always given at the hospital: when your child has a fever, it is important to know that it could be malaria. In such cases, one should go to a healthcare facility. When there are delays at home and treatments are self-administered, it can lead to situations like this. (midwife, female, rural Health Centre)

For adults, as soon as the first symptoms appear, they head straight to a healthcare facility. But for children, it’s only when the malaria becomes severe that they are sent to the hospital. Because children cannot speak, they cannot express that they are in pain. (focus group, Fg10)

Discussions also highlighted that some patients preferred to seek care at private facilities, citing familiarity and the reduced number of care providers as primary reasons.

In the neighbourhood, there was an elderly man next to us, who said that he prefers clinics to hospitals because, once at the clinic, he can confide in just one person, who takes care of everything for him. However, in hospitals, when he arrives, he is told to go there, and move to another area, which means his health condition is exposed to everyone. Therefore, he prefers clinics over public hospitals. (focus group, Fg9)

Health facility use improvements noted by care providers

Six codes were identified in relation to recent improvements at healthcare facilities, including early recourse to certain healthcare facilities, availability of malaria commodities, closure of unauthorised drug sales points, deployment of community relays, intensification of awareness activities and modernisation of case management approaches in hospitals. Providers believe that the operationalisation of community relays has brought malaria care services closer to communities and enhanced community knowledge of malaria prevention.

You know, at health centres, we work in collaboration with community relays who have access to the anti-malaria commodities, such as rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) and Coartem. They are directly in contact with the community and provide free anti-malarial services. They play a significant role in malaria control within our community, and each month, they report to the centre heads regarding case management. They are supplied with RDTs and Coartem monthly to ensure they can conduct screenings. They are trained in screening, and once they conduct a test, if the result is negative, they refer the patient to the hospital. If the result is positive, they are trained to manage uncomplicated cases and to refer complicated cases. (focus group, Fg9).

Financing policy of malaria control

Care providers discussed national financing for malaria control (financing actors, services included in the free policy, care not included in the free service and proposed improvements). The most frequently discussed subthemes were the services included in the free healthcare policy and those not covered (online supplemental figure 3). Care providers identified the following free services as part of malaria control measures in Guinea: ITNs, RDTs, chemoprophylaxis drugs and curative antimalarial drugs. The financing actors mentioned in the providers’ statements included the NMCP, representing the state/government, international donors (WHO) and non-governmental organisations (NGOs). This pattern of responses highlights that, while healthcare providers have a relatively clear understanding of the institutional architecture of malaria financing, they remain acutely aware of the discrepancy between official policies of free healthcare and their uneven implementation at the level of healthcare facilities. This discrepancy often compromises service equity and community trust in the healthcare system. The providers clearly explained the healthcare services not included in free malaria case management, which included prescriptions, supportive treatments, hospitalisation for complicated cases, medical transport and laboratory tests. In some cases, the free healthcare policy creates confusion around what services are covered and which are not.

For uncomplicated malaria treatment, it doesn’t even reach $0.60, the artemether-lumefantrine we give here is free. It’s just paracetamol that might be paid for, and it doesn’t even cost $0.40. (nurse, male, rural health centre)

The malaria care services available here and at the community level are free. But you should know that taking only artemether-lumefantrine or its derivatives doesn’t fully address the patient’s condition. Symptomatic treatment is needed too for fever, to reduce vomiting, or for other malaria-related issues, but what’s available in our community is free. (focus group, Fg14)

People get confused. They hear that malaria treatment is free. Yes, but there are other issues that come with malaria that we need to address; otherwise, it’s these issues that endanger the patient. The antimalarial drugs are free, but if you need other accompanying medications, you must pay. That’s why many are hesitant to come. They think what we’re telling them isn’t true, but it is. We explain that the antimalarial drugs are free, here are the injectables, here are the tablets provided by the government for free. But when complications arise, additional treatments are needed, which you must pay for. That’s how it is with the free policy. (focus group, Fg10)

Providers also highlighted that those costs associated with care-seeking and treating severe malaria cases deterred some patients from seeking care. Providers who overcharge for care services explained that they do so in the absence of a formal contract or because of the low salaries at the primary healthcare level. In light of these issues, they proposed setting a fixed fee or ensuring total cost coverage for malaria care to improve malaria case management in public health facilities. Some providers suggested establishing a health insurance system to facilitate access to care.

There’s the issue of resources for getting to the hospital. If you don’t have enough to make the trip, that creates problems. Suppose someone, for example, is in Siguirini, 150 kilometres from Siguiri. The trip to the hospital might cost US$ 13 to US$ 20, and they earn less than US$ 2 a day. Once they get to the hospital, there’s a whole protocol to follow, from registration to laboratory tests. It’s enough to discourage them. They would rather go into the bush, seek herbal medicines, boil them and use the decoction. (focus group, Fg4)

The salary is not enough for us. Among the nurses, there are interns, they come to work and are not paid. When someone arrives, you tell them that treatment should be free, but this can be a bit difficult, and everyone is closely monitored. You must give the medicine and related services for free, and here they are, they are not paid and must do this work. (health technician agent, male, rural health centre)

Measures should be put in place to encourage people to come to the hospital more often, such as health insurance. Every time an illness arises, you’re covered. It’s not easy to always pay out of pocket for new illnesses. Such systems would encourage hospital visits. (focus group, Fg6)

Healthcare provider responsibilities

Discussions about care provider responsibilities included critiques of healthcare practices, the roles of healthcare providers and CHWs, specifically community relays. Providers also discussed challenges with knowledge of care services offered at the health system level and their reasons for non-compliance with national guidelines. Common examples of non-compliance with guidelines included overconfidence and overcharging, resulting in errors in malaria case detection or treatment.

Healthcare provider practices

Care providers discussed the common behaviours among their peers in the daily management of malaria cases. A total of 10 behaviours were identified under this subtheme, ranging from overconfidence in case management to complacency in medication selection and therapeutic approaches. Other behavioural elements included lack of training, incompetence among some staff, medical errors, poor reception, negligence, overcharging for services, misuse of RDTs and failure to apply the national treatment protocols. These critiques highlight conflicts among different categories of healthcare providers and underline gaps in training, knowledge harmonisation and availability of qualified personnel. Healthcare providers believe that overcharging constitutes a barrier to communities using healthcare facilities.

In the laboratory, there’s no such thing as a slight positivity; when it’s positive, it’s positive. Clinicians tend to be stingy with details and don’t consider the thresholds of the tests. Out of ten clinicians, only one knows the thresholds. Sometimes, you see a positive RDT, yet they administer treatment for uncomplicated malaria, even though it isn’t simple malaria. If the RDT is positive, how many thousands of parasites does that imply? (focus group, Fg6)

When you are in the hospital and have severe malaria, they suggest an intravenous infusion, with medications that come with a bill of US$ 12 to US$ 17. If you don’t have that amount, you make do at home with whatever antimalarial you know, just to relieve yourself temporarily, but then there will be a relapse, and you will continue self-medicating until it gets worse. (focus group, Fg6)

This overcharging is often associated with non-compliance with national guidelines for malaria case management. Providers attribute this behaviour to a lack of training and a shortage of qualified personnel in primary-level facilities, as well as the complacency and negligence among healthcare providers in referral facilities.

Some patients do not receive adequate treatment according to the type of malaria, mainly due to the ignorance of some providers who simply don’t know better. Others do it just to pocket some money. For example, if someone comes to the health facility and oral Coartem would suffice to solve the problem, they might instead opt for a parenteral route just to gain something extra. However, others do this because they genuinely do not know the difference between the two forms of malaria. (focus group, Fg4)

Ah, I can go two or three days without any patients, and when a child is brought in, if I want to make money, I have to put in a few intravenous infusions and claim that the child has severe malaria, just so I can earn some money. (health technician, male, district hospital, urban area)

Healthcare provider responsibilities

According to care provider discussions, their responsibilities ranged from patient reception and medical history follow-up to continuous training aimed at providing quality healthcare services. For community relays, activities included health promotion, screening, managing uncomplicated cases and referring severe cases. For some healthcare providers, patient reception is a key factor in ensuring adherence to the services offered. In some narratives, providers in remote areas requested the availability of injectable forms for the treatment of severe cases of malaria in health centres, and formal working contracts between CHWs and NGOs or the government. In addition, the findings highlight the need to strengthen community awareness and availability of commodities. Overall, these findings highlight that healthcare providers view their role as encompassing not only clinical care, but also patient engagement, community trust-building and advocacy for improved working conditions and resource availability. All these factors are key to effective malaria control at all levels of the healthcare system.

When you greet someone well, they will listen to you and understand what you are saying. But if you don’t welcome them properly, how will they understand what you’re trying to tell them? I do this daily, when a caregiver comes for the expanded vaccination program, I welcome them and explain the importance of the drug from the outset. Then, I tell them that by doing this, they will protect their child. Now the mother has this in mind and will attend her next appointment. (focus group, Fg11)

The malaria program does not send the injectable form that care providers in the rural areas have often requested. Right now, they send tablets and artesunate suppositories. You have a severe malaria case, and we are not allowed to use the injectable form or serum; we are told to refer patients to more competent structures. This situation makes it difficult to manage severe cases. If the person has only $4 and the child is in convulsions or in a coma, and they need to be referred to the hospital, they might die on the way. (health technician agent, male, rural health centre)

To improve care services, the first thing is to strengthen awareness within the community and community mobilization, then ensure the availability of antimalarial inputs at the health facilities. This can encourage patients to visit health facilities. Lastly, improve care at the personnel level and strengthen their capacity. If these three things are combined, I believe that patients will have the courage to visit health facilities because they know that when they come, all the ingredients are there, and with that, I think there won’t be any issues. (focus group, Fg9)

Health promotion

Discussions about health promotion focused on awareness-raising activities and the strengthening of community health education activities. Care providers discussed the roles of healthcare providers, community agents, media, religious leaders, traditional chiefs and telecommunication services in improving awareness. Providers proposed strengthening awareness efforts, integrating new community actors, adapting communication materials to local languages and conducting educational activities regularly to enhance community health education activities. Care providers believe that involving actors and enhancing awareness activities can help improve patients’ health-seeking behaviour, optimise the use of healthcare facilities and reduce out-of-pocket expenses associated with care.

It is essential to explain and raise awareness among the population about the severity of the disease. People need to understand that if the disease is detected early, it can be treated at health centres or posts, but if it worsens, treatment must be sought at hospitals. When anaemia sets in, as someone mentioned, blood transfusions are not free, so to avoid additional expenses, it is crucial to seek care promptly at healthcare facilities. (focus Group, Fg14)

With the radio, the entire community listens; we need to discuss malaria frequently through this medium. (nurse, male, rural health centre)

Conceptual framework on malaria case management practices

The thematic analysis of malaria case management, providers’ perceptions of patients and their work in malaria control provides a framework for understanding decision-making in malaria case management in health facilities. This interaction is shaped by several factors, including the level of the health facility, the availability of diagnostic and therapeutic commodities, perceptions of the effectiveness of these commodities, the provider’s perception of the patient, knowledge and skills acquired through initial and continuous training, the ability to charge for health services, the desire for rapid resolution of symptoms and the patient’s choice of treatment method. The level of the health facility plays an important role in decisions about malaria case management. At the university hospital level, facilities have advanced technical platforms and incorporate training and research components. Case management practices are influenced by the health status of the patient, the expertise of senior staff and the decisions of junior doctors, who often adapt management protocols based on individual cases, sometimes at the expense of national guidelines. First-level and second-level referral hospitals (district and regional hospitals) share similarities with university hospitals but have less advanced technical platforms and fewer specialties. Professionals in these facilities are involved in the initial theoretical and practical training of doctors, nurses, midwives and other related disciplines, making them important reference points for professionals deployed in primary healthcare structures.

The results of the interaction between initial training (theoretical and practical) and continuing professional development provided by the NMCP shape the therapeutic approach of healthcare providers. Additional factors include the need to charge for services, which leads to overprescribing practices, often driven by the goal of quickly relieving malaria-related symptoms. Discussion

Interviews (63) and FGDs (18) with care providers at different levels of the health system in Guinea revealed major challenges in implementing free malaria care policies, as well as key determinants of care-seeking behaviour and trust in clinical practice. Findings also highlighted a lack of confidence in the effectiveness of some antimalarial treatments, which contributes to non-compliant case management practices. Furthermore, negative perceptions of patients among some providers appeared to shape their clinical behaviour. These findings underline the need to strengthen systemic support for effective free healthcare policies, combined with provider-focused interventions that promote guideline adherence, rebuild patient trust and ultimately improve malaria case management outcomes.

A thorough analysis of the themes, subthemes and modalities revealed significant variations in language and terminology by professional category. Focus groups comprising more physicians exhibited a tendency to use scientific terminology (eg, schizonts, gametocytes, neurological complications, electrolyte disturbances) to describe the parasite cycle and severe malaria. Furthermore, they were more inclined to criticise specific interventions (eg, mass ITN distribution without adequate awareness) and to propose corrective measures (larval source management, revised treatment protocols). Conversely, interviews with predominantly nurses and health technicians working in community relay settings in rural areas reflected more pragmatic, field-based experiences: The phenomenon of ‘yellowing of the eyes’, constraints imposed on health posts, instances of stock-outs and difficulties experienced by patients in accessing healthcare are all matters of concern. These discrepancies imply that professional level and work context exert a significant influence on providers’ comprehension and execution of malaria interventions. This finding is supported by a study in Kenya showing that clinicians were significantly more likely than nurses to have high knowledge of severe malaria treatment recommendations.18 Furthermore, another study on outpatient case management found that facility type, commodities availability and malaria endemicity were key determinants of provider compliance.19

Taken together, these findings highlight that variations in technical knowledge and contextual challenges shape how malaria interventions are understood and applied across different settings and facility levels. Consequently, there is a clear need for targeted capacity-building and supervision strategies adapted to these diverse professional profiles. The implementation of rural field practice modules for technicians, and governance systems modules for physicians and managers, could help bridge the existing gap between national guidelines and daily practice. It is also noteworthy that discussions concerning SMC emerged primarily from districts where this intervention is implemented and were highly valued by care providers for their effectiveness in reducing malaria incidence during peak transmission periods.

The findings on malaria transmission modes and severity of clinical signs demonstrate that both healthcare providers and CHWs possess a solid understanding of the fundamental concepts related to malaria transmission and its severity in young children. This level of knowledge reflects the outcomes of past capacity-building initiatives targeting malaria control stakeholders, which have successfully reinforced the biomedical understanding of the disease. However, the narratives also reveal the ways in which providers conceptualise and communicate malaria control, often integrating their biomedical knowledge with local perceptions of risk and environmental factors. This blend of scientific understanding and contextual reasoning illustrates that knowledge alone does not automatically translate into uniform practice. Studies carried out in endemic areas reveal that while communities and healthcare providers understand malaria transmission and symptoms, coma and convulsions are often seen as signs of severe cases, and sometimes linked to supernatural causes.2022

Nevertheless, the results point to the urgent need to revise and update the content of both initial and continuing training programmes to equip providers with the skills and knowledge required to face emerging malaria challenges. These include the increasing presence of urban and daytime-active vectors, the growing threat of parasite resistance to ACTs and their derivatives, and the expanding range of mosquito breeding sites linked to urbanisation and climate change. This interpretation and implication are supported by a multicountry cluster randomised trial that found that CHWs required specific training to manage malaria and pneumonia diagnostics and care.23 In Kenya, evidence of shifting vector behaviours further emphasises the need for updated provider training on evolving transmission dynamics.24 This evidence underscores the fact that training curricula must keep pace with evolving vector ecology, resistance patterns and environmental dynamics, thereby ensuring that malaria control personnel remain adaptive, evidence-informed and capable of implementing integrated vector management strategies tailored to local contexts.

The findings indicate that healthcare providers in Guinea generally perceive SMC and ITNs to be highly effective preventive interventions despite concerns about their operational deployment. While acknowledging the significant contribution of ITNs to malaria prevention, providers also noted several persistent shortcomings, particularly the rectangular design of distributed nets, which many households find difficult to hang and use correctly, the limited community awareness campaigns on proper utilisation and the inconsistent emphasis on their importance during health promotion activities. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia, which reported that net design, practicality and household awareness substantially influence sustained ITN use.25 26 The direct interaction of healthcare providers with communities explains their nuanced perspective: interventions such as ITNs remain effective only when accompanied by behavioural reinforcement and context-appropriate communication.

Furthermore, discussions underscored the need to expand vector control efforts to include environmental management and larval source reduction, particularly around areas of night-time social activity, such as markets and entertainment venues. Providers argued that such integrated measures would complement ITN and SMC interventions by targeting residual transmission caused by outdoor and early-biting vectors. This recommendation aligns with evidence from Kenya and Uganda, where community-based larval habitat destruction and environmental sanitation interventions were shown to reduce local vector density and malaria incidence.24 27 Experiences from countries that have successfully achieved malaria elimination reinforce this integrated vision. Egypt’s recent certification as malaria-free in 2024 demonstrates that sustained elimination depends on combining vector surveillance, environmental management and intersectoral collaboration. Addressing vector prevalence linked to hydro-agricultural projects, strengthening early case detection and treatment, and maintaining continuous community sensitisation were identified as essential success factors.7 28 29 This integrated approach is consistent with the WHO African Region’s 2022 Guidelines, which advocate for a One Health framework engaging non-health sectors such as agriculture, water management and urban planning in vector control and surveillance.30

According to the study results, the impact of SMC, which covers 17 of the country’s 33 districts, could be further strengthened by including June and expanding coverage to include school-aged children (aged 5–10). A study conducted in Ghana found that mass screening and treatment of school-aged children reduced the incidence of clinical malaria cases and improved community productivity during agricultural seasons.31 Integrating such approaches into Guinea’s SMC campaigns could therefore enhance malaria control outcomes while supporting household economic stability in rural areas.

Healthcare providers’ narratives on malaria case management revolved around patients’ care-seeking behaviour, the availability of essential commodities and the management of both uncomplicated and severe cases. The findings reveal that treatment decisions are shaped by providers’ perceptions of patients, their confidence in diagnostic tools and the pressure to achieve rapid symptom relief. Similar observations in Kenya show that provider confidence and adherence to treatment protocols are critical determinants of rational case management.32 However, several practices diverge from national guidelines, particularly those linked to informal cost recovery mechanisms. The persistence of user fees, overprescription and treatment initiation before diagnostic confirmation reflects both systemic financing constraints and limited trust in diagnostic tools. A cost analysis in Guinea found that malaria patients spend between US$ 7.8 and US$ 38.7 per episode.10 This situation is explained by cost-recovery policies, low care provider remuneration and the high malaria burden in outpatient and inpatient services. Such practices undermine the policy of free malaria care and perpetuate inequities in service access. Ensuring effective free care for vulnerable populations, particularly children aged under 5 years, while introducing a transparent flat-rate system for others could balance patient protection with health facility sustainability. This model should include clinical and laboratory services and be developed collaboratively with provider representatives to ensure ownership and compliance. Evidence from Kenya’s free maternal healthcare policy and Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme shows that partial cost exemptions increased service use but did not necessarily improve health outcomes, partly due to hidden fees for diagnostics and ancillary care.33 34 These findings highlight the importance of coupling financial protection with quality and accountability measures. Finally, providers’ accounts also explained why some patients prefer private facilities: perceived reliability, faster care pathways and stronger patient–provider rapport. Similar findings in Ethiopia demonstrated that welcoming environments, shorter waiting times and personalised care are major drivers of private-sector preference.35 Strengthening patient experience and service responsiveness in public facilities could therefore reduce these disparities and restore trust in the public health system.

The results reveal persistent criticism of both primary and secondary malaria prevention guidelines. Many physicians and other healthcare providers, particularly those in referral hospitals, perceive national recommendations as overly bureaucratic and detached from field realities. As supervisors and mentors for medical, nursing and paramedical students, these senior practitioners often serve as informal reference points whose clinical practices may diverge from official protocols. Consequently, their influence can perpetuate non-standardised approaches to malaria diagnosis and treatment, especially when national guidelines are not regularly disseminated or adapted to operational constraints. The tension between programme directives and frontline realities mirrors broader concerns highlighted in the literature about overly centralised health programmes that prioritise administrative compliance over service quality.36 In the Guinean context, the perceived disconnect between the policy stakeholder and healthcare providers fosters limited ownership of policies and contributes to uneven implementation across facility levels. Community relays also voiced critical perspectives on their interactions with technical agents and nurses, citing issues such as limited communication, irregular supply of diagnostic commodities and hierarchical barriers in managing uncomplicated and severe malaria cases. Similar tensions were reported in Ghana, where provider adherence to RDT guidelines varied by facility type and by how well staff integrated new practices into existing workflows.37

These findings highlight the necessity to strengthen guideline adherence and service cohesion; malaria control policies must be repositioned as collaborative frameworks rather than top-down directives. Involving representatives from professional associations, frontline providers and community relay in the revision and dissemination of national guidelines would enhance their relevance and uptake. Furthermore, integrating feedback from subnational actors on topics such as ITN design, laboratory strengthening and communication around free healthcare would improve implementation fidelity. By promoting inclusive and adaptive governance, Guinea’s NMCP could bridge the gap between policy and practice, reinforcing provider ownership and accountability at all levels of the health system.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. Only interviews and FGDs conducted in French were included in the analysis, which may have excluded some nuances from participants who communicate more comfortably in local languages. Furthermore, the perspectives of healthcare service users were not explored and will be addressed in a subsequent study to provide a more comprehensive understanding of care dynamics. Nevertheless, the study’s breadth and methodological depth represent key strengths. With 63 individual interviews out of 81 and 18 focus groups out of 19 conducted across multiple levels of the health system, data saturation was achieved, ensuring robust and credible insights. The triangulation of perspectives from healthcare providers, CHWs and facility types also enhances the validity and transferability of the findings for informing malaria control policies in Guinea.

Conclusions

This qualitative study highlights the complex and interconnected challenges facing malaria control in Guinea, from gaps in intervention implementation and healthcare delivery to systemic limitations in financing, coordination and provider accountability. While healthcare providers demonstrate a strong conceptual understanding of malaria transmission and prevention, persistent deviations from national guidelines, fragmented communication within the health system and resource constraints continue to undermine the effectiveness of malaria case management and prevention strategies. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive and inclusive approach that strengthens capacity-building, ensures equitable resource allocation and promotes patient-centred and evidence-based care. Engaging frontline providers, community relays and professional associations in the revision, dissemination and ownership of national malaria control guidelines would enhance compliance, improve service quality and reinforce trust between communities and the health system. Moreover, policies should give greater weight to operational feedback from providers regarding the design and usability of ITNs, the availability of essential commodities and the implementation of free-care initiatives, ensuring that interventions are both technically sound and contextually adapted. From a policy perspective, these findings underscore the need to transition from a top-down programme model to an integrated and participatory malaria control framework, grounded in the realities of providers and communities alike. Strengthening intersectoral collaboration, particularly through the One Health approach, and investing in continuous training, supervision and data-driven monitoring will be essential to sustain gains and move closer to the elimination of malaria. Future research should incorporate the perspectives of healthcare users to complement provider narratives and build a more comprehensive understanding of barriers to malaria prevention and care. The subsequent stage of the project will entail the modelling of optimisation strategies, including a selective free-care package and a differentiated diagnostic approach.
