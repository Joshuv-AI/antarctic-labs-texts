Experimental and microstructural investigation on the strength and frost resistance of basalt fiber reinforced steel slag foamed concrete
Authors: Jianying Jiang, Mingzhou Chen, Xigang Yu, Yingli Jin, Weinan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aiming to facilitate the high-value repurposing of industrial steel slag, this investigation synthesized basalt fiber-reinforced steel-slag foamed concrete (BFSFC) incorporating fiber volume fractions of 0%, 0.15%, 0.30%, and 0.45%. The experimental study assessed compressive and flexural capacities alongside resistance to freeze-thaw cycling, while micro-void was reconstructed via X-ray CT and corroborated by SEM imaging. Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) was subsequently utilized to correlate pore metrics with strength degradation under freezing conditions. Empirical findings identify 0.30% as the critical fiber volume fraction; this optimal mix yielded a 28-day compressive strength of 1.322 MPa (a 12.03% increment over the reference) and elevated flexural strength to 0.517 MPa (an increase of approximately 64%). Following 15 freeze-thaw iterations, the BFSFC2 specimen maintained a mass loss below 5% and restricted strength deterioration to 8.69%, a sharp contrast to the 31.14% decay observed in the control group. Microscopic analysis attributes this stability to a fiber-induced reduction in pore network complexity and morphological refinement. Furthermore, GRA confirms that fiber dosage exhibits the strongest correlation with fractal dimension (0.991), whereas frost-induced strength decline is primarily governed by fractal dimension (0.805) and large pore size (> 200 μm, 0.743), highlighting the dominance of pore network complexity and large pore-defects in freezing behavior. Conclusively, BFSFC2 provides a balanced improvement in mechanical performance and freeze-thaw resistance, supporting a microstructure-based optimization route for producing lightweight foamed concrete using steel slag for cold regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42606-9.

Introduction

Steel slag, a by-product generated in large quantities during steel production, presents a growing environmental challenge due to its continuous accumulation1. To address this issue, many countries have initiated research on recycling steel slag across various sectors, including steelmaking cycles2–4, road construction5–7, and building materials8–11, to promote its comprehensive utilization. Among these applications, incorporating steel slag into concrete has garnered particular research interest, as recent studies have demonstrated that incorporating solid wastes (e.g., e-waste12,13, construction waste14,15, etc.) into cement-based composites is an effective strategy to conserve natural aggregates and reduce the environmental impact of concrete production. This dual-purpose approach positions concrete as a promising solution for both the sustainable management and productive reuse of steel slag.

Current research demonstrates that processed steel slag can be effectively used as an aggregate16–21 or a supplementary cementitious material22–25 in concrete production. Maslehuddin et al.16 compared the properties of steel slag with those of crushed limestone as concrete aggregates and found that steel slag concrete exhibits superior durability and specific physical properties, particularly in water permeability and dimensional stability. Similarly, Qasrawi17 showed that steel slag aggregates could enhance the mechanical properties of recycled concrete aggregates, contributing to environmental sustainability by partially substituting recycled concrete aggregates. Further research by Pang et al.18 indicated that substituting conventional natural aggregates with carbonized steel slag granulated aggregate significantly improves compressive strength and volume stability, while also reducing water absorption and porosity, thereby enhancing both the structural integrity and durability of concrete. Guo et al.20 explored the effects of using steel slag as fine aggregate on the static and dynamic behaviors of concrete, discovering that a 20% inclusion of steel slag fine aggregate optimally enhances compressive strength and structural stiffness, while maintaining a failure mode similar to that of conventional concrete. Chen et al.21 investigated the application of autoclaved steel slag aggregate in concrete and demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional steel slag aggregates, with the best improvements observed at a 50% replacement rate, particularly in compressive strength and durability. In addition to its use as an aggregate, research also suggests steel slag’s potential as a supplementary cementitious material. Wang et al.22 observed that mechanically ground ultrafine steel slag exhibits higher early activity than conventional steel slag, although its long-term cementing performance is inferior to that of cement and fly ash. However, the use of ultrafine steel slag may compromise the overall cementing properties of concrete, presenting both economic and efficiency challenges. Xiang et al.23 developed a cement-free steel slag cementitious material that achieves high strength and durability through the synergistic effects of steel slag, composite agents, and admixtures. Chen et al.24 enhanced the properties of steel slag via an indirect carbonization process, using it as a partial replacement in cementitious materials. Their research indicated that carbonized steel slag significantly improves volume stability and 28-day strength, primarily due to the carbonization reaction reducing the free calcium oxide content in steel slag and filling micro-cracks and pores with the resulting calcium carbonate.

The above research underscores the significant potential of steel slag for application in conventional concrete. However, the dual imperatives of high performance and environmental sustainability in the development of advanced concrete materials pose distinct challenges. The rigorous technical standards imposed on conventional concrete stand in contrast to the escalating necessity for materials that offer density reduction25, thermal conservation26, minimal carbon footprint27, and superior structural robustness28. Consequently, the incorporation of steel slag into novel concrete formulations, particularly those aimed at simultaneously improving ecological benefits and material performance, has attracted increasing research interest29,30. Within this context, steel slag–based foamed concrete has emerged as a promising yet relatively underexplored research direction. Preliminary studies have demonstrated the feasibility of utilizing treated steel slag in the production of foamed concrete. For example, Xiang et al.31 reported that aged steel slag can be effectively used as a fine aggregate in steel slag–based foamed concrete. By optimizing parameters such as particle fineness, replacement ratio, and the water-to-binder ratio, significant improvements in the mechanical properties of the foamed concrete were achieved. Nevertheless, steel slag-based foamed concrete still faces inherent challenges, including relatively low mechanical strength, reduced toughness, and increased susceptibility to erosion and damage under freeze-thaw conditions. These limitations constrain its wider application, underscoring the need for further research and development.

In response to these challenges, the integration of fiber materials has been proposed as a practical strategy to enhance both the strength and long-term performance of foamed concrete. Among various options, steel fibers, glass fibers, and polypropylene fibers32–34 have been extensively investigated and are currently the most commonly applied in related studies. The effectiveness of these fibers depends on several factors, including fiber length, proportion, and the uniformity of their dispersion within the cement matrix35. Although the improvement of foamed concrete properties through fiber reinforcement is well-documented, it is crucial to consider the environmental implications. For example, polypropylene fibers are not readily biodegradable, posing long-term environmental risks, while the use of steel fibers contributes to increased steel consumption. As the construction industry increasingly adopts stricter environmental standards, there is growing interest in natural fibers as sustainable and renewable alternatives36,37. Natural fibers not only offer a more eco-friendly option compared to synthetic fibers but also enhance the overall sustainability of reinforced foamed concrete. Among these, basalt fibers stand out due to their excellent tensile strength and thermal resistance38. Consequently, research on incorporating basalt fibers into steel slag foamed concrete shows considerable potential for enhancing its mechanical properties and durability, potentially leading to significant advancements in the field.

Although there is extensive literature on fiber-reinforced foamed concrete and steel slag-based cementitious materials, a systematic understanding of the scientific mechanisms by which basalt fiber content affects pore structure and influences the mechanical properties and freeze-thaw resistance of steel slag foamed concrete is still lacking. Existing research primarily focuses on macroscopic properties (such as strength changes and freeze-thaw mass loss), treating fiber content as a parametric variable. Nevertheless, a quantitative assessment regarding how fibers modulate void characteristics, specifically size distribution and morphology remains insufficient, and crucially, the correlation linking these micro-structural features to mechanical performance and frost resistance is yet to be defined. This knowledge gap is particularly important for steel slag foamed concrete, as pore structure not only governs the mechanical response of foamed concrete but also controls the formation and expansion of damage during freeze-thaw cycles. Therefore, clarifying the intrinsic relationship between fiber content-pore structure-performance enhancement and freeze-thaw damage mechanisms in basalt fiber-reinforced steel slag foamed concrete is crucial for developing design-oriented theoretical guidance rather than relying solely on empirical rules.

To address the aforementioned knowledge gap, this study used basalt fibers, steel slag powder, and Portland cement as the primary raw materials. Specimens of basalt fiber-reinforced steel slag foamed concrete (BFSFC) were formulated with four distinct fiber volume fractions (0%, 0.15%, 0.30%, and 0.45%) to evaluate the effect of fiber incorporation. X-ray computed tomography (X-CT) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were employed to analyze the microstructural characteristics of the specimens, while their mechanical properties and freeze-thaw resistance were assessed based on these analyses. This study specifically focuses on the effect of basalt fiber content on BFSFC performance under freeze-thaw conditions.Based on microstructural analysis, this study reveals how basalt fiber content affects the mechanical behavior and freeze-thaw resistance of BFSFC. Gray relational analysis is used to explore the relationship between microstructure, mechanical properties, and freeze-thaw resistance, thus clarifying the mechanism of fiber action in BFSFC. This research provides a data foundation and guidance for optimizing the application of other types of fibers in steel slag foamed concrete.

Experimental materials

The mix formulation for the test specimens incorporated several key constituents, including silicate cement, finely ground steel slag powder (200-mesh), water, basalt fibers, and a plant-based foaming agent. The steel slag powder, characterized by high purity and fine particle size, was procured from Longze Materials Co., Ltd., located in Zhengzhou, Henan. The foaming agent, originating from plant proteins, underwent a 1:40 dilution with tap water to achieve optimal foam generation suitable for integration into the foamed concrete matrix. Mix design of BFSFC.

All BFSFC specimens were prepared in accordance with JGJ/T 341–201439, and the overall process is illustrated in Fig. 2. Briefly, cement and steel slag powder were first dry-mixed at low speed for approximately 60 s to obtain a homogeneous binder. Water was then added, and the mixture was stirred for about 120 s to form a uniform gel-like paste. Subsequently, basalt fibers were gradually incorporated and dispersed evenly (approximately 90 s), followed by the addition of the foaming agent and continued mixing for another 120 s until a stable foam slurry was formed. The fresh mixture was immediately assessed for workability and then cast into molds, where it was allowed to set under ambient conditions for 24 h. After demolding, the specimens were cured in a standard curing chamber (20 ± 2 °C, relative humidity ≈ 95%) until the designated ages (up to 28 days) prior to mechanical and durability testing. The overall procedure is consistent with the team’s previous method for preparing steel slag foamed concrete40, with the primary modification being the incorporation of basalt fibers.

Test methods

Strength test

Compressive strength evaluations were executed in strict compliance with Standard41. Cubic samples with dimensions of 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm underwent testing at curing ages of 7 and 28 days, utilizing a universal testing apparatus operated under displacement control at a rate of 0.5 mm/s. The ultimate load at failure was registered to compute the compressive stress relative to the cross-sectional area. A set of three cubic specimens was assessed, and the arithmetic mean was documented as the final result. Concurrently, the flexural performance was characterized following the protocols outlined in Standard42. Prismatic beams (100 mm × 100 mm × 400 mm) were subjected to bending tests at 7 and 28 days over a support span of 300 mm, with the load applied at a constant velocity of 0.2 mm/s until fracture occurred. The peak force observed during rupture served as the basis for calculating flexural strength per the standard. Each experimental group involved the testing of three prisms to establish a representative mean value.

Freeze-thaw cycle test

Frost resistance evaluations were performed on foamed concrete samples following the specific protocols of JGJ/T 341–201439, distinct from standard concrete testing. Initially, specimens underwent oven-drying until weight stabilization to establish a baseline mass, followed by a 48-hour water submersion to ensure complete saturation. The cyclic regimen comprised a 6-hour freezing phase in air at -20 °C, succeeded by a 5-hour thawing period in water maintained at 20 °C. Given that the standard categorizes frost grades as D15, D25, D35, and D50, with Grade D15 mandating performance verification after 15 iterations (Table A.0.2 in JGJ/T 341–201439, this investigation set the testing limit at 15 cycles. During testing, specimens were inspected every 5 cycles following the procedures outlined in the standard39, and the test was terminated early if severe deterioration was observed. The standard also provides specific termination criteria, including an average mass loss exceeding 5% or a strength loss of 25%. After completion of the designated number of cycles (or premature termination), the specimens were dried and reweighed to determine mass loss, and then subjected to compressive strength testing to evaluate strength reduction. In terms of international standards for porous cement-based materials, EN 15,304 for AAC43 similarly requires determination of mass loss after a specified number of freeze-thaw cycles (not fewer than 15) and notes that the required number of cycles may be defined by national regulations depending on environmental exposure conditions. Finally, the intent of the freeze-thaw test aligns with ASTM C66644: the test evaluates material resistance to freeze-thaw deterioration, rather than providing a direct quantitative prediction of service life.

Microstructure test (SEM)

A representative fragment (~ 2 cm3) was taken from the core damaged region of the fractured specimen and immediately immersed in absolute ethanol to promptly quench hydration and stabilize the microstructure. After solvent exchange, the specimens were dried in an oven at 50 °C until constant mass to remove residual ethanol and moisture with limited thermal influence. For SEM observation, the surfaces were intentionally left unground and unpolished to retain the original fracture morphology. Small chips were carefully split from the interior of the dried fragment to obtain a freshly exposed natural fracture surface. The prepared pieces were mounted on stubs and vacuum gold-coated to enhance electrical conductivity and reduce charging. Microstructural imaging was then performed using a FEG650 field-emission scanning electron microscope to examine hydration products, fiber-matrix interfacial features, and pore characteristics.

X-CT test

To minimize edge effects and ensure scanning precision during X-ray computed tomography (X-CT), the initial 100 mm cubes were trimmed to yield 50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm test specimens. The samples cured for 28 days were examined using an X-CT system operated at 130 kV and 115 µA, corresponding to a total power of 15 W. During the scan, each specimen was rotated through 360° and projection images were acquired with an angular increment of 0.120°. The projection dataset was then reconstructed into a three-dimensional volume, yielding an effective voxel size of approximately 30 μm, which enabled reliable identification and quantitative assessment of the internal pore structure.

Results and discussion

Mechanical properties

Compressive strength

Fig. 3Compression test results of the BFSFC.

Compression test results of the BFSFC.

The variation in BFSFC compressive strength as a function of basalt fiber dosage is illustrated in Fig. 3. After 28 days of curing, all BFSFC mixes exhibited approximately a 30% increase in compressive strength compared with their 7-day values. To ensure the reliability of the findings, statistical analysis was performed on the 28-day data. One-way ANOVA confirmed that basalt fiber content had a significant effect on compressive strength (F = 14.892, p = 0.00123). Mean values and standard deviations were calculated for each group, and pairwise Welch’s t-tests were conducted to identify significant differences among the different fiber-content BFSFC specimens. The compressive strength results reveal a clear trend: strength increases with basalt fiber addition up to an optimal dosage, beyond which further increases lead to strength reduction. Specifically, the 0.30% fiber group exhibited significantly higher compressive strength than both the 0% and 0.15% groups (p < 0.05), indicating that moderate basalt fiber incorporation effectively improves mechanical performance. This enhancement is attributed to the influence of fiber-regulated pore structure. Although basalt fibers tend to increase pore size, they also improve bonding with the cementitious matrix and inhibit crack propagation, thereby enhancing compressive strength within a certain fiber content range. However, once fiber content exceeds the optimal threshold, the positive effects diminish. The 0.45% group showed significantly lower compressive strength than the 0.15% and 0.30% groups (p < 0.01), primarily due to excessive fiber addition leading to enlarged and interconnected pores as well as fiber agglomeration or balling phenomena45. These defects disrupt matrix uniformity and compromise mechanical integrity. Among all mixes, the BFSFC2 specimen (0.30% fiber content) achieved the highest 28-day compressive strength of 1.322 MPa (t = 3.204, p = 0.048), representing a 12.03% improvement over the control SFC group (1.180 MPa). Considering its statistically significant performance enhancement, 0.30% was identified as the most suitable basalt fiber content among the four designs evaluated in this study.

Flexural strength

Fig. 4Three-point bending test results of the BFSFC.

Three-point bending test results of the BFSFC.

Figure 4 presents the flexural strength of BFSFC specimens with different basalt fiber volume fractions. The addition of basalt fibers markedly enhances the flexural performance of foamed concrete: mean flexural strength increases from 0.315 MPa for the control SFC (0%) to 0.466 MPa (0.15%), 0.517 MPa (0.30%), and 0.491 MPa (0.45%), corresponding to an improvement of approximately 48–64% relative to the control group. One-way ANOVA confirms that fiber content has a statistically significant effect on flexural strength (F = 158.20, p < 0.001), and Tukey’s post-hoc test shows that all fiber-reinforced mixtures have significantly higher flexural strength than the control (p < 0.001). Moreover, the 0.30% group is significantly higher than the 0.15% group (p < 0.01). The slight reduction observed at the highest fiber content (0.45%, BFSFC3) is attributed to fiber agglomeration45,46, which can create defects and weak interfaces, partially offsetting the reinforcement benefits. Overall, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of basalt fibers in enhancing BFSFC flexural strength. Therefore, the optimal fiber content for practical applications should be determined by jointly considering both compressive and flexural performance to achieve a balanced design.

This 3D reconstruction serves as a foundation for further quantitative analysis of the internal characteristics of the material. Pore size analysis

The shift in the pore size spectrum across BFSFC samples with diverse basalt fiber levels is visualized in Fig. 6. The comparative analysis robustly demonstrates that the incorporation of an optimal amount of basalt fiber significantly enhances the pore size distribution. A distinct trend towards finer porosity is evident, the proportions of voids below 100 μm increased, whereas the prevalence of medium (100–200 μm) and coarse (> 200 μm) pores diminished. This refinement in pore size is primarily attributed to the bridging and stabilizing effects of basalt fibers embedded within the cementitious matrix, which help suppress pore expansion and promote matrix densification. When uniformly distributed, basalt fibers effectively bridge across large voids or macropores, significantly reducing their size. These fibers span across expanded void spaces, limiting the formation of macropores during the drying or curing processes. However, at higher concentrations, basalt fibers tend to agglomerate, resulting in localized pore formation and reduced pore connectivity. In areas with sparse fiber distribution, this agglomeration leads to an increase in pore size, fostering the development of larger pores and reducing the compressive strength of the BFSFC. From the analysis of basalt fiber content’s impact on BFSFC’s pore size distribution, a fiber volume fraction of 0.3% emerges as the optimal content. This level effectively balances fiber distribution and the integrity of the concrete, yielding improved performance.

Fractal dimension analysis

Microstructural examination reveals that both the homogeneity and complexity of the pore distribution play a critical role in determining the overall stability of the material. To assess the pore structure characteristics, two-dimensional (2D) fractal dimensions were calculated based on computed tomography (CT) slice data along the z-axis direction. The box-counting method47 was employed to derive the fractal dimensions. As shown in Fig. 7, the results demonstrate the fractal dimensions for BFSFC samples with different fiber dosages. For the control specimen (SFC), the fractal dimension ranges from 1.619 to 1.640, indicating a relatively complex internal pore morphology. In contrast, the fractal dimensions for BFSFC1 and BFSFC2 fall between 1.593 and 1.621, and 1.569 and 1.599, respectively, suggesting that BFSFC2 exhibits a simpler pore structure, characterized by fewer interconnected and complex pores. The fractal dimension for BFSFC3, ranging from 1.614 to 1.640, is closer to that of the control group, reflecting a similarly complex pore structure. It is noteworthy that the range of fractal dimension values across the different BFSFC dosages remains relatively narrow, indicating that the overall pore distribution uniformity is well-maintained across the samples. This uniformity is crucial for ensuring the structural integrity and stability of the foamed concrete.

SEM analysis

Figure 10 presents scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of BFSFC specimens with varying basalt fiber contents at 500x magnification. At this magnification, it is evident that the maximum pore diameter initially decreases and then increases as the fiber content rises. The morphology of BFSFC, in comparison to the blank SFC group, shows that some fibers are strongly bonded to the matrix. When cracks occur due to interfacial damage, these fibers experience minor sliding, generating frictional resistance. This mechanism helps in transferring and distributing deformation across the BFSFC, leading to a more uniform stress distribution. However, when excessive basalt fibers are added, agglomeration occurs, resulting in larger pore sizes, thicker pore walls, and an overall increase in pore quantity. These changes lead to the formation of a significant number of interconnected pores, which can be detrimental, potentially compromising the structural integrity and performance of the foamed concrete.

Frost resistance analysis

The frost resistance evaluation of BFSFC specimens primarily involves freeze-thaw testing to assess their compliance with established standards and to investigate the reinforcing mechanism of basalt fiber. Figure 12 depicts the mass degradation percentages of BFSFC samples following 15 freeze-thaw iterations. As stipulated by JGJ-T341-201439, the mass reduction must not surpass a 5% compliance threshold. While all groups underwent weight reduction, the control SFC notably breached this 5% limit after only 10 cycles, rendering it non-compliant with engineering norms39. Conversely, the three basalt fiber-enhanced groups (BFSFC1, BFSFC2, and BFSFC3) maintained mass loss rates well within the < 5% criterion throughout the test, thereby satisfying the specification39. These outcomes underscore the efficacy of basalt fiber in enhancing the frost resilience of steel-slag-based foamed concrete, as fiber inclusion reinforces matrix cohesion and offers superior defense against environmental stressors.

Fig. 12Mass loss ratio of BFSFC after 15 freeze-thaw cycles.

Mass loss ratio of BFSFC after 15 freeze-thaw cycles.

According to the technical guidelines specified in JGJ-T341-201439, the allowable reduction in compressive strength due to freeze-thaw cycling should not exceed 20%. Figure 13 illustrates the compressive strength values of BFSFC specimens before and after exposure to freeze-thaw cycles. Post-15 cycles, the SFC specimen suffered a 31.14% drop in compressive strength, a magnitude that clearly exceeds the mandated limit. In contrast, the compressive strength losses in BFSFC1 and BFSFC2, which contained basalt fibers, were considerably lower 13.78% and 8.69%, respectively both well within the 20% limit, satisfying the specification requirements. However, BFSFC3, which had an excessive amount of basalt fibers, exhibited a substantial compressive strength loss of 30.92%, likely due to fiber agglomeration and resulting defects. Despite this, the compressive strengths of BFSFC1 and BFSFC2 remained surpassing the 1 MPa benchmark post-15 cycles, thereby fulfilling the minimum mechanical performance criteria for practical engineering use. These results underscore the effectiveness of incorporating an appropriate volume fraction of basalt fiber in reducing strength degradation under freeze-thaw cycles. However, excessive fiber content can negatively affect frost durability. The optimal fiber content, represented by the 0.3% volume fraction in BFSFC2, demonstrated improved compressive strength and superior frost resistance, emphasizing the importance of carefully controlling fiber dosage to enhance the frost resistance of foamed concrete.

The morphological features of BFSFC samples exposed to varying numbers of freeze-thaw cycles were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figure 14 shows an SEM image at 500X magnification, highlighting significant microstructural changes. After 15 cycles of freeze-thaw exposure, the pore wall regions of the SFC specimen exhibited considerable damage, including increased porosity, enlarged pore sizes, reduced matrix compactness, and a greater presence of microcracks and internal defects. These alterations were closely linked to the observed decline in compressive strength. In contrast, BFSFC samples containing basalt fiber, particularly BFSFC2, displayed fewer internal defects. The addition of basalt fiber appears to enhance the connectivity between the matrix pore walls, improving the structural integrity of the material. The excellent tensile strength of basalt fiber plays a key role in resisting freeze-thaw induced frost heave damage. This reinforcement mechanism helps preserve the continuity of the pore wall framework, thereby mitigating structural degradation under cyclic freeze-thaw conditions. These observations underscore the critical role of basalt fiber in enhancing both the frost resistance and structural integrity of foamed concrete under harsh environmental conditions.

Further examination of hydration products at 1000X magnification (Fig. 15) revealed additional changes following freeze-thaw exposure. Before the freeze-thaw cycles, as shown in Fig. 11, a significant amount of Ca(OH)2 was observed. Following 15 freeze-thaw iterations, a distinct proliferation of ettringite (Aft) crystals was detected in the BFSFC specimens. This surge is ascribed to the expansive forces of frost heave, which compromise the pore skeleton and facilitate moisture ingress into the matrix. Such saturation catalyzes the growth of acicular Aft crystals, a process that ultimately loosens the micro-texture and accelerates the deterioration of the pore wall architecture. This degradation increases the incidence of internal defects, subsequently decreasing the compressive strength of the samples. Interestingly, the quantity of needle-shaped Aft crystals observed in BFSFC1 and BFSFC2 was significantly lower than in SFC and BFSFC3. This outcome is primarily due to the optimal dosage of basalt fiber incorporated in BFSFC1 and BFSFC2, which helps to reinforce the pore wall framework and mitigate the detrimental effects of freeze-thaw cycles. These findings further suggest that basalt fiber plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity and frost resistance of foam concrete under adverse environmental conditions.

Grey relational analysis and underlying mechanism

To further clarify the mechanism by which basalt fibers enhance the freeze-thaw resistance of BFSFC, it is necessary to link macroscopic frost-deterioration indicators to the evolution of pore-structure parameters. Previous observations indicate that variations in fiber dosage lead to systematic changes in pore structure, which are closely related to performance degradation during freeze-thaw cycles. However, the pore system of BFSFC is characterized by multiple interacting descriptors (e.g., pore size distribution, fractal dimensions of pore structure, and pore morphology/sphericity), and their effects on freeze-thaw performance are often coupled rather than independent. Therefore, a quantitative approach is required to identify the pore descriptors most strongly associated with freeze-thaw resistance and to elucidate how fiber content regulates these pore-structure parameters, thereby revealing the dominant pore characteristics governing frost resistance. In this context, gray relational analysis (GRA) was employed to assess the relationships among fiber content, pore characteristics, and corresponding frost-resistance indicators. GRA evaluates associations by comparing the similarity in the evolution patterns of different variables, without requiring prior assumptions about data distributions, making it particularly useful when dealing with limited or incomplete datasets. The method characterizes inter-variable relationships through gray relational coefficients. Fundamentally, GRA functions by quantifying the geometric similarity between a specific pore-parameter data series and an ideal benchmark vector. Through this alignment, the method identifies the degree of association with the target performance metric and establishes a hierarchy of importance for each micro-structural descriptor50,51.

In this analytical framework, the macroscopic indicators (specifically basalt fiber content or the strength-loss ratio after freeze-thaw cycling) are designated as the reference series Y={yi(k)|k = 0, 1, 2, ···, n}. Conversely, the individual micro-pore parameters function as the comparative series X={xi(k)|k = 0, 1, 2, ···, n}. Using the above formulas, the grey relational degrees between basalt fiber content / the post freeze-thaw strength-loss ratio and the pore-structure parameters of BFSFC were calculated. The pore-size distribution was first analyzed (Table 3). The results show that the proportion of coarse pores (> 200 μm) exhibits the strongest synchronicity with both reference sequences, with grey relational degrees of 0.919 (basalt fiber content) and 0.800 (strength-loss ratio after freeze-thaw cycling). In contrast, the correlations for pores in the 100–200 μm range are moderate (0.570 and 0.599), whereas those for fine pores (< 100 μm) are relatively weak (0.399 and 0.464). These findings indicate that variations in basalt fiber dosage and the progression of freeze-thaw damage are more closely associated with changes in the coarse-pore fraction. This suggests that the dominant pore-structure regulation induced by fiber incorporation as well as the pore-structure evolution occurring during freeze-thaw deterioration is primarily reflected in the formation or transformation of large pores, rather than in modifications to the fine-pore system. Consequently, the proportion of voids exceeding 200 μm was adopted as the representative metric for the ensuing GRA.

Subsequently, a grey relational analysis was performed between the reference sequences and the pore-morphology (sphericity) distribution. To facilitate a more intuitive interpretation of the influence of pore shape, the sphericity range was divided into two intervals 0.2–0.6 and 0.6-1.0 following classification schemes commonly used in grey relational analyses of foamed-concrete pore structures52,53. This grouping enabled clearer comparison between pores with poor sphericity and those with good sphericity. As shown in Table 4, when basalt fiber content was used as the reference sequence, the grey relational coefficient for pores within the 0.6-1.0 sphericity range reached 0.624, markedly higher than the value of 0.429 obtained for the 0.2–0.6 range. These findings demonstrate that high-sphericity pores (i.e., pores closer to a spherical shape) exhibit stronger correlations with both basalt fiber content and freeze-thaw strength loss. This suggests that increasing the proportion of pores within the 0.6-1.0 sphericity range is more conducive to enhancing the freeze-thaw resistance of BFSFC.

Ultimately, GRA was utilized to quantify the impact of basalt fiber dosage on key pore-structural indices (specifically, > 200 μm, fractal dimension, and sphericity) and to discern which morphological features exert dominant control over strength attenuation following frost exposure (Table 5). The results show that the grey relational degree between basalt fiber content and the fractal dimension is the highest (0.991), exceeding that of the large pore-size parameter (> 200 μm, 0.798) and the proportion of high-sphericity pores (0.6-1.0, 0.366). This indicates that the primary regulatory effect of basalt fibers on the pore system lies in modifying the overall geometric complexity and heterogeneity i.e., the fractal characteristics of the pore network rather than simply altering pore shape. Mechanistically, basalt fibers induce a bridging-skeleton and spatial-constraint effect within the fresh slurry, which suppresses pore coalescence, stabilizes pore walls, and alters pore tortuosity. As a result, the fractal dimension exhibits the strongest synchronous response to fiber content. Meanwhile, the correlation ranking between the freeze-thaw strength-loss ratio and each pore parameter is: fractal dimension (0.805) > (pore size > 200 μm (0.743)) > high-sphericity pores (0.6-1.0, 0.479). This suggests that freeze-thaw deterioration is governed more strongly by the complexity and connectivity of the pore network than by pore morphology alone. A higher fractal dimension, reflecting increased pore complexity and connectivity, facilitates water transport and local saturation54, both of which accelerate ice-crystal growth and microcrack propagation during freeze-thaw cycling. Furthermore, the significant correlation with large pores (> 200 μm) indicates that macroscopic voids act as critical defects that intensify strength degradation. Therefore, an appropriate dosage of basalt fibers enhances the frost resistance of BFSFC primarily by effectively refining the fractal characteristics of the pore network and limiting the formation of large defects.

Conclusions

An experimental investigation was conducted on basalt fiber steel slag powder foamed concrete (BFSFC) with four distinct basalt fiber dosages. The mechanical properties, specifically compressive and flexural strengths, were rigorously tested. Furthermore, the internal void architecture was utilized via X-CT, complemented by SEM for micro-level examination. To further understand the enhancement mechanisms of the mechanical properties and frost resistance of BFSFC, the GRA method was applied. The principal findings include:

(1) In designed basalt fiber steel slag powder foam concrete (BFSFC), incorporating basalt fiber at an optimal volume fraction of 0.3% (BFSFC2) significantly enhances mechanical properties, evidenced by a 12.03% increase in compressive strength over the control group. This formulation also maintains satisfactory flexural strength. Nevertheless, an excessive amount of basalt fiber negatively impacts the performance of BFSFC.

(2) When the optimal volume fraction of basalt fiber is 0.3%, the pore structure of BFSFC shows an increase in small pores and a decrease in large pores. Excessive addition of basalt fiber, however, reverses this effect, resulting in fewer small pores and more large pores. Additionally, 2D fractal dimension analysis reveals that an appropriate amount of basalt fiber optimizes the pore structure of BFSFC by reducing pore complexity and enhancing pore morphology, thereby improving sphericity.

(3) Microstructural analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that BFSFC2 demonstrated substantial improvements over the control group lacking basalt fiber. Notably, the Ca(OH)2 crystals in BFSFC2 were tightly integrated with the C-S-H gel, accompanied by a minimal presence of Aft crystals. Furthermore, BFSFC2 exhibited more complete crystallization, resulting in a denser, more uniform structure, and a densified matrix.

(4) The frost resistance testing showed that basalt fiber incorporation significantly enhances the freeze-thaw performance and structural stability of BFSFC specimens, primarily by enhancing the pore structure. The BFSFC2 group, representing the optimal fiber dosage, complied with the requirements for freeze-thaw performance outlined in the Chinese standards. The judicious addition of basalt fiber significantly boosts the frost resistance of roadbed foam concrete in environments subjected to freeze-thaw cycles, fulfilling the specific application demands of such regions.

(5) GRA reveals that basalt fibers regulate the pore system primarily by modulating the fractal characteristics specifically the complexity and heterogeneity of the pore network. The dominant correlation between fiber content and fractal dimension (0.991) confirms that fibers exert a stronger influence on network than on individual pore size or shape. Crucially, freeze-thaw induced strength loss is shown to be governed fundamentally by pore connectivity and the presence of large pore-defects (fractal dimension: 0.805; pore size > 200 μm: 0.743), rather than by pore sphericity (0.479). Consequently, the superior frost resistance of BFSFC2 is quantitatively attributed to the fiber-induced reduction of pore network complexity and the suppression of large detrimental voids, which collectively mitigate freeze-thaw damage.
