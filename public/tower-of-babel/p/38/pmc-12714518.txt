Treatment of Acute Lower-Extremity Arterial Occlusions With a Combined Mechanical and Aspiration Thrombectomy System
Authors: Jonathan Bowman, Pavan Khanna, Christian Salinas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with acute limb ischemia require immediate intervention to treat limb-threatening arterial occlusions. The Artix thrombectomy system has been designed to leverage both aspiration and mechanical elements for clearing thrombus burden of varying size and chronicity. It also provides proximal flow restriction utilizing a covered funnel catheter to reduce the risk of thrombus migration. Here, we report a series of 3 cases using the Artix system for treating acute limb ischemia patients with arterial occlusions in both a synthetic femoropopliteal bypass graft and native arteries extending from the common femoral artery to the tibial runoff vessels. Notably, Artix was utilized bilaterally in 1 patient to address occlusions in each lower extremity after overnight thrombolytic therapy demonstrated no improvement. By utilizing the Artix thrombectomy system, the treating physicians successfully revascularized all intended segments resulting in symptom resolution, limb preservation, and no need for adjunctive thrombolytic therapy or other thrombectomy devices.Key words: endovascular therapy, peripheral vascular disease, thrombosis, thrombus, thrombectomy

Overview and Clinical Outcomes of the Artix Thrombectomy System

Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is a life- and limb-threatening condition associated with a high mortality rate of 43% and amputation rate of 11% at 1 year.1 The Artix system (Inari Medical) is a dual mechanical and aspiration thrombectomy platform designed to remove arterial thromboemboli of varying size and chronicity, while providing proximal flow restriction utilizing a covered funnel (CF) catheter (Figure 1, Video 1). It provides a comprehensive toolkit that includes a mechanical thrombectomy (MT) device, an aspiration (AX) catheter, a thin-walled (TW) sheath, and the aforementioned CF catheter for a safer, more complete treatment of peripheral arterial thromboembolism. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported experience of the outcomes of its initial use in patients with acute lower-extremity occlusions.

The Artix Thrombectomy System

Case 1

Presentation and medical history

A 69-year-old male presented with acute left lower-extremity (LLE) pain and numbness for 2 days, consistent with Rutherford IIa classification of ALI. The patient had a history of coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, and peripheral artery disease with a left common femoral artery (LCFA) to above-knee popliteal artery bypass polytetrafluorethylene graft.

Investigations

Computed tomography angiography revealed thrombosis of the femoropopliteal bypass graft extending into the LCFA with distal reconstitution of a critically stenosed left popliteal artery via collateral circulation (Figure 2A).

Management

Anesthesia and anticoagulation

With the patient supine, local anesthesia was administered. Activated clotting time was maintained >250 seconds with heparin boluses as needed.

Access

The right CFA was accessed under ultrasound guidance.

Thrombectomy

A wire and catheter were advanced into the LCFA. The occlusion was crossed with a 0.035-inch Glidewire Advantage guidewire (Terumo Interventional Systems). An 8-F Artix TW sheath was exchanged over the guidewire and advanced up-and-over into the left common iliac artery. Angiography showed high-grade stenosis of the left external iliac artery, occlusive thrombus starting from the LCFA, an occluded profunda that filled via collaterals from a patent left internal iliac artery, and distal reconstitution of the popliteal artery with three-vessel runoff to the left foot (Figure 2B). Angioplasty and stenting with an 8-mm × 4-cm balloon expandable stent (Boston Scientific) were performed to treat focal stenosis of the left external iliac artery (Figure 2C). Aspiration through the TW sheath, which was advanced proximal to the thrombus, was performed to remove acute-appearing thrombus from the LCFA. An 8-F Artix CF catheter was deployed above the graft to restrict proximal flow (Figure 2D). The guidewire was exchanged for a 0.014-inch Choice PT guidewire (Boston Scientific). An 8-F Artix MT8 device was deployed with its element distal to the occlusion, and over-the-wire MT was performed, removing thrombus which was cleared from the element with a cleaning accessory (Figure 2E). MT was repeated, progressing down the graft and into the popliteal artery. A large amount of acute- and subacute-appearing thrombus was removed in 5 passes (Figure 2F). Intravascular ultrasound revealed a patent graft with a residual wall-adherent laminar thrombus (Figures 2G and 2H). After angioplasty with a 7-mm Mustang balloon (Boston Scientific) to address a focal area of thrombus, MT was resumed to remove residual thrombus via 6 additional passes. Repeat angiography demonstrated widely patent LCFA and graft (Figure 2I). Distal embolization was noted in the left tibioperoneal trunk (Figure 2J), which was removed in 2 passes with an 8-F Artix MT6 device. Popliteal artery stenosis was treated with a 6-mm Ranger drug-coated balloon (Boston Scientific). Completion angiography confirmed patent tibial vessels (Figure 2K). Estimated blood loss was 50 mL.

Outcome

The following day, the patient was ambulating without symptoms and was discharged on enoxaparin. On day 7 post procedure, duplex ultrasound showed widely patent bypass graft and deep femoral artery (Figure 3A). His anticoagulation regimen was switched to apixaban. Duplex ultrasound at the 5-month follow-up visit confirmed sustained patency (Figures 3B to 3E).

Case 2

Presentation and medical history

A 92-year-old male with a history of hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and ischemic cardiomyopathy presented with worsening LLE pain, numbness, coldness, and acute skin discoloration.

Investigations

On physical examination, the patient's symptoms were consistent with Rutherford IIA. Angiography showed acute occlusion involving LCFA extending into the entirety of the LLE with no associated runoff (Figure 4A).

Imaging Findings in Patient 2

(A) Acute occlusion involving LCFA extending down into the entirety of the LLE with no associated runoff. (B) Deployment of an 8-F Artix CF catheter above the CFA to provide proximal flow arrest. (C) Acute and chronic-appearing thrombus haul. (D-L) Reconstitution of brisk in-line flow from the LCFA through the SFA to the foot, supplied via a single-vessel runoff by the peroneal artery, after thrombectomy with Artix. LCFA = left common femoral artery; LLE = left lower extremity, CF = covered funnel; CFA = common femoral artery; SFA = superficial femoral artery.

Management

Anesthesia and anticoagulation

Conscious sedation was administered with the patient supine. Activated clotting time >250 seconds was maintained with heparin boluses as needed.

Access

The right CFA was accessed under ultrasound guidance.

Thrombectomy

Using an up-and-over technique, a 0.035-inch guidewire and catheter were advanced to the LCFA. Angiography revealed occlusion of the LCFA and proximal superficial femoral artery (SFA) secondary to significant thrombus burden. A TW sheath was advanced into the LCFA. A CF catheter was advanced through the sheath and deployed above the CFA to arrest proximal flow (Figure 4B). MT was performed to remove acute- and chronic-appearing thrombus in 4 passes using an MT8 device that had been deployed distal to the occlusion (Figure 4C). Postintervention angiography demonstrated reconstitution of a brisk in-line flow through the LCFA (Figures 4D to 4F) and SFA (Figures 4G to 4I). Flow was restored to the foot, supplied via a single-vessel runoff by the peroneal artery (Figures 4J to 4L). The proximal and mid-anterior tibial artery were chronically occluded. The posterior tibial artery reconstituted distally above the ankle, as did the anterior tibial artery. Estimated blood loss was 5 mL.

Outcome

The next day, the patient exhibited normal motor function and complete resolution of pain and numbness. His LLE was normothermic, and skin color returned to baseline. Doppler examination revealed normal dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses. Given his history of persistent atrial fibrillation, the patient was discharged 3 days post procedure on rivaroxaban and remained symptom-free.

Discussion

ALI management has evolved with advances in endovascular therapies that offer a minimally invasive alternative to surgery. However, beyond procedural inefficiencies and safety concerns, percutaneous thrombectomy devices prior to the availability of the Artix system are also limited by their single mechanism for thrombus removal, which often leads to decreased efficacy, particularly in cases of large or more organized thrombus burden.2, 3, 4, 5 Consequently, they often rely on adjunctive thrombolytics, with reported use in 20% to 60% of thrombectomy cases.2,4,6 While CDT has similar limb salvage rates as surgery, it is associated with an increased risk of major bleeding complications and necessitates long infusion time.7,8

The study device leverages both aspiration and mechanical thrombectomy for thrombus clearance with an aspiration-capable sheath, a wall-apposing MT device, and a separate aspiration catheter. A large multilevel thrombus burden of varying chronicity was removed in each case without the need for adjunctive thrombolytics or incision. The MT device's expandable nitinol element fully engaged with the vessel wall, enabling it to mechanically disrupt and remove nonacute and wall-adherent thrombus in a controlled manner. In Cases 1 and 3, aspiration through the AX catheter and TW sheath removed the remaining thrombus after the initial extraction via MT. Notably, overnight CDT failed in treating bilateral occlusions in the latter.

Aspiration-only thrombectomy devices have proven effective on acute thrombus and may also be employed after failed thrombolysis. However, they are likely ineffective in clearing more organized thrombus due to their mechanism of action. Larger bore catheters can improve the success of aspiration techniques on more chronic lesions, but these require larger arterial access. Furthermore, substantial procedural blood loss and high rates of adjunctive thrombolytics have been reported with these devices.2 Certain MT devices have built-in fragment aspiration capability to aid thrombus clearance; however, they lack the level of strength and adaptability that the study device provides. They also may not be suitable below the knee and have been associated with vessel perforation and acute kidney injury.3,5,9

Distal embolization can lead to serious complications and additional interventions.10 These cases highlight the study device's utility in both prevention and management of distal embolization. In contrast, other thrombectomy systems do not have the ability to arrest proximal flow and may necessitate adjunctive devices to reduce the risk of embolization or additional interventions to address it.

These cases illustrate the over-the-wire thrombectomy platform's versatility in efficiently treating a broad spectrum of thrombus in a femoropopliteal bypass graft and native vessels extending from the CFA to tibial runoff vessels, resulting in immediate symptom resolution. The dual mechanism of action provided flexible options, enabling a customized approach tailored to each patient's needs. With that said, different clinical situations may require additional therapies due to the complexity of treating arterial occlusions, and even a multifunctional device may not be a universal solution for every patient.

Future studies, including a prospective, multicenter, single-arm clinical trial, are expected to assess the device's comparative performance in diverse clinical settings. Exploring the use of the study device for managing chronic limb ischemia would also be beneficial.
