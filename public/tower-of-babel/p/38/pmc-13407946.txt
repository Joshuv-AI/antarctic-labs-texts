High-yield production of α-glucosylglycerol using Xanthomonas campestris WU-9701 derived XgtA
Authors: Kanon Suzuki, Yoshitaka Ishii, Kohtaro Kirimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractα-Glucosylglycerol (αGG), an α-glucoside found in alcoholic beverages, exhibits beneficial physiological effects. However, high costs and low yields limit its availability in the market. Therefore, we aimed to optimize the conditions for high-yield αGG production and establish a method for effective αGG separation. Accordingly, maltose and glycerol were used as the α-glucosyl donor and acceptor, respectively. Enzymatic production was executed using XgtA, an α-glucosidase derived from Xanthomonas campestris WU-9701, and glucose isomerase (GI). The products were αGG comprising 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG. αGG was separated from the reaction solution using activated carbon, followed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry. Under optimal conditions, the concentrations of αGG and 1-O-αGG reached 1141 mM and 1072 mM, respectively, within 48 h. These are the highest values obtained by enzymatic production to date. 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG were separated using an HPLC system equipped with the DCpak PMPC column. The area ratio of the two separated peaks of the products was 94:6 for the enzymatic reaction solution containing 1141 mM αGG. The products corresponding to these peaks were isolated for nuclear magnetic resonance analysis. The main product was confirmed to be 1-O-αGG, whereas the other was 2-O-αGG. Furthermore, the melting point of 1-O-αGG was 126 °C. Finally, the enzymatic products were confirmed to be 1-R-O-αGG, 1-S–O-αGG, and 2-O-αGG. These findings confirmed the successful high-yield and enzymatic production of αGG, and the first isolation and chemical identification of 1-O-αGG. Introduction

Glucosylglycerol (GG) is a compound comprising glycerol and glucose bound by glycosidic bonds. It exists in several forms, such as regioisomers and stereoisomers, based on the bond position between glucose and glycerol. For example, sake contains approximately 0.5% α-glucosylglycerol (αGG), which is also found in traditional Japanese foods brewed using koji (Takenaka et al. 2000). The three isomers of αGG are 1-R-O-αGG, 1-S–O-αGG, and 2-O-αGG.

GG has approximately 0.55 times the sweetness of sucrose. Moreover, it exhibits high thermo-stability, low heat-colorability, low Maillard reactivity, low hygroscopicity, high water-holding capacity, non-cariogenicity, and low digestibility (Takebaka and Uchiyama 2000). Physiologically, αGG increases AQP3 expression to help moisturize the skin (Boury-Jamot et al. 2009; Schrader et al. 2012). Thus, it contributes to increased skin elasticity (Harada et al. 2010). This compound has various potential applications, including in functional foods (Sawangwan 2015), diabetes treatment (Dabhi et al. 2013), and protein stabilization (Sawangwan et al. 2010). However, the high price of αGG limits its availability in the market (Tan et al. 2016). Therefore, various chemical and biological methods have been established to produce this compound (Takenaka and Uchiyama 2000; Goedl et al. 2008; Ojima et al. 2012; Jeong et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2020). Although selective production of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG using chemical methods has been confirmed, the reported yields were markedly low (Takenaka et al. 2000). Similarly, biological methods such as the enzymatic production of 2-O-αGG from sucrose and glycerol using sucrose phosphorylase have also been reported (Goedl et al. 2008; Zhang et al. 2020). In addition, αGG production using α-glucosidase generates both 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG (Takenaka and Uchiyama 2000; Ojima et al. 2012; Jeong et al. 2014). Although further attempts were made to produce GG using microorganisms, the αGG yield remained low (Roder et al. 2005; Xu et al. 2013; Tan et al. 2015). Despite previous reports, no study has reported high-yield selective production of 1-O-αGG to date.

XgtA is a glucosyl transfer enzyme belonging to glycoside hydrolase family 13. It was isolated from Xanthomonas campestris WU-9701 by screening microbial enzymes that produce l-menthyl α-D-glucopyranoside (Nakagawa et al. 2000; Watanabe et al. 2020). This enzyme can regioselectively transfer sugars without producing maltooligosaccharides. Additionally, it has high glucosyl transfer activity, especially for alcohols and phenolic compounds (Sato et al. 2012). For example, the production yields of (+)-catechin 3'-O-α-D-glucopyranoside, α-arbutin, and the prodrug eugenol α-glucoside were 57% (Sato et al. 2000), 93% (Kurosu et al. 2002), and 52% (Sato et al. 2003), respectively.

In this study, we sought to produce high yields of αGG using XgtA. We further aimed to establish a simple high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method for the analysis of αGG isomers.

Hypotheses

H1: XgtA produces various α-glucosides as the main products and does not produce maltooligosaccharides as by-products (Sato et al. 2000, 2003, 2012; Do et al. 2002; Kurosu et al. 2002; Kirimura et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2023). Although αGG production using XgtA remains largely understudied, it may be achievable. Given that the by-product glucose strongly inhibits the transglycosylation reaction of XgtA, we hypothesized that the addition of glucose isomerase (GI) would enable the production of αGG to exceed levels previously reported.

H2: Previous studies on αGG have mainly focused on the analysis of isomer mixtures or gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) involving derivatization (Takenaka and Uchiyama 2000). To our knowledge, the separation and isolation of isomers without derivatization have not been reported. Therefore, evaluation of the structural and physical properties of 1-O-αGG exclusively has been difficult. In addition, basic physical properties such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) data and melting point have not been determined. Therefore, we theorized that the separation and isolation of αGG isomers without derivatization would elucidate the structural characteristics and physical properties of 1-O-αGG.

H3: Reaction temperature affects the regioselectivity and stereoselectivity of enzymatic reactions. XgtA preferentially produces 1-O-αGG but also produces small quantities of 2-O-αGG. The common consensus is that the isomer ratio varies depending on the reaction temperature. However, no systematic investigation has been conducted on the effect of reaction temperature on the αGG isomer ratio. Therefore, we investigated this effect in our present study.

Materials and methods

Materials

Maltose monohydrate, glycerol, and p-nitrophenyl α-D-glucopyranoside were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Acetonitrile was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). GI was kindly provided by Godo Shusei Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan), as the commercially available product GODO AGI-EG. All other chemicals used were commercially obtained and chemically pure.

Enzyme preparation

Recombinant His-tagged XgtA was produced and purified as described by Sato et al. (2012).

Protein concentration was determined via the Bradford method (Laemmli 1970) using a Coomassie Protein Assay Kit (Pierce Chem. Co., Rockford, IL, USA) and bovine serum albumin solution. The protein concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 595 nm using a BIO RAD iMark microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

The glucosyl transfer activity of the enzyme was measured based on the amount of p-nitrophenol (PNP) produced after reaction with 10 mM p-nitrophenyl α-glucopyranoside for 5 min at 40 °C. The amount of PNP was determined by measuring absorbance at 405 nm using a microplate reader. One unit (U) of glucosyl transfer activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 μmol of PNP per minute.

Enzymatic production of αGG

A reaction solution (1 mL) containing 1.2 M maltose, 1.8 M glycerol, 5.0 U･mL−1 XgtA, 30 mM KCl, and 30 mM Tricine-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) was incubated for 24 h at 40 °C. GI was added at a concentration of 3000 GIU･mL−1 (Kirimura et al. 2022).

Analytical methods

The products were quantitatively analysed using an HPLC (Nexera i-Series HPLC system; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) under the following conditions: Column: Shimadzu Shim-pack SCR-101N (10 μm, 7.9 × 300 mm; Shimadzu); eluent: water; flow rate: 0.5 mL･min−1; column temperature: 60 °C; detection: RID-20A (Shimadzu). Because a standard αGG was not available, the amount of αGG produced was calculated based on the amount of glycerol consumed.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis was performed using a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry system (LC-Q-TOF–MS; LC, Acquity UPLC H-Class, Waters, Milford, MA, USA; MS, Xevo G2-XS QTOF, Waters) under the following conditions: Column: Asahipak NH2P-40 2D (4 μm, 2.0 × 150 mm Showa Denko K. K., Tokyo, Japan); mode: negative; flow rate: 0.5 mL･min−1; temperature: 40 °C; eluent: A (acetonitrile) and 0.5% B (ammonia water). For 0–13, 13–15, and 15–21 min, measurements were performed with gradients of A: B = 80: 20 (v/v), 40: 60 (v/v), and 80: 20 (v/v), respectively.

GC–MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled with an Agilent 5977B MSD (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The column was a J&W Scientific DB-225 column (Agilent Technologies); analysis was conducted as described previously (Takenaka and Uchiyama 2000). For GC–MS analysis, Forty grams of activated carbon was added to 20 mL of the reaction mixture and stirred to adsorb and remove glucose and glycerol. The resultant solution was subjected to column chromatography using activated carbon as a carrier and eluted with 2% ethanol to isolate αGG. The solvent was then removed using an evaporator, and powdered αGG was obtained. Subsequently, the powdered αGG was dissolved in methanol to prepare a 10% (w/v) αGG methanol solution. Next, One millilitre of TMS–imidazole reagent (GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) for sugar derivatization was added to 10 μL of the αGG solution and allowed to react at room temperature for 30 s.

Structural analysis was performed using a BRUKER high-resolution NMR spectrometer (AVANCE NEO, 600 MHz; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). The acetonitrile solvent was removed from the αGG solutions of each isomer separated by HPLC using an evaporator. The water was subsequently removed using a freeze dryer to yield powdered 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG. For NMR analysis, 16.0 mg of powdered αGG was dissolved in 500 μL of D2O to prepare the sample, and measurements were performed.

The melting points of αGG were measured using a Perkin Elmer differential scanning calorimeter (DSC8500). The heating rate was set to 20 °C･min−1 for 1-O-αGG and 10 °C･min−1 for 2-O-αGG. The extrapolated onset temperature (Ti) was measured from the endothermic reaction of the sample and used as the melting point.

Results

Enzymatic production of GG

XgtA was used for the enzymatic production of GG from maltose and glycerol (Fig. 1). The reaction solution was subjected to HPLC analysis with Shim-pack SCR-101N. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, four peaks were detected: maltose, an unknown substance, glucose, and glycerol at approximately 10.8 min, 11.6 min, 13.2 min, and 15.9 min, respectively. The reaction solution was further subjected to LC–MS analysis to identify the unknown peak. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, the three largest signals were detected at 253.0921 m/z, 299.0974 m/z, and 507.1925 m/z. As the monoisotopic mass of GG was 254.1002, the masses of [M-H]−, [M-HCOO]−, and [2M-H]− were 253.0923, 299.0978, and 507.1925, respectively. Therefore, the masses of the glycoside product were identical to those of [M-H]−, [M + HCOO]−, and [2M-H]− calculated from GG. These results indicate that GG was produced without the formation of by-products such as maltooligosaccharides. Since all glycosides produced by XgtA to date have been confirmed as α-glucosides (Nakagawa et al. 2000; Sato et al. 2000, 2003, 2012; Do et al. 2002; Kurosu et al. 2002; Kirimura et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2023), it can be concluded that the GG produced by this enzymatic reaction was indeed αGG.

A simple HPLC analytical method for αGG

As shown in Fig. 1, αGG exists as two regioisomers: 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG. Although methods for GC–MS analysis after derivatization have been reported, no quantitative method has been established for the separation of αGG isomers without derivatization. Therefore, we investigated analytical conditions for the separation of these isomers using HPLC. As shown in Table S1, we examined several HPLC columns (Shim-pack SCR-101N, Luna Omega Polar C18, TSK-gel ODS-100V, TSK-gel Amido 80, DCpak PTZ, and DCpak PMPC) that have been employed to analyze sugars. Each column featured a unique analytical mode, such as gel filtration, ligand exchange, reversed-phase, or hydrophilic interaction modes. Using these columns, we investigated optimal HPLC analysis conditions for the separation of αGG isomers by changing the eluent composition, column temperature, and flow rate. The analytical modes of the columns used, and a summary of the results are shown in Table S1.

Shim-pack SCR-101N was used for quantitative analysis of αGG. However, changing the analytical conditions did not result in αGG separation (Supplementary Fig. S1). As shown in Supplementary Figs. S3–S6, the αGG peak overlapped with the glucose peak when Luna Omega Polar C18, TSK-gel ODS-100V, TSK-gel Amido 80, and DCpak PTZ were used. In contrast, DCpak PMPC detected unknown peaks (peaks a and b) presumed to be 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG (Supplementary Fig. S7), which did not overlap with other carbohydrates. As shown in Fig. 2, the optimal analytical conditions for DCpak PMPC were determined to be a water: acetonitrile (v/v) ratio of 25: 75, column temperature of 60 °C, and a flow rate of 1.0 mL･min−1. In addition, glucose and maltose were detected as broad peaks using the DCpak PMPC, likely resulting from anomeric separation.

Structural analysis by NMR

Products A and B from each of the two unknown peaks (peaks a and b) separated by HPLC were isolated and subjected to NMR analysis: 1H NMR, 1H-1H COSY, 1H-13C Hydrogen Sensor with Quick Diffusion (HSQC), and 1H-13C HMBC. The 1H-13C HSQC spectra of these products are shown in Supplementary Figs. S8–S10. The 1H NMR signal assignments for each product are listed in Table 1. The H-1 peak of products A and B was identified as a singlet positioned at δ = 5.11 and 4.94 ppm, respectively, based on the 1H NMR and 1H-1H COSY spectra. These results indicate that products of peaks a and b were α-glycosides. As the values for product A matched with previously reported NMR values for 2-O-αGG (Nihira et al. 2014), this product was determined to be 2-O-αGG. Furthermore, the product B detected in unknown peak b was determined to be 1-O-αGG based on the NMR spectra. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the NMR spectrum of 1-O-αGG. These results demonstrated separation of two isomers: 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG. The ratio of the αGG isomers produced was determined to be 1-O-αGG: 2-O-αGG = 94: 6 based on HPLC analysis using DCpak PMPC (Fig. 2).

Determination of melting points of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG

Products from each of the two peaks were isolated and subjected to Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) analysis. The melting points of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG were determined to be 126 °C and 121 °C, respectively (Supplementary Figs. S11 and S12). Although the melting point of 2-O-αGG was consistent with that reported in previous studies, this study is the first to measure and report the melting point of 1-O-αGG.

Time-course of αGG production

The optimal reaction conditions were determined as: a pH of 8.0, a temperature of 40 °C, 1.2 M maltose, 1.8 M glycerol, and 5.0 U･mL−1 XgtA (data not shown). Glucose inhibits XgtA, and its GI-induced conversion to fructose increases the amount of glucoside produced by XgtA (Kirimura et al. 2022). Therefore, we sought to increase the αGG yield by combining XgtA with GI. The optimum concentration of GI was determined to be 3000 GIU･mL−1 (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Figure 3 shows the time-course of αGG production with GI under the optimal reaction conditions. The final amount of αGG was 1141 mM. This is the highest yield of αGG reported to date. The yield of 1-O-αGG was determined to be 1072 mM, as this isomer accounted for 94% of the total. This is the highest amount of 1-O-αGG produced by enzymatic methods to date. Table S2 shows the enzymatic production of αGG in the current and previous studies. αGG production in the absence of GI was 852 mM (data not shown), whereas that in the presence of GI at the optimal concentration reached 1141 mM in 48 h. These results indicate that the addition of GI results in a 1.35-fold increase of αGG production.

Effect of the production ratio of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG on temperature

αGG production with GI reached its peak at 40 °C (1141 mM, 290 g･L−1), and the production ratio of 1-O-αGG increased with decreasing reaction temperature (Supplementary Fig. S14). At 0 °C, 1-O-αGG accounted for 97% of the product. Table S3 lists the production amount and ratio of αGG. Furthermore, αGG production reached its maximum at 40 °C (852.3 mM, 217 g･L−1) even in the absence of GI; the production ratio of 1-O-αGG increased with decreasing reaction temperature (Supplementary Fig. S15 and Supplementary Table S4). At 0 °C and without GI, 1-O-αGG accounted for 98% of the product. In αGG production using XgtA, no significant difference was observed between the ratio of αGG isomers produced with or without the addition of GI. This indicates that GI does not affect the production ratio of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG. Furthermore, the production of 1-O-αGG reached its maximum at 40 °C regardless of the addition of GI, and 1-O-αGG production significantly exceeded that of 2-O-αGG with increasing reaction temperature.

Stereoisomers of 1-O-αGG

1-O-αGG exists as two stereoisomers: 1-R-O-αGG and 1-S–O-αGG. The reaction product obtained under optimal conditions and isolated by activated carbon was analyzed using GC–MS to determine the ratio of 1-R-O-αGG and 1-S–O-αGG. As shown in Fig. 4, three peaks were detected. The peaks around 21.4 min, 22.2 min, and 22.4 min were 2-O-αGG, 1-R-O-αGG, and 1-S–O-αGG, respectively. Additionally, the production ratio of each isomer was determined from the area of the peak. The ratio of each αGG was 1-R-O-αGG: 1-S–O-αGG: 2-O-αGG = 70: 25: 5. Supplementary Fig. S16 shows the time-course of the total production of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG at various temperatures. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S16, the production amount at 0 °C is lower than that at 40 °C throughout the reaction duration.

Hydrolysis of αGG

Both the hydrolysis of αGG and the reverse reaction, the production of αGG from glucose and glycerol, were performed using XgtA. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S17, thin layer chromatography analysis revealed that a small amount of glucose was produced from 10 mM αGG after 1 h of reaction, and increased to approximately 5 mM glucose after 24 h. This indicates that the hydrolytic activity of XgtA toward αGG was significantly weak. On the other hand, when αGG production was carried out using 500 mM glucose and 1.2 M glycerol, neither αGG, maltose, nor maltooligosaccharides were produced, indicating that the reverse reaction of XgtA does not occur under these conditions.

Discussion

In this study, αGG was selectively produced from glycerol and maltose using XgtA or a combination of XgtA and GI. The conversion rate of glucose to fructose was 25–30% which decreased as the reaction progressed. Because the reaction was carried out under optimal conditions for XgtA transglycosylation, the catalytic potential of GI may not have been fully utilized. Furthermore, in Fig. 3, the glucose concentration reached 500 mM between 8 and 12 h, while the αGG productivity began to decline after 8 h. It has been reported that α-EG production by XgtA is inhibited by glucose, with significant inhibition occurring at 500 mM or higher (Kirimura et al. 2022). In this study too, XgtA was likely inhibited when the glucose concentration exceeded 500 mM. In this study, the addition of GI increased the αGG yield by 1.35-fold. In our previous study, GI increased the production of α-ethyl glucoside and α-octyl glucoside by 1.44-fold and 1.48-fold, respectively (Kirimura et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2023). These findings suggest that the addition of GI enhances glycoside production independent of the aglycone substrate employed. This increase in production was attributed to the GI-mediated isomerization of glucose to fructose. This isomerization suppressed the inhibition of XgtA by glucose and maintained the activity of the transglycosylation reaction. The stereoselectivity of the isomers was attributed to its dependence on the three-dimensional structure of XgtA. Thus, GI functions as an auxiliary enzyme to improve yields. In addition, it is markedly advantageous from an engineering perspective as it does not impair the optical purity of the product. This characteristic is particularly important in industrial processes where 1-O-αGG is the primary target; it reduces separation efficiency and purification costs.

During the transglycosylation reaction catalyzed by XgtA, hydrolysis of maltose also occurs concurrently. Based on the product concentrations depicted in Fig. 3, the transglycosylation reaction remained dominant, with the proportion of the hydrolytic reaction decreasing over time. This observation led us to believe that there was minimal hydrolysis of αGG by XgtA.

Previously, the only method for analyzing αGG isomers was GC–MS with TMS derivatization, making it challenging to directly observe underivatized αGG. In this study, we aimed to establish a simple HPLC method that did not require derivatization for the analysis of αGG isomers. Among the tested columns with various separation modes, only DCpak PMPC efficiently separated the αGG isomers. Other columns either failed to separate the αGG isomers, or the αGG peak overlapped with other carbohydrates such as glucose, rendering these columns unsuitable for the analysis. Furthermore, anomeric separation was observed in the glucose and maltose peaks while using DCpak PMPC, but not with the other columns. Their ability to minutely distinguish between varying anomeric separations may have led to the effective separation of the αGG isomers. DCpak PMPC exhibits both hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions with a substrate (Poly(2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine)) derived from a phosphate choline structure bonded to silica gel (Supplementary Fig. S8). The strong hydrophilic interaction owing to the phosphate choline structure may have resulted in an effective separation of the αGG isomers. Thus, this analytical method provides a new avenue for the separation of carbohydrate isomers, enabling precise analysis of αGG.

NMR analysis revealed that products B and A correspond to 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG, respectively. In particular, 1-O-αGG exists in two diastereomers: 1-R-O-αGG and 1-S–O-αGG. These two 1-O-αGGs have asymmetric positions: C1' and C3' of glycerol relative to the pyranose ring of glucose; the peaks of both carbons appear unequal in the HSQC spectrum (Table 1). This evidence is important for structural identification; notably, this is the first report of the NMR spectrum of 1-O-αGG.

Maltitol, a reduced maltose, is a compound formed by the glycosidic bond between glucose and d-sorbitol. It differs from αGG in the number of carbon atoms in the aglycone but has a similar structure. Moreover, the melting point of maltitol is approximately 150 °C. Considering the small number of carbon atoms in the aglycone, the melting points of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG were considered reasonable values.

We investigated the production of αGG by XgtA at different reaction temperatures to selectively produce 1-O-αGG. The production ratio of 1-O-αGG increased with decreasing reaction temperature. The amount of 1-O-αGG significantly exceeded that of 2-O-αGG with increasing reaction temperature. These results indicate that the optimum temperature for overall αGG production is highly dependent on 1-O-αGG. The increase in the production ratio of 1-O-αGG with decreasing reaction temperature was an interesting result in the selective production of 1-O-αGG.

The ratio of αGG produced by XgtA was 1-R-O-αGG: 1-S–O-αGG: 2-O-αGG = 70: 25: 5. enzymatic αGG production reported to date includes sucrose phosphorylase and α-glucosidase. αGG production by sucrose phosphorylase selectively produces 2-O-αGG in which glucose is bound to the 2-OH group of glycerol. Furthermore, a mixture of 1-O-αGG and 2-O-αGG has been produced using α-glucosidase. The ratio of 1-R-O-αGG: 1-S–O-αGG: 2-O-αGG = 52: 38: 10 has been reported in HaG, an α-glucosidase with high homology to XgtA (Ojima et al. 2012). In addition, α-glucosidase derived from Aspergillus niger was produced at a ratio of 1-R-O-αGG: 1-S–O-αGG: 2-O-αGG = 73: 20: 7 (Ojima et al. 2012). The ratio of 1-R-O-αGG: 1-S–O-αGG: 2-O-αGG = 70: 25: 5 was produced using XgtA; ratio of 1-O-αGG was the highest at 95%. The production ratio of the R- and S- isomers differs from that of other α-glucosidases. This suggests that this ratio was not reached by spontaneous transition. Rather, it is derived from the nature of the enzyme. Furthermore, αGG production with and without GI at 0 °C reached 359.3 mM and 75.41 mM, respectively (Supplementary Figs. S14 and S15). As shown in Fig. S16, the amount of αGG production at each reaction temperature decreased with decreasing temperature over the reaction time. Therefore, it can be concluded that the production of αGG at 0 °C is due to the long-term reaction.

Considerations and limitations

In this study, we achieved high-yield production of αGG using XgtA. Furthermore, we separated and isolated the αGG isomers. However, several points must be considered when interpreting the results. First, although we demonstrated that the ratio of the produced αGG isomers varied with reaction temperature, the molecular mechanism underlying this variation remained unclear. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as phenomenological findings, rather than directly relating them to the enzyme structure and reaction mechanism.

Although we demonstrated successful separation of αGG isomers using a DCpak PMPC, the details of the interaction between the stationary phase and the analyte molecules were not elucidated. The main purpose of this study was to establish the separation conditions. Thus, understanding the separation mechanism remains a future challenge.

Finally, while this study focused on the production and analysis of αGG, it did not investigate its direct physiological functions. Furthermore, comparisons between αGG and β-glucosylglycerol, as well as the feasibility of industrial production were not explored. These aspects need to be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

We successfully developed a system for the selective and high-yield production of αGG by combining two enzymes: XgtA and GI. We achieved 1141 mM αGG production within 48 h; this value represents the highest reported amount of 1-O-αGG produced by enzymatic methods to date. Furthermore, it was worth noting that the ratio of isomers produced varied depending on the reaction temperature. 1-O-αGG has diverse functionalities, including moisturizing, cytoprotective, and protein-stabilizing properties. The high-yield, highly selective production method established in this study will serve as a fundamental technology for the acceleration of applied research into cosmetics, food additives, and cell and enzyme stabilizers. In particular, the combination of high 1-O-αGG selectivity and improved yield with the addition of GI can substantially promote the industrial applicability of this method.
