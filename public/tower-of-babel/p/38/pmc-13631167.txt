Combination antifungal therapy against resistant C. albicans: efficacy and immune response in mice
Authors: Ali Taghizadeh-Maleki, Farahnaz Hatami, Sima-Sadat Seyedjavadi, Farnoush Asghari-Paskiabi, Zahra Jahanshiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study demonstrates the therapeutic effects of combination therapies against Candida albicans in mouse models, as opposed to mono-therapeutic treatments, while also comparing the pathogenicity of both sensitive and resistant strains. Fluconazole-resistant strain of Candida albicans was isolated from an infected patient, and its infectivity potential was compared with a susceptible strain by injecting a fungal cell suspension into the mice model. Additionally, the level of secretory interleukin 17 in the mice was examined. Furthermore, the efficacy of combination therapy was assessed using Fluconazole-Caspofungin (Flu-Cas) and Fluconazole-Amphotericin B (Flu-AmB) in comparison to monotherapy. The results indicate that the resistant strain exhibits higher pathogenicity compared to the susceptible strain in inducing systemic infection within the mouse model, as significant differences between the resistant and susceptible groups were observed at day 5 (p = 0.0401) and day 7 (p = 0.0182). The concentrations of IL-17 (pg/ml) on days 1 to 7 were 43.66 to 259 pg/ml, and 39.66 to 210 pg/ml in resistant and susceptible groups, respectively, which were only significant on days 5 (p = 0.0356) and 7 (p = 0.0280). Combination therapies using Flu-Cas and Flu-AmB demonstrated an effective reduction in fungal colonization after 17 days. This study demonstrates that Fluconazole-resistant Candida albicans strains are more pathogenic than susceptible ones in mice. Combination therapies with Fluconazole-Amphotericin B were most effective in reducing fungal colonization, outperforming monotherapy. These results support combination treatment for resistant Candida infections.

Introduction

Invasive candidiasis (IC) is a serious and life-threatening infection caused by various Candida species and represents the most prevalent fungal disease in high-income countries, particularly among hospitalized patients. IC encompasses both candidemia and deep-seated tissue candidiasis, arising from dissemination of Candida spp. to sterile body sites such as the abdomen, peritoneum, or bone. Among hospital-acquired infections, Candida-related diseases carry the highest mortality burden, with fatality rates within 30 days of infection estimated at 40–55% (Soriano et al. 2023; CDC 2017). Despite advances in antifungal therapy, treatment options remain limited, and azole antifungals—while widely used—face growing challenges due to the emergence of resistant isolates (Whaley et al. 2017).

Fluconazole, a triazole antifungal, has long served as a cornerstone of candidiasis management owing to its cost-effectiveness and oral bioavailability. However, the increasing prevalence of fluconazole-resistant strains, particularly among C. albicans and non-albicans Candida species, has substantially complicated clinical decision-making (Berkow et al. 2017). Resistance mechanisms in C. albicans involve mutations in regulatory genes such as MRR1 and UPC2, resulting in overexpression of efflux pumps (CDR1, MDR1) and alterations in ergosterol biosynthesis pathways (Zhang et al. 2015). While these adaptations reduce intracellular drug accumulation, they may also affect pathogen fitness in non-selective environments, creating a trade-off between drug tolerance and virulence (Popp et al. 2017). Moreover, resistant strains often display niche-specific behavior, sustaining gastrointestinal colonization while exhibiting variable systemic virulence, which further complicates eradication (Berkow et al. 2017). Biofilm formation additionally amplifies resistance by generating extracellular matrices that impede drug penetration and protect fungal cells from host immune surveillance (Daneshnia et al. 2023). Notably, altered fungal cell wall components such as β-glucan masking interfere with recognition by neutrophils and macrophages, enabling immune evasion (Escribano et al. 2022).

To overcome these obstacles, combination antifungal strategies have garnered increasing attention. Pairing fluconazole with echinocandins such as caspofungin—which inhibit β-1,3-glucan synthesis—or with polyenes such as amphotericin B—which disrupts fungal membrane integrity—offers complementary mechanisms of action that may enhance fungal clearance and reduce the likelihood of resistance development (Jenkins et al. 2023; Lewis et al. 2000). Such regimens provide several theoretical advantages over monotherapy, including broader-spectrum activity, synergistic potency at lower drug concentrations, reduced toxicity, and simultaneous targeting of multiple fungal pathways (Wake et al. 2024; Vitale et al. 2021). Clinical evidence supports the promise of these approaches; for instance, combination regimens involving amphotericin B have shown favorable outcomes in refractory invasive candidiasis (Teng et al. 2024). Nevertheless, barriers including drug interactions, cost burden, and limited large-scale clinical trial data continue to hinder their routine adoption (Johnson et al. 2010; Odds et al. 2003).

Beyond direct antifungal activity, host immune responses play a pivotal role in determining infection outcomes. Fungal recognition by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on CD4 + T cells triggers activation of the Th17 immune pathway, leading to IL-17A secretion, which orchestrates neutrophil recruitment and mucosal defense (Puerta-Arias et al. 2020). IL-17A has been proposed as a potential immunotherapeutic target in systemic C. albicans infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals such as those with malignancy or advanced HIV/AIDS (Huang et al. 2004). Experimental evidence further supports this role, as mice with deficient IL-17 signaling demonstrate heightened susceptibility to both systemic and mucosal candidiasis, and in vivo delivery of mIL-17A has been shown to confer protection against lethal C. albicans challenge, underscoring the necessity of the mIL-17A/mIL-17AR axis in antifungal host defense (Huang et al. 2004; Van de Veerdonk et al. 2010).

Despite growing evidence on antifungal combination strategies and immune responses, limited in vivo data exist on the comparative pathogenicity of fluconazole-resistant versus susceptible C. albicans isolates and the concurrent IL-17 immune response in the context of combination therapy. The present study, therefore, aimed to compare the pathogenicity of a fluconazole-resistant clinical isolate with a susceptible reference strain in a murine model, assess IL-17 serum levels as an indicator of immune activation, and evaluate the efficacy of Fluconazole-Caspofungin (Flu-Cas) and Fluconazole-Amphotericin B (Flu-AmB) combination regimens relative to monotherapy in controlling systemic candidiasis.

Materials and methods

Strains, cultivation, and antifungal susceptibility

In the present study, C. albicans ATCC 10231 and a clinical Flu-resistant C. albicans PFCC1404-117 were obtained from the Pathogenic Fungi Culture Collection of the Pasteur Institute of Iran, Tehran, Iran. C. albicans PFCC1404-117 was isolated from a patient with a kidney infection following abdominal surgery who was enrolled in this study. The Candida strain was identified by culturing on CHROMagar Candida medium and ITS region sequencing by ITS1-ITS4 primers (Jahanshiri et al. 2022). C. albicans ATCC10231 was used as a sensitive control.

Antifungal susceptibility test

The susceptibility of isolated Candida species to five antifungal agents including fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, caspofungin, and amphotericin B (all obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), was evaluated following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). In brief, fungal suspensions containing 2 × 103 cells/mL were aliquoted into the wells of microtiter plates containing RPMI 1640 medium (buffered with 0.165 M MOPS and adjusted to pH 7.0–7.2). Various concentrations of the antifungal drugs, were then added to the 96-well plates, which were incubated at 35 °C for 24 to 48 h. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined according to CLSI documents M27-S4, M60, M59, and epidemiological cutoff values (ECVs) (CLSI 2012, 2018, 2020) as summarized in Table 1. Quality control was performed using the reference strain C. albicans ATCC 10231. Animals

In this study, female BALB/c mice aged 6 to 8 weeks (weighing 20–22 g) were procured from the Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran). Prior to experimentation, animals were acclimated for one week under controlled conditions, with ad libitum access to food and water. Environmental parameters were maintained at a temperature range of 20–25 °C, relative humidity of 40–70%, and a 12 h light/dark photoperiod. All procedures involving animals were performed in strict compliance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals established by the Pasteur Institute of Iran, Tehran (approval no. IR.PII.AEC.1401.011).

Systemic infection by susceptible and resistant Candida isolates

To compare the pathogenicity of sensitive and resistant strains, C. albicans PFCC1404-117 and the reference strain C. albicans ATCC10231 were grown in YNB medium at 28 °C for 4 h until reaching the stationary phase. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation, washed with sterile PBS buffer, and adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 107 cells/ml. To assess their potential to cause systemic infection, three groups of BALB/c mice (9 in each) were each injected with 0. mL of the fungal suspension via the lateral tail vein. A control group received 0.2 mL of PBS buffer. The mice were monitored daily for 7 days to observe morbidity and mortality rates. Deceased animals underwent autopsy to evaluate fungal invasion of organs. To determine CFU in the organs, mice were euthanized after seven days. Kidneys were collected under sterile conditions, as they contain the highest number of Candida cells in systemic candidiasis mouse models. For fungal load assessment, kidneys were weighed, homogenized in 1 ml of PBS, and the homogenates were serially diluted before being plated on SDA. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and fungal burden was calculated as the log10 CFU per gram of organ tissue (Lionakis et al. 2011).

Interleukin 17 assay in serum

At 1, 3, 5, and 7 days post-infection in the mouse model, blood samples were collected, and serum was isolated for subsequent cytokine analysis. Interleukin-17 (IL-17) levels were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Mabtech, Sweden) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Cytokine concentrations (pg/mL) were determined by interpolation from a standard curve generated with known concentrations, enabling the calculation of the absolute IL-17 concentration in each individual sample (Ahmadbeigi et al. 2020).

Treatment-combination therapy

Six groups of mice (18 mice in each) were considered for this experiment. After inducing infection as described above, antifungal therapy was performed at a final monotherapy dose of 1 mg/kg Flu, 5 mg/kg Cas, 0.5 mg/kg AmB, and combination therapy: 1 mg/kg Flu with 5 mg/kg Cas, 1 mg/kg Flu with 0.5 mg/kg AmB, and control in six groups respectively (treatment was performed daily from day 5). In the control group, just sterile PBS buffer was injected. Treatment was carried out for 3 sequential weeks after infection. The mice were monitored for 17 days to observe morbidity and mortality rates Survival investigation was achieved using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test (Segal et al. 2018). In order to measure the fungal burden, the kidney as an adjective target organ was examined in a separate experiment. Infected mice were euthanized on days 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, and 17 from the time of infection. After euthanasia, the kidney was removed instantly under aseptic conditions, after weighing, the organ was homogenized in 1 ml sterile saline solution and diluted ten-fold serially in PBS buffer. A 50 μl of each dilution were cultured on SDA medium containing penicillin and streptomycin, then incubated at 37 °C for 24h. The mean of Colony-forming units (CFU)/kidney in three mice was expressed as CFUs/g tissue (Segal et al. 2018).

Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

To qualitatively confirm the findings, histopathological analysis was conducted. Kidney specimens were paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 4 µm thickness, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Leagene, Beijing, China) following standard protocols (Wu et al. 2020). Tissue sections were examined under a light microscope at × 400 magnification, and representative images were captured.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are represented as the mean ± standard deviation. Two -way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Prism Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for determination of significant differences between the experimental groups. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. For comparisons of CFU counts, a Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Šidák's multiple comparisons test was performed. Two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test was applied to analyze interleukin-17 (IL-17) levels as well as CFU counts in the combination therapy group. In all analyses, differences with a P-value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Interleukin 17 detection in serum

Serum IL-17 cytokine concentrations were quantified on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-infection in a murine model to evaluate the immune responses elicited by fluconazole-susceptible and fluconazole-resistant C. albicans isolates. Results demonstrated differential IL-17 levels (pg/ml) between mice infected with susceptible versus resistant isolates. On days 1, 3, 5, and 7, the concentrations of IL-17 in the resistant group were measured at 43.66, 215.33, 302.66, and 259 pg/ml, respectively, whereas in the susceptible group, the corresponding values were 39.66, 186, 256.66, and 210 pg/ml. Two-way ANOVA with Tukey's comparisons test revealed a statistically significant difference between the resistant and susceptible groups only at the later days of 5 and 7, but not at earlier days (0, 1, or 3). Specifically, at day 5, the resistant group showed a mean difference of 46.00 (95% CI 2.675 to 89.32, p = 0.0356) compared to the susceptible group, with an adjusted p-value of 0.0356. At time point 7, the resistant group exhibited a mean difference of 49.00 (95% CI 5.675 to 92.32, p = 0.0240) compared to the susceptible group, with an adjusted p-value of 0.0240. These results indicate that while there is no significant divergence between the two groups initially, the resistant group develops significantly higher values than the susceptible group by days 5 and 7. Furthermore, at days 3, 5, and 7, both the resistant and susceptible groups were highly significantly different from the control group (all p < 0.0001), confirming that both treatments induce a measurable effect relative to the untreated control. Fungal burden in kidneys over time after treatment with Flu, AmB, or Flu in combination with AmB and Cas

Our results show that during the initial phase (days 1–5 post-infection), all experimental groups showed low baseline fungal colonization in the kidneys. By day 5, the control group and the fluconazole (Flu) monotherapy groups (for resistant isolate) showed a sharp increase in renal colony-forming units (CFU), with log CFU/g values exceeding 1.0 and continuing to increase. During the initial phase of treatment (days 5–7), animals infected with the resistant isolate and treated with Flu monotherapy showed the highest fungal load on day 7, which was slightly lower than the control group (which received PBS). Conversely, a decreasing trend was observed in animals infected with the susceptible isolate. Notably, all infected animals in the control group, as well as animals infected with the resistant isolate and treated with Flu, died within seven days of the intervention. The groups receiving caspofungin (Cas), amphotericin B (AmB), and combination therapy (Flu + Cas or Flu + AmB), were monitored for up to 17 days, showed a trend of decreasing CFU counts by day 7, indicating treatment efficacy compared with Flu monotherapy and control conditions. During the convalescence phase (days 7–17), all treatment groups, including the combination regimens, showed a rapid reduction in renal fungal burden. By day 15, CFU counts had decreased in all groups, which continued through day 17. Regarding comparative efficacy, Flu monotherapy was the least effective in controlling initial fungal proliferation in animals infected with resistant isolates, resulting in higher CFU levels than the other treatments during the first week. Cas and AmB monotherapies provided better control than Flu alone in animals infected with resistant or susceptible isolates, reducing renal CFU counts early in the course of infection. Among all regimens, the combination treatments (Flu + Cas and Flu + AmB) were effective. The Flu + Cas and Cas treatment groups showed almost the same pattern. Discussion

The emergence of fluconazole-resistant Candida albicans strains not only complicates therapy but may also enhance pathogenic potential. It has been demonstrated that resistant isolates maintain robust growth and display rapid hyphal formation and strong adherence to host tissues (Angiolella et al. 2008). Similarly, another study reported that resistant strains often carry elevated virulence traits compared to susceptible ones (Fekete et al. 2000). In urinary Candida isolates from hospitalized patients, higher fluconazole MICs positively correlated with biofilm formation and phospholipase production (Zeitoun et al. 2024).

Similarly, experimental analyses of systemic infection models have revealed that certain resistant strains demonstrate more robust hyphal development and greater organ invasiveness (Kilbas et al. 2025). Consistent with these findings, our in vivo results showed that by day 7 post-infection, the kidney fungal burden in mice infected with the resistant isolate (log₁₀ CFU/g = 2.67) was notably higher than in those infected with the susceptible strain (log₁₀ CFU/g = 1.92), indicating enhanced pathogenicity of the resistant isolate, which were also statistically significant.

Although our study did not directly assess molecular mechanisms, previous transcriptomic and genomic analyses have demonstrated that fluconazole-resistant C. albicans strains frequently upregulate virulence-associated genes such as ALS3, HWP1, and SAP6, which may help explain the enhanced pathogenicity observed in our in vivo model (Suwannaphong et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025).

Together, these findings support a model in which fluconazole resistance (or tolerance) and enhanced virulence can be tightly coupled, underlining the need for therapies that target both.

Numerous studies have investigated the synergistic potential of combining azoles, echinocandins, and polyenes to improve outcomes in invasive candidiasis. For instance, two studies reported that azole–echinocandin and azole–polyene combinations not only improved fungal clearance but also reduced mortality in high-risk patients (Fioriti et al. 2022; Wake et al. 2024). Jacobs and Chaturvedi further emphasized the importance of tailoring antifungal regimens to fungal susceptibility profiles, highlighting the superior efficacy of fluconazole–caspofungin combinations in early-phase infection control (Jacobs et al. 2024).

Our findings are in line with these reports. While caspofungin and amphotericin B monotherapies effectively reduced kidney fungal burden, their combinations with fluconazole (Flu + Cas and Flu + AmB) achieved the most rapid and pronounced clearance, particularly during the treatment. This suggests that dual therapy not only enhances fungal control but may also contribute to improved survival outcomes, as all animals in the Flu (resistant isolate) and control groups succumbed by day 7, whereas combination-treated groups showed significantly better survival and fungal clearance.

Moreover, a recent review by Jacobs and Chaturvedi in Open Forum Infectious Diseases emphasized that combination antifungal therapy (CAF) can reduce resistance development, improve drug penetration, and accelerate fungal eradication, albeit with considerations regarding cost and toxicity (Jacobs et al. 2024). Similarly, a comprehensive review by Rauseo et al. in the Journal of Fungi underscored that combination regimens, especially those involving amphotericin B, are promising against invasive candidiasis, particularly in immunocompromised hosts (Rauseo et al. 2023). Further mechanistic insight is provided by Hefny et al., whose transcriptomic meta-analysis suggests that combination therapies may act by disrupting multiple metabolic and stress-response pathways, supporting multi-targeted approaches in refractory infections (Hefny et al. 2024). Given that the present study utilized a fluconazole-resistant isolate and demonstrated that combination therapy with amphotericin B and fluconazole was more effective than amphotericin B monotherapy, these findings suggest that combination treatment may modulate specific pathways or mechanisms that restore fluconazole susceptibility in an otherwise resistant strain. Nevertheless, further validation using a larger panel of resistant isolates in vivo is required to substantiate this hypothesis. Our data demonstrated that only at day 7, Amphotericin B + Fluconazole had a significantly greater effect on infection compared to Caspofungin + Fluconazole in the resistant isolate; this difference was significant in the susceptible isolate on days 7 and 9. It is possible that a larger sample size could have rendered this difference statistically significant.

Taken together, our study reinforces the growing body of evidence supporting the use of combination antifungal therapies in cases involving resistant Candida strains. Future research should further explore optimal dosing strategies, timing, and potential toxicity profiles to refine these regimens for clinical application.

The role of interleukin-17 (IL-17) in host defense against C. albicans has been increasingly recognized as a critical component of the innate and adaptive immune response. Recent studies have demonstrated that IL-17-producing cells, including Th17 cells and innate lymphoid cells, are rapidly activated upon fungal invasion, orchestrating neutrophil recruitment and enhancing mucosal and systemic clearance of Candida species. It has been demonstrated that IL-17 is essential for epidermal clearance of C. albicans, even in the absence of canonical fungal pattern recognition receptors, underscoring its central role in antifungal immunity (Iwasawa et al. 2022). In line with these findings, our study revealed significantly elevated IL-17 levels in mice infected with fluconazole-resistant C. albicans isolates compared to those infected with susceptible strains, particularly from day 3 post-infection onward. This observation is consistent with the work of Sun et al. who reported that IL-17 responses were more pronounced in systemic candidiasis models involving resistant or biofilm-forming strains, suggesting that heightened IL-17 production may reflect a compensatory immune mechanism in response to more virulent or treatment-refractory infections (Sun et al. 2024). Moreover, Jungnickel and Jacobsen emphasized the utility of murine models in dissecting the temporal dynamics of cytokine responses during systemic candidiasis, noting that IL-17 levels often peak during the early phase of infection and correlate with fungal burden and tissue damage (Jungnickel et al. 2022). Our data support this temporal pattern, with IL-17 concentrations rising sharply by day 3 (not significant) and remaining elevated through day 7, particularly in the resistant group, although this increase was only statistically significant on days 5 and 7. These findings suggest that IL-17 may serve as a potential biomarker for early immune activation and could inform therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating host responses in drug-resistant fungal infections (Jungnickel et al. 2022).

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential link between fluconazole resistance and increased virulence in C. albicans, as well as the distinct IL-17-mediated immune responses associated with resistant strains. While our findings support the efficacy of combination antifungal therapies and suggest IL-17 as a possible biomarker of infection severity, several limitations should be acknowledged. These include the exclusive use of a murine model, limited cytokine profiling, and the absence of direct correlation between immune responses and tissue-specific fungal burden. Future studies should incorporate broader immunological markers, diverse host models, more resistant isolates, and mechanistic analyses to better understand how resistance, virulence, and host immunity interact and to optimize combination treatment strategies accordingly.
