Quantifying crustal stress in the Sinai Peninsula caused by gravitational potential energy and its tectonic implications
Authors: Chokri Jallouli, Ali K. Abdelfattah, Hassan Alzahrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study uses numerical models of topography and Moho depth to quantify horizontal force per unit length (ΔFx) and assess crustal deviatoric stress caused by variations in Gravitational Potential Energy (ΔGPE) across Sinai Peninsula and its vicinity. Results show ΔFx reaches 2 × 1012 N/m, with deviatoric stress ranging from −20 MPa (compression) in lowland regions (Gulf of Suez and Gulf of Aqaba) to 20 MPa (tension) in central and southern Sinai terrains. This GPE-derived stress, does not account for the far-field tectonic component. Using seismicity data, including Magnitude-Frequency distribution and focal mechanisms, we reveal a predominately extensional stress regimes in the Gulf of Suez, central and southern Sinai, with a strike-slip to transtensional regime in the Gulf of Aqaba. The northward traction of Sinai reduces the impact of GPE-derived compressive stress in Aqaba and Suez, however this motion, supported by geodetic evidences, appears insufficient to relieve the high GPE-derived extensional stress in high-elevation of central and southern Sinai, where it plays an important role. These findings indicate that where plate motions are significant, tectonic stresses dominate over GPE-derived stresses, whereas in more stable zones, GPE contributes significantly. Integrating GPE-based stress calculations with earthquake focal mechanism, seismicity, and previous geodetic studies provides a comprehensive understanding of the prevailing stress regimes and crustal deformation patterns. This approach underscores the complexity of tectonic interactions in Sinai and provides vital input for seismic hazard assessments and geodynamic models.

Introduction

Isostasy, defined as the equilibrium state of the Earth’s crust, is essential for comprehending the responses of different crustal segments to variations in load and stress. In response to changes in loading above or beneath the lithosphere, vertical adjustments and mass redistributions take place until a new equilibrium is attained. Such isostatic adjustments can engender alterations in gravitational potential energy (ΔGPE), significantly influencing local elevations and mass distributions. ΔGPE creates horizontal lithospheric stress, and thus there exists a relationship between ΔGPE and horizontal lithospheric stress1. Although this relationship is complicated by variances in material properties and topographical changes over time, various researchers have estimated the horizontal lithospheric stress from lateral variations in GPE2–6. Stress derived from GPE should be considered and may impact the deformation. Such methods have been applied in the Tibetan Plateau2,5, Africa7,8 and the central Andes and Colorado Plateau9, where stress magnitudes are estimated and discussed based on GPE. For instance, the calculated lateral variation of GPE in the Tibetan Plateau ranges from 10 to 12 × 1012 N/m, implying an average lithospheric deviatoric stress between 50 and 90 MPa2,10,11.

Understanding the regional stress state and deformation style in the Sinai Peninsula remains a challenge due to the complex interplay between gravitational potential energy variations and far-field tectonic forces. Existing studies have not fully resolved how lateral variations in gravitational potential energy (ΔGPE) contribute to horizontal crustal stresses and how these stresses interact with plate boundary kinematics and crustal deformation patterns. This study aims to quantify the horizontal crustal stress induced by ΔGPE variations using numerical models of topography and Moho depth, and evaluate how it influences the current stress regime. A distinguishing feature of this area is the availability of a well-imaged Moho-depth model derived from geophysical data12,13. Given the well-constrained variations in crustal thickness, the horizontal crustal stress can be calculated. However, the stress obtained from GPE does not consider the far-field tectonic events which could either enhance or diminish the effects of the stress linked to GPE. It’s a component that could impact the total observed stress. The actual stress regime of the region has been also determined from the analysis of seismicity data, including Magnitude-Frequency distribution and focal mechanisms. By integrating GPE derived stresses with seismicity analyses and geodetic observations, we seek to clarify the relative contributions of gravitational and tectonic forces to the prevailing stress regimes and deformation styles in the region. This approach addresses the unresolved issue of how GPE influences extensional, compressional, and strike-slip regimes within the Sinai Peninsula, providing insights crucial for geodynamic modeling and seismic hazard assessment.

Geological and tectonic settings

The geology and tectonic evolution of the Sinai Peninsula have been extensively documented in previous studies, including regional syntheses addressing stratigraphy, rift development, and plate kinematics2,14,15. The southern segment of Sinai is characterized by Precambrian basement rocks, exhibiting geological similarities with Arabian and Nubian Shield (Fig. 1). The Sinai Peninsula is a tectonically active subplate situated between the African and Arabian plates, with its motion influenced by the adjacent Eurasian plate. The morphotectonic structures of the Sinai region are shaped by the inter-plate movements at its active boundaries, as well as by intraplate adjustments within the crust and lithospheric mantle. Such processes are manifested through various forms of geological displacements, rifting, uplift, and faulting16,17. It is noteworthy that the western and eastern boundaries of Sinai are characterized by high seismic activity, indicative of active tectonic processes and ongoing crustal deformation16,18.

The interaction between the African and Eurasian plates is convergent, engendering considerable geological ramifications along the Cyprian Arc, which extends from the Turkish plate towards the Aegean Sea19. This region is subjected to compressional forces, resulting in phenomena such as orogenesis, thrust faulting, and intensified seismicity20. The persistent convergence at this boundary generates stress that can accrue significant seismic events.

The western boundary of Sinai, marked by the Gulf of Suez Rift, is characterized by extensional tectonics resulting from the divergence between the African and Arabian plates15,21. This rifting leads to crustal thinning and reduced crustal thickness, causing significant lateral variations in gravitational potential energy. The eastern boundary, represented by the Gulf of Aqaba transform fault system, exhibits a transtensional regime combining left-lateral strike-slip and extensional motions20,21. This transform boundary accommodates the clockwise rotation of the Sinai subplate and introduces shear stresses that interact with GPE-induced forces.

Crustal thickness variations across Sinai are well-imaged through geophysical data, revealing thinner crust beneath the Gulf of Suez and Gulf of Aqaba compared to the thicker crust in central and southern Sinai12. These variations are essential inputs for calculating GPE and the resultant horizontal stresses, as they reflect mass distribution differences within the lithosphere.

Major fault systems, including the Dead Sea Transform to the north and the shear zones traversing central Sinai, serve as structural boundaries influencing stress transmission and deformation localization. Their inclusion in the model is justified by their role in accommodating plate motions and modulating stress fields derived from both tectonic forces and gravitational potential energy gradients.

When deviatoric stress τₓₓ is derived from ΔGPE representing lateral differences in gravitational potential energy, it quantifies the magnitude of horizontal stress associated with variations in lithospheric buoyancy rather than absolute forces. In this formulation, ΔGPE behaves as a scalar measure of horizontal forcing; consequently, the horizontal stress tensor becomes isotropic, with τₓₓ = τᵧᵧ and τₓᵧ = 0.

To calculate the gravitational potential energy variation (ΔGPE) and the corresponding changes in horizontal force per unit length (ΔFx), a numerical representation of the crustal geometry is essential. Previous investigations indicate that local isostatic equilibrium is not uniformly attained across the entirety Arabian Plate, Egypt, and northern Red Sea. Notably, the central block of Sinai exhibits substantial isostatic anomalies, reaching up to 100 mGal13. The loading dynamics within the Sinai lithosphere cannot be adequately accounted for by the Airy isostatic model, which assumes complete compensation. Instead, a regional isostatic mechanism that employs a flexural model or alternative frameworks is suggested to more accurately interpret the observed gravity anomalies. Numerous studies have endeavored to delineate the true geometry of the Moho discontinuity through various methodologies, including seismic profiling11, earthquake travel time inversion27, receiver function analysis28, Rayleigh wave analysis29, and gravity methods30–32, as well as through integrated geophysical techniques13. In this study we considered the Moho-depth model for Sinai derived by a combination of topography, gravity, and seismological data. This model is refined through a joint inversion of residual gravity and residual topography. Initially, observed Moho depths were adjusted for Airy isostatic compensation, incorporating considerations for surface loading, which accounts for both topographic and bathymetric features, as well as variations in sediment density. The validity of the Moho model is substantiated through seismic measurements from the US Geological Survey database33 and recent analyses of receiver functions28. Furthermore, the model accounts for and removes the effects of deep mantle heterogeneities, as delineated by the global dynamic model12. The resulting Moho-depth model reveals a crustal thickening beneath Sinai, reaching a depth of 35 km, followed by a gradual reduction to 30 km as it approaches the coastal Mediterranean area, and further decreasing to 25 km toward the Red Sea (Fig. 3). Notably, this Moho-depth model does not illustrate any significant thinning beneath the Gulf of Suez and the Gulf of Aqaba, despite the existence of a mass deficiency at the surface represented by seawater. In the central block of Sinai, the identified Moho-depth of 35 km signifies a crustal thickening. However, considering that the elevation in this region exceeds 2000 m, this thickening is not as pronounced as would be anticipated under conditions of complete isostatic compensation. Consequently, a portion of the topographic load is supported by the lithosphere’s rigidity24 and evidenced by a significant isostatic anomaly33. Result

The stress caused by ΔGPE

We implemented a two-layer geophysical model, comprising the Earth’s crust and lithospheric mantle (Fig. 2), deliberately omitting any influence from far-field tectonic stress. This model operates under the assumption of constant horizontal dimensions, thereby isolating the driving forces of flow to variations in gravitational potential energy. For the computation of ΔGPE, it is essential to ascertain key parameters: the density of the crust (ρc), density of the lithospheric mantle (ρm), elevation (he), normal crustal thickness (hc), the root associated with the Moho discontinuity (hr), and the mantle thickness (hm). Data for elevation (he) were extracted from the global relief model (ETOPO1)34, while the crustal root values (hr) (Fig. 3) were derived from the model derived by the integration of different geophysical techniques13. We adopted a standard crustal thickness (hc) of 28 km, which aligns with the observed thickness in coastal zones35. Using the above-mentioned (hr) values (Fig. 2), we determined the mantle thickness (hm) by presuming an average lithospheric thickness of 120 km. For the densities, we utilized values previously adopted to construct the Moho-depth model applicable to Sinai and its vicinity13,36. Table 1 shows input parameters and their uncertainties used to calculate ΔGPE and deviatoric stress. We note that crust and mantle density estimate are derived from seismic velocity data36,37. We applied the methodology described above to quantify ΔGPE in Sinai, recognizing that ΔGPE denotes the change of the horizontal force per unit length (ΔFx) independent of any assumptions regarding isostasy. The resultant ΔFx ranges from −2 × 1012 N/m in the lowlands to 2 × 1012 N/m in the highlands (Fig. 4). Notably, in the central and southern block of Sinai, ΔFx shows positive values, while negative values are recorded in the Gulf of Suez and Gulf of Aqaba. These variations in GPE and ΔFx are attributed to crustal thickness differences and density changes across the Moho discontinuity, leading to the generation of tectonic overpressure and deviatoric crustal stresses. As we are focusing on GPE-derived stress, it’s important to quantify uncertainties of calculated GPE. The uncertainty analysis is implemented as a forward Monte-Carlo propagation in which the input parameters are treated as random variables and repeatedly sampled to generate an ensemble of model outputs. For each grid node, independent realizations number (typically N = 200) are generated by drawing perturbed values of the material densities ρc and ρm from normal distributions, and by perturbing the elevation and crustal thickness fields. The thickness of the lithospheric mantle hm and gravity g ​ are treated as fixed in the current implementation. For each realization, the GPE is computed from the prescribed energy equation. Uncertainty in GPE is summarized at each grid point providing spatially resolved estimates of both central tendency and uncertainty which is less than 0.8 × 1012 N/m (Fig. 4b).

The thickness of the lithospheric mantle (hm), derived from the used lithospheric thickness, significantly influences the ΔGPE values and, consequently, the calculated magnitude of deviatoric stress. Seismic tomography indicates that the lithospheric thickness of the Arabian plate ranges from 90 to 160 km38,39. In this study, an average thickness of 120 km was employed. To assess the effect of lithospheric thickness, ΔGPE was calculated using various thickness values. When a reduced lithospheric thickness of 80 km was used instead of 120 km, both the ΔGPE and the resulting deviatoric stress decreased by approximately 25%. ΔGPE decreases from ± 2 × 1012 N/m to ± 1.5 × 1012 N/m. However, the stress remains compressional in Aqaba and Suez, and extensional in southern and Central Sinai.

GPE-derived stress analysis

Figure 5 presents the spatial distribution of the crustal deviatoric stress field \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau }_{xx}^{c}\left(x,y\right)$$\end{document} derived from lateral variations in Gravity Potential Energy (GPE), together with the higher horizontal gradient orientations. The mapped stress magnitude, expressed in MPa, represents the gravitationally induced tectonic overpressure arising from contrasts in lithospheric structure, including variations in crustal thickness, density distribution, and topography. Negative stress values correspond to compressional regimes, whereas positive values indicate extensional regimes. The calculations are performed under the assumption that GPE constitute the dominant horizontal driving force, and that far-field tectonic stresses are not explicitly imposed. These forces drive horizontal stress redistribution as the system tends to minimize potential energy.

Several previous studies have derived principal stress orientations from GPE using thin-sheet force-balance formulations7–9. In those approaches, GPE gradients are incorporated into the full horizontal force-balance equations, allowing recovery of the complete horizontal stress tensor. In this work we do not solve the full thin-sheet equilibrium equations and therefore do not obtain the complete deviatoric stress tensor (τₓₓ, τᵧᵧ, τₓᵧ). Our analysis is restricted to evaluating the magnitude and spatial distribution of ΔGPE-related deviatoric stress, rather than reconstructing the full stress field. As a consequence, the orientations of the principal stresses, as well as the P- and T-axes, are not determined. Figure 5 shows a strong stress (up to 30 MPa) in southern Gulf of Aqaba and Gulf of Sinai, suggesting that gravitational body forces associated with GPE variations exert a strong control on the present-day stress. The map of crustal deviatoric stress τₓₓᶜ derived from lateral variations in gravitational potential energy (ΔGPE) highlights first-order buoyancy-driven contributions to the regional stress magnitude in the absence of far-field tectonic forcing. Negative values of τₓₓᶜ indicate compressional overpressure, whereas positive values reflect extensional tectonic overpressure associated with regions of elevated GPE. The strongest stress magnitudes are concentrated along the Gulf of Aqaba, the Gulf of Suez, and southern Sinai, where sharp lateral gradients in lithospheric structure and elevation generate pronounced GPE contrasts. In these regions, the large amplitudes of τₓₓᶜ (tens of MPa) suggest that buoyancy forces alone are sufficient to impose significant horizontal stress levels capable of modulating fault activity and crustal deformation. To better emphasize the GPE-derived stress component and its contribution in the effective actual stress, it’s useful to study the stress regime derived from seismic analysis.

The range of deviatoric stress magnitude inferred from our ΔGPE model (Fig. 5) is broadly consistent with independent in-situ stress measurements in the region. In particular, borehole and geomechanical analyses in the Southern Gulf of Suez report present-day stress magnitudes with minimum horizontal stress gradients on the order of ~ 11–17 MPa/km implying upper crustal stress magnitudes on the order of tens of MPa at depths of a few kilometers40,41. These measured stress magnitudes overlap the range of GPE-derived crustal stress and therefore the range of independent empirical constraints supports the physical plausibility of the GPE-derived stress field for the study area.

Seismicity in the Gulf of Aqaba is both frequent and vigorous, marked by the largest recorded instrumental earthquake in the region, the Mw 7.2 event on 22 November 1995. The dominant focal mechanisms reveal primarily left-lateral strike-slip faulting, with nodal planes generally oriented NNE–SSW (Fig. 6), aligning parallel to the Dead Sea Transform Fault (DSTF). In addition to strike-slip motion, a secondary normal faulting component is often observed, indicating a transtensional regime in the Gulf. This seismicity pattern reflects the active shear between the Sinai subplate and the Arabian plate, accommodating a relative motion of approximately 5–7 mm/year. The Gulf of Suez exemplifies a passive extensional rift system, with seismicity predominantly driven by pure normal faulting along NW–SE trending basin-bounding faults. The compiled focal mechanisms reveal high-angle normal faults with T-axes oriented NE–SW (Fig. 6), consistent with Red Sea rifting and the divergence between the African (Nubian) and Sinai subplates. Central Sinai displays moderate seismic activity, with magnitudes between Mw 3.5 and 5.5, and focal mechanisms suggesting normal faulting consistent with extensional stresses derived from ΔGPE. Seismic events are mainly concentrated along the eastern margin of the Gulf, where extensional strain rates are higher and fault systems are more developed. Notable events include the 1969 Mw 6.8 Shedwan Island earthquake, which exhibited a pure extensional mechanism, and recent earthquakes in 2020 that reaffirm ongoing active deformation. The regions of the Gulf of Suez and central Sinai are typified by a prevalence of small to moderate magnitude earthquakes, accompanied by relatively higher b-values compared to those observed in the Gulf of Aqaba45. Unlike the plate boundary-dominated gulfs, central Sinai appears more influenced by lithospheric buoyancy forces, with GPE-induced stresses directly contributing to brittle deformation. This is especially evident in regions of elevated topography, where the stress field transitions from compression at lower elevations to extension at higher elevations. The diversity in focal mechanisms also reflects inherited structural fabrics from older tectonic systems, possibly related to the Syrian Arc, which may act as a stress barrier or guide for strain localization.

Discussion

We applied a two-layer model to analyse variations in gravitational potential energy (ΔGPE), the horizontal driving force per unit length (ΔFx in N/m), and the resulting tectonic deviatoric stress (MPa). Calculated ΔFx values range between −2 × 1012 and 2 × 1012 N/m.

In the Sinai region and adjacent areas, crustal deviatoric stress and tectonic overpressure vary from −20 MPa in the Gulf of Aqaba and Gulf of Suez to 20 MPa in southern Sinai, where elevations exceed 2000 m. These ΔFx values are significantly lower than those reported for the Tibetan Plateau (7 × 1012 N/m)6, reflecting the higher elevation (> 4000 m) and Moho morphology differences that primarily drive ΔGPE. The stress estimates assume fixed lateral boundaries with no horizontal shortening or extension from basal lithospheric traction linked to mantle buoyancy flow; thus, they represent stress solely from GPE variations. Positive deviatoric stress in southern Sinai indicates strong extensional stress of about 20 MPa due to GPE variations. Total stress must incorporate additional dynamic tectonic stresses.

Seismological data show dominant extension in the Gulf of Suez and transtension in the Gulf of Aqaba, yet the ΔGPE-derived deviatoric stress τₓₓᶜ indicates mainly negative values (apparent compression) there. This contrast highlights the difference between buoyancy-driven tectonic overpressure and the total earthquake-recorded stress field. The GPE-derived stress reflects lithospheric gravitational forces from lateral density and elevation contrasts, constraining stress magnitude but not kinematics or principal stress orientation. In rift and transform zones like the Gulf of Suez and Gulf of Aqaba, extension and transtension are mainly controlled by far-field plate boundary forces, plate motions, and shear tractions from Arabia-Africa interactions, which are excluded from the ΔGPE model. The stress field inferred from ΔGPE represents only the buoyancy-driven component of the total lithospheric stress. Hence, compressional or extensional overpressure from ΔGPE can coexist with extensional or strike-slip focal mechanisms, reflecting multiple stress sources.

In central and southern Sinai, GPE-derived deviatoric stress τₓₓᶜ is predominantly positive, indicating extensional overpressure consistent with earthquake focal mechanisms. This suggests lithospheric buoyancy forces dominate the horizontal stress field here, enhancing the extensional tectonic regime. Conversely, northern Sinai shows low GPE-derived stress, with stress dominated by northward basal lithospheric traction.

Structural analysis shows faults in central and southern Sinai oriented in multiple directions, with a dominance of N-S faults from horizontal gradient filtering of Bouguer anomalies21, indicating extension consistent with GPE-derived deviatoric stress. Earthquake focal mechanisms in southern Sinai show normal faulting with NE–SW extension46, matching the extensional regime driven by GPE. Elevated GPE and deviatoric stress in these regions underscore the fundamental role of ΔFx in deformation, with northward basal lithospheric traction insufficient to counteract GPE effects.

At Sinai’s western and eastern boundaries, negative GPE-derived deviatoric stress values indicate strong compressional stress. Interaction between the Sinai Subplate and Arabian and African plates leads to notable seismicity. GPS data show the Sinai Subplate moves N16°W relative to Africa at 1.6 mm/year, with left-lateral deformation rates of 1.9 mm/year and extensional rates of 1.5 mm/year along the Gulf of Suez, and left-lateral rates of 4.4 mm/year along the Gulf of Aqaba-Dead Sea transform fault47.

Seismicity in the Gulf of Aqaba is frequent and strong, dominated by left-lateral strike-slip faulting with nodal planes-oriented NNE-SSW. In contrast, the Gulf of Suez exhibits NE-SW extensional forces with very low deformation velocities (< 0.003 mm/year). Despite GPE modeling indicating compressive deviatoric stresses in these gulfs, focal mechanisms reveal extensional or strike-slip regimes, implying that plate motions and Red Sea divergence exert stronger control than lithospheric buoyancy stresses. The northward Sinai Subplate motion reduces the compressive influence of GPE in the Gulf of Aqaba and Gulf of Suez, resulting in an extensional stress regime with low deformation rates moderated by GPE compressive stress.

The Sinai Subplate experiences a complex force regime, with compressional strain from Arabian–Eurasian plate interactions. Northward motion relative to the Eurasian Plate, via the Cyprian Arc, contributes to compressional stress in northern Sinai, while in southern and central Sinai, GPE-related deviatoric stress contributes significantly. This northward traction alleviates compressive stresses in the Gulf of Aqaba-Dead Sea and Gulf of Suez, reflecting intricate tectonic interactions involving local geology and broader regional processes. Sinai’s position at the convergence of three major plates results in distinctive geodynamics: convergence along the Cyprian Arc drives compressional deformation, while divergence along the African-Arabian boundary and clockwise subplate rotation produce extensional stresses48 that promote fault development and reactivation, influencing seismicity patterns.

Magnitude-frequency distributions support these findings: higher seismicity and lower b-values in Aqaba indicate concentrated stress, while higher b-values in Suez and Sinai suggest more distributed or partially aseismic deformation. Focal mechanisms show normal faulting in the Gulf of Suez and strike-slip faulting in Aqaba. In central and southern Sinai, modeled extensional stresses align with earthquake focal mechanisms, highlighting GPE as a key driver of crustal extension. Conversely, in the Gulf of Suez and Gulf of Aqaba, tectonic forces associated with Red Sea rifting and plate motions dominate over GPE, explaining extensional and strike-slip faulting despite compressive GPE-derived stresses.

The mechanical significance of the computed GPE-related stresses can be also assessed by comparison with regional lithospheric strength estimates derived from yield-strength envelope (YSE) models. Our GPE-derived deviatoric stresses, generally on the order of ~ 3–20 MPa across Sinai and locally reaching ~ 25–30 MPa near major gradients, fall within the lower to intermediate range of brittle crustal strength (∼10–50 MPa), as inferred from published YSEs49–51. These stress levels contribute significantly to the observed extensional stress regime and are sufficient to promote fault reactivation or in mechanically weaker parts of southern and central Sinai. In contrast, in the Gulf of Aqaba and Gulf of Suez, even GPE-derived stress is high, reaching ~ 30 MPa, the plate-boundary and rift-related forces dominate and GPE-derived stresses represent only a secondary contribution and are partly mitigated.

Conclusion

Numerical models incorporating topography and Moho depth in Sinai subplate demonstrate that ΔGPE generates horizontal forces per unit length (ΔFx) of approximately 2 × 1012 N/m, producing crustal deviatoric stresses (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau }_{xx}^{c}$$\end{document}) ranging from –20 MPa in the low-lying Gulf regions to 20 MPa in the elevated terrains of Sinai.

GPE-induced stress is a component of the actual observed stress field and it does not account for far-field tectonic influences. Considering the earthquake focal mechanism, we deduce that GPE-derived stress prevails in southern and central Sinai showing extensional stress. The estimated GPE-stress magnitudes fall within the brittle strength range of the crust and are therefore mechanically capable of contributing to fault reactivation in mechanically weaker parts of southern and central Sinai. However, the high magnitude GPE-compressional derived stresses observed in Gulf of Sinai and Gulf of Aqaba, were mitigated by the tectonic forces induced by the northward movement of the Sinai subplate resulting in an extensional to shear regime, though deformation rates remain modest. These findings suggest that in regions of significant plate motion, tectonic stresses dominate over GPE-derived stresses, whereas in more stable zones, GPE may play an important role.

While GPE-derived stress primarily explains the extensional regime interior Sinai, tectonic traction dictates deformation along major boundaries such as the Gulf of Aqaba and Suez Rift. Integrating GPE-derived stresses with earthquake focal mechanisms, seismicity, and previous geodetic studies provides a comprehensive understanding of stress distribution and crustal deformation across the region. This multi-parameter approach underscores the complexity of tectonic interactions in Sinai and provides vital input for seismic hazard assessments and geodynamic models.

Numerical models of topography and Moho depth effectively elucidate the influence of GPE on crustal stresses. By integrating GPE-based stress calculations with focal mechanism analysis, seismicity statistics, and geodetic data, this approach deepens our understanding of the prevailing stress regimes and tectonic deformation patterns. This comprehensive methodology advances knowledge of the region’s geodynamics, crucial for seismic risk mitigation and crustal deformation modeling.
