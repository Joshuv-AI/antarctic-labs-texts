Longitudinal Improvements in Health‐Related Quality of Life Among Children Enrolled in the CANadian Paediatric Weight Management Registry (CANPWR) Over 3 Years
Authors: Marina Ybarra, Grace Rebekah, Geoff D C Ball, Annick Buchholz, Jill Hamilton, Josephine Ho, Ian Zenlea, Rajibul Mian, Lehana Thabane, Katherine M Morrison, the CANPWR Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealth‐related quality of life (HRQoL) can be impaired in children with obesity. Utilising data from the CANadian Paediatric Weight Management Registry (CANPWR), we examined changes in HRQoL up to 3‐year after enrollment in paediatric weight management programs (PWMP) and demographic, socioeconomic, behavioural and health‐related factors associated with it.MethodsData were collected from 2013 to 2020. Child (≥ 8 years) and parent‐reported HRQoL data were assessed via questionnaire (PedsQL4.0) at baseline, 6‐month, 1‐, 2‐ and 3‐year post‐baseline in PWMP. Over time, HRQoL and associations with demographic, anthropometric, behavioural and health services data were examined using linear effects and mixed effects models.FindingsA total of 1145 participants were included at baseline (47.8% male; age 12.4 ± 3.3 years; BMI z‐score: 3.5 ± 1.2; 68.8% White), 793 (69%) at 1‐ and 615 (54%) at 3‐year post‐baseline. Total HRQoL improved from baseline to 6‐month and was higher at 3‐year (child‐total score: 69.9 ± 15.2 vs. 74.6 ± 14.5, p < 0.001; parent‐total score: 64.6 ± 17.4 vs. 68.6 ± 17.0; p < 0.001). Total child‐reported HRQoL was higher in boys (ß: 2.71, CI: 1.2–4.2; p < 0.01), children who identified as non‐White (ß: 2.63, CI: 0.8–4.5; p < 0.01), and those with higher household incomes (ß: 3.65, CI: 1.6–5.7; p < 0.01), independent of BMI z‐score. Higher total child‐reported HRQoL scores were positively related to higher hours of clinic attendance (ß: 0.09 CI: 0.06–0.12; p < 0.01). In contrast, sleep disturbance, lower physical activity, and higher screen time were associated with lower total child‐reported HRQoL independent of BMI z‐score.ConclusionsBoth child‐ and parent‐reported HRQoL scores improved over 3 years, and these changes were associated positively with clinic attendance and negatively with unhealthy behaviours.Keywords: CANPWR, children, health‐related quality of life, longitudinal study, obesity intervention, paediatric obesity, weight management

Introduction

Childhood obesity is a complex disorder with multifactorial origins including genetic, biological, behavioural, and environmental factors [1]. Associated health issues include impairments in physical health, mental health, and well‐being [2]. In Canada, the prevalence of obesity among children and youth aged 5 to 17 years was 10.1% in 2019 [3]. Paediatric weight management programs (PWMP) that are family‐based and focused on changing health behaviours are considered the foundation of management and are moderately efficacious [2].

Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) can be impaired in children with obesity [4], in part related to experiences with weight‐related stigmatisation, victimisation, teasing, and bullying, leading to the development of heightened social isolation [4]. The internalisation of weight bias has been linked to detrimental effects on mental health, including negative impacts on body image and social anxiety [5]. Low HRQoL may influence other health outcomes, and practitioners are encouraged to counsel children with adverse weight‐related experiences [6]. Enhancing HRQoL is an important target in the comprehensive management of obesity in this age group [7] and has been highlighted by caregivers as a top priority [8].

Several studies have identified improvements in HRQoL in children and adolescents enrolled in PWMP, with programs varied in duration of follow‐up between 16 weeks and 2 years [9, 10, 11, 12]. These improvements can remain after families have left PWMP [13], and have been linked to reductions in body mass index (BMI) z‐score in some paediatric studies but others reported changes independent of BMI z‐score change [11, 12, 14]. Gender [15], race [16], age [15, 16], and socioeconomic status [16] have been linked to HRQoL in children with obesity, though the extent to which these factors influence changes in HRQoL among children in PWMP remains uncertain. Leveraging data from the CANadian Paediatric Weight Management Registry (CANPWR), this study aimed to examine (1) changes in child‐ and parent‐reported HRQoL up to 3 years after enrollment in PWMP and (2) demographic, socioeconomic, behavioural, and health‐related factors associated with the change in HRQoL over time.

Methods

Study Population

CANPWR is a multi‐site, prospective cohort study [17] that enrolled children (2–17 years old) with overweight or obesity at the time they entered one of nine interdisciplinary PWMP across Canada between May 2013 and October 2017. Participating clinics were McMaster Children's Hospital (Hamilton, Ontario); BC Children's Hospital (Vancouver, British Columbia); Stollery Children's Hospital (Edmonton, Alberta); Alberta Children's Hospital (Calgary, Alberta); The Hospital for Sick Children (Toronto, Ontario); Trillium Health Partners (Mississauga, Ontario); Children's Hospital of Eastern Ontario (Ottawa, Ontario); Montreal Children's Hospital and CHU Sainte‐Justine (Montreal, Quebec). Although all clinics offered family‐based behavioural weight management services, they varied in program entry criteria, clinic size (50–220 new patients/year), and length of intervention (from 10 weeks to 3 years).

The study protocol [17] and baseline and 1‐year results have been previously reported [18]. Briefly, this is a longitudinal study with planned research visits at baseline, 6 months, 1, 2 and 3 years post‐baseline, independent of clinic visits. The intent was to continue follow‐up even if participants withdrew from clinical care. Data collection was standardised with an administered questionnaire, anthropometric measurements at each visit and a standardised chart review was also undertaken. For the present analysis, children were eligible if they had a baseline child‐reported or parent‐reported HRQoL. All study participants provided assent, and their parent or legal guardian provided informed, written consent before data collection. The CANPWR study was approved at the coordinating centre, Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (#10‐215) and each of the nine participating sites obtained approval from their local Research Ethics Boards. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Health Related Quality of Life (HRQoL)

HRQoL was assessed using the PedsQL 4.0, a validated questionnaire comprised of 23 items to evaluate physical, emotional, social, and school functioning and the total (maximum 100) and domain scores were analysed [19]. To examine whether longitudinal changes in the PedsQL are clinically meaningful, minimal important difference (MID) estimates were established for this questionnaire (Total Score: child‐reported > 4.36; parent‐reported > 4.50). In addition, domain‐specific MID estimates have been identified: Physical Functioning (child‐reported: > 6.66; parent‐reported: > 6.92), Emotional Functioning (child‐reported: > 7.00; parent‐reported: > 5.62), Social Functioning (child‐reported: > 4.51; parent‐reported: > 5.30), and School Functioning (child‐reported: > 3.93; parent‐reported: > 4.73) [20].

Parents were asked to complete age‐specific PedsQL questionnaires for their children (5–7, 8–12 and 13–18 years) at the baseline, 6‐month, 1‐, 2‐ and 3‐year post‐baseline assessments and children and adolescents 8 years of age and older completed the age‐specific child‐reported HRQoL questionnaire (either 8–12 or 13–18 years). Following the methodology established by the PedsQL developers [21], each item was scored 0–4, and these items are reverse‐scored and linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale (e.g.,: 0 = 100, 1 = 75, 2 = 50, 3 = 25, and 4 = 0), with a higher score indicating a better HRQoL [21]. A total score was calculated as the sum of the 23 items divided by the number of items answered, and the psychosocial score was calculated in a similar manner, including the 15 items in the emotional, social, and school scores [19]. At least 50% of the items had to be completed to calculate the total score [19]. Child‐ and parent‐reported scores were analysed and reported independently.

Individual and Family Characteristics

Individual and family characteristics, including anthropometric indicators for each participant, were collected on a questionnaire, directly assessed or extracted from the participant's medical record in a standardised manner [17]. Questionnaires were completed by parents/caregivers and children together if children were < 12 years of age or by parents/caregivers and children independently if children were ≥ 12 years of age [22].

Demographic characteristics included age (mean, SD), biological sex (percentage male), and ethnicity (self identified as White, Black, Latin American, Other or Mixed ethnicities). Socioeconomic indicators included parental education (no high school diploma, high school diploma, college/university/postgraduate diploma), household income (< $50 000, $50 000–$100 000, > $100 000) and living arrangements (number of people living in the child's primary residence). Each participant's visit included a measure of weight to the nearest 0.1 kg via an electronic digital scale, height to the nearest 0.1 cm via a calibrated stadiometer. Sex‐ and age‐specific BMI z‐score was calculated for each child using the WHO child growth criteria [23]. Data about modifiable health behaviours (sleep, physical activity and screen time) and family eating behaviours (frequency of eating out, having meals as a family, having meals in front of the television, and frequency of having breakfast per week) were adopted from the Canadian Health Measures Survey questionnaire [24]. Sleep behaviours included trouble falling asleep, daytime sleepiness (frequency categories), and average nightly sleep hours. Child‐reported physical activity measures included number of days/week with > 60 min of physical activity, weekly hours of organised and unorganised physical activity, and daily screen time. Parents were also asked to report on additional health measures, including family history of obesity, diabetes, heart disease and/or mental health disorder [24]. The child's experiences with bullying and mental health history (with or without prescribed medication) were also reported.

Clinic participation status was recorded (active, inactive, discharged upon program completion, or discharged due to loss to follow‐up) at each visit and hours of clinic attendance and health care professional were extracted from the clinic records in a standardised manner. Clinic site was included in the models as participating PWMP sites differed in program structure and entrance criteria [25]. However, given the considerable variation of the patient population and their level of engagement even within a site, these data were also collected in a standardised manner and analysed at the individual level rather than being assigned based on site.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported using mean ± SD for continuous variables. Median (IQR) categorical variables were represented using N (%). A linear mixed‐effects model was used to model the variability within and across participants and visits. Associations between demographic, anthropometric, lifestyle behaviour and health services characteristics with HRQoL were assessed at all time points.

To estimate the correlation between each of the HRQoL measurement total scores and potential covariates, we used a mixed effect model. Since each child's value is correlated at each time point, the effect of within‐subject and within‐group variability was addressed using a mixed effects model, assuming an unstructured covariance structure. A pooled unstandardized beta coefficient represents the comparison with the baseline measure over time. The covariance structure of the error term was modelled using an unstructured correlation matrix. In addition, the method to compare the score over time for HRQoL for each visit was based on choosing the significant parameters in unadjusted models using a stepwise method, and a final adjusted model was derived from the significant parameters.

Using the MID estimates for the PedsQL [20], we calculated the proportions that improved their HRQoL by the 3‐year post‐baseline visit. These calculations were performed for each different PedsQL domain and for the total score.

Role of the Funding Source

The funders had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of this report. The decision to submit for publication was made by the authors only. G.R. and R.M. had full access to the raw data.

Results

The child‐reported total HRQoL score improved at 6 months (p < 0.001) and remained higher at the 3‐year follow‐up visit (69.9 ± 15.2 to 74.6 ± 14.5; p < 0.001) (Table 3). The mean increase in HRQoL total score for child‐reported PedsQL was 4.7, above the MID of 4.5, and close to one‐half of participants (48.4%) experienced an increase in total HRQoL that exceeded the MID over the 3‐year. The domain‐specific scores increased similarly, although the child‐reported physical and psychological domain sub‐scores did not meet their respective MID of 6.66 and 5.3, respectively. Compared to those without a clinically significant increase in MID, children with an increase above the MID were similar in age, sex, ethnicity, and SES, but did have lower BMI z‐scores and higher total HRQoL scores at baseline.

The parent‐reported HRQoL total scores also improved by 6 months and remained higher up to 3 years (from 64.6 ± 17.4 to 68.6 ± 17.0; p < 0.001) as illustrated in Table 3. The mean increase in the total parent‐reported score was 4.0, below the MID of 4.5, although 51.9% of parent‐reported scores increased by greater than the MID over the 3 years. The parent‐reported scores for specific domains also improved from baseline to 3 years (Table 3). Although there was an increase in the parent‐reported physical and psychological domain scores, this increase did not meet their respective MIDs of 6.92 and 5.49. Furthermore, the parent‐reported score was lower than the child‐report scores from baseline to 3 years.

Given the loss to follow‐up, we compared the baseline characteristics of those with and without HRQoL at 1 year (Tables S1 and S2). Child without follow‐up HRQoL at 1‐year (Table S1) were slightly more likely to report ‘Other’ as ethnicity, less likely to report a positive parental mental health history and had a slightly higher the BMI z‐score at baseline (3.4 vs. 3.3). Participants without parent‐reported HRQoL at 1 year (Table S2), were more likely to be aged 2–7‐years and to report ‘Other’ as ethnicity. They were slightly less likely to report a parental mental health history, while rates of child mental health problems with medication use were similar between groups (34.1 vs. 33.6%).

Factors Related to Child‐Reported HRQoL

The final multivariable model of covariates related to child‐reported total HRQoL score over time included demographic, health behaviour, anthropometric and clinic characteristics (Table 4). Child HRQoL score was higher in boys, participants who identified their ethnicity as ‘Other’, children who lived in homes with more people and with higher household income. Independent of these demographic factors and BMI z score, higher scores were reported in those with a higher frequency of meeting the recommended 60 min of daily physical activity and with greater total hours of clinic attendance. Lower total HRQoL scores over time were reported in those who reported sleep disturbance (trouble falling asleep and/or daytime sleepiness) and had higher screen time. Similar factors predicted parent‐reported scores (Table 5), although no clinic‐related variables were related to parent‐reported HRQoL scores over time.

Discussion

In this large, multisite prospective cohort study, we observed significant improvements in HRQoL over 3 years in children with obesity enrolled in a PWMP. Both child‐ and parent‐reported HRQoL increased over time, and nearly one‐half of participants reported increases that exceeded the clinical MID. Importantly, these improvements were associated not only with reductions in BMI z‐score but with modifiable lifestyle factors, including physical activity, screen time, sleep, and time spent in clinical care. These factors were related to improvements in HRQoL independent of demographic factors or changes in BMI z‐score, indicating that the observed benefits were not solely dependent on changes in weight. Our findings reinforce the multidimensional benefits of PWMP and highlight the importance of capturing patient‐reported outcomes such as HRQoL to evaluate intervention impact.

Previous research has demonstrated improvements in HRQoL within PWMP, typically lasting up to 1 or 2 years in duration [9, 10, 11, 12]. In this study, HRQoL increased in the first 6 months and was sustained over 3 years, although remaining below average values reported in children without obesity. A recent meta‐analysis of typically developing children and adolescents worldwide reported pooled PedsQL total scores of approximately 81 (95% CI 79–83), supporting that HRQoL in our cohort remained below normative levels [26]. Nearly one‐half of participants reported an improvement in HRQoL that exceeded the MID, indicating that these changes were both statistically significant and clinically meaningful. Few previous studies have reported whether HRQoL improvements in PWMP exceed the MID threshold, limiting the ability to interpret the clinical relevance of those findings. This study adds to the field by demonstrating that improvements were not only significant on average but also meaningful at the individual level for a substantial proportion of participants. The significance of these findings is further illustrated by the importance that parents of children with obesity place on improved HRQoL as a central indicator of treatment benefit [27].

The PedsQL measures physical, emotional, social, and school functioning. In our study, improvements were observed across all domains, with the greatest gains noted in physical and emotional functioning, particularly among child‐reports. These findings align with the reported benefits of PWMP in improving energy, mobility, and mood [8]. Parent‐report also reflected improvements, though to a slightly lesser extent, with smaller changes in the social and school domains. This may reflect the differing perspectives of children and parents on internal experiences such as emotional well‐being and peer interactions.

We observed that a decrease in BMI z‐score was significantly associated with improved HRQoL. This finding varies from previous data in adults that suggested weak associations between weight loss and HRQoL improvement [28], and several paediatric studies that have demonstrated more variable or modest relationships [29]. However, our data also support a growing body of evidence suggesting that BMI reduction is not the sole determinant of HRQoL gains [30]. Independent of BMI changes, children who engaged in regular physical activity, reported fewer sleep disturbances, had less screen time, and spent more time in clinic showed greater HRQoL improvements. These findings emphasise the importance of a holistic approach in PWMP that prioritises not only weight management but also healthy behaviours, mental health, and ongoing engagement with multidisciplinary teams [8, 14, 24].

We found that child‐reported HRQoL was higher in boys, which is consistent with the literature and most mental health measures, where boys score higher than girls (i.e., they report less psychopathology) [21]. HRQoL was also higher in those identifying as non‐White or from higher‐income households. These associations have been noted in other studies and may reflect differential experiences of stigma, perception or social support [15, 16]. However, it is important to recognise that racial and ethnic identities are social constructs influenced by broader social determinants and systemic inequities, and interpretations should be made with caution. Further research is needed to better understand how intersectional identities relate to HRQoL and treatment experiences.

Another key finding was the positive association between time in clinic and child‐reported HRQoL. Children who spent more hours with clinical teams reported greater improvements, suggesting a potential ‘dose effect’ of treatment exposure [31]. While parent‐report did not show this same relationship, this may reflect differing perceptions between children and parents/guardians, which has been previously reported in paediatric HRQoL research [32]. Our findings support the inclusion of sustained, team‐based care in PWMP models and raise questions about how best to tailor program intensity and duration to optimise patient outcomes.

This study is not without limitations. The observational design precludes causal inference, and the lack of a control group means we cannot definitively attribute improvements in HRQoL to the PWMP. Additionally, regression to the mean may partially explain the observed changes over time. Attrition over the 3‐year follow‐up period represents an important limitation and may have influenced our findings. Participants without child‐reported HRQoL at 1 year did differ somewhat as they had higher child‐reported ethnicity in the ‘Other’ category, lower reported mental health issues in the parents and a higher mean BMI z‐score of 3.4 compared to 3.3. We cannot rule out that these differences may have influenced our findings, although the higher mental health history in those who did report HRQoL might have been expected to reduce the HRQoL, not increase it as we observed over time. Nonetheless, these differences may limit generalizability and suggest that participants who remain engaged in follow‐up may differ systematically from those who do not. Although we included a broad range of behavioural and clinical factors to account for potential contributors to HRQoL change, unmeasured influences may have affected trajectories over time.

Despite these limitations, our study has several notable strengths. These include its large, prospective cohort, clinics across the country and the use of both child‐ and parent‐reported validated measures (PedsQL). To our knowledge, this is the longest and most comprehensive evaluation of HRQoL in children enrolled in PWMP in Canada and among the largest globally. In contrast to many previous studies and systematic reviews, our study incorporated a wider range of modifiable behavioural and clinic‐related factors. This allowed us to explore associations that are often missed due to variability in data collection methods across studies. Systematic reviews of HRQoL in paediatric obesity have noted this limitation, as heterogeneity in outcome measures and study designs often restricts the number of variables that can be meaningfully analysed [30]. By addressing these gaps, our findings contribute new insights into the complex, multidimensional drivers of quality of life change in paediatric obesity treatment.

In conclusion, children enrolled in the CANPWR study experienced statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in HRQoL over a three‐year period. These improvements were related to a range of modifiable behaviours and engagement with clinical services, independent of change in BMI z‐score. These findings underscore the need for continued emphasis on holistic, long‐term care models that prioritise HRQoL and other patient‐reported outcomes. Future research should explore how to optimise the dose and delivery of interventions to enhance HRQoL, particularly among populations at risk of lower engagement or poorer outcomes.
