Temporopolar blurring signifies abnormalities of white matter in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy
Authors: Yuming Li, Peiwen Liu, Qiuxing Lin, Wei Li, Yingying Zhang, Jinmei Li, Xiuli Li, Qiyong Gong, Heng Zhang, Luying Li, Xiutian Sima, Danyang Cao, Xiang Huang, Kailing Huang, Dong Zhou, Dongmei An
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe single‐center retrospective cohort study investigated underlying pathogenic mechanisms and clinical significance of patients with temporal lobe epilepsy and hippocampal sclerosis (TLE‐HS), in the presence/absence of gray–white matter abnormalities (usually called “blurring”; GMB) in ipsilateral temporopolar region (TPR) on MRI.MethodsThe study involved 105 patients with unilateral TLE‐HS (60 GMB+ and 45 GMB−) who underwent standard anterior temporal lobectomy, along with 61 healthy controls. Resected specimens were examined under light microscope. With combined T1‐weighted and DTI data, we quantitatively compared large‐scale morphometric features and exacted diffusion parameters of ipsilateral TPR‐related superficial and deep white matter (WM) by atlas‐based segmentation. Along‐tract analysis was added to detect heterogeneous microstructural alterations at various points along deep WM tracts, which were categorized into inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), uncinate fasciculus (UF), and temporal cingulum.ResultsComparable seizure semiology and postoperative seizure outcome were found, while the GMB+ group had significantly higher rate of HS Type 1 and history of febrile seizures, contrasting with significantly lower proportion of interictal contralateral epileptiform discharges, HS Type 2, and increased wasteosomes in hippocampal specimens. Similar morphometric features but greater WM atrophy with more diffusion abnormalities of superficial WM was observed adjacent to ipsilateral TPR in the GMB+ group. Moreover, microstructural alterations resulting from temporopolar GMB were more localized in temporal cingulum while evenly and widely distributed along ILF and UF.InterpretationTemporopolar GMB could signify more severe and widespread microstructural damage of white matter rather than a focal cortical lesion in TLE‐HS, affecting selection of surgical procedures.

Introduction

Epilepsy surgery remains an optimal treatment option for adult patients with drug‐resistant temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), 1 among which hippocampal sclerosis (HS) is the most prevalent histopathological diagnosis. 2 Although HS has long been proposed as a leading site in the genesis of seizures of TLE, various additional abnormalities of connected structures are always being detected beyond the hippocampus, 3 which may affect selection of surgical procedures. Abnormalities in ipsilateral temporopolar region (TPR), generally called “gray–white matter blurring” (GMB), 4 , 5 is a common imaging sign along with HS. The boundary of TPR is slightly larger than the Brodmann's area 38 and considered as the 2–3 cm of cerebrum that extended from the limen insulae to the anterior tip of the temporal lobe. 6 The temporopolar GMB varied from 28% up to 66% in retrospective series, 5 characterized by relatively normal cortical thickness with well‐demarcated gray–white matter junction in T1‐weighted images, and increased T2 signal intensity and/or poor delineation of the temporopolar gray–white matter interface on T2‐fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images. 4 , 5

Previous studies indicated that the temporopolar GMB could contribute to lateralizing the epileptogenic zone 7 , 8 but with controversial impact on postoperative seizure outcomes. 4 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Moreover, the temporopolar GMB has been attributed to a variety of underlying etiologies, 4 , 5 , 11 , 12 including the focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), delayed myelination, arrest of white matter (WM) development, and partial volume effect. Two recent studies highlighted the association between temporopolar GMB and degeneration of WM with advanced MRI sequences including high‐field 7T MRI, 4 T1 mapping, and a specific diffusion microstructure imaging (DMI) sequence. 12 They further revealed variations in axonal density reductions and degree of gliosis in resected temporal lobes using electron micrographs, but both were limited by small sample size. 4 , 12 Hence, there is still no consensus regarding the genesis, pathological mechanism, or the clinical characteristics of the temporopolar GMB in patients with TLE‐HS.

Advanced morphometry analysis and quantitative tractography provide powerful techniques to precisely investigate the abnormalities at a large‐scale network level in vivo and serves as a unique supplement to microscopic histopathological findings of focal lesion. The superficial white matter (SWM) is regarded as a key candidate region to investigate the temporopolar GMB because it refers to the WM located directly beneath the cortex which contains short‐range association fibers arching through cortical sulci to connect adjacent gyri and terminations of deep white matter (DWM) bundles. 13 , 14 Moreover, along‐tract analysis enables us to accurately quantify the abnormalities along local trajectories of fiber bundles and determine whether temporopolar GMB tends to be a focal lesion or the sign of constant alterations of the whole DWM tract. 15 , 16 , 17

In this retrospective study, we aimed to investigate electroclinical characteristics and postoperative seizure freedom of patients with TLE‐HS plus temporopolar GMB, evaluate the pathological substrate of surgical specimens with light microscopy, and characterize microstructural properties within TPR, adjacent temporal cortex, and related WM bundles with morphometry statistics and probabilistic atlas‐derived analysis of diffusion MRI tractometry.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

From the single‐center database for the investigation of medically intractable TLE between 2014 and 2023, we retrospectively selected a consecutive cohort of 105 patients with unilateral TLE 18 who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) evaluated with prolonged video‐EEG monitoring (≥24 h), clinical imaging, additional research‐dedicated high‐resolution 3T MRI scan, and the (18)F‐FDG PET/CT or PET/MRI if available; (2) underwent standard anterior temporal lobectomy at the Department of neurosurgery in West China Hospital; (3) diagnosed with HS pathologically and resected specimen of temporal neocortex suitable for histological analysis; (4) no preoperative radiological evidence of bilateral HS or other principal lesions including tumors, vascular malformations, cysts, scars, or FCD. Patients were excluded if they had etiologies other than hippocampal sclerosis, such as stroke and traumatic brain injury. Detailed demographic and clinical data were obtained by medical records, office visits, and telephone contact, including seizures semiology, 19 epilepsy risk factors, and results of video‐EEG. Postsurgical seizure outcome was classified according to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification system, 20 with a minimum follow‐up of 12 months. The preoperative MRI images were visually and independently reviewed by two experienced observers (P. W. Liu and Y. Y. Zhang) blinded to the neuropathological findings. When disagreement arose, images were reviewed and discussed with the third investigator (D. M. An) until consensus was reached. The HS was radiologically diagnosed with qualitative detection of atrophy, loss of internal structure, and decreased T1 and increased T2 signal intensity in the hippocampus proper. 21 The criteria for temporopolar GMB were loss of gray–white matter demarcation in the FLAIR sequence but not in T1‐weighted images, and/or increased signal intensity of TPR in T2‐weighted images, both present on at least two contiguous MRI slices as illustrated previously. 5 , 11

The control group consisted of 61 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy individuals. The ethics committee of the West China Hospital, Sichuan University, approved all the data in this study for retrospective analysis and waived the demand for informed consent.

Histopathology

Microscopic examination was carried out on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded surgical specimen at the Department of Pathology in West China Hospital. All sections were serially cut into 3–5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). We qualitatively evaluated the immunohistochemistry staining of temporal neocortex for mature neuronal markers (NeuN, MAXIM), neurofilament (SMI‐32, Biolegend, 1:400), and astrogliosis (GFAP, ZSGB‐BIO, ZA‐0529, 1:200). The FCD and HS were diagnosed based on the ILAE system. 22 , 23 The hippocampal sections were semiquantitatively analyzed, and HS was classified into three subtypes by cell loss patterns of Cornu Ammonis (CA): Type 1 (classic or complete patterns), Type 2 (CA1 predominant sclerosis), and Type 3 (CA4 predominant sclerosis), along with the fourth group named “no‐HS, gliosis only”. 23

Preoperative MRI data acquisition

Images were acquired on a 3.0T MRI system (Tim Trio; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with an eight‐channel head coil, including a 3D T1‐weighted spoiled gradient‐recalled sequence (repetition time (TR) = 1900 ms; echo time (TE) = 2.26 ms; field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2; voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3), a coronal T2‐weighted turbo spin‐echo sequence perpendicular to the axis of the hippocampal formation (TR: 6100 ms; TE: 97 ms; FOV: 230 × 230 mm2; voxel size = 0.6 × 0.6 × 3.9 mm3), a spin‐echo 3D‐FLAIR images (TR: 4000 ms; TE: 393 ms; FOV: 250 × 250 mm2; voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3), and the single‐shell diffusion tensor imaging (DTI; spin‐echo planar sequence; TR: 6800 ms; TE: 91 ms; FOV: 240 × 240 mm2; slice thickness = 3 mm, no gap). The diffusion sensitizing gradients were applied along 64 noncollinear directions (b = 1000 s/mm2) as well as a reference image with no diffusion weighting (b0 image).

Morphometric analysis

T1‐MPRAGE images were processed automatically using voxel‐based morphometry (VBM), surface‐based morphometry (SBM), and region‐of‐interest (ROI) analysis based on the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12) toolbox of Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12) running in MATLAB (R2016a). The weighted average image quality rating (IQR) was inspected to comprehensively assess the noise, resolution, and inhomogeneity. We conducted the ROI analysis based on the SPM Neuromorphometrics atlas to match the known cytoarchitectonic posterior limit and anatomic landmark of TPR, which was defined by the first coronal section intersecting the uncinate bundle. 8 , 11 For VBM, images were segmented into gray matter (GM), WM, and cerebrospinal fluid after correction of bias‐field inhomogeneities and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm (FWHM). For SBM, we used an automated projection‐based thickness estimation 24 and extracted additional surface parameters including the gyrification index, 25 sulcus depth, and cortical complexity. 26

DTI preprocessing

TPR‐related structural connectivity was processed using MRtrix3.0 (https://www.mrtrix.org), 27 based on Anatomically Constrained Tractography algorithm. 28 We calculated tensor‐based fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) maps from the b = 1000 s/mm2 shell of the preprocessed DTI. Whole‐brain probabilistic tractography was calculated using the second order Integration over fiber orientation distributions (iFOD2), 29 which were estimated by an advanced diffusion MRI modeling approach known as Single‐Shell 3‐Tissue Constrained Spherical Deconvolution (SS3T‐CSD) 30 with default parameters. Thirty million of fibers were generated in native diffusion space with following parameters: step size = 0.5 × voxel_size, max length = 250 mm, maximum angle = 45°, FOD amplitude threshold = 0.06. Subsequently, tractography datasets were filtered using spherical‐deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms (SIFT2) to improve the accuracy of reconstructed fibers and reduce false positive connections. 31

WM segmentation

The SWM was extracted similar to previous studies, 32 with a deep white matter (DWM) bundle atlas 33 (Table S1) and the contralateral white matter as the exclusion mask. We derived the mask of ipsilateral TPR and other ROIs in temporal lobe (amygdala, hippocampus, entorhinal area (Ent), superior/middle/inferior/transverse temporal gyrus (STG/MTG/ITG/TTG), fusiform/ parahippocampal gyrus (FuG/PHG), and planum temporale (PT)) from the SPM Neuromorphometrics atlas. Both SWM and DWM masks defined in the standard MNI space were nonlinearly registered to the individual diffusion space. Short fibers originating from TPR were filtered to reduce the noise, with length between 20 and 80 mm. 32 According to the previous research, 34 the DWM bundles from TPR were categorized into three major tracts with the masks from a brain tractography atlas, 33 composed of the uncinate fasciculus (UF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), and temporal cingulum fibers (TCF).

Average and along‐tract microstructural white matter analyzes

The mean diffusion parameters (FA, MD, AD, and RD) were calculated within streamlines of SWM between TPR and adjacent cortex regions of temporal lobe, as well as DWM including the UF, ILF, and TCF. Additionally, we performed along‐tract analyzes using an automated approach as described previously. 17 , 35 Diffusion parameters were resampled within vertices every 3 mm to obtain cross‐sectional scalar mean and variance estimates along the length of DWM bundles using MRtrix3 tckresample and tcksample tools. We also resampled each streamline every 2 mm and every 5 mm considering the influence of sampling length on diffusion scalar values.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) program (version 27.0) and RStudio (R version 4.3.2). We analyzed the electroclinical and histopathological features between patients with and without temporopolar GMB using Kruskal–Wallis or Pearson chi‐squared tests and investigated the rate of postoperative seizure freedom with Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Independent two‐sample t‐test was used to compare the differences of GM volume, WM volume, cortical thickness, gyrification index, sulcus depth, and cortical complexity with age and gender as covariates. For VBM analysis, we added total intracranial volume (TIV) as a nuisance variable. A Bonferroni–Holm correction of P < 0.05 was applied to correct for multiple comparisons based on SPM12.

For DTI data, we compared the average diffusion parameters using permutation test (5000 times) and removed age and gender effects using linear regression with the lmperm package. A linear mixed‐effects model is applied assess along‐tract microstructure properties using permlme function in butils package and methods proposed by Colby et al (https://github.com/johncolby/along‐tract‐stats/wiki/between‐group). 17 The fixed effects consisted of an overall intercept, age, gender, group, position, and a “group:position” interaction, with a subject level random effect term to explain repeated measures within subjects. The results of the group term were analogous to traditional tract‐averaged mean estimates and showed the overall offset between groups. Therefore, we mainly focused on the F‐test across the group‐by‐position interaction term, which determined whether the effect of group on cross‐section diffusion parameters varied with position. 17 , 35 For a single DWM bundle, the permutation method (5000 times) was used to create a new empiric null distribution of the maximum test statistic across all tests. The familywise‐error‐rate‐corrected (FWER) p‐values were produced based on the maximum observed T‐statistics. 36 Finally, the false discovery rate (FDR) was applied to correct multiple comparisons in the permutation tests and set corrected P < 0.05 as significant resembling previous studies. 37 We implemented the Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc test for pairwise comparisons and reported effect sizes of the WM ROIs with significant alterations using Cohen's d or omega squared (ω 2).

Results

Preoperative demographic and electroclinical data

The main characteristics of the cohort were shown in Table 1, including 60 TLE‐HS with temporopolar GMB (GMB+), 45 without GMB (GMB−), and 61 demographically matched healthy controls (HC). The mean interval between preoperative 3T scans and surgery was 0.5 years (SD = 1.0), and temporopolar GMB was only found ipsilateral to HS. The proportion of febrile seizures (P < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.16–0.55]) and HS Type 1 (P = 0.023, 95% CI = [0–0.53]) in the GMB+ group was significantly higher than those in GMB−. In contrast, the GMB+ group had the significantly lower rate of contralateral interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) (P = 0.012, 95% CI = [−0.45 to −0.04]), HS Type 2 (P = 0.050, 95% CI = [−0.45 to −0.04]), and increased amount of wasteosomes in hippocampus under light microscope (P = 0.047, 95% CI = [−0.19 to −0.07]). Moreover, the mean age at epilepsy onset (P = 0.054, GMB+: 13.4 ± 8.1 years vs. GMB−: 17.4 ± 10.4 years) and the incidence of focal autonomic seizures (P = 0.066, GMB+: 71.7% vs. GMB−: 53.3%) were barely not statistically significant. A small proportion of patients with poorly demarcated cortical–subcortical transition under light microscope between the GM and WM was found in both groups. Gliosis was observed all temporal tissue samples, but no FCD could be detected. No significant difference was found between the GMB+ and GMB− group in terms of mean age at surgery, proportion of observed (18)F‐FDG‐PET interictal hypometabolism of ipsilateral TPR, side of epileptogenic focus, or histopathology of resected temporal neocortex under light microscope. The rate of postoperative seizure freedom at the last follow‐up was higher in the GMB+ group but failed to achieve a customary level of statistical significance (P = 0.26, K‐M curve, Fig. 2), with the rate of lost to follow‐up less than 20%.

Between‐group comparisons of volumetry and surface features

The IQR of images was rated as satisfactory to excellent (grades C to A). We found no significant difference of GM volume, cortical thickness, gyrification index, sulcus depth, or cortical complexity between the GMB+ and GMB− group in whole‐brain VBM or SBM analysis after the Holm–Bonferroni correction of P‐values. The ROI‐based analyses only showed significantly less GM volume in ipsilateral parietal operculum (PO) of the GMB+ group than GMB− (P FWER = 0.032, t = −3.02, Fig. 3A), whereas comparable GM volume was found in ipsilateral TPR or other temporal ROI. However, the GMB+ group had significantly less WM volume than GMB−, the main cluster of which was localized near ipsilateral TPR (P FWER = 0.024, peak‐level, t = −5.35, Fig. 3B). Compared with HC, loss of GM volume in PO was only significant in the GMB+ group (P FWER < 0.001, t = −5.35) and more extensive loss of WM volume was observed in GMB+ group than GMB− in ipsilateral temporal, frontal, and parietal regions (Figures S1 and S2).

Furthermore, the FA of the GMB+ group was significantly lower than GMB− in SWM streamlines connecting TPR with STG (P FWER = 0.002, t(103) = −3.71), TTG (P FWER = 0.013, t(97) = −2.94), and PT (P FWER = 0.012, t(93) = −3.12), while MD (P FWER = 0.045, t(97) = 2.57) and RD (P FWER = 0.037, t(97) = 2.59) were significantly higher between TPR and hippocampus. Along‐tract diffusion abnormalities

Significant group‐by‐position interaction of MD (ω 2 = 0.008, F(20,1993) = 1.85, P FWER = 0.038) and RD (ω 2 = 0.01, F(20,1982) = 1.98, P FWER = 0.018) was widespread along ipsilateral UF fibers from TPR, in contrast to focal regions along the TCF for MD (ω 2 = 0.02, F(20,1719) = 2.71, P FWER < 0.001) and RD (ω 2 = 0.02, F(20,1719) = 3.11, P FWER < 0.001) between the GMB+ and GMB− group (Fig. 5). However, significant difference of focal group‐by‐point interaction was only robust in MD and RD of TCF after changing the sampling length, which was localized to the anterior portion of the tract. Additionally, we observed more widespread and severe significant along‐tract diffusion abnormalities for UF, ILF, and TCF in the GMB+ group than GMB− compared to the HC (Tables S2 and S4, Figures S3 and S4).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study investigated electroclinical, histological, and multimodal imaging characteristics in patients with TLE‐HS and presence/absence of temporopolar GMB. We found similar seizure semiology, postoperative seizure freedom rate, and morphometric features of cortex between two groups. Patients with ipsilateral temporopolar GMB had significantly higher rate of febrile seizures, contralateral IEDs, and ILAE HS Type 1, along with lower proportion of Type 2 and increased wasteosomes in resected hippocampus, providing novel data of pathological features. Combined structural MRI and DTI further revealed focal atrophy of WM adjoining the ipsilateral TPR and more diffusion abnormalities of SWM and related DWM tracts in the GMB+ group. Collectively, our study demonstrates that patients with ipsilateral temporopolar GMB could be a specific subtype of TLE‐HS and the development of GMB is more correlated to concomitant abnormalities of WM volume and innate features.

Clinical correlation and pathological profiles

The study confirms that temporopolar GMB is a common MRI finding ipsilateral to HS in more than half of patients with TLE‐HS. 4 , 7 , 38 Inconsistent with earlier findings, 4 , 8 , 10 , 11 our data raises possible correlation between febrile seizures and GMB corresponding to the higher proportion of ILAE HS Type 1, given that initial precipitating injuries, particularly early febrile seizures, more often associates with Type 1 than other HS subtypes. 23 Additionally, several studies have found patients with temporopolar GMB had significantly younger age of seizure onset. 4 , 8 , 10 , 39 Patients with and without temporopolar GMB in our study were both over 10 years old at seizure onset, with a marginally significant after between‐group comparison (P = 0.054), which is much older than the pediatric cohort, 39 and consistent with findings in adult cohorts. 4 , 8 However, retrospective evaluation of early stage in series of adult patients mainly relies on the vague recollection from their guardian, requiring confirmation with a prospective approach. 4 , 23

Our data support that surface ictal EEG patterns may have no correlation with temporopolar GMB, 4 , 40 though TPR has proved to be a crucial part of the epileptogenic zone of TLE aside from hippocampus in intracranial recordings. 41 , 42 Furthermore, we observed an interesting trend toward significance for more postoperative seizure freedom with less contralateral IEDs in the GMB+ group. It may indicate more localized and lateralized seizure networks since studies have demonstrated the correspondence between scalp IEDs‐related EEG‐fMRI hemodynamic responses and synchronized neuronal activities. 43 Nevertheless, the result was in contrary to prior work, 4 , 8 which showed comparable interictal video‐EEG features between groups. It should be noted that these findings are both based on qualitative analysis of clinical detectable seizures resulting from long‐lasting epileptogenic processes propagating rapidly across the cortex. Noninvasive studies alone are inadequate to determine whether the TPR is implicated in epileptogenesis and associated with secondary imaging abnormalities. 40 , 41 , 42

The MRI features on T2‐weighted and FLAIR sequences of temporopolar GMB are similar to FCD type II a, 22 but relatively well‐demarcated cortical–subcortical transition without cortical thickening on T1‐weighted images help to differentiate it from the FCD. 5 Histopathologically, no FCD but various degree of neuronal degeneration, increased gliosis, and similar inflammatory cellular responses were found in all temporal neocortical specimens of both groups in our data consistent with previous immunohistochemical 44 and postmortem studies, 45 suggesting these histopathological abnormalities cannot fully explain the genesis of GMB. Surprisingly, we observed significantly higher rate of increased wasteosomes, or corpora amylacea, in resected hippocampus of the GMB− group, which is composed of a polyglucosan structure involved in containing and accumulating waste elements. 46 Wasteosomes are taken as a manifestation of chronic glymphatic insufficiency relevant to aging, sleep disorders, vascular disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases, 46 reported in around 60% of hippocampal specimens 47 and the neocortex of TLE‐HS. 48 A previous study illustrated that in hippocampal CA1 subregion, the amount of wasteosomes was the highest and inversely correlated with the neuronal density, 47 which partially corresponds to lower proportion of HS Type 1 (classic or complete patterns) and higher rate of Type 2 (CA1 predominant sclerosis) 23 in the GMB− group of our data.

Temporopolar blurring and white matter degeneration

Histologic techniques are sensitive to subtle changes but limited to regional tissue sampling. Therefore, the morphologic information from large‐scale VBM and SBM analysis is an effective supplement to pathological findings. We observed that neither GM volume nor surface‐based characteristics differed in the ipsilateral TPR or any other region of temporal lobe between two groups, while significant decreased WM volume adjacent to ipsilateral TPR was found in patients with temporopolar GMB. So far, few studies with small sample size have quantitatively examined structural changes of the TPR, providing consistent findings. Coste et al 11 pooled right and left TLE populations in the same analysis with an ipsi‐to‐contra asymmetry index (IC‐AI) and only found patients with GMB had a greater IC‐AI of WM (P < 0.05) in TPR than those without GMB. The other study observed a tendency for lower intraaxonal volume fraction of TPR ipsilateral to HS in the GMB+ group with DMI/DTI metrics, although failing to reach a consistent significance level after correction (P FWER = 0.091; P holm = 0.03). 12 Our results also agree with reduced number and density of axons and more extra‐axonal space in the ultrastructural study 4 , 12 and corroborate the correlation between ipsilateral GMB and white matter atrophy.

The diffusion parameters add more information about abnormal integrity and organization of WM tracts, with reduced FA as a summary measure of WM abnormalities over time, high RD associated with myelin degeneration, AD alterations relevant to axonal damage, and increased MD attributed to more extra‐axonal space. 13 , 37 We utilized a highly reproducible atlas‐based method to extract the SWM, which plays a pivotal role in temporopolar GMB with little research involved. Compared with the GMB− group, as expected, decreased FA with increased RD or MD, in the SWM ipsilateral to HS were observed in the GMB+ group. Our findings differ from the prior study which reported no significant difference in diffusion properties of WM adjacent to TPR but was limited by insufficient participants and great bias. 12 The more severe microstructural alterations in SWM between ipsilateral TPR and hippocampus may be related to higher rate of HS Type 1 in the GMB+ group, which is characterized by the widest range of neuronal loss and gliosis affecting all hippocampal subregions. 23 Furthermore, emerging studies have suggested functional relevance of SWM alterations to pathological network reconfigurations in TLE. 13 , 49 Patients with temporopolar GMB may perform more poorly before surgery in a variety of cognitive domains including immediate and long‐term memory, executive functions, 7 and abstract reasoning. 4 Wider diffusion anomalies of SWM could contribute to more functional disruptions in the GMB+ group, so more evidence, including but not limited to resting‐state functional MRI investigations, are required.

We conducted an along‐tract profile analysis of three TPR‐specific pathways coupled with overall mean values to localize specific abnormalities within the long axis, revealing that effect of temporopolar GMB on FA, MD, or RD was localized in TCF and wide in ipsilateral ILF and UF. TLE‐HS has long been identified as a network disorder with both temporal and extra‐temporal cortical involvement, 50 linked by a complex web of axonal fibers. The ILF, linking with parietal and occipital lobe, and the UF, connecting with inferior frontal cortex, were two major association DWM bundles of TPR. 34 Our data indicate that ipsilateral GMB, a common and focal lesion accompanied by HS, may signify greater and widespread microstructural alterations of DWM in patients with TLE. The abnormalities within subdivisions of tracts far away from TPR probably reflect the gliosis related to chronic seizure activity in the vicinity of focus. 16 Unexpectedly, the robust significant difference of group‐by‐position interaction was only found in MD and RD at the anterior part of TCF with different sampling length. This suggests that those regions display the greatest GMB‐related increases in diffusion parameters and microstructural properties may vary at the beginning versus the body or end portions of the tract. The cingulum contains fibers of various lengths from parietal or occipital cortex and goes down the temporal lobe toward the pole. 51 It can be segmented into several subdivisions, with changes in specific subregions resulting in different functional disorders. 51 Nevertheless, with few studies targeting the cingulum itself and challenges of detailed postmortem study, there is a gap in our knowledge concerning lesions of the temporal (parahippocampal) cingulum.

Limitations

Several limitations of our study need to be addressed. Firstly, as a retrospective cohort study, it is difficult to analyze the correlation between pathological changes of specimens and imaging parameters quantitatively. Also, further pediatric prospective cohort studies are required to explore genesis of temporopolar GMB and related factors including febrile seizures, age of onset, and duration of epilepsy. Secondly, we offer novel information regarding the distinct distribution of HS subtypes related to ipsilateral GMB, although cases of indeterminate subtype might be subjective to confounding bias because of insufficient tissue. Finally, more targeted imaging sequences such as multi‐shell diffusion model, neurite orientation density and dispersion imaging (NODDI), and myelin water fraction imaging should be used to depict potentially abnormal progressive brain development and myelination in multi‐center longitudinal data.

Conclusion

Combined neuropathological and MRI data in the cohort study strengths the idea that temporopolar GMB ipsilateral to HS should be identified as a sign of WM atrophy and degeneration in patients with TLE. Our findings support the concept of TLE as a disorder of distributed neural networks constrained by the WM architecture, further suggesting TPR could be an important hub in spread of pathology outside the hippocampus. Additional information pointing toward neocortical epileptogenesis is needed for choice of surgical approaches.
