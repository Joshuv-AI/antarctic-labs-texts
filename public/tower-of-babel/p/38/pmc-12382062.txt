The association of bone mineral density with cardiovascular disease and mortality among individuals with/without type 2 diabetes: a population-based retrospective cohort
Authors: Ran Chen, Qingshan Guo, Chuanqing Bai, Jie Li, Yiyang Chen, Jiaxin Tan, Lianyang Zhang, Jun Fei, Siru Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesWhile type 2 diabetes (T2D) disrupts bone metabolism, T2D individuals often exhibit normal or elevated bone mineral density (BMD), complicating the relationship between BMD and cardiovascular disease (CVD). This study aimed to clarify the associations of BMD with CVD and mortality (CVM) in individuals with/without T2D.Study designa retrospective cohort study.MethodsT2D and non-type 2 diabetes (NT2D) individuals were selected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2005–2018). They were classified into three groups based on femoral neck BMD: T1(< 0.8 g/cm²), T2(0.8–1 g/cm²), and T3(≥ 1 g/cm²). Weighted multivariate analysis examined the association between BMD and CVD/CVM, while restricted cubic spline assessed their nonlinearity between BMD and CVM.ResultsThe study comprised 10,586 participants (NT2D: 8,006; T2D: 2,580). During a median follow-up of 125 months, CVD accounted for 201 and 128 deaths in NT2D and T2D groups. After multivariate adjustment, lower BMD was associated with higher CVD risk in the both groups, while higher BMD exhibited lower risk. However, higher BMD in T2D group was linked to increased CVD risk in males[ORT3vs.T2: 1.092 (1.089, 1.095)], ≥ 60 years[ORT3vs.T2: 1.160 (1.156, 1.163)], whites[ORT3vs.T2: 1.024 (1.022, 1.027)], or obese individuals[ORT3vs.T2: 1.288 (1.282, 1.294)]. T2D patients also exhibited increased CVM at both lower and higher BMD levels[HRT1vs.T2: 1.457 (1.454, 1.461); HRT3vs.T2: 1.268 (1.263, 1.272)]. Furthermore, NT2D individuals displayed a J-shaped nonlinear relationship between BMD and CVM, whereas T2D patients showed a U-shaped pattern.ConclusionsWhile low BMD serve as a risk factor for CVD and mortality in NT2D/T2D individuals, high BMD may be associated with elevated cardiovascular risk in T2D patients.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has long been a leading global cause of morbidity and mortality, threatening human health. In 2017, CVD accounted for approximately 17.9 million deaths, or 31% of total global fatalities [1]. Confronting this health challenge requires early identification of CVD risk factors for intervention. Traditional risk factors—such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, and obesity—are widely recognized, and their management is essential for the prevention of CVD [2]. However, solely focusing on these known predisposing factors may be insufficient, given the rising CVD mortality rates—especially in low- to middle-income countries, where annual CVD mortality increases by millions [2]. This highlights the urgent need for a comprehensive identification of additional cardiovascular risk factors.

Osteoporosis is a prevalent metabolic bone disorder characterized by diminished bone mineral density (BMD) and compromised bone microstructure, increasing the risk of spontaneous fractures and mortality [3]. While osteoporosis has conventionally been recognized as a metabolic bone condition, emerging evidence closely links it to CVD, with low BMD identified as a novel cardiovascular risk factor [1]. And this association can be supported by the following evidence. Firstly, same risk factors are shared by both osteoporosis and CVD, such as smoking, drinking and dyslipidemia [4]; Secondly, similar pathological features have been observed in both atherosclerosis and osteoporosis, including mild chronic inflammation, calcification processes, and the involvement of regulatory factors such as osteocalcin and osteopontin [4]; Thirdly, other pathological bases for CVD, such as aortic valve calcification and atherosclerosis, have also been found to be significantly associated with low BMD in individuals with osteoporosis [5]. Most importantly, mounting evidence has directly demonstrated a significant association between BMD, CVD, and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) [6–17]. These findings emphasized the cardioprotective benefits of high BMD and the adverse effects of low BMD. Such consistent findings have been observed across a variety of population, including general populations [7–9] the elderly [10, 11] postmenopausal females [12] males [13] and individuals with chronic kidney disease [14, 15] cholangiocarcinoma [16] or rheumatoid arthritis [17].

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a common risk factor for both CVD and osteoporosis. In the context of CVD, T2D exacerbates the progression of atherosclerosis through insulin resistance [18]; Conversely, evidence suggests that the elevated TNF-α associated with CVD-related high Gamma-glutamyl transferase can inactivate c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) -mediated insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1), thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of insulin resistance [19]. Similarly, with respect to osteoporosis, T2D contributes to bone loss by impairing osteoblast function and diminishing bone turnover [4]. Previous studies have indicated a significant association between reduced BMD and increased CVD risk in T2D patients, but these studies primarily focused on left ventricular diastolic dysfunction [20] atherosclerosis [21, 22] and all-cause mortality risk [23, 24]. The limited evidence for prevalent CVD and cardiovascular mortality may restrict the generalizability of these findings. Furthermore, while T2D disrupts bone metabolism, individuals with both T2D and osteoporosis often display normal or elevated BMD [25]. This complicates the relationship between BMD and CVD, and also raises the question of whether the association between BMD and CVD in T2D patients is similar to that in NT2D individuals. This study aimed to clarify the associations of BMD with CVD and CVM in individuals with/without T2D.

Methods

Study population

Data for this study were extracted from the NHANES spanning from 2005 to 2018. NHANES, led by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), serves as a research initiative to assess the health and nutritional status of American adults and children. Each year, NHANES conducts surveys on approximately 5,000 nationally representative individuals, collecting data through structured interviews, health examinations, and laboratory analyses. Ethical approval was obtained for this study [26] and informed consent was acquired from all participants [27]. Following the STROBE guidelines [28] a set of exclusion criteria (Fig. 1) was applied to screen 8,006 individuals with non-type 2 diabetes (NT2D) and 2,580 individuals with T2D from a total of 70,190 participants. Among them, 620 NT2D and 517 T2D individuals were diagnosed with CVD. Over a median follow-up duration of 125 months, CVD accounted for 201 and 128 deaths in NT2D and T2D group.

BMD measurement

BMD was predominantly assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). The DXA equipment utilized in the NHANES study was the Hologic QDR 4500 A fan-beam densitometer manufactured by Hologic, Inc. Common measurement sites for BMD include the total hip, lumbar spine, and femoral neck, with the latter two serve as key indicators for osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture risk assessment. However, due to the significant loss of lumbar spine BMD data within NHANES, this study primarily selected femoral neck BMD for analysis. The left hip is the primary site for femoral neck DXA measurement, with assessment shifting to the right hip in cases of left hip metallic implantation to mitigate potential interference. Additionally, to ensure data integrity and validity, all DXA examinations were conducted by rigorously trained radiologic technologists, and participants with a history of bilateral hip fractures or metallic implants were excluded [29].

Type 2 diabetes diagnosis

Consistent with published study [24] diabetes was defined in this study by meeting one of the following criteria: (1) being asked by a doctor or other health professional, “Do you have diabetes?” and answering “yes”; (2) fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL; (3) random blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL; (4) OGTT 2-hour blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; (5) glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%; (6) use of insulin; (7) use of oral hypoglycemic agents. This study primarily focuses on type 2 diabetes; therefore, patients with T1D were excluded from the diabetic cohort. According to the latest American Diabetes Association guidelines, diagnostic criteria for T1D include (Fig. 1): age < 35 years, BMI < 25 kg/m², unintentional weight loss, ketoacidosis, or blood glucose > 360 mg/dL [30].

CVD diagnosis and CVD mortality ascertainment

Participant diagnosis of CVD was established through a series of inquiries. Those affirming a history of congestive heart failure, CHD, angina, or heart attack when queried by healthcare professionals were categorized accordingly. Participants afflicted with any of these conditions constituted the total CVD study cohort.

This study acquired mortality follow-up data for the included population by linking NHANES data with the National Death Index (NDI). Follow-up for participant mortality extended from the survey participation date to December 31, 2019. NDI data were categorized using ICD codes, with CVD listed as a primary cause of death under codes I00–109, I11, I13, and I20 - I51.

Covariates

Consistent with published studies [24, 31] this study identified potential confounding factors affecting both BMD and CVD mortality. These include: (i) demographic characteristics—age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, income, BMI, smoking, physical activity, and alcohol consumption, hypertension, general health status; (ii) laboratory parameters—ALP, total calcium, total cholesterol, creatinine, glucose, total protein, triglycerides, and HDL; and (iii) dietary factors—total energy intake, protein, carbohydrates, fat, total cholesterol, phosphorus, and calcium.

Statistical analysis

This study compared various variables between NT2D and T2D individuals, and between T2D individuals and those with T2D + CVD. Continuous data were presented as mean (standard deviation), with normally distributed variables analyzed using weighted t-tests and non-normally distributed ones assessed via weighted Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were represented as weighted percentages and assessed using chi-square tests. To facilitate interpretation, we categorized individuals with CVM hazard ratios (data from restricted cubic splines of T2D cohort) approximately < 1, and BMD range from 0.8-1gcm2 as the reference group T2. Meanwhile, those with BMD < 0.8 gcm2 or ≥ 1 gcm2 were respectively assigned to the T1 and T3 groups. Weighted logistic regression models were employed to assess the association between BMD and CVD. Kaplan-Meier curves evaluated CVM, survival time, and proportions among individuals with different BMD levels (T1-T3), while weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to analyze the association of BMD with CVM. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were conducted to examine the nonlinear relationship between BMD and CVM, with knots placed at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the BMD distribution.

We identified a series of covariates meeting criteria of p < 0.20 or variance inflation factor < 10 and established two models. Model 1 adjusted for age, gender, race/ethnicity, and income, while Model 2 included Model 1 + BMI, smoking status, physical activity, alcohol consumption, hypertension, general health status, serum ALP, total calcium, total cholesterol, creatinine, glucose, total protein, triglycerides, HDL, and daily intake of energy, protein, carbohydrates, fat, total cholesterol, phosphorus, and calcium. Considering the complex multi-stage sampling of NHANES, weight data used in this study were derived from 24-hour dietary recall samples from 2005 to 2018 (7 cycles), representing the non-institutionalized US population (weight of the smallest subsample * 1/7). Statistical analysis was conducted using R (http://www.Rproject.org), SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM, USA), and Graphpad Prism 9.0 (https://www.graphpad-prism.cn), with significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Higher BMD in T2D individuals compared to NT2D

T2D patients exhibited significantly lower BMD (0.814 g/cm²) compared to NT2D individuals (0.828 g/cm²), and this trend remained consistent across subgroup analyses of gender, race, and BMI (Fig. 2A). However, a reversal was observed in the age subgroup: T2D patients over 60 had significantly higher BMD (0.778 g/cm²) than NT2D individuals (0.744 g/cm²). Similar results also emerged in the analysis for total CVD and CHD (Fig. 2B), where BMD levels were significantly higher in the T2D group than in the NT2D group.

Baseline characteristics of individuals with both CVD and T2D

Compared with T2D patients, individuals with both CVD and T2D were older (67y), males (65.2%), white (75.0%), in poor health (53.7%), and had a higher prevalence of hypertension (56.6%) (Table 1,). Furthermore, individuals with CVD and T2D exhibited lower level of serum calcium (2.34 mmol/L), cholesterol (4.4 mmol/L), total protein (70.0 g/L), HDL (1.2 mmol/L), and BMD (0.772 g/cm²), and they had a lower intake of energy (1805 Kcal), protein (73 g), carbohydrates (209 g), fat (72 g), cholesterol (275 g), calcium (845 g), and phosphorus (1206 g).

Association between BMD and CVD in NT2D and T2D individuals

After multivariate adjustment, compared to the T2 group, low BMD levels in NT2D individuals (T1 group) were associated with increased risk of total CVD [ORT1 vs. T2: 1.296 (95% CI: 1.295, 1.298)], whereas those individuals with high BMD levels (T3 group) showed a decreased risk [ORT3 vs. T2: 0.642 (95% CI: 0.641, 0.643)] (Fig. 3A). Similar results were also found in subgroup analyses of individuals with different CVDs, gender, age, race and BMI (Fig. 3A-B).

Association between BMD and CVM in NT2D and T2D individuals

The Kaplan-Meier curves showed that, compared to individuals with higher BMD (T1 and T3 group), both T2D and NT2D individuals with lower BMD (T1 group) exhibited a higher CVM risk, shorter mean survival time, and a lower survival proportion (Fig. 4A and B). In the fully adjusted model, Cox regression and subgroup analyses across age, sex, race, and BMI showed that, NT2D individuals (T1 group) with lower BMD had a higher CVM [HRT1 vs. T2: 1.312 (1.310, 1.314)], while no significant association was observed between higher BMD and CVM in NT2D individuals (T3 group) [HRT3 vs. T2: 0.816 (0.456, 1.460)] (Fig. 4C). However, the subgroup analysis indicated that NT2D individuals with higher BMD (T3 group) exhibited lower CVM.

Nonlinear association between BMD and CVM in NT2D and T2D individuals

In the fully adjusted model, RCS analysis demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between BMD and CVM in both T2D and NT2D individuals (Fig. 5). NT2D individuals exhibited a J-shaped association, while T2D patients demonstrated a U-shaped pattern (Fig. 5A, Model 2; Fig. 5B, Model 2; nadir BMD associated with CVM was 0.858 g/cm²). Consistent results emerged from subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, race, and BMI (Fig.s 5A1-A4; 5B1-B4). The J-shaped association in NT2D individuals indicated a continuous decline in CVM with increasing BMD. Conversely, the U-shaped relationship in T2D patients showed that for BMD < 0.858 g/cm², CVM decreased with rising BMD, whereas for BMD > 0.858 g/cm², CVM increased with higher BMD.

Discussion

In the present study, we found that low BMD levels were identified as a risk factor for CVD and mortality, independent of T2D status. Conversely, high BMD might be associated with increased risks of CVD and mortality in individuals with T2D, which contrasted with the cardioprotective effects observed in those without T2D. T2D has traditionally been recognized as a significant risk factor for cardiovascular health [32, 33] and previous studies have reported the relationship of BMD with atherosclerosis [21, 22] cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy [34] left ventricular diastolic dysfunction [20] as well as all-cause mortality [23, 24, 31] in T2D patients. However, direct evidence supporting the association between BMD and common CVD and cardiovascular mortality in T2D individuals remains insufficient.

Our study revealed the association between low BMD and increased risks of CVD and mortality in both T2D and NT2D individuals, supporting low BMD as a novel cardiovascular risk factor. However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Bone health is influenced by multiple factors, such as smoking, drinking, and a sedentary lifestyle. These factors inhibit bone remodeling and promote bone loss, leading to increased risk of fragility fractures. Moreover, they may also be closely associated with conditions such as dyslipidemia, arteriosclerosis, vascular calcification, and cardiac dysfunction [1]. Therefore, low BMD or osteoporosis suggests that individuals have predisposing factors, lifestyle factors, or poorer health status, which may account for higher risks of CVD and mortality.

Moreover, this association could be attributed to prevention or treatment associated with osteoporosis. For instance, large-scale observational studies have observed an increased risk of CVD mortality with high intake of calcium supplementation [35]. With respect to T2D patients, aberrant glucose metabolism and elevated blood sugar levels are intricately linked to atherosclerosis and vascular calcification. These pathological mechanisms entail the deposition of minerals or proteins within the extracellular matrix of bone cells, resulting in adverse consequences on the skeletal system [36]. To some extent, this also elucidates the association between low BMD and CVD. Additionally, due to the close physiological and functional relationship between skeletal muscle and bone, osteoporosis patients often present with sarcopenia, a condition characterized by loss of skeletal muscle mass and reduced strength. Many studies have demonstrated that sarcopenia is a critical factor in the pathogenesis of CVD [37] and the chronic inflammatory associated with sarcopenia can contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, ultimately causing damage to endothelial cells and myocardial cells [38]. Therefore, low BMD and osteoporosis should not solely be viewed as a metabolic bone disease but also as a risk factor for CVD [39].

Furthermore, the study unveiled an adverse effect of high BMD on CVD and mortality in T2D patients. T2D patients often exhibit a BMD level approximately 5% − 10% higher than healthy individuals [25, 40] but this abnormally elevated BMD does not seem confer additional benefits to T2D patients. This has been confirmed in the skeletal system; for example, studies have shown that higher BMD in T2D patients fail to reflect their risk of fractures, and they have a higher risk of osteoporotic fractures [40]. This may also apply to the cardiovascular system in T2D patients, where the higher BMD is associated with an increased risk of CVD and mortality, rather than contributing to cardiovascular health. The underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood, but some indirect evidence may support this hypothesis.

Hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia are two major direct consequences of severe insulin resistance in T2D patients. Persistent hyperinsulinemia is implicated in atherosclerosis and CVD by disrupting the insulin-growth hormone-insulin-like growth factor axis and promoting the synthesis and accumulation of adipose tissue [41]. In the skeletal system, hyperinsulinemia can influence bone formation by binding to the IGF-1 receptors on osteoblasts, resulting in higher bone mass [40]. Hyperglycemia in T2D patients may also play a role in bone metabolism by mediating osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Several clinical studies have found that high glucose concentrations can inhibit the differentiation and functional activity of osteoclasts by suppressing receptor activator of nuclear factor κ B ligand (RANKL), resulting in decreased bone resorption activity and reduced degradation of the bone matrix [42, 43]. However, results from animal experiments and observational studies suggest that hyperglycemia can promote atherosclerotic responses by inducing osteogenic differentiation in endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle cells in various vascular wall [44, 45].

In addition, the skeleton is well recognized as an endocrine organ capable of synthesizing and secreting a diverse array of biomolecules, which can influence cardiovascular health through the bone-vascular axis [39]. Some factors related to bone formation, such as alkaline phosphatase [46] osteoprotegerin [47]and osteocalcin [48] have been found to be associated with increased risk of CVD. In T2D patients, bone matrix proteins such as osteocalcin and osteopontin, which are involved in maintaining bone structure and function, have similar detrimental effects on the cardiovascular system [49]. Additionally, the elevated prevalence of CVD in T2D patients is closely associated with their prevalent aortic calcification [50]. The deposition of calcium salts in the arterial wall may interfere with the measurement of BMD (such as DXA scans), potentially leading to misinterpretation of calcium deposits as part of bone density, resulting in an overestimation of BMD [51, 52]. This at least partly interprets the association between high BMD and the increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality in T2D patients.

This study has several potential limitations. Firstly, unlike randomized controlled trials, this observational study precludes inference of causality between BMD and cardiovascular risk. Secondly, while we controlled for various potential confounders that may influence the relationship between BMD and cardiovascular risk, we cannot overlook the impact of unconsidered factors such as differences in single BMD measurement, serum lipid and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels as well as medical services for hypertension, diabetes or osteoporosis etc. Thirdly, dietary recall of nutrient intake from NHANES may be prone to errors due to inconsistencies in dietary or supplement intake during the follow-up period. Finally, due to the lack of longitudinal follow-up data in NHANES, we employed logistic regression in analyzing the association between BMD and CVD. However, this statistical method may introduce potential bias into the findings.

Conclusions

Low BMD levels serve as a risk factor for CVD and mortality, irrespective of T2D status. While high BMD confers cardioprotective effects for NT2D individuals, high BMD may be associated with increased risks of CVD and mortality in T2D patients.
