Resting-state and task-state fNIRS fusion assessment method for upper limb motor function in stroke patients
Authors: Ziwen Yuan, Weiwei Xu, Zhehao Hu, Jing Guo, Yi Tao, Lisha Wang, Yu Peng, Michael Houston, Yingchun Zhang, Jin Qiao, Gang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPost-stroke rehabilitation strategies rely heavily on accurate motor function assessment. Scale-based assessments face inherent limitations, such as inter-rater variability and long assessment time. Neurophysiological biomarkers are emerging as more objective, complementary assessment tools. However, few studies have integrated multi-states functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) data to fit the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) scores of stroke patients with machine learning.MethodsThis study enrolled 57 stroke patients and acquired fNIRS signals covering the sensorimotor cortex during both resting-state and grip-task conditions. A modified sequential forward selection algorithm was employed to select optimal features from three domains: functional connectivities (FCs) and graph theory metrics of resting-state fNIRS and task-state fNIRS, and clinical characteristics. Support vector regression was then applied on the multi-domain feature set to predict FMA scores.ResultsBoth for cortical and subcortical lesion-type stroke patients, the resting-state + task-state fNIRS fused model demonstrated significantly reduced prediction errors compared to single-state models. Optimal performance was achieved at 45% network sparsity [R2 = 0.8434 (0.6889–0.9311), RMSE = 6.75 (4.26–8.78), MAE = 5.11 (3.17–7.00)], with R2 values exceeding 0.73 across all network sparsity levels for cortical stroke. In subcortical stroke patients, the fused model demonstrated superior performance at 35% network sparsity [R2 = 0.7546 (0.5255–0.8728), RMSE = 10.55 (7.51–13.83), MAE = 7.39 (5.35–10.17)]. And the fusion model demonstrated a better fit than models incorporating either clinical or fNIRS datasets alone.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that the fusion model integrating resting-state and task-state fNIRS significantly improves motor function assessment accuracy in stroke patients compared to single-state or single-dataset models.

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of long-term disability globally, characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates [1]. Post-stroke motor impairment significantly impacts patients' functional independence and quality of life. Rehabilitation strategies rely heavily on accurate motor function assessments to tailor therapeutic interventions. Currently, standardized clinical scales such as the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) and Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) serve as the cornerstone for evaluating upper-limb motor recovery [2, 3]. These tools quantify motor performance through predefined tasks, providing a structured framework for tracking rehabilitation progress. However, scale-based assessments face inherent limitations. Single evaluations often require 30 min per session, imposing time burdens on clinicians and patients [4]. Additionally, results are susceptible to inter-rater variability due to subjective interpretation of scoring criteria [5]. Frequent reassessments, while necessary for monitoring recovery, may inadvertently introduce "learning effects," where patients improve task familiarity rather than genuine motor function, thereby skewing outcome validity [6]. These challenges have spurred interest in neurophysiological biomarkers as more objective, complementary assessment tools.

Emerging evidence suggests that electrophysiological signals, including electroencephalography (EEG), correlate with motor recovery trajectories [7, 8]. Techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) effectively map cortical activities during motor tasks, revealing reorganization patterns in sensorimotor networks after stroke [9]. Unlike scales, neurophysiological data offer continuous, operator-independent insights into cortical reorganization. Despite these advances, limitations persist. fMRI requires costly infrastructure, prolonged scanning times (> 30 min), and strict head immobilization, making it impractical for motor assessment [10]. EEG suffers from low spatial resolution and susceptibility to motion artifacts, limiting its utility in quantifying localized cortical responses [11]. Emerging as a promising alternative, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) combines moderate spatial resolution with high temporal sampling, while tolerating moderate head movements [12].

It’s demonstrated that fNIRS is capable of monitoring task-induced hemodynamic responses within motor regions, which exhibit significant correlations with concurrent clinical assessments such as the FMA (r = –0.75, P < 0.001) [13]. Another study also suggested that small-world network properties, calculated based on fNIRS data, exhibit a linear correlation with FMA scores [14]. These findings establish a foundation for utilizing fNIRS-derived signals in the evaluation of motor function. Furthermore, several investigations have employed machine learning (ML) techniques to categorize levels of motor impairment in stroke patients based on fNIRS data, reporting promising classification outcomes [14–16]. However, the classification of motor impairment severity remains insufficient to meet the demands of more granular motor function assessment. Therefore, this study attempted to evaluate the continuous motor function scores (e.g., FMA) by a ML algorithm based on resting-state and task-state data of fNIRS and clinical indicators. Besides, for feature selection across multiple datasets, traditional sequential forward/backward search is a method with low computational overhead; however, it inherently risks suboptimal feature selection by merging multiple datasets into a single one for ranking—prioritizing incremental performance gains without exhaustively testing all combinations, which may lead to local optima [17, 18]. Therefore, it is necessary to optimize the sequential search strategy specifically for multiple datasets.

Methods

Experimental protocol and data acquisition

The patients were screened from two registered trial (clinicaltrials.gov: NCT04617366, NCT05318586). The inclusion criteria were as follows: 30–79 years of age, first stroke onset within 2 weeks to 6 months prior to study participation, hemiplegia, right-handedness, and a good signal quality of fNIRS data without missing channels. Exclusion criteria included: significant aphasia, dysphrenia or cognitive impairment; obvious spasm, pain, or limited joint movement of the affected upper limb. A total of 57 stroke subjects were included. The motor function of the patients was evaluated by FMA of upper limb (FMA-UL). fNIRS-based FMA-UL estimation algorithm

The algorithm workflow is illustrated in Fig. 3. Based on the incorporated fNIRS datasets, three distinct models were developed: (1) a resting-state fNIRS model, (2) a task-state fNIRS model, and (3) a resting-state + task-state fNIRS fusion model; all the models incorporated the clinical feature set. In addition, two models that incorporated only the clinical dataset or the fNIRS dataset (including both resting-state and task-state data) were also analyzed. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) [19] was employed, where for each sample serving as the test set, one randomly selected sample from the remaining dataset was designated as the validation set, with all other samples constituting the training set. To ensure comparability across multiple models, the validation and training sets corresponding to each test sample remained strictly consistent across all models whenever that particular sample was being tested. Since fNIRS detects hemodynamic activity in the cerebral cortex, cortical damage inherently alters the intrinsic blood flow patterns in the affected cortical areas. Therefore, we initially classified participants into two groups for separate analyses: those with cortical lesions and those with subcortical lesions.Fig. 3Flowchart of the proposed machine learning algorithm. fNIRS features extraction

The NirSpark software (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) was used to preprocess fNIRS data. The preprocessing pipeline has been described in our previous study [20]. Briefly, a bandpass filter with 0.01–0.20 Hz was used to remove physiological noise; and three times spline interpolation was used to remove motion artifacts [21]; then the raw fNIRS data were converted into hemoglobin concentration data. Scalp blood flow interference was further eliminated through independent component analysis (ICA) [22]. fNIRS channels of right-hemisphere stroke patients were flipped, standardizing all patient data to represent left-hemisphere lesions.

Functional connectivity (FCs) between channels of the preprocessed fNIRS data were derived using Pearson's correlation coefficients, calculated following the same methodology as described in our previous study [23]. FCs of the resting-state task were computed using the entire data recording. For task-state analysis, FCs were calculated for each trial separately and finally averaged across trials to obtain the final task-state FCs. Functional connectivity analyses of both resting-state and motor tasks yielded 48 × 48 symmetric adjacency matrices representing the functional brain networks (Fig. 3). All correlation coefficients were subsequently standardized to Fisher's Z-scores to facilitate statistical analyses at the group level.

In functional connectivity networks, not all connections are biologically meaningful or physiologically genuine. Weak or insignificant connections may potentially represent spurious correlations [24]. To address this issue, the present study employed an exhaustive thresholding approach to eliminate spurious connections. Specifically, network sparsity thresholds were systematically examined from 10 to 50% with 5% increments [24], resulting in nine sparse networks for subsequent brain network metric calculations. Therefore, model training and testing were performed separately at each sparsity level.

Further ROI-specific FCs and graph theory metrics were computed after establishing the functional brain networks. The calculation procedure for FCs between ROIs was performed as follows: First, we counted the number of non-zero inter-channel FCs between each pair of ROIs. Then, we summed the correlation coefficients of all these non-zero inter-channel FCs between each region pair. Finally, the inter-regional correlation coefficient was obtained by dividing this sum by the number of non-zero valued inter-channel connections. Through this computation, a total of 14 × (14–1)/2 = 91 FCs between ROIs were obtained (Fig. 3). In addition, the clinical feature set included five key variables: age, sex, stroke type, onset time, and motor attempt label. Support vector regression model

Support Vector Machines (SVM) represent a classical discriminative approach in machine learning [25]. As an important extension, Support Vector Regression (SVR) adopts the core SVM framework while introducing an ε-insensitive tolerance for deviations between predicted and true values [26]. SVR optimization involves the following key steps: (1) Kernel selection: map input data to a high-dimensional feature space via Gaussian kernel function; (2) Optimization problem: the objective is to maximize the margin while minimizing prediction error; (3) Introduce Lagrange multipliers for each constraint, transforming the objective function into a Lagrangian dual problem; (4) Solve the Lagrangian to obtain the optimized parameters; (5) The regression function is constructed through a linear combination of the support vectors; (6) Hyperplane determination: define the decision boundary using support vectors and their corresponding Lagrange multipliers. The SVR optimization objective is formally expressed as [26]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\underset{w,b,{\xi }_{i},{\xi }_{i}^{*}}{\mathrm{min}}\frac{1}{2}{\parallel w\parallel }^{2}+C\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left({\xi }_{i}+{\xi }_{i}^{*}\right)$$\end{document}9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s.t. \left\{\begin{array}{c}{y}_{i}-\left({w}^{T}{x}_{i}+b\right)\le \varepsilon +{\xi }_{i}\\ \left({w}^{T}{x}_{i}+b\right)-{y}_{i}\le \varepsilon +{\xi }_{i}^{*}\\ {\xi }_{i},{\xi }_{i}^{*}\ge 0, i=\mathrm{1,2},\dots ,m\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

In the formulation, w denotes the weight vector, while b represents the bias term. The slack variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\xi }_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\xi }_{i}^{*}$$\end{document} quantify prediction errors exceeding the ε-insensitive margin (ε ≥ 0) for upper and lower deviations respectively. ε is a key SVR parameter that balances model complexity and prediction accuracy, larger ε values result in fewer support vectors. C indicates the regularization parameter, and T represents the total number of training samples. Each training sample is represented by (xi, yi), where xi is the feature vector and yi is the corresponding target value.

In this study, the parameters—C and γ (governs the locality of the model) were optimized through LOOCV on the training and validation sets, with the grid search method employed to identify the optimal parameter combination.

Feature selection

Feature selection occurs through incremental subset modification, where features are sequentially incorporated or excluded until achieving optimization targets. The process follows three fundamental strategies: sequential forward selection (SFS), sequential backward search (SBS), and bidirectional selection [17, 18]. These sequential feature selection aggregates all features into a single candidate pool during the ranking process, potentially converging to suboptimal local solutions [17, 18]. Based on the limitation, we developed a modified SFS strategy organizing features into multiple domain-specific sets to enhance opportunities for domain-specific feature inclusion. Similar to standard SFS, features within each set were first ranked in descending order based on the absolute value of their correlation with FMA-UL scores. The number of optimal top-ranked features (n) was determined through an iterative selection process: (1) From each feature set, the highest-ranked unselected feature was identified; (2) These three candidate features were individually tested in the model, the feature (rank x) demonstrating maximal performance improvement was selected; (3) In subsequent iterations, the next-ranked feature (x + 1) from the selected feature's set was compared against the previous candidates from other two sets; (4) This process continued until no additional features could improve model performance. The feature selection process was performed exclusively on the training set and validation set via LOOCV to identify optimal features. In each iteration, one sample was held out as the test set, another randomly chosen sample from the remainder served as the validation set, and all other samples formed the training set. To prevent information leakage during the process of extracting the top “n” most relevant features, both the calculation of feature correlations with FMA-UL and the subsequent feature ranking were performed exclusively on the training set of the current fold. Once the optimal features were identified, the test set was fed into the model to obtain the corresponding FMA score. Although there were models with datasets containing five combinations, the feature selection strategy followed the same protocol as described above for the three feature sets in fusion model.

Performance assessment

This study employs three evaluation metrics to assess model performance: the Coefficient of Determination (R2), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). The 95% confidence interval for each metric was estimated using the Bootstrap method. Furthermore, computations were performed across nine distinct network sparsity levels, with each level yielding corresponding model evaluation results. To comprehensively evaluate the overall performance of the model across these sparsity levels: (1) The mean values of all three metrics (R2, RMSE, MAE) were calculated to assess the model's aggregate performance. (2) The standard deviations of these metrics were computed to quantify the variability in model effectiveness under different sparsity conditions. Comparisons of performance between the models were conducted using paired t-tests with a two-tailed α level of 0.05.

Results

Cortical stroke

Figure 5 presents the performance of the three models in subjects with cortical lesions. For the resting-state model, optimal fitting was achieved at 45% sparsity [R2 = 0.7024 (0.4684–0.8256), RMSE = 9.30 (5.93–12.30), MAE = 7.16 (4.55–9.90)], though some samples in higher segments exhibited larger prediction errors. Across all nine sparsity levels, R2 values consistently exceeded 0.62. The averaged metrics demonstrated robust overall performance (R2 = 0.6674 ± 0.0238, RMSE = 9.83 ± 0.35, MAE = 7.16 ± 0.67), indicating stable fitting results with minimal variability across different sparsity levels.Fig. 5The performance of the three models using different fNIRS dataset in cortical stroke patients. The task-state model also achieved optimal predictive performance at 45% sparsity [R2 = 0.7675 (0.5079–0.8729), RMSE = 8.22 (5.51–11.43), MAE = 6.42 (4.24–9.30)]. Compared to the resting-state model, the task-state paradigm partially mitigated the larger prediction errors observed in high-segment samples. The averaged metrics across all nine sparsity levels demonstrated comprehensive fitting results (R2 = 0.7194 ± 0.0237, RMSE = 9.02 ± 0.39, MAE = 6.98 ± 0.41), with evaluation outcomes remaining relatively stable across different sparsity thresholds.

The fused model demonstrated significantly reduced prediction errors compared to single-state models (P < 0.01, Fig. 5), with R2 values exceeding 0.73 across all sparsity levels, outperforming both single-state fNIRS models. Optimal performance was achieved at 45% sparsity [R2 = 0.8434 (0.6889–0.9311), RMSE = 6.75 (4.26–8.78), MAE = 5.11 (3.17–7.00)], where 11 of 19 subjects exhibited absolute prediction errors ≤ 5. Comparable results were observed at 30%, 40%, and 50% sparsity levels. Aggregate metrics across all nine sparsity levels (R2 = 0.7945 ± 0.0392, RMSE = 7.70 ± 0.73, MAE = 5.55 ± 0.60) confirmed both superior performance and stable fitting across varying sparsity conditions.

Subcortical stroke

For patients with subcortical lesions (Fig. 6), the resting-state model predicted FMA-UL scores with R2 values ranging 0.61–0.69, achieving optimal performance at 35% sparsity [R2 = 0.6888 (0.4742–0.8177), RMSE = 11.88 (9.03–14.71), MAE = 8.79 (6.71–11.66)]. Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that the fusion assessment method integrating multi-state fNIRS datasets achieved promising predictive performance in the assessment of motor function in post-stroke patients. Notably, the fusion model maintained stable predictive accuracy across network sparsity levels, suggesting robustness against variations in network topology parameterization. Furthermore, due to fNIRS’s portability and ease of operation, it is well-suited for bedside assessments in hospital wards or rehabilitation therapy rooms, offering the potential to provide clinicians with an objective, quantitative tool for monitoring motor function. In the future, its scalability could be further enhanced by developing automated analysis workflows—for example, by exploring integration with routine rehabilitation training to enable real-time functional feedback and dynamic evaluation of therapeutic efficacy during treatment.

The multi-state fusion model (combining brain network metrics of both resting-state and task-state fNIRS data) achieved superior performance compared to single-state models utilizing resting-state or task-state data alone. This aligns with the hypothesis that resting-state and task-state fNIRS capture complementary neurophysiological signatures [27, 28]. Prior studies using multimodal neuroimaging to assess post-stroke motor outcomes support the advantages of data fusion. For instance, Pilmeyer et al. [29] found that multi-modal ensemble classifiers with T1-weighted, resting-state functional MRI and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) features improved classification performance for diagnosis of depression, outperforming the highest uni-modal (DTI) performance (AUC = 0.746 vs 0.701). Similarly, Zhou et al. [15] demonstrated that integrating fNIRS features with clinical characteristics significantly enhanced classification accuracy of SVM model for upper limb recovery (accuracy = 0.805 vs. 0.735 for clinical features-only model and 0.765 for fNIRS features-only model). In summary, multi-state fusion models leverage synergistic information across functional brain states, achieving higher predictive accuracy and stability than single-state paradigms. This strategy aligns with emerging trends in neurorehabilitation to optimize biomarker discovery through multimodal integration [30].

This study also found that the three models incorporated two predominant features: the “motor attempt label” and FCs linked to the contralesional PMC (Table 4). The “motor attempt label” provides a novel stratification framework. While prior studies have associated grip performance with cortical activation patterns, (e.g., Lin et al. [31] correlated grip kinematics with ipsilesional motor cortex hemodynamics) none have explicitly translated discrete motor capacities into binary classification labels for predictive modeling. The prominence of contralesional PMC connectivity aligns with its emerging role in post-stroke network reorganization. Structural and functional neuroimaging evidence suggests that the contralesional PMC compensates for damaged ipsilesional motor networks by strengthening interhemispheric connections and integrating with secondary motor areas [32, 33]. Lotze et al. [32] demonstrated that PMC exhibits a beneficial role during motor recovery, particularly in patients with subcortical lesions. Our results extend these observations by proposing that PMC-centered FC metrics may serve as dynamic biomarkers for assessing the motor impairment. Importantly, this region plays a significant role in both cortical and subcortical stroke patients. The stratification of stroke patients into cortical and subcortical subgroups corresponds with fNIRS's neuroanatomical specificity for cortical hemodynamics [34], where cortical lesions directly alter local blood flow patterns. Consequently, the models demonstrated a strong fit for the cortical stroke population in this study. However, in subcortical stroke, changes in cortical neural activity are indirect, which also leads to a lack of direct one-to-one correspondence between the severity of motor impairment—as interpreted from cortical hemodynamic signals acquired by fNIRS—and the size of the subcortical lesion. This likely accounts for the lower prediction accuracy observed in the subcortical stroke cohort in this study. Notably, PMC-related functional connectivity emerged as a critical predictor across groups (Table 4), consistent with its role in motor planning and execution [35]. However, cortical stroke patients exhibited additional reliance on functional connections involving the ipsilesional SAC (Table 4), likely reflecting compensatory network reorganization. For instance, post-stroke SAC hyperactivity has been linked to sensory-motor integration during recovery, such as enhanced cross-modal plasticity in upper-limb rehabilitation [36]. Li et al. [37] found that dual-tDCS combined with sensorimotor training can significantly enhance the FC between the left SAC and right PMC/SMA, which was positively correlated with the improvement of FMA-UL in the experimental group. Cortical stroke EEG analyses revealed stronger interhemispheric connectivity deficits in motor networks compared to subcortical cases [38]. Conversely, subcortical lesions show greater reliance on cortico-cerebellar functional connectivity in fNIRS studies, suggesting divergent compensatory mechanisms [39]. This stratification enhances model interpretability and personalizes rehabilitation targets.

The sequential forward selection (SFS) algorithm, while computationally efficient, inherently risks suboptimal feature selection [17, 18]. To mitigate this limitation, we implemented a modified SFS strategy by partitioning the feature pool into three domain-specific subsets: resting-state fNIRS metrics, task-state fNIRS metrics, and clinical variables. This stratification ensured balanced representation of heterogeneous feature categories during iterative selection, and the modified SFS strategy exhibited better performance than conventional SFS both for cortical (best R2: 0.8434 vs 0.8028) and succortical stroke (best R2: 0.7546 vs 0.7326; Table 5). For cortical stroke, the mSFS strategy outperformed the traditional SFS strategy at six sparsity levels (including the optimal sparsity), with p-values as follows: R2: 0.104, RMSE: 0.094, MAE: 0.056. For subcortical stroke, the performance was slightly worse than that of the traditional SFS strategy at only one sparsity level, with p-values as follows: R2: 0.021, RMSE: 0.016, MAE: 0.030. Alternative SFS modifications, such as sequential floating forward selection (SFFS), dynamically add or remove features to escape local minima [40]. However, SFFS increases computational complexity and overfitting risks in high-dimensional neuroimaging-clinical datasets. Our simplified subset-based SFS achieved comparable robustness with lower computational overhead, particularly advantageous for multimodal integration. Regarding the selection of ML algorithms, we also experimented with other regression methods, including lasso regression, random forest regression, and elastic net regression. When incorporating all datasets, the results of these methods—both in terms of goodness of fit and error metrics—were inferior to those of SVR. Particularly, the performance of Elastic Net Regression was notably poorer (Table 6). Furthermore, SVR is theoretically well-suited for the modeling requirements of this study, primarily for the following reasons: (1) The feature dimension of fNIRS data is relatively high while the sample size is limited. SVR, based on the principle of structural risk minimization, exhibits stronger generalization ability compared to methods like random forest regression [41]. (2) The relationship between brain function and motor scores may be nonlinear. SVR can effectively capture such complex nonlinear relationships through kernel tricks, whereas linear models like Lasso struggle to model such associations [42]. (3) SVR demonstrates good robustness against signal noise and outliers, making it suitable for clinical application scenarios [41, 43]. This study has limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, the modest sample size restricts statistical power, and the model generalization ability and the risk of overfitting still require careful evaluation. To address this, future efforts should validate models in larger, multi-center cohorts and leverage open-source datasets to enhance robustness. Secondly, the underrepresentation of patients with moderate FMA-UL scores (mid-range impairment) may bias predictions; resolving this imbalance requires targeted recruitment of mid-range impairment cases and stratified sampling to improve model performance across the full stroke-severity spectrum. Thirdly, while SVR effectively integrates multimodal fNIRS and clinical metrics, the persistent absolute prediction error highlights the need to adopt even more advanced algorithms (e.g., deep neural networks) to improve nonlinear fitting and reduce residual variance. Fourth, regarding lesion laterality handling, since our cohort included patients with both left- and right-hemisphere lesions, we adopted the hemisphere flipping method. While this approach facilitates the differentiation of functional metrics between the ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres, it may also overlook potential influences related to hemispheric dominance [23]. Fifth, to meet the requirements for fitting the FMA scores, our study included patients with varying degrees of motor impairment, which led to differences in the completion of the grip task. In particular, for patients who could not physically perform the grip movement, motor imagery served as the primary form of motor engagement. Quantifying the level of effort in these individuals is challenging and may confound task‑state FC estimates. However, during the task‑state measurements, all patients received the same standardized instruction: “Try to follow the video and attempt to grip with your maximum effort.” Additionally, a meta‑analysis has reported consistent activation in the sensorimotor regions of the brain during both motor imagery and motor execution tasks [44]. Together, these measures help to minimize potential confounding on the task‑state FC estimates.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that the fusion model integrating resting-state and task-state fNIRS features significantly improves motor function assessment accuracy in stroke patients compared to single-state models, with FCs linked to the contralesional PMC identified as predominant predictive features. The modified subset-based SFS method further improved feature selection efficacy, proving particularly advantageous for multimodal data integration.
