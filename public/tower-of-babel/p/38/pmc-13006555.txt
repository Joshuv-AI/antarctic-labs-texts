Rethinking Pediatric Dyspepsia: Early Gastric Remodeling as a Unifying Mechanism Behind the Findings of Kim
Authors: Nav La, Schawanya K Rattanapitoon, Patpicha Arunsan, Nathkapach K Rattanapitoon
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

The study by Kim [1] provides a timely opportunity to reconsider how pediatric gastroenterologists interpret mucosal abnormalities in children with dyspepsia. Although the reported prevalence of abnormal endoscopic and histologic findings is high, what stands out even more is the persistent disconnect between gross and microscopic pathology. This pattern, also observed in large pediatric esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) series, such as that by Sheiko et al. [2], suggests that the pediatric gastric mucosa may not follow the linear injury–lesion model traditionally applied to adults. Instead, the observations presented by Kim may be better understood through a framework of early gastric mucosal remodeling, a concept that merits broader discussion.

In this interpretation, the coexistence of normal-appearing mucosa with microscopic inflammation is not simply a diagnostic discrepancy, but may represent a transitional phase in a dynamic inflammatory–reparative cycle. The pediatric gastric environment, shaped by an evolving diet, microbiome maturation, immune programming, and intermittent microbial exposure, likely undergoes microinjury and repair far more rapidly than adult gastric mucosa. Histological abnormalities, particularly nonspecific chronic gastritis, may therefore represent snapshots of this remodeling continuum rather than stable disease states. This hypothesis may also explain the unexpected detection of early atrophic signs in some children, which Kim linked to Helicobacter pylori infection. While the classic adult progression from H. pylori infection to intestinal metaplasia is well described [3], Kim’s findings raise the possibility that such remodeling may begin earlier in life and fluctuate before stabilizing into recognizable adult patterns.

Translationally, this reframing suggests that symptom–pathology relationships in children require different interpretive rules. Kim’s finding that melena and anemia strongly predicted mucosal lesions, while nausea predicted the absence of abnormalities, aligns with evidence from Ford et al. [4], showing that alarm features, not symptom severity, carry diagnostic weight. If pediatric mucosal remodeling is cyclical, symptom-based decision-making must emphasize red flags while recognizing that chronic dyspepsia without alarms may correspond to early-phase remodeling rather than an established structural disease.

This view also supports a refined risk stratification approach. Children with indications of bleeding, older age groups, and a family history suggestive of H. pylori clustering should be prioritized for early EGD with biopsy. Those with mixed or prolonged dyspeptic symptoms but no alarm signs remain in the intermediate-risk group, where endoscopy is justified, and biopsies remain essential. Isolated nausea without systemic features, consistent with Kim’s inverse association, should be approached conservatively because of the weak association with mucosal pathology. These principles align with the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy/European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition framework [5] but offer more pediatric-specific nuances.

Some methodological issues in Kim’s study deserve consideration: a single endoscopist may limit generalizability, histological overdiagnosis of chronic gastritis is common in pediatric pathology, and the absence of dietary or microbiome data limits the ability to interpret the biological context. Nonetheless, the observed patterns strongly indicate that the pediatric gastric mucosa may behave as a more adaptable and rapidly remodeling tissue than previously thought. Recognizing this would shift the conversation from detecting abnormalities to understanding where each child lies along the remodeling trajectory.

By viewing Kim’s findings within this mechanistic model, we hope to encourage future work to determine whether early gastric remodeling predicts long-term outcomes and whether targeted interventions—microbiome-directed, dietary, or H. pylori-focused—can modulate this trajectory before adulthood.
