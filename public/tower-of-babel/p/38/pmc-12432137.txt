Massive Cystic Nephroma in a 13-Month-Old Girl: Case Report and Differential Diagnostic Outcome*
Authors: Barkın Malkoç, Yusuf Doruk Bilgili, Filiz Kutlu, B. Haluk Güvenç
Published: 2025

Abdominal masses are common and important clinical problems during childhood.1 Renal neoplasms involve a wide range of tumors in the pediatric age group, mostly Wilms’ tumor, cystic partially differentiated nephroma (CPDN), cystic hamartoma, and cystic nephroma (CN), and may be determined to be either benign or malignant.2,3 Cystic nephroma is rare, benign, and cystic in structure and may be found in children and adults.2,4 Cystic nephromas are reported to reach massive sizes and present as resectable, well-bordered, soft, and non-invasive neoplasms at first sight. Still, they may also be prediagnosed as malignant cystic renal neoplasms. Nephrectomy is curative if the vascular, parenchymal, and mesenchymal borders are intact. Chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy is unnecessary under such circumstances.5

Here, we present a massive renal tumor measuring 16 cm in the long axis and extending from the right upper to the left lower quadrant. The differential diagnosis included a simple cyst of the kidney, unilateral autosomal dominant polycystic disease, partially differentiated cystic nephroblastoma, and a cystic Wilms' tumor. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s legal guardians who agreed to take part in the study.

A 13-month-old female prediagnosed with hepatomegaly at a local public hospital was admitted to our department. Her history revealed a progressively enlarging abdominal mass, which became stiffer each day. The only clinical complaint was bloody diarrhea, which occurred two or three times a day. On clinical examination, a painless, immobile, semisolid, right upper quadrant abdominal mass extending more than 10 cm was present (Figure 1A). The patient had no fever and urinary symptoms. We failed to clarify the presence of a congenital abnormality or malformation in the neonatal background.

Ultrasound revealed a multicystic neoplasm of unknown origin, 12 cm (the largest cyst being 4.5 cm) in diameter. Contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a multiloculated, thin septated multicystic neoplasm (16 cm in diameter) originating from the right kidney and extending to the left lower quadrant with no calcification or solid components. The inferior vena cava, small intestines, and large intestines were displaced by the mass (Figure 1B).

Diagnostic workup excluded Wilms’ tumor and other malignant renal neoplasms. A total right nephroureterectomy was performed via a supraumbilical transverse incision (Figure 1C). There was no evidence of invasion in surrounding tissues or any enlarged lymph nodes. The left kidney was normal in size and structure by palpation. The operation was completed without any complications. Postoperative urea and creatinine values were calculated within the normal range (29-7 mg/dL and 0.2-0.3 ng/dL, respectively) during the six-month follow-up. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed the non-existence of any renal or tumoral remnant on the right, and renal anatomy within normal limits on the left side at three years post-op.

According to gross pathological examination, the 18×15×10 cm-long nephrectomy material showed a 16.5 cm cystic tumor in total, with a normal inferior renal component 2.5 cm in diameter. The tumor encompassed multiple septated cysts, the largest of which was 2 cm in diameter without any solid component. The cystic septae exhibited a fibrous, hypocellular appearance with chronic inflammatory elements that rarely aggregate and differentiate into tubule structures. Wavy collagen bundles were absent. The cyst epithelium failed to show any epithelial complexity comparable to that of the branching glands, with a cribriform and hobnail appearance. The cyst stroma resembled an ovarian structure, and immunohistochemical estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) staining were positive (Figure 2).

Cystic nephroma was originally described by Edmunds in 1989 as a “cyst adenoma”.6 There are two peaks in incidence, one in childhood and the other in the adult population. As mostly described in the adult population, mixed epithelial and stromal tumor (MEST) is regarded as a similar entity mimicking CN. The latest edition of the classification of urogenital tumors (WHO “Blue Book”) includes pediatric CNs, classified as “MESTs.”7 However, MESTs are characterized by solid growth areas, stromal hypercellularity, or complex epithelial lesions, unlike the morphology of CNs.4,7,8,9 Adsay et al10 reported 12 patients whose pathological features were remarkably similar to those of CNs, with minor differences, identical to those of scar-like fibrous tissue and leiomyoma-like interlacing fascicles in the stromal component. The epithelial component also shared a similar structure with CN, where estrogen and PRs were detected immunohistochemically in some of the patients.10 Immunohistochemical profiles of 22 female patients revealed stromal reactions with ER protein antibodies in nine patients and epithelial reactions in five. Progesterone receptors were positively expressed in the spindle cell component in five patients and the epithelium in one.7 In our patient, the stromal element mimicked the ovarian stroma and reacted to the ER antibody immunohistochemically. Cystic spaces and cyst epithelium reacted to PanCK cells. Cystic septae had chronic inflammatory elements and differentiated tubule structures. Several authors shared the same histological and clinical features in obtaining a CN diagnosis.2,11-13 On the other hand, Blanco Parra et al11 suggested that there is increasing evidence that MESTs and CNs are the same disorder based on both immunohistochemical and genetic expression profile similarities.

Differential diagnoses include CPDN, unilateral polycystic disease of the kidney, medullary cystic kidney disease, diabetes syndrome/HNF1B-associated renal disease, cystic variant of mesoblastic nephroma, clear cell sarcoma, cystic degeneration in renal cell carcinoma and cystic Wilms’ tumor.5 Cystic partially differentiated nephroma contains blastemal and other embryonal cells in the cystic septae. Cystic variants of mesoblastic nephroma, such as leiomyoma, contain tubules surrounded by hypercellular mesoblastic spindle cells.1,3 In our case, the diagnostic workup excluded both Wilms’ tumor and other malignant renal neoplasms. The pathogenesis of CN in childhood is uncertain and was initially considered a unilateral and sporadic neoplasm. According to the literature, bilateral CN patients demonstrate more complicated clinical features, such as hematuria, hypertension, and recurrence, after several years.3,14 Recently, McGill University addressed the possible risk of anaplastic sarcoma of the kidney in patients with CN.12 Most of the CN cases in the English literature were unilateral, sporadic, and never recurred, similar to our case.11,13

It seems that the CN is the benign end of the pathologic spectrum of CPDNs and Wilms’ tumors, which are congenitally present.3 Our case highlights the clinical, radiological, and histological features and differential diagnosis of CN in children. Contemporary studies have reported DICER1 mutations as the underlying cause of a major genetic event in the development of CN.8,15 They hypothesized that CNs, such as pulmonary blastomas in the lung, represent a spectrum of abnormal renal organogenesis events with a risk for malignant transformation.15 The ethio-pathologic features of pediatric CNs, such as DICER1 mutations and familial syndromes, require further investigation.

Limitations

We were unable to perform DICER1 sequencing analysis in our patient.
