Prediction of the Dose and Dose-Rate of Ionizing Radiation that Provides the Greatest Biological Benefit
Authors: Katsuhito Kino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Because the existence of linear no-threshold (LNT) hypothesis in the low-dose region has not been confirmed, we previously proposed the “hormesis-coupled LNT theory.” Because this theory does not consider the effect of dose-rate, we applied our theory to different dose-rates using data from the previous reference (Yoshida et al. 2008). As a result, we calculated that the biological benefit via radiation can be observed at a dose-rate of <1 mGy/h. In the future, biologically beneficial factors may be identified by exploring the exposure region with cumulative doses of >350 mGy at this low-dose-rates.

Introduction

Ionizing radiation causes tumors, and the probability of cancer development is proportional to radiation at high doses. However, the linearity of biological effects with respect to radiation dose remains unclear at low doses. 1 Consequently, the linear no-threshold (LNT) hypothesis 2 and radiation hormesis 3 have been proposed for low-dose exposure. We previously proposed a theory that radiation risk is approximately proportional to high doses, whereas hormesis effects occur at low doses. 4 However, the “hormesis-coupled LNT theory” does not account for dose-rate. Therefore, by integrating our theory with a previous report, 5 we predicted the dose-rate and cumulative dose at which hormesis effects are most effective.

Prediction

In our previously proposed equation, (1) R=R0+ρE−kP(0)aEe−aE we substitute specific values from previous literature for R0 and ρ (For the definition of R, see the sentence after next.). Assuming that the exposure from the Hiroshima atomic bomb was instantaneous, EH = 101 mGy at a dose-rate of 100 Gy/h is reasonable. The Ministry of the Environment’s view, based on Hiroshima atomic bomb data, 6 is that R0 = 30% and that the probability of cancer (R) increases by 0.5% per 100 mGy, so ρ = 5 × 10−3%/mGy. This value applies to high dose-rates (>0.42 Gy/h). The dose-rate effect is known, where high dose-rate irradiation has greater biological effects than low dose-rate irradiation. 7 This previous study 7 reported that the slope at 0.0007−0.8 R/min (3.7 × 10−4 − 0.42 Gy/h) is 0.334 times that at 72–90 R/min (36–47 Gy/h). Thus, we assumed that ρ at low dose-rates (≤0.42 Gy/h) is 0.00167%/mGy.

For instance, for 0.52 mGy/h, using EL = 81 mGy and EH = 1780 mGy, a and kP(0) in equation (1) are calculated as follows. When the increase in R when exposed to 1780 mGy (which is 0.00167 × 1780% = 2.97%) is considered an error, the R’s range between R(81) = 27.03% and R(1780) = 30% is considered “noneffect.” Substituting the values of EL and EH into the two R equations, we obtain a = 0.00185 and kP(0) = 24.1, yielding the following equation: R=30+0.00167E−0.0445Ee−0.00185E

Graphically calculating the minimum value of this equation, we obtain R = 22.0% when E = 490 mGy, indicating a 5.0% hormesis effect based on R(81). Table 2 illustrates that at high dose-rates, the benefit from R (%) at EL is less than 1%; thus, even if a hormesis mechanism exists, the beneficial effect on living organisms would be virtually unobservable. Conversely, at low dose-rates, if a hormesis effect of >3% (R0 × 0.1) was achieved and the population is large enough, the phenomenon of biological benefit would be observable. In particular, the biological benefit via radiation can be observed at a dose-rate of <1 mGy/h, which is partially consistent with the theory of another study. 8 As a future direction, biologically beneficial factors may be identified by exploring the exposure region with cumulative doses of >350 mGy at dose-rates of <1 mGy/h.
