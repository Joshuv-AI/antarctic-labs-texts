Comparison of hedonic hunger and intuitive eating status between pregnant and non-pregnant women
Authors: Merve Lazoğlu, Sibel Ejder Tekgündüz, Serap Ejder Apay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare hedonic hunger and intuitive eating status between pregnant and non-pregnant individuals. The research was conducted with 722 participants (357 pregnant and 365 non-pregnant) in different units of Kars Harakani State Hospital. Data were collected using a Personal Information Form prepared by the researcher, the Power of Food Scale, and the Intuitive Eating Scale. Data were obtained through face-to-face interviews and analyzed using appropriate statistical methods in the SPSS software. No significant difference was found between pregnant and non-pregnant women in terms of socio-demographic characteristics. The total Power of Food Scale score was 3.01 ± 0.89 for pregnant women and 3.28 ± 0.88 for non-pregnant women, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The total Intuitive Eating Scale score was 3.26 ± 0.61 for pregnant women and 3.34 ± 0.55 for non-pregnant women, and this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.070). Regression analysis showed that, among pregnant women, the total Intuitive Eating Scale score was not a significant predictor of the hedonic hunger score (R = 0.083, R²=0.007, p > 0.05), suggesting that intuitive eating behavior alone does not account for hedonic hunger during pregnancy and that other physiological or psychosocial factors may play a more prominent role in this population. Among non-pregnant women, however, the total Intuitive Eating Scale score was a significant negative predictor of the hedonic hunger score (R = 0.132, R²=0.018, p < 0.05), indicating that higher levels of intuitive eating were associated with lower hedonic hunger. This finding suggests that women who are more attuned to their internal hunger and satiety cues may be better protected against food environment-driven, pleasure-based eating beyond metabolic need — a clinically relevant insight for nutrition counseling and weight management interventions targeting non-pregnant women. Hedonic hunger was higher in the non-pregnant group compared to the pregnant group, while intuitive eating behavior was similar between the two groups.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a unique process in which significant biopsychosocial changes occur in all body systems of women1. This period represents a critical physiological and psychosocial transition in a woman’s reproductive life course. It involves multiple physical, emotional, hormonal, and social changes, ranging from changes in physical activity to changes in eating habits2. In their study, Kuga et al. reported that pregnant women have higher taste thresholds than non-pregnant women and that a marked decrease in taste function is particularly noticeable during the first trimester3. They stated that this decrease in taste function during the first trimester is due to hormonal changes occurring at this stage. All these changes may lead to changes in lifestyle behaviors and eating habits4.

In today’s world, food consumption is no longer driven solely by physiological hunger; individuals may consume food for various reasons, even in the absence of metabolic hunger and despite having abundant fat reserves5. This condition, also known as hedonic hunger, is defined as a type of hunger characterized by the desire for food due to its taste, smell, and other sensory properties, increased appetite, and the pleasure obtained while eating, even when the body has no physiological need for energy6. Hedonic hunger leads to an excessive desire for palatable foods by overriding the body’s physiological balance, even when energy stores are sufficient. Changes in energy intake, along with the widespread availability of energy-dense foods in large portions and quantities, stimulate “hedonic” food intake unrelated to the body’s needs. These factors can override internal regulatory systems and disrupt body weight balance7,8. Many factors influence hedonic hunger, including impulsivity, existing eating habits, excessive food cravings, self-esteem, food availability, age, and sex8.

In addition, the increased caloric requirement during pregnancy may also promote intuitive eating. Intuitive eating is a way of eating that relies on the body’s natural hunger and satiety signals rather than on emotional or situational eating. In intuitive eating, individuals consume an adequate amount of food in response to physiological hunger and stop eating before becoming overly full; thus, the mind, body, and food are in balance9. Tribole and Resch stated that this method serves as a bridge between a non-diet approach and traditional health approaches that include dieting, helping individuals reach their ideal body weight10. As an alternative to restrictive eating patterns or diets, intuitive eating encourages individuals to consume energy only when they are physiologically hungry. It focuses on allowing people to eat what they desire, to listen to the body’s needs, to avoid eating due to emotional or environmental reasons, and to attend to natural satiety cues11. Intuitive eating behavior has been reported to play an important role in body weight control. During pregnancy, intuitive eating involves eating in response to physiological hunger and fullness signals rather than to emotional changes or strict dietary rules12.

During pregnancy, daily energy requirements and nutrient needs increase to support the healthy growth and development of the fetus and to ensure adequate, balanced nutrition for the mother13. Women who experience excessive weight gain or obesity during pregnancy face numerous problems, including gestational diabetes, hypertension, preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, postpartum weight retention, and difficulties initiating and maintaining breastfeeding14–16. Moreover, inadequate intake of essential macro- and micronutrients by the mother can seriously affect the baby’s health in both the short and long term17. Infants born to overweight or obese mothers are also at increased risk for stillbirth, congenital anomalies, fetal macrosomia, and obesity in childhood and adolescence18,19. Excessive gestational weight gain has been associated not only with maternal and child health problems but also with an increased likelihood of obesity in both mothers and children in the postpartum period20.

Overall, eating habits during pregnancy and related weight status are critically important for both maternal and infant health. In light of this information, this study was conducted to determine and compare hedonic hunger and intuitive eating status among pregnant and non-pregnant women.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a descriptive and comparative design. The study was conducted between 01.05.2023 and 30.12.2024 in different units of Kars Harakani State Hospital (Pregnant group: Pregnancy Counseling Outpatient Clinic and Obstetrics and Gynecology Outpatient Clinic; Non-pregnant group: women who applied to the hospital). The study population consisted of pregnant and non-pregnant women who applied to Kars Harakani State Hospital between 01.04.2023 and 01.12.2024. A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1) for an independent samples t-test, based on the observed group means and standard deviations (pregnant group: 3.01 ± 0.89; non-pregnant group: 3.28 ± 0.88), a significance level of α = 0.05, and the achieved sample sizes (n₁ = 357, n₂ = 365). The analysis yielded a statistical power of 0.95, indicating that the total sample of 722 participants provided more than adequate power to detect the observed between-group differences. The sample consisted of 357 pregnant and 365 non-pregnant women who met the selection criteria and were selected through convenience sampling.

Data collection tools

The Personal Information Form prepared by the researchers, the Power of Food Scale, and the Intuitive Eating Scale were used to collect the research data.

Personal Information Form This form was prepared by the researcher and comprised 11 questions to determine the socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics of pregnant and non-pregnant individuals.

Power of Food Scale (PFS) Developed by Cappelleri et al. in 2009 and adapted into Turkish by Hayzaran et al. in 2018, this 15-item scale uses a 5-point Likert-type response format21,22. It was developed to assess hedonic hunger. The scale has three subfactors: Food Availability, Food Presence, and Food Tasting. All items are scored on a scale of 1 to 5. The total score is divided by the number of items to obtain the mean score. Higher scores indicate a greater psychological impact of the food environment on individuals (i.e., higher hedonic hunger). The scale is evaluated out of 5 points, and an average score above 2.5 indicates the presence of hedonic hunger and sensitivity to food. The reliability coefficients for the subfactors range from 0.81 to 0.91. The overall Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.8522. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.72 for Food Availability, 0.84 for Food Presence, 0.86 for Food Tasting, and 0.91 for the total scale.

Intuitive Eating Scale (IES) The first intuitive eating scale was developed by Hawk et al. in 200423. The original scale was later developed by Tylka in 2006 as a 21-item instrument with three subdimensions24. Tylka and Kroon Van Diest subsequently revised it into the Intuitive Eating Scale-2 (IES-2), consisting of 23 items and four subdimensions: Unconditional Permission to Eat, Eating for Physical Rather Than Emotional Reasons, Reliance on Hunger and Satiety Cues, and Body-Food Choice Congruence25. The scale was adapted into Turkish by Baş et al. (Cronbach’s α = 0.82)26. The Intuitive Eating Scale is a 5-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging from “strongly disagree”1 to “strongly agree”5. Items 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, and 10 are reverse scored. The total score is calculated by dividing the sum of item scores by the number of items. Subscale scores are also calculated separately by dividing the sum of relevant items by the number of items in each subscale. Higher scores indicate stronger intuitive eating behavior26. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.56 for Unconditional Permission to Eat, 0.61 for Eating for Physical Reasons, 0.93 for Reliance on Hunger and Satiety Cues, 0.91 for Body-Food Choice Congruence, and 0.80 for the total scale.

Data collection

Data were collected by the researcher using a face-to-face interview technique at times convenient for the participants. Before data collection, participants were informed about the study and provided verbal consent. Subsequently, the Personal Information Form prepared in accordance with the literature was administered, followed by the PFS and IES. Completing the forms took approximately 10–15 min.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0. Descriptive statistics (number, percentage, mean, standard deviation), chi-square test, Pearson correlation, and regression analysis were used. Results were evaluated at a 95% confidence interval, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Table 4 presents the results of the simple linear regression analysis examining the predictive effect of intuitive eating on hedonic hunger. The analysis revealed that intuitive eating was not a significant predictor of hedonic hunger among pregnant women (β = 0.089, p = 0.11). However, among non-pregnant women, intuitive eating significantly predicted hedonic hunger in a negative direction (β = −0.159, p = 0.011), indicating that women with higher intuitive eating scores tended to report lower levels of hedonic hunger. Therefore, intuitive eating appears to function as a protective factor against hedonic hunger in non-pregnant women, whereas this relationship was not evident during pregnancy.

Discussion

During pregnancy, dietary patterns can affect both maternal and fetal health in the short- and long-term17. Therefore, pregnant women need to develop healthy eating habits. This study aimed to evaluate hedonic hunger and intuitive eating status among pregnant and non-pregnant women. In Table 1, the descriptive characteristics of the groups were compared, and the groups were found to be homogeneous.

When the total scores on the Power of Food Scale in Table 2 are examined, both groups show high levels of hedonic hunger. However, hedonic hunger is significantly higher in the non-pregnant group compared to the pregnant group. Many environmental and external factors may trigger hedonic hunger and increase food consumption, including portion size, food salience, social aspects of food, lifestyle, peer influence, number of meals consumed, meal duration, physical activity, sleep, and food cravings. Various eating environments, such as lighting, smell, ambiance, noise, and music, may also influence immediate food consumption. In the study by Mankad and Devaki, hedonic hunger was more common among women (24.5%) than among men (21.4%), although the difference was not statistically significant27. Similarly, Ewoldt reported that hedonic hunger is associated with gender and is more prevalent in women than in men28. Evidence from studies conducted with non-pregnant samples suggests that higher levels of hedonic hunger (i.e., focus on and desire for food for pleasure in the absence of physical hunger), particularly when combined with higher impulsivity, may contribute to greater consumption of ultra-processed foods29. This finding is consistent with our results. Further research is needed to investigate the mechanisms underlying the relationship between hedonic hunger and pregnancy. In addition to individual eating behaviors, cultural dietary habits and social norms during pregnancy may also contribute to the observed differences in hedonic hunger. In many cultures, pregnancy is accompanied by increased social support and encouragement to eat more, often based on the belief that pregnant women should “eat for two” to support fetal growth. Family members and close relatives may actively influence food choices and meal patterns, leading pregnant women to prioritize nutritional adequacy and fetal well-being over pleasure-driven eating. Furthermore, traditional dietary recommendations, food taboos, and culturally accepted pregnancy-related eating practices may modify food preferences and eating motivations during pregnancy. These social and cultural influences may partially explain why pregnant women in the present study reported lower levels of hedonic hunger despite experiencing increased physiological and nutritional demands. Future studies should further explore how cultural context and social eating environments shape hedonic hunger and intuitive eating behaviors during pregnancy.

When the mean scores of the Intuitive Eating Scale in Table 2 are examined, both groups demonstrate similarly high levels of intuitive eating behavior. In a study by Nogué et al., women also exhibited similar levels of intuitive eating behavior30. Likewise, a study by Yamamiya et al. among Japanese women reported intuitive eating levels similar to those observed in our study31.

In Table 3, a significant relationship is observed between hedonic hunger in pregnant women and the “Eating for Physical Reasons” subdimension of intuitive eating. The literature indicates that food intake is regulated by homeostatic and hedonic systems, with the homeostatic system sending peripheral endocrine and metabolic signals to the hypothalamus and brainstem nuclei, depending on the body’s energy resources and needs32,33. This suggests that hedonic hunger in pregnant women may be associated with these systems. Further research on this topic is needed. Similarly, the significant relationship between hedonic hunger and eating for physical reasons in women may reflect the regulation of food intake by these physiological systems. In addition, the positive relationship between the Food Presence subdimension and Eating for Physical Reasons among pregnant women may be explained by the fact that available foods act as stimuli that trigger appetite, particularly during pregnancy, a period characterized by pronounced physiological changes and fluctuating appetite. The weak but positive relationship between the Food Presence subdimension and intuitive eating subdimensions suggests that food availability may influence eating behavior. However, strong body awareness may prevent unnecessary eating driven solely by the presence of appealing foods. The positive relationship between eating for physical reasons and intuitive eating indicates that greater body awareness among pregnant women is associated with a greater alignment of nutrition with the body’s physical needs.

In the non-pregnant group, the positive relationship between food availability and eating for physical reasons indicates that available foods in the environment may attract women and stimulate the desire to eat beyond metabolic needs. The higher level of hedonic hunger in the non-pregnant group may be due to pregnant women adopting more protective eating behaviors to safeguard both their own health and that of the fetus during pregnancy. Jouppi and Levine reported that decreased consumption of ultra-processed foods and reduced hedonic hunger levels during pregnancy may act as protective factors, consistent with the present study’s findings34. Furthermore, the weak relationships between food presence and other subdimensions in the non-pregnant group may indicate that non-pregnant women listen less to their bodies and are more susceptible to factors that encourage eating beyond metabolic needs.

The finding that intuitive eating was not a significant predictor of hedonic hunger in pregnant women suggests that many other factors may contribute to eating beyond metabolic needs. Physiological and psychological changes during pregnancy, as well as social perceptions that pregnant women should eat whatever they desire, may play an important role.

In contrast, the finding that intuitive eating predicts hedonic hunger in non-pregnant women indicates that listening to the body’s needs and requirements is an important factor in preventing eating beyond metabolic needs. Intuitive eating—being in harmony with one’s body and responding to internal cues—may serve as a protective factor against many triggers of hedonic hunger, such as snacking, easy access to appealing foods, individual differences, and frequent exposure to palatable foods. Women who are in tune with their bodies tend to eat in line with their physiological needs and may be better protected against factors that could lead to unhealthy eating behaviors.

Conclusion and recommendations

In conclusion, although hedonic hunger was higher in the non-pregnant group than in the pregnant group, intuitive eating behavior was similar in both groups. Further research is needed to determine which factors influence nutrition among pregnant women, which mechanisms of hedonic hunger are affected during pregnancy, and the applicability of intuitive eating during pregnancy. Collaboration with pregnancy education programs or maternity schools could help provide more comprehensive education on healthy nutrition, eating patterns, and appropriate eating behaviors for pregnant women. Informational brochures and booklets explaining that proper nutrition and dietary patterns are among the most critical determinants of both maternal and fetal health could also be prepared and distributed.

Limitations of the study

The generalizability of the findings is limited to the population and setting in which the study was conducted. The presence of the researcher during face-to-face data collection may have consciously or unconsciously influenced the responses of some participants. Furthermore, the relatively low Cronbach’s alpha coefficients obtained for two subscales of the Intuitive Eating Scale-2 — namely Unconditional Permission to Eat (α = 0.56) and Eating for Physical Reasons (α = 0.61) — represent an additional limitation of this study. Although the total scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.80), the suboptimal reliability of these two subscales may have attenuated the precision of subscale-level estimates and should be considered when interpreting the corresponding findings. Future studies are encouraged to evaluate the psychometric performance of these subscales specifically within pregnant populations.
