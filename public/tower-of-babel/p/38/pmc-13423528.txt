Short Segment Fixation with Inclusion of the Fractured Vertebra versus Long Segment Fixation for the Treatment of Thoracolumbar Fractures: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Nikhil Dhage, Vikas C. Jha, Rahul Jain, Anand K. Das, Vivek S. Sinha, Nitish Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThoracolumbar fractures often necessitate surgical stabilization to prevent instability and kyphotic deformity. Long-segment posterior fixation (LSF) provides robust mechanical support, but at the expense of increased surgical morbidity and reduced spinal mobility. Short-segment fixation with inclusion of the fractured level (SSFIFL) has emerged as a motion-preserving alternative with the potential for comparable outcomes. This study compares clinical and radiological outcomes between SSFIFL and LSF in patients with unstable thoracolumbar fractures.Materials and Methods In this prospective randomized controlled trial, 54 patients with single-level unstable thoracolumbar fractures (T11–L2) were assigned to either LSF ( n = 27) or SSFIFL ( n = 27). Neurological function (ASIA grade, MRC scale) and radiological parameters (local kyphotic angle [LKA], anterior and posterior vertebral body heights) were evaluated preoperatively, postoperatively, and at 3 and 6 months. Operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, hospital stay, and complications were also assessed. Results Both groups showed significant postoperative correction of LKA ( p < 0.001), with no significant intergroup difference. LSF achieved superior vertebral height restoration ( p < 0.001). Neurological improvement was observed in both groups, with comparable progression of ASIA grades. SSFIFL had advantages in operative time ( p = 0.012), blood loss ( p = 0.001), and hospital stay ( p = 0.001). Complication rates were slightly lower in the SSFIFL group, with no implant failures observed. ConclusionSSFIFL offers clinical and radiological outcomes comparable to LSF while reducing operative morbidity. It is a safe, effective, and motion-preserving alternative for select thoracolumbar fractures with intact pedicle support.

Introduction

Thoracolumbar spine fractures are among the most common spinal injuries and are often associated with significant neurological deficits, spinal instability, and progressive deformity. 1 More than half of these injuries are classified as unstable and demand surgical intervention to prevent long-term disability and kyphotic deformity. 2 The thoracolumbar junction (TLJ), encompassing T11 to L2, represents a transitional zone between the relatively rigid thoracic spine and the more mobile lumbar region. This unique anatomical and biomechanical configuration renders it highly susceptible to injury under axial and flexion-compression forces. 3

Historically, conservative management with external immobilization was the mainstay of treatment; however, the evolution of surgical techniques and growing clinical experience have led to the development of standardized operative strategies aimed at restoring spinal stability and optimizing neurological outcomes. 4 The introduction of pedicle screw fixation by Roy–Camille in the 1970s revolutionized the surgical management of thoracolumbar fractures by enabling three-column fixation and rigid segmental stabilization. 5

Long-segment posterior fixation (LSF), which involves instrumentation at two levels above and below the fracture site, has traditionally been favored due to its superior biomechanical stability and reduced stress per screw. However, it is also associated with increased operative time, greater blood loss, reduced postoperative spinal mobility, and a higher incidence of adjacent segment degeneration and chronic back pain. 6

In contrast, short-segment fixation (SSF), which stabilizes one vertebra above and below the fracture, is a motion-preserving technique. Although biomechanically less rigid, it is associated with decreased surgical morbidity. Yet, concerns remain regarding implant failure, progressive kyphosis, and loss of correction, particularly in fractures with significant comminution or instability. 7 In response to these limitations, Dick et al introduced a modified short-segment technique in 1994, incorporating pedicle screws at the fractured vertebra itself (also known as SSF with intermediate fracture level fixation or SSFIFL), thereby providing enhanced stability while preserving segmental motion. 8

In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether short-segment fixation, including the fractured level (SSFIFL), yields comparable, if not superior, clinical and radiological outcomes compared with LSF in patients with unstable thoracolumbar burst fractures, while minimizing implant-related complications and preserving spinal motion. This study aims to compare these two surgical techniques in terms of operative parameters, complication rates, postoperative neurological recovery, radiographic outcomes, and implant integrity.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This prospective, randomized comparative study was conducted at the Department of Neurosurgery at a tertiary care center hospital between February 2023 and August 2024. A total of 54 patients presenting with unstable thoracolumbar burst fractures were enrolled after obtaining institutional ethical clearance and informed consent.

Patients were randomly assigned using a computer-generated randomization table into two treatment groups:

Group A ( n = 27): underwent long-segment posterior transpedicular screw fixation (two levels above and below the fracture).

Group A ( n = 27): underwent long-segment posterior transpedicular screw fixation (two levels above and below the fracture).

Group B ( n = 27): underwent short-segment posterior transpedicular screw fixation, including instrumentation of the fractured vertebra.

Group B ( n = 27): underwent short-segment posterior transpedicular screw fixation, including instrumentation of the fractured vertebra.

All parameters were documented at four time points: preoperatively, immediately postoperatively, at 3 months, and at 6 months postsurgery.

Surgical Technique

All patients underwent surgery via a standard posterior midline approach under general anesthesia and intraoperative fluoroscopic guidance. In Group A, transpedicular screws were placed two levels above and below the fractured vertebra. In Group B, screws were inserted one level above, one level below, and into the pedicle(s) of the fractured vertebra if at least one pedicle was intact.

Where indicated, posterior spinal canal decompression was performed using a laminectomy at the fracture level. Appropriately contoured titanium rods were fixed to restore sagittal alignment based on thoracic kyphosis or lumbar lordosis. Autologous bone grafts harvested during decompression were placed in the lateral gutters to facilitate intertransverse fusion.

Postoperative Protocol

Patients were mobilized with thoracolumbar orthoses on postoperative day 2 or 3, depending on their neurological status. Routine follow-up was scheduled at 3 and 6 months with clinical evaluation and radiological imaging to assess maintenance of reduction, hardware integrity, and fusion progression.

8. ComplicationsIn our study, four patients experienced intraoperative dural injury, with an equal distribution across both groups—two patients each in the LSF and SSFIFL cohorts. Postoperatively, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage necessitated prolonged drain placement in two patients, and one patient underwent re-exploration for CSF leak repair.Wound-related complications were also documented. Three patients in the LSF group and one in the SSFIFL group developed superficial wound infections requiring partial suture removal for drainage, followed by secondary suturing. Additionally, three patients in the LSF group and two in the SSFIFL group developed wound discharge, necessitating repeated suturing and thereby prolonging postoperative hospitalization. Neurological deterioration was observed in two patients from each group in the immediate postoperative period; however, subsequent clinical improvement was noted in all cases. Importantly, no cases of implant failure—defined as construct bending, breakage, loosening, or screw pullout—were observed in either group. ( Table 5 )

Discussion

Rationale and biomechanical considerations: TLJ fractures constitute a substantial portion of spinal injuries, comprising over 54% of all spinal fractures. This vulnerability is attributable to the anatomical transition zone between the rigid thoracic and the more mobile lumbar spine, predisposing it to injury during high-energy trauma. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 The optimal surgical stabilization technique remains contentious, with traditional LSF being historically preferred due to its mechanical robustness. However, LSF is associated with increased operative duration, greater blood loss, higher implant costs, and unnecessary immobilization of adjacent motion segments. Recent advancements in fixation strategies have favored SSF, especially with the inclusion of the fractured vertebral level (SSFIFL), which aims to preserve motion segments while maintaining adequate stability. Dick et al were pioneers in advocating the inclusion of the index vertebra, demonstrating that intermediate screw placement enhances construct integrity, reduces stress on adjacent pedicle screws, and better preserves sagittal alignment. 8 Subsequent biomechanical investigations have substantiated these findings. Mahar et al confirmed through cadaveric studies that the inclusion of a pedicle screw at the fracture level improved construct stiffness under axial torsion and provided superior resistance to anterior loading. 9 Guven et al reported improved intraoperative kyphosis correction and better postoperative alignment maintenance when intermediate screws were used. 10 Pedicle screws placed at the fracture site function as fulcrums, enabling restoration of vertebral body height and improved load-sharing. Theoretical advantages include reduced postoperative vertebral collapse and enhanced kyphosis correction. 10 11 12 Thus, SSFIFL offers a biomechanically viable and functionally sparing alternative to LSF, particularly in cases where pedicle integrity is preserved, aligning with modern principles of motion preservation and minimally invasive surgery. 10 11 12 13

Sample size and demographic context: our study enrolled fewer patients compared with some previously published series, mainly due to a shorter accrual period. Nevertheless, the cohort size remains comparable to or larger than several similar studies. Sallam et al 13 reported 11 patients, Guven et al 10 included 72, and Al Mamun Choudhury et al 14 studied 80 patients. El-Sharkawi et al 15 analyzed 50 patients (16 per arm), while Sanderson et al 16 examined 60 patients, predominantly male, with mean ages of 49.23 and 52.63 years in the LSF and SSF groups, respectively. In contrast, Al Mamun Choudhury et al 14 studied a younger cohort (mean age: 32.7 years), and Nagaty et al 17 reported a mean age of 38 ± 9.2 years among 61 patients. The mean age in our study was higher than in several series, likely reflecting regional differences in trauma epidemiology and referral patterns. Nonetheless, the present study offers robust comparative insight into the clinical utility of LSF and SSFIFL.

El-Sharkawi et al 15 analyzed 50 patients (16 per arm), while Sanderson et al 16 examined 60 patients, predominantly male, with mean ages of 49.23 and 52.63 years in the LSF and SSF groups, respectively.

In contrast, Al Mamun Choudhury et al 14 studied a younger cohort (mean age: 32.7 years), and Nagaty et al 17 reported a mean age of 38 ± 9.2 years among 61 patients. The mean age in our study was higher than in several series, likely reflecting regional differences in trauma epidemiology and referral patterns. Nonetheless, the present study offers robust comparative insight into the clinical utility of LSF and SSFIFL.

Clinical outcomes: neurological outcomes, assessed via the ASIA Impairment Scale and MRC grading, showed significant postoperative improvement in both groups, with no neurological deterioration noted. These outcomes mirror the findings of Al Mamun Choudhury et al, 14 Hamdan et al, 18 and Nagaty et al, 17 all of whom reported no statistically significant differences in neurological recovery between LSF and SSFIFL. While some authors suggested slightly faster early recovery in SSFIFL due to less extensive dissection, long-term functional outcomes remain broadly equivalent.

SSFIFL is best suited for fractures with at least one intact pedicle at the fractured vertebra, commonly seen in compression or mild burst fractures. Conclusion

SSFIFL achieves equivalent, if not superior, outcomes compared with LSF in appropriately selected thoracolumbar fractures. It offers advantages in terms of reduced operative time, lower intraoperative blood loss, fewer complications, and shorter hospital stays, without compromising radiological or neurological outcomes. Given these benefits, SSFIFL emerges as a pragmatic and efficient surgical option.
