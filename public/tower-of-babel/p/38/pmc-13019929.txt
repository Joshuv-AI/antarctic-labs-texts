Congenital aromatase deficiency in an adult male: A case report of clinical course and symptom reversal with estrogen supplementation
Authors: Peyton Coady, Arnaav Walia, Beatriz Hernandez, David E. Hinojosa-Gonzalez, Adnan El-Achkar, Mohit Khera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital aromatase deficiency is an autosomal recessive CYP19A1 loss-of-function disorder that blocks conversion of androgens to estrogens. A man with homozygous CYP19A1 c.628G > A (exon 5) presented with longstanding fatigue, low libido, and desire to maintain fertility. Physiologic estrogen replacement was started with transdermal estradiol 0.0375 mg. After three months of estrogen therapy, the patient reported markedly improved energy and libido. Follow-up evaluation showed a rise in serum estradiol with normalization of gonadotropins, while maintaining spermatogenesis. This case illustrates the essential role of estrogen in male physiology, sexual function, and fertility. Estradiol replacement can alleviate hypoestrogenic symptoms with preservation of spermatogenesis.

Introduction

Congenital aromatase deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in CYP19A1, which encodes the aromatase, the enzyme responsible for converting androgens (testosterone, androstenedione) to estrogens (estradiol, estrone). Since the first case description in 1991, fewer than 50 male cases have been reported worldwide. The condition typically manifests in 46,XX individuals with maternal virilization during pregnancy, and later during childhood with primary amenorrhea, delayed puberty, and polycystic ovarian morphology.1

In males, the phenotype can be more subtle, presenting in early adulthood often leading to delays in recognition. Key clinical features include eunuchoid tall stature with unfused epiphyses, reduced bone mineral density, insulin resistance, lipid abnormalities, and infertility due to estrogen deficiency.1 While testosterone is the primary sex hormone in males, estrogen exerts considerable influence. Estrogen regulates reproductive physiology via negative feedback of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis and local modulation on the testes, such as through aiding fluid reabsorption in the epididymis/efferent ductules. Published cases have shown estrogen supplementation can improve bone density, close epiphyses, and reduce metabolic dysfunction in this population, while the effects on libido and fertility remain poorly described.2,3

To address this gap, we present the case of a male with congenital aromatase deficiency due to a homozygous missense mutation in CYP19A1 (c.628G > A in exon 5) who presented with fatigue, decreased libido, and desired future fertility. His symptoms improved following 0.0375 mg transdermal estradiol therapy with continued spermatogenesis at 3 months, underscoring the role of estrogen in male reproductive physiology and the benefits of physiologic supplementation in managing this rare condition.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old male with a known history of congenital aromatase deficiency due to a homozygous missense mutation in CYP19A1 (c.628G > A in exon 5) presented to our outpatient urology clinic with complaints of significant fatigue, low libido, and desire for future fertility. He reported significant fatigue upon waking and throughout the course of the day. On examination he was well appearing, notable for markedly tall stature.

He endorsed prior transdermal estrogen use for bone health about 8 years earlier, which was later discontinued for unknown reasons, and he is not currently on any form of hormone replacement therapy. His past medical history included diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and osteopenia managed by Endocrinology. Surgical history was unremarkable. Physical examination was unremarkable with a normal genital examination without varicocele.

Family history was remarkable for heterozygous missense mutations in CYP19A1 c.628G > A in exon 5 in the mother and father, as well as homozygous missense mutations in c.628G > A in both sisters. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated normal testosterone (607 ng/dL), very low estradiol (3.0 pg/mL), and high normal FSH (10.77 mIU/mL) and LH (6.94 mIU/mL). Semen analysis showed normal volume (2.5 mL), concentration (34.4 million/mL), with 7% normal forms. The patient was started on physiologic replacement of estrogen with 0.0375 mg transdermal estrogen applied twice weekly, with the goal of relieving hypoestrogenic symptoms while preserving fertility. At the three month follow up visit, the patient reported significant improvements in overall energy and libido. Follow-up lab tests showed that his serum estradiol increased from 3.0 to 20.5 pg/mL, FSH decreased from 10.77 to 3.90 mIU/mL, and LH decreased from 6.94 to 2.44 mIU/mL. His total testosterone decreased from 607 to 243 ng/dL (with free testosterone 54.9 pg/mL and SHBG 25.6 nmol/L at three months), which was consistent with restored estradiol-mediated negative feedback. Repeat semen analysis at three months showed sperm concentration within the laboratory reference range (19.6 million/mL) improved motility (45%), increased normal forms (11%), while total sperm number and total motile count fell below the lab reference range, which may reflect semen variability or volume effects due to a reduced ejaculate volume.

The patient was continued to be followed and actively monitored through serial hormone panels with estrogen dose titration as needed, semen cryopreservation and periodic semen analysis to ensure persistence of spermatogenesis, with continued resolution of hypoestrogenic symptoms at 6 month follow up.

Discussion

This case illustrates the reproductive and endocrine features of a man with congenital aromatase deficiency, and the beneficial impact of treatment with physiologic estradiol replacement on patient reported symptoms and hormonal profile. Few reports have included serial semen monitoring while supplementing estrogen in a man with aromatase deficiency.2,4 At baseline he had estrogen deficiency with high-normal gonadotropins, normal testosterone, and semen parameters that showed ongoing spermatogenesis. After three months of estradiol therapy, his symptoms significantly improved, estradiol improved, and gonadotropins normalized, accompanied by a decrease in total testosterone while free testosterone remained in the normal range. Semen parameters remained compatible with ongoing spermatogenesis, though total sperm number and total motile count decreased at 3 months. These changes reflect estrogen's role in the HPG axis in men as well as reproductive physiology.

In male physiology, pulsatile secretion of GnRH from the hypothalamus signals the pituitary to release LH and FSH. LH acts on Leydig cells to drive production of testosterone, a portion of which is aromatized to estrogen via CYP19A1. While FSH acts on Sertoli cells to stimulate spermatogenesis. Estradiol and testosterone provide negative feedback to the hypothalamus to regulate gonadotropin secretion, with estradiol in part through acting upon arcuate nucleus to mediate negative feedback via kisspeptin regulation.5 In our patient, the near absence of estradiol removed the negative inhibition resulting in elevated FSH and LH, with supplementation restoring this negative feedback as evidenced by the corresponding decline in testosterone at the three month follow up. This case demonstrates resolution of hypogonadal symptoms (despite the patient having normal testosterone levels), including fatigue and low libido, with restoration of physiologic estradiol levels. Typically, hypoestrogenism is due to primary or secondary hypogonadism but it can also be caused by iatrogenic causes such as through aromatase inhibitor therapy, during androgen deprivation therapy, or following bilateral orchiectomy.6 These causes share features with many of the symptoms in congenital aromatase deficiency such as insulin resistance, decreased libido, fatigue, and reduced bone mineral density.7 One study found that the effect of estrogen deficiency on libido in men is most prominent when less than 10 pg/mL with 31% decrease sexual desire scores compared to only 13% above 10 pg/mL as was the initial laboratory finding in our patient.6 Estrogen systemic effects within the endothelium, via ERα and ERβ receptors stimulation of nitric oxide synthase, have also been posited to play a role in erectile function.7

Semen quality in congenital aromatase deficiency displays wide phenotypic variation, ranging from azoospermia to normal parameters. One of the key considerations when supplementing estrogen is that either estrogen excess or estrogen deficiency can negatively impact fertility, which necessitates the need for careful dosing with low dose estrogen chosen to avoid this dose dependent complication. Low levels of Estrogen can impair fertility due to effects on the epididymis and efferent ducts, where estrogen increases fluid reabsorption and promotes sperm maturation. High levels exhibit suppression of the HPG axis through inhibitory endocrine feedback.8 Short term supplementation with low dose estrogen (20 μg/d) has been previously shown to not impair spermatogenesis, although concerns arise regarding periods of estrogen if continued longer periods.9 Supplementation to low physiologic levels has not been comprehensively characterized, one study using histologic evaluation at the time of orchiectomy in transgender populations demonstrated the length of gender-affirming hormone therapy (Estrogen and spironolactone in this study) does not increase the likelihood of decreased spermatogenesis.10 Overall, the decrease in total sperm number and total motile count is most consistent with reduced ejaculate volume and normal biologic variability. A potential contributor is partial negative feedback on the HPG axis after estradiol replacement, as well as a reduction in intratesticular testosterone due to the decrease in total testosterone. Although short-term low-dose estradiol therapy did not show clinically meaningful impairment, it is still important to advise patients prior to starting supplementation on potential long term deleterious effects on fertility.

This case report demonstrates the importance of estrogen beyond well-known effects in bone health and metabolism and highlights the importance of this hormone in male sexual health and fertility. By restoring estrogen within the normal range, we were able to improve our patient's quality of life as well as preserve his fertility. In understanding this rare condition, hormonal pathways which impact every patient are illustrated. Careful estrogen supplementation can be a viable therapeutic strategy in men with aromatase deficiency or analogous hypoestrogenism, balancing symptom relief with the goal of fertility preservation.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the clinical impact of hypoestrogenism in men and the role of estradiol in overall sexual health and reproductive endocrinology. Estradiol replacement improved patient-reported fatigue and libido, while semen analysis demonstrated ongoing spermatogenesis during short-term follow-up. Long-term reproductive outcomes in aromatase deficiency remain uncertain, so fertility counseling, cryopreservation, and serial monitoring should accompany treatment. Careful estrogen supplementation can be a viable therapeutic strategy in men with aromatase deficiency or analogous hypoestrogenism, balancing symptom relief with the goal of fertility preservation.
