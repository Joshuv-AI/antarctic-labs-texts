ROS-scavenging ultrasonicated graphene oxide/alginate microgels for mesenchymal stem cell delivery and hindlimb ischemia treatment
Authors: Seungjun Lee, Goeun Choe, Jongdarm Yi, Junghyun Kim, Sun Hong Lee, Jin Jeon, Hee Seok Yang, Jae Young Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) transplantation is widely recognized as a promising treatment for peripheral artery diseases because of their unique ability to secrete multiple growth factors and immunomodulatory cytokines. However, direct administration of MSCs frequently results in insufficient therapeutic efficacy due to low viability and poor retention at the implantation site. The delivery of MSCs in microsized hydrogels allows for simple injection, improved retention, and enhanced cell protection. However, the high oxidative stress present in ischemic tissues significantly impairs the viability and therapeutic activity of transplanted MSCs. This study aimed to develop a simple and effective method for fabricating reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging microgels to enhance the MSC efficacy for ischemic hindlimb treatment. Specifically, tip-sonicated graphene oxide (GO)/alginate (sGO/alginate) microgels exhibited significantly increased antioxidizing activity against various ROS compared with pristine GO/alginate microgels. MSCs encapsulated in sGO/alginate microgels (MSC/sGO/alginate) demonstrated higher viability than those encapsulated in alginate or GO/alginate microgels under various oxidative stress conditions. Furthermore, human umbilical vein endothelial cells co-cultured with MSCs encapsulated in sGO/alginate microgels formed more tubes under both normal and H2O2-treated conditions, implying enhanced pro-angiogenic potential of the MSCs. In vivo experiments using hindlimb ischemia mouse models revealed significant improvements in blood perfusion, limb salvage, vascularization, and MSC survival in the MSC/sGO/alginate group compared with the other groups (MSC, MSC/alginate, and MSC/GO/alginate). The strategy developed in this study offers a straightforward and powerful method for treating various ROS-related diseases, including ischemia.

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is a medical condition characterized by narrowing or blockage of peripheral arteries, primarily caused by plaque, fat, and cholesterol accumulation [1,2]. Severe outcomes of PAD, such as critical limb ischemia (CLI), ca lead to pain, tissue death, and, in extreme cases, necessitating amputation [3]. Cell therapy has emerged as a promising approach for restoring blood flow and regenerating ischemic tissues in the CLI treatment [4,5]. Among various cell sources, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been extensively utilized in tissue engineering and CLI treatment owing to their ability to produce a variety of anti-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and prostaglandins. These bioactive molecules further support cell growth, modulate the immune system, and promote blood vessel formation [6,7]. However, the effectiveness of current MSC-based therapies is limited primarily because of the low survival rate and poor retention of transplanted MSCs at injury sites. Injury sites present harsh microenvironments (e.g., high oxidative stress and immune responses) significantly impairing the viability of the transplanted cells [8]. Especially, excessive oxidative stress can cause lipid peroxidation, non-specific protein oxidation, and DNA strand breaks, resulting in cellular death [9]. In addition, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) induce the chemotactic migration of inflammatory cells into injury sites and activation of inflammatory tissue reactions [10].

Functional biomaterials have been extensively developed to improve the efficacy of cell-based therapies by enhancing cell viability and retention in vivo. For instance, MSC encapsulation in hydrogels can provide a protective environment from host immune system and persistent tissue-like structures that support cell growth [11]. For example, Ricles et al. fabricated PEGylated fibrin hydrogels combined with MSCs for ischemic injury repair and found that they promoted angiogenesis and enhanced mature blood vessel formation in hindlimb ischemic injury models [12]. However, these hydrogels presented limited antioxidant capacity to effectively protect and support MSCs in oxidative stress environments. Because excessive ROS at injury sites frequently induce inflammation and impede the formation of new blood vessels [[13], [14], [15]], antioxidizing agents can enhance cell survival and tissue repair by mitigating excessive free radicals and inflammation in injured tissues [[16], [17], [18]]. Therefore, multifunctional materials capable of effectively protecting transplanted MSCs from oxidative stress and encouraging their regeneration are highly promising for MSC delivery and CLI treatment.

A variety of antioxidants (e.g., enzymes, polyphenols, and nanomaterials) have been incorporated into diverse materials for cell delivery [[19], [20], [21]]. Notably, graphene and its derivatives have gained substantial attention for formulating antioxidizing hydrogels with various polymers owing to their inherent excellent antioxidizing capabilities and strong interactions with various small and large molecules [17,[22], [23], [24]]. We previously demonstrated that MSC delivery using reduced graphene oxide (rGO)-embedded alginate microgels was effective in treating infarcted hearts [17]. MSCs encapsulated in these antioxidizing microgels exhibited high survival rates under oxidative stress conditions and secreted various anti-inflammatory cytokines and pro-angiogenic molecules. However, the fabrication procedures involved the chemical reduction of MSC-laden GO/alginate microgels, which raised several concerns, such as complicated fabrication processes and potential alterations in MSC characteristics during reduction. Importantly, the potential toxicity of graphene derivatives remains an issue, although their biocompatibility varies depending on size, synthesis method, derivatization, and application conditions [[25], [26], [27]]. Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop a simple fabrication strategy for antioxidizing microgels using reduced amounts of graphene derivatives to improve the delivery of MSCs for ischemic disease treatment. In addition, Saw et al. prepared injectable collagen hydrogels consisting of antioxidizing melanin nanoparticles and MSCs genetically engineered to produce pro-angiogenic factor (Gremlin1) [28]. However, cell delivery using collagen gel requires thermal-triggered gelation, which may introduce complexity and inconsistency in handling and application. Moreover, this approach involves challenges in the preparation and characterization of melanin nanoparticles and the use of genetically manipulated MSCs.

In this study, we developed sonicated-GO/alginate (sGO/alginate) microgels for MSC delivery to treat CLI (Fig. 1). Alginate is a polyanionic polysaccharide derived from seaweed [29]. It has been widely used in various tissue regeneration applications owing to its biocompatibility and ease of crosslinking with divalent ions, such as Ca2+ and Ba2+. Interestingly, high-energy sonication can alter the structural and chemical properties of the GO flakes, such as their fragmentation and reduction [30]. Sonication was used to reduce GO content and simplify the production of antioxidant alginate microgels without chemical reduction. Then, microgels were fabricated via electrospraying and characterized for their material properties, followed by in vitro and in vivo studies to assess their feasibility for MSC delivery and CLI treatment (Fig. S1). Production of sonicated GO/alginate (sGO/alginate) microgels

Preparation of various GO-containing alginate solutions

A GO solution (6 mg/mL, 3 mL) was diluted with 3 × PBS (6 mL) and deionized water (DI, 9 mL) to obtain a final GO solution of 1 mg/mL in 1 × PBS. This solution was then sonicated using a tip sonicator (VC750, Sonics & Materials Inc., Newtown, CT, USA) at 40 % amplitude with an on/off pulse interval of 8/7 s for 30 min, which was termed sGO. In addition, an alginate solution (1 wt%) was prepared by dissolving alginate in 1 × PBS using a magnetic stirrer at 200 rpm and 25 °C for 24 h. The GO/alginate solution was prepared by dissolving alginate in the GO solution (1 mg/mL in 1 × PBS) to obtain a final concentration of 1 wt% alginate using a magnetic stirrer at 200 rpm and 25 °C for 24 h. The GO/alginate solution was further sonicated under the same conditions to prepare sonicated GO/alginate (sGO/alginate). The absorbance of the GO-containing solutions was measured from 190 to 500 nm using a spectrometer (TouchDuo, BioDrop, Holliston, MA, USA). The nomenclature and descriptions of various solutions are presented in Table S1.

Characterization of GO flakes in various solutions

The sizes and zeta potentials of the GO flakes in various solutions were measured using a zeta potential & particle size analyzer (ELSZ-2000, Otsuka electronics, Osaka, Japan) at 37 °C. For measurement, 10 μL of each GO-containing solution was diluted with 990 μL of DI water. The stability of the GO flakes in various GO-containing solutions (GO, sGO, GO/alginate, and sGO/alginate) was assessed by incubation at 37 °C. In brief, 0.2 mL of each solution was mixed with 1.8 mL of a growth medium (MEM-alpha medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic). Aggregation or sedimentation in each solution was monitored for up to 12 h. For atomic force microscopy (AFM), individual GO-containing solutions were drop-cast onto a silicone substrate and dried overnight in a desiccator. AFM was performed using an XE-100 system (Park Systems, Suwon, Republic of Korea) in non-contact mode at a scanning frequency of 0.3 Hz. Visualization and dimensional analyses were conducted using XEI software version 4.3 (Park Systems, Suwon, Republic of Korea). Raman spectroscopy was performed to analyze the characteristics of GO flakes in various solutions. The GO-containing solution was centrifuged to remove alginate and washed with DI water. The collected GO solution was drop-cast onto a glass slide and dried overnight in a desiccator. The prepared samples were analyzed using a Raman spectroscope with a 532 nm laser (n = 5). The intensities of the D and G peaks from the individual Raman spectra were measured using WiRE 3.2 software (RENISHAW), and the intensity ratio (ID/IG) of the D and G peaks was calculated.

Production of GO-containing alginate microgels

For microgel production, individual GO-containing solutions were electrosprayed using an electrospraying system (Nano NC, Seoul, Republic of Korea) at 15 kV through a 30 G nozzle. The flow rate was set to 8 mL/h with the nozzle positioned 25 cm from the collecting bath. The microgels were crosslinked in a bath containing 20 mM BaCl2 in saline (0.15 M NaCl). The microgels were collected and rinsed twice with saline. Microgel morphologies and sizes were analyzed using an optical microscope (Leica DMI3000 B, Wetzlar, Germany). The size distribution of the microgels was measured from optical micrographs using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Antioxidizing ability tests

DPPH assay

DPPH was dissolved in 80 % methanol to prepare a 0.1 mM stock solution. The stock solution was further diluted with 80 % methanol to prepare an assay solution with an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 517 nm. Each sample (100 μg) was mixed with 900 μL of the DPPH assay solution and incubated at 25 °C for 1 h. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 517 nm using a plate reader (SpectraMax M2, San Jose, CA, USA). For the control, 100 μL of a 0.15 M NaCl solution was used. The scavenging activity was calculated using the following equation (1):(1)Scavengingactivity(%)=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100

ABTS assay

A stock solution containing 7.4 mM ABTS and 2.6 mM potassium persulfate in PBS were prepared. The stock solution was further diluted with PBS to prepare an assay solution with an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 732 nm. Each sample (100 μg) was mixed with 900 μL of the ABTS assay solution and incubated at 25 °C for 1 h. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 732 nm using a microplate reader. For the control, 100 μL of 0.15 M NaCl solution was used. The scavenging activity was calculated using equation (1) as described for the DPPH assay.

Hydroxyl radical scavenging assay

An assay solution containing 2 mM FeSO4 and 1 mM Safranin O was prepared in DI water. Each sample (50 μg) was mixed with 300 μL of an assay solution and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 400 μL of 6 wt% H2O2 was added to each sample and incubated at 55 °C for 1 h. For the control, 400 μL of DI water was used. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 492 nm using a microplate reader. The scavenging activity was calculated using the following equation (2):(2)Scavengingactivity(%)=Asample−AblankAcontrol−Ablank×100

Superoxide radical assay

Individual solutions of NADH (468 μM), NBT (156 μM), and PMS (60 μM) in PBS were prepared. Then, 400 μL of each solution was sequentially added to a sample (50 μL). The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min. For the control, 50 μL of 0.15 M NaCl solution was used. After incubation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 560 nm using a plate reader. The scavenging activity was calculated using equation (1) as described for the DPPH assay.

In vitro cell culture studies

Cell culture

MSCs at passages less than 8 were used for all in vitro experiments. The MSCs were maintained in growth medium (MEM-alpha supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic) in an incubator with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. The medium was replaced every three days. When cell confluency reached approximately 80–90 %, the cells were subcultured using 0.05 % trypsin/EDTA.

Cell encapsulation

To fabricate MSC-laden microgels, solutions of alginate (1.25 wt%), GO/alginate (1.25 mg/mL GO and 1.25 wt% alginate), and sGO/alginate (1.25 mg/mL GO and 1.25 wt% alginate) were prepared using sterile PBS. A cell stock solution (3 × 107 cells/mL) was mixed with each solution at a 1:4 vol ratio. These mixtures were transferred to a 10 mL syringe and electrosprayed using a UV-exposed electrospray system equipped with a 30 G syringe needle at a flow rate of 8 mL/h and 15 kV. The microgels were collected in a bath containing a sterile crosslinking solution (20 mM BaCl2 in saline) and washed twice with sterile 0.15 M NaCl at 25 °C for 5 min each. The microgels were then transferred to growth medium and incubated in an incubator with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. All sample preparation and electrospraying were performed in biosafety cabinets with sterile equipment (e.g., syringes and needles) to ensure sterility.

Live/dead cell staining

Live/dead staining was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In brief, 20 μL of EthD-1 (2 mM) and 5 μL of calcein AM (5 mM) were mixed with 10 mL of DPBS to prepare the staining solution. Prior to staining, MSC-laden microgels were prepared as described above and incubated in growth medium with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, MSC-laden microgels were incubated in the staining solution with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 10 min and washed twice with DPBS for 5 min each. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI3000 B, Wetzlar, Germany). Live (green) and dead (red) cells were counted from randomly selected fluorescence images (n = 5). The cell viability was calculated using the following equation (3):(3)Cellviability(%)=NumberoflivecellsNumberoflivecells+Numberofdeadcells×100

For the culture of MSC-laden microgels under oxidative stress conditions, MSC-laden microgels were incubated with 500 μM H2O2, menadione, or TBHP for 24 h. Then, the Live/Dead staining was performed as described for ROS-free culture.

Intracellular ROS level measurement of HUVECs co-cultured with microgels

For ROS scavenging tests, HUVECs were co-cultured with various microgels in the presence of 300 μM H2O2, and their intracellular ROS levels were measured by H2DCFDA staining. In brief, HUVECs (1.2 × 105 cells) were cultured in growth medium (DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic) in an incubator with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 3 h. Then, 100 μL of the individual microgels was added to Transwell inserts. HUVECs were further cultured in growth medium containing 300 μM H2O2. After additional 3 h incubation, the HEVECs were detached using 0.05 % trypsin/EDTA and collected by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The collected cells were resuspended in PBS containing 10 μM H2DCFDA at 25 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with 2 % FBS (in DPBS). Flow cytometry analyses were performed using a FACS-Canto II flow cytometer and FlowJo software 10.8.1 (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

Tube formation assay

Tube formation assay was conducted by co-culturing HUVECs and MSC-laden microgels in a Transwell setup. HUVECs were maintained in the growth medium (DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic) in an incubator with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. For the assay, HUVECs were starved by incubation in serum-free DMEM for 12 h. The cells were then harvested by Trypsin/EDTA treatment. The cells were collected by centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in tube formation medium (a 1:1 mix of DMEM and MEM-alpha). Prior to cell seeding, 24-well plates were coated with Matrigel by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. HUVECs (1.2 × 105 cells) were seeded onto each Matrigel-coated well, followed by the placement of Transwell inserts onto the HUVEC-seeded plates. Individual samples (100 μL, 6 × 105 cells), including MSCs, MSC/alginate microgels, MSC/GO/alginate microgels, and MSC/sGO/alginate microgels, were seeded into the upper compartments of the Transwell plates and incubated in an incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 3 h. Each MSC sample was prepared one day before co-culture with HUVECs. HUVECs cultured in growth medium containing 10 % FBS and the FBS-free medium were used as the positive and negative controls, respectively. After an additional 3 h of co-culture, optical micrographs of the HUVECs in each group were acquired using a microscope (Leica DMI3000 B, Wetzlar, Germany). The total length, covered area, total loops, and total branching points of the tubes were measured from optical micrographs using the WimTube software package in the Wimasis system (https://www.wimasis.com/en/WimTube). In addition, tube formation assays were conducted under oxidative stress to mimic the in vivo ischemic environment. Specifically, co-culture experiments were performed using the culture medium containing 300 μM H2O2 while other experimental conditions and analytical procedures were identical to those of ROS-free co-culture described above.

In vivo cell studies

Animal care and experimentation

All animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees (ACUC) of the Gwangju Institute of Science and Technology, Republic of Korea (study approval number: GIST-2021-086). Ten-week-old male Balb/c-nude mice (25–30 g; Damul Science, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) were housed in a controlled environment at a constant temperature (28 °C) with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. The mice had free access to food and water before and after surgery.

Establishment of the hindlimb ischemia model and treatment

Surgeries were performed under general anesthesia using 2 % isoflurane. Hindlimb ischemia was induced by double-knot ligation of both femoral and saphenous artery using 8-0 sutures. The mice were randomly divided into five groups: untreated, treated with MSCs, treated with MSC-laden alginate microgels (MSC/alginate), treated with MSC-laden GO/alginate microgels (MSC/GO/alginate), and treated with MSC-laden sGO/alginate microgels (MSC/sGO/alginate). Various MSC-laden microgels were prepared as described in section 2.5.2. Each sample (0.6 × 106 cells) was intramuscularly injected into the central area of the quadriceps muscle of the ischemic mouse using a 26-gauge needle and a 1 mL syringe The incision was then closed with 6-0 sutures, and the animals were placed in a recovery room within the animal facility.

Semi-quantitative functional assessment and blood perfusion measurement

For semiquantitative functional assessment, ischemic damage was assessed by blinded observers and categorized into four progressive levels: salvage, nail blackening, necrosis, and loss. Noninvasive imaging to assess superficial tissue blood perfusion was performed using a Laser Doppler perfusion imager (PeriScan PIM3, Perimed, Stockholm, Sweden) on days 3, 7, and 14 post-surgery. In brief, the mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 300 μL of a ketamine and xylazine mixture (4:1 ratio). Digital color-coded images were analyzed to measure blood flow from the knee joint to the toe region. The perfusion rate was calculated by comparing the blood flow in the ischemic hindlimb with that in the normal hindlimb.

Histological evaluation

The mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide 14 days after hindlimb ischemia induction and treatment. The quadriceps and gastrocnemius muscles were harvested and fixed in 3.7 % (w/v) paraformaldehyde solution for 2 days. The tissues were dehydrated in ethanol, cleaned with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were sliced at 5-μm thickness using a microtome and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to a previously reported protocol [31]. Masson's trichrome (MT) staining was performed using a kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (TRM-500 ScyTek, Logan, UT, USA). Images of stained tissue sections were acquired using a VS200 Research Slide Scanner (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan). The hindlimb fibrosis area was quantified from MT staining images using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) by applying the same threshold value to obtain binary images and measuring the fibrosis area relative to the total area. Muscle cell degeneration and inflammatory cell infiltration were examined by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. For immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections were treated with 0.1 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, heated in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave for 40 s, and incubated at 98 °C for 20 min for antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked with 3 % goat serum in PBS at 25 °C for 60 min and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-von Willebrand factor monoclonal antibody at 1:10 and anti-human nuclear antigen monoclonal antibody at 1:25). The sections were then washed twice with DPBS for 30 min each, followed by incubation in secondary antibody solution (Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor 555-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), diluted 1:200 in blocking solution) at 25 °C for 60 min. Next, the sections were washed twice with DPBS for 15 min each and incubated with Sudan Black B (0.1 w/v% in 70 % ethanol) at 25 °C for 2 h to reduce autofluorescence. Finally, the sections were incubated with DAPI solution (1:1000 in DPBS) at 25 °C for 2 min and washed three times with DPBS. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (DMI3000B; Leica). The stained area was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) by applying the same threshold value to obtain binary images and measuring the stained area relative to the total area.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless stated otherwise. Statistical significance was calculated using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc comparison of the means using Origin Pro 9.1 software, at a significance level of 0.05.

Results and discussion

Production and characterization of GO-containing solutions

To examine the effects of high-power sonication on the characteristics of GO flakes, both alginate-free GO and GO/alginate solutions were sonicated. Sonication of GO has been widely explored to produce a uniform dispersion of GO and control its size, exfoliation, and degree of oxidation [[32], [33], [34]]. In particular, high-energy sonication leads to substantial changes in the chemical structure, including the partial restoration of sp2 carbon bonds [[35], [36], [37]]. In our study, high-power tip sonication was found to substantially decrease the size of GO flakes (Fig. 2a and b). For instance, hydrodynamic sizes of the GO decreased from 1707 ± 78 nm to 341 ± 3 nm after the sonication of the alginate-free GO solution. The sonication of the GO/alginate solution led to a decrease in hydrodynamic size of GO from 2671 ± 80 nm to 479 ± 71 nm. Alginate in solution appeared to reduce the fragmentation degree of the GO flakes during sonication and preferentially adhere to the GO flakes. The larger size of the GO flakes in the GO/alginate solution after sonication can be attributed to two possible reasons. First, the GO flakes adsorb hydrophilic alginate on their surfaces, thereby increasing their hydrodynamic size. In addition, the high viscosity of the GO/alginate solution dampened the sonication energy, reducing the degree of fragmentation of the GO flakes. As the sonication time increased, the hydrodynamic size of GO in the GO/alginate solutions decreased until 30 min and remained constant up to 60 min (Fig. S2). Hence, sonication for 30 min was determined to be suitable for subsequent studies. Importantly, sonication did not influence the zeta potential of the GO flakes in either DI water or the alginate solution (Fig. 2c). The zeta potentials of the GO flakes in alginate solution (-51.53 ± 0.92 mV) were significantly lower than those in alginate-free solution (-24.40 ± 0.05 mV) regardless of sonication. This reduction is primarily due to the negative zeta potential of alginate, which lowers the overall zeta potential for both the GO/alginate and sGO/alginate composites compared to GO nanoparticles alone. AFM analysis revealed significant morphological changes in the GO flakes after sonication (Fig. 2d). Pristine GO exhibited a well-dispersed and large flake morphology with a lateral size of 0.50 ± 0.05 μm and a thickness of 3.16 ± 0.70 nm. In contrast, sonicated GO (sGO) had a fragmented morphology with reduced lateral size of 0.16 ± 0.07 μm and a thickness of 0.95 ± 0.71 nm. Interestingly, the GO flakes in the alginate solution exhibited severe agglomerations, but sonication resulted in uniform fragmentation of the GO, achieving a lateral size of 0.15 ± 0.05 μm and a thickness of 1.05 ± 0.13 nm without noticeable agglomerations. Aggregation of the GO flakes in the GO/alginate solution may result from molecular interactions between large alginate chains and GO flakes (e.g., hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions), which induces the chain and flake entanglement. Raman spectroscopy was performed to assess the effects of sonication on the reduction of GO flakes by analyzing the intensity ratio (ID/IG) of the D band to the G band in Raman spectrum, which is indicative of GO reduction (Fig. 2e and f). The D band at 1350 cm−1 is typically related to structural defects, whereas the G band at 1580 cm−1 reflects sp2 carbon atom vibrations. Hence, an increased ID/IG ratio suggests a high degree of GO reduction [38]. In our studies, sonication increased the ID/IG ratios from 1.05 ± 0.01 to 1.14 ± 0.02 for GO and from 1.05 ± 0.01 to 1.11 ± 0.01 for GO/alginate solutions, indicating effective reduction and an increase in structural defects by high-powder sonication [39,40]. The absorbance at 220–230 nm, indicative of the aromatic and conjugated carbon-carbon double bond structures in GO [41], increased by sonication (Fig. S3), suggesting that sonication enhanced the dispersion and reduction of the GO flakes [30]. Colloidal stability of GO flakes in aqueous environments is crucial for the production of reliable and effective composites for biomedical applications [42,43]. The stability of GO/alginate was assessed by incubation in the serum-containing media at 37 °C (Fig. 2g). GO in the unsonicated solutions (both GO and GO/alginate) sedimented within 1 h of incubation. In contrast, the sGO and sGO/alginate solutions remained stable without noticeable aggregation or sedimentation for up to 12 h. The enhanced dispersity and stability of the sGO flakes may be attributed to their reduced sizes.

Production and characterization of microgels

The microgels were fabricated through electrospraying of various alginate-based solutions (alginate, GO/alginate, and sGO/alginate). All the microgels exhibited spherical shapes and relatively uniform size distributions (Fig. 3a). Microgel sizes were in a range of 200–400 μm, and the difference in size was not significant among the samples (Fig. 3b). These small microgels facilitate minimally invasive administration through simple injection techniques. The microgels maintained their rheological properties and shapes after injection through a 26-gauge needle (Fig. S4). Rheological analysis revealed that the individual microgels had moderate viscosity and shear-thinning behavior suitable for injection (Fig. S4a). For instance, at a shear rate of 1 s−1, the viscosities of alginate, GO/alginate, and sGO/alginate microgel were 6.75, 23.37, and 11.11 Pa s, respectively. Moreover, all microgels demonstrated excellent thixotropic behavior and viscosity recovery (greater than 90 %) (Fig. S4b), maintaining their viscoelastic properties after deformation. All groups presented no significant change in microgel diameter after injection (Fig. S4c). These results indicated that incorporation of small amounts of GO into alginate and subsequent sonication did not significantly affect fluidic dynamics at the nozzle during electrospraying, thus preserving consistent microgel sizes and distributions.Fig. 3Production and characterization of various microgels. The incorporation of GO into the microgels was primarily aimed at imbuing them with an antioxidant activity to protect the encapsulated MSCs from ROS and support their therapeutic efficacy in CLI treatment. GO possesses an antioxidizing ability because it can directly react with ROS to form stable adducts and donate electrons to neutralize radicals, converting them into less reactive or non-reactive species [44,45]. Consequently, numerous GO-incorporated scaffolds have been demonstrated to scavenge ROS and promote tissue regeneration [[21], [22], [23]]. In this study, we performed multiple assays, including DPPH, ABTS, ·OH−, and ·O2− assays, to evaluate the ROS-scavenging activities of the microgels (Fig. 3c–f). The assays confirmed the microgels' robust antioxidizing properties with high fidelity, while also ruling out the possible interference from unwanted molecular interactions, such as adsorption between GO flakes and chemicals, during assay. The GO-containing microgel groups (GO/alginate and sGO/alginate) exhibited significantly higher DPPH radical scavenging activities than GO-free alginate microgels (4.9 ± 2.2 %). The scavenging activity of sGO/alginate microgels (35.3 ± 0.4 %) was significantly higher compared to that of GO/alginate microgels (23.9 ± 0.7 %). The ABTS assay showed a similar trend to that of the DPPH assay. For instance, ABTS scavenging abilities of alginate, GO/alginate, and sGO/alginate microgels were 19.8 ± 2.6 %, 68.2 ± 0.8 %, and 79.7 ± 2.1 %, respectively. Similarly, the sGO/alginate microgels had 1.4-fold and 2.1-fold higher hydroxyl radical and superoxide radical scavenging abilities, respectively, than the GO/alginate microgels. Furthermore, the GO/alginate and sGO/alginate microgels demonstrated superior ROS scavenging capabilities to GO and sGO, respectively, for four types of ROS species (Fig. S5). The increased surface area and improved dispersion of GO and sGO within the alginate matrix appeared to enhance the accessibility of GO and sGO to ROS.

We also observed that as the GO and sGO contents in the microgels increased, their radical scavenging abilities progressively improved. Notably, at equivalent GO concentrations, sGO/alginate microgels were consistently superior to GO/alginate microgels in all assays (Fig. S6). Interestingly, the sGO/alginate microgels containing 2 mg/mL sGO showed slightly higher or comparable ROS scavenging abilities to those containing 1 mg/mL sGO. Given that high GO concentrations may raise concerns regarding potential toxicity, 1 mg/mL sGO was determined to have the most balanced properties for producing ROS-scavenging microgels and delivering MSCs for CLI treatment. sGO/alginate microgels containing 1 mg/mL sGO displayed ROS-scavenging abilities comparable to our previously reported microgels containing 2 mg/mL reduced GO, which effectively protected encapsulated MSCs and regenerated infarcted cardiac tissues (Fig. S7) [35].

Intracellular ROS level measurement of HUVECs co-cultured with microgels

The ROS scavenging efficiency of the microgels was further evaluated using an in vitro model that can simulate the ischemic environment by culturing HUVECs under oxidative stress conditions. After 3 h of co-culture, the cells were stained with H2DCFDA, and the intracellular ROS levels were subsequently analyzed using flow cytometry (Fig. 4a). HUVECs cultured with sGO/alginate microgels exhibited a lower DCFDA-high population (4.6 ± 0.9 %) than those cultured with alginate (34.2 ± 2.9 %) and GO/alginate (12.6 ± 1.4 %) microgels (Fig. 4b). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of DCFDA-stained cells was significantly lower in the HUVECs cultured with sGO/alginate microgels (55.7 ± 5.8) compared to those cultured with alginate (69.5 ± 10.1) and GO/alginate (143.7 ± 15.3) microgels (Fig. 4c). These results demonstrate that sGO/alginate microgels effectively scavenged ROS in the medium and significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels in HUVECs. In vitro culture of MSC-laden microgels under oxidative stress conditions

MSC-laden microgels were prepared by electrospraying MSC-containing alginate solutions. MSCs were successfully encapsulated in various alginate microgels, with most cells located inside the microgels (Fig. 5a). The viability of the MSCs encapsulated in various microgels was assessed under both normal and oxidative stress conditions (Fig. 5b). In normal ROS-free conditions, all groups (MSC/alginate, MSC/GO/alginate, and MSC/sGO/alginate) exhibited excellent cell viability (>80 %), indicating generally good cytocompatibility of the materials (alginate and GO) and the MSC-laden microgel fabrication process. Moreover, MSC viability was higher in the GO-containing microgels (MSC/GO/alginate and MSC/sGO/alginate) than in the GO-free microgels, indicating that both GO and sonicated GO did not adversely affect cell viability and instead supported cell growth. This effect could be attributed to GO's ability to interact with various biomolecules, stabilizing the hydrogel matrix and potentially adsorbing proteins from the extracellular matrix [46]. Additionally, the controlled incorporation of GO ensures minimal direct contact between GO flakes and MSCs, potentially mitigating cytotoxicity. The potential influence of the microgel size on cell viability was investigated by dividing the microgels into two groups based on microgel sizes (<300 μm and >300 μm in diameter) (Fig. S8). No significant difference in cell viability was observed between the two groups, indicating that microgel size did not affect cell viability and might not significantly alter therapeutic efficacy in hindlimb ischemic treatment in our studies. Furthermore, cell viability was evaluated under oxidative stress conditions induced by various radicals such as H2O2, menadione, and TBHP. Cell viability in the MSC/alginate group was significantly low (<40 %) in the presence of 500 μM H2O2, menadione, or TBHP, whereas significantly higher survival rates were observed in the MSC/GO/alginate and MSC/sGO/alginate groups under the same ROS conditions. Therefore, MSCs encapsulated in sGO/alginate are expected to maintain viability at ischemic injury sites and exert therapeutic actions (e.g., secretion of angiogenic molecules) to promote regeneration in CLI.

In vitro co-culture of HUVEC with MSC-laden microgels

MSCs are particularly beneficial for ischemic disease treatment because they secrete various growth factors and cytokines that can promote the proliferation, migration, and tube formation of endothelial cells [49]. In our study, the angiogenic potential of the MSC-encapsulated microgels was examined by co-culturing them with HUVECs in a Transwell setup (Fig. 6). Tube formation in HUVECs co-cultured with MSCs was first analyzed under normal ROS-free conditions based on total tube length, covered area, and the numbers of loops and branching points (Fig. 6a–e). HUVECs co-cultured with MSCs (not encapsulated in the microgels) formed well-organized capillary-like structures. Interestingly, co-culture with MSC/alginate microgels led to poor tube formation of HUVECs, potentially due to the lack of cell-interactive moieties in alginate that are essential for supporting the functional activities of encapsulated MSCs, such as growth factor secretion. In contrast, both the MSC/GO/alginate and MSC/sGO/alginate groups exhibited a significant enhancement in tube formation of the co-cultured HUVECs. Tube formation indices (total tube length and covered area) and tubular organization indices (the numbers of loops and branching points) were the highest in the MSC/sGO/alginate group. For example, the number of total loops per image for the negative, MSC, MSC/alginate, MSC/GO/alginate, and MSC/sGO/alginate groups were 25 ± 15, 166 ± 8, 41 ± 13, 153 ± 27, and 208 ± 7, respectively. The enhanced tube formation observed in the MSC/sGO/alginate group may be attributed to the positive roles of the sGO flakes within the alginate microgels in supporting MSC growth and enhancing the angiogenic potential of MSCs. Excessive ROS impedes vascularization by damaging both transplanted cells and vascular endothelial cells [54,55]. Therefore, we co-cultured HUVECs and MSCs in an H2O2-containing medium (300 μM H2O2) to mimic the oxidative stress environment in ischemic tissue (Fig. 6f–j). Both the positive (10 % FBS) and negative (no FBS) groups exhibited minimal tube formation under this oxidative stress condition. In contrast, the co-culture groups showed higher tube formation and organization than monocultured HUVEC groups (positive and negative controls). Interestingly, HUVECs co-cultured with MSC, MSC/alginate, and MSC/GO/alginate microgels demonstrated similar tube formation with no significant differences, whereas the MSC/sGO/alginate co-culture group exhibited the highest degrees of tube formation and organization among the tested groups. The superior antioxidant activity of the sGO/alginate microgels effectively attenuated oxidative stress, thereby preventing damage to both transplanted cells and vascular endothelial cells. These results indicate that sGO flakes in the microgels could protect both encapsulated MSCs and co-cultured HUVECs from ROS and enhance their angiogenic potential to form vasculature.

In vivo angiogenic activity for hindlimb ischemia treatment

The in vivo efficacy of MSC-laden antioxidizing microgels in promoting angiogenesis was assessed using a murine hindlimb ischemia model. The experimental groups included untreated controls, MSCs only (MSC), MSC/alginate microgels, MSC/GO/alginate microgels, and MSC/sGO/alginate microgels. MSC viability after injection through a 26-gauge needle was first evaluated to determine possible cellular damage due to shear forces during injection (Fig. S9a). The results demonstrated that the injection did not significantly affect the viability of the MSCs encapsulated in the alginate-based microgels (Fig. S9b). Post-surgery and treatment, the condition of the mice's foot was assessed on days 3, 7, and 14 according to a functional grading scale ranging from limb salvage to complete limb loss (Fig. 7a and b). This ischemia scoring system can indicate functional recovery of murine hindlimbs following the various treatments. In the untreated control group, severe outcomes prevailed, with 60 % of the mice experiencing limb loss and 40 % displaying necrosis. The MSC-treated group exhibited marginal improvements, achieving reductions in both limb loss and necrosis to 40 %, and 20 % of the mice showed nail blackening. Notable progress was observed in the MSC/alginate group, which demonstrated a 20 % increase in nail blackening, suggesting moderate improvement. More substantial improvements were observed in the MSC/GO/alginate microgel group, which achieved 20 % complete limb salvage while maintaining 40 % necrosis, 20 % nail blackening, and 20 % limb loss. In addition, blood perfusion recovery was evaluated using Laser Doppler perfusion imaging at 3, 7, and 14 days after treatment (Fig. 7c). No significant recovery in the blood perfusion rate was observed in the untreated, MSC, and MSC/alginate groups (Fig. 7d). Although the MSC/GO/alginate microgel group exhibited improved blood perfusion, this enhancement was not significant. Among all groups, the MSC/sGO/alginate treatment significantly enhanced blood perfusion recovery, demonstrating superior efficacy in promoting vascularization. For example, the blood perfusion ratio of the MSC/sGO/alginate group on day 14 was 3.5-fold and 2.6-fold higher than that of the MSC/alginate and MSC/GO/alginate microgel groups, respectively (Fig. 7e). The MSC/alginate group exhibited limited perfusion improvement, highlighting the importance of the antioxidizing ability of cell delivery matrices. Furthermore, the MSC/GO/alginate groups showed no significant improvement in blood perfusion ratios, likely because the functions of the GO components were insufficient to effectively scavenge ROS and promote angiogenesis in the ischemic tissue. However, sGO with elevated ROS scavenging abilities may effectively protect transplanted MSCs and induce them to exert pro-angiogenic effects in the ischemic tissue environment. These findings demonstrate the excellent therapeutic potential of MSC/sGO/alginate microgels for inducing angiogenesis in ischemic tissues.

Histological assessment

For the histological evaluation of angiogenesis in the ischemic hind limb, mice were sacrificed on day 14 after surgery and treatment. The gastrocnemius muscles and quadriceps were histologically examined for tissue organization, fibrosis, and vasculature formation (Fig. 8). Initially, fibrosis associated with ischemic tissue was evaluated by analyzing collagen deposition using Masson's trichrome (MT) staining (Fig. 8a) [56,57]. The fibrosis area in the MSC/sGO/alginate group was the smallest among all tested groups, representing approximately 58 % of that observed in the untreated group. The other groups displayed extensive fibrotic scarring; however, the differences were not statistically significant. For example, the fibrosis area percentages were 47 ± 10 %, 43 ± 2 %, 44 ± 4 %, 36 ± 5 %, and 26 ± 3 % for the untreated, MSC, MSC/alginate microgel, and MSC/sGO/alginate groups, respectively (Fig. 8b). H&E-staining images of the gastrocnemius muscle of untreated ischemic limbs demonstrated extensive muscle cell degeneration and infiltration of inflammatory cells, which are typical hallmarks of ischemic injury. Conversely, muscles treated with the MSC/sGO/alginate microgel exhibited substantial reductions in deleterious structural changes and inflammatory cell infiltration. Notably, muscle fibers appeared to be mostly regular and round, with nuclei appropriately positioned at the periphery, indicating non-pathological muscle tissue morphologies [58]. In the untreated group, no micro blood vessels were detected in the quadriceps. Only a limited number of narrow microvessels were observed in the MSC group. However, in the MSC/alginate groups, small, round blood vessels were observed around the microgels. Similarly, the MSC/GO/alginate group exhibited blood vessels around the microgels. The MSC/sGO/alginate group exhibited a more diverse and expansive vascular network around the microgels than the other groups, suggesting the enhanced angiogenic effects of the sGO/alginate microgels.

Interestingly, no significant infiltration of inflammatory cells was observed in the quadriceps of both untreated and MSC-only groups. However, the MSC/alginate and MSC/GO/alginate groups showed dense infiltration of inflammatory cells around the microgels, suggesting tissue repair in progress. Although alginate is not biodegradable in vivo, free alginate is cleared from the circulation via hepatic and splenic pathways within two days after administration [59]. In addition, alginate is biocompatible [57,58] and widely used for medical applications [60,61]. In our study, only mild inflammatory responses around the microgels (MSC/alginate) were observed without severe symptoms typically associated with foreign body reaction. These histological findings collectively demonstrate the therapeutic effects of MSC/sGO/alginate microgels in promoting angiogenesis and mitigating fibrosis in ischemic muscle tissues. Because of their ability to mitigate ischemic injury symptoms and enhance vascularization, MSC/sGO/alginate microgels hold promise for treating peripheral artery disease, where the prevention of tissue ischemia and subsequent fibrosis is crucial.

Microvascular formation was further examined by immunostaining quadriceps tissues for von Willebrand Factor (vWF) 14 days after surgery and treatment (Fig. 8a). Distinct vWF signals were detected in the MSC-containing microgel groups. Image analyses indicated that vWF expression was the highest in the MSC/sGO/alginate group. For example, vWF-stained areas were 2.6 ± 0.8 %, 4.0 ± 1.6 %, 3.9 ± 1.2 %, 5.5 ± 0.8 %, and 11.1 ± 2.7 % for the untreated, MSC, MSC/alginate microgel, MSC/GO/alginate microgel, and MSC/sGO/alginate microgel groups, respectively (Fig. 8c). Treatment with MSC/sGO/alginate microgels induced the formation of round blood vessels [62]. Such enhanced blood vessel formation with an appropriate shape in MSC/sGO/alginate group implies improved production of angiogenic factors by the encapsulated MSCs, which were effectively protected against the harsh microenvironments of ischemic tissues. Additionally, the residence and in vivo viability of the transplanted MSCs were assessed by HNA staining. Distinct HNA signals were observed in the MSC/sGO/alginate group, whereas minimal signals were detected in the other groups. The HNA-stained areas were 1.7 ± 0.2 %, 2.6 ± 0.9 %, 2.7 ± 0.9 %, 4.5 ± 0.9 %, and 17.0 ± 2.5 % for the untreated, MSC, MSC/alginate microgel, MSC/GO/alginate microgel, and MSC/sGO/alginate microgel groups, respectively (Fig. 8d). These observations indicate that MSC/sGO/alginate microgels can provide an environment conducive to MSC survival and support angiogenic activity in ischemic tissues. Furthermore, we performed live/dead staining of the microgels retrieved on day 14 post injection (Fig. S10). MSC viability in MSC/alginate microgel, MSC/GO/alginate, and MSC/sGO/alginate microgel groups were 45.1 ± 4.1 %, 71.4 ± 6.5 %, and 89.2 ± 2.7 %, respectively. Altogether, these results suggest that the sGO/alginate microgels exhibit robust ROS-scavenging ability, playing a pivotal role in protecting the encapsulated MSCs from the oxidative stress and inflammatory damage prevalent in ischemic environments. These findings also demonstrated that sGO/alginate microgels can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-based cell therapies by inducing angiogenesis and cell survival in ischemic tissues.

Conclusion

To develop an efficient MSC delivery platform with excellent antioxidizing abilities that protect MSCs from harsh environments and support their proangiogenic potential, we developed injectable microgels composed of sGO, alginate, and MSCs. The MSC-laden antioxidizing microgels with improved ROS-scavenging activity were prepared using a small amount of GO (1 mg/mL). Notably, high-power sonication significantly enhanced the antioxidizing ability of GO, effectively protecting the encapsulated MSCs from oxidative stress. The MSC/sGO/alginate microgels enhanced tube formation in co-cultured HUVECs under both normal and oxidative stress conditions. Animal studies demonstrated that MSC/sGO/alginate microgels significantly stimulated angiogenesis in ischemic tissues, evidenced by substantial improvements in hindlimb salvage, blood perfusion, and blood vessel formation. Furthermore, significantly more viable MSCs remained in the injected ischemic tissues in the MSC/sGO/alginate group on day 14 post-treatment. This study successfully demonstrated the benefits of MSC/sGO/alginate microgels in promoting angiogenesis and tissue regeneration; however, several aspects further investigation. The precise mechanisms or pathways underlying angiogenesis, long-term efficacy, potential toxicity, and process optimization were not clearly investigated and therefore required to be addressed in future studies. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that MSC/sGO/alginate microgels are promoting platform for enhancing angiogenesis and regenerating various injured or diseased tissues. These microgels can be further modified to deliver various therapeutic substances (e.g., chemical compounds, nucleic acids, and other types of cells) to improve their therapeutic potential for CLI treatment and expand their applications to treat diverse diseases.
