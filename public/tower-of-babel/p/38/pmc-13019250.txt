Formulation of Hydrogel from Synthesized Silica-Reinforced Poly(Acrylamide/PVP) Polymer for Water Shut-Off in High-Temperature Reservoirs
Authors: Milan Mandal, Seth Agrippa Oppong, Keka Ojha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study investigates the synthesis of silica-enforced poly­(acrylamide/polyvinylpyrrolidone) (AM/PVP) polymer and its properties in formulating a hydrogel for conformance control in high-salinity reservoirs. The synthesis of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)-acrylamide (AM) copolymer and the presence of silica in its structure were confirmed through FTIR spectroscopy, in addition to the evaluation of characteristics including rheology, thermal stability, swelling characteristic, and pore-plugging capability through various analytical studies. The nanohydrogel of the synthesized silica-PVP-AM copolymer displays prominent shear-thinning, viscoelastic behavior, with the elastic modulus (G′) higher than the viscous modulus (G″). The DSC results indicate an increase of approximately 22 °C (from ∼131 °C to ∼152 °C) in the glass transition temperature (T g) of the silica-impregnated copolymer hydrogel compared to the gel in the absence of silica nanoparticles, suggesting a notable improvement in its thermal stability compared to its counterpart. An increased bound water-holding capacity from 25.26% to 33.39% was observed in the nanohydrogel compared to its counterpart, which was further confirmed by the microstructure of the hydrogels observed in FESEM images. The efficacy of the hydrogel was further evaluated through sandpack experiments, with 95.7% permeability reduction of the sandpack after placement of the nanohydrogel, proving its capability in controlling water channeling.

Introduction

As the demand for energy continues to rise and fewer new reserves are being discovered, there’s a stronger push to extend the lifespan of existing oil/gas wells in mature oil fields. , The most common challenge in handling wells that produce a high amount of water compared to oil is that sometimes over 95% of the total liquid output is just water. On a global scale, it is estimated that for every barrel of oil, about 3 to 5 barrels of water are extracted. In some older oil-producing areas, that number can rise significantly, reaching as high as 10 to 14 barrels of water for each barrel of oil. Managing this huge volume of water still poses a tough challenge for the global oil and gas industry. Reservoir heterogeneity and viscous fingering play the most dominant role in excessive water production, in addition to mechanical issues such as casing leaks, water coning, and channeling behind the casing. High volumes of unwanted water production pose several adverse impacts on oil production as well as its economics, including (i) disposal and treatment challenges, (ii) poor oil recovery with large remaining oil saturation in the unswept zones, (iii) corrosion, (iv) hydrostatic loading in the oil well, (v) migration of fines, etc., which significantly affect the overall economics of production. ,

In recent years, numerous water shut-off methods have been introduced to improve the recovery efficiency from unswept reservoir zones, thereby contributing significantly to increased oil recovery. To overcome the issues pertaining to excess water production in oil wells, various chemical methods have been proposed, including polymer flooding and gel treatment, in addition to the mechanical ones. , Traditionally, in situ gels have been widely used to plug the areas contributing to excessive water production. , In this technique, a gelant solution comprising a polymer and a cross-linking agent is introduced into the porous formation. Upon exposure to in situ reservoir temperature and salinity conditions, the gelant undergoes a cross-linking reaction, resulting in the formation of a viscoelastic gel, which can block the highly permeable zones and thereby mitigate unwanted fluid channeling.

However, the implementation of this technique is often hindered by several critical limitations, including thermal or chemical destabilization of the gel structure under extreme reservoir conditions and insufficient mechanical stability. Such limitations have driven the advancement of nanocomposite hydrogels, which exhibit enhanced thermal and mechanical resilience compared to conventional hydrogel systems.

Polyacrylamide (PAM), hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM), and xanthan gum, often used in conjunction with suitable cross-linking agents, are among the most extensively applied polymers in both water treatment processes and enhanced oil recovery (EOR) operations. Their primary role lies in improving the water-to-oil mobility ratio, which helps in achieving more effective displacement of trapped oil within the reservoir. Despite their widespread use, conventional polymers are often limited by their high costs and inadequate thermal stability, which make them unsuitable for applications requiring sustained performance at temperatures above 80 °C. , Therefore, it is essential to explore newer copolymer formulations capable of maintaining their structural integrity and performance under harsh thermal and saline conditions. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) is a nontoxic and biocompatible polymer that offers excellent adsorption characteristics. The incorporation of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) into polymer matrices enhances their hydrophilic nature, improves thermal stability, and contributes to better mechanical strength, − making the resulting materials more suitable for challenging environmental conditions. The presence of PVP enhances the thermal stability of polymer solutions, primarily due to its high glass transition temperature, which is imparted by the rigid pyrrolidone ring structure within its backbone. This structural feature helps to resist thermal degradation under elevated temperature conditions. However, PVP exhibits relatively low viscosity and, therefore, requires significantly higher concentrations to achieve viscosity levels comparable to those of polyacrylamides. This limitation can affect its efficiency when it is used as a primary viscosifying agent in fluid formulations. So, to overcome this, acrylamide is used as a monomer to increase the viscosity. Acrylamide (AM) is a low molecular weight, water-soluble monomer that provides viscosity at considerable levels and appears as a white, odorless crystalline solid. Acrylamide (AM) is utilized in the production of a variety of polymer-based materials, particularly those functioning as binding agents and flocculants in numerous industrial applications.

Silica nanoparticles represent a versatile additive due to their controllable particle size at the nanoscale, high thermal and chemical stability, low toxicity, and strong reinforcing capabilities. Additionally, their surfaces can be readily modified with various small molecules or macromolecules, enabling tailored functionality for specific applications. Nanocomposite hydrogel has gained acceptance, but the major problem for field application is the uniform dispersion of nanoparticles through ultrasonication, which is not feasible at the field site. (A). A side-by-side comparison of polymer gel technologies utilizing PVP, HPAM, and silica materials is provided in Table . The aim of this study is to synthesize a novel silica-reinforced copolymer by using PVP as the polymer, AM as the monomer, and potassium persulfate as the initiator, by a simple and easy free radical graft copolymerization method. This approach eliminates the need for an ultrasonication step during hydrogel preparation at the field site, thereby enhancing its practicality and feasibility for field applications. Insightful investigations have been carried out to understand the effect of silica-reinforced copolymers on the viscosity of the gelant solution, gelation time, mechanical strength of the formed gel, and the overall rheological behavior of polymer-based gels. In addition, the thermal stability of the polymer gels was examined to assess their performance under elevated temperature and salinity conditions. Sandpack flooding experiments were conducted to evaluate the blocking efficiency of the polymer gels under reservoir-like conditions.

Methodology for Bulk Gel Preparation

The bulk gel solutions were carried out by mixing 2% brine (NaCl) solution with 1.0 wt % of silica-reinforced copolymer (PVP + AM + SILICA), i.e., Gel-A. The solution was then stirred at 600 rpm mechanically for 14 h at room temperature (25 °C). This step was followed by the addition of HQ (0.25 wt %) and HX (0.25 wt %) cross-linkers, and the mixture was continuously stirred for ∼6 h until a uniform mixture was formed. The same steps were repeated for a 1.0 wt % copolymer (PVP + AM) solution, but in the absence of silica nanoparticles, i.e., Gel-B. The gelling solutions thus prepared were poured into 100 mL ampules to approximately one-third of their capacity to accommodate any expansion. The glass ampules were sealed using glass-blowing equipment to ensure an airtight closure. The sealed ampules were then put in an oven maintained at 120 °C for gelation.

Characterization of Polymer

FT-IR analysis was carried out utilizing a PerkinElmer Spectrum 2 instrument over a spectral range of 4000–400 cm–1. Characteristic absorption bands corresponding to the functional groups were identified to confirm the copolymerization with silica grafting.

Gelation Kinetic Study

Gelation time represents an essential factor for ensuring the successful placement of the gel at the targeted formation depth, where it must effectively withstand and block unwanted water flow. The progression of the gelation process and the corresponding gelation time were evaluated using the bottle test procedure, a semi-quantitative approach for evaluating the gelation rate and the resulting gel strength. This method offers a rapid and cost-effective technique for examining the kinetics of gelation, , making it suitable for the preliminary evaluation of gel systems. In water shut-off hydrogel, gelation time is described as the time required for the gelling solution to achieve the flowing gel stage, designated as code “C” from its initial code “A” state. This method assessed the hydrogel’s strength during gelation using an alphabetical classification system from A to I, as illustrated in Figure . In the current study, all of the gelation tests were conducted at a temperature of 120 °C to simulate moderate to high-temperature reservoir conditions. In this procedure, the progression of gelation was monitored at predefined time intervals by inverting the bottles and observing the gel’s flow response under gravity. The objective of this study was to achieve a gel classified as code G, which represents a moderately deformable, nonflowing gel, within 100 h to ensure effective placement at the targeted formation depth.

Gel strength classification based on the bottle test method.

Measurement of Gel Strength

Gel strength was assessed through the breakthrough vacuum method, as depicted in Figure . In this experimental arrangement, a pair of suction flasks connected in series was utilized to conduct the breakthrough vacuum test. A pipette tube was attached to one of the suction flasks and inserted into the glass ampule containing the gel sample, while the second suction flask was linked to a vacuum pump. Upon insertion of the pipette into the glass ampule containing the hydrogel, the vacuum pump was turned on to initiate suction. The suction pressure gradually builds up until a critical point is reached, beyond which a sudden drop in pressure is observed, indicating the gel’s failure point. The maximum vacuum pressure recorded during the test was considered the gel strength, as it represents the point at which the gel fails or breaks. The experiments were carried out three times under consistent conditions to confirm the system’s repeatability, and the average of the measured values was taken to quantify the gel strength of the sample.

Apparatus configuration for measuring gel strength using the breakthrough vacuum technique.

Determination of Swelling Ratio

The swelling ratio represents the extent to which a gel expands when exposed to formation fluid while maintaining the integrity of its three-dimensional network structure without disintegration. Swelling measurements were conducted to verify the gel’s ability to effectively block reservoir fractures and high-permeability zones. This test checks the structural integrity of the gel under high drag forces within the reservoir. To evaluate the swelling ratio, a known volume of gel was placed inside individual graduated bottles, followed by the addition of brine solution with varying salinity levels. The changes in the samples were then monitored over 72 h at room temperature (120 °C). The change in gel height, corresponding to the volumetric expansion, was measured after a 72-h period and reported as VsVi , where V i represents the initial volume of the gel, and V s is the swollen gel volume.

FESEM Analysis

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) [Model: Zeiss Supra 55] was employed to capture high-resolution images of the gel samples, providing insight into their surface morphology and structural characteristics. Gel films were prepared by putting the prepared gel onto clean glass slides, followed by drying the samples thoroughly in an oven maintained at 60 °C to ensure the complete removal of moisture. Adequate drying of the samples is crucial for accurate FESEM analysis, as the presence of residual moisture can lead to water evaporation during imaging, which may compromise the image resolution and overall clarity of the surface images. The sample surface was coated with platinum to make it conductive.

DSC Analysis

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) analysis of the gel samples was performed using a Netzsch DSC 3500 Sirius instrument to determine their thermal stability. For each measurement, a fixed amount (∼20–25 mg) of the gel sample was sealed in a hermetic aluminum pan and examined under a nitrogen environment with a continuous purge flow of 50 cm3/min. The thermal behavior of the gel samples was evaluated by heating them from −25 °C to 200 °C at a uniform rate of 10 °C/min.

Rheological Characterization

Rheological properties were measured with a cone–plate configuration on a compact rheometer (MCR-52, Anton Paar, Physica, Austria). Viscosity measurements were carried out over a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s–1. The viscoelastic behavior of the gel samples was examined over a frequency sweep spanning the range of 0.1 to 50 rad/s, under constant strain amplitude throughout the measurements under the linear viscoelastic region. All experimental procedures were carried out under constant temperature conditions of 25 °C.

Fluid Flow Experiments through Sand-Pack

To evaluate the plugging performance and effectiveness of the hydrogel, sandpack experiments were performed under simulated reservoir conditions (120 °C) with the aid of a specially designed sandpack flooding apparatus, as illustrated in Figure . The efficiency and plugging capability of the hydrogel system were evaluated by determining the residual resistance factor to water (F rrw) and the associated reduction in permeability. This investigation evaluated the impact of the hydrogel on sandpack permeability, and the water shut-off efficiency was determined using the methodology reported in a previous publication. Initially, the sandpack was made by ramming sand uniformly within the core holder to ensure proper packing. Brine with a concentration of 20,000 ppm of NaCl was then injected into the sandpack at a steady flow rate of 1 mL/min. Results & Discussion

Characterization of Silica-Reinforced Copolymer

FT-IR analysis was carried out to detect and confirm the functional groups present in the synthesized polymer. The bands observed at 1651 cm–1 and 1661 cm–1 of Figure a,b correspond to the CO stretching vibrations from both, which are attributed to AM and the lactam ring of PVP, respectively. Both the FT-IR spectra in the figure display a broad peak at about 3428–3454 cm–1 representing O–H stretching due to hydrogen bonding., The prominent Si–O peak observed at 1100 cm-1 in Figure a confirms the presence of silica nanoparticles in the silica-reinforced copolymer. The IR spectra of both the synthesized copolymers show the disappearance of bands at 3033 cm–1 and 840–820 cm–1, assigned to the stretching vibrations of the CH bond, indicating the successful opening of the double bond (−CHCH2) of the vinyl group during polymerization, which confirms the formation of the copolymer, both for Gel-A and Gel-B.

Gelation Performance

The effectiveness of gel placement and its plugging performance are primarily governed by the gelation time and the resulting gel strength within the reservoir. Sufficient gelation time is essential to ensure that the gel can be accurately delivered to the intended zones within the reservoir before solidification occurs. A detailed evaluation of the bottle test results was conducted to assess the gelation time and the influence of silica nanoparticles on the gelation kinetics, as well as the resultant gel properties. To assess gelation kinetics and gel strength, polymer–cross-linker solutions with predefined compositions were prepared, sealed in glass ampules, and kept at 120 °C.

Upon continuous monitoring, the same gelation time (Code C) for hydrogels (Gel-A and Gel-B) was obtained at 18 h, indicating a similar rate at the early stage of gelation. However, a transition to a moderately deformable and nonflowing gel (Code G) was specifically observed in Gel-A, representing the active participation of silica nanoparticles in gel development (Figure and Table ). Gel-A took 52 h to form a moderately deformable and nonflowing gel (Code G), while Gel-B was unable to form a gel with Code G even within 100 h of the observation period, and stopped further cross-linking beyond gel Code-F. It can be clearly seen from Figure b that the strength of the gel increases significantly to Code G for Gel-A. The enhanced gel strength observed can be attributed to the addition of silica nanoparticles into the grafted PVP–AM hydrogel matrix, which can be attributed to a synergistic reinforcement mechanism offered by the nanoparticles. The large surface area and reactive surface −OH groups of silica particles promote their interaction with functional groups, particularly the amide and pyrrolidone units present on the polymer backbone. These interactions predominantly occur through hydrogen bonding, giving rise to additional cross-linking points commonly referred to as pseudo cross-links, besides the chemical cross-linking. The additional cross-linking domains contribute to a denser and more entangled three-dimensional polymer network and are likely to be responsible for the accelerated gelation transition (from Code C to Code G) for Gel-A.

Influence of Salt Concentration on Gel Strength and Swelling Behavior

One of the key factors that severely affects gel performance under reservoir conditions is the presence of dissolved salts. For effective water shut-off, it is essential that the gel, once positioned in the target zone, undergoes sufficient swelling to obstruct water flow and, consequently, reduce water production. Achieving swelling without compromising the structural strength of the gel is required for water shut-off applications, as it effectively restricts water movement through high-permeability zones. A comprehensive insight into the swelling behavior and structural strength of the gel is critical for successful field implementation, as it reflects the gel’s capacity to endure saline conditions, especially in the presence of bivalent ions, which are responsible for gel syneresis, and to withstand the mechanical stresses resulting from flowing water. The salinity resistance and swelling behavior of hydrogels, Gel-A and Gel-B, were observed in saline water with varying salinity (2% to 6% KCl) and in formation water (FW), as presented in Figure . The findings indicate that the incorporation of silica into the polyvinylpyrrolidone-acrylamide copolymer enhances the robustness of the resulting hydrogel by forming a rigid and strong polymer network, capable of maintaining the gel strength (Figure a) and opposing uncontrolled swelling (Figure b), in comparison with Gel-B, where silica was not reinforced into the gel network structure. The incorporation of silica into the copolymer matrix contributes to enhancing the mechanical strength of the network structure, which can resist the impact of ions on the gel structure. In contrast, as shown in Figure b, hydrogels without silica-reinforced copolymer display relatively higher swelling ratios. However, we need to control the swelling of the gel, as too much swelling can weaken the gel’s structure, making it more likely to break when exposed to stress or deformation. The enhancement in gel strength in varying salinity conditions may be due to increased cross-linking density, which limits the expansion of polymer chains owing to its stronger network integrity. This reduces the amount of water the gel can absorb, leading to lower swelling , and also be due to energy dissipation mechanisms that arise due to physical interactions between polymer chains and the surface of silica particles, where the chains become adsorbed and contribute to network reinforcement.

Rheological and Viscoelastic Analysis

Flow Behavior

Rheological analyses were conducted at a controlled temperature of 25 °C, with varying shear rates and angular frequency. Prior to rheological assessments, thermal stability was achieved by keeping the samples at 120 °C for one month. Figure presents the viscosity profiles of Gel-A and Gel-B plotted against shear rates varying between 0.1 and 100 s–1. The findings show that shear viscosity decreases as the shear rate increases for both hydrogel samples, reflecting typical shear-thinning behavior, which contributes to low injection pressure during gelant placement or injection in existing fractures, , while Gel-A always maintained a higher viscosity value than that of Gel-B. At low shear rates, the mild shear-thickening behavior observed in Figure in the hydrogels is likely attributed to increased polymer chain entanglement and/or enhanced hydrogen-bonding interactions relative to the initial condition, which contributes to higher flow resistance. As shear rate increases, polymer chains and particles likely adjust their orientation to align with the flow direction under shear stress, which in turn reduces polymer chain entanglement. ,, The higher viscosity in the hydrogel with silica-reinforced copolymer can be attributed to their occupation of microcavities, formed between micrometer-sized polymer domains, by silica nanoparticles. These nanoparticles enhance physical cross-linking through dipolar interaction and hydrogen bonding, restrict polymer chain mobility, and result in a denser, more stable network structure. This phenomenon is also the reason behind the lower swelling of Gel-A compared to Gel-B.

Shear-thinning behavior of hydrogel of Gel-A (PVP + AM + SILICA) and Gel-B (PVP + AM) prepared with and without silica-reinforced copolymer at 25 °C.

Frequency Sweep Test

The rheological characteristics, especially the elastic modulus (G′) and the viscous modulus (G″), can be used to assess the mechanical strength of the hydrogels. The elastic modulus (G′) indicates the elastic or solid-like behavior of the gel, representing the amount of energy stored during deformation that can be recovered. In contrast, the viscous modulus (G″) reflects the viscous or liquid-like behavior, representing the energy dissipated that cannot be recovered. Gel strength of the hydrogels is also evaluated by analyzing the ratio between the elastic modulus (G′) and the viscous modulus (G″). , An increased G′/G″ ratio signifies a predominantly solid-like response, whereas a lower ratio reflects more pronounced liquid-like characteristics within the hydrogel network. The studied samples were kept at 120 °C for one month to ensure stability before conducting the rheological test. The frequency sweep tests were conducted over an angular frequency range of 0.1 to 50 rad/s under constant strain. It is evident from Figure a,b that both the elastic modulus (G′) and the viscous modulus (G″) of Gel-A are significantly higher compared to Gel-B. For both hydrogels, the elastic moduli dominate over the viscous moduli for the studied range of angular frequency, which indicates the dominance of elastic behavior, i.e., predominantly solid-like behavior in their rheological responses.

Thermal Stability Test

A comparative investigation was carried out to understand the key insights into the role of silica nanoparticles in thermal transitions and the stability of the resulting hydrogel networks, and to identify the thermal degradation temperature using Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC). This degradation point typically corresponds to the breakdown of cross-links and other associative interactions that maintain the integrity of the three-dimensional matrix. The thermal behavior observed through DSC reflects not only the robustness of the cross-linked structure but also the nature and extent of physicochemical interactions, including van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and network entanglements, that collectively contribute to the mechanical strength and functional stability of the hydrogel. Therefore, a higher degradation onset temperature is generally indicative of a more thermally stable and mechanically resilient hydrogel system.

The thermal analysis was carried out by heating the samples at a constant rate of 10 °C per minute, using the exothermic-up setting on the differential scanning calorimeter. The scan was carried out across a temperature range of 50 °C to 200 °C. Prior to analysis, hydrogel samples underwent thermal aging at 120 °C for one month.

Figure presents the DSC thermograms of the hydrogel samples, each displaying a single endothermic peak. As the temperature rises, the gels progressively absorb thermal energy, culminating at a characteristic inflection temperature. This thermal response is attributed to the sequential loss of free and bound water from the polymeric network. Beyond the point of inflection, a pronounced decline in heat flow is observed, signifying the complete removal of the water content. The elimination of water leads to the disruption of the hydrogel’s three-dimensional matrix, ultimately resulting in the collapse of its structural integrity and loss of thermal stability. As illustrated in Figure a, the degradation temperature (T deg), at which the structural breakdown in the hydrogel occurs, was observed to be 130.95 °C for Gel-B, which increases to 151.91 °C for Gel-A, as shown in Figure b. This shift in thermal behavior suggests that incorporating silica nanoparticles into the copolymer matrix enhances the structural strength of the network, resulting in improved thermal stability of the hydrogel system.

Water Distribution in the Gels

The mechanical strength of hydrogels is predominantly governed by a combination of direct and indirect molecular interactions within the polymeric network, as noted in previous studies. Hydrogels are capable of undergoing a wide range of direct and indirect interactions. In the case of the poly­(PVP/AM)–SiO2 system (Gel-A) cross-linked with hexamethylenetetramine (HX) and hydroquinone (HQ), several types of direct interactions may take place, including covalent bonds between poly­(PVP/AM)–SiO2–HX-HQ and HX-HQ. Indirect interactions arise primarily due to the presence of water within the hydrogel matrix, which contributes to network dynamics. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the water content in the hydrogels. Cross-linked polymer gels generally hold water in two forms: one being free water and the other being bound water. Free water is loosely held and interacts mainly with other water molecules through hydrogen bonding. In contrast, bound water is directly attached to the polymer surface through chemical bonds and has a crucial role in the structure and behavior of PHPA-based hydrogel. The phase transition of free water from solid to liquid typically occurs near 0 °C. In contrast, bound water exhibits a markedly different phase transition temperature, significantly higher than 0 °C. Such a shift is attributed to the strong molecular interactions occurring between the bound water and the surrounding polymer chains, which restrict its mobility and alter its thermal behavior. As the temperature approaches 0 °C during gradual heating from −30 °C, significant heat absorption corresponding to the melting of frozen free water present in the hydrogel matrix was observed. At this stage of the investigation, the thermal scan was carried out within a temperature window spanning from −30 to 50 °C. For the present investigation, prior to DSC analyses, all hydrogel samples were completely gelled by aging them at 120 °C for 30 days.

Figure a,b illustrates the DSC thermograms of Gel-A and Gel-B, where a single endothermic peak is observed around a temperature of approximately 10 °C for each of the samples. The peak is attributed to the phase transition of frozen free water present in the hydrogel matrix. The prepared gelant solutions, comprising all chemical constituents, were injected into the sandpacks and maintained at 120 °C for 96 h to facilitate the formation of nonflowing gels (designated as Code G). Both gel systems contributed to a reduction in sandpack permeability through their retention within larger flow pathways, their ability to bridge pore throats, and their interaction with sand surfaces via adsorption. However, the hydrogel incorporating the silica-reinforced copolymer exhibited a more pronounced permeability reduction compared to its nonsilica-reinforced counterpart. The initial brine permeability of the sandpacks was measured to be 3.61 Darcy for the PVP + AM gel system and 4.10 Darcy for the PVP + AM + SILICA gel system, respectively. Subsequently, the gelant solution was introduced into the sandpack and maintained at 120 °C for 96 h to ensure complete gelation. After complete gelation, the absolute permeability to brine was recorded as 0.42 Darcy for the PVP + AM copolymer gel system and 0.23 Darcy for the PVP + AM + SILICA copolymer gel system, respectively. The observed percentage permeability reduction (PPR) was 88.36% for the hydrogel based on the nonsilica-reinforced copolymer, whereas the silica-reinforced copolymer hydrogel exhibited a higher reduction of 95.76%. Additionally, the residual resistance factor to water (F rrw) was determined to be 8.59 for the nonreinforced hydrogel and 23.62 for the hydrogel with silica-reinforced copolymer. The enhanced PPR and F rrw values associated with the silica-reinforced hydrogel suggest that incorporating silica nanoparticles during the synthesis of the polyvinylpyrrolidone–acrylamide copolymer significantly enhances its capacity to control water flow in the high-permeability zones commonly encountered in mature oil reservoirs.
