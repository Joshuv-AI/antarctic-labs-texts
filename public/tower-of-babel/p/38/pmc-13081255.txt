Prognostic stratification of chordoid meningiomas: the role of chromosome 1p/22q Loss
Authors: Rosina Paterra, Matteo Zago, Fabio Agistri, Gonzalo Hernandez Gamero, Andrea Mafficini, Serena Pedron, Arianna Berlendis, Filippo Nozzoli, Francesco Di Meco, Valeria Barresi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chordoid is a rare WHO grade 2 meningioma subtype with unpredictable clinical behavior. This study evaluated the prognostic significance of chromosomes 1p and 22q deletions, DNA methylation-based classification, and immunohistochemical expression of ACADL, MCM2, and H3 K27me3 in 44 chordoid meningiomas to assess whether these factors can be used to improve the stratification of these tumors. Deletion of chromosome 1p was detected in 53% of cases and was significantly associated with tumor recurrence and shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS). The concurrent deletion of 1p and 22q was further correlated with poorer outcomes and remained an independent prognostic factor in the multivariate analysis. MCM2 expression was also linked to higher recurrence rates and reduced RFS, whereas DNA methylation classes and H3 K27me3 expression showed no prognostic relevance in this study. These findings support the use of 1p/22q copy number status and MCM2 immunohistochemistry for improved prognostic stratification of chordoid meningiomas, whereas DNA methylation profiling and H3 K27me3 assessment appear less informative for this subtype.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02275-1.

Introduction

The fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of the central nervous system subdivides meningiomas into fifteen histological subtypes and three grades of malignancy [1]. Along with the extent of surgical resection, the WHO grade is a major prognostic factor in patients with meningiomas and is integral to therapeutic decision-making. However, whereas the postsurgical management of WHO grade 1 and 3 meningiomas is well established [1], the treatment strategy for WHO grade 2 meningiomas following gross total resection remains controversial. Owing to the unpredictable clinical course of these tumors, observation or radiotherapy is considered an option for these patients [2]. Therefore, factors enabling the prognostic stratification of WHO grade 2 meningiomas are necessary to identify high-risk cases that would benefit from adjuvant therapy. WHO grade 2 meningiomas include the atypical, chordoid and clear cell subtypes [1]. Among these, the atypical subtype is the most frequent [1], and recent studies have demonstrated that genetic, transcriptomic, or epigenetic features are useful for the prognostic stratification of these tumors [2–6]. Owing to its rarity, with a reported prevalence ranging from 0.36% to 1.64% among meningiomas [7], data on the prognostic value of molecular features in chordoid meningiomas are limited. Due to recurrence rates comparable to those of atypical meningiomas, the chordoid subtype is classified as grade 2, irrespective of the presence of high-grade histopathological features [1, 8, 9]. However, several studies have indicated that the recurrence of these tumors is primarily associated with incomplete resection or the presence of a high mitotic index or necrosis [8, 10–12]. Consequently, the classification of chordoid meningioma as WHO grade 2 in all cases, or its grading in alignment with other subtypes based on histopathological and molecular criteria, remains debatable.

Recent advancements have proposed alternative systems to the WHO grading for the prognostic stratification of meningiomas, such as classifications based on DNA methylation or transcriptomic profiling. According to their DNA methylation profiles, meningiomas can be classified into six methylation classes (MC): three associated with a benign clinical course (MC ben-1, ben-2, and ben-3), two with an intermediate prognosis (MC int-A and int-B), and one with an adverse outcome (MC mal) [6]. Additionally, using DNA methylation, proteomic, and transcriptomic data, meningiomas have been classified into four molecular groups (MG1, MG2, MG3, and MG4), each with a distinct prognosis [5]. Despite the prognostic relevance of molecular-based classifications surpassing that of WHO grading, their adoption in routine practice is hampered by the requirements for advanced technology and associated high costs. However, clinically unfavorable MCs or molecular groups can potentially be identified through the assessment of chromosomal copy number variations (CNV), particularly the loss of chromosome 1p, of which they are enriched, or epigenetic alterations, such as the immunohistochemical loss of the expression of histone 3 trimethylated in lysine-27 (H3 K27me3) [3, 4, 6, 13–15]. Furthermore, unfavorable molecular groups are enriched in the immunohistochemical expression of specific proteins, with MG3 identified by ACADL and MG4 by MCM2 [5, 16], which can aid in their identification. Although the prognostic significance of 1p deletion and ACADL and MCM2 immunoexpression has been demonstrated in atypical meningiomas [4, 16, 17], no data are available on chordoid meningiomas. Similarly, the prognostic relevance of MC is underexplored in this subtype. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of DNA methylation-based classification, chromosome 1p deletion, other chromosomal copy number variations (CNV), and ACADL, MCM2, and H3 K27me3 immunohistochemical expression in chordoid meningiomas to assess whether these factors can be used to improve the stratification of these tumors.

Materials and methods

Cases

We included all primary chordoid meningiomas diagnosed between 2010 and 2024, with available paraffin blocks, at the Units of Anatomic Pathology of Istituto Neurologico Carlo Besta, Milan, Italy, Careggi University Hospital, Florence, Italy, and the University and Hospital Integrated in Verona, Italy.

Ethical issues

This study was approved by Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 4 (protocol n. 49 − 25, 2025/04/16), Local Ethics Committee of Tuscany (protocol n. 27080_BIO) and Comitato Etico per la Sperimentazione Clinica delle province di Verona e Rovigo (protocol n. 40400, 2019/07/19), and was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Histological revision

We reviewed the histological slides of all cases to assess the number of mitoses per 1.6 mm2, brain invasion, and “minor atypical criteria” (hypercellularity, macronucleoli, small cells with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, spontaneous necrosis, and sheeting), as previously described [18].

Clinical data

Information on tumor localization, extent of surgical resection, and recurrence-free survival (RFS) was retrieved from clinical records and operatory registries. Patients lost to follow-up were censored at their last documented clinical encounter.

Tumor sites were classified into four groups: (1) convexity, (2) parasagittal/tentorium, (3) skull base, and (4) ventricle.

Immunohistochemistry

All samples were immunostained using antibodies against MCM2 (clone 1E7, dilution 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology), ACADL (polyclonal, dilution 1:200, Sigma-Aldrich), H3 K27me3 (clone C36B11; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; dilution 1:200), and an automated immunostainer. To assess ACADL and MCM2 immunostaining, the immunostained slides were first scanned at low-power magnification to identify areas with positive cells. Thereafter, we counted the positively stained tumor cell nuclei among 1000 tumor cells. Cases with > 5% stained cells were considered positive for MCM2 or ACADL [16, 17]. Based on MCM2 and ACADL positivity, meningiomas were subdivided into three immunohistochemical groups (IHC-G): (i) IHC-G1, comprising cases negative for both ACADL and MCM2; (ii) IHC-G2, including cases positive for ACADL but negative for MCM2 (presumably corresponding to MG3); and (iii) IHC-G3, consisting of cases positive for MCM2 regardless of ACADL immunostaining (presumably corresponding to MG4).

H3K27me3 immunohistochemical expression was classified as follows: (i) retained when nuclear staining was observed in ≥ 5% neoplastic cells; (ii) lost when staining was absent in > 95% neoplastic cells and present in internal positive controls (endothelium and neurons); (iii) non-conclusive when staining was absent in both normal and neoplastic cells [19].

DNA methylation analysis

Methylation analysis was performed in all cases, using the Illumina Infinium Methylation EPIC v2.0 BeadChip targeting ~ 930 K unique methylation sites on 500 ng of DNA extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections in enriched tumor areas (tumor purity > 90%). Raw DNA methylation files (.idat) were imported and processed using the minfi package in R. Cases were classified using the Heidelberg methylation classifier v. 12.8 (accessible at " https://epignostix.com/“) and the Bethesda NCI CNS tumor classifier v2.0 (accessible at https://methylscape.ccr.cancer.gov/).

DNA methylation arrays were used to assess CNV. Generation of the copy number profiles was based on conumee2 [20].

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis

The copy number of chromosomes 1p and 22q was analyzed by FISH in twenty-seven meningiomas.

In detail, 3 μm sections were processed with LSI 1p36/1q25 Dual-Color Probe Set assay (Vysis/Abbott, Molecular Europe, Wiesbaden, Germany) and NF2/22q13.33 FISH Probe Kit (Cytotest Inc, MD, USA), following the manufacturers’ protocols. Slides were examined using an Olympus BX61 fluorescence microscope equipped with a 100x oil immersion objective and a triple band pass filter for simultaneous detection of Spectrum Orange, Spectrum Green, and DAPI signals. A total of 500 non-overlapping nuclei were counted for each sample. Cases were classified as follows: (i) 1p deleted when two reference probe signals (1q) and one target probe signal (1p) were detected in at least 50% of cells; (ii) 1p non-deleted when two reference probe signals (1q) and two target probe signals (1p) were present in > 50% of cells; (iii) 22q-deleted when one signal for each probe was detected in at least 50% of cells; (iv) 22q non-deleted when two probe signals were present in > 50% of cells.

Next-generation sequencing

NGS was performed for 12 meningiomas. These cases were explored using the SureSelectXT HS CD Glasgow Cancer Core assay (www.agilent.com), hereafter referred to as CORE, as described previously [21]. This spans 1.85 Mb of the genome and interrogates 174 genes for somatic mutations, copy number alterations, and structural rearrangements (details in Supplementary File 1).

Gene copy number alterations were detected using GeneCN software (https://github.com/wwcrc/geneCN). Whole-chromosome or chromosome-arm alterations were assessed by evaluating the ratio of normalized GC-adjusted coverage of tumor sample alignments to the mean normalized GC-adjusted coverage of 20 non-neoplastic samples for all targeted regions of a chromosome arm. The targeted regions included both targeted genes and a set of “backbone” regions probing each chromosome at 1 megabase interval. Each large alteration was further confirmed by checking the copy number status of the targeted genes included in the large alteration, as reported by GeneCN software.

Copy-neutral LOH events and gain after LOH events were detected by examining the b-allele frequency (BAF) for all SNPs (population frequency) detected in the targeted regions using Varscan2 software [22]. Sequencing artifacts were removed by subtracting SNPs calls recurring in all samples and by visual verification of alignments. A minimum of five contiguous SNPs on independent target regions showing a coherent median absolute deviation (Wilcoxon signed rank test p < 0.05) from the expected 50% variant allele frequency was used as a threshold for copy-neutral LOH calling. BAF analysis was also used to verify the GeneCN calls.

Following the five-tier classification system recommended by the joint consensus of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) [23], variants were classified: Benign (class 1); Likely Benign (class 2); Variant of Un-certain Significance (VUS – class 3); Likely Pathogenic (class 4); Pathogenic (class 5). When available, variant classification was retrieved from the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) and accepted when the record complied with the following criteria: review by an expert panel according to the ACMG/AMP guidelines and/or report by multiple submitters with evaluation criteria according to the ACMG/AMP guidelines and no conflicts. When a consistent classification was unavailable or when the variant was not present in the ClinVar database, variants were evaluated in-house, according to the ACMG/AMP guidelines using also the following databases and software to gather and integrate all relevant information: My Cancer Genome (https://www.mycancergenome.org), Intogen [24] (https://www.intogen.org/), QIAGEN Clinical Insight (QCI) software (https://variants.Qagenbioinformatics.eu/qci/) and Franklyn (https://franklin.genoox.com).

Statistical analyses

We used the chi-square test to analyze the correlations between the various clinical (sex and age of the patients), histopathological (mitotic index, brain invasion, or other atypical criteria), immunohistochemical (expression of ACADL, MCM2, and H3 K27me3), or genetic (1p and 22q deletion) features, and tumor recurrence, or between 1p/22q codeletion and histopathological or immunohistochemical features, or the presence of other chromosomal losses.

Finally, RFS was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with the date of primary surgery as the entry point and the detection of a recurrent tumor as the endpoint. The Mantel-Cox log-rank test was used to assess the strength of the association between clinical, histological, immunohistochemical, or genetic parameters and RFS. Subsequently, multivariate analysis (Cox regression model) was used to determine the independent effects of the variables on RFS.

A probability (P) value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc 12.1.4.0 statistical software (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium).

Results

Cases

Forty-four chordoid meningiomas were included in this study. These tumors were resected from 18 males and 26 females (median age: 50 years; range: 28–80 years).

Twenty-six cases were localized at the brain convexity, six were sagittal or tentorial, 11 were located at the skull base, and one was ventricular (Fig. 1).

The extent of resection, determined according to the Simpson grade [25], was grade 1 in 34 cases, grade 2 in 3, grade 3 in 2, and grade 4 in 5. Six patients (including four with Simpson grade 4) received adjuvant radiotherapy after surgery.

Follow-up information was available for 41 patients in the study. Twelve meningiomas recurred during follow-up. RFS ranged between 4 and 156 months (median: 26 months) for recurring cases, and between 4 and 157 months (median: 56 months) for cases that did not recur. Mean follow-up time was 56 months.

Conversely, the recurrence frequency and RFS length were not associated with the extent of surgical resection or tumor site (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Histopathological features

Eight meningiomas had a high mitotic index (≥ 4 mitoses/1.6 mm2). Four cases, including one with a high mitotic index, were brain-invasive, and three meningiomas displayed spontaneous necrosis (Fig. 1). Hypercellularity, sheeting, and prominent nucleoli were not observed in any of the cases.

Recurrence was significantly more frequent in meningiomas with a high mitotic index (P = 0.0314) or spontaneous necrosis (P = 0.0057) (Table 1). Patients with meningiomas characterized by these histopathological features had a significantly shorter RFS than those with meningiomas featuring a low mitotic index (P = 0.0001) or lacking spontaneous necrosis (P = 0.0031) (Table 2).

Immunohistochemical markers

ACADL immunohistochemical expression was detected in 27 (61%) cases, with positive neoplastic cell ranging between 20% and 90% (Fig. 2A).

MCM2 immunostaining was observed in 26 (59%) meningiomas, with stained cell ranging between 10% and 50% (Fig. 2B).

Seven cases lacked both ACADL and MCM2 expression (IHC-G1), 11 meningiomas were ACADL+/MCM2- (IHC-G2), and 23 cases were MCM2+ (IHC-G3).

Recurrence was significantly more frequent in meningiomas with MCM2 expression (IHC-G3) than in those with only ACADL expression (IHC-G2) and those lacking both MCM2 and ACADL expression (IHC-G1) (P = 0.0104) (Table 1). Patients with meningiomas characterized by MCM2 expression (IHC-G3) had significantly shorter RFS compared to patients with meningiomas lacking MCM2 expression (IHC-G1/G2) (P = 0.0296) (Table 2).

H3 K27me3 expression was retained in 32 meningiomas, lost in 5, and inconclusive in 7 (Fig. 2C). No significant correlations were found between H3 K27me3 expression and recurrence or RFS (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

DNA methylation analysis

DNA fragmentation hindered the acquisition of conclusive results in seven cases of this study.

Using the Heidelberg classifier, eleven cases did not match any MCs (Fig. 1). The remaining 26 cases matched the MC meningioma benign (calibrated scores > 0.90 in 19 cases, between 0.5 and 0.9 in 6 cases, and < 0.5 in one case), with 5 meningiomas assigned to ben-2 methylation subclass (calibrated scores > 0.90 in 1 case, and between 0.5 and 0.9 in 4 cases), and 19 to ben-3 methylation subclass (calibrated scores > 0.90 in 9 cases, between 0.5 and 0.90 in 8 cases, and between 0.3 and 0.5 in 2 cases) (Fig. 1).

Using the Bethesda Classifier, three cases did not match any MCs, 15 matched the ben-2 MC (calibrated scores > 0.90 in 6 cases, and between 0.5 and 0.9 in 9), 17 matched the ben-3 MC (calibrated scores > 0.90 in 13 cases and between 0.5 and 0.9 in 4), one case matched the MC ben-1 (calibrated score: 0.734), and 1 matched the MC Int-A (calibrated score: 0.815). The MC and calibrated scores are detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

No differences in recurrence rates or RFS length were observed according to either the Heidelberg or Bethesda MCs (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Three patients had MGMT promoter methylation (cases 6, 16, and 17).

CNV and NGS analysis

1p and 22q copy number was analyzed using DNA methylation arrays in all meningiomas. The obtained results were compared with those achieved using FISH in 27 meningiomas and NGS in 12. DNA methylation analysis was inconclusive in seven cases owing to DNA fragmentation, whereas FISH analysis was inconclusive in three cases owing to technical artifacts. Finally, the copy number of chromosomes 1p and 22q was determined in 40 meningiomas using either DNA methylation arrays or FISH (Fig. 1). Twenty-one cases (53%) exhibited a 1p deletion (Fig. 3A), and 14 (35%) harbored a 22q deletion (Fig. 3B), with results consistent with DNA methylation arrays, FISH, or NGS. 1p deletion involved the whole chromosomal arm in 5/21 cases, whereas the remaining 16 cases (76%) harbored a segmental deletion of chromosome 1p, invariably involving the 1p36 region. For the following analyses, both cases with complete and segmental deletions were classified as “1p-deleted”. The 22q deletion involved the entire chromosome arm in all the cases. 1p and 22q deletions co-occurred in 10/21 (48%) patients.

Meningiomas harboring concurrent 1p and 22q deletions exhibited a significantly higher mitotic index (P = 0.0185) relative to non-codeleted tumors, whereas necrosis (P = 0.111) and MCM2 expression (P = 0.1531) did not differ significantly between the two groups.

1p deletion was significantly associated with meningioma recurrence (P = 0.0364) (Table 1) and, in the log-rank test, including 37 cases, it was significantly associated with shorter RFS length (P = 0.0014) (Table 2) (Fig. 4A). Notably, RFS was significantly shorter in patients with meningiomas harboring both 1p and 22q deletions than in those with meningiomas featuring only 1p deletion without 22q deletion (P = 0.0005; Table 2; Fig. 4B).

CNV of other chromosomes was inferred using DNA methylation arrays, in 37 evaluable cases (Fig. 3C).

2p and 3p deletions were detected in 9/37 (24%) and 8/37 (22%) cases, respectively (Fig. 1), with no significant associations with recurrence or RFS (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Four meningiomas harbored chromosomal losses on 6, 10, 14, and 18. Patients with meningiomas featuring at least two of these chromosomal losses had significantly higher frequency of tumor recurrence and shorter RFS (Tables 1 and 2). However, these genetic alterations were consistently associated with the loss of chromosomes 1p and 22q (Fig. 1) (4/4 cases; P = 0.0002).

All 1p/22q codeleted meningiomas exhibited additional chromosomal CNVs (Fig. 1).

CDKN2A/B hemizygous deletion was detected in 5/37 (14%) cases, with no association with recurrence or RFS (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). No cases exhibited CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion.

Cox regression model, including only variables with a significant prognostic value on RFS (age and sex of the patients, mitotic index, spontaneous necrosis, IHC group, and 1p/22q deletion), demonstrated that 1p/22q deletion was significantly and independently associated with a shorter RFS (P = 0.0232; Hazard Ratio: 18.2; 95% Confidence Interval: 1.4–227) (Table 2). None of the other variables incorporated into the Cox proportional hazards model demonstrated a statistically significant association with RFS. Not all variables assessed at univariate analysis were included in multivariate models to avoid overparameterization relative to the number of events.

Gene mutations

Of the 12 meningiomas analyzed using NGS​, one (case 3) had a pTERT mutation (c. 124 C > T; variant allele frequency [VAF]: 42%), two (cases 3 and 5) had mutations in NF2 (p. R310*, VAF 77%; p. Q320*, 58%), two (cases 6 and 11) had mutations in PIK3CA (p. K409Q, VAF 43%; p. E542K VAF 30%), and one (case 9) had a KLF4 mutation (p. K409Q, VAF 43%) (case 9).

Discussion

Chordoid meningioma is a rare subtype characterized histologically by a “chordoma-like” appearance, with epithelioid or occasionally spindled cells arranged in chords within a mucoid matrix [1]. Although classified as WHO grade 2, several studies have indicated that the recurrence of chordoid meningioma is primarily linked to histological features of malignancy or partial surgical resection [8, 10–12], suggesting that the universal assignment of WHO grade 2 may not accurately reflect the biological heterogeneity of this subtype.

In this cohort of 44 chordoid meningiomas, a high mitotic index (≥ 4/ 1.6 mm2) and spontaneous necrosis were significantly associated with tumor recurrence and shorter RFS. However, only four out of twelve recurring meningiomas displayed a high mitotic index, and only three showed spontaneous necrosis, suggesting that histopathological features alone are insufficient to predict the recurrence risk of chordoid meningiomas. Regarding the impact of surgical resection, 10 of 12 recurring meningiomas were classified as Simpson grade 1, demonstrating that other factors play a significant role in chordoid meningioma recurrence.

The Consortium to Inform Molecular and Practical Approaches to CNS Tumor Taxonomy-Not Official WHO (cIMPACT-NOW) has recently issued an update recommending molecular testing in scenarios where histopathological grading may be uncertain, such as that of chordoid meningiomas [26]. In detail, cIMPACT-NOW recommends that meningiomas with 1p/ 22q codeletion, or a DNA methylation profile consistent with intermediate (Int) or malignant (Mal) MCs should be biologically considered as WHO grade 2. Conversely, cases lacking 1p/22q codeletion or those with a DNA methylation profile consistent with the benign MCs ben-1 and ben-2 should be biologically considered as WHO grade 1 [26]. However, this approach was mainly validated in non-chordoid meningiomas [3–6, 26], whereas specific data on the chordoid subtype were limited. Findings of the present study support the cIMPACT-NOW recommendation to assess the 1p/22q copy number status for the prognostic stratification of chordoid meningiomas [26]. Indeed, 1p/22q codeletion was significantly associated with chordoid meningioma recurrence, being identified in 6/10 (60%) recurring meningiomas and only 4/27 (15%) nonrecurring meningiomas. Furthermore, it was significantly correlated with a shorter RFS and maintained prognostic significance in the multivariate analysis including histological factors as covariates. Although the presence of 1p deletion alone demonstrated prognostic value, this alteration also occurred in 41% of nonrecurring chordoid meningiomas. Moreover, cases with only 1p deletion exhibited significantly longer survival than those with 1p/22q codeletion. The consistent results obtained from DNA methylation arrays, FISH, and NGS indicate the reliability of all these methods for detecting 1p and 22q copy number variations in routine practice. Confirming the previous findings reported by Daoud et al. that chordoid meningiomas are genetically different from non-chordoid meningiomas [7], 1p deletion was the most frequent genetic alteration, occurring in 53% of cases, whereas 22q deletion was found in 35%, and the two alterations co-occurred in only 50% of cases. In addition, in line with previous observations, this study confirmed 2p deletion as a genetic hallmark of chordoid meningiomas, present in 25% of cases compared to 3.8% in non-chordoid meningiomas [7, 9].

Although cIMPACT-NOW recommends [26] that the presence of at least two chromosomal losses among chromosomes 6, 10, 14, and 18 is prognostically equivalent to the loss of 1p/22q, these chromosomal alterations invariably coincided in this study. Moreover, it should be emphasized that 1p/22q codeletion invariably co-occurred with additional chromosomal CNV, demonstrating that these tumors are characterized by pronounced genomic instability.

Consistent with previous observations, 2p deletion was not prognostically significant [7, 9].

Established molecular markers associated with aggressive behavior in meningiomas include CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion and pTERT mutation, both of which are currently recognized as criteria for assignment to CNS WHO grade 3, regardless of histological subtype [1]. In the present cohort, no cases showed CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion, and pTERT mutation was detected in only a single tumor, preventing meaningful evaluation of their prognostic impact in chordoid meningiomas. The rarity of these alterations in our series is consistent with previous studies, which reported no pTERT mutations in 32 chordoid meningiomas and only a single case harboring CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion [7, 9]. CDKN2A/B hemizygous deletion was detected in five cases; however, in contrast to what has been reported for atypical meningiomas, this alteration was not associated with recurrence in our cohort [17].

In contrast to that reported in two previous studies on chordoid meningiomas [6, 20], methylation-based classification was not prognostically informative in this series. Indeed, most meningiomas were classified as ben-3 by both the Heidelberg (53%) and Bethesda (47%) classifiers, whereas only one matched Int MC using the Bethesda classifier, and none matched Mal MC using both classifiers. Because IMPACT-NOW does not provide any recommendations on whether meningiomas matching ben-3 MC should be considered low- or high-risk [26], prognostic stratification based on DNA methylation profiling was unfeasible in the majority of chordoid meningiomas in this cohort.

This study is the first to compare DNA methylation-based classification of chordoid meningiomas obtained using Heidelberg and Bethesda classifiers. Our findings demonstrated inconsistency in the attributed MC in 35% of cases, with 28% of cases not matching any MC using the former classifier, and 5% using the latter classifier. Five meningiomas (13%) with the Heidelberg and 15 (40%) with the Bethesda Classifier matched the ben-2 MC, and, according to the cIMPACT-NOW recommendations, should be considered as biologically grade 1. Consistently, none of the cases classified as ben-2 by the Heildeberg classifier, and only one classified as such by the Bethesda classifier, exhibited 1p/22q deletion.

Meningiomas classified in the unfavorable MCs were previously demonstrated to exhibit higher frequency of H3 K27me3 immunohistochemical loss [5], and the loss of H3 K27me3 immunohistochemical expression was significantly correlated with shorter RFS in patients with grade 2 meningiomas [27]. In this series, 13.5% of cases lost H3 K27me3 expression, in accordance with reported rates of 10–14% in other cohorts of chordoid meningiomas [14, 28]. Although our findings may be influenced by a consistent percentage (16%) of inconclusive assays, the loss of H3 K27me3 expression was not prognostically relevant.

Beyond the 1p/22q codeletion, MCM2 immunoexpression, along with male sex and age over 65, showed prognostic relevance in this cohort, though they did not emerge as independent prognostic variables in the multivariate analysis. The prognostic significance of ACADL and MCM2 immunohistochemical expression in chordoid meningiomas had not been previously explored. The findings revealed a significant association between MCM2 expression and tumor recurrence, as well as reduced RFS, highlighting its link to unfavourable outcomes in the chordoid subtype, similar to observations in the atypical meningioma subtype [17].

Finally, consistent with findings in the atypical subtype [18], male sex emerged as a negative prognostic factor in this series of chordoid meningiomas. Although age was not associated with shorter RFS in other meningioma cohorts of meningiomas, as observed in this study, this might depend on the different cut-offs utilized in the analyses [29–31].

Finally, consistent with findings in the atypical subtype [13], male sex emerged as a negative prognostic factor in this series of chordoid meningiomas. Although age was not associated with shorter RFS in other meningioma cohorts, as observed in this study, this might depend on the different cut-offs used in the analyses [12, 16, 29].

Conclusions

Our study underscores the prognostic significance of histopathological characteristics, such as a high mitotic index and spontaneous necrosis, in chordoid meningioma. However, a notable proportion of tumors that do not exhibit histopathological features of malignancy still recur, indicating the potential influence of additional variables on the clinical outcomes. 1p/22q deletion emerged as the most significant prognostic factor for recurrence risk in chordoid meningiomas, maintaining its significance in the multivariate analysis for RFS. Comparative analyses of DNA methylation arrays, FISH, and NGS revealed that all these assays are reliable for identifying both 1p and 22q deletions in routine practice. These findings support the incorporation of routine assessment of 1p/22q status into the diagnostic workup of chordoid meningiomas, even after gross total resection. The immunohistochemical expression of MCM2, previously proposed as a surrogate marker for the unfavorable meningioma molecular group termed “hyperproliferative“ [5], was also associated with higher recurrence rates and shorter RFS in patients with chordoid meningiomas. In contrast, the evaluation of methylation classes was not pertinent for the prognostic stratification of chordoid meningiomas, as nearly all cases were classified as benign methylation classes, particularly ben-3 MC, which is not included in the biological risk assessment flowchart proposed by cIMPACT-NOW [26]. Finally, our findings do not support the use of H3 K27me3 immunoexpression for the prognostic stratification of chordoid meningioma.
