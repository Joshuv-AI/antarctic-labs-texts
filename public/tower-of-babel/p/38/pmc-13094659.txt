Sex-specific gut mycobiome dysbiosis activates macrophage dectin-1 signaling to drive neurobehavioral deficits in EcoHIV-infected mice
Authors: Yannan Li, Shinji Sakamoto, Matthew D Smith, Yuto Hasegawa, Meixiang Huang, Naigang Li, Manjot Dhaliwal, Tessa Schauermann, Feiyi Xiong, Bin Luo, Hui Li, Qingsheng Li, Peter A Calabresi, David Volsky, Barbara S Slusher, Atsushi Kamiya, Xiaolei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHIV-associated neuropsychiatric and neurocognitive disorders remain prevalent in people living with HIV (PWH) despite antiretroviral therapy (ART), underscoring the need for novel mechanistic insights and therapeutic strategies. Emerging evidence suggests that gut fungal dysbiosis contributes to neuroimmune dysfunction. Nevertheless, its contribution to HIV-associated psychosocial and cognitive deficits is poorly understood. Here, using the murine EcoHIV infection model that recapitulates key features of HIV neuropathogenesis, we identify a female-specific gut fungal signature associated with EcoHIV-induced psychosocial and cognitive impairments. Although EcoHIV infection induces behavioral impairments in both male and female mice, the present study identifies a distinct, female-associated mechanism involving gut Malassezia globosa enrichment and activation of a dectin-1+ intestinal macrophage axis. Treatment with pachyman, a fungal 1,3-β-D-glucan derived from Poria cocos and an established dectin-1 ligand, reversed these deficits without affecting locomotor or anxiety-related behaviors. Mechanistically, EcoHIV infection increased the frequency of colonic dectin-1+ macrophages, which correlated with both social avoidance and Malassezia globosa abundance, and pachyman treatment reversed this increase. EcoHIV infection also disrupted immune-related metabolic pathways in the gut, which were restored by pachyman treatment. We further observed a parallel increase in dectin-1+ circulating monocytes in ART-treated PWH, supporting a conserved dectin-1–driven innate immune signature across mouse and human studies. These findings identify a female-associated gut Malassezia globosa–dectin-1+ macrophage axis linked to HIV-associated psychosocial and cognitive deficits, and suggest pachyman as a potential candidate for integrative therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Gut mycobiome, Macrophage, Dectin-1, EcoHIV, Psychosocial and cognitive deficits, β-glucan

Introduction

HIV-associated neuropsychiatric and neurocognitive impairments remain stubbornly prevalent despite the widespread success of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART). More than 30% of people living with HIV (PWH) experience depression—nearly twice the rate observed in the general population—and responses to current antidepressants remain limited, reflecting gaps in mechanistic understanding that impede prevention and treatment (Bengtson et al., 2017; Eshun-Wilson et al., 2018; Malhi and Mann, 2018). In parallel, HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) persist in the cART era: large cohorts and meta-analyses estimate an overall HAND prevalence of roughly 40–50% across clinical phenotypes, even in treated populations, underscoring enduring CNS vulnerability and unmet therapeutic need (Saylor et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020).

Recent evidence suggests that disruption of the gut–immune–brain axis is implicated in neurocognitive impairments observed in PWH (Carrico et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2024; Rich et al., 2020). While most studies have focused on alterations in gut bacterial communities and their metabolites, far less attention has been given to the gut mycobiome (Hager and Ghannoum, 2018; Vujkovic-Cvijin and Somsouk, 2019), even though cross-kingdom ecological interactions are increasingly recognized as important contributors to gut microbial remodeling (Kruger et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2025; Santus et al., 2021). Interestingly, circulating 1,3-β-D-glucan—a fungal cell-wall polysaccharide—has emerged clinically as a biomarker of leaky gut and has been associated with neurocognitive deficits in PWH (Hoenigl et al., 2016a; Morris et al., 2012). Fungal cell wall β-D-glucans are potent immunomodulators that signal through pattern recognition receptors such as dectin-1, making the gut mycobiome as a potential regulator of mucosal immunity and metabolic pathways with downstream effects on behavior (Iliev, 2015; Kamiya et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024).

Sex shapes mucosal immunity and microbial ecology—including fungal recognition pathways—raising the possibility that mycobiome-immune effects on behavior differ by sex (Santinelli et al., 2020; Scully et al., 2019; Vemuri et al., 2019; Ziegler and Altfeld, 2016). In PWH, women typically exhibit stronger innate and adaptive immune responses than men, along with distinct patterns of immune activation, differences that can modulate gut-immune-brain crosstalk (Santinelli et al., 2020; Scully et al., 2019; Ziegler and Altfeld, 2016). Given that sex influences interactions among microbiota, hormones, and immunity, identifying the mechanistic substrate underlying sex-dependent vulnerability in neuroinflammation and behavior is crucial for understanding the pathophysiology of neurocognitive impairments in PWH (Caldarelli et al., 2024; Vemuri et al., 2019).

In this study, we found that EcoHIV infection in mice produced sex-specific gut mycobiome changes. Female mice exhibited selective expansion of the fungal species Malassezia globosa, which was associated with impaired social interaction and recognition memory deficits. Treatment with pachyman, a fungal 1,3-β-D-glucan derived from Poria cocos and an established dectin-1 ligand, reversed these behavioral deficits, reduced EcoHIV-induced colonic dectin-1+ macrophages elevation, and corrected EcoHIV-related disturbances in tryptophan and lipid metabolites. These findings characterize a fungal-microbiome-driven, dectin-1-dependent immunometabolic pathway in the HIV gut-brain axis and support pharmacologic dectin-1 modulation as a therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics statement

Eight-week-old male and female C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and acclimated in the Johns Hopkins animal facility for one week prior to experimentation. Unless otherwise specified, mice were housed in groups according to their EcoHIV infection status under standard laboratory conditions (21 °C, 40–60% humidity) with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and free access to food and water.

All experimental procedures and animal care were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Johns Hopkins University Animal Care and Use Committee.

EcoHIV preparation and infection of mice

EcoHIV was generated according to established protocols (Gu et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2019). Briefly, HEK293T cells were transfected with a plasmid containing the EcoNDK backbone engineered to include the gp120 V5C5 fragment (Gu et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2019; Potash et al., 2005). Virus was harvested from clarified culture supernatants by centrifugation, and concentrations were quantified using a p24 ELISA kit (Advanced Biosciences Laboratory, Cat#5421, Rockville, MD, USA). EcoHIV infection was performed as previously reported (Nedelcovych et al., 2019). In brief, mice received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 4 × 106 pg of EcoHIV viral particles, quantified by p24 ELISA (Advanced Biosciences Laboratory, Cat#5421, Rockville, MD, USA), or an equal volume of saline as a control.

Analysis of human gut mycobiome ITS clone-library data

Human gut mycobiome data were obtained from a previously published study of PWH that performed fungal ITS profiling of fecal samples using an ITS clone-library approach (Hamad et al., 2018). Detection was quantified as clone count, defined as the number of cloned ITS sequences recovered from a given sample that were sequenced and assigned to a given molecular operational taxonomic unit (MOTU). Participants were stratified by HIV status (control vs PWH) and sex (male vs female) based on the demographic metadata reported in the original study.

Drug and schedule of treatment

Pachyman treatment was initiated three weeks after EcoHIV infection and administered daily until the experimental endpoint. Mice received oral gavage of pachyman (200 mg/kg; >98% purity, Megazyme, Bray, Ireland) or vehicle for 14 consecutive days. This dosing regimen has previously been validated for modulating immune function in murine models (Zhu et al., 2023b). All procedures were performed with minimal handling to reduce animal stress.

Mouse fecal sample collection and DNA extraction

Fresh fecal pellets were collected and immediately stored at − 80 °C until processing. Approximately 100 mg of frozen feces were transferred to QIAGEN PowerBead tubes and homogenized using a QIAGEN PowerLyzer at 1,500 rpm for 90 s, followed by a 90 s rest and a second 90 s homogenization at the same speed. Genomic DNA was then isolated using the PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

ITS sequencing and analysis of mouse feces samples

ITS amplicon sequencing and analysis were performed using a workflow adapted from our ISNV 2025 abstract and refined based on publicly available protocols and vendor documentation (Li et al., 2025; Simms et al., 2025). Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from freshly collected fecal samples and quantified using the Qubit 2.0 DNA HS Assay (ThermoFisher, Cat# Q32854, Massachusetts, MA, USA). Approximately 50 ng of DNA per sample was amplified via PCR using vendor-synthesized, internally validated primer pairs covering the fungal ITS1-ITS2 regions (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA). Following library preparation, Illumina® 8-nt dual-end indices were added as barcodes. Libraries were quantified and quality-assessed using Qubit 2.0 (ThermoFisher, Massachusetts, USA) and TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), pooled at molar equivalence, and sequenced on an Illumina® MiSeq V2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) platform using a read length configuration of 250 PE for 0.1 M PE reads per sample (50 K in each direction). Amplicon data were processed using the CosmosID amplicon workflow (CosmosID Inc., Germantown, MD, USA) following the vendor’s standard protocol. Briefly, raw FASTQ files underwent adapter and quality trimming using bbduk from the BBMap suite (per-base Q ≥ 25, average Q ≥ 20, minimum read length = 36). For paired-end data, overlapping read pairs were merged and non-overlapping reads were retained. Reads were then converted to FASTA and clustered by closed-reference OTU picking at 97% similarity against the CosmosID ITS reference database; taxonomic labels were assigned from this database. Feature tables (OTU counts and relative abundances) were exported for downstream analyses.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and analysis of mouse fecal samples

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed on fecal DNA from female control and EcoHIV-infected mice, following the previously published workflow (Zhu et al., 2023b). Briefly, sequencing libraries were generated using a Nextera XT library preparation kit (Illumina) with minor modifications, and library yield was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (paired-end, 2 × 150 bp) according to the manufacturer’s standard procedures. Raw, unassembled reads were analyzed using the CosmosID bioinformatics platform (CosmosID Inc.) to obtain multi-kingdom taxonomic profiles and species-level relative abundance estimates.

Behavioral tests

A battery of behavioral assays was conducted in the following sequence: open field test (OFT), social interaction test (SIT), elevated plus maze (EPM), and novel object recognition test (NORT).

OFT

The OFT was performed following published protocols with minor modifications (Zhu et al., 2023b). Each mouse was gently placed in the center of the open field chamber (40 × 40 cm for each chamber) and allowed to explore freely for 30 min. Locomotor activity was assessed using the Photobeam Activity System (PAS-Open Field, San Diego Instruments), which uses two stacked 16 × 16 grids of infrared beams to detect movements such as ambulation and rearing. Single-beam interruptions were automatically recorded as activity counts, and the totals were used for analysis. The apparatus was housed in a ventilated cabinet to ensure consistent testing conditions.

SIT

The SIT was conducted in a three-chambered apparatus (40 × 20 × 26 cm for each chamber) following previously described methods (Sakamoto et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2023b). Mice were first habituated in the center chamber for 10 min, then allowed to explore all three empty chambers for another 10 min. In the final phase, a stranger mouse of the same strain was placed in a cage located in one side chamber, while an inanimate object was placed in the opposite side. Mice were then allowed to freely explore the arena for 10 min. Movements and interactions were recorded using the TopScan 3.0 system. The apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues, and all tests were performed in the dark to reduce anxiety.

EPM

The elevated plus maze was performed using our previously published protocol (Zhu et al., 2023b). The EPM apparatus consisted of two opposing open arms (30 × 5 × 0.5 cm each) and two enclosed arms (30 × 5 × 15 cm each), connected by a central platform (5 × 5 cm), which was elevated 61 cm above the floor. Mice were acclimated in a dimly lit room for at least 30 min before testing. During the 5-minute trial, time spent in both open and closed arms was recorded using the TopScan 3.0 video tracking system (CleverSys). Time spent in the open arms was used as an index of anxiety-like behavior.

NORT

NORT was conducted using a Plexiglas arena (20 × 40 × 22 cm) as previously reported, with slight modifications (Hasegawa et al., 2023). During the 10-minute training phase, mice were exposed to two identical objects positioned in adjacent corners. After a 30-minute delay, one familiar object was replaced with a novel object, and mice were allowed 5 min to explore. The time spent interacting with each object was recorded. Object recognition performance was calculated as the ratio of time spent exploring the novel object to the total exploration time.

Isolation of colonic lamina propria mononuclear cells

Colonic lamina propria (LP) mononuclear cells were isolated using a protocol adapted from our previously published method (Peters et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023b). Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and then perfused transcardially with 20 ml of cold PBS to remove circulating blood. The colon was carefully excised, Peyer’s patches were removed, and mesenteric fat was trimmed away. The tissue was then opened longitudinally and thoroughly rinsed in PBS to remove fecal contents. The cleaned colon was cut into 0.5–1 cm fragments and incubated three times in HBSS containing 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), 4 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, and 8% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C with agitation (250 rpm, 20 min per wash) to remove the epithelial layer. Following this, tissues were washed with Ca2+/Mg2+-containing PBS to eliminate EDTA, minced into fine fragments, and digested in HBSS supplemented with 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), 5% FBS, and 0.2 mg/ml collagenase D (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) for 20 min at 37 °C with shaking (250 rpm). Samples were vortexed briefly (20 s) and passed through a 70 μm cell strainer using the blunt end of a 1 ml syringe plunger. The resulting single-cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Cell pellets were resuspended in 8 ml of 44% Percoll and overlaid with 5 ml of 67% Percoll (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) to establish a discontinuous density gradient. Gradients were centrifuged at 500 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and LP mononuclear cells were collected from the interface. Cells were washed in PBS, pelleted (1000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and then resuspended for downstream flow cytometric analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

We performed the flow cytometry according to previously published protocols (Peters et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023b). A total of 1 × 105 cells were resuspended in 50 μl FACS buffer (0.5% BSA in PBS) for surface marker staining. To block Fc receptor-mediated nonspecific binding, cells were preincubated with anti-CD16/CD32 (BioLegend, clone 93; 5 ng/μl) at 4 °C for 10 min. Surface staining was then carried out for 30 min at 4 °C using the following antibodies: CD3 (BioLegend, clone 17A2; 2 ng μl−1), CD4 (BioLegend, clone GK1.5; 2 ng μl−1), CD8a (BioLegend, clone 53–7.6; 2 ng μl−1), TCR-β (BioLegend, clone H57–597; 2 ng μl−1), TCR-γδ (BioLegend, clone GL3; 2 ng μl−1), CD45 (Invitrogen, clone 30F-11; 5 ng μl−1; or BioLegend, clone 30F-11; 2 ng μl−1), CD11b (BioLegend, clone M1/70; 0.6 ng μl−1), CX3CR1 (BioLegend, clone SA011F11; 2 ng μl−1), dectin-1 (Abcam, clone 2A11; 1 ng μl−1), Ly6C (BioLegend, clone HK1.4; 2 ng μl−1) and MHC-II (BioLegend, clone M5/114.15.2; 2 ng μl−1). For intracellular staining, cells were stimulated with 0.2% eBiolegend Cell Stimulation Cocktail (with protein transport inhibitors, Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C for 6 h. Following surface staining, cells were fixed (15 min, 4 °C) and permeabilized using reagents from eBiosciences according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular staining was then performed at 4 °C for 30 min using the appropriate antibodies in Permeabilization Buffer. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended in 300 μl FACS buffer for acquisition. Data were collected using a Cytek Aurora 3-laser (Violet, Blue, and Red) spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Bethesda, MD, USA) and analyzed in FlowJo software (version 10.7, BD Biosciences). Cell viability and gating accuracy were assessed using LIVE/DEAD Fixable Dead Cell Stain Kits (Green, Cat#L23101; Red, Cat#L23102; Aqua, Cat#L34957; Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Data acquisition and analysis were performed by an investigator who was blinded to the group assignment. All antibodies are listed in Table 4 (Supplementary Data).

Untargeted metabolomic profiling

Fecal samples were collected from control, EcoHIV-infected, and EcoHIV plus pachyman-treated mice for untargeted metabolomic analysis. Metabolites were extracted using 80% methanol in LC-MS-grade water, followed by solvent removal through speed vacuum and lyophilization. The dried metabolites were then resuspended in 50% acetonitrile. Metabolomic data acquisition was performed using a Thermo Scientific IQ-X mass spectrometer (MS) coupled with a Vanquish Horizon Binary ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system. Data processing and metabolite identification were carried out using Thermo Scientific Compound Discoverer and FreeStyle software. Chromatographic peak intensities were integrated and normalized to the protein concentration of each sample to obtain final normalized metabolite intensities.

Analysis of human peripheral blood single-cell RNA-seq (scRNAseq) data

Human peripheral blood scRNAseq data were obtained from GEO (GSE202410) and processed following the previously published workflow (Huang et al., 2025). Briefly, cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, greater than 3000 detected genes, fewer than 2000 UMIs, more than 15,000 UMIs or more than 10% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Highly variable genes (n = 2,000) were selected, followed by PCA (20 PCs), neighborhood graph construction (using top 20 PCs), and UMAP embedding using Seurat v5.3.1. CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes were defined using canonical markers (CD14 monocytes: CD14+LYZ+S100A8+S100A9+; CD16 monocytes: FCGR3A+). CLEC7A (Dectin-1) was evaluated using the proportion of CLEC7A+ cells (χ2 test). To ensure global comparability, all analyses used the same UMAP embedding generated from the full blood dataset.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism v10. Two-group comparisons used two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For designs with more than two groups or factors, one-way or two-way ANOVA was used, with post-hoc multiple-comparison procedures as indicated in the figure legends. Where multiple hypotheses were tested (e.g., taxonomic relative abundances), p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). Associations between microbial features and behavioral readouts were evaluated by Spearman rank correlation. All quantitative data were presented as mean ± SEM. The significance levels were indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Female-associated gut Malassezia globosa signature in EcoHIV-infected mice and PWH

Given increasing evidence that fungal components such as β-D-glucan are linked to immune activation and neurocognitive dysfunction in virologically suppressed individuals with HIV (Hoenigl et al., 2016a; Hoenigl et al., 2015; Hoenigl et al., 2016b; Mehraj et al., 2020; Morris et al., 2012; Ramendra et al., 2019), we investigated whether EcoHIV infection alters the gut mycobiome, a potentially critical contributor to HIV-associated neuropathogenesis. To this end, we performed ITS sequencing on fecal samples from EcoHIV-infected and control male and female mice (Fig. 1A). For α-diversity, no significant difference was observed between groups in both male and female mice (Fig. 1B, C). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) of Bray-Curtis β-diversity showed modest visual separation among the four groups; however, within-sex EcoHIV vs. control comparisons were not statistically significant (Fig. 1D). At the species level, composition plots revealed shifts in fungal communities, but the only statistically significant species-level change was an increased frequency of Malassezia globosa in female EcoHIV-infected mice compared to controls (Fig. 1E, F, Table 1 in Supplementary Data). In contrast, no significant difference in Malassezia globosa was detected between groups in male mice (Fig. S1). To determine whether the observed fungal alterations reflect broader bacterial dysbiosis or bacterial-fungal co-variation, we performed fecal shotgun metagenomic sequencing in the same female cohort (Fig. S2, Table 2 in Supplementary Data). At the species level, EcoHIV infection did not induce significant shifts in the overall bacterial community composition after multiple-comparison correction. In addition, correlation analyses showed that Malassezia globosa abundance did not exhibit associations with specific bacterial taxa across individuals (Table 3 in Supplementary Data), suggesting that the female-associated Malassezia globosa signal is not simply a surrogate for overt bacterial dysbiosis in this cohort. Taken together, these results indicate that EcoHIV infection alters gut mycobiota composition, with a notable and sex-specific increase in Malassezia globosa observed only in female mice. This highlights a potential sex-dependent fungal signature associated with EcoHIV infection.

To evaluate whether a similar sex-associated Malassezia signal has been reported in humans, we examined a published fecal mycobiome study in PWH that performed fungal ITS profiling of fecal samples (Hamad et al., 2018). Analysis of ITS clone library sequences revealed a sex-associated detection pattern for Malassezia globosa, which was detectable only in HIV-infected female participants, but not in HIV-infected males or in uninfected controls of either sex (Fig. S3). This sex-associated pattern closely parallels our murine findings and provides supportive translational evidence.

Malassezia globosa correlated with the social interaction ratio and novel object recognition index in EcoHIV-infected female mice

To evaluate the behavioral phenotypes induced by EcoHIV infection and determine whether changes in Malassezia globosa abundance are associated with these phenotypes, we performed a battery of behavioral tests in 8-week-old female C57BL/6, five weeks post-infection.

We first assessed spontaneous locomotor activity using the OFT. EcoHIV infection did not affect locomotion, as indicated by comparable total movement counts between EcoHIV-infected and control mice (Fig. 2A). Anxiety-like behaviors were evaluated using both OFT and the EPM. EcoHIV-infected mice did not show increased time spent in the peripheral zone of the OFT or in the open arms of the EPM compared to controls (Fig. 2B, C), suggesting no significant impact of EcoHIV infection on anxiety-like behaviors in females.

Sociability was assessed using the three-chamber SIT. The social interaction ratio (SIR), calculated as the time a mouse sniffed the stranger divided by the time the mouse sniffed the inanimate object, was significantly decreased in EcoHIV-infected mice (Fig. 2D), In addition, control mice showed a significant preference for spending time in the stranger chamber compared with the inanimate chamber, whereas EcoHIV-infected mice failed to exhibit this preference (Fig. S4). These results indicated social avoidance behavior commonly associated with depression-like phenotypes.

To examine recognition memory, we conducted the NORT. The discrimination index, reflecting preference for a novel over a familiar object (Denninger et al., 2018), was significantly reduced in EcoHIV-infected mice compared to the controls (Fig. 2E), indicating cognitive impairment.

Finally, we assessed the relationship between behavioral outcomes and the abundance of Malassezia globosa. Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between Malassezia globosa abundance and both social interaction ratio and recognition memory performance (Fig. 2F, G). These findings suggest that increased Malassezia globosa abundance may contribute to EcoHIV-induced psychosocial and cognitive deficits in female mice.

Effects of pachyman on EcoHIV-induced social avoidance and recognition memory deficits

Prior studies have shown that in PWH, circulating 1,3-β-D-glucan—a major fungal cell-wall component and canonical ligand for dectin-1—is associated with systemic immune activation and inflammatory mediators, consistent with fungal product translocation contributing to HIV-associated inflammation (Mehraj et al., 2020; Morris et al., 2012). Functional studies further demonstrate that dectin-1 stimulation in aging HIV populations elicits a distinct pro-inflammatory myeloid signature, supporting the biological plausibility of a dectin-1–linked inflammatory pathway in HIV (Kumar et al., 2023). Importantly, in ART-treated, virologically suppressed cohorts, higher circulating 1,3-β-D-glucan levels have been associated with worse neurocognitive performance, providing translational support for a gut-derived fungal product–immune activation axis with CNS-relevant correlates (Hoenigl et al., 2016a). Based on this rationale, we selected pachyman—a fungal 1,3-β-D-glucan from Poria cocos with documented immunomodulatory and neuroprotective activity (Shi et al., 2020; Song et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2023b)—to determine whether targeting this Dectin-1 axis could ameliorate EcoHIV-related behavioral impairments. Given the observed elevation of Malassezia globosa in EcoHIV-infected female mice and its correlation with impaired social interaction and recognition memory, female mice were treated with pachyman (200 mg/kg, >98% purity, oral gavage) or vehicle daily for two weeks, beginning three weeks after EcoHIV infection. Behavioral assessments, including the OFT, SIT, EPM, and NORT, were conducted following treatment (Fig. 3A).

We first examined locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior. Total movement in the OFT was comparable across all groups, indicating no effect of EcoHIV infection or pachyman treatment on general locomotor activity (Fig. S5A). Similarly, anxiety-related metrics—time spent in the periphery in the OFT and time in the open arms of the EPM—were not significantly altered (Fig. S5B, C), suggesting that neither condition influenced anxiety-like behavior.

To assess sociability, we analyzed performance in the SIT by measuring time spent in each chamber and sniffing behavior. As expected, control mice (vehicle-treated) displayed a significant preference for the stranger mouse over the inanimate object, both in chamber time and active sniffing (Fig. 3B). In contrast, EcoHIV-infected mice treated with vehicle lacked this preference, indicating social avoidance. Notably, pachyman treatment restored social preference in EcoHIV-infected mice, with significantly increased time spent in the chamber containing the stranger mouse and in active sniffing (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the SIR was significantly reduced in vehicle-treated EcoHIV-infected mice and was restored by pachyman administration (Fig. S5D). These results were supported by heatmap visualizations (Fig. 3C): vehicle-treated EcoHIV mice showed greater presence in the inanimate object chamber, while pachyman-treated EcoHIV mice displayed increased activity near the stranger mouse, indicating reversal of social avoidance behavior.

Recognition memory was evaluated using the NORT. EcoHIV-infected mice treated with vehicle spent significantly less time exploring the novel object, indicating impaired recognition memory (Fig. 3D). Pachyman treatment significantly improved performance, restoring novel object exploration to levels comparable to control mice (Fig. 3D). Heatmap visualizations further confirmed these findings (Fig. 3E): vehicle-treated EcoHIV mice showed dispersed exploratory patterns without object preference, whereas pachyman-treated mice demonstrated focused engagement with the novel object.

Together, these findings indicate that pachyman treatment effectively ameliorates EcoHIV-induced deficits in social interaction and recognition memory in female mice, without affecting locomotor activity or anxiety-like behaviors.

Pachyman treatment reduces EcoHIV-induced elevation of dectin-1-expressing macrophages in the colonic lamina propria

Dectin-1, the β-glucan receptor, is primarily expressed on intestinal monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells and plays a crucial role in maintaining gut immune homeostasis (Brown, 2006; Brown and Gordon, 2005; Brown et al., 2003; Brown et al., 2002; Iliev et al., 2012; Li et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2015). As a β-glucan, pachyman is recognized by dectin-1 and has been shown to modulate immune responses (Brown et al., 2003; Taylor et al., 2007; Zhu et al., 2023b). To examine whether pharmacological modulation of dectin-1 affects EcoHIV-induced immune changes in the gut, we performed flow cytometric profiling of immune cell subsets in the colonic LP (Fig. 4A, Fig. S6A). Myeloid populations were defined as follows: monocytes (CD45+CD11b+Ly6C+CX3CR1+), macrophages (CD45+CD11b+Ly6C−CX3CR1+), dendritic cells (CD45+CD11c+MHC II+CX3CR1−), and neutrophils (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+). EcoHIV infection significantly increased the frequency of dectin-1+ macrophages (Fig. 4B), as well as their proportion among total leukocytes (Fig. 4C). Remarkably, pachyman treatment reversed the EcoHIV-induced elevation of dectin-1+ macrophages, reducing their frequency to near-control levels (Fig. 4B, C). In contrast, pachyman treatment did not significantly alter dectin-1 expression in other myeloid subsets, including MHC-II+ transitioning monocytes and MHC-II−quiescent monocytes (Fig. S7G, H), neutrophils (Fig. S7I), and dendritic cells (Fig. S7J). Moreover, the overall proportions of CD45+ leukocytes, monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells in the colonic LP were unaffected by either EcoHIV infection or pachyman treatment (Fig. S7A–F). These findings suggest that the immunomodulatory effect of pachyman on dectin-1 expression is specific to the macrophage population.

Spearman correlation analyses revealed that the percentage of dectin-1+ macrophages negatively correlated with social interaction behavior (Fig. 4D) and positively correlated with Malassezia globosa abundance (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that pachyman mitigates EcoHIV-associated psychosocial deficits, potentially through effects on dectin-1+ macrophages and associated fungal–immune interactions in the gut.

EcoHIV infection and pachyman treatment do not alter colonic lymphocyte populations

To assess whether EcoHIV infection or pachyman treatment affects lymphocyte populations in the colonic LP, we performed flow cytometric analyses (Fig. S6B). We quantified major lymphocyte subsets, including B cells (CD45+CD19+), total T cells (CD45+CD3+), CD4+ T cells (CD45+CD3+CD4+), CD8+ T cells (CD45+CD3+CD8+), and γδ T cells (CD45+CD3+TCRγδ+). No significant differences were observed in the frequencies of these subsets across control, EcoHIV-infected, and pachyman-treated groups (Fig. 4F–J). In addition, the proportion of dectin-1+ cells within each lymphocyte subset—including CD19+, CD4+, CD8+, and γδ T cells—remained unchanged among all groups (Fig. S8A–D). These results suggest that EcoHIV infection and pachyman treatment selectively impact myeloid populations without affecting lymphocyte composition or their dectin-1 expression in the colonic LP.

Pachyman modulates EcoHIV-induced alterations in gut metabolite levels in mice

HIV infection is known to activate inflammatory macrophages, which undergo metabolic reprogramming to support their effector functions (Real et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). This shift—characterized by enhanced glycolysis, altered lipid utilization, and dysregulated amino acid metabolism—facilitates immune responses but may also drive chronic inflammation and disease progression (Batista-Gonzalez et al., 2019; Real et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). In the context of EcoHIV infection, robust activation of gut-resident dectin-1+ macrophages likely reflect similar metabolic adaptations within the inflamed intestinal microenvironment. To assess how EcoHIV and pachyman treatment impact these metabolic alterations at the gut level, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling of fecal samples using UPLC–MS. Among the 392 detected metabolites, six showed significant differences between the control group and EcoHIV-infected group after FDR correction, including five that were upregulated in EcoHIV + vehicle mice—4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutanoate, 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetate (5-HIAA), 5-phosphoribosylamine (5-PRA), palmitoyl-aspartate, and sphinganine—and one that was downregulated, indole-5,6-quinone (Fig. 5A). Notably, pachyman treatment recovered the EcoHIV-induced increases in fecal levels of 4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutanoate, 5-HIAA, 5-PRA, and palmitoyl-aspartate (Fig. 5A–E). We next explored the relationships between these altered metabolites and EcoHIV-associated outcomes, including behavioral and microbial indices. Spearman correlation analysis revealed that palmitoyl-aspartate was negatively correlated with social interaction and recognition memory performance, and positively correlated with Malassezia globosa abundance and the proportion of colonic dectin-1+ macrophages (Fig. 5F). Metabolomic pathway analysis further highlighted dysregulation of the tryptophan metabolism pathway, particularly involving 4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutanoate and 5-HIAA (Fig. 5G; Table 5 and 6 in Supplementary Data).

Taken together, these results suggest that EcoHIV infection in female mice leads to changes in gut metabolism, particularly in the metabolism of tryptophan catabolites and lipid intermediates. These changes are linked to behavioural abnormalities and alterations in dectin-1+ macrophages. Pachyman treatment reverses these changes, contributing to the restoration of microbial, metabolic, and behavioral homeostasis in EcoHIV-infected female mice.

Increased CLEC7A (Dectin-1) proportion in circulating blood monocytes from PWH

To evaluate whether our mouse finding—EcoHIV-induced upregulation of colonic dectin-1+ macrophages—extends to human monocyte–macrophage lineages, we analyzed CLEC7A expression in peripheral blood CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes from a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of PWH receiving ART (GSE202410). In CD14+ classical monocytes, PWH exhibited a significantly higher proportion of CLEC7A+ cells compared with HIV-negative controls (Fig. 6A). In CD16+ nonclassical monocytes, the CLEC7A+ proportion was also significantly elevated in PWH relative to controls (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that systemic enhancement of dectin-1 signaling in PWH occurs in both CD14+ and CD16+ circulating monocyte subsets, with the strongest magnitude in CD14+ monocytes—the major precursors of tissue macrophages. This peripheral blood pattern parallels our mouse data demonstrating dectin-1 upregulation in colonic macrophages following EcoHIV infection.

Discussion

The gut microbiota shapes host physiology, regulating metabolism, intestinal homeostasis, and neurobehavioral processes (Durack and Lynch, 2019; Hou et al., 2022). Recent studies have shifted focus from cataloging compositional shifts to defining the functional roles of specific microbes and their bioactive metabolites as key mediators of host-microbe interactions (Koh and Backhed, 2020; Liu et al., 2022). The EcoHIV-infected mouse model is a well-established system for studying HIV-associated immune activation, particularly the role of macrophages in sustained inflammation (Gu et al., 2018; Sindberg et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2022). Infected macrophages serve as a long-term viral reservoir and produce proinflammatory cytokines that contribute to persistent inflammation (Sindberg et al., 2015). The EcoHIV-infected mouse model does not recapitulate all aspects of human HIV infection, such as lifelong chronicity and species-specific differences in the gut ecosystem and neurobiology. Nevertheless, EcoHIV-infected mice exhibited neurocognitive and psychiatric impairments similar to those reported in PWH (Bell et al., 2022; Nedelcovych et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2022). Given this background, we investigated the unexplored contribution of the gut mycobiome and its sex specificity to neurobehavioral deficits in the EcoHIV-infected mice. Our findings reveal a previously underappreciated mycobiome-immune-metabolic interaction linked to psychosocial and cognitive phenotypes, suggesting a tractable pathway for understanding- and potentially treating-HIV-associated neuropsychiatric and neurocognitive disorders.

A key finding of our study is a sex-specific alteration of the gut mycobiome in EcoHIV-infected mice. We observed a significant increase in the fungal species Malassezia globosa in the guts of female EcoHIV-infected mice, but not in males. This increase correlated with impaired social interaction and recognition memory, and this sex-specific fungal expansion emphasizes the importance of biological sex as a critical factor in HIV-related gut-brain axis pathology, which is consistent with recognized sexual dimorphisms in disease progression (Rao and McClelland, 2024; Rechtien and Altfeld, 2019; Shobeiri et al., 2022). In addition, analysis of published human data showed that Malassezia globosa was detectable exclusively in HIV-infected female participants, but not in HIV-infected males or in healthy controls of either sex (Hamad et al., 2018). This sex-associated pattern provides supportive translational evidence that the female-associated Malassezia globosa signature observed in EcoHIV-infected mice may extend to the context of HIV-associated immune dysregulation in humans. In males, behavioral deficits (SIT, NORT) were also observed; however, fungal community analyses did not reveal significant differences after multiple-comparison correction. Instead, male behavioral variance appeared to align with sex-dependent alterations in bacterial taxa (notably Christensenella), which will be described in a separate report. Although EcoHIV infection produces comparable deficits in social interaction and recognition memory in both sexes, the Malassezia globosa–dectin-1+ macrophage pathway described here represents a sex-associated neuroimmune mechanism in females rather than a universal driver of EcoHIV-related behavioral dysfunction. These findings imply that distinct, sex-dependent microbial–immune pathways may converge on similar behavioral phenotypes, with bacterial factors likely contributing to the observed deficits in males.

To further evaluate whether the female-associated fungal signal is accompanied by parallel alterations in bacterial communities, we performed fecal shotgun metagenomic profiling in the same female cohort. At the species level, EcoHIV infection did not induce overt bacterial dysbiosis, nor were statistically significant correlations detected between Malassezia globosa abundance and bacterial species. These findings indicate that, in this experimental context, the female-associated fungal–immune and behavioral signatures are not accompanied by major bacterial community remodeling, supporting a primary role for the female mycobiome–Dectin-1 axis in the observed phenotypes. Notably, lipid-dependent Malassezia species have been detected in the gut during inflammatory states and exhibit higher abundance in the intestines of HIV patients (Abdillah et al., 2025; Cho et al., 2025; Hamad et al., 2018), motivating the investigation of fungal-immune pathways in HIV-related neuropsychiatric and neurocognitive phenotypes.

Multiple factors may contribute to the female-biased expansion of Malassezia globosa under EcoHIV infection. Sex hormones are one potential driver, as estrogens modulate the gut microbiome and can promote fungal growth through immune regulation (Kumwenda et al., 2022; Org et al., 2016; Peters et al., 2024). Immune dimorphism provides another explanation: females typically mount stronger immune responses, which may both restrain viral replication and create an inflammatory environment permissive for fungal expansion (Addo and Altfeld, 2014; Klein and Flanagan, 2016). Metabolic context may also be relevant, since Malassezia are lipid-dependent fungi and HIV infection perturbs host lipid metabolism, often with sex-linked differences (Funderburg and Mehta, 2016; Sarkar and Brown, 2000; Willig and Overton, 2016; Yang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023a). Supporting these possibilities, higher Malassezia abundance has been reported in the gut of PWH relative to controls (Abdillah et al., 2025; Hamad et al., 2018). In summary, hormonal, immune, and metabolic factors may underlie the observed female-biased mycobiome shift.

Another key finding is the involvement of dectin-1 in this axis. Dectin-1 is essential for antifungal immune responses and for maintaining gut immune homeostasis (Brown, 2006; Brown and Gordon, 2005; Brown et al., 2003; Brown et al., 2002; Iliev et al., 2012; Li et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2015). Notably, dectin-1 signaling has been implicated in macrophage or microglial activation in certain neurodegenerative disease models (Ennerfelt et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022), highlighting the context- and disease-stage–dependent nature of dectin-1–mediated immune responses; thus, further investigation of dectin-1–associated pathways in long-term, virologically suppressed HIV progression is warranted. In the intestinal environment, dectin-1 contributes to regulating gut barrier integrity and shaping the composition of the gut microbiota and mucosal immunity (Iliev et al., 2012; Li et al., 2024). Dysbiosis of the gut mycobiome can disrupt systemic immunity and intestinal barrier integrity, allowing fungal components such as β-glucans to translocate and trigger systemic inflammation (Goodridge et al., 2009; Singh and Bhardwaj, 2023). Furthermore, alterations in the gut mycobiome can modulate the migration of immune cells from the gut into the central nervous system, exacerbating neuroinflammation and contributing to neuropsychiatric disorders like depression and cognitive impairment (Bostick et al., 2022; Hadrich et al., 2024; Leonardi et al., 2022; O’Riordan et al., 2025). Given this, our findings highlight a specific pathway: an increase in Malassezia globosa leads to the activation of dectin-1+ macrophages, which promotes gut and systemic inflammation that may contribute to the observed social avoidance and cognitive deficits in EcoHIV-infected female mice.

Remarkably, our results show that pachyman intervention on the fungal-immune axis improved neurobehavioral impairments in EcoHIV-infected female mice, restoring social preference and recognition memory, while reducing the EcoHIV-induced increase in dectin-1+ macrophages in the colon. As a β-glucan ligand of dectin-1, pachyman may limit macrophage activation by competing with pro-inflammatory fungal signals or by promoting tolerogenic pathways. Consistent with this, our prior work showed that oral pachyman bound colonic dectin-1, suppressed pro-inflammatory γδ T-cell activity, and alleviated chronic stress-induced social avoidance in mice (Zhu et al., 2023b). Together, these findings support a dectin-1–mediated mechanism underlying pachyman’s therapeutic immunomodulatory effects.

Beyond immune modulation, pachyman also normalized EcoHIV-associated gut metabolic disturbances. In vehicle-treated EcoHIV females, metabolomics showed elevations in tryptophan-pathway catabolites and a lipid-derived metabolite, palmitoyl-aspartate. Such changes are important because chronic inflammation often redirects tryptophan metabolism towards neurotoxic kynurenine pathway products (Parrott et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2022), while palmitate-containing metabolites are linked to microglial activation and neuroinflammation (Chausse et al., 2019; Gope and Mauro, 2025). Pachyman reduced the levels of these altered metabolites in EcoHIV-infected mice, suggesting a restoration of metabolic homeostasis. For instance, pachyman-mediated normalization of fecal 5-HIAA suggests a shift in tryptophan metabolism toward the serotonin pathway and away from excessive diversion into the kynurenine pathway (Li et al., 2022). Because 5-HIAA is the principal metabolite of serotonin, its restoration indicates correction of EcoHIV-associated serotonergic imbalance, which may contribute to neuroprotection and the observed improvements in social and cognitive behaviors in EcoHIV-infected female mice. Likewise, the reduction in palmitoyl-aspartate following pachyman administration is noteworthy, given its positive correlation with Malassezia globosa abundance and the frequency of dectin-1+ macrophages in our study. This decrease may help constrain pro-inflammatory lipid signaling. Overall, by correcting immune and metabolic aberrations, pachyman re-established a homeostatic gut environment that underpinned its therapeutic effect.

The strong correlations observed among Malassezia globosa, dectin-1+ macrophages, and behavioral deficits provide compelling evidence of a potential mechanistic link. Future studies employing approaches such as fungal depletion and macrophage-specific interventions will be important for directly testing causality and further strengthening these findings. Notably, although direct in vivo manipulation of Malassezia globosa would in principle provide additional causal resolution, currently available experimental paradigms introduce substantial interpretational complexity. Antibiotic (ABX)-based microbiota depletion primarily targets bacteria rather than fungi (Liu et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2022) and is frequently associated with increased relative fungal abundance and cross-kingdom restructuring (Dollive et al., 2013; Nettles et al., 2020), limiting its utility for species-specific fungal interrogation. Germ-free colonization models likewise exhibit baseline alterations in social interaction and cognitive phenotypes (Arentsen et al., 2015; Desbonnet et al., 2014; Lu et al., 2018), which would confound interpretation of behavioral endpoints central to this study. In addition, Malassezia species are lipid-dependent commensals that lack de novo fatty acid synthesis pathways (Limon et al., 2017; Park et al., 2021), making stable and physiologically relevant gut colonization technically challenging in standard mouse systems.

In alignment with our mouse findings—where EcoHIV infection induced marked upregulation of dectin-1+ macrophages in the colon—we observed a parallel increase in CLEC7A expression in circulating monocytes from PWH receiving ART. Both CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes showed higher proportions of CLEC7A+ cells, indicating a sustained systemic innate immune activation state. Because CD14+ monocytes serve as the primary precursors for tissue macrophages, their enhanced dectin-1 proportion suggests a mechanism by which peripheral innate immune responses may interface with downstream tissue inflammation. The concordance between mouse colonic macrophage responses and human circulating monocytes supports a conserved β-glucan/dectin-1 pathway relevant to immune dysregulation, inflammation, and potentially neurobehavioral alterations in HIV.

In considering the translational relevance of these findings, it is notable that standard antifungal therapy has been explored in HIV-associated neurocognitive impairment. In a randomized clinical trial of fluconazole in ART-treated individuals with HIV (Sacktor et al., 2018), the fluconazole-only treatment arm was small and composed exclusively of male participants, and no cognitive benefit was observed. Together with our female-specific fungal and behavioral findings, these data raise the possibility that antifungal interventions may exert sex-dependent effects on neurobehavioral outcomes in HIV, warranting further investigation.

In summary, our study identified an expansion of Malassezia globosa specific to females in EcoHIV-infected mice, which was linked to social avoidance behavior and cognitive deficits. Targeting the fungal-immune axis using pachyman treatment improved these impairments and restored immune and metabolic balance. These results highlight the crucial role of the gut mycobiome in the HIV gut-brain axis and indicate the potential of modulating fungus-immune interactions as a promising approach to treating HIV-related neuropsychiatric and neurocognitive disorders.
