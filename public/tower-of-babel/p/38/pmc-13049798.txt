Multidomain clinical and biological remission with tezepelumab in severe asthma: a 12-month multicentre real-world study
Authors: Juan Luis García-Rivero, Adil Hannaoui Anaaoui, Abel Pallarés-Sanmartín, Marina Blanco-Aparicio, Raquel García-Hernáez, Victoria García-Gallardo Sanz, Uxío Calvo-Álvarez, Luis Carazo-Fernández, Tamara Hermida-Valverde, Silvia Dorronsoro, Inés Carrascosa-Anguiano, Ignacio Lobato Astiárraga, Idania de los Santos, Ana Isabel Enríquez-Rodríguez, Luis Perez de Llano, Pablo Álvarez Vega, Beatriz Abascal-Bolado, Miguel Santibañez

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTezepelumab, an anti–TSLP monoclonal antibody, has shown broad efficacy across severe asthma phenotypes. However, real-world evidence evaluating multidomain remission—integrating clinical, functional, and inflammatory domains—remains limited. The primary objective was to assess 12-month effectiveness and multidomain remission (clinical, biological, and complete remission) stratified by inflammatory phenotype, in a prospective multicentre real-world cohort of patients with severe asthma treated with tezepelumab.MethodsThis prospective observational study included adults with severe asthma initiating tezepelumab across 14 specialised severe asthma units in Spain. Clinical outcomes (exacerbations, ACT, OCS use), lung function, and type 2 (T2) biomarkers (blood eosinophils, FeNO) were evaluated at baseline and 12 months. Clinical remission was defined using strict (no exacerbations, no maintenance OCS, ACT ≥ 20, FEV₁ ≥80%) and pragmatic criteria (no exacerbations, no OCS, ACT ≥ 20, and no FEV₁ decline > 5%). Biological remission required FeNO < 25 ppb and eosinophils < 300/µL.ResultsNinety-three patients were included. The annualised exacerbation rate decreased by 68% (3.59 to 1.14; p < 0.001). ACT improved from 13.7 to 19.9 (p < 0.001) and 77.8% of patients achieved an ACT increase ≥ 3 points. Maintenance OCS use fell from 40% to 21% (p < 0.01). FEV₁ (% predicted) increased from 70.7% to 75.6% (p < 0.001). T2 biomarkers significantly decreased (eosinophils p = 0.004; FeNO p = 0.034). Of the 93 patients included, 86 had complete data for strict clinical remission analysis. Among evaluable patients, 22.1% (19/86) achieved strict clinical remission and 38.4% (33/86) pragmatic remission. Biological remission occurred in 73.1% (38/52), and complete remission in 22.0% (11/50).ConclusionIn this multicentre real-world cohort, tezepelumab produced sustained multidomain improvements with consistent benefit across phenotypes, including marked exacerbation reductions in allergic biologic-naïve patients. Incorporating biological remission into a multidomain framework provides new insight into the depth and heterogeneity of response achievable with upstream TSLP blockade.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03597-3.

Background

Severe asthma affects 5–10% of the asthma population and is associated with a disproportionate burden of exacerbations, impaired quality of life, and systemic corticosteroid (OCS) use [1, 2]. Despite optimized treatment with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and long-acting β2-agonists (LABA), many patients remain uncontrolled and require advanced therapies [3, 4].

The introduction of monoclonal antibodies has transformed the management of severe asthma, particularly in type 2 (T2) inflammatory phenotypes [5, 6]. Tezepelumab, an anti-thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) monoclonal antibody, represents a novel upstream strategy that blocks multiple inflammatory pathways [7, 8]. In pivotal randomized controlled trials (PATHWAY and NAVIGATOR), tezepelumab significantly reduced severe exacerbations, improved lung function, and enhanced asthma control across a broad range of patients, including those with low T2 biomarkers [9, 10].

However, clinical trial populations often differ from those seen in real-world practice due to strict eligibility criteria and structured follow-up. Real-world studies have therefore become crucial to assess the external validity of trial findings and to explore outcomes such as OCS sparing and clinical remission in heterogeneous patient populations. Recent European and US cohorts have confirmed the effectiveness of tezepelumab in daily practice, but baseline severity and treatment patterns vary substantially across countries, influencing both utilization and outcomes [11–15].

Clinical remission—defined as the absence of exacerbations, withdrawal of OCS, good symptom control, and preserved lung function—has recently emerged as a desirable treatment goal in severe asthma, reflecting a shift from disease control to disease modification. However, there is still no universal consensus on its definition, and the relative contribution of each clinical domain remains under debate [16–20].

Real-world evidence on clinical remission with tezepelumab remains scarce, with only a study reporting 12-month outcomes [13]. Building on this emerging evidence, we aimed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness and multidomain remission outcomes of tezepelumab in a real-world Spanish cohort.

In this context, we conducted a prospective multicentre study with severe asthma patients treated with tezepelumab and followed for 12 months. The primary objective was to assess the effectiveness of tezepelumab and the proportion of patients achieving clinical and biological remission under routine clinical conditions, stratified by inflammatory phenotype.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a prospective, observational, multicentre real-world study conducted in 14 specialised severe asthma units across Spain. Patients were managed according to routine clinical practice, and the decision to initiate tezepelumab was made independently of study participation. Tezepelumab was administered at the approved dose of 210 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks.

Patients

Consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) with severe asthma, as defined by the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2024 criteria [2], who initiated tezepelumab between October 2023 and October 2024 were eligible. Severe asthma was defined by persistent symptoms and/or exacerbations despite high-dose inhaled corticosteroids plus additional controller(s), or by the need for such treatment to maintain control. In Spain, reimbursement criteria for tezepelumab require uncontrolled severe asthma despite high-dose ICS/LABA, typically with a history of ≥ 2 severe exacerbations in the previous year and/or maintenance oral corticosteroid (OCS) use, according to national regulations. In a small number of patients recorded as receiving low- or medium-dose ICS at baseline, treatment reflected the highest tolerated ICS dose in routine clinical practice, including cases of intolerance or adverse effects, while still fulfilling criteria for severe asthma through persistent uncontrolled disease and/or maintenance oral corticosteroid use. Patients were included irrespective of prior biologic exposure (biologic-naïve or switch), phenotype, or baseline biomarker profile. All patients provided written informed consent before enrolment.

Assessments and data collection

Clinical assessments were performed at baseline (treatment initiation, V1) and at the 12-month follow-up visit (V2). Data were extracted from electronic medical records and structured clinical visits and entered into a specific electronic case report database using predefined fields and standardised definitions. Changes in background controller therapy (ICS dose, add‑on bronchodilators, macrolides and maintenance OCS) during follow‑up were allowed according to routine clinical practice and were recorded when clinically relevant, although they were not formally modelled in the effectiveness analyses.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary objective was to evaluate the 12-month effectiveness of tezepelumab and the proportion of patients achieving multidomain remission under real-world conditions. Accordingly, primary outcomes included changes in annualised severe exacerbation rate (AER) and the proportion of patients achieving strict and pragmatic clinical remission, biological remission, and complete remission at 12 months. Secondary outcomes included changes in ACT, maintenance OCS use, lung function, FeNO, blood eosinophils, and sinonasal symptoms (SNOT-22).

Severe exacerbations were defined as a worsening of asthma symptoms requiring a course of systemic corticosteroids for ≥ 3 days and/or an unscheduled medical visit, emergency department attendance, or hospital admission [2]. At baseline (V1), investigators recorded the number of severe exacerbations that had occurred in the 12 months preceding tezepelumab initiation (pre‑index period). During follow‑up, all severe exacerbations were prospectively captured and assigned to the 12‑month on‑treatment period after the first tezepelumab dose. For each patient, the annualised exacerbation rate (AER) was calculated as the number of severe exacerbations divided by the exposure time (in years). In the present analysis, AER comparisons were restricted to patients with at least 12 months of documented follow‑up; therefore, exposure time was 1.0 year for both the pre‑treatment and on‑treatment periods, and the AER is numerically equivalent to the mean number of severe exacerbations per patient over 12 months in each period. Absence of maintenance OCS was defined based on treatment status at the 12-month visit.

At baseline, a T2‑high phenotype was pragmatically defined as blood eosinophils ≥ 150 cells/µL and/or FeNO ≥ 25 ppb and/or evidence of allergic sensitisation with clinical relevance. A blood eosinophil threshold of 150 cells/µL is widely used in international guidelines and clinical trials to identify eosinophilic or T2‑high severe asthma and to select candidates for biologic therapy [2]. Likewise, FeNO values ≥ 25 ppb are considered indicative of residual type 2 airway inflammation despite high‑dose inhaled corticosteroids. We therefore adopted an inclusive definition (eosinophils ≥ 150 and/or FeNO ≥ 25, or allergic asthma) to capture the full spectrum of T2‑high disease while remaining aligned with current guideline‑based cut‑offs [2].

Patients receiving maintenance OCS could have suppressed eosinophil counts and FeNO, and were therefore classified according to measured biomarkers at baseline.

Strict clinical remission at 12 months was defined as: no severe exacerbations, no maintenance OCS use, ACT ≥ 20, and FEV₁ ≥80% predicted.

Pragmatic clinical remission was defined as: no severe exacerbations, no OCS, ACT ≥ 20, and stable lung function, i.e. no FEV₁ decline > 5% from baseline.

Biological remission at 12 months was defined, following Gates et al., as simultaneous achievement of blood eosinophils < 300 cells/µL and FeNO < 25 ppb [13]. These thresholds mirror the “low T2” biomarker targets used in post‑hoc analyses of the NAVIGATOR trial and other tezepelumab studies, where the proportions of patients attaining BEC < 150 or < 300 cells/µL and FeNO < 25 ppb were reported as key markers of upstream T2 suppression [21]. A blood eosinophil level ≥ 300 cells/µL and FeNO ≥ 25 ppb are also widely recognised cut‑offs for high T2‑driven exacerbation risk and for eligibility to several biologics [2, 22]. Thus, requiring BEC < 300 cells/µL and FeNO < 25 ppb provides a pragmatic yet clinically meaningful definition of biological remission that is comparable across real‑world tezepelumab cohorts.

Complete remission required fulfilment of both strict clinical remission and biological remission criteria.

Clinical and biological remission analyses were performed in patients with complete baseline and 12-month data for the respective domains (complete-case analysis).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), according to distribution assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables are summarised as counts and percentages. Within-patient changes from baseline to 12 months were analysed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. Between-group comparisons (e.g. patients with vs. without clinical remission) used Student’s t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ² or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Changes in annualised exacerbation rate were assessed with negative binomial regression adjusted for exposure time, and results were expressed as incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). To explore factors associated with strict clinical remission, a multivariable logistic regression model was fitted including prespecified baseline covariates (age, sex, BMI, phenotype [T2 high vs. T2 low], biologic naïve vs. switch, ACT score, Baseline FEV₁, blood eosinophils, and FeNO) and reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI. All tests were two-sided with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Given the sample size, this multivariable model should be considered exploratory. Analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software package 22.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R, version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), run via RStudio, version 2025.9.1.401 (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Ethics considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Spanish regulations for observational research. In Spain, multicentre non-interventional studies with medicinal products are reviewed and approved by a single reference Clinical Research Ethics Committee (CEIm), whose approval is legally binding for all participating centres, in accordance with Spanish Law 14/2007 on Biomedical Research and Royal Decree 957/2020. Accordingly, the study was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Cantabria (CEIm Cantabria), which acted as the reference ethics committee. All participating centres were formally informed and agreed to take part in the study under the same approved protocol.

Results

Patient population

A total of 93 patients with severe asthma initiated tezepelumab and were included in the effectiveness analysis and followed for 12 months. Eighty-six patients had complete data at 12 months (V2) for the clinical remission composite scores (7 patients with missing data) and fifty-two for the biological remission composite outcome. See flowchart showed as Figure S1. Seventy-nine patients had complete basal (V1) and 12 months (V2) paired data for the clinical remission composite scores (14 patients with missing data). Excluded patients showed slightly lower baseline blood eosinophil counts; however, other baseline characteristics, including exacerbation burden, lung function, and treatment exposure, were broadly comparable (Supplementary Table S2). The mean age in the total sample (N = 93) was 55.9 years, 71% were female, and the mean BMI was 30.4 kg/m². 55% were biologic-naïve, while 45% had switched from another biologic. Regarding inflammatory phenotype, 67% were T2-high and 33% T2-low. At baseline, patients exhibited a high disease burden, with a mean annualized exacerbation rate (AER) of 3.51, 40% receiving maintenance oral corticosteroids (OCS), and 18% with chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP). Severe exacerbations

The annualized exacerbation rate (AER) decreased from 3.51 ± 2.38 in the 12 months prior to initiation (n = 92) to 1.14 ± 1.47 at 12 months (n = 90) (− 68%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). In a negative binomial model, the incidence rate ratio (IRR) for exacerbations at 1 year was 0.32 (p < 0.001). Of those with at least one severe exacerbation in the previous year and paired data, 57.8% (N = 44/76) became exacerbation-free. Improvements were consistent across subgroups: biologic-naïve (IRR 0.27), biologic-switch (IRR 0.41). By phenotype, reductions were also maintained in allergic asthma (IRR 0.32), eosinophilic (IRR 0.28) and T2-low asthma (IRR 0.36) (Table 2. Figure 1B and D). Asthma control

Asthma symptom control improved significantly, with ACT scores increasing from 13.7 ± 4.8 at baseline to 19.9 ± 4.6 at 12 months (p < 0.001). At 1 year, 66.7% of patients with paired data (n = 54/81) achieved a good asthma control (ACT ≥ 20) and 63.2% (n = 48/76) of patients with uncontrolled symptoms at baseline (ACT < 20) achieved ACT ≥ 20 at 1 year. Figure S1. Table 2. Overall, 77.8% of patients achieved an improvement in ACT score ≥ 3 points, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference.

Lung function

FEV₁ (% predicted) improved significantly from 70.7% ± 23.5 at baseline to 75.6% ± 24.6 at 12 months (p < 0.001). Absolute FEV₁ increased numerically (1892 ± 827 mL to 1983 ± 806 mL; Δ + 91 mL, p = 0.008). Table 2. At baseline, 63% of patients (n = 58/92) had an FEV₁ <80% predicted, and finally 39/90 patients with paired data (43.3%) normalised their lung function (FEV₁ ≥80% predicted) after 12 months of tezepelumab treatment. Figure S1. Table 2.

Oral corticosteroid use

At baseline, 40% of patients (n = 36/93) were receiving maintenance OCS. After 12 months of treatment, this proportion decreased to 21% (n = 19/91, p = 0.002). Table 2. Among OCS-dependent patients, more than half (n = 22/36) discontinued systemic steroids during follow-up. Figure S1. Table 2.

Biomarkers

Blood eosinophil counts decreased from 215 ± 311 cells/µL at baseline to 126 ± 100 cells/µL at 1 year (p = 0.004). FeNO also declined significantly, from 25.3 ± 24.52 ppb to 19.3 ± 16.1 ppb (p = 0.034). Total IgE showed a nonsignificant downward trend (165 ± 240 IU/mL to 136 ± 191 IU/mL, p = 0.077). Table 2.

Sinonasal outcomes

In patients with CRSwNP, sinonasal symptoms improved substantially. Mean SNOT-22 scores decreased from 27.0 ± 17.6 at baseline to 16.5 ± 9.4 at 1 year (p < 0.001). In addition, 50% (n = 15/30) of patients achieved the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 8.9 points, indicating clinically meaningful improvement in sinonasal symptoms. Table 2.

Remission outcome

For the strict definition of clinical remission, as mentioned above, data were available for 86 out of 93 patients (92.5%). Among these, 19 patients (22.1%) achieved strict clinical remission. When applying the pragmatic definition, 38.4% (33/86) achieved clinical remission. In a sensitivity analysis when computed only patients with basal (V1) and 12 months (V2) paired data, % for strict clinical remission was 21.5% (17/79) and 36.7% (29/79) for pragmatic clinical remission. The most frequently achieved domains were OCS withdrawal and symptom control, whereas lung function normalisation remained the most difficult. See % showed in Figure S1.

Discussion

In this multicentre real-world study including 93 patients with severe asthma, tezepelumab was associated with a substantial reduction in exacerbations, improvements in asthma control and lung function, decreased OCS dependence, and significant biomarker suppression after 12 months of follow-up. In addition, a relevant proportion of patients achieved clinical and biological remission, supporting the broad effectiveness of this upstream biologic in routine practice.

Exacerbations and oral corticosteroid use

The reduction in exacerbations observed in our cohort (–68%) is consistent with the magnitude reported in other real-world studies. In the Israeli series of 103 patients, the AER decreased from 2.9 to 1.0, while in the UK cohort (n = 175) the reduction was from 3.1 to 0.8. Similarly, the US PASSAGE study reported a decrease from 3.0 to 0.7. Our results therefore confirm the reproducibility of clinical trial data in real-world settings, despite differences in baseline severity. Importantly, we observed a halving of OCS dependence (from 40% to 21%), comparable to the OCS reduction described in Israel and the UK, and clinically relevant given the well-recognised long-term toxicity of systemic corticosteroids.

Symptom control and lung function

Symptom control improved significantly, with two-thirds of patients achieving ACT ≥ 20. This rate is comparable to the proportion of patients with ACQ-6 < 1.5 in the UK study (55%), confirming that tezepelumab translates into meaningful improvements in quality of life. Importantly, 77.8% of patients experienced an improvement in ACT ≥ 3 points, confirming that these changes were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful. Lung function also improved significantly in relative terms, although the absolute gain was modest, as reported in other real-world series. This reinforces the observation that lung function is often the most difficult domain to normalise in severe asthma.

Biomarkers and comorbidities

Tezepelumab significantly reduced blood eosinophils and FeNO, consistent with its upstream anti-inflammatory action. It is important to note that the magnitude of biomarker reduction is intrinsically dependent on baseline values, with larger absolute decreases expected among patients with higher initial eosinophil counts or FeNO levels. This baseline-dependent dynamic has been consistently observed in both clinical trials and real-world studies of tezepelumab. In our cohort, substantial variability in baseline T2 biomarkers likely contributed to heterogeneity in absolute reductions. Although we did not perform a formal stratified analysis according to baseline biomarker levels due to sample size considerations, the higher remission rates observed among patients with elevated baseline T2 biomarkers (Fig. 2) are consistent with this expected biological pattern. Of note, sinonasal symptoms also improved, as shown by a marked decrease in SNOT-22 scores in patients with CRSwNP. This supports the dual impact of tezepelumab on both lower and upper airway inflammation and mirrors findings from post-hoc analyses of the NAVIGATOR trial.

Remission outcomes

To our knowledge, this is the first multicentre real-world cohort to evaluate remission with tezepelumab using both a stringent lung function–based definition and a pragmatic composite, together with a biologically driven definition based on FeNO and blood eosinophils.

Using the strict criteria, just over one in five patients achieved clinical remission, whereas more than one third met the pragmatic definition. This illustrates how remission rates vary according to the stringency of lung function thresholds and supports the need for harmonised, multidomain definitions in severe asthma. Our findings are broadly consistent with the pooled remission rate of around 30% reported in the recent meta-analysis by Shackleford et al. [20], while highlighting the impact of different FEV₁ cut-offs on observed remission frequencies.

We deliberately adopted FEV₁ ≥80% predicted as the lung function criterion for strict remission, in line with recent expert recommendations and the REMAS 2024 consensus, which propose functional normalisation as an indicator of true disease modification rather than partial control [18, 19]. As expected, this more demanding threshold reduced the proportion of patients classified as in remission but provides a conservative and clinically meaningful benchmark. The most frequently achieved domains were symptom control and OCS withdrawal, whereas FEV₁ normalisation emerged as the most challenging, in agreement with previous remission studies across different biologics [13, 14, 23]. The inclusion of lung function as a component of clinical remission requires careful interpretation in severe asthma, as a relevant proportion of patients present with persistent or partially irreversible airflow obstruction related to long-standing disease and airway remodelling [24–26]. In addition, a history of smoking, present in a subset of real-world cohorts, may further limit the potential for full lung function normalisation despite effective anti-inflammatory treatment. While a stringent remission definition requiring FEV₁ ≥80% predicted may better reflect true disease modification, a pragmatic approach allowing stable lung function without significant decline captures clinically meaningful improvement in patients with established airflow limitation. The use of both definitions in the present study was intended to reflect this heterogeneity and to facilitate comparison with prior real-world cohorts.

Our choice of biomarker thresholds deserves comment. We used a lower eosinophil cut‑off (≥ 150 cells/µL) to characterise T2‑high disease, in line with international guidelines and severe‑asthma trials (2 [27], , whereas biological remission required suppression below the higher‑risk threshold (BEC < 300 cells/µL plus FeNO < 25 ppb), as proposed in the UK tezepelumab cohort and supported by NAVIGATOR biomarker analyses [13, 21]. This distinction explains why some patients classified as T2‑high at baseline could still achieve biological remission after treatment.

Biological remission was frequent, occurring in more than 70% of evaluable patients, despite relatively low baseline eosinophil counts and FeNO levels in this mixed naïve/switch population. This confirms the potent upstream anti-inflammatory effect of tezepelumab, consistent with the biomarker reductions seen in NAVIGATOR and other real-world cohorts [13, 28]. The partial overlap between clinical and biological remission suggests that inflammatory quiescence and symptomatic recovery do not always align temporally and underscores the value of assessing both clinical and biomarker domains when characterising remission in severe asthma.

Beyond confirming the effectiveness of tezepelumab, our study provides two key contributions that are seldom addressed in real-world cohorts. First, we applied a multidomain remission framework —including strict, pragmatic and biological remission— which allows a more complete characterisation of treatment response than conventional single-domain endpoints. Second, we performed a detailed phenotypic stratification (allergic, eosinophilic and T2-low), an approach rarely implemented in real-world tezepelumab studies, enabling a more granular understanding of how distinct inflammatory profiles influence remission probability and overall treatment outcomes. This phenotypic resolution adds clinical interpretability and enhances comparability across T2-driven and non-T2-driven asthma populations.

Comparison with international cohorts

Our findings are broadly in line with other real-world cohorts of tezepelumab conducted in Europe, the United States, and Israel, all of which have consistently reported marked reductions in exacerbations, improvements in symptom control, and meaningful OCS sparing [11–14]. However, these observational series differ considerably in design, data collection structure, and case-mix composition. Some cohorts are heavily enriched for biologic-naïve patients, whereas others include a high proportion of switchers who have previously failed anti-IgE, anti-IL5/IL5R, or anti-IL4Rα therapies. Baseline severity profiles also vary widely among healthcare systems, particularly in exacerbation burden, long-term OCS exposure, and access to biomarker-guided decision-making. Beyond these clinical factors, the relative prevalence of allergic, eosinophilic, and T2-low phenotypes is strongly shaped by local regulatory and reimbursement requirements, resulting in substantial heterogeneity across real-world studies.

In our cohort, patients initiated tezepelumab with a high baseline exacerbation rate and substantial maintenance OCS use, and nearly half had previous exposure to another biologic. Despite this enriched severity profile and broad phenotypic heterogeneity, substantial reductions in exacerbations were observed across all subgroups, particularly among biologic-naïve patients, supporting the relevance of prior biologic exposure in treatment response interpretation. These differences in prior treatment exposure, baseline risk, and inflammatory phenotype distribution help explain why absolute remission rates and effect sizes cannot be directly compared across real-world cohorts, even when nominally similar endpoints are used.

Taken together, current real-world evidence supports the robust and reproducible effectiveness of tezepelumab across diverse practice settings. Importantly, our multicentre Spanish study adds a systematic multidomain assessment of strict, pragmatic, and biological remission, providing a framework that extends beyond conventional single-domain outcomes. Moreover, our cohort offers a granular phenotypic stratification (allergic, eosinophilic, T2-low) scarcely explored in previous real-world tezepelumab series. This anatomical and immunological resolution enhances clinical interpretability and provides insight into how distinct T2-driven and non-T2-driven mechanisms influence the likelihood of achieving remission. Rather than enabling strict numerical comparison across studies, this heterogeneity underscores the need to contextualize remission outcomes according to each cohort’s baseline characteristics while promoting the use of multidomain, phenotype-aware definitions of remission that can be applied consistently across future research.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include its prospective multicentre design, the largest Spanish real-world cohort of patients treated with tezepelumab reported to date, and complete 12-month follow-up in specialised severe asthma units. Importantly, the study provides a detailed phenotypic stratification (allergic, eosinophilic and T2-low), which is seldom reported in real-world tezepelumab cohorts and allows a more nuanced interpretation of treatment response across distinct T2-driven and non-T2-driven profiles. In addition, the systematic assessment of strict, pragmatic and biological remission offers a multidomain perspective that complements conventional effectiveness endpoints.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the pre–post observational design without a parallel control group introduces potential residual confounding and regression to the mean, and changes in background therapy during follow‑up could not be fully captured. Airway hyperresponsiveness was not systematically assessed in routine practice and therefore could not be incorporated into the remission definition. In addition, severe exacerbations were recorded as events requiring systemic corticosteroids and/or hospital admission, but emergency department visits without admission were not collected as a separate category, which may limit comparability with registries that distinguish outpatient, ER and inpatient exacerbations.

Inter‑centre variability in FeNO measurement devices and the lack of formal adjustment for clustering by centre may also have influenced biomarker estimates. Although the overall sample is larger than in previous Spanish reports, the number of patients with complete biomarker data is modest, which reduces power for subgroup and multivariable analyses and may partly explain the wide confidence intervals and the absence of additional independent predictors of remission. Baseline OCS exposure may have led to conservative phenotyping (potential under-identification of eosinophilic inflammation) and should be considered when interpreting phenotype-stratified analyses. In addition, precise disease duration was not systematically available in the electronic medical records across participating centres and could therefore not be incorporated into the remission predictor analysis. While age of onset was recorded, it does not necessarily reflect total disease duration, which may be more relevant when evaluating remission probability in the context of airway remodelling and long-standing disease. Future studies incorporating accurate disease duration may provide further insight into predictors of clinical remission. Finally, missing data for FeNO and blood eosinophils at 12 months may have led to a conservative estimation of biological remission, and the results may not be fully generalisable beyond specialised tertiary‑care settings. No multiple imputation was performed, and results should therefore be interpreted as complete-case analyses.

Clinical implications

This 12-month multicentre real-world cohort supports tezepelumab as an effective upstream biologic option for patients with severe asthma across a broad spectrum of phenotypes. Consistent reductions in exacerbations, maintenance OCS use, and T2 biomarkers, together with meaningful improvements in asthma control, indicate that tezepelumab can deliver clinically relevant benefits even in patients with high baseline disease burden and mixed naïve/switch profiles. By jointly assessing strict and pragmatic clinical remission alongside biological remission, our findings highlight the importance of using multidomain remission as a realistic, practice-oriented treatment target rather than focusing solely on isolated outcomes. In daily care, these results suggest that tezepelumab should be considered not only for patients with classical T2-high disease but also for those with more complex phenotypes, while recognising that the deepest remission is more likely to be achieved in allergic biologic-naïve patients.

Conclusions

In this 12-month, multicentre real-world study, tezepelumab produced sustained improvements across clinical, functional, and inflammatory domains, with substantial reductions in severe exacerbations and maintenance oral corticosteroid use, improvements in asthma control and lung function, and marked suppression of type 2 biomarkers. By applying both strict and pragmatic definitions of clinical remission and incorporating a biomarker-based definition of biological remission, we characterised the depth and heterogeneity of response achievable with upstream TSLP blockade in routine care. The partial overlap between clinical and biological remission, together with the greater benefit observed in allergic biologic-naïve patients, underscores the need for multidomain remission targets and phenotype-aware treatment strategies when optimising biologic therapy in severe asthma.
