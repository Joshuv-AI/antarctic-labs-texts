Cross-sectional study and clinical prediction of post-thyroidectomy discomfort
Authors: Jin Du, Zhaohua Shen, Yang Song, Xiaonan Huang, Fanfu Fang, Chenlu Ding, Qiuxia Ni, Yan Guo, Hengyu Li, Junyi Shi, Jing Chen, Zhiping Huang, Chaoqian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThyroid lobectomy may result in various postoperative adverse symptoms, which are closely associated with patient satisfaction and recovery outcomes. The aim of this study was to investigate the incidence of discomfort symptoms in postoperative patients following thyroid lobectomy and to develop a predictive model for early identification of patients at high risk of postoperative pain and vomiting, which would be helpful in implementing preventive management.MethodsWe enrolled a total of 532 patients hospitalised for thyroid lobectomy under general anaesthesia between October 2024 and March 2025, and assessed patients’ postoperative discomfort symptoms by means of a questionnaire. Risk factors were identified by multivariate logistic regression and nomograms were created, followed by performance assessment and clinical utility assessment of nomograms.ResultsThe most common complications after thyroidectomy include pain in the incision, pharyngeal symptoms (sore throat, dysphagia, cough, etc.) and sleep disturbance. The postoperative pain frequency prediction model incorporated five correlates: thyroxine (T4), thyroglobulin antibody (TGAb), lymph node metastasis, age, and body mass index (BMI), the postoperative pain level prediction model incorporated three correlates: thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), BMI, and nodal benign or malignant status, and the postoperative vomiting prediction model incorporated four correlates: TGAb, sex, nodal diameter, and nalbuphine use.ConclusionsThe established nomogram may help accurately identify high-risk patients for post-thyroidectomy pain and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), facilitating the timely provision of personalized pain and vomiting management strategies.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 532 patients undergoing thyroid lobectomy, the most prevalent postoperative discomforts were pharyngeal symptoms, pain, and sleep disturbance. Three validated nomograms were established to predict postoperative pain frequency (5 predictors), pain severity (3 predictors), and vomiting (4 predictors), with favorable discriminative performance.

What is known and what is new?

• Post-thyroidectomy pain and postoperative nausea and vomiting are highly prevalent, but targeted predictive models for thyroid lobectomy remain lacking.

• This study profiles multi-dimensional postoperative discomfort in Chinese thyroid lobectomy patients, and develops 3 validated nomograms for personalized risk prediction of pain and vomiting.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The nomograms enable preoperative identification of high-risk patients. Clinicians should implement personalized prophylactic analgesic and antiemetic strategies for high-risk individuals to optimize perioperative management and improve postoperative recovery.

Introduction

Thyroidectomy is the mainstay for the treatment of benign and malignant thyroid diseases, with surgical approaches including thyroid lobectomy, total or near-total thyroidectomy, and cervical lymph node dissection (1). In recent years, the rising incidence of thyroid cancer has increased the demand for thyroid surgery, and short-term postoperative complications significantly affect patients’ recovery quality and healthcare resource consumption (2). Previous studies have focused on complications such as recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and haemorrhage, which have been significantly reduced with advances in surgical techniques and equipment (3-6). Meanwhile, postoperative pain, nausea, and vomiting are considered to be “minor problems” despite their high incidence. In fact, as patients increasingly prioritise their postoperative experience, such discomfort has become a significant concern for patients and a key issue in the field of surgery. Previous studies have identified neck pain and dysphagia as the most frequent complaints after thyroidectomy (7,8). Although pain following thyroid lobectomy is moderate, it remains a significant and common issue faced by patients (9). The incidence of postoperative sore throat after general anaesthesia has been reported to be as high as 62% (10) and up to 80% among thyroidectomy patients, which may be associated with the extent of the operation, age, duration of the operation, intraoperative blood loss and other factors (8). Pain not only reduces patients’ quality of life but also induces opioid-related nausea and vomiting, further aggravating postoperative discomfort (11).

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is the second most common discomfort following thyroid surgery after pain, and the incidence is as high as 80% in high-risk patients (12). Risk factors for PONV include female sex, non-smoker status, intraoperative and postoperative opioid use, and anaesthesia duration (13).

In addition, postoperative sleep quality has received attention (7). Sleep disturbances can occur in up to 40% of patients on the first day after thyroidectomy (14). The onset of it may be attributable to the aforementioned discomfort symptoms, as well as psychological factors such as the patient’s fear and anxiety (15).

Previous studies have predominantly focused on postoperative discomfort in patients undergoing general anaesthesia, and this paper will concentrate on the period following thyroid lobectomy. This study aims to evaluate the frequency and intensity of problems in the early post-operative period following thyroid lobectomy, and to identify factors associated with pain severity and vomiting in order to develop effective prevention strategies. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-0208/rc).

Methods

Population and sample of research

The descriptive cross-sectional study population comprised patients undergoing thyroid lobectomy who were admitted to the Breast and Thyroid Surgery Department of Changhai Hospital between October 2024 and March 2025. Our inclusion criteria were: age 18–75 years; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade I or II with good general condition; undergoing open thyroid lobectomy; returning to the ward on the first postoperative day and staying for at least 6 hours; consenting to participate in the assessment of surgical-related pain outcomes. Exclusion criteria included: preoperative symptoms of sore throat or hoarseness, or a history of upper respiratory tract infection within two weeks; use of sedative-analgesic medications within 24 hours preoperatively or a history of analgesic dependence; endoscopic thyroidectomy; lateral neck lymph node dissection; patients who refusal to participate, or whose impaired consciousness or cognitive function precluding valid assessment. All patients received general anaesthesia, with prophylaxis medication (dolasetron or ondansetron) administered intraoperatively. The malignant patients routinely undergo central neck dissection (CND). Postoperatively, patients routinely received propacetamol (2 g × 1 or 2 g × 2) and/or dexketoprofen (50 mg × 1).

Data collection

In this study, Questionnaire Star was used to collect data on patients’ basic characteristics and postoperative discomfort within 24 hours after surgery. Baseline clinical data were obtained from medical and follow-up records, including home address, occupation, marital status, hypertension, diabetes and coronary artery disease history; intraoperative anaesthetic medication and dosage (propofol, betamethasone, dolasetron, etc.); intraoperative bleeding, surgery duration, anaesthesia duration, the use of analgesic pumps, and postoperative analgesic (propacetamol, dexketoprofen); triiodothyronine (T3), thyroxine (T4), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), thyroglobulin antibody (TGAb), thyroglobulin (TG), thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels; pathological type, tumor size, and lymph node metastasis. Quick response (QR) codes were distributed for patients to complete on postoperative day 1 before discharge. Questionnaires with incomplete information will be excluded.

The questionnaire form consisted of 35 questions (Table S1). It was developed according to domestic and international clinical guidelines for thyroid disease management and expert consensus on postoperative rehabilitation and nursing. It also draws upon thyroid-specific quality of life measures [Thyroid Cancer-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (THYCA-QoL), Thyroid-Related Patient-Reported Outcome (ThyPRO), etc.] and established general scales for cancer patients’ quality of life, anxiety, and depression (16-19). The questionnaire adheres to standard protocols for medical research survey design. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale is 0.919, with a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.877, indicating good internal consistency and reliability.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changhai Hospital (No. CHEC2025-074), and all participants signed an informed consent form or gave verbal consent.

Statistical analyses

Given the stepwise progression in the severity of the patient’s pain response, we classified patients into two categories—mild pain and severe pain—based on whether their pain was tolerable. Severe pain is defined as score ≥6 based on median scores. The frequency of pain is classified into two categories based on median occurrence frequency, for similar reasons. Frequent postoperative pain is defined as pain frequency ≥3. Overweight is defined as BMI ≥24 kg/m2.

Clinical variables related to postoperative pain and vomiting were rated a priori based on clinical importance, existing evidence, and predictors identified in the published literature (20-23). All factors considered are presented in the basic information form. A Mann-Whitney U test or t-test (mean ± standard deviation) was adopted to examine the continuous parameters. A Chi-squared (Fisher’s exact) test was adopted to examine the categorical parameters. Then, a univariate logistic regression analyzes was performed to assess those factors associated with postoperative pain and vomiting, with variables exhibiting P<0.10 being included in the multivariate analysis. Finally, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted using forward stepwise regression, with P<0.05 as the criterion to identify the final independent predictors and establish nomograms. The predictive ability and model compliance were evaluated using concordance index (C-index) and calibration curves. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) was performed to evaluate the discriminative performance of the model. The accuracy of the model can be determined by comparing the area under the ROC curve. Results of logistic regression were used to generate the nomogram. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant in all statistical analyses. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics software (version 26), R software (Version 4.1.2), and Prism GraphPad (Version 9.0.0).

Results

In this study, we distributed 600 questionnaires, with 532 returned, representing a non-response rate of 11.3%. Among these, 357 participants were female, with an average age of 47.15 years and an average body mass index (BMI) of 26.34 kg/m2. 93.05% were married, 77.44% of the patients had no chronic diseases and 87.78% were non-smokers. Surgery was performed in the supine position with unilateral or bilateral thyroidectomy, and the duration of surgery ranged from 0.5 hours to 3.5 hours. A small number of patients received patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) postoperatively, with only 15 cases. Also, the rate of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use on the first postoperative day was 11.28% (Table 1).

Overall, 85.34% of patients reported pain following thyroidectomy (Figure 1). Among patients with pain, most experienced pain 1–2 times (52.26%) (Figure 2C), with mild-to-moderate pain scores [2–5] accounting for 69.16%, and each episode generally lasting 30 minutes (68.59%). Pain mainly occurred within 6 hours postoperatively (57.29%) and some lasted up to 12 hours (20.35%) (Figure 2B). More than half of patients reported peak pain at 2 hours postoperatively (52.3%). Of the patients with postoperative pain, 82.91% had a sore throat and 64.07% had anterior neck (surgical site) pain, with the highest percentage of patients having pain in the throat and operative zone (43.47%) (Figure 2A). In addition, 58.83% of patients felt discomfort, pain, and muscle stiffness in neck and shoulder movement postoperatively.

Distribution of postoperative vomiting

Postoperatively, 36.09% of patients experienced nausea, of which 85.42% was mild (score 1–5 on the rating scale). A total of 31.39% of patients experienced postoperative vomiting, mostly within 6 hours of surgery (71.88%) (Figure 2D). Twenty-six patients reported a significant increase in pain following vomiting, while 7 perceived that vomiting was triggered by a marked exacerbation of pain. Additionally, 45.11% of patients had decreased appetite on postoperative day 2, most of which was mild (score 1–3, 70.42%) (Figure 1).

Distribution of other discomfort symptoms

Pharyngeal symptom survey results showed that almost all patients (89.10%) had a sore throat, but most (80.38%) scored ≤5 points. Most patients experienced postoperative pharyngeal discomfort and dysphagia (87.97%), 76.32% felt dry mouth, 59.59% felt hoarse, and 70.49% coughed, but these symptoms were usually mild (Figure 1).

Regarding sleep status on the first postoperative day, 73.87% of patients reported disturbed sleep, including difficulty falling asleep or early wakening. Among these, 25.19% experienced a higher degree of disturbance (scores of 6–10). Postoperatively, 47.56% of patients felt lethargic, mostly mild (scores 1–5, 88.93%). Additionally, 48.31% of patients experienced distress, with 65.04% fearing pain and 43.80% fearing vomiting (Figure 1).

Furthermore, 13.72% of patients experienced diarrhoea, 21.62% had skin problems (e.g., itchiness, dryness) present, and 34.59% felt a rapid heartbeat (Figure 1).

Analysis of risk factors of postoperative pain and construction of prediction model

Univariate logistic regression analysis of clinical data showed that TGAb, central lymph node metastasis, age >40 years, and BMI ≥24 kg/m2 were significantly associated with postoperative pain frequency (all P<0.05); TPOAb, age >40 years, and BMI ≥24 kg/m2 were significantly associated with postoperative pain severity (all P<0.05). No significant correlations were observed between T4, nodule size and pain frequency, nor between nodule benignity/malignancy, sex and pain severity (all P>0.05). Multivariate logistic regression further identified independent predictors of frequent postoperative pain (pain frequency ≥3): T4 [odds ratio (OR) =0.1; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.012–0.821], TGAb (OR =2.171; 95% CI: 1.072–4.396), lymph node metastasis (OR =2.058; 95% CI: 1.288–3.289), age >40 years (OR =0.621; 95% CI: 0.395–0.976), BMI ≥24 kg/m2 (OR =0.58; 95% CI: 0.377–0.893); independent predictors of severe postoperative pain (pain score ≥6): TPOAb (OR =2.015; 95% CI: 1.04–3.904), BMI ≥24 kg/m2 (OR =0.524; 95% CI: 0.315–0.871), and nodal malignancy (OR =2.04; 95% CI: 0.936–4.442). Analysis of risk factors of postoperative vomiting and construction of prediction model

Univariate logistic regression analysis of clinical data revealed that TGAb, sex, history of alcohol consumption, nodule size, and intraoperative use of sufentanil, oliceridine, and nalbuphine were significantly associated with postoperative vomiting (all P<0.05). No significant associations were observed for smoking history or serotonin receptor antagonists (e.g., ondansetron) (all P>0.05). Multivariate logistic regression further identified independent predictors of postoperative vomiting: TGAb (OR =2.302; 95% CI: 1.147–4.619), male (OR =0.379; 95% CI: 0.214–0.673), and nodule diameter >15 mm (OR =1.69; 95% CI: 0.977–2.924), nalbuphine use (OR =1.929; 95% CI: 1.129–3.294) (Table 2).

The nomogram for postoperative vomiting is presented in Figure 3E. Internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resampling yielded a C-index of 0.664 for postoperative vomiting. The calibration curves (Figure 3F) illustrated good consistency between predicted and actual probabilities. ROC curve analysis was then performed (Figure 4), with an AUC of 0.6640, indicating moderate discriminative ability of the model.

Discussion

With increasing focus on patient experience, early postoperative discomfort after thyroid lobectomy under general anesthesia deserves more attention. In our epidemiological survey, early postoperative sore throat was the most common complaint, affecting 89.10% of patients. Pharyngeal discomfort, dysphagia, dry mouth, and cough were also very common, affecting more than 70% of patients. Nausea and vomiting were also relatively frequent postoperative discomforts. A 2023 cross-sectional study on symptoms following thyroid surgery reported that neck pain was the most common symptom (52.3%), followed by voice changes (38.4%) and dysphagia (37.7%). Most symptoms occurred within 1 hour after anesthesia emergence and were generally mild (8). Endotracheal intubation during anesthesia may be a contributing factor to laryngopharyngeal symptoms such as pharyngeal discomfort and dysphagia. A systematic review published in 2026 indicated that the incidence of postoperative dysphagia is approximately 25%, with a significant increase occurring within 1–2 weeks after surgery. Factors associated with persistent or more severe dysphagia include surgical extent, advanced age, surgical technique, central or lateral lymph node dissection, and the need for radioactive iodine therapy (24). A 2023 prospective study reported that the incidence of sore throat after general anesthesia was as high as 61.8% (25). Our findings indicated an even higher incidence following thyroid lobectomy.

In our results, independent risk factors for postoperative pain included abnormal T4, abnormal TGAb, abnormal TPOAb, lymph node metastasis, age ≤40 years, BMI <24 kg/m2, and nodal malignancy. Postoperative pain arises from direct surgical wound injury, postoperative hemorrhage, local inflammation, and complications; routine postoperative drain placement may also contribute to related discomfort and pain. Research has shown that hypothyroidism leads to overactivation of central pain pathways, thereby lowering the pain threshold in mice (26,27). Positive TGAb and TPOAb indicate enhanced autoimmune reactivity of thyroid tissue, which is commonly observed in patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. TGAb-positive patients were more likely to experience more than three postoperative pain episodes, while TPOAb positivity was associated with higher postoperative pain severity. Reduced thyroid hormone secretion in Hashimoto’s patients may be the cause of their postoperative pain. This also explains why elevated T4 was linked to fewer pain episodes. Additionally, preoperative elevated T4 levels suggest that the patient may have subclinical or clinical hyperthyroidism. Studies have demonstrated that the endogenous analgesic system in hyperthyroid mice may be enhanced, resulting in an elevated pain threshold (28). Older patients had less frequent pain, which may be related to higher pain tolerance in the elderly population. Overweight patients in our study exhibited lower pain frequency and severity, a finding inconsistent with previous reports. This discrepancy may be explained by higher anaesthetic drug dosage in obese patients, and hyperalgesia in severely obese individuals may also be an important contributor to reduced postoperative pain (29). Furthermore, prior research on total knee arthroplasty has reported better postoperative pain recovery in obese patients compared with those with normal weight (30). In our findings, patients with malignant nodules experienced greater postoperative pain intensity than those with benign nodules. This may be attributed to the fact that malignant patients routinely undergo CND, which requires extensive anterior cervical tissue dissection, anatomical dissection of the tracheoesophageal groove, and lymph node dissection, resulting in greater surgical trauma and local inflammatory responses. Additionally, it may irritate branches of the cervical plexus, thereby exacerbating postoperative pain. Positive lymph nodes are associated with more advanced stages of thyroid cancer, larger tumours and increased surgical difficulty; these factors may explain why positive lymph nodes lead to increased pain. However, a single-centre study of colorectal resection showed no difference in pain levels between benign and malignant patients (31). The specific mechanism remains to be studied.

Given the limited research on postoperative discomfort following thyroidectomy, investigations into its associated risk factors are even scarcer. In the general setting of general anaesthesia, previous studies have identified age ≤55 years, head and neck surgical site, anaesthesia duration ≥4 hours, and a history of chronic pharyngitis as independent risk factors for postoperative sore throat (20). The aforementioned 2023 study also noted that postoperative sore throat was associated with large tracheal tube size, patient age, and prolonged anaesthesia duration (25). Another study similarly demonstrated that thyroid surgery, age <40 years, female sex, short thyromental distance (<6.5 cm), neck circumference <34 cm, anaesthesia duration >100 min, and the presence of bloody sputum were independent risk factors for postoperative sore throat (32). In a 2024 retrospective review of acute postsurgical pain (APSP), younger age, female sex, and smoking history were identified as risk factors for acute postoperative pain, while the impact of obesity on postoperative pain remains controversial (33). With regard to age, our findings are consistent with these previous studies; however, anaesthesia duration and sex showed no significant association with postoperative pain in our results.

PONV is the second most common postoperative discomfort after pain. Our results identified abnormal TGAb, female sex, nodule diameter >15 mm, and intraoperative nalbuphine administration as independent risk factors for post-thyroidectomy vomiting. Univariate analysis revealed a significant association between alcohol consumption and postoperative vomiting, as well as a borderline significant association for smoking history (P<0.10); however, multivariate analysis showed no independent association for these two factors, which may be attributed to covariance with the sex factor. Previous studies have consistently identified female sex as an independent risk factor for PONV (13,34-37). A 2016 review concluded that young age, non-smoking status, prior history of motion sickness or vomiting, and multiple anaesthesia-related factors (including opioids such as nalbuphine) are all associated with postoperative vomiting (13). Notably, pain may also be an independent risk factor for vomiting (13,38). In addition, a 2024 study on nausea and vomiting after radical thyroid cancer showed that age, Apfel score, modified Frailty index (mFI) score, free fatty acids, uric acid, homocysteine (HCY), and fasting glucose were independent risk factors for PONV (39).

Notably, 73.87% of patients experienced sleep disturbances on the first postoperative day. Concurrently, many patients reported anxiety and concerns about pain, which may contribute to the occurrence of these sleep disturbances. In addition, nausea and drowsiness are common adverse effects of postoperative analgesic therapy.

In this study, we constructed nomograms to predict postoperative pain and vomiting based on multivariate regression results, with separate models established for frequent pain and severe pain. These nomograms can be applied to the preoperative risk assessment and personalised perioperative management of patients undergoing thyroid lobectomy: prior to surgery, routine data is collected on the patient’s serum T4, TGAb and TPOAb levels, age, BMI, preoperative ultrasound-indicated risk of lymph node metastasis, nodule characteristics, gender and nodule diameter. Using the risk-scoring model, the probabilities of postoperative frequent pain, severe pain and vomiting are calculated, and stratified interventions are implemented according to risk level: high-risk patients (probability >70%) receive prophylactic dexamethasone preoperatively, undergo ultrasound-guided bilateral superficial cervical plexus nerve block during surgery, and are administered non-opioid multimodal analgesia postoperatively, alongside dual antiemetic therapy combining a 5-hydroxytryptamine 3 (5-HT3) receptor antagonist and dexamethasone; for medium-risk patients (probability 30–70%), routine postoperative use of NSAIDs and administration of antiemetics on demand; for low-risk patients (probability <30%), postoperative use of analgesics and antiemetics only on demand. This approach enables precise perioperative management of pain and vomiting, thereby optimising the patient’s postoperative recovery experience.

To improve patients’ postoperative experience, numerous studies have investigated intraoperative and postoperative strategies to alleviate post-thyroidectomy discomfort. Ultrasound-guided bilateral superficial cervical plexus block and bupivacaine wound infiltration both effectively reduce postoperative pain in patients undergoing thyroidectomy (40,41). Multiple studies have demonstrated that perioperative low-dose dexamethasone alleviates postoperative pain and reduces the incidence of PONV (42,43). These findings indicate that optimization of intraoperative anaesthetic protocols may reduce postoperative pain and vomiting in this patient population. In a randomised controlled trial, Papoian et al. demonstrated that non-opioid analgesia provided comparable analgesic efficacy to opioid-based regimens, with reduced perioperative opioid exposure (44). Furthermore, opioid-free anesthesia can improve the quality of recovery and reduce PONV after thyroidectomy, indicating that non-opioid analgesics can be used as alternatives (45-47). The favourable analgesic effects of Traditional Chinese Medicine wrist-ankle acupuncture following thyroidectomy suggest that non-pharmacological pain management strategies warrant further investigation to reduce medication use and associated adverse effects, including PONV. Our team has initiated a multicentre randomised controlled trial, which is currently underway (48).

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. Firstly, this study was a single-centre cross-sectional investigation employing retrospective questionnaire surveys; the applicability and stability of the nomograms across different regions and populations in different medical centres still require further confirmation through multicentre, large-sample external validation studies. Moreover, postoperative pain, as a patient-reported outcome, is inherently subjective, which may have affected the predictive performance of our models. Secondly, we did not explore other potential predictive factors, including a history of chronic pharyngitis and motion sickness. Thirdly, this study only evaluated acute discomfort symptoms within 24 hours postoperatively and did not address chronic discomfort at 1 week, 1 month, or beyond. Future studies should extend the follow-up period to systematically analyze the patterns of occurrence and risk factors for long-term symptoms such as chronic pain and dysphagia following thyroid lobectomy. Finally, several of the identified independent risk factors are non-modifiable, which limits their clinical utility in optimizing perioperative management strategies.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that the most prevalent postoperative symptoms following thyroid lobectomy include pain, pharyngeal symptoms, and sleep disturbance. We also constructed nomogram models with high predictive accuracy for the risk of postoperative pain and vomiting in these patients. Individualised preventive interventions should be implemented for patients with high-risk scores. Moving forward, our team will conduct a multicentre clinical study to further investigate postoperative pain in this patient population, with the aim of providing novel strategies for postoperative pain management.
