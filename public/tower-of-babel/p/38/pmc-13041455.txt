Evaluating the technical efficiency of primary health facilities in delivering adolescent mental, sexual and reproductive health services in Burkina Faso
Authors: Ludovic Deo Gracias Tapsoba, Michel Adurayi Amenah, Nassirou Ibrahim, Roxane Borgès Da Silva, Ama Pokuaa Fenny, Jacob Novignon, Ziemlé Clément Meda, Herman Bazié, Annick Gladzah, Irene A Agyepong, Tim Ensor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is a critical phase of life, with sexual, reproductive, and mental health being essential to overall well-being. Despite global and national initiatives to improve adolescent health, substantial gaps persist in service delivery, especially in low-resource settings like Burkina Faso. Primary healthcare (PHC) facilities play a pivotal role in addressing adolescent mental, sexual, and reproductive health (AMSRH) needs. However, evidence on the technical efficiency of these facilities in delivering AMSRH services remains limited. This study evaluates the technical efficiency of PHC facilities in Burkina Faso in providing AMSRH services and identifies key factors influencing efficiency.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in 132 PHC facilities across the West-Central and Hauts Bassins regions of Burkina Faso from September to October 2022. Data on facility characteristics, resource availability, and service delivery were collected. Technical efficiency of ASRH services was assessed using Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA) with a Translog production function, while a truncated regression identified determinants of efficiency. The analysis included inputs such as laboratory tests, consultation rooms, and trained providers, with efficiency scores ranging from 0 to 1. Data analysis was performed using STATA 16.ResultsOf the surveyed facilities, 77% were in rural areas, and 23% were in urban areas. While all facilities offered adolescent sexual and reproductive health (ASRH) services, only 42% provided adolescent mental health (AMH) services, which were excluded from efficiency analysis due to low adolescent consultation numbers. Average technical efficiency was 0.66, with urban facilities (0.73) and those with electricity (0.71) showing higher efficiency. Access to electricity (Coef. = 0.061, p = 0.004) and laboratories (Coef. = 0.053, p = 0.020) significantly enhanced efficiency. These findings highlight critical infrastructure gaps and the need for targeted investments to optimize service delivery.ConclusionThis study highlights critical gaps in AMH service availability within PHC facilities in Burkina Faso. Key determinants of technical efficiency in delivering adolescent sexual and reproductive health ASRH services included access to electricity, laboratories, and trained providers. Conversely, longer facility manager tenure negatively impacted efficiency, emphasizing the need for dynamic leadership. Targeted investments in infrastructure, capacity-building initiatives, and digital health adoption are essential. Expanding AMH services and aligning with national guidelines are urgent priorities for optimizing AMSRH service delivery and improving adolescent health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03240-6.Keywords: Adolescent sexual health services, Technical efficiency, Primary healthcare, Adolescent mental health services

Introduction

Adolescence is a transformative phase of life, marked by significant physical, emotional, and social development [1–3]. During this period, the health and well-being of adolescents play a pivotal role in shaping their future potential and contributions to society [4]. The global importance of adolescent health has been consistently highlighted in international forums and policy frameworks, particularly concerning adolescent sexual and reproductive health (ASRH) and mental health [5–8]. ASRH is essential for reducing early and unintended pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and related health complications [9, 10]. Likewise, addressing adolescent mental health is critical, as mental disorders are among the leading causes of disability worldwide [11].

Global initiatives have underscored the significance of these areas. For instance, the 1994 International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) in Cairo emphasized the need for comprehensive sexual and reproductive health services for adolescents [12]. Similarly, the 2002 United Nations General Assembly Special Session on Children called for policies and programs to ensure adolescents' sexual and reproductive health rights [13]. Despite these efforts, gaps remain, especially in mental health, where global estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO) reveal that nearly one in seven adolescents suffers from a mental disorder [14]. These conditions often go unrecognized and untreated, leading to significant impacts on educational attainment, employability, and quality of life [15]. Suicide remains a leading cause of death among young people, underscoring the urgent need to prioritize adolescent mental health [16].

In sub-Saharan Africa, the challenges of providing adolescent health services are compounded by limited resources and systemic inefficiencies [17, 18]. The region is characterized by high rates of adolescent pregnancy, early marriages, and a significant burden of STIs, including HIV [19–21]. Social stigma, cultural norms, and gender inequality further restrict adolescents' access to sexual and reproductive health services [22, 23]. Mental health services are even more neglected, with few programs addressing the unique mental health needs of adolescents [24, 25]. This neglect persists despite evidence indicating that early intervention in mental health can significantly improve life outcomes [26, 27].

Burkina Faso exemplifies these challenges. As a member state of the West African Health Organization (WAHO), the country has adopted regional strategies to improve adolescent health. National initiatives, such as the Adolescent and Youth Health Strategic Plan (2016–2020), have sought to enhance ASRH services [28, 29]. However, mental health services remain grossly inadequate, with limited resources and interventions at the primary healthcare level [30, 31]. Most mental health services are concentrated in urban centers or higher-tier facilities, leaving large segments of the adolescent population underserved, particularly in rural areas [30]. Cultural and religious beliefs, alongside widespread stigma, further hinder the uptake of mental health services [32].

In Burkina Faso, the provision of Adolescent Mental, Sexual, and Reproductive Health (AMSRH) services reflects a mixed landscape of progress and persistent gaps [33]. Sexual and reproductive health services for adolescents have been integrated into the primary healthcare system, with national guidelines in place to standardize care [34]. However, the availability and quality of these services vary significantly across regions, particularly between urban and rural areas [33]. While all surveyed primary healthcare facilities in the West-Central and Hauts Bassins regions offer sexual and reproductive health services, mental health services remain severely limited, with only 42% of facilities providing even basic care for mental health conditions [33, 35]. This disparity is more pronounced in rural settings, where infrastructural deficits, such as inconsistent electricity supply and the absence of laboratories, further constrain service delivery [36]. Trends indicate that adolescent sexual and reproductive health services are increasingly acknowledged in policy frameworks, but implementation challenges, including a lack of trained personnel, inadequate funding, and minimal integration of mental health into broader adolescent health initiatives, hinder progress [37].

The primary healthcare (PHC) system plays a critical role in addressing the gaps in AMSRH services, particularly in low-resource settings like Burkina Faso [34]. As the first point of contact for many adolescents, primary healthcare facilities are uniquely positioned to deliver accessible and community-centered AMSRH services [38, 39]. These facilities have the potential to reduce barriers to care, such as distance, cost, and stigma, by integrating AMSRH into their existing service delivery frameworks [40]. However, their capacity to fulfil this role effectively depends on the efficiency with which they utilize available resources to maximize service delivery. Inefficiencies in the primary health system, including misallocation of resources, inadequate infrastructure, and a lack of trained personnel, often undermine the potential impact of these services [41]. Evaluating the technical efficiency of primary healthcare facilities in rendering AMSRH services is therefore essential to identify bottlenecks, optimize resource use, and enhance service delivery. Such analysis not only highlights areas requiring targeted interventions but also informs policy decisions aimed at strengthening the capacity of the primary health system to address AMSRH needs comprehensively and equitably. Without addressing efficiency, the goal of ensuring universal access to quality AMSRH services for all adolescents remains unattainable.

While studies on healthcare efficiency in sub-Saharan Africa have focused primarily on secondary and tertiary facilities [42–46], there is a notable gap in research on the efficiency of PHC in providing AMSRH services. This study seeks to fill this gap by assessing the efficiency of PHC in Burkina Faso in delivering AMSRH services. Specifically, it aims to (i) estimate the technical efficiency of PHC in providing AMSRH services using Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA) and (ii) identify environmental and structural factors that influence efficiency levels. By providing evidence on the performance of PHC and the determinants of efficiency, this study aims to inform targeted policy interventions and resource allocation strategies to enhance the delivery of AMSRH services in Burkina Faso and similar settings. Addressing these challenges is critical to achieving health equity and improving outcomes for adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to evaluate the provision and efficiency of selected PHC in delivering AMSRH services. The design was chosen for its ability to provide a snapshot of healthcare service delivery and efficiency at a single point in time, offering insights into the current state of AMSRH services in the targeted regions. While the initial intent was to comprehensively assess both mental and sexual health services, the study found that mental health services for adolescents were marginalized and limited in availability.

Data

This study was part of a larger project titled “Adolescent mental, sexual and reproductive health and wellbeing policy, program and primary care implementation priorities in West Africa (AdoWA). The ADOWA project was implemented in three West African countries, Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Niger.

In Burkina Faso, we conducted a field survey from September to October 2022, targeting PHC facilities that provide AMSRH services. The survey captured comprehensive data on AMSRH services across multiple regions. Data collection focused on facility characteristics, service availability, staffing, infrastructure, and resource allocation. This specific focus on Burkina Faso ensures detailed insights into the country's AMSRH service landscape. The survey instrument used in this study was specifically developed for the AdoWA project to assess AMSRH service delivery in PHC facilities. A detailed English-language version of the survey is provided as a supplementary file (See supplementary file).

Sampling techniques

We selected the study sites using a multi-stage stratified sampling approach. In the first stage, we purposively selected two mutually exclusive regions—West-Central and Hauts Bassins—based on their accessibility amidst security challenges and their relevance to key health indicators, particularly those related to mental, sexual, and reproductive health.

In the second stage, five districts per region were selected using simple random sampling in Microsoft Excel, based on randomly generated numbers. we randomly selected three urban districts and two rural districts in each region. For the West-Central region, the urban districts selected were Léo, Réo, and Koudougou, while the rural districts were Tenado and Sabou. In the Hauts Bassins region, the urban districts included Dafra, Orodara, and Houndé, and the rural districts were Dandé and Karangasso Vigué.

In the third stage, we systematically selected 160 primary healthcare facilities proportional to the size of each district, with 80 facilities in each region. These included all Health and Social Promotion Centers (HSPC), medical centers, as well as private facilities providing mental, sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services. These facilities represent the two steps of the first level of care in Burkina Faso: the HSPCs, which are the first point of contact for primary care, and the medical centers with surgical branches, which provide more advanced services.

Out of the initially planned 160 healthcare facilities, data from 132 facilities were included in the analysis. Data from 28 facilities (approximately 18% of the original sample) were excluded due to unavailability of data for key variables, which rendered them unsuitable for analysis. These exclusions arose from factors such as security concerns, missing or outdated data, and facilities not meeting the criteria of being primary healthcare facilities. The 10 health facilities that were not surveyed due to security constraints were located in rural areas across the two regions. The 6 medical centers with Surgical branches (CMA) were excluded in order to focus exclusively on the first tier of the primary level of the health system; these facilities were predominantly located in urban areas. The remaining twelve health facilities excluded from the analysis due to missing or outlier values in key variables were mainly primary health centers (HSPC) under public management, evenly distributed between urban and rural areas. The final sample of 132 facilities was representative of the study regions. A structured questionnaire was administered at each facility, gathering data across sections such as general facility information, adolescent sexual and reproductive health services, mental health services, staffing, medical consumables, laboratory tests, and funding sources for general and adolescent-specific activities.

Variables and their measurements

The output variable is the number of adolescent SRH consultations, calculated as the sum of adolescents aged 10–14 and 15–19 who received SRH consultations in 2021. This variable represents the primary measure of service delivery output, reflecting the volume of care provided to adolescents. The input variables reflect resources for AMSRH service delivery, including the proportion of laboratory tests conducted for adolescents, consultation rooms dedicated to adolescents, medicines dispensed for adolescents, and the number of providers trained in ASRH services (labor). The proportion of laboratory tests is calculated by multiplying the total number of tests by the share of adolescent outpatients, while trained providers represent the critical labor component. This aligns with production function theory, which emphasizes labor and capital as essential inputs. Here, capital is represented by infrastructure and consumables, and labor by the availability of trained healthcare providers, ensuring comprehensive service delivery.

Explanatory variables capture factors influencing technical efficiency. Facility characteristics, such as access to electricity, laboratories, and digital tools, are included. For instance, the presence of electricity (a dummy variable: 1 for yes, 0 for no) is crucial for operating essential equipment. Human resources, such as the number of providers trained in ASRH over the past two years, are also considered. Additionally, population coverage, measured within and beyond 5 km, reflects the facility's reach and potential demand for services. Other factors, such as the gender of head of facility and tenure, and the percentage of adolescents in the catchment area, provide contextual insights.

Stochastic frontier analysis

We employed stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) to assess the technical efficiency of primary healthcare facilities (PHC) in delivering ASRH service [47]. We chose SFA over non-parametric methods like Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) because of its ability to account for statistical noise and isolate inefficiency.

Our analysis followed a two-stage approach. In the first stage, we calculated technical efficiency scores, ranging from 0 (inefficient) to 1 (efficient), to evaluate how well facilities utilized their inputs to maximize AMSRH service delivery. In the second stage, we regressed these scores against facility-specific factors using a truncated regression model to identify determinants of inefficiency. Key factors examined included access to infrastructure (e.g., electricity, laboratories), staff training in ASRH, and population coverage.

A Translog production function was used in the analysis due to its greater flexibility in capturing complex input–output relationships compared to the Cobb–Douglas function. Results

Characteristics of healthcare facilities

Out of the 132 healthcare facilities, 69 facilities (52.3%) were located in the Centre West region and 63 (47.7%) in the Hauts Bassins region. A majority (77%) of the facilities were situated in rural areas, while 23% were urban. Most facilities (85%) were managed by state-employed staff, and 15% were privately managed. All facilities (100%) reported provided sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, with 79% having at least one provider trained in ASRH services in last two years at the time of the survey. However, only 42% of the facilities provided adolescent mental health (AMH) services, and only 27% had AMH-trained providers (Table 2).

Discussion

This study evaluated the efficiency of ASRH services in PHC facilities in Burkina Faso while examining the availability of MH services. Although service availability for MH was assessed, it was excluded from the efficiency analysis due to the extremely low number of consultations recorded annually. The findings highlight a mixed landscape of progress and persistent challenges, offering actionable insights for policymakers and programmatic interventions to strengthen AMSRH service delivery in resource-constrained settings.

Our estimated average technical efficiency of 0.66 is broadly consistent with previous evidence from sub-Saharan Africa, which generally reports moderate efficiency levels in primary healthcare facilities. For instance, Novignon et al. [49] found a mean efficiency score of 0.60 for AMSRH services in Ghana using Stochastic Frontier Analysis, while other studies document substantial variation and inefficiencies in PHC performance across the region [41, 42]. Similar to our findings, these studies identify infrastructure availability and human resource capacity as key drivers of efficiency, although contextual differences remain, such as higher efficiency among rural facilities in Ghana compared to urban facilities in Burkina Faso in our study. Together, this evidence underscores persistent structural constraints, particularly access to electricity and laboratory services, as major bottlenecks to improving PHC efficiency in low-resource settings.

The integration of ASRH services into PHCs is a positive development, as all surveyed facilities provided SRH services for adolescents. However, the provision of AMH services remains limited, with only 42% of facilities offering such services. This aligns with broader research in SSA, which attributes these gaps to underfunded systems, stigma, and a lack of trained providers [50, 51]. Addressing these disparities requires integrating AMH into primary healthcare, investing in infrastructure, training, and community-based solutions, and implementing evidence-based policies to ensure equitable, comprehensive adolescent healthcare [15].

Infrastructure deficiencies emerged as critical barriers to service delivery, with only 55% of facilities having regular electricity access. Reliable electricity is essential for effective AMSRH service provision, and facilities with consistent electricity demonstrated significantly higher efficiency scores. This underscores the importance of targeted infrastructure investments to optimize service delivery. These findings align with Khogali et al. [52], who highlighted that electrification improves the availability and quality of primary health services, including reliable oxygen supplies, refrigeration, and enhanced medical supply chains in LMICs. Similarly, Leslie et al. [53] emphasized that while infrastructure is crucial for healthcare delivery, it is often insufficient by itself to ensure quality care, highlighting the need for investments that go beyond physical inputs. Scholz et al. [54] further emphasized the importance of reliable infrastructure, noting serious deficiencies in primary care facilities and recommending routine assessments to identify gaps and guide targeted interventions. These studies collectively highlight that addressing infrastructure deficiencies, especially electricity, is critical to improving service readiness and outcomes in resource-limited setting.

Access to a laboratory was positively and significantly associated with the technical efficiency of PHC facilities in delivering ASRH services, underscoring its critical role in optimizing service delivery. Laboratories are essential for providing diagnostic services that enhance the accuracy of care, streamline clinical workflows, and improve the timeliness of interventions. Facilities with access to laboratory services are better equipped to perform necessary diagnostic tests, enabling them to manage adolescent health needs more effectively and efficiently. This aligns with findings by Jain and Rao [55], who demonstrated a strong positive association between PHC laboratory services and outpatient visits, with facilities offering in-house testing for malaria and tuberculosis showing significantly higher performance. Similarly, Almuarik et al. [56] highlighted that laboratory information systems enhance organizational efficiency, economic performance, and care quality by reducing communication errors. These studies emphasize the need for strengthened laboratory infrastructure and capabilities to optimize primary healthcare service delivery.

Digital tools, such as computers and electronic systems, were available in 52% of the surveyed facilities. However, their impact on efficiency in this study was not statistically significant, which contrasts with findings from other studies. Ansari et al. [57] highlighted the potential of electronic health records to enhance healthcare infrastructure in LMICs, while Biga et al. [58] identified the digital divide, encompassing connectivity and digital literacy, as a key barrier to effective implementation. Labrique et al. [59] further emphasized the importance of factors like end-user engagement, adaptability, and sustainable funding for the successful scaling of digital health initiatives.

The negative impact of longer facility manager tenure on efficiency highlights the need for dynamic and adaptive management practices. While longer tenures can offer stability and institutional knowledge, they may also foster complacency and resistance to innovation, limiting operational efficiency [60]. Shorter tenures often bring fresh perspectives, promoting adaptability and alignment with emerging technologies and evidence-based practices. However, frequent leadership changes could disrupt continuity and hinder long-term planning, especially in resource-constrained environments.

Shohet and Lavy [61] emphasized the importance of modernizing healthcare management by integrating innovative practices, such as telemedicine and distributed healthcare networks, to enhance facility performance. Effective facilities management (FM) plays a critical role in healthcare, as highlighted by Azrin et al. [62], who identified key success factors like performance management, ICT integration, and supply chain optimization to maintain service quality. Amos [63] further proposed a performance measurement framework focused on setting performance goals, evaluating service delivery, and addressing gaps to improve FM practices in public hospitals. Applying such strategies in primary healthcare settings could enhance operational efficiency by fostering adaptability, aligning practices with performance goals, and leveraging advanced tools, ultimately optimizing AMSRH service delivery and addressing systemic inefficiencies.

Policy implications

The findings have important policy implications for improving AMSRH services in Burkina Faso. First, prioritizing mental health in national policies and programs is essential to address the significant service gaps identified. Expanding AMH services and ensuring adherence to national guidelines should be a priority, particularly in regions with lower coverage.

Second, targeted investments in infrastructure, such as electricity and laboratory facilities, are critical for optimizing efficiency. These investments should be coupled with initiatives to improve digital health adoption, enabling facilities to leverage technology for better service delivery.

Third, capacity-building programs must be scaled up to train more providers in AMSRH, with a particular focus on mental health. Addressing the shortage of trained personnel will require sustained investment in health worker training and retention strategies.

Finally, managerial practices should be strengthened through regular training and performance evaluations. Implementing leadership development programs and promoting accountability can enhance efficiency and drive improvements in service delivery.

Limitations

We acknowledge two key limitations of this study. First, the reliance on self-reported data may have introduced desirability bias, as respondents might have overstated or understated facility performance or resource availability. Future research could address this limitation by incorporating independent audits or triangulating with external data sources to enhance accuracy. Second, the cross-sectional design captures a snapshot of efficiency but fails to account for temporal changes in facility performance, which could be influenced by evolving policies, funding, or socio-economic shifts. Longitudinal studies would offer a more dynamic understanding of efficiency over time.

However, we emphasize the study’s strengths. By employing stochastic frontier analysis alongside truncated regression, we identified critical factors influencing technical efficiency, providing valuable, evidence-based recommendations for policy and practice. This rigorous, dual-method approach ensures the findings are actionable and relevant, offering a robust foundation for targeted interventions to enhance AMSRH service delivery in low-resource settings.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant gaps and opportunities in the provision and efficiency of AMSRH services in Burkina Faso. While all surveyed facilities offered sexual and reproductive health services, only 42% provided mental health services, exposing critical disparities in service availability. Key determinants of efficiency included access to infrastructure, such as electricity and laboratories, and the presence of ASRH-trained providers. Conversely, longer manager tenure negatively impacted efficiency, underscoring the need for dynamic leadership and periodic training.

Targeted investments in infrastructure, capacity-building programs, and digital health adoption are essential to optimize service delivery. Expanding mental health services and adhering to national guidelines are urgent priorities to ensure comprehensive care. By addressing these gaps and leveraging the study's insights, policymakers can implement strategic interventions to enhance AMSRH service delivery, ultimately improving health outcomes for adolescents in low-resource settings like Burkina Faso.
