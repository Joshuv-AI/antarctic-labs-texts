Acarbose or Canagliflozin vs. Placebo to Ameliorate Post‐Bariatric Hypoglycaemia: The Clinical Outcomes of the HypoBar I Randomised Clinical Trial
Authors: Carolina B. Lobato, Clara Tornøe Winding, Kirstine N. Bojsen‐Møller, Bolette Hartmann, Christoffer Martinussen, Simon Veedfald, Jens J. Holst, Sten Madsbad, Nils Bruun Jørgensen, Carsten Dirksen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTreatment options for post‐bariatric hypoglycaemia (PBH) are empirical. We aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of acarbose and canagliflozin for the treatment of PBH.Materials and MethodsWe performed a randomised, double‐blinded, cross‐over, placebo‐controlled clinical trial where placebo, acarbose 50 mg thrice daily, and canagliflozin 300 mg twice daily were administered for 4 weeks. Treatment effects on blood glucose were monitored through blinded continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) for 10 days at the end of each intervention along with collection of patient‐reported outcomes. The primary outcome was the percentage of time below range during CGM (interstitial glucose below 3.9 mM/70 mg/dL).ResultsEleven participants with Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass and documented PBH completed the trial. When compared with placebo, neither canagliflozin nor acarbose reduced the percentage of time below range (primary outcome, time below 3.9 mmol/L, median (IQR): screening 2.6 (0.7–5.1)%, placebo 1.2 (0.5–2.4)%, acarbose 1.1 (0.8–1.7)% and canagliflozin 0.8 (0.5–3.4)%). Both interventions, however, showed exploratory improvements in pre‐specified secondary outcomes, including time in range and glycaemic variability, as well as a reduction of symptoms of hypoglycaemia and frequency of symptomatic hypoglycaemic events, particularly with canagliflozin. Acarbose was associated with moderate and common side effects that caused treatment discontinuation in one participant. For both treatments, no severe adverse events were documented.ConclusionsHypoBar I is the first randomised, placebo‐controlled study of the treatment of PBH with acarbose or canagliflozin. Reduction in time below range was not achieved by either treatment; nevertheless, improvements in hypothesis‐generating secondary findings merit further investigation and validation. EudraCT number 2022‐000157‐87.

Introduction

Post‐bariatric hypoglycaemia (PBH) is characterised by steep and exaggerated postprandial glucose excursions, followed by enhanced insulin secretion and late reactive hypoglycaemia [1]. The exact pathophysiological mechanisms and especially the different susceptibility to PBH among individuals with similar phenotypes and surgical interventions remain poorly understood [2].

Because of the apparent relation of PBH with meal size, texture and composition, the first line management approach to PBH is dietary interventions, aiming at reducing meal size, avoiding carbohydrates with high glycaemic index and sugary drinks and increasing the relative protein intake [3].

When nutrition regimens fail, empiric pharmacotherapy is commonly attempted, and several treatment options have been explored [4, 5, 6, 7]. Acarbose is commonly used for PBH management despite lacking formal validation. It has been shown to raise the glycaemic nadir during meal tests, reduce time above range in a non‐placebo‐controlled one‐week assessment using continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) [4] and decrease time below 3.3 mmol/L in a cohort of eight patients treated only with acarbose [8]. However, multiple daily doses and frequent gastrointestinal side effects, such as abdominal distention, pain and flatulence, limit adherence [9].

Canagliflozin is used for the treatment of Type 2 diabetes (T2D), chronic kidney disease and heart failure. It inhibits sodium‐dependent glucose absorption mostly in the kidney, and less potently, in the intestine [10]. We hypothesised that these effects could be used for PBH treatment if the drug is timely administered before meals. Our group has shown that a single dose of 600 mg of canagliflozin reduces the rate of glucose absorption and the postprandial secretion of incretin hormones and insulin as well as increases the secretion of glucagon [11]. Others have successfully used it in a case report of PBH [12]. Chronic treatment of PBH with canagliflozin 300 mg daily resulted in a reduction of the rate of reactive hypoglycaemia during an oral glucose tolerance test [8, 13]. Canagliflozin had, however, not been previously investigated in free‐living nor placebo‐controlled conditions.

The clinical aim of HypoBar I was to investigate the efficacy and safety of acarbose and canagliflozin for the treatment of PBH.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A detailed description of the study protocol has been published [14]. In brief, patients with a clinical diagnosis of PBH were screened using symptom diaries and blinded continuous glucose monitoring (CGM; Dexcom G6) for 10 days. To be enrolled in the trial, patients had to fulfil at least one of the following GCM‐derived inclusion criteria for hypoglycaemia: at least one Level 2 hypoglycaemic event (interstitial fluid glucose (IFG) < 3.0 mmol/L for 5 min or longer), at least one symptomatic Level 1 hypoglycaemic event (IFG < 3.9 mmol/L accompanied by symptoms of hypoglycaemia within ±30 min), or Level 1 hypoglycaemia or worse (IFG < 3.9 mmol/L) at least 4.0% of the time recorded.

This was a double‐blinded, crossover clinical trial, in which participants were treated for 4 weeks with acarbose 50 mg thrice daily (before breakfast, lunch and dinner), canagliflozin 300 mg twice daily (before breakfast and dinner; placebo before lunch) or placebo thrice daily. All tablets looked and tasted alike and were ingested approximately 10 min before meals. Each participant completed all three treatments in random order, separated by at least 1 week of washout. During treatment, participants had weekly phone calls inquiring about hypoglycaemia symptoms, adverse events (AEs) and self‐reported compliance. During the last 2 weeks of each treatment intervention, the participants wore a blinded CGM for 10 days. In the last week of each intervention, participants underwent a bioelectrical impedance analysis (SECA mBCA 515) and a solid mixed meal tolerance test.

In this paper, we present the clinical findings of the trial, while the pathophysiological and mechanistic outcomes will be reported separately.

Outcomes and Sample Size

The primary outcome was time below range (below 3.9 mM/70 mg/dL) during CGM evaluation, comparing canagliflozin (C) and acarbose (A) against placebo (P).

Other pre‐specified clinical outcomes included time with hypoglycaemia Level 2 (< 3.0 mmol/L/< 54 mg/dL) (key secondary outcome), time in range (TIR) (3.9–7.8 mmol/L/70–140 mg/dL), side‐effects and participant compliance with the treatment interventions (measured through tablets accountability), among others [14].

Patient reported outcomes included symptoms of hypoglycaemia and AEs, both of which were either spontaneously reported by the participants in the diaries or revealed during the scheduled phone visits. These were later classified according to the Edinburgh Hypoglycaemia Scale [15].

Additionally, quality of life was assessed on the day of the meal test using SF‐36 Health Survey (RAND 36‐Item Short Form Health Survey) [16]. A Danish version was downloaded in January 2023 from the website www.spoergskemaer.dk and has previously been formally validated [17, 18, 19]. Physical and Mental Component Summary measures (PCS and MCS) were calculated against a reference population, as previously established [20].

We aimed at recruiting 16 participants to account for potential participant dropout and enable explorative analysis, as the formal power calculation based on previous CGM data yielded a required sample size of six participants for our primary outcomes and 10 participants for our key secondary outcome as explained in the protocol paper [14].

We pre‐defined satisfactory compliance as adherence exceeding 80% [14].

Calculations

We performed CGM data analysis manually, using a previously described method [21]. In brief, data from the first 24 h were excluded to account for sensor inaccuracy [22]. We calculated capture rate and metrics targeting TIR (defined as 3.9–7.8 mmol/L/70–140 mg/dL) and central tendency measures (percentiles). Additionally, we transformed and normalised glucose values to estimate the risk of hypo‐ and hyperglycaemia (low/high blood glucose index [adjusted for people without diabetes], LBGIFGMGT and HBGIFGMGT, respectively). Additionally, the transformation of glucose values also enabled detecting the risk of significant daily glucose shifts in either direction (average daily risk ratio [adjusted], ADRRFGMGT). To target the magnitude of glycaemic variability and glycaemic profile stability, we calculated indices that estimate the rate of interstitial fluid glucose (IFG) fluctuation every 5 min (two consecutive records), every hour and every 24 h. These are the mean absolute glucose change (MAG change), the continuous overlapping net glycaemic action (CONGA1) and the mean of daily differences (MODD), respectively.

Nighttime was defined as CGM records from 1 AM to 7 AM. We excluded one participant from day versus night analysis because the participant worked night shifts and had distinct eating patterns.

Statistical Methods

Data are presented as mean [95% CI] for normally distributed variables; geometric mean [95% CI of geometric mean] for ln transformed variables; and median (interquartile range) for non‐normally distributed variables. All statistical tests were 2‐sided, and we considered p values below 0.05 significant. All statistical tests took advantage of the crossover design by using paired/matched comparisons.

As pre‐specified in our published statistical analysis plan [14], we analysed the primary outcome using the delta‐values (A‐P and C‐P) and a one‐sample t‐test with adjustment for multiple comparison (p value significant below 0.025).

We analysed other continuous variables using mixed‐effects analysis (LME), with REML (Restricted Maximum Likelihood) analysis and Geisser–Greenhouse correction (fixed effect: treatment [screening, placebo, acarbose, canagliflozin]; random: participants), if normally distributed or normally distributed after ln transformation. If significant, post hoc versus placebo comparisons were performed using Fisher's LSD t‐test. Non‐normally distributed variables were analysed with Friedman test, followed by post hoc uncorrected Dunn's test. In either case, multiple comparisons were interpreted without correction for multiplicity.

For categorical variables, we used Fisher's exact tests.

Primary and Secondary Data Analysis

The manuscript refers to an analysis where all randomised participants that initiated all three treatment interventions were included. A secondary analysis including only per‐protocol completers, that is, treatment periods with over 80% compliance with the treatment regime and without major protocol deviations, is presented whenever relevant.

Results

Participant Flow

From November 2022 to November 2023, we screened 23 patients with a clinical diagnosis of PBH, of which 10 did not qualify for the study (Figure S1). Enrolment was terminated prematurely due to challenges in recruitment.

Two participants dropped out early: one during the first treatment intervention due to a busy work agenda; the other during the first washout period for personal reasons. The remaining 11 participants completed all three interventions and were included in the primary analysis (Figure S1).

We conducted a secondary analysis after excluding active intervention periods with major protocol deviations: the acarbose intervention period was excluded in one participant due to AEs leading to discontinuation of treatment during the third week (i.e., missing on‐treatment CGM data), and in another case because the CGM was accidentally unblinded. Finally, we excluded the canagliflozin intervention period in one participant that experienced a significant life event which was accompanied by a dramatic shift in eating patterns. Thus, the number of participants included in the secondary data analysis was 11 for placebo, 9 for acarbose and 10 for canagliflozin.

Demographics and Anthropometrics

Participants included 10 women and one man who were 52.0 [45.1–59.0] years old at enrolment. Time since gastric bypass surgery was 7.7 [5.0–10.4] years. All participants were glucose tolerant (HbA1c 34.0 (31.0–38.5) mmol/mol, max 39 mmol/mol; HbA1c 5.3 (5.0–5.7)%, max 5.7%) and had normal haemoglobin concentrations (8.5 (8.1–8.8) mmol/L, minimum 7.5 mmol/L). Nine of the 11 participants (82%) had previously experienced Level 3 hypoglycaemic events (this is, hypoglycaemic events leading to altered mental or compromised physical functioning requiring assistance from others).

Treatment with canagliflozin resulted in modest weight loss, as compared to placebo. However, body weight and body composition were unaltered during treatment with acarbose (Table 1).

Safety, Tolerability and Patient‐Reported Outcomes

The rate of any AE was approximately double during treatments with acarbose and canagliflozin, as compared with placebo, and was mainly driven by an increase in gastrointestinal AEs (Table 2). One participant had a Level 3 hypoglycaemic event during a washout period. No serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported during any of the treatment interventions; specifically, no ketoacidosis or major infections were reported.

The self‐report of symptoms of hypoglycaemia by the end of the first and second week of treatment was more prevalent during the placebo intervention compared with the two pharmacological interventions, whereas AE‐related symptoms appear to be most prevalent during treatment with acarbose (Figure 1).

Self‐reported symptoms during weeks 1 and 2 of each treatment intervention.

Biochemically, by the third week of each treatment intervention, treatment with canagliflozin resulted in slightly higher haemoglobin and creatinine, as compared to placebo. Across all treatment periods, the maximum fasting level of beta‐hydroxybutyrate was 1.02 mmol/L (Table 1).

Health‐related quality of life was not changed by the pharmacological interventions, but physical health was numerically better after the active treatments, especially for canagliflozin where PCS was 4.3 points [0.5–8.2] higher than after placebo (mean [95% CI]) (Table 1).

At the last visit, we asked participants which treatment they preferred (first, second or third). While one participant reported that all three treatment interventions were equally unsatisfactory, 4 (40%) preferred canagliflozin, 4 (40%) placebo and 1 (10%) acarbose (N = 10; information for one participant is missing).

Continuous Glucose Monitoring

During CGM monitoring, time < 3.9 mmol/L (primary outcome) and time < 3.0 mmol/L (key secondary outcome) were low and did not differ significantly across the three treatment interventions (Table 3 and Figure 2). TIR was improved by both acarbose and canagliflozin, as compared to placebo, while time above range (> 7.8 mmol/L) decreased significantly only for canagliflozin (Table 3). In the secondary analysis, however, time above range decreased during both acarbose (12.1 [9.6–15.2]%; p = 0.0360) and canagliflozin (10.7 [7.5–15.3]%; p = 0.0013) treatments versus placebo (15.5 [12.1–20.0]%).

The number of self‐reported events with symptoms of hypoglycaemia during CGM was similar during the three interventions, but markedly lower than the number of events reported during screening (p = 0.0153). In contrast, the number of symptomatic events matching CGM documented hypoglycaemia and the number of participants experiencing such events were unchanged by placebo compared with screening, but were numerically reduced by acarbose (events p = 0.2380; participants p > 0.9999) and more than halved by canagliflozin (events p = 0.0334; participants p = 0.0210), as compared to placebo (Table 3).

Most symptomatic events were reported during daytime (Table S1), with no symptomatic hypoglycaemic events recorded during nighttime (Table S2).

Treatment with canagliflozin significantly reduced the magnitude of postprandial glucose excursions, with a lower 90th percentile (P90) and lower risk of hyperglycaemia (HBGIFGMGT). The same tendency was documented for the treatment with acarbose, despite not reaching statistical significance (P90: p = 0.2498; HBGIFGMGT: p = 0.2602; Table 3).

Metrics describing glycaemic variability were significantly reduced by both acarbose and canagliflozin. In detail, they reduced the rate of glycaemic fluctuations (MAG change and CONGA, 5‐min and 1‐h glycaemic variability indexes), increased consistency of the daily glycaemic profiles across the monitored period (24‐h variability index, MODD) and reduced the overall coefficient of variation (Table 3). These features were most significant during daytime (Table S1).

The duration of CGM monitoring was 8.91 ± 0.03 days and the capture rate of 99.22% ± 1.39% (mean ± SD). There were no differences across the study periods.

Discussion

We performed a randomised, crossover, double‐blinded clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of acarbose and canagliflozin, compared with placebo, for the treatment of patients with an established clinical diagnosis of PBH following RYGB surgery. We report that neither pharmacological intervention significantly changed the time in CGM‐documented hypoglycaemia, as compared with placebo (primary outcome), but they did reduce the number of symptomatic hypoglycaemic events and symptoms of hypoglycaemia, particularly canagliflozin, and both were tolerated and safe.

The participants had an established clinical diagnosis of PBH and were followed in our clinic for PBH management that includes dietary counselling. The majority had a documented history with Level 3 hypoglycaemic events, and all had CGM documented hypoglycaemia during the screening period, validating the diagnosis prior to enrolment in the trial. All had undergone gastric bypass more than 2 years earlier, and most were females, which is consistent with PBH populations described in the literature [23, 24, 25].

Treatment with acarbose and canagliflozin failed to reduce time below range when compared to placebo, which was our primary endpoint. This is consistent with previous reports from a non‐placebo‐controlled one‐week intervention with acarbose [4] and from a RCT with 20‐days intervention with 25 mg empagliflozin once daily [7], both of which also relied on CGM for hypoglycaemia documentation.

However, the failure to reduce time below range in our study should be interpreted with caution and does not necessarily mean that the treatments are ineffective for the treatment of PBH. Time below range was much lower during placebo than expected [9] and lower than in previous studies [6, 7], perhaps partly driven by a substantial study effect (placebo effect), as supported by the higher percentage of time below range and rate of self‐reported hypoglycaemic symptoms during the screening period compared to placebo. Although we did not provide dietary counselling, it is likely that participants modified their diet or behaviour after entering the trial. Taken together, this strong placebo effect resulting in a low percentage of time in hypoglycaemia on placebo (~1%) may have led to a floor effect, that is, it might have limited the sensitivity of the CGM‐based primary endpoint to detect further reductions with active treatments. It is, therefore, worth noticing the improvements in secondary outcomes as discussed below, while keeping in mind that these are hypothesis‐generating findings that require further investigation and validation in adequately powered studies. During CGM, both acarbose and canagliflozin increased TIR whereas only canagliflozin decreased time above range. However, in our secondary analysis, both treatments decreased time above range. These findings are consistent with previous studies, where both acarbose and canagliflozin attenuated glycaemic excursions following a meal test and raised the postprandial glycaemic nadir [4, 11, 13]. Acarbose also increased TIR and decreased time above range during a non‐placebo‐controlled one‐week intervention [4]. While no previous chronic intervention with canagliflozin has been conducted in the PBH population, another inhibitor of the sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT2i), empagliflozin, has been studied and was also shown to reduce time above range [7].

Both acarbose and canagliflozin reduced glycaemic variability and increased glycaemic profile stability across the CGM monitored periods, with these effects being more pronounced during daytime in connection with food intake. While increased glycaemic variability is a hallmark of post‐bariatric surgery glucose profiles [26], it has not yet been directly linked with PBH [21, 27]. The reduction in glycaemic variability with acarbose and canagliflozin might explain the improvement in symptoms, despite the lack of documented effect on the rate of hypoglycaemia. The latter has been suggested in an observational cohort study linking CGM and symptom profiles of people with PBH [28].

Participants were encouraged to report all symptoms throughout the trial, both during weekly appointments and via a symptom diary completed during CGM monitoring. It seems that a therapeutic intervention even without an active pharmacological component (i.e., placebo) can significantly reduce the number of events with symptoms of hypoglycaemia (improved by about 50% in placebo) but importantly without changing the number of symptomatic events with documented hypoglycaemia. This seems to confirm the complex nature of PBH and the notion that symptoms of hypoglycaemia in patients with PBH may be related to other factors including preceding hyperglycaemia, the rate of changes in plasma glucose, certain drug‐related adverse effects, such as abdominal pain with acarbose and diarrhoea with both [9, 29]; early dumping syndrome, abdominal pain syndromes [4, 30, 31], and the clinical observation that susceptibility for symptoms may also vary over time and between individuals.

Acarbose and canagliflozin both decreased the self‐reporting of symptoms of hypoglycaemia (vs. placebo), as classified by the Edinburgh Hypoglycaemia Scale [15]. The reduction of hypoglycaemia symptoms matched a reduction in the rate of hypoglycaemic events during CGM, particularly for canagliflozin. This is consistent with the literature reports favouring the use of acarbose to ameliorate PBH [3, 9], while there is not enough literature available to make such inferences for canagliflozin.

Both treatments were safe during the 4‐week treatment interventions, with no severe AEs reported. Additionally, the study retention rate and compliance with the treatment interventions were acceptable. Only two participants dropped out early due to external factors not related to the trial despite the demanding protocol, with weekly appointments (in person or over the phone) for approximately 4 months.

AEs were twice as common with acarbose and canagliflozin compared to placebo. Flatulence and diarrhoea were particularly common during treatment with acarbose, leading to treatment interruption by one participant. The latter is consistent with previous reports and our clinical experience, where gastrointestinal side effects are appointed as the primary reason for discontinuation of acarbose treatment for PBH [3, 9]. Treatment with canagliflozin resulted in a mild increase in creatinine, without detectable changes in body water percentage but with a lower body weight. The acute increase in creatinine with canagliflozin has been described in patients with and without chronic kidney disease and is attributed to a transient acute decrease in the glomerular filtration rate due to hemodynamic shifts in the kidney that does not reflect acute kidney injury [32, 33]. Canagliflozin also caused a slight elevation in haemoglobin, which has also been widely described previously [33, 34, 35, 36] and is attributable to hemoconcentration acutely but also to increased haematopoiesis due to enhanced production of erythropoietin [37]. Moreover, when enquired on preferred treatment, study participants favoured equally treatments with canagliflozin or placebo over acarbose. This may suggest that side effects outweigh the objective improvements in hypoglycaemic events, especially for treatment with acarbose. Likewise, no statistically significant improvement in self‐reported health‐related quality of life was observed for any of the treatments, although the non‐significant improvement in physical quality of life, particularly with canagliflozin, suggested an effect size that may be clinically relevant had the sample size been just slightly larger [38]. Furthermore, SF‐36 is a generic questionnaire and may lack sensitivity to detect changes specific to PBH‐related quality of life.

The reduction in symptomatic hypoglycaemic events with canagliflozin and, less evidently, with acarbose may translate to fewer episodes requiring rescue carbohydrates, reduced anxiety about hypoglycaemia and improved ability to engage in daily activities without fear of severe hypoglycaemic episodes. However, the lack of improvement in quality of life scores suggests that these benefits may not be sufficient to improve patients' overall well‐being in the short term substantially.

Acarbose has been widely used as first‐line pharmacological treatment for PBH, despite the lack of prior clinical trials supporting this approach until now [3]. Empiric dosing of acarbose in PBH varies [3] and ranges from 50 to 300 mg daily distributed in 50 mg doses [4, 9]. In this study, we chose to use a dose of 150 mg daily divided in three doses prior to the main meals of the day. This regimen has been proven safe in other patient populations [39] and maximises the alpha‐glucosidase inhibitory effect in the postprandial periods when it is most clinically relevant.

The dose of canagliflozin used was higher than the one routinely used for treatment of T2D (100 or 300 mg daily). We chose to use 300 mg twice daily and before the main meals of the day to maximise the SGLT1‐inhibitory activity [10]. This dosage has been used in people with T2D and was found to be safe and not increase the risk of hypoglycaemia [40, 41]. Our group has also successfully used a single 600 mg dose of canagliflozin in the post‐bariatric population [11]. While SGLT2 inhibitors carry a risk of euglycaemic ketoacidosis, this potentially life‐threatening AE is very rare in patients with T2D [42] and the prevalence is unknown in patients without diabetes [43]. Given the very low a priori risk, the short intervention period, the close follow‐up, and together with concerns about participant burden, we did not instruct our participants to routinely measure blood ketones. Instead, all participants were instructed to contact the trial team with any suspected adverse effects including common symptoms of ketoacidosis such as malaise, abdominal pain and vomiting. No events of ketoacidosis were documented during the study and fasting beta‐hydroxybutyrate levels in the third week of each treatment were only slightly elevated and well below common thresholds for ketoacidosis (< 3.0 mmol/L) [44]. Nevertheless, in clinical practice when patients with PBH are treated for longer periods with canagliflozin or other SGLT2 inhibitors, it is important to inform about the theoretical risk of ketoacidosis, relevant signs and symptoms that should prompt contact to a doctor, and situations such as prolonged fasting or infections where it is recommended to pause treatment.

Our trial has limitations. Firstly, we acknowledge that the trial was powered for the primary and key secondary outcomes that both failed to reach statistical significance and, as such, other outcomes should be interpreted with caution and not as definitive evidence of clinical benefit. The failure to reach statistical significance for our key outcomes could, however, also be due to a type II error; our sample size calculation was based on the very limited evidence available at the time the study was designed [14], but the estimates are far from actual results, hence questioning its validity. Correspondingly, time below range during placebo treatment was much lower than anticipated [14] and lower than in other previous trials [6], perhaps in part driven by a marked study participation effect (> 50% reduction from screening to placebo). Additionally, we experienced a slow recruitment rate (< 2 participants/month) and a high rate of screening failures (43%), which resulted in a lower sample size than originally scheduled. Secondly, relying solely on CGM for hypoglycaemia documentation is not ideal, especially as the equipment used reportedly misses 35.1%–35.8% events of Level 2 and 14.0%–15.0% of Level 1 hypoglycaemia [22, 45] and overestimates glucose concentrations at the hypoglycaemic range when compared to the gold standard methods (YSI analyzer using arterialized blood) [46]. Yet, we chose this approach as CGM is the only tool available for representative glycaemic profiling over a relatively short period of time (10 days). Because Dexcom G6 does not require capillary glucose calibrations and because it was used in blinded mode, we were able to assess which reported symptoms truly corresponded to low glucose, minimise trial burden and enhance participant retention. Furthermore, the risk of underestimating hypoglycaemia by using CGM was the same throughout the trial, as the same CGM device was used for screening and for assessing treatment effects during all treatment interventions. Thirdly, we chose a crossover design to allow for a smaller sample size and serial testing, but we acknowledge it can introduce study fatigue and the risk of carryover effect. We tried to mitigate that through block randomisation of the sequence of the interventions and to include more patients than indicated by the power calculation to allow for some dropouts. Fourthly, we only included patients with a well‐documented PBH diagnosis after RYGB followed at our tertiary bariatric centre, thus one cannot be certain that our findings apply to PBH management in other settings where access to specialised care, including dietary counselling, might be more limited, or after other surgical interventions, such as sleeve gastrectomy, or in patients diagnosed shorter after surgery or who experience hypoglycaemic events less frequently.

In conclusion, the HypoBar I placebo‐controlled clinical trial tested the efficacy and safety of acarbose and canagliflozin for treatment of patients with a clinical diagnosis of PBH and did not demonstrate a reduction in time below range (the primary outcome) with either acarbose or canagliflozin compared to placebo. Exploratory analyses of secondary outcomes, however, revealed improvements in TIR, glycaemic variability and symptomatic hypoglycaemia, particularly with canagliflozin, warranting further investigation in larger studies. Acarbose was associated with mild but common adverse effects that led to treatment discontinuation in one of 11 participants, whereas canagliflozin seemed to be better tolerated. Thus, treatment with acarbose or canagliflozin in patients with PBH may offer clinically relevant benefits, although an individual approach to management is warranted.
