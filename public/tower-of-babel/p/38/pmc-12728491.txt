Unfolded protein response kinase PERK supports survival and metastasis of circulating tumor cell clusters via SAM synthesis and H3K4me3‐dependent PDGFB signaling
Authors: Rui Tang, Yan Sun, Ao Deng, Jiahe Liu, Peijin Dai, Jing Chen, Chaoqun Deng, Hui Liu, Yuhang Hai, Yanran Tong, Yan‐e Du, Manran Liu, Haojun Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetastasis is the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality, with circulating tumor cell (CTC) clusters serving as highly efficient precursors of distant metastasis. Survival of CTC clusters in the bloodstream is the primary contributor to tumor metastasis. However, the underlying mechanisms of how CTC clusters respond to the blood environment and drive metastasis remain elusive. This study aimed to elucidate the potential mechanisms that enable CTC clusters to adapt and survive in the bloodstream.MethodsCTC clusters were detected using a microfluidic system in cancer patients, as well as in patient‐derived xenograft (PDX), cell line‐derived xenograft, and syngeneic models. The key molecules responsible for the adaptive survival of CTC clusters were characterized using RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq), gene interference, and flow cytometry. To investigate the underlying mechanisms of adaptive survival, RNA‐seq, targeted metabolomics, isotope tracing experiments, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) sequencing, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining were employed. The therapeutic potential of survival pathway inhibitor combined with chemotherapy drug was evaluated in patient‐derived CTCs and the PDX model.ResultsCTC clusters exhibited superior survival and metastatic capacity compared to single CTCs and were associated with adverse clinical outcomes. The unfolded protein response mediator protein kinase R‐like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) was activated in CTC clusters and maintained S‐adenosylmethionine (SAM) availability, facilitating their adaptive survival in the bloodstream. Mechanistically, PERK mediated the upregulation of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), which enhanced methionine adenosyltransferase 2A (MAT2A) expression, contributing to SAM synthesis. Increased SAM enhanced H3K4me3 modification of the platelet‐derived growth factor B (PDGFB) promoter, leading to elevated PDGFB secretion and its accumulation in the intercellular region within CTC clusters. PDGFB functioned as a shared survival signal, triggering the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) pathway via platelet‐derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRβ), supporting CTC cluster survival in the bloodstream. Inhibition of PERK and PDGFRβ profoundly impaired the survival signaling and suppressed the metastatic dissemination of CTC clusters.ConclusionsOur findings revealed a PERK/MAT2A/PDGFB axis that confers adaptive survival capabilities to CTC clusters in the bloodstream. Targeting this survival signaling pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy for metastatic cancer.

BACKGROUND

Tumor metastasis is the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality, and remains a major challenge in improving the outcomes of cancer patients [1]. Dissemination and survival of tumor cells in the bloodstream are crucial steps in the metastatic cascade [2, 3]. Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) serve as seeds for metastasis and play a pivotal role in the dissemination of primary tumors to distant organs. Although their presence in the bloodstream is strongly correlated with a poor prognosis and advanced disease stages [4], the molecular characteristics of CTCs remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, elucidating the intrinsic properties of CTCs that drive metastasis remains a significant challenge in cancer research.

The survival of CTCs in the bloodstream is essential for metastasis. Once shed from the primary tumor, CTCs enter into the bloodstream, where they face a hostile environment characterized by physical stress, immune surveillance, and the lack of a supportive niche [3]. Notably, fewer than 0.02% of CTCs successfully form metastases, indicating the heterogeneity within this population [3, 5, 6, 7]. CTC clusters, aggregated by two or more tumor cells, exhibit superior metastatic potential compared to single CTCs, despite their rarity in the bloodstream [8, 9, 10, 11]. For example, colorectal cancer metastasis to the liver and lymph nodes is predominantly driven by CTC clusters rather than by single CTCs [12]. Although these findings highlight the enhanced metastatic potential of CTC clusters, their adaptive strategies for collectively resisting external pressures remain unexplored. Cell‐cell communication can drive collective changes in tumor cells, influencing their biological behaviors [13]. Emerging evidence suggests that tumor cell cluster exhibit distinctive characteristics such as metabolic reprogramming and radiotherapy resistance [14, 15]. Based on these insights, we speculated that CTC clusters employ specific adaptive survival strategies, potentially driven by metabolic pathways and intercellular communication, to collectively overcome external stresses in the bloodstream.

Here, we aimed to identify the key factors that enable CTC clusters to adaptively survive in the bloodstream and enhance their metastatic efficiency in distant organs, and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms that promote their adaptive survival. Uncovering these mechanisms may reveal novel therapeutic targets for preventing metastatic progression and improving patient outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Tumor patients

The blood samples and corresponding primary tumor tissues were acquired from patients treated at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China). The patients were diagnosed with breast cancer, melanoma, colon cancer or lung adenocarcinoma at the first diagnosis before surgery between January 2019 and March 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) no prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, or other antitumor therapies before surgery; (2) histopathological confirmation of breast cancer, melanoma, colon cancer, or lung adenocarcinoma by two independent pathologists; and (3) signed informed consent for participation in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of any other malignancy or concurrent systemic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases; (2) receipt of preoperative treatments, such as neoadjuvant chemotherapy or immunotherapy; and (3) refusal by patients to permit the use of clinical specimens for scientific investigation. Blood samples were also acquired from healthy donors who met the following eligibility criteria: free from cancer, systemic inflammatory diseases, or chronic illnesses. The clinical characteristics of patients selected for CTC detection are listed in Supplementary Tables S1‐S4, while those selected for protein kinase R‐like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) expression analysis are provided in Supplementary Tables S5‐S6. The American Joint Committee on Cancer's 8th edition of the tumor‐node‐metastasis (TNM) classification system was used for cancer staging. All samples were conducted with informed consent and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chongqing Medical University (license number: 2022118).

Cell culture

The human breast cancer cell line MDA‐MB‐231, human non‐small cell lung cancer cell line H1299, murine colorectal carcinoma cell line CT26, and murine melanoma cell line B16F10 were purchased commercially from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). All cell lines employed in this study were verified using short tandem repeat profiling before the start of the experiments. The MDA‐MB‐231 and B16F10 cell lines were transduced with lentiviral vector encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP)‐firefly luciferase or firefly luciferase, respectively, for bioluminescence imaging, followed by selection with puromycin (1 µg/mL, #540222, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 14 days. The MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 cells are the lung‐preferential metastatic derivatives obtained from two screens in NOD/ShiLtJGpt‐Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt (NCG) mice (GemPharmatech, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), protocol as previously described [16]. The MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were isolated from the peripheral blood of NCG‐MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 mice.

MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 and H1299 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, CellBox, Changsha, Hunan, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (C0222, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). CT26 and B16F10 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were grown in DMEM/F12 (#11320033, Gibco) medium supplemented with 1× B27 (#17504044, Gibco), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF, Peprotech, Grand Island, NY, USA), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, Peprotech) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cell lines were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C.

Mouse experiments

All animal studies were received approval from the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committees of Chongqing Medical University (license number: 2022118) and performed following the guidelines in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. NCG mice were sourced from GemPharmatech. BALB/c and C57BL/6 were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of the Chongqing Medical University. Female mice from 6 to 8 weeks of age were used for these studies. Mice were housed in a pathogen‐free conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of Chongqing Medical University.

For cell line‐derived xenograft model (CDX) and syngeneic model construction, primary breast cancer tumors were established in NCG mice by injecting 1 × 106 MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 cells into the mammary fat pad. Additionally, 1 × 106 H1299 were injected subcutaneously into the right flanks of NCG mice. For B16F10 and CT26 models, 1 × 106 cells were implanted subcutaneously into C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice, respectively. Blood collection for CTCs analysis and organ dissection were performed at 4‐5 weeks post‐implantation for NCG‐MDA‐MB‐231/LM2, NCG‐H1299, and BALB/c‐CT26 mice, and at 3 weeks post‐implantation for C57BL/6‐B16F10 mice. To collect blood from tumor intravasating vessels in mice, the tumor site was surgically exposed under aseptic conditions. The largest tumor‐associated vessel was identified, and drainage fluid was gently collected using a syringe. For cardiac blood collection, mice were anesthetized and placed in a supine position. Using sterile surgical instruments, the thoracic cavity was opened to expose the heart. A 25G needle attached to a 1 mL syringe was then gently inserted into the heart to aspirate blood. All collected blood samples were transferred into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid‐coated tubes.

To investigate the metastatic potential of single CTCs and CTC clusters, we intravenously injected 2 × 105 MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC (single or clustered) or 5 × 105 B16F10 cells (single or clustered) into mice via the tail vein. To investigate the effect of CTC cluster dissociation on tumor metastasis, 2 × 105 single cells, singles dissociated from clusters, and clustered cells (MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC) were injected into mice via the tail vein. To evaluate the effects of drug‐induced cluster dissociation, 1 × 106 MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 cells were implanted into the fat pads of NCG mice. One week later, mice were randomly assigned to vehicle, digitoxin (0.5 mg/kg, HY‐B1357, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), or ouabain (0.86 mg/kg, HY‐B0542, MedChemExpress) groups, receiving treatment every other day from week 1 to week 4. To explore the impact of endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) on tumor metastasis, vehicle‐ or azoramide (10 µmol/L, SML1560, Sigma Aldrich)‐treated MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were injected into mice via the tail vein. To examine the impact of PERK deletion on tumor metastasis, PERK knockout (PERK‐KO) and scramble controls cells (MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10) were implanted orthotopically or injected into mice via the tail vein. To assess methionine metabolism on tumor metastasis, methionine adenosyltransferase 2A (MAT2A) knockdown or methionine‐starved MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were injected into mice via the tail vein. To investigate the role of platelet‐derived growth factor B (PDGFB) in tumor metastasis, PDGFB knockdown and control MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were injected into mice via the tail vein.

To evaluate the effects of PERK and platelet‐derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRβ) on metastasis, patient‐derived xenograft model (PDX) model was established by implanting tumor tissues into NCG mice. Four weeks post‐implantation, mice were randomly divided into four groups: vehicle, AMG44 (PERK inhibitor, 12.5 mg/kg, HY‐12661A, MedChemExpress), imatinib (25 mg/kg, HY‐15463, MedChemExpress), and AMG44 combined with imatinib. Treatments were administered every other day from week 4 to week 8.

Metastatic burden was assessed using bioluminescence imaging of luciferase‐expressing cancer cells. D‐luciferin (150 mg/kg, HY‐12591B, MedChemExpress) was administered via intraperitoneal injection, and images were acquired using the in vivo imaging system (LB983, Berthold Technologies GmbH & Co. KG, Bad Wildbad, Baden‐Württemberg, Germany). Data were analyzed using IndiGo software (Berthold Technologies).

Humane endpoints were defined as the observation of body weight loss (<70% of control littermates), difficulty in movement, or signs of distress. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation at the end of the experiment or when they reached any of the predetermined humane endpoints.

Establishment of PDX model

For the implantation of fresh specimens, human breast tumor samples were obtained from female patients with primary breast cancer undergoing surgical resection at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. The patients were 66, 70, and 52 years old, with TNM stages III, IV, and III, respectively. The samples were received within 1 h after surgery. Tumors were minced into small fragments and suspended in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 50% Matrigel (#354234, Corning, Corning, NY, USA). The tumor tissue suspension was then implanted into the mammary fat pads of NCG mice using a syringe fitted with a 21G needle. Tumor growth was monitored weekly, and tumor volume was measured using the formula: Volume = (length × width)2/2. When primary xenograft tumors reached a volume of approximately 500‐1,000 mm3, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, following institutional guidelines for ethical care. Tumor fragments were subsequently re‐implanted into new recipient mice to expand the xenograft model. CTCs and tumor tissue were collected at each passage. The collected tissues were fixed in formalin for histological analysis.

Fabrication of microfluidic chip

Microfluidic chips were constructed utilizing photolithography and soft lithography techniques [17]. Microchannel designs were created using AutoCAD software (Autodesk Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA) and transferred to a photomask. The silicon wafer was coated with SU‐8 3035 photoresist, exposed to ultraviolet light through the photomask, then developed to remove unexposed areas, followed by post‐baking to complete the mold. The mold was then used as a template for polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, #761036, Sigma Aldrich) soft lithography. A PDMS mixture, prepared at a 10:1 ratio of base to curing agent, was poured onto the mold and subjected to curing at 75°C for 60 min. Following the curing process, the PDMS was carefully peeled from the mold, and access ports were created by punching to form inlets and outlets. The PDMS layer was adhered to a cleaned glass slide through oxygen plasma treatment. To minimize non‐specific binding, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution was injected into the microchannels, and the chip was incubated at 4°C overnight. The completed microfluidic chips were inspected for defects and used in further experiments.

CTC capture

Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients before surgery or tumor‐bearing mice into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid‐coated tubes. Single CTCs and CTC clusters were isolated using microfluidic chip, and then red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using RBC Lysis Buffer (BL503B, Biosharp, Hefei, Anhui, China). The remaining cell suspension was then subjected to magnetic cell sorting to remove white blood cells (WBCs) using the EasySep™ Release Human CD45 Positive Selection Kit (#100‐0105, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada,) or the EasySep™ Mouse CD45 Positive Selection Kit (#18945, STEMCELL Technologies) for mouse samples, following the respective protocols. The separated single CTCs and CTC clusters counted and for further experiments.

Expansion of patient‐derived CTCs and drug treatment

Human CTCs obtained from a 68 years old female breast cancer patient diagnosed with stage III invasive ductal carcinoma. The patient had not yet undergone surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy at the time of peripheral blood collection. Blood sample was collected with informed consent and approval by the Institutional Review Board of Chongqing Medical University (license number: 2022118). We expanded CTCs isolated from patient peripheral blood using the method described in [18], with modifications to optimize the culture conditions. Briefly, isolated CTCs were embedded in Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel (#356231, Corning), and cultured in advanced DMEM/F12 medium (#12634010, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1× B27, 50 ng/mL EGF, 20 ng/mL bFGF, 500 ng/mL R‐spondin1 (#120‐38, Peprotech), 10 µmol/L Y‐27632 (HY‐10071, MedChemExpress), 10 µmol/L nicotinamide (N0636, Sigma Aldrich), 1 mmol/L acetylcysteine (HY‐B0215, MedChemExpress), 500 nmol/L A83‐01 (HY‐10432, MedChemExpress) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Once cluster formation was observed, the cells were harvested, dissociated with accutase (A1110501, Gibco), and passaged. The culture medium was refreshed every 2‐3 days.

To investigate the effects of AMG44 and imatinib on cell viability, expanded human CTC cells were treated with of AMG44 (1 µmol/L) or imatinib (1 µmol/L) for 6 h, followed by Cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay.

Knockdown and overexpression strategies

Transfection of small interfering RNA (siRNA) was carried out by following the manufacturer's instructions (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Briefly, cells at 50% confluency were transfected with siNC, siPERK, siRNA targeting inositol‐requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α), or siRNA targeting specificity protein 1 (SP1) in the presence of Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Fresh medium was replaced 6 h after transfection. For stable knockdown activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) or MAT2A, lentiviruses carrying short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting ATF4 or MAT2A, along with control shRNAs, were designed and synthesized by GenePharma. Cells were infected with these lentiviruses along with 5 µg/mL Polybrene (C0351, Beyotime) for 24 h. After transduction, cells were subjected to puromycin selection for two weeks to establish stable knockdown lines. Sequences of all siRNAs and shRNAs are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

For overexpression analysis, lentiviral vectors for PERK overexpression and control were synthesized by GenePharma. Packaged lentiviruses were used to infect tumor cells in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Infected cells were then selected with puromycin for two weeks to establish stable overexpression cell lines. Plasmid vectors for MAT2A and PDGFB overexpression were purchased from Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout of PERK

PERK‐KO cell lines were generated in MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10 cells using CRISPR/Cas9. Cells were transfected with plasmids encoding spCas9, two single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting distinct sites within the PERK gene, and a puromycin resistance cassette. Following transfection, cells underwent puromycin selection to enrich for successfully transfected populations. Surviving cells were then single‐cell sorted into 96‐well plates at a density of 1 cell/well using flow cytometry. Single‐cell clones stably expressing the sgRNAs were expanded and validated by immunoblotting and DNA sequencing. The sequences of all sgRNAs are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Suspension culture

MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10 cells were cultured in suspension using ultra‐low attachment plates (#3261, Corning) with DMEM/F12 medium containing 1× B27, 20 ng/mL EGF, 20 ng/mL bFGF, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cell clusters were observed and imaged under microscope, and cluster size was measured using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/downloads).

Drug treatment

To assess the cellular resilience to ER stress, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were exposed in thapsigargin (an ERS inducer, 100 nmol/L, HY‐13433, MedChemExpress) and azoramide (an unfolded protein response [UPR] modulators, 10 µmol/L), followed by Annexin V and tetraphenylethene‐maleimide (TPE‐MI) detection. To investigate the effect of PERK inhibition on cell survival under ERS conditions, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10 cells were pretreated with AMG44 (1 µmol/L), followed by thapsigargin (100 nmol/L) exposure and Annexin V detection. To investigate the effects of PERK activation or inhibition on cell survival, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were treated with MK‐28 (PERK activator, 15 µmol/L; HY‐137207, MedChemExpress) or AMG44 (1 µmol/L), followed by the assessment of Annexin V staining, cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and transendothelial migration. To investigate the role of apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy or poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition in thapsigargin‐induced cell death, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were pretreated with the following inhibitors: ZVAD‐FMK (ZVAD, a pan‐caspase inhibitor, 10 µmol/L, S7023, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA), necrostatin‐1 (Ncro, a necroptosis inhibitor, 2 µmol/L, HY‐15760, MedChemExpress), ferrostatin‐1 (Ferro, a ferroptosis inhibitor, 5 µmol/L, SML0583, Sigma Aldrich), 3‐methyladenine (3‐MA, an autophagy inhibitor, 60 µmol/L, S2767, Selleck Chemicals), and niraparib (PARPi, a PARP inhibitor, 5 µmol/L, S2741, Selleck Chemicals). Cells were then exposed to thapsigargin (100 nmol/L), followed by Annexin V detection. To assess the impact of MAT2A inhibition on S‐adenosylmethionine (SAM) synthesis, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC clusters were treated with PF9366 (a MAT2A inhibitor, 10 µmol/L; HY‐107778, MedChemExpress) for 24 h. To evaluate the effect of imatinib on the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathway, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC, B16F10, and PDX‐1 cells were treated with imatinib (1 µmol/L. HY‐15463, MedChemExpress) for 12 h. Western blotting analysis was then performed to assess changes in PI3K, phosphorylated AKT (p‐AKT), and total AKT (t‐AKT) levels.

Methionine restriction treatment

For cell culture, methionine‐restricted medium (MRM) was prepared from methionine‐deficient DMEM (#21013024, Gibco) containing 10% FBS (Cellbox) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Control medium (CM) was prepared by adding 0.2 mmol/L L‐methionine (M9625, Sigma Aldrich) to the methionine‐deficient DMEM. Tumor cells were initially seeded in standard DMEM overnight and then treated with CM or MRM for 24 h.

Flow cytometry

For annexin V detection analysis, Annexin V‐Elab Fluor 647/propidium iodide (PI) Apoptosis Kit (E‐CK‐A213, Elabscience, Wuhan, Hubei, China) or Annexin V‐allophycocyanin (APC)/4',6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) Apoptosis Kit (E‐CK‐A258, Elabscience) was employed to detect Annexin V+ cells. Briefly, cells were collected and stained with 5 µL Annexin V‐APC or Annexin V‐Elab Fluor 647, along with 5 µL PI or DAPI for 15 min, and then Annexin levels were analyzed using flow cytometry. FlowJo V10 software (FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, OR, USA) was used in data analysis.

For cell cycle detection, cells were fixed in 70% ethanol at 4°C overnight, followed by staining with PI solution containing RNase A. DNA content was measured using flow cytometry to determine cell cycle distribution.

For ER tracker and TPE‐MI detection, cells were stained with ER‐Tracker (E34250, Invitrogen) and TPE‐MI (HY‐143218, MedChemExpress) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence signals were measured using flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For the staining of CTCs, the enriched CTCs were spread onto glass slides and air‐dried before being fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Following fixation, the slides were incubated overnight with primary antibodies Ki67 (1:100, 27309‐1‐AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR, 1:100, ab30, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), pan‐cytokeratin (CK, 1:100, ab7753, Abcam), CD45 (1:100, ab40763, Abcam), E‐cadherin (1:200, ab287970, Abcam), N‐cadherin (1:200, ab18203, Abcam), phosphorylated PERK (pPERK, 1:100, DF7576, Affinity Biosciences, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), PDGFB (1:100, ER1914‐92, HUABIO, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), phosphorylated PDGFRβ (pPDGFRβ, 1:50, ET1611‐29, HUABIO). Then, slides were treated with secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescence, and nuclei were visualized using DAPI staining. Imaging was performed with Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Hessen, Germany).

For terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining, fixed and permeabilized CTCs were labeled using the TUNEL assay kit (E‐CK‐A322, Elabscience) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. CTC cluster was classified as TUNEL‐positive or Ki67‐positive if more than 50% of the cells within the cluster exhibited TUNEL positivity or Ki67 positivity. CTC clusters with 50% or fewer TUNEL‐positive or Ki67‐positivie cells were classified as TUNEL‐negative or Ki67‐negative. The percentage of TUNEL‐positive CTC clusters was then quantified, and patients were categorized into “high” or “low” TUNEL+ CTC cluster groups based on the median percentage.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC staining was conducted on lung tissue sections from NCG‐MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 mice. Briefly, the tissue sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval. After inactivating endogenous peroxidase and blocking non‐specific binding at room temperature, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against pPERK (1:100, ab192591, Abcam). Tissues were incubated with an enhanced peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody, visualized with 3,3'‐diaminobenzidine (DAB, ZLI‐9018, ZSGB‐BIO, Beijing, China). Images were captured using a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). For TUNEL staining, IHC was performed using a TUNEL assay kit (E‐CK‐A331, Elabscience). Lung tissue sections were incubated with the TUNEL reagent following the manufacturer's instructions.

IHC staining was reviewed and scored by two independent pathologists blinded to patients' clinical data to quantify protein expression. Staining intensity was scored as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong), while the proportion of positive cells was categorized as 0 (<5%), 1 (5%‐25%), 2 (26%‐50%), 3 (51%‐75%), and 4 (>75%). The final score was calculated by multiplying the intensity and proportion scores. Consensus between pathologists was reached through discussion when discrepancies occurred.

CCK‐8 assay

To assess the impact of ERS on cell viability, 3 × 103 MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10 cells were treated with 100 nmol/L thapsigargin under suspension for 6 h, followed by the addition of 10 µL of CCK‐8 solution (BS350A, Biosharp) to each well and incubation at 37°C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated using the formula: (A450 sample ‐ A450 blank) / (A450 control ‐ A450 blank) × 100%. For cell proliferation assay, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10 cells were cultured in suspension for 24 h before seeding into 96‐well plates (3 × 103 cells/well), while MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were also seeded at the same density and treated with vehicle or MK‐28 (15 µmol/L). Net absorbance (A450 sample ‐ A450 blank) was measured at the indicated time points and used to plot proliferation curves.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were prepared using radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) containing phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, ST507, Beyotime) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail A (P1082, Beyotime). Protein concentrations were quantified with the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Concentration Measurement Kit (P0010, Beyotime). Protein samples (30 µg) were loaded and separated on 10%‐12% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) gels and then electro‐transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat milk in tris‐buffered saline with tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies specific to the target proteins. After washing, membranes were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (#1705061, Bio‐Rad) and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system (Bio‐Rad). Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/downloads). β‐Actin was utilized as an internal control. RNA isolation and quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cell lines using Trizol reagent (#15596026, Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, RR047A). For primary CTCs, cDNA was amplified using the SuperScript IV Single‐Cell/Low‐Input cDNA PreAmp Kit (#11752480, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, CTCs were lysed directly, and RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA in a single‐tube reaction using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase, followed by pre‐amplification with preamplification primers provided in the kit. The amplified cDNA served as a template for qRT‐PCR, performed using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II FAST qPCR (Takara, CN830A). The reaction conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 10 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to β‐actin, and primer sequences used for qRT‐PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S8.

Targeted metabolomics

Scramble or PERK‐KO MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC clusters were washed thrice with PBS prior to metabolic profiling using 600MRM analysis (Biotree, Shanghai, China). For sample preparation, 1, 500 µL of a mixture of acetonitrile, methanol, and H2O (2:2:1, v/v) containing isotopic internal standards was added to the cell pellets in Eppendorf tubes, which were precooled on dry ice. The samples underwent three cycles of freezing and thawing between liquid nitrogen and a 37°C water bath, followed by 30 s of vortex mixing. Subsequently, the samples were sonicated in an ice‐water bath for 15 min, incubated at ‐40°C for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 13, 800 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant (1, 200 µL) was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube and dried using a centrifugal concentrator. Dried residues were reconstituted with 120 µL of 60% acetonitrile, vortexed until fully dissolved, and centrifuged at 13,800 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Finally, 60‐70 µL of the supernatant was transferred into glass vials for liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) analysis. A standard metabolite mixture was prepared and used as a quality control sample. Data were processed using SCIEX Analyst Work Station Software (v1.7.2), and metabolite quantification was conducted using Data Driven Flow.

13C‐labeled metabolite tracing and analysis

For [13C6]‐D‐glucose tracing, scramble or PERK‐KO MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were washed three times with PBS, followed by culturing in DMEM without glucose supplemented with 25 mmol/L [13C6]‐D‐glucose (#660663, Sigma Aldrich) for 8 h. For [13C3]‐serine labeling, cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 0.4 mmol/L [13C3]‐serine (#604887, Sigma Aldrich) for 8 h. For [13C5]‐methionine labeling, cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 0.2 mmol/L [13C5]‐methionine (#908339, Sigma Aldrich) for 8 h. After incubation, cells were washed three times with ice‐cold PBS to remove residual medium and immediately snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cells were ground in liquid nitrogen, resuspended in 1 mL of cold 50% aqueous methanol, and kept on dry ice for 30 min, followed by thawing on ice. Subsequently, 0.4 mL of chloroform was added, the mixture was vortexed for 30 s, and then centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to new 1.5 mL tubes and stored at ‐80°C until analysis. Water‐soluble metabolites were analyzed using a Q Exactive PLUS hybrid quadrupole‐orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled with hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) using an XBridge BEH Amide column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.5 µM particle size, Waters). The gradient elution was performed with solvent A (95% H2O: 5% acetonitrile with 20 mmol/L ammonium acetate, 20 mmol/L ammonium hydroxide, pH 9.4) and solvent B (100% acetonitrile) under the following conditions: 0 min, 90% B; 2 min, 90% B; 3 min, 75% B; 7 min, 75% B; 8 min, 70% B; 9 min, 70% B; 10 min, 50% B; 12 min, 50% B; 13 min, 25% B; 14 min, 25% B; 16 min, 0% B; 20.5 min, 0% B; 21 min, 90% B; 25 min, 90% B. The flow rate was 150 µL/min with an injection volume of 5 µL, and the column was maintained at 25°C. Mass spectrometry was conducted in negative ion mode with a resolution of 140,000 at m/z 200, an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1 × 106 ions, and a scan range of m/z 75‐1000. Data were analyzed using El‐MAVEN software with natural abundance correction.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP was conducted using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, #9003), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. In brief, cells were subjected to cross‐linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and then quenched with 125 mmol/L glycine. Cells were lysed and chromatin was enzymatically cleaved to an average fragment size of 200‐500 bp. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4°C using 2 µg of antibody per sample, including anti‐H3K4me3 antibody (ab213224, Abcam), anti‐SP1 (#9389, Cell Signaling Technology) or control IgG (#2729, Cell Signaling Technology) with protein G beads. After thorough washing, chromatin complexes were eluted and crosslinks were reversed. Purified DNA was used as a template for qRT‐PCR to assess the enrichment of H3K4me3 or SP1 in the PDGFB promoter region. Primers used to amplify the promoter regions of PDGFB are shown in Supplementary Table S8.

RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq)

Cells were lysed in 1 mL TRIzol (#15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by RNA extraction according to the manufacturer's instructions. Total RNA quality was assessed using a Nano Drop and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Termo Fisher Scientifc). RNA‐seq was then conducted using the BGISEQ500 platform (BGI). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of RNA‐seq were performed on the Dr. Tom analysis system (BGI, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China).

ChIP sequencing (ChIP‐seq)

MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were cultured in CM or MRM for 24 h, followed by cross‐linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and quenched with 125 mmol/L glycine. ChIP‐seq library preparation and sequencing were performed by E‐GENE Tech (Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). Briefly, chromatin was isolated and fragmented to an average size of 200‐500 bp using sonication. ChIP was performed using an H3K4me3 antibody and immunoprecipitated DNA was purified. The sequence reads generated by Illumina platform, and raw reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 algorithm with the default settings. Peak calling was conducted using MACS2 to identify H3K4me3‐enriched regions. BigWig files were imported into the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, v2.17.3) for the visualization of specific loci.

Bioinformation analysis

Gene expression profiles of single CTCs and CTC clusters from the single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset GSE111065 were analyzed using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA, v1.47.1) to assess UPR pathway enrichment. To evaluate the relationship between PERK expression levels and overall survival (OS) in patients, PERK expression data and clinical information were retrieved from the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) database via the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/), and patients were stratified into high and low PERK expression groups using the median as cut‐off. The correlation between PERK and MAT2A expression was analyzed using TIMER2.0 (http://compbio.cn/timer2/) by calculating the Spearman correlation coefficient. The effect of methionine on H3K4me3 at the PDGFB promoter was analyzed using data from the GSE72131 dataset. The H3K4me3 enrichment at the PDGFB promoter region across various cell lines was acquired from the WashU Epigenome Browser (http://epigenomegateway.wustl.edu/browser/). Upstream transcription factors of PDGFB were predicted using the TRRUST database (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/). Transcription factors that bind to the PDGFB promoter region were evaluated by motif analysis using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using an unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test while analyses involving more than two groups utilized one‐way or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests, such as Tukey's or Bonferroni corrections. OS and distant metastasis‐free survival (DMFS) were analyzed using the log‐rank test. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause, while DMFS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the occurrence of distant metastasis or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Non‐parametric tests were employed when data did not meet normality assumptions. All experiments included at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. A cutoff of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

CTC clusters were highly efficient metastatic precursors of multiple malignant tumors

To obtain high‐quality CTCs, we employed a microfluidic chip based on hydrodynamic size‐based separation to initially separate single CTCs and CTC clusters from whole blood, followed by the removal of hemocytes using CD45+ magnetic beads and erythrocyte lysis buffer (Supplementary Figure S1A). To assess enrichment efficiency, GFP‐labeled MDA‐MB‐231 cells were mixed with blood from healthy donors. The capture efficiencies for single and clustered cells by the microfluidic chip were 95% and 98%, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1B‐D). Additionally, we established PDX models for subsequent CTC detection and analysis. HE staining revealed that PDX tumors exhibited morphological similarity to the corresponding primary tumors (Supplementary Figure S1E).

We then analyzed blood samples from patients with breast cancer, colon cancer, melanoma, and lung adenocarcinoma. CTCs were identified using cancer‐specific markers (pan‐CK and EGFR), while WBCs were distinguished using CD45 (Supplementary Figure S2A). Patients with at least one CTC (either a cluster or single cell) identified in 7.5 mL of peripheral blood exhibited significantly worse DMFS than those without detectable CTCs (Figure 1A). We further quantified the number of single CTCs and CTC clusters in these patients (Supplementary Figure S2B). Consistent with previous reports [4, 19], the detection of CTC clusters was significantly associated with unfavorable DMFS in patients (Figure 1B). Notably, single CTCs and CTC clusters were observed across multiple tumor mouse models, including PDX, CDX, and syngeneic models, indicating that CTCs were common in both tumor patients and tumor‐bearing mice (Supplementary Figure S2C‐F). Notably, the number and size of CTC clusters were markedly increased when the blood was collected from the tumor intravasating vessels rather than from the heart (Supplementary Figure S2G‐H), suggesting that CTC clusters were shed early and could be trapped in the microvasculature of distant organs before entering the bloodstream.

To further investigate the metastatic potential of single CTCs and CTC clusters, equivalent numbers of single and clustered cells were injected into mice via the tail vein and visualized by bioluminescence imaging. Mice injected with clustered cells developed apparent metastases more rapidly than those injected with single cells (Supplementary Figure S3A‐D). Additionally, mice injected with clustered cells developed more lung metastases and had a shorter OS compared to those injected with single cells (Supplementary Figure S3E‐F). These data indicated that CTC clusters constitute a highly efficient metastatic subpopulation, and that their presence is associated with poor outcomes in cancer patients.

To explore the key factors contributing to the metastasis of CTC clusters, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10 cells were cultured in suspension to simulate the bloodstream microenvironment. The results revealed that clustered cells exhibited higher proliferation and a lower proportion of Annexin V‐positive cells compared to single cells (Supplementary Figure S4A‐C). These observations were confirmed by IF staining of CTCs from both patients and model mice, which showed higher Ki67 positivity and lower TUNEL positivity in CTC clusters compared to single CTCs (Figure 1C‐D, Supplementary Figure S4D‐E). Similarly, a lower proportion of Annexin V‐positive cells was detected in CTC clusters than in single CTCs from the same mouse (Supplementary Figure S4F). Importantly, patients with higher levels of TUNEL‐positive CTC clusters generally had a more favorable prognosis than those with lower levels (Figure 1E). However, no significant differences were observed between single CTCs and CTC clusters in phenotypes related to epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, cell cycle status, or the mRNA expression of immune checkpoint proteins and stem cell markers (Supplementary Figure S4G‐J).

Mutual cooperation between aggregated cells potentially affects tumor metastasis [9, 10, 20, 21]. To evaluate the influence of cluster dissociation on metastatic potential, we injected single cells, single cells derived from dissociated clustered cells, and clustered cells into mice via the tail vein, and monitored them using bioluminescence imaging (Figure 1F). Mice injected with single cells exhibited the lowest lung colonization, whereas single cells from dissociated clustered cells showed significantly reduced colonization compared to clustered cells (Figure 1G‐H). Consistent with these findings, the pharmacological dissociation of CTC clusters using digitoxin or ouabain, which have been approved for efficient dissociation of CTC clusters without significant cytotoxicity [22], significantly reduced spontaneous CTC cluster formation, metastatic burden, and improved OS without affecting the primary tumor size (Supplementary Figure S5). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that CTC clusters are highly efficient metastatic precursors with superior survival capacity in the bloodstream, contributing to increased distant metastasis and poor patient outcomes.

The adaptive UPR facilitated the survival of CTC clusters within the bloodstream

The bloodstream provides a complex and harsh environment for CTCs [23]. Adapting to the hostile environment is a major challenge for the survival of CTC clusters. To explore the adaptive survival mechanism of CTC clusters in the bloodstream, single CTCs and CTC clusters were isolated from tumor‐bearing mouse models and subjected to RNA‐seq analysis (Figure 2A). GO analysis revealed that the UPR was significantly enriched in CTC clusters (Figure 2B). GSVA of single‐cell RNA‐seq data of single CTCs and CTC clusters from patients and PDX‐Br16 further revealed a significant enrichment of the UPR in CTC clusters (Figure 2C). A set of UPR‐related genes was upregulated in CTC clusters compared to single CTCs isolated from the NCG‐MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 mouse model (Figure 2D). Single CTCs and CTC clusters are theoretically subjected to the same environmental stresses [24]. However, compared to single CTCs, CTC clusters exhibited weaker ER enlargement and lower unfolded protein accumulation (Figure 2E‐F), indicating their greater ability to restore ER homeostasis.

Since unfavorable survival conditions cause cells to undergo ERS, and the UPR may be a potential strategy for cells to mitigate ERS [25]. To investigate the role of UPR activation in CTC cluster survival, tumor cells cultured in suspension were treated with ERS inducers to mimic the conditions encountered by CTCs in the bloodstream. The adaptive UPR genes were upregulated in both single cells and clustered cells after thapsigargin (an ERS inducer) treatment (Supplementary Figure S6A). Notably, single tumor cells showed higher susceptibility to thapsigargin than clustered cells, as demonstrated by the increased Annexin V positivity and mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization in single cells after thapsigargin treatment (Figure 2G, Supplementary Figure S6B). Moreover, clustered cells consistently exhibited greater viability with increasing ERS intensity compared to single cells (Figure 2H), indicating that clustered cells were more adaptable to unfavorable environments. Based on these findings, we investigated the relationship between CTC cluster hematogenous metastasis and UPR. Clustered cells were pretreated with azoramide, a pharmacological modulator of ERS, to protect them from ERS‐induced unfolded protein accumulation and cell death (Supplementary Figure S6C‐D). Equal amounts of vehicle‐ or azoramide‐treated cells were injected into mice via the tail vein and visualized by bioluminescence imaging (Figure 2I). Notably, azoramide pretreatment led to a significant increase in lung metastatic burden (Figure 2J‐K). However, treatment with inhibitors of apoptosis, necrosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, or PARP had no significant effect on thapsigargin‐induced cell death (Supplementary Figure S6E). These findings suggest that UPR activation in CTC clusters plays a crucial role in their adaptation to the bloodstream and subsequent metastatic progression.

Activated UPR‐PERK signaling endowed CTC clusters with survival ability in the bloodstream

Subsequently, we investigated how activated UPR contributes to the adaptive survival of CTC clusters. The UPR is an evolutionarily conserved defense mechanism comprising three primary branches: ATF6, IRE1α, and PERK signaling [25]. To identify potential effector molecules and signaling pathways of the UPR that affect CTC cluster survival, we assessed the expression levels of key genes associated with three UPR signaling branches. Our results revealed that CTC clusters exhibited higher levels of PERK and IRE1α compared to single CTCs (Figure 3A). Knockdown of PERK endowed clustered cells with higher sensitivity to ERS‐induced cell death compared to knockdown of IRE1α (Figure 3B, Supplementary Figure S7A‐B). Notably, elevated Annexin V‐positive cells were also observed in clustered cells pretreated with AMG44 (a selective PERK inhibitor) prior to thapsigargin exposure (Figure 3C), without affecting the expression levels of other branches of the UPR (Supplementary Figure S7C). Moreover, elevated PERK expression was detected in CTC clusters compared to single CTCs in both tumor patients and PDX mouse models (Figure 3D). Notably, activated PERK was observed in CTC clusters but not in single CTCs within the vasculature of lung metastases in mice (Figure 3E). Consistently, IF staining revealed that pPERK levels were significantly higher in CTC clusters compared to single CTCs in both patient samples and mouse models (Figure 3F‐G). Additionally, CTC clusters exhibited fewer TUNEL‐positive cells compared to single CTCs (Figure 3H‐I).

To investigate whether PERK activation can rescue cell death, MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC cells were treated with PERK‐specific activator MK‐28. PERK phosphorylation level was significantly increased under MK‐28 treatment (Supplementary Figure S7D). MK‐28 effectively activated PERK without affecting the other two branches of the UPR (Supplementary Figure S7E). Notably, MK‐28 treatment markedly reduced Annexin V levels in clustered cells (Figure 3J), but had no significant effect on cell proliferation, trans‐endothelial migration capacity, or cell cycle (Supplementary Figure S7F‐H). Subsequently, PERK‐KO cells were generated through the CRISPR‐Cas9‐based ablation of PERK (Supplementary Figure S8A). Loss of PERK had no effect on the expression of core proteins from other UPR branches (Supplementary Figure S8B). Interestingly, MK‐28 rescued cell death in scramble control cells but not in PERK‐KO cells (Supplementary Figure S8C). Moreover, PERK‐KO clustered cells exhibited increased misfolded protein accumulation, elevated Annexin V levels, and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential under thapsigargin treatment compared to the control group (Supplementary Figure S8D‐F). In addition, PERK depletion impaired CTC cluster formation, whereas PERK overexpression exhibited the opposite effect (Supplementary Figure S8G‐J). These findings collectively demonstrate that activated PERK signaling plays a pivotal role in promoting the survival and formation of CTC clusters under ERS.

PERK ablation hindered tumor metastasis

To examine the impact of PERK deletion on the metastatic ability of CTCs in vivo, equivalent numbers of PERK‐KO and scramble clustered cells were injected into mice via the tail vein. The results showed that PERK deletion significantly impaired lung metastasis (Figure 4A‐C). Additionally, orthotopic implantation of equal numbers of scramble or PERK‐KO tumor cells revealed that mice bearing PERK‐KO tumors exhibited a significantly reduced number of CTCs, especially CTC clusters, compared to the scramble control mice (Figure 4D‐E). The proportion of TUNEL‐positive CTC clusters was notably higher in PERK‐KO tumor‐bearing mice than in the scramble group (Figure 4F). Accordingly, TUNEL‐positive CTC clusters were observed in the lung vasculature from PERK‐KO tumor‐bearing mice (Figure 4G). Meanwhile, PERK depletion resulted in reduced spontaneous lung metastasis and prolonged OS in mice (Figure 4H‐J).

Higher PERK expression in patients with breast cancer and melanoma was significantly associated with shorter DMFS and advanced TNM stages (Supplementary Tables S5‐S6, Supplementary Figure S9A‐B). In addition, analysis of different cancer datasets from TCGA, including breast cancer, melanoma, lung adenocarcinoma and colon cancer, unveiled that patient with high PERK expression had decreased OS compared to those with low PERK expression (Supplementary Figure S9C). Together, these data demonstrate that PERK plays a key role in supporting the survival and metastasis of CTC clusters.

PERK signaling maintained SAM availability via the methionine metabolism cycle

To understand the mechanisms by which PERK promotes CTC cluster survival, an unbiased RNA‐seq analysis was conducted on scramble and PERK‐KO MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC clusters. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that several amino acid metabolic pathways, particularly cysteine and methionine metabolism, were significantly enriched in scramble clustered cells compared to PERK‐KO clustered cells (Figure 5A). Targeted metabolomic analysis further confirmed that cellular methionine‐related metabolites, including L‐serine, L‐glycine, 5‐methyltetrahydrofolic acid, L‐methionine, SAM, and S‐adenosyl‐L‐homocysteine (SAH), were significantly reduced in PERK‐KO clustered cells compared to those in scramble controls (Figure 5B‐C). Conversely, methionine, SAM, and SAH were accumulated in PERK‐overexpressed clustered cells (Figure 5D).

Methionine cycle flux has been shown to specifically influence the epigenetic landscape of cancer cells, driving tumor progression [26]. To determine how PERK regulates the methionine cycle, we employed LC‐MS‐based 13C carbon tracing to profile the dynamic metabolic activities. Labeled [13C6]‐glucose tracing showed that while PERK ablation reduced M+3 3‐phosphoglycerate (3‐PG), M+3 serine, and M+2 glycine levels, the incorporation of glucose‐derived carbons into M+1 methionine or M+1 SAM remained minimal, indicating a limited contribution of glucose to the methionine cycle (Supplementary Figure S10A). In addition, [13C3]‐serine tracing confirmed that PERK increased extracellular M+3 serine uptake and M+2 glycine conversion (Supplementary Figure S10B). However, the one‐carbon unit provided by exogenous [13C3]‐serine to methionine cycle was negligible (Supplementary Figure S10B), suggesting that PERK did not promote the methionine cycle through the de novo synthesis pathway. Subsequently, [13C5]‐methionine labeling was used to trace SAM production within the methionine cycle. PERK significantly promoted the conversion of exogenous [13C5]‐methionine into SAM (Figure 5E), further confirming that PERK‐driven SAM synthesis relied on the exogenous uptake of methionine. Notably, methionine deficiency resulted in the most pronounced cell death compared with other one‐carbon metabolism‐related products, as shown by Annexin V staining in MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC and B16F10 cells (Figure 5F, Supplementary Figure S10C).

We then detected the expression levels of enzymes related to one‐carbon metabolism and methionine metabolic pathways. The mRNA and protein levels of MAT2A were significantly downregulated in PERK‐KO clustered cells compared to those in scramble control. In contrast, the expression levels of PSAT1, PHGDH and MTHFR remained largely unchanged between PERK‐KO and scramble clustered cells (Figure 5G, Supplementary Figure S10D).

Activated PERK signaling has been reported to regulate pathways involved in amino acid metabolism, primarily through the enhancement of ATF4 translation [25]. Therefore, we investigated the effect of PERK‐regulated ATF4 on MAT2A expression. As expected, PERK depletion significantly reduced ATF4 protein levels (Figure 5H), and ATF4 knockdown led to significant downregulation of MAT2A (Figure 5I‐J, Supplementary Figure S10E), suggesting that PERK‐mediated ATF4 enhanced MAT2A expression. MAT2A is the rate‐limiting enzyme in the methionine cycle that catalyzes the conversion of methionine and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) into SAM [27]. Intracellular SAM levels were significantly reduced either in shMAT2A cells or in cells treated with PF9366 (Supplementary Figure S10F‐G). Similar results were observed under methionine‐limited conditions (Supplementary Figure S10H). Additionally, PERK expression was positively correlated with MAT2A expression in TCGA datasets (Supplementary Figure S10I).

To explore the effects of SAM availability on CTC cluster survival and metastasis, Annexin V levels were assessed. Increased Annexin V levels were detected in MAT2A knockdown or enzyme‐inhibited clustered cells, which were partially rescued by SAM supplementation (Figure 5K). Correspondingly, MAT2A‐deficient clustered cells exhibited decreased lung metastasis compared with control clustered cells in mice (Figure 5L‐N). Mice injected with methionine‐restricted clustered cells had a reduced metastatic burden compared to controls (Figure 5O‐P). Collectively, PERK signaling enhanced ATF4 expression, which promoted MAT2A‐mediated synthesis of SAM from methionine and maintained SAM availability, thereby facilitating the metastasis formation of CTCs.

Enhanced PDGFB via SAM‐mediated H3K4me3 methylation modification served as a shared survival signal within CTC cluster

Histone methylation is a key post‐translational modification (PTMs) that plays an important role in the epigenetic regulation of gene expression in various cell types [28]. SAM, as a principal methyl donor, is involved in histone methylation reactions [29]. To assess the effect of SAM accumulation induced by the PERK‐MAT2A axis on histone modifications and the expression of survival‐related genes in CTC clusters, we first examined several histone methylation markers. Notably, PERK overexpression resulted in a significant increase in H3K4me3, but not in H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and H3K36me3 modifications, and this effect was attenuated by MAT2A inhibition (Supplementary Figure S11A). SAM supplementation partially restored H3K4me3 levels in clustered cells with a disrupted PERK‐MAT2A axis (Figure 6A). Moreover, methionine starvation significantly impaired intracellular H3K4me3 modifications (Supplementary Figure S11B), likely due to decreased cellular SAM availability.

H3K4me3 is a typical chromatin methylation modification that plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression [30]. To understand the regulatory role of SAM‐dependent H3K4me3 modifications in CTC cluster survival‐related gene expression, we performed ChIP‐seq and RNA‐seq on MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC clusters cultured in CM or MRM. Methionine treatment significantly affected gene expression and H3K4me3 enrichment at the promoter region (Figure 6B‐C, Supplementary Figure S11C). Integrated analysis of ChIP‐seq and RNA‐seq data identified 10 target genes with both high H3K4me3 enrichment at their promoter regions and elevated mRNA expression levels under methionine treatment (Figure 6D‐E, Supplementary Table S9). Among these methionine‐induced high‐expression candidates, PDGFB, a secreted protein that triggers tumor cell proliferation, survival, and metastatic progression [31], caught our attention. Notably, we also found that the highly conserved H3K4me3 modification was enriched in the PDGFB promoter in several cell lines, as visualized by the WashU Epigenome Browser (Supplementary Figure S11D). Methionine restriction reduced H3K4me3 modification in the PDGFB promoter (Figure 6F, Supplementary Figure S11E‐F), while MAT2A inhibition significantly decreased the mRNA and H3K4me3 modification levels of PDGFB, which were rescued by SAM supplementation (Supplementary Figure S11G‐H).

To investigate the H3K4me3‐mediated transcriptional activation mechanism of PDGFB, we first predicted upstream regulatory transcription factors using the TRRUST database. SP1 was selected based on its high binding affinity to the PDGFB promoter region, as evaluated by motif analysis using the JASPAR database (Supplementary Figure S12A). Previous studies have demonstrated that SP1 could recruit SET domain‐containing 1A (SETD1A, also known as SET1), a key histone methyltransferase responsible for catalyzing H3K4 trimethylation [32]. Interestingly, under methionine‐restricted conditions, SP1 occupancy at the PDGFB promoter was significantly reduced (Supplementary Figure S12B). To further assess the functional role of SP1 in modulating H3K4me3 modification, we established SP1‐knockdown cell lines (Supplementary Figure S12C). SP1 knockdown significantly reduced H3K4me3 levels at the PDGFB promoter, accompanied by decreased PDGFB mRNA expression (Supplementary Figure S12D‐E), indicating that SP1 facilitates PDGFB transcription by promoting H3K4me3 enrichment at its promoter.

To further validate the role of the PERK‐MAT2A‐PDGFB axis in CTC cluster survival, we generated MAT2A‐ and PDGFB‐overexpressing cell lines (Supplementary Figure S13A‐B) and conducted rescue experiments. MAT2A overexpression restored PDGFB expression in PERK‐KO tumor cells (Supplementary Figure S13C), indicating that MAT2A acts downstream of PERK to regulate PDGFB expression. Moreover, MAT2A overexpression significantly reduced Annexin V levels in PERK‐KO cells (Supplementary Figure S13D). Additionally, PDGFB overexpression effectively alleviated the increase in Annexin V levels induced by PERK or MAT2A depletion (Supplementary Figure S13E‐F). These findings demonstrate that PDGFB serves as a crucial survival signal downstream of the PERK‐MAT2A axis.

Given the specific structure of clustered cells (Figure 6G), we hypothesized that secreted PDGFB localizes to intercellular regions, facilitating the transmission of survival signals among neighboring cells within CTC cluster. Indeed, IF staining showed that PDGFB was primarily localized at the cell‐cell border of CTC clusters (Figure 6H). Notably, Intercellular PDGFB signal intensity was decreased after tumor cell cluster dispersion or PDGFB knockdown (Figure 6I‐J, Supplementary Figure S14A). PDGFRβ is the primary receptor for PDGFB, which dimerizes upon binding to PDGFB and triggers auto‐phosphorylation, subsequently activating downstream PI3K/AKT signaling [31]. As expected, PDGFRβ phosphorylation levels were significantly reduced after PDGFB knockdown, accompanied by a significant downregulation of the downstream PI3K/AKT signaling (Supplementary Figure S14B‐C). Intracellular PI3K/AKT signaling activation was diminished following treatment with the tyrosine kinase inhibitor imatinib, which inhibited activities of PDGFRβ and corresponding downstream signaling (Supplementary Figure S14D). Correspondingly, Annexin V levels were elevated in MDA‐MB‐231/LM2‐CTC clusters after PDGFB knockdown in suspension culture (Figure 6K), accompanied by a reduction in tumor cell metastatic potential (Figure 6L‐N). In summary, SAM availability in CTC clusters regulated the H3K4me3 modification of PDGFB and significantly affected the secretion of PDGFB, which acted as a survival signal shared among neighboring cells in CTC clusters, contributing to the maintenance of CTC cluster survival in the bloodstream.

Targeting PERK/PDGFRβ acted as a therapeutic strategy for metastatic tumors

Finally, we investigated whether simultaneously inhibiting PERK and PDGFRβ signaling could effectively suppress tumor metastasis. MDA‐MB‐231/LM2 or B16F10 clustered cells cultured in suspension were treated with AMG44 and imatinib alone or in combination, followed by tail vein injection into mice (Supplementary Figure S15A). The combination of AMG44 and imatinib significantly reduced the ability of clustered cells to colonize in distant lung tissues compared to AMG44 or imatinib alone (Supplementary Figure S15B‐E).

To further evaluate the antimetastatic efficacy in vivo, NCG mice bearing the patient‐derived xenograft model (PDX‐1) were treated with AMG44, imatinib alone, or in combination (Figure 7A). AMG44 combined with imatinib markedly reduced primary tumor expansion, but did not affect body weight (Figure 7B‐D). Notably, this combination decreased CTC cluster numbers in the bloodstream (Figure 7E), attenuated PERK signaling and PDGFRβ activation in CTC clusters, and increased the number of TUNEL‐positive cells (Figure 7F‐H). To further confirm the therapeutic potential of targeting PERK and PDGFRβ signaling, we cultured CTCs isolated from breast cancer patients and expanded them in vitro (Figure 7I). Consistently, the combined treatment with AMG44 and imatinib treatment significantly reduced cell viability compared to monotherapy with either agent alone (Figure 7J), indicating that dual inhibition of PERK and PDGFRβ signaling effectively impaired CTC cluster survival. Taken together, these data reveal that the PERK–ATF4–MAT2A signaling axis sustains CTC cluster survival by inducing SAM‐dependent PDGFB expression, which confers a survival advantage to CTC clusters in bloodstream (Figure 8).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that CTC clusters exhibited greater adaptive survival and metastatic ability compared with single CTCs. Through the integration of transcriptomics, metabolomics, and ChIP‐seq, we uncovered that UPR‐arm PERK signaling supported CTC cluster survival by maintaining SAM availability and enhancing PDGFB expression through H3K4me3 modifications. The accumulation of PDGFB in the intercellular space provided a shared survival signal to neighboring cells within the cluster, supporting their survival and metastatic capability in the bloodstream.

CTC clusters promoted metastasis at a higher efficiency than single CTCs. Metastasis is primarily driven by the dissemination of CTCs, which are commonly detected in patients with advanced‐stage cancer [33]. The metastatic potential of CTC clusters is 23‐50 times higher than that of single CTCs, which is attributed to their enhanced chemoresistance and immune evasion [8, 34, 35]. Our study corroborated these observations by demonstrating the presence of CTC clusters in both cancer patients and mouse models, with their occurrence linked to poor prognosis. Importantly, our findings revealed that CTC clusters exhibited superior adaptive survival ability compared to single CTCs, reinforcing their critical role in promoting distant organ metastasis.

CTC clusters are highly adaptable to the bloodstream environment. As tumor cells detach from their primary sites and migrate into the bloodstream, they encounter significant environment alterations, including shear stress, oxidative stress, and immune surveillance. Previous studies have shown that adhesion molecules such as CD44, ICAM1, and plakoglobin facilitate CTC cluster formation, enhancing their resistance to mechanical stress [8, 9, 10, 36]. Additionally, CTC clusters maintain intratumoral hypoxia to limit ROS production and prevent oxidative stress [37]. The dynamic reduction in terminal sialylation of glycoproteins in CTC clusters promote cellular dormancy and enhances chemoresistance [34]. These findings collectively confirmed that CTC clusters exhibited high plasticity in the bloodstream. Here, we discovered a novel survival mechanism in CTC clusters, namely adaptive UPR signaling, which enables them to withstand the stressful conditions of the bloodstream. The UPR is an evolutionarily conserved adaptive mechanism that helps cells adapt to unfavorable environments [38]. Activated UPR has also been observed in disseminated tumor cells within the bone marrow of cancer patients, aiding their adaptation to hypoxic and nutrient‐deficient environments [39]. However, its role in CTC clusters has not been previously elucidated.

The adaptive UPR mediator PERK determines cell fate through downstream signaling pathways. PERK acts as an effector molecule of the UPR and is crucially involved in the regulation of amino acid metabolism, autophagy, and antioxidant responses [38]. PERK has also been shown to enhance chemoresistance and anoikis resistance in cancer cells [40, 41]. However, current studies have primarily focused on the role of PERK in primary tumors, with limited investigations into its function during metastasis, particularly in hematogenous dissemination. In this study, we identified PERK as an adaptive signal in CTC clusters that activates the downstream transcription factor ATF4 to regulate MAT2A‐mediated SAM biosynthesis. SAM promotes the enrichment of H3K4me3 at the PDGFB promoter, with SP1 contributing to this locus‐specific modification, ultimately leading to elevated PDGFB expression and promoting the survival and metastatic dissemination of CTC clusters.

PDGFB acts as a signaling molecule that facilitates cell‐to‐cell communication. Previous studies have shown that anoikis‐resistant gastric cancer cells enhance PDGFB secretion, promoting angiogenesis and peritoneal metastasis via autocrine and paracrine signaling [42]. In this study, we identified a novel role of PDGFB in CTC clusters. Specifically, we found that PDGFB accumulated in the intercellular regions of CTC clusters and served as a shared survival signal. This signaling is mediated through PDGFRβ, a high‐affinity receptor for PDGFB, which activates the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, thereby protecting CTCs from cell death [43, 44]. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of PDGFB in the metastatic progression and its provision of substantial survival advantage to CTC clusters.

Effectively targeting CTCs represents a promising strategy for improving cancer patient survival. Despite significant advancements in medical interventions such as surgery, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, which have markedly improved patient survival rates, these approaches cannot completely eliminate the risk of metastasis, as metastasis may have already occurred [3]. CTCs represent a major risk for tumor metastasis and recurrence, making their effective detection and therapeutic targeting a pivotal strategy in advancing cancer treatment and improving long‐term patient outcomes [45]. CTC counts serve as a reliable biomarker for guiding therapeutic decisions between chemotherapy and single‐agent endocrine therapy in the first‐line treatment of hormone receptor‐positive ERBB2‐negative metastatic breast cancer [46]. Given that CTC clusters have a higher metastatic potential than single CTCs, increasing efforts have been made to develop strategies targeting CTC clusters. Inhibition of heparanase with a specific inhibitor has been shown to prevent cluster formation and inhibit breast cancer metastasis in mice [47]. Additionally, a proof‐of‐concept trial demonstrated that digoxin can effectively disaggregate CTC clusters in patients with metastatic breast cancer [48]. Building on these findings, our study provided new insights into targeting CTC clusters to inhibit metastatic dissemination. Our work confirmed that AMG44 combined with imatinib reduced the counts and viability of CTC clusters in the bloodstream.

Despite our promising results, this study had several limitations. First, although our study focused on the role of histone methylation in regulating gene expression and CTC cluster survival, we did not investigate other important epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation and RNA methylation, which may also contribute to the regulation of CTC cluster biology. Second, the specific effects of the downstream signaling pathways activated by PDGFB on CTC cluster survival require further investigation. Third, due to the limited availability of CTCs in clinical samples, we were unable to validate our findings in a larger patient cohort. Finally, although we proposed a combined treatment strategy using PERK inhibitors and imatinib, its therapeutic efficacy requires further validation across diverse metastasis models.

CONCLUSION

In summary, our study elucidated a critical role of the PERK/MAT2A/PDGFB axis in the survival and metastasis of CTC clusters. PDGFB functioned as a shared signal in the intercellular region, activating the pro‐survival pathway via PDGFRβ on neighboring cells within the cluster. Targeting PERK and PDFGRβ to disrupt the survival pathway of CTC clusters presented a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of malignant metastatic tumors.
