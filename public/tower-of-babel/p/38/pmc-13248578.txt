Metabolic Strategies Under Heat Stress in Non‐Adapted and High‐Temperature‐Adapted Lines of a Marine Diatom
Authors: María Aranguren‐Gassis, Angel P Diz, Suzana G Leles, María Huete‐Ortega, Andrew Allen, Naomi M Levine, Elena Litchman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the last decade, numerous laboratory experiments have demonstrated that when marine phytoplankton are exposed to thermal stress, they can evolve high temperature tolerance in a short time (months). This evolutionary potential may ensure the persistence of marine phytoplankton species under current and future global warming. However, the effect of such adaptation on the phytoplankton's interaction with the environment and other organisms depends on how cellular metabolism shifts during the evolutionary process. In order to elucidate which cellular strategies allow the emergence of thermo‐tolerant lines, we analysed the proteomic changes in a marine diatom ( Chaetoceros simplex ) following both thermal acclimation and evolutionary adaptation. We found that the high temperature‐tolerant lines exhibit a conservative cellular strategy when acclimated to high, above‐optimal temperature, where nitrogen recycling, reallocation of carbon and nitrogen, and protein repair or protection are favoured at the expense of new proteins and photosynthetic structures biosynthesis and rapid growth. While this strategy gives the lines that evolved high temperature tolerance an advantage under thermal stress, the shift to resource reallocation may explain the absence of high‐temperature adaptation when cells are exposed to low nitrate availability.Keywords: acclimation, evolution, marine phytoplankton, metabolism, nitrate limitation, proteomics, temperature

Introduction

Temperature is a key factor that can be a strong selective agent for marine species (Husby et al. 2011), including diatoms (Koester et al. 2013), and a critical environmental element influencing, for example, phytoplankton growth (Eppley 1972), key physiological rates such as photosynthesis or respiration (Padfield et al. 2015; Regaudie‐de‐Gioux and Duarte 2012) or species distribution in the ocean (Thomas et al. 2017). Its importance is increasing under the ongoing climate change. Ocean is warming at a non‐precedented rate (IPCC 2021), and temperature increase is already affecting marine species distribution and extinction rates (Barton et al. 2016; Lenoir et al. 2020; Pinsky et al. 2019, 2020). This effect could be less dramatic for unicellular organisms, such as phytoplankton, which may be able to readily adapt to novel conditions due to their short generation times, large line numbers, and typically high genetic diversity (Rynearson and Armbrust 2000 and 2005). Thermal adaptation in unicellular organisms shifts their cellular strategies, understood as the set of regulatory pathways activated by the cell to respond to the environment and ensure survival or growth, allowing species to persist in new environments (Schaum, Buckling, et al. 2018). Those metabolic changes can modify the adapted cells' interaction with the environment and other organisms (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019; Padfield et al. 2015; Schaum, Student Research Team, et al. 2018; Yvon‐Durocher et al. 2015) but are not well characterized.

The short‐term response (acclimation) of phytoplankton to elevated temperatures has been widely studied, but long‐term adaptation may be different from short‐term acclimation responses (Boyd et al. 2016; Chan et al. 2021 and Collins et al. 2020 for reviews), and plasticity itself, as the capacity to acclimate to a range of temperatures, can evolve (Collins et al. 2020). From the current literature, some generalities on phytoplankton adaptation to thermal stress emerge, for example, adaptive changes involve such traits as cell growth rate, nutrient and polyunsaturated fatty acid content, photo‐physiological performance or extracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) level (O'Donnell et al. 2019, 2021; Chan et al. 2021 and references therein). However, the information on the cellular process and the reorganization of metabolic pathways that drive such physiological changes is scarce. Such information is crucial to envisage long‐term responses in new environments where temperature and other environmental drivers can interact. By characterizing cellular mechanisms behind thermal adaptation, we can infer potential trade‐offs and synergies with responses to other drivers that improve our understanding of adaptive outcomes and constraints. In this regard, warming is often associated with decreased nutrient availability in the ocean (Sarmiento et al. 2004). Nutrient limitation, such as by nitrogen (N), also alters key physiological functions, and the joint effects of both drivers (warming and N limitation) may prevent phytoplankton adaptation to warming environments (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019).

To better understand the cellular mechanisms behind phytoplankton adaptation to high temperatures, we studied the proteomic responses of marine diatom lines that are resistant to high temperatures. We exposed these lines to above‐optimal temperatures and compared their responses to a non‐temperature‐resistant line at both optimal and above‐optimal temperatures. These lines were obtained during an evolution experiment with a marine diatom (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019). In this experiment, non‐resistant lines were subjected to over‐optimal temperatures for about 200 generations, which led to the development of temperature‐resistant lines. The results revealed specific cellular strategies under stressful thermal conditions that allowed the lines to persist. However, exposure to nitrogen limitation during the evolution experiment prevented the emergence of these temperature‐resistant lines. We discuss how shifts in metabolic pathways improved cells' survival under high, previously lethal temperatures, and using a proteome allocation model we explore why the interaction with other environmental drivers, specifically low nitrate availability, suppressed high temperature tolerance.

Experimental Procedures

Lines of Chaetoceros simplex Ostenfeld (strain CCMP 200 from the National Center for Marine Algae and Microbiota, NCMA) were cultured for ~200 generations at an above‐optimal temperature (31°C), with four lines at N‐replete and another four under N‐limited conditions. Additional replicates of those ancestral lines were cryopreserved prior to the evolution experiment, and one line was maintained at 25°C as a control. All lines were derived from a single culture that we bottlenecked via sequential dilution to reduce genetic variation as much as possible. After ~200 generations (calculated from growth rates), thermal performance curves (TPC) were characterized for the lines evolving under N‐replete conditions, and tolerance to 34°C was estimated for all lines under both N‐replete and N‐limited conditions. Signs of sexual reproduction were not observed throughout the experiment.

After evolving at above‐optimal temperatures (31°C) for ~200 generations, some marine diatom lines became tolerant to extremely high temperatures (34°C) when growing under N‐replete conditions but not under N‐limited conditions (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019). Preliminary work showed that our ancestral experimental line grew from 13°C to 32°C, with an optimum temperature of 25°C (not shown). The 31°C treatment exceeded the strain's optimum yet permitted sufficient growth (0.5–0.7 per day). 34°C was chosen as a lethal temperature for the initial lines to test for the emergence of thermotolerant lines. The emergence of the thermotolerant lines could be due to either new mutations or selection of existing diversity, as we are not certain that the original culture originated from a single cell.

The details of the evolution experiment are described in Aranguren‐Gassis et al. (2019).

Here we analysed the proteome response of those lines that evolved extremely high temperature tolerance when they grew at high temperature (34°C) to identify cellular processes that allowed such adaptation (Figure 1 and Box 1). We compared their responses to the responses of ancestral lines (cryopreserved from the original lines) and the lines that were maintained at benign temperature (25°C) when they grew at 25°C and high temperature (31°C) (Figure 1 and Box 1).

None of them were able to grow at 34°C and both had similar TPCs (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019).

Thermo‐tolerant lines (Tt): cultures that evolved experimentally over ~200 generations at 31°C and nitrate‐replete conditions and could grow at 34°C (called tolerant strains in Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019) and showed a significantly increased optimum temperatures relative to the Nt lines (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019).

Nt31: non‐tolerant lines acclimated at 31°C (above‐optimum temperature) and nitrate‐replete conditions for the proteomics analysis.

Tt34: thermo‐tolerant lines acclimated at 34°C (above‐optimum temperature) and nitrate‐replete conditions for the proteomics analysis.

Culture Conditions

All lines were maintained in regular nutrient‐enriched artificial L1 seawater medium from NCMA, modified from Guillard and Hargraves (1993). Only the nitrate concentration was modified during evolution experiments, establishing two experimental N‐availability conditions: 884 μM NO3 − and 5 μM NO3 −.

Proteomics

Two evolved lines were randomly selected from the ones that developed high‐temperature (34°C) tolerance (thermo‐tolerant, Tt lines, see Box 1), and two other lines were randomly selected from the control replicates (nontolerant, Nt lines, see Box 1), one maintained at 25°C and one from the cryopreserved ancestral lines. Those lines were grown at the ancestral optimum temperature (25°C) and at the above‐optimal temperatures (31°C for the Nt and 34°C for the Tt, see Table 1). 34°C was selected as a stressful over‐optimal temperature for the Tt lines at which the Nt lines were not able to grow (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019). Lines were maintained in the same L1 medium specified before, in 500 mL glass flasks, at 100 μmol quanta m−2 s−1 cool white fluorescent light on the 14/10 h day/night cycle. We gently swirled and randomly repositioned flasks daily. Every 3 days, 5–10 mL from each line were transferred to fresh medium. We monitored lines by measuring in vivo chlorophyll‐a fluorescence daily (excitation wavelength: 436 nm, emission wavelength: 680 nm) using a SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA).

Transcriptomics

For transcriptomics, three replicates of the ancestral line were grown in similar conditions than described before, at 20°C and 31°C. Filtration was made as described above for proteomics. The results from these transcriptomic analyses were used only for protein annotation in this work.

RNA Extraction and Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from filters using Macherey‐Nagel NucleoMag RNA kit (Macherey‐Nagel, Düren, Germany). Cleared lysate was loaded into a 96 deep‐well plate and put on an epMotion 5075 TMX liquid handler (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) to complete the RNA extraction following the Macherey‐Nagel standard protocol. RNA quality was analysed on a 2200 TapeStation System with Agilent RNA ScreenTape System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and quantified using Quant‐iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). PolyA enrichment mRNA transcriptome was constructed using 400 ng‐1ug of total RNA as input into the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), following the manufacture's Low‐Throughput protocol. Library quality was analysed on a 2200 TapeStation System with Agilent High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape System (Agilent Technologies). Resulting libraries were subjected to paired‐end sequencing via Illumina HiSeq4000.

Protein Extraction

The equipment was washed before extraction, first with methanol, then with ethanol 70%, and finally with 100% ethanol. All reactants were prepared with HPLC grade water, and the tubes used for processing samples were low binding types. Frozen filters were washed with 0.5 M Tetraethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) buffer solution with 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) detergent. The filtered biomass was ground in a precooled mortar with liquid nitrogen and 1 mL 1% protease inhibitor solution (Thermo Fisher protease and phosphatase inhibition cocktail without EDTA). The ground samples were sonicated for 5 min with ice, and then 100 μL glass beads (Sigma acid washed glass beads 425–600 μm) were added to each sample. They were bead beaten five times, alternating 2 min beating and 2 min resting in ice. After beating, samples were centrifuged at 4°C and 18,000g for 30 min. The supernatant was then transferred to new tubes and stored at −80°C.

Proteomic Analyses

Quantification

Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) microplate assay (Thermo Scientific Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit) was used for protein quantification with an albumin (Sigma Aldrich bovine serum albumin) calibration standard. For each sample, 15 μg protein was diluted in acetone to obtain 1 μg/μl protein concentration and stored at −20°C for 12 h. Samples were then centrifuged (4°C, 10000g, 10 min), and the pellets were resuspended in 15 μL of TEAB 20 mM solution.

In‐Gel Digestion of Proteins

Protein samples containing 10 μg of total protein were loaded onto a standard Laemmli‐type polyacrylamide gel and allowed to stack and enter the resolving gel, but not allowed to separate. Comassie blue stain was used. Then, gel pieces were excised and subjected to in‐gel digestion. Briefly, gel pieces were washed sequentially with ammonium bicarbonate 25 mM and 50% ACN/ammonium bicarbonate 25 mM, in an ultrasonic bath; proteins were reduced by treatment with 10 mM DTT for 1 h, and alkylated with 55 mM IAA for 30 min. Protein digestion was accomplished with 40 ng of trypsin at 37°C overnight and tryptic peptides were extracted from the gel matrix in two steps with 0.5% TFA and 100% ACN.

LC–MS/MS Analysis

The tryptic peptides were dried in a speed‐vacuum concentrator at 45°C (Concentrator plus, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), reconstituted in LC/MS‐grade water containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and analysed by electrospray ionization‐tandem mass spectrometry on a hybrid high‐resolution LTQ‐Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer coupled to a Proxeon Easy‐nLC 1000 UHPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific).

The peptides were delivered onto a reverse phase column (PepMap RSLC C18, 2 μm, 100 Å, 75 μm × 50 cm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and were eluted with an ACN gradient of 5%–30% containing 0.1% formic acid for 240 min. The elutes were directly delivered into the mass spectrometer, which was set in positive ion mode in a data‐dependent manner. A full MS scan was performed from 380 to 1600 m/z with resolution at 120,000. The tandem mass spectrometer (MS/MS) CID (Collision‐Induced Dissociation) was performed with top 15 at 38% of the normalized collision energy (NCE) with a dynamic exclusion time at 30 s, a minimum signal threshold at 1000, a resolution at 30,000, and an isolation width at 1.50 Da.

Protein Annotation

Transcriptomics

Transcripts were assembled using the RNAseq Annotation Pipeline (RAP). The raw paired‐end Illumina fastq files were trimmed of any existing primers and the low‐quality bases (primer‐trim & qtrim) and filtered for rRNA sequences. The preprocessed (filtered) sequences were initially used for assembling the contigs at library level. The resulting contigs were then merged by groups. Open reading frames (ORFs) were identified for the merged assembled contigs. Read counts for each ORF were tabulated by mapping the original reads to the assembled contigs of each group. Functional annotation of each ORF was generated using BLAST, HMMER, TMHMM, transporter, organelle & KOG searches of the curated reference sequences in the phyloDB database.

Protein Identification and Quantification

PEAKS Studio v.8.0 software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Canada) was used for matching MS/MS spectra against the database (open reading frames from RNAseq) obtained from the transcriptomic analyses described above. Prior to identification, a data refinement was made with default predefined parameters.

PEAKS DB tool was used for the proteins identification against Ch. simplex transcriptomics results. 10 ppm and 0.02 Da were set for precursor and fragment ion tolerance respectively; up to one missed trypsin cleavage site allowed. Oxidation of methionine and N‐terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications, while carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues was set as fixed modifications. False Discovery Rate (FDR) was set at 0.1% at PSM level.

For Label Free quantification, peptide intensities for each sample were normalized based on the total ion chromatogram (TIC). Only identifications of unique peptides were used for protein quantification. Parameter settings were: Mass Error Tolerance: 10.0 ppm; Retention Time Shift Tolerance: 5.0 min; Dependent on PID: 46; FDR Threshold: 1%.

From the identified proteins, only those present in at least 50% of the replicates for each treatment were considered for the subsequent analyses.

Missing values were replaced by the minimum intensity value of that same sample (Liu and Dongre 2021), assuming the missing values arise from protein abundance below the limit of detection.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Functional annotation for all the identified proteins and a gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis based on Fisher exact test (FDR = 0.05) were performed using Omicsbox software (version 1.4.12). To identify the enriched terms for each comparison, the set of differentially expressed proteins was used as test set whereas all quantified proteins in the experiment were considered the reference set. The reduced option was used for the analysis, showing more specific GO terms. Each enriched GO term was manually classified in a functional category depending on its role in cell metabolism. The categories used are: carbon (C) metabolism, cell organization, DNA–RNA, energy use and storage, fatty acid metabolism, nitrogen (N) metabolism, photosynthesis, protein metabolism (including protein biosynthesis, protein catabolism and protein regulation), respiration, stress response and transport. Those GO terms with general functions that cannot be uniquely assigned to a specific category are classified in the other category.

Upregulated GO Terms for Each Treatment and Three Comparisons

For each enriched GO term at each comparison, the number of proteins upregulated in each treatment was checked. A GO term is considered upregulated for a particular treatment in a comparison when at least 75% of the significant proteins that correspond to such GO term are upregulated in that treatment.

Model Simulations of Proteome Allocation

We applied a proteome allocation model (Leles and Levine 2023; Leles et al. 2025) to extrapolate our results and investigate the metabolic responses of the thermo‐tolerant and the nontolerant lines under nitrogen limitation. The model consists of a system of ordinary differential equations defined as a constrained optimization problem in which the objective function is to maximize the steady‐state growth of a phytoplankton cell. Here, we solve the proteome model over a range of temperatures and contrast two nitrogen regimes (i.e., replete and limited conditions) allowing thermal growth curves to emerge as a function of molecular and biophysical constraints. The model predicts different cellular traits, such as size and the carbon to nitrogen quota of the cell, and metabolic shifts by optimizing the relative proteome investments across metabolic functions, the concentration of macromolecular pools, and the surface area‐to‐volume ratio of the phytoplankton cell. Following Leles and Levine (2023), temperature accelerates all enzymatic reactions in the model except photochemical reactions (Raven and Geider 1988), which we assumed to be temperature independent, and nitrogen transporters have a lower thermal dependency relative to other reactions in the model based on previous empirical evidence. The model also accounts for protein damage due to warming. To minimize heat‐damage, the cell can invest in repair pathways and heat‐mitigation strategies, such as producing antioxidant enzymes and/or storing fatty acids. Here, we use the model version where antioxidant machinery refers to lipid storage in the form of TAGs (Tryacylglicerols) that indirectly reduces oxidative stress by maintaining membrane integrity. For the full model description and equations, we refer the reader to Leles and Levine (2023) and Leles et al. (2025).

We parameterized the model to represent the evolved Tt and Nt lines given their differences in heat‐tolerance. We assumed all parameters to be equal following Leles et al. (2025) except for the heat‐mitigation capacity of the cell (α) and the energetic cost associated to heat‐damage (e d). Specifically, the Tt line possesses higher heat‐mitigation capacity, which enables the cell to build more antioxidant machinery, and the cost associated with heat‐damage is higher for the Nt line relative to the Tt line (Table S3). While the higher heat‐mitigation capacity provides protection against oxidative stress, this strategy comes with a trade‐off requiring intracellular space and proteomic investment that divests resources away from protein synthesis and growth. To confirm that our parameter choices reflect the differences observed among the evolved lineages, we compared the emergent thermal growth curves, cell biovolume, and carbon to nitrogen quotas from our model against our physiological data under nitrogen‐replete conditions (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019). Additionally, we compared the simulations against the proteomic dataset qualitatively, based on the up‐regulation or down‐regulation of specific pathways. Finally, we applied the proteome model to contrast the thermal growth curves and metabolic responses of the Nt and Tt lines under nitrogen‐limited conditions. These results were then contextualized in light of our evolution experiment to explain why thermo‐tolerant lines did not evolve under heat‐stress (i.e., 31°C) and nitrogen limitation.

Under heat‐stress (at 31°C for Nt and 34°C for Tt), the model predicted that (Figure S1) the investments in C metabolism, glycolysis, and protein biosynthesis/ribosomes are higher for the Nt31 line relative to the Tt34 line, and that the Tt34 line invests more in light harvesting (Figure S1), which agrees with the empirical results described above when comparing Tt34 and Nt31 against Nt25. The model also predicted that the investment in N metabolism and N transporters is higher for the Nt31 line. In the empirical results, we didn't detect such an effect when comparing Nt31 against Tt34, but from the comparison with Nt25 we deduced that N metabolism was more underregulated in Tt34 than in Nt31.

–Model simulations under N limitation

Model simulations under N limitation

Under nitrogen limitation, the model predicts lower maximum growth rates and the flattening of the thermal growth curves relative to nitrogen‐replete conditions for both evolved lines (Figure 6). However, at the temperature in which the evolution experiment was performed (i.e., 31°C), the growth rate of the Nt line (1.03 day−1) is predicted to be higher than the growth rate of the Tt line (0.88 day−1) in our simulations. This is explained by the fact that the thermal‐tolerant strategy (Tt) requires more intracellular space dedicated to antioxidants, which results in larger cell sizes, to achieve higher thermal tolerance. However, under nitrogen‐limiting conditions, a cell must also maximize nutrient uptake efficiency, which requires smaller cell sizes. Thus, the Tt line achieved higher maximum growth temperature at the cost of growth, protein synthesis, and nitrogen uptake at 31°C while the Nt line showed a nitrogen‐efficient strategy which resulted in a growth advantage under heat‐stress.

Discussion

The results described make it clear that metabolic pathways differ between the non‐thermotolerant lines of Chaetoceros simplex and the thermotolerant lines when cells respond to heat stress. Here, we analyse how those pathways shape the cellular response as a whole, generating distinct cellular strategies, and how this may explain the reduction in adaptive potential observed when cells evolved under N‐limitation and heat stress. We end the discussion by describing the ecological implications of the shift from non‐thermotolerant to thermotolerant cells, with cellular strategies similar to those observed in this work.

–Acclimated non‐tolerant lines' response to thermal stress (Nt25–Nt31), a classical response

Acclimated non‐tolerant lines' response to thermal stress (Nt25–Nt31), a classical response

In this study, we observed in Nt31 the upregulation of chaperones, ROS protection mechanisms, and repair mechanisms, a trend that has been widely reported as a cellular response to temperature increase (Chakravarti et al. 2020; Liang et al. 2019; Tomanek 2011; Williams et al. 2011; Xu et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2022) enabling the cell to cope with malfunctions (due to misfolding or aggregation) of proteins and the accumulation of ROS caused by destabilization of the photosynthetic electron transport chain. This confirms that the cells are experiencing heat stress. The enrichment and upregulation of C metabolism processes as glycolysis, gluconeogenesis and the pentose phosphate pathway, combined with the enrichment of terms related to energy use and storage, suggest that the acclimation process in Nt31 is highly energy‐demanding. This observation agrees with previous work describing the upregulation of energy generation to cope with thermal stress (Bonora et al. 2012; Tomanek 2011; Xu et al. 2014). A cell needs to increase the ATP generation to manage the increase in investment in metabolic processes. This result also agrees with the upregulation of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, a pathway that releases stored energy in the cells and suggests the increase of respiration, as it has been previously described during thermal acclimation (Barton et al. 2020; Toseland et al. 2013).

Furthermore, the response of the C fixation and photosynthetic apparatus to heat stress is not universal in marine algae. Some studies have reported a decrease in photosynthesis and/or chlorophyll as a response to heat (Fan et al. 2018; Liang et al. 2019; Rhee and Gotham 1981; Xu et al. 2014), while others report an increase (Boyd et al. 2016; Raven and Geider 1988; Thrane et al. 2017) or a more complex response depending on the variable studied and temperature tested (Baker et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2022); results from the present work suggest the increase of cellular investment in both photosynthetic pigments and C fixation in non‐tolerant lines when acclimated to above‐optimal temperatures.

Additionally, the enriched terms in the protein metabolism category suggest that cellular investment in protein biosynthesis increased during the acclimation to high temperature in Nt lines. This increase in protein metabolism may result from the need for protein repair and replacement, as indicated by the enrichment of chaperones to support cell growth under stressful conditions. Moreover, the enrichment of terms related to ribosome constitution and protein catabolism suggests the concurrent increase of ribosome concentration as a response to heat (Fan et al. 2018) and the lysis of damaged proteins to protect the cell from its toxicity (Chakravarti et al. 2020). Likewise, the terms upregulated in the DNA–RNA category suggest the increase of cell investment in nucleic acid biosynthesis, which could be related to the increase in ribosomes.

In relation to fatty acids metabolism, the upregulation and enrichment of fatty acid biosynthesis indicate increased cell investment in lipids in the Nt31 line. A similar increase or the composition shift of cellular fatty acids as a response to thermal stress has been previously reported for algae (Chakravarti et al. 2020; Xing et al. 2018), including diatoms (Feijão et al. 2020; O'Donnell et al. 2019; Rousch et al. 2004). However, the observed trends are contradictory, with some papers reporting an increase in the total amount or synthesis of fatty acids (Rousch et al. 2004; Xing et al. 2018) or an increase in fatty acid abundance (Feijão et al. 2020), and others reporting catabolism (Chakravarti et al. 2020) under thermal stress. Whatever change is observed, it is usually hypothesized to be related to the cellular membrane composition. In our study, while we cannot determine the function of those new fatty acids with the available data, it might be linked to the observed enrichment of terms related to transport membranes that may be related to cell growth and duplication.

Looking at the response in N metabolism, the enriched terms in this category, which are upregulated in Nt31, suggest an increase in cellular investment in ammonia metabolism (probably coming from protein catabolism or photorespiration) and the reallocation of N for the synthesis of proteins (Allen et al. 2011; Guerra et al. 2013; Parker et al. 2008). However, those terms upregulated in Nt25 indicate the upregulation of cell investment in nitrate uptake and metabolism in Nt25 or, from another perspective, the downregulation of nitrate assimilation in Nt31. It has been described before that nitrate uptake decreases with temperature increase in diatoms (Lomas and Glibert 1999 and references therein; Parker and Armbrust 2005). Those studies hypothesize that diatoms upregulate nitrate metabolism as a dissipative pathway for the excess of energy generated at lower temperatures when light energy harvesting is unbalanced with carbon metabolism. Therefore, at higher temperatures, less diffusion is needed, and N metabolism is not upregulated. However, when above‐optimal temperatures are considered in marine diatoms, nitrate reductase activity (NR, the enzyme catalysing the first reaction in nitrate assimilation) decreases at higher temperatures (Gleich et al. 2020; Kristiansen 1983), consistent with a lower optimum temperature for NR activity (Rhee and Gotham 1981). At the same time, NR gene expression is higher (Gleich et al. 2020), probably compensating for reduced NR efficiency. Conversely, we found that at above‐optimum temperatures (for both NR activity and population line growth), NR abundance does not change significantly in the non‐tolerant population lines (NR is not among the significant proteins, Table S1). So, contrary to the findings of Gleich et al. (2020), the expression level of NR does not compensate for the NR efficacy reduction due to temperature. In addition, nitrate or nitrite transporters are downregulated. In summary, cell investment in nitrate uptake and assimilation decreased in Nt31 lines, and cells compensated for the decline of N from this source, increasing the reallocation of N from ammonia, probably to synthesize new components. The activation of ammonia recycling is a general response to a decrease in N availability in diatoms (Hockin et al. 2012; Remmers et al. 2018; Scarsini et al. 2022).

Summing up, in Nt31, we observed a classical short‐term (acclimation) thermal stress response with increased energy demand to sustain the upregulation of protein synthesis, C fixation, and cellular protection mechanisms. But we also found the downregulation of nitrate assimilation under stressful temperature conditions, probably due to lesser efficacy of the NR enzyme at above‐optimal temperature, and the upregulation of ammonia metabolism, probably as a strategy to provide N to the highly demanding biosynthesis of proteins and photosynthetic pigments.

–Acclimated thermo‐tolerant lines' response to thermal stress (Nt25–Tt34) and its comparison with nontolerant lines

Acclimated thermo‐tolerant lines' response to thermal stress (Nt25–Tt34) and its comparison with nontolerant lines

The enriched GO terms when comparing the Nt25 and Tt34 responses reveal the acclimated thermal response of the thermo‐tolerant lines, resulting from both acclimation and adaptation of the Tt lines (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019; Collins et al. 2020).

As described before for the Nt31 lines, enrichment in energy and respiration categories suggests the increase of cellular energy demand when Tt34 lines are under thermal stress, and similarly to our observations in Nt31 response, thermal acclimation involves increased protein biosynthesis in Tt34 lines. However, the simultaneous enrichment of protein catabolism suggests the recycling of damaged proteins to obtain amino acids. The acclimated response of Tt34 to the above‐optimum temperature did not show the enrichment of ribosome‐related GO terms described for Nt31. Eight ribosomal proteins were significantly upregulated for Tt34 compared to Nt25 (Table S1); however, there were 50 ribosomal proteins upregulated in Nt31. From those, only six were also significant for Tt34 (the mean p value for the others is 0.85 ± 0.05 standard error). Therefore, contrary to what we observed in the non‐tolerant line, the increase of ribosomes is not a primary cellular response to heat stress for thermo‐tolerant lines.

Enriched terms related to stress response suggest that Tt34 lines activate chaperone proteins to ensure the correct protein's function during the heat stress, a commonly observed cellular response to heat stress (Shi et al. 2017; Tomanek 2011; Williams et al. 2011). On the contrary, several terms related to ROS protection that were enriched and upregulated for Nt31 were not enriched in Tt34 compared to Nt25 (Table S2). However, some proteins related to those GO terms are significantly upregulated in Tt34 (Table S1). Therefore, there was an increase in ROS protection for Tt34 during acclimation to stressful high temperatures, but such an effect is more pronounced in Nt31 than in Tt34.

We observed similar trends for other functions, such as C metabolism or photosynthesis, suggesting an increase in cellular investment in these processes, though less than in Nt31 lines. For example, there were only four C metabolism enriched GO terms upregulated for Tt34, compared to the 17 in Nt31 for this same category and the enriched and upregulated GO terms from the photosynthesis category in Tt34 lines suggest the increase of cellular investment in light harvesting and C fixation, but not as much for photosynthetic pigments synthesis as in Nt31.

Regarding fatty acids metabolism, the described enriched terms imply an increase of cellular investment in fatty acid catabolism and desaturation, contributing to cellular respiration (Ghisla and Thorpe 2004). Previous observations in high temperature‐adapted diatoms showed that thermo‐resistant species had higher fatty acid content, and the synthesis of fatty acids increased more when exposed to thermal stress than in non‐thermo‐tolerant species (Rousch et al. 2004). Our results did not suggest this same trend; on the contrary, they suggest an increase of fatty acid biosynthesis in the non‐tolerant lines under thermal stress but an increase of catabolism in the thermo‐tolerant lines.

There were also responses in the cellular transport and cell division GO terms that we didn't detect for the Nt31. Transport GO terms are related to transmembrane transport and the activity of the endoplasmic reticulum, suggesting the increase of intracellular reallocation of compounds. The two terms enriched in the cell organization category describe the constitution of cellular structures like membranes, microtubules, or cytoplasm. The upregulation and enrichment of these terms can be related to different processes, like membrane organization for cell division or the cytoskeleton reorganization process previously reported for other organisms during thermal stress (Chakravarti et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2014). In addition, the purine‐nucleoside phosphorylase activity enrichment suggests increased cellular investment in nucleic acid biosynthesis, possibly related to cell division and line growth.

Concerning the N metabolism, on the one side, there is only one GO term enriched and upregulated for Tt34: agmatinase activity. This term suggests, similarly to Nt31, the increase of cellular investment in ammonia metabolism. On the other side, eight GO terms in this category are enriched and upregulated in Nt25 (Table S2), suggesting a strong effect of thermal stress on the downregulation of cellular investment in nitrate metabolism in the thermo‐tolerant lines. In this case, thermal stress affects not only the transporters but also the nitrate reductase (significantly more abundant in Nt25, Table S1), so nitrate assimilation seems more inhibited for thermal‐adapted lines.

In summary, as in non‐tolerant lines, the acclimation of the thermo‐tolerant lines to thermal stress implies the upregulation of core metabolic pathways such as protein metabolism, photosynthesis, and respiration, and the increase of cellular protection mechanisms and energy metabolism to supply the increased cells' demand. However, several differences suggest divergent cellular strategies to cope with thermal stress. Non‐tolerant lines invest more in ribosomes, photosynthetic pigments, carbon fixation, and fatty acid biosynthesis, prioritizing growth and cell division. Thermo‐tolerant lines, however, invest less in photosynthetic pigments and C fixation, probably reducing ROS generation, which allows reducing the expense of ROS protection. At the same time, instead of increasing ribosomes, the increase of proteins and fatty acid catabolism and transport indicates higher recycling activity and C and N reallocation in Tt34 lines. Surprisingly, both lines downregulated nitrate metabolism, but the inhibition seems stronger in thermo‐tolerant ones. However, lower investment in pigments, ribosomes, and protein biosynthesis (all rich in N) in the thermo‐tolerant lines would reduce the cellular N demand, allowing the cells to cope with lower N assimilation.

–Thermo‐tolerant lines' adaptive response to thermal stress (Nt31–Tt34)

Thermo‐tolerant lines' adaptive response to thermal stress (Nt31–Tt34)

The enriched GO terms in the comparison between Nt31 and Tt34 reveal the processes that differentiate the acclimated response of the non‐tolerant and thermo‐tolerant lines to temperature stressful conditions, that is, the result of the evolutionary adaptation to thermal stress.

One can think that the disparities between Nt31 and Tt34 could come from other reasons apart from the inherent ability of Tt lines to tolerate high temperatures, possibly due to different temperatures affecting enzyme dynamics, for example. If that were the case, we would expect a similar response from both lines at 34°C. However, Nt doesn't survive at such a temperature, but Tt does, so the direct effect of temperature increase is insufficient to explain our observations. Another possible explanation for the differences found is that 34°C is more stressful for Tt lines than 31°C for Nt lines. However, both temperatures are at a similar thermal distance (6°C for Nt and 5°C for Tt) from the corresponding optimum temperatures (25°C for Nt and 29.2°C for Tt, Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019). Therefore, in any case, stress levels may be higher for Nt31 than Tt34, but growth rates are comparable, being slightly higher for Nt31 (Nt31 = 1.1 ± 0.01; Tt34 = 0.9 ± 0.01 growth rate ± standard error; from figure 1.c in Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019). Therefore, we assume that the differences observed between Nt31 and Tt34 come from the inherent ability of Tt lines to tolerate high temperatures.

The GO terms enriched in the C metabolism, respiration, and energy categories are upregulated in Nt31, suggesting higher upregulation of cellular investment in those pathways in Nt, probably to cope with the higher demand of energy and C skeletons from the active metabolism in Nt31. The energy cost for the response to thermal stress in the Nt lines may be higher than for Tt lines. In addition, as was expected from the results described above, the GO terms related to protein biosynthesis and ribosomes were upregulated in Nt31. This difference confirms that the response to high temperatures in Nt31 implies higher cellular investment in protein synthesis and ribosomes than in Tt34. The upregulation of photosynthesis‐related terms agrees with the increase in photosynthetic pigment biosynthesis described in Nt31, and there is one GO term enriched and upregulated in Nt31 for the DNA–RNA category, suggesting higher investment in cell division by non‐tolerant lines.

This comparison confirms the main differences between the two cellular strategies described above: (1) non‐thermo‐tolerant lines invest more in growth, upregulating the proteins and the photosynthetic structures biosynthesis and increasing C fixation and respiration to satisfy higher energy demand. Upregulation of chaperones and ROS protection is needed to maintain the enhanced cell's metabolism under thermal stress. (2) The thermo‐tolerant lines invest more in recycling and protein repair or protection than new structures. They increase C fixation, but not so much to deal with excessive ROS or to need so much energy. It suggests a more conservative strategy that prioritizes survival over rapid growth.

The remaining question is: why does this cellular strategy, prioritizing survival over rapid growth, allow Tt population lines to survive at higher temperatures? It has been demonstrated that warming increases the minimum N requirement (N*), especially at above‐optimal temperatures (Lewington‐Pearce et al. 2019; Thomas et al. 2017), but at the same time, according to our results and previous observations (Lomas and Glibert 1999 and references therein; Parker and Armbrust 2005), it reduces N uptake. In such a context, the N‐conservation strategy in the thermo‐tolerant lines (prioritizing recycling over new structures biosynthesis and more N‐demanding processes) may allow them to maintain cellular functioning at higher temperatures, promoting N recycling and reducing highly N‐consuming processes.

–Adaptation to high temperatures under low nitrate availability

Adaptation to high temperatures under low nitrate availability

One of the most exciting results of our evolution experiment (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019) was that thermo‐tolerant lines did not persist when lines were evolving under low nitrate availability.

According to our proteomic analysis, the strategy of the non‐tolerant lines was to invest in protection and growth. In contrast, thermo‐tolerant lines activated more recycling pathways and downregulated metabolism, reducing oxidative stress. Those results would suggest that thermo‐tolerant lines may be better competitors at low N availability conditions, thanks to the activation of recycling pathways that reduce the need for N incorporation. However, we need to also consider the cellular response to N deprivation, which would interact with the thermal response at 31°C.

To explore such interaction, we applied a proteome allocation model to simulate the thermal growth curves and the metabolic responses of the Tt and Nt lines under nitrogen‐limited conditions (Figure 6). In the model, the Tt strategy requires more antioxidants in the form of lipid storage inside the cell to mitigate heat‐damage. In fact, previous proteomic analyses showed that, under heat stress, phytoplankton must rearrange the membrane composition by synthesizing de novo saturated lipids and sequestering unsaturated lipids in the form of triglycerides (TAGs) inside the cell, which acts as a sink for reactive oxygen species (Hemme et al. 2014). This activity would agree with the enrichment of fatty acid catalysis described for Tt34. However, a higher concentration of lipid storage results in larger cell sizes, which decreases the nutrient uptake efficiency of the cell that is inversely proportional to cell size (Leles et al. 2025).

In turn, the modelled Nt line invests less in storing TAGs, which come at the cost of survival at extreme heat stress, but allows this line to achieve higher nutrient uptake efficiency under heat stress by requiring smaller cell sizes, ultimately resulting in higher growth rate at the evolved experimental temperature (31°C). These model predictions could explain why during the high‐temperature evolution experiment under low N concentrations, the Tt either didn't arise or did not persist, while becoming frequent at the same temperature but high N concentration (Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019).

–Ecological implications

Ecological implications

As described above, adaptation to higher temperatures involves core metabolic pathways that drive the primary cellular response to temperature and other environmental factor changes. From our observations, temperature increase due to climate change may allow the coexistence of lines with different metabolic strategies, especially in natural environments where nutrient availability fluctuates. This is unsurprising, as high intraspecific diversity has been described for marine diatom species (Godhe and Rynearson 2017; Rynearson and Armbrust 2000, 2005; Sjöqvist 2022). However, according to our results, only some strains will cope well with future marine heat waves, events of extremely high temperature that are expected to increase in frequency, duration, and maximum temperatures reached (Frölicher et al. 2018). Those extreme thermal conditions will favour the most thermo‐tolerant strains, whose cellular strategies may be similar to the one described for the thermo‐tolerant lines in this study. The increase in the abundance of cells with a more conservative strategy (like the one in our thermo‐tolerant lines) may restrict the biomass produced by the diatom blooms. Even with high nitrogen availability, thermo‐tolerant lines reduce the upregulation of protein biosynthesis and carbon fixation in response to elevated temperatures compared to thermotolerant lines. In addition, the C:N ratio of thermotolerant lines would be lower (consistent with previous C:N ratio measurements, Aranguren‐Gassis et al. 2019); thus, less biomass with different nutritional value would be available for upper trophic levels (John and Davidson 2001).

We realize that generalizations from the current results may not be possible. Limitations come from several aspects: first, we have studied one species from warm waters, and the thermal response may be species‐ or strain‐specific (Aranguren‐Gassis and Litchman 2020). Second, this work is based on evolution from a very limited standing variation, so other adaptations may evolve in natural populations. Third, as in many proteomics analyses of non‐model organisms, only a portion of existing proteins were identified, and among them some have unknown function, resulting in a significant loss of information, and a complete map of the metabolic pathways does not exist. However, we can still derive conclusions that could motivate future research. Even though a generalization from our laboratory experiments may not be straightforward, our findings reveal successful cellular strategies that warrant further consideration and analysis. This will facilitate better predictions of phytoplankton responses to warming and other stressors in the near future.

In conclusion, evolutionary adaptation may ensure the persistence of marine diatom species, but the resulting cellular strategies can alter the ecological function of those species. Moreover, the interaction of diverse environmental selective factors would influence the adaptation outcome and may limit the potential to adapt to extreme temperatures.

Table S1: Compilation of the p values corrected for multiple comparisons with five different methods and the q‐value for each protein. The red colour indicates p < 0.05. The proteinAccession is the identification code for each protein; Description is the descriptive identification of each protein; ttest_pvals are the original p values without correction; Bisnomial_SGoF is the Binomial SGoF method (Carvajal Rodríguez et al. 2009); Conservative_SGoF is the conservative SGoF method (de Uña Álvarez 2011); BH is the Benjamini & Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995); SFisher is the sequential Fisher method (Diz et al. 2011); and Bonferroni is the sequential Bonferroni method (Holm 1979); q_val is the q value. The original data can be downloaded from the repository specified in the main text of the paper (files Multiple_comparison_p_C25vsC31_Descr_Smallest.csv, Multiple_comparison_p_C25vsE34_Descr_Smallest.csv, and Multiple_comparison_p_C31vsE34_Descr_Smallest.csv).

Table S2: List of all the enriched GO terms for the three comparisons (Nt25 Nt31, Nt25‐Tt34, and Nt31‐Tt34) with their corresponding functional category. For each term at each comparison, the populations with more than 75% of proteins upregulated are indicated with the name of the population and the colour: Nt25 (dark blue) is non‐tolerant at 25°C, Nt31 (light blue) is non‐tolerant at 31°C, and Tt34 (red) is thermo‐tolerant at 34°C. None indicates that none of the populations for the corresponding comparison has more than 75% of the proteins upregulated for the GO term. C means carbon and N means nitrogen. The original data can be downloaded from the repository specified in the main text of the paper (file: Table_PAPER_GOupregulated_format.xlsx).
