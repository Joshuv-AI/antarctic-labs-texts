Improving vaccine communication to the often-overlooked young adult population: A qualitative study of 20–29 year olds in British Columbia, Canada
Authors: Gurvir S Dhutt, Julie A Bettinger, Devon Greyson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYoung adults in their 20s have shown slower uptake of COVID-19 vaccines relative to older adults, potentially endangering themselves and their communities. Despite this, little vaccine communication has specifically targeted this age group. This study explored why “20-somethings” in British Columbia (BC), Canada delayed COVID-19 vaccination, and how to better encourage their vaccine uptake. From August 2022 to March 2023, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 25 young adults aged 20–29 years living in southwest BC. Interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis. Young adults attributed slower vaccine uptake among their demographic to the perceived lower risks to young and healthy individuals not justifying the effort involved in obtaining vaccination and to lack of trust in the vaccines’ safety and effectiveness. To address these factors, participants recommended that vaccine communications attract and maintain young adults’ attention, take advantage of the affordances of social media platforms, be clear about both individual advantages and social responsibility to vaccinate, and provide a way to take immediate action. Salient messengers were those deemed credible based on education/credentials or familiarity/similarity to the audience. Marginalized sub-communities may be best reached by health messengers who are already trusted community members. Tailoring vaccine communication to a young adult demographic and using trusted and relatable messengers to deliver vaccine information may facilitate swifter uptake of vaccination in settings with high access but lagging uptake.KEYWORDS: COVID-19, vaccination, vaccine uptake, vaccine hesitancy, message framing, social media, youth

Introduction

COVID-19 vaccines have been shown to protect against transmission and infection, as well as severe disease and death.1,2 However, even in regions such as Canada with high access to vaccination services, uptake has differed based on characteristics such as age and geographic residence. One way this was evident was the slower uptake of COVID-19 vaccines among younger adults compared to adults aged 30 or above.3 Relative to all adults (age 18+), individuals aged 18–29 years old in Canada showed slower uptake of the initial COVID-19 vaccine series, and consistently lower receipt of subsequent recommended doses.4 Similarly, among pregnant people, those under 25 years old had lower uptake of COVID-19 vaccination than comparators aged 25–44.5 This phenomenon is not limited to COVID-19 vaccines, as younger age has also been found to be a predictor of lower vaccine uptake in the context of both tetanus, diphtheria, and acellular pertussis (Tdap) and influenza vaccination during pregnancy, raising questions about how to effectively communicate about vaccinations to this age demographic.6,7

Outside pandemic circumstances, vaccination is not something most young adults in Canada think about. Canadian young adults in their 20s are beyond the routine childhood/adolescent vaccination schedule, yet decades before eligibility for vaccines aimed at older adults (e.g., those against shingles, pneumonia). In British Columbia, seasonal influenza vaccination was not part of the publicly funded vaccine program for most young, healthy adults until the 2021–22 flu season, and even with no-cost vaccination <40% of all adults aged 18–64 without chronic medical conditions receive an influenza vaccine.8 However, the average age at which British Columbians give birth to their first child is 31.6 years,9 meaning many individuals in their twenties are planning or starting families and therefore approaching or making crucial decisions about vaccination during pregnancy and for infants. It is likely that establishing a strong and positive relationship between 20-somethings and vaccination could improve vaccine uptake in pregnancy and beyond.

Prior research has found that, much like older adults, young adults’ understanding of vaccine safety, effectiveness, and overall importance influences their vaccine uptake,10 along with issues of access and convenience.11 However, much of the literature exploring vaccine intentions and vaccine communication among younger adult populations is limited to university students, and to date has largely been quantitative in nature.12–22 Exploring the nuances of vaccine intentions and hesitations within broader young adult populations is important, since these can differ based on geographical, cultural, and other contextually dependent factors.10,23–28 Similar to older populations, factors that have been found to be statistically associated with vaccine hesitancy among young adult populations include questions about vaccine efficacy,18,20,29–32 negative attitude toward vaccines (e.g., belief vaccination is unnecessary),19,20,30,31,33,34 lack of trust,19,30,32,33 concern over side effects,18,21,22,29,30,32–37 and believing the vaccine approval process was rushed (and vaccination may therefore be unsafe).19,22,30 It is important to triangulate and explain these findings using in-depth and context-sensitive qualitative methods to understand the “how” and “why” behind these vaccine intentions and uptake, and how improved vaccine communication to young adults can address this.

To fill gaps in our knowledge about how to improve young adult vaccine uptake, this study aimed to provide insight into the reasons why young adults aged 20–29 had slower uptake of COVID-19 vaccination than older adults; and explore what information sources, messengers, or modes of distribution of information about vaccines should be used by public health to reach this age cohort, and subgroups therein. Additionally, we probed how past history of vaccination contributed to current vaccine uptake decisions and how COVID-19 vaccination related to young adults’ thinking about vaccinations during pregnancy and infant/child vaccination.

Materials and methods

This exploratory qualitative study applied reflexive thematic analysis38 to interviews with young adults aged 20–29 years old to explore reasons for slower uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine among this demographic. The research team consisted of two young adult research assistants and two investigators, one of whom has advanced qualitative research experience. The study was conducted in partnership with the Island Health Authority and approved in Harmonized Review (multi-jurisdictional review among participating institutions in the province) by the UBC Children’s and Women’s Research Ethics Board and Island Health (VIHA) (H22–00556).

Study setting

Canada has a publicly funded health-care system that provides essential health services including vaccinations to residents. Provincial and territorial governments are responsible for administration of most of the health care delivered to the Canadian population,39 including vaccines. In Canada, COVID-19 vaccination began in December, 2020, with over 100,000,000 doses administered as of June 2024.40 The province of British Columbia’s COVID-19 immunization plan rolled out in phases, with vaccination offered to high-risk populations first (e.g., medical workers, older people, and those with underlying health conditions or other risk factors).41 The province employed a “delayed second dose” strategy, lengthening the interval between first and second doses to 4 months in order to provide first doses to all eligible adults by July.42 Young adults in their 20s with no prioritized risk factors were eligible to receive their first COVID-19 vaccine doses in BC starting in May. Vaccinees were entered into a provincial immunization database to receive invitations for future doses when they became eligible.

The initial 2020–2021 two-dose vaccination program used mRNA vaccines from Pfizer and Moderna, as well as limited use of the recombinant viral vector Oxford-AstraZeneca vaccine. Subsequent years’ COVID-19 vaccination (e.g., to protect against additional strains) have used mRNA vaccines, and Novavax’s recombinant protein vaccine on a more limited basis. While large cities delivered many initial COVID-19 vaccine doses in large, temporary, mass vaccination clinics, COVID-19 vaccinations have been distributed at various sites such as hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, community vaccination centers, and mobile vaccination clinics. Starting in September, 2021, a proof-of-vaccination credential, or “vaccine passport,” system was temporarily used in BC to limit access to non-essential venues such as restaurants and large sporting events to vaccinated attendees.43

British Columbia’s health services are administered by Health Authorities, one of which is Island Health, covering the geographic region in the province’s southwest including Vancouver Island (home of the capital city, Victoria, as well as smaller cities, suburbs, and rural regions), the smaller neighboring Gulf Islands, and specific rural areas along the coast of mainland British Columbia.44 This health authority serves approximately 885,000 individuals.44 Young adults in Island Health showed lower uptake of COVID-19 vaccination compared with older adults, and slower uptake compared with adults the same age in the nearby Vancouver Coastal Health region,45 therefore they were selected as the population for this study.

Participant sampling and recruitment

Young adults aged 20–29 years old living in the Island Health region of British Columbia were purposively sampled using a maximal diversity approach through a combination of online advertising and promotion of the study to community groups serving young adult subpopulations. We deliberately sought diversity on factors previously identified to be associated with vaccine confidence, including ethnicity, education, chronic health conditions, parental status, immigration status, and gender, as well as diversity of geography within the health region.

We recruited participants through a combination of online advertising and promotion of the study to community groups serving young adult subpopulations. Online advertising included targeted social media advertising to specific populations based on geographic region, ethnicity, caregiving responsibilities, and gender. Community promotion included contacting people registered with the Island Health research volunteer database and advertising through postsecondary institutions, hospitals, community organizations, and public health units. Prospective participants completed an online response form with questions about their demographic characteristics, COVID-19 vaccination status, and vaccine perspectives. This response form was used to screen potential participants for eligibility and eliminate likely-fraudulent responses. Our purposive sampling strategy was employed to identify diverse participants (e.g., by age, ethnicity, geography, occupation, parenting status) from among the online responses.

Data collection

Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted via online videoconference (Zoom) or telephone by one of two young adult interviewers, GD (male, graduate student) and MO (female, research staff). An exploratory interview guide was developed around the research questions and not based on one a priori theory, and interviewers probed for further information from participants in an individualized way based on their stories. All participants provided written informed consent, verified consent verbally at interviews, and were offered a $40 gift card honorarium. Interviews were 45–60 minutes long, audio recorded, and transcribed using an automated service (Otter). Staff checked transcripts for accuracy, de-identified them, and assigned pseudonyms. Participants were invited to review their transcripts for accuracy and privacy. Data collection was stopped when interviewers, in conversation with coauthors DG and JB, perceived they did not hear anything new after three consecutive interviews, which we used as indication of data saturation.46–48

Data analysis

Transcripts were imported into NVivo software to facilitate coding. Analysis consisted of two-stages, deductive coding followed by inductive thematic analysis, and was conducted by the lead author (GD) in conversation with coauthors. First, we (GD) applied a deductive codebook that our research group had used in other vaccine hesitancy studies to index the data. The coded extracts were discussed and reviewed with co-investigators (DG, JAB) on multiple occasions as analysis progressed. Following deductive coding, transcripts were analyzed inductively using reflexive thematic analysis38 focusing on four key sensitizing topics informed by the overarching objectives of the study. These sensitizing topics were as follows: (1) vaccine uptake decisions; (2) attributes of influential information; (3) past histories of vaccination; (4) future vaccination thoughts or plans. An inductive codebook was developed, and codes were iteratively added, grouped, split, and revised throughout the second stage of the analysis. GD employed reflexive journaling to detail his impressions, feelings, questions, possible new themes, recurring themes, etc. after coding each transcript. The inductive thematic analysis codebook, relevant code extracts, and themes were discussed with the co-investigators (DG and JAB) on multiple occasions as the second stage of the analysis progressed.

Results

From August 2022 to March 2023, 25 eligible participants were interviewed. Table 1 outlines the demographic characteristics of the participants interviewed. Mean participant age was 23.9 years (median 24). Compared with all young adults in the region, our sample had a greater proportion of women (68% vs 49%) and visible minorities (44% vs. 11.4%; possibly due to our purposive sampling strategy and the visible minority ethnicities of staff conducting recruitment and interviews) but had a similar distribution of formal education level.49 Five participants (20%) identified as parents, of one child each. Two additional participants described living with one child in their household for which they were not a parent or guardian, and one lived with multiple children. Nine (36%) reported having been diagnosed with chronic health conditions such as insomnia, social anxiety, depression, or fibromyalgia.

“The advantage that it would give me maybe isn’t very big”: perceived minimal advantages and lack of trust

Two interrelated themes were identified to explain slower uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine within the young adult demographic, addressing our first objective: insufficient justification for vaccination and mistrust in the vaccines. Together, these created a lack of urgency and importance for vaccination against COVID-19, and especially for healthy young adults to receive doses beyond the initial series.

Participants suggested the perceived risks of COVID-19 for younger adults did not justify the effort needed to get vaccinated, especially to receive doses beyond the initial series. They described a belief that vaccination was important for older adults and immunocompromised individuals, but both perceived need and urgency were lower for healthy, younger adults.

I think, personally, for the elderly, I think that all of them should have to get [vaccinated], and people that are compromised with their immunity […] I think its probably a good idea for them to get the booster. But I think for someone like myself who is very healthy and doesnt have any underlying conditions, I dont think I really need to get a booster. (Dayna, 21 years old)

Some recalled adverse reactions following COVID-19 vaccination, weighing the consequences of feeling ill including potentially needing time off from work or school, against this perceived lack of personal benefit from vaccination.

[H]onestly, I didnt get the fourth booster. Because I feel like the advantage that would give me maybe isn’t very big in terms of resisting COVID. And when I got the [previous] shots it made me quite sick afterwards for a few days. And I didnt feel like being sick for a few days if it wasnt really going to help me stay safe from getting COVID. (Ben, 29 years old)

Despite the general pro-vaccination attitudes reported by this study sample, participants expressed some lack of trust in the COVID-19 vaccines being offered. A major reason cited for this mistrust was concern about safety, including questions about vaccine ingredients and associated adverse effects. However, distrust in the government was also identified as a contributor to lack of trust in vaccination. One participant described how the different roll out of COVID-19 vaccines to Indigenous groups compared with the rest of the population contributed to skepticism and hesitancy among her Indigenous family members.

Especially in relation to First Nations, the government lies all the time. So, if theyre like, “Oh, were giving [this brand] to you because its the most available.” [People wonder] are you giving it to them because you dont trust it and you dont want to use it on [the rest of the population] first? (April, 25 years old)

While experiencing mild COVID-19 disease reduced the sense of urgency for vaccination, getting sick despite being vaccinated led to mistrust because the vaccine effectiveness did not meet participants’ expectations of disease prevention. For example, Dayna (21 years old) told us, “I have had COVID two times after being fully vaccinated. So, I had a feeling that it really wasn’t doing much at all.”

Communicating with young adults

Participants stressed the importance of improving vaccine communication specifically targeting young adults and subcommunities therein. To address their feelings of lack of urgency and distrust, as well as COVID-related frustration and burnout, participants made specific recommendations for effective communication strategies for public health, particularly regarding the content conveyed and the messengers delivering it.

“Something that would grab attention”: recommendations for message framing and content

Participants emphasized the importance of ensuring messaging attracts and keeps the reader’s attention – especially on social media where, otherwise, users simply continue scrolling. Although there was general agreement on the need to “hook” a user, suggestions regarding how to do this varied, and included using jarring statistics as cues to action, emphasizing benefits to others as well as themselves, debunking myths, and highlighting concrete incentives to vaccination. Calvin (24 years old) suggested using relevant statistics to spur action among those who had not been vaccinated, describing a recent experience with this approach:

Something that would grab [attention] would be something like […] “Only this percentage have had the booster,” you know, “sign up here to do yours.” I was in [the motor vehicle registration office] the other day, and there was an ad [saying] only 20% of people have signed up for their decision on organ transplants. And thats like, “Oh, yeah, you know, 20%–dang, I haven’t either! I should get on that.” So like, putting a number, like a statistic, you know, urging people to get it done. It worked for me.

Participants suggested making the advantages of vaccination – including the indirect benefits provided to others – very clear. Emphasizing young adults’ role in the collective responsibility of vaccination was described as important framing:

I feel like we all had a personal connection. Like, you know, I’m doing this for my grandparents. I’m doing this so I can get back to work. I’m doing this so that I dont get sick, or my family or something. It definitely felt like a duty, but it wasn’t like we were doing it reluctantly, it was like, okay, I’m motivated. (Megan, 22 years old)

Clarifying that vaccination would help not only the young adults themselves, but also their more vulnerable loved ones and their entire community was suggested to potentially combat the perception that vaccination was of low value to healthy, younger adults.

Some more controversial suggestions for message content and framing included trying to correct misinformation and emphasizing incentives beyond disease prevention. Clementine (21 years old) described how a misinformation-debunking approach might hook a reader or viewer’s attention:

The biggest issue is [that the] easy, accessible information is not always the most accurate information. I think the best way to go about that would be to state a bunch of common misconceptions […] and then saying that thats not the truth. I think that kind of captures peoples attention more than just like a poster with a bunch of facts on it does. And then [it should have] a reference at the bottom or something to, like, credit, what they’re reading, because I think that is the biggest problem: weeding out misinformation with the truth.

A contrasting recommendation was to rely less on providing vaccine information and more on clearly outlining incentives for getting vaccinated at the start of the post.

I think people in their twenties always want something […] like with an incentive or food maybe or […] free services. (Vivian, 24 years old)

”No one my age really watches the news”: the influence of social media affordances

Younger adults’ attention and expectations were heavily shaped by the norms and affordances of social media. As April (25 years old) explained:

No one my age really watches the news. But then from that [televised] newscast, they usually make like … quick sheets … [highlighting the] entire report … like colorful, fast, quick, fact sheet or whatever. And then I’ll see those on social media.

Social media messaging tends to rely on visuals and minimal words compared with traditional public health communications such as brochures or even broadcast news. Participants recommended that vaccine promotion to their demographic keep the wordiness of posts to a minimum and makes sure videos being posted on social media platforms are short and to the point (e.g., under 15 seconds long).

[Younger adults] tend to like the short videos [and] some sort of post that has minimal information and text on it, just enough to get the idea across, not one of those big, long, heavy text ones that people read two words, then give up on … something thats eye catching, so people dont just scroll right past it. (Emma, 23 years old)

Interviewees underscored the need to provide the key message quickly and keep the reader’s attention throughout the entirety of the messaging being communicated, as failure to do so may result in the intended recipient losing interest before getting to the important part.

Capitalizing on the affordances of online media, it is possible – and indeed was recommended by participants – to provide an immediate and direct way for the message recipient to act. Steve (24 years old), for example, suggested “providing a direct way to take action … like a QR code that leads you to register for a vaccine, or a link that you can click.” On a highly visual platform such as Instagram, it was suggested that a post should be highly visual and include an attention-grabbing statement, quick and short explanation, and an actionable link to book a vaccination appointment (or to a list of local locations where one could walk in and receive vaccination). Unlike traditional news or informational media, social media can be more interactive, and vaccine communication to young adults who are fluent in online navigation should take advantage of the potential not only to inform but empower audiences to act.

“Make sure it’s not going to be detrimental down the line”: use of appropriate messengers

Participants preferred to get their information from a trusted source, be an individual or organization. However, what defined a trusted source or spokesperson was described differently by participants and was heavily dependent on an individual’s context and lived experiences. Many stressed that education or credentials were an important factor contributing to trustworthiness. For members of marginalized groups, authoritative government or medical sources could hold less weight, however, than sources from within their own communities, with experiential expertise and shared values. The use of celebrity or “influencer” messengers was endorsed by some participants as highly engaging, but cautioned against by others, underscoring yet again the importance of targeting subpopulations with different communication approaches.

Health-care providers, such as physicians, were frequently cited as trusted spokespersons, due to the widespread belief that health-care providers were knowledgeable and experienced about health. Many participants described getting information from what Brittany (24 years old) called “a verified account … like Island Health or Government of BC.” Similarly, the provincial Public Health Officer, who appeared frequently on televised and streamed pandemic updates, was considered trustworthy because of the position as a physician as well a government spokesperson. Some participants, on the other hand, shared that personal or group experiences of exclusion or marginalization had led to distrust of government or medicine and described placing more trust in fellow community members, emphasizing the credibility of those considered “relatable” or like themselves. This was highlighted by Steve (24 years old), a transgender man, who discussed the importance of transgender messengers for community members.

There arent a ton of [transgender specific] resources … and so for people who knew that I was trans and had gone through some of those things they [would often ask] “Hey, can you tell me about your experience?” And I would say that that kind of healthcare related networking is very common within trans communities.

One participant, who lived in an Indigenous community, discussed the importance of having Indigenous leaders communicating with Indigenous communities. This was because not only were these leaders seen as reputable sources, but because Indigenous community members had lower levels of trust in the government and health authorities due to historical traumatic experiences with the healthcare system (e.g., the colonial Indian Hospitals and Indian Residential Schools in Canada).50,51 Having an Indigenous community leader endorse health messaging served to increase trust in the information campaign.

In our community, whenever the Health Authority kind of bands together with our [Indigenous community], that kind of makes an impact, because they have a pretty strong impact and rapport with the community. So they kind of trust what their Band says to do … like, when they said to [get vaccinated], they all did it. (April, 25 years old)

Social media influencers or celebrities were brought up by several participants as examples of messengers they perceived as good information sources. However, some, such as Ashley (22 years old), warned “to be very careful” regarding such partnerships. This is because, although these individuals may have a large audience in the young adult population, collaborating with influencers with problematic pasts or agendas of their own could potentially be damaging for a vaccination campaign. Ashley suggested when considering partnerships with social media influencers, public health should, “[m]ake sure it’s not going to be detrimental down the line” by looking into the influencers’ pasts and “making sure that the influencer’s beliefs do kind of line up with [public health].”

Discussion

Young adults reported that their age group’s lower and slower uptake of COVID-19 vaccination compared with older adults was related to a perception of insufficient justification for vaccination among young, healthy adults and lack of trust in information sources and the vaccines themselves. Participants stressed the importance of improving the framing and content of vaccine communication, making better use of social media affordances, and selecting appropriate messengers to reach specific communities. Specifically, participants suggested the content be concise, attractive, and clear about both personal benefits and social responsibility of vaccination and, importantly, provide a direct way to act upon the information provided. They suggested identifying and partnering with both expert and relatable messengers who have existing relationships of trust with undervaccinated communities.

The findings from this study support and add specificity to previous literature exploring vaccination perspectives and uptake among the young adult population. Previous research has similarly found that concerns about vaccine safety and effectiveness,10,18,20,29,30,32 general lack of trust in vaccines,19,30,32,33 and having negative vaccine attitudes such as believing vaccination is unnecessary19,20,30,31,33,34 impact vaccine uptake among young adults. Prior literature has also concluded that young adults view themselves as being generally healthier34 and living busier lifestyles52 than older adults, while at the same time lacking health-care access.53 Our participants similarly described a self-perception as healthy and busy as rationale for the perceived risks of COVID-19 not justifying the effort needed to get vaccinated, with concerns about safety and effectiveness being present but a lesser issue. However, trust in government and medicine for historically marginalized communities was raised as an important barrier to vaccine uptake.

Our findings around a perceived lack of urgency resulting from an insufficient justification for vaccination and general lack of trust support the idea unvaccinated or undervaccinated young adults may often be more “vaccine apathetic” than antivaccine.54 To overcome this complacency, outlining the collective responsibility of young adults in vaccination campaigns, as suggested by participants, holds promise for increasing vaccine uptake. This finding is supported by previous literature which suggests that conveying the personal benefits and the social responsibility of vaccination encourages uptake of vaccines among young adults.55 However, there is also prior research concluding that loss-framed messaging, highlighting social norms and risks, may be effective with young adults,56–58 especially those classified as being avoidance-oriented, whose motivation stems from fear of negative outcomes.59,60 These differing findings may in part be explained by the fact that the effectiveness of different communication approaches is partly dependent on the specific psychological characteristics (e.g., attitudes toward vaccines and concern over safety) or cultural norms of the audience.61

Young adults consume media differently from older demographics, potentially making them harder to reach by traditional public health media and messaging.62 Social media advertising is often highly targeted to specific demographics, and participants suggested collaborating with young adults to determine common misconceptions, fears, and needs among subpopulations and directly addressing them in targeted vaccine communications. Some young adults expect reference links to be included in health messaging so they can verify the credibility of the information themselves. Others require eye-catching messages that avoid jargon and employ brief and easily digestible bits of information. Strategies to visually appeal to specific subpopulations are promising, such as using an image of a mother and child to attract the attention of people concerned about possible impacts of vaccination on fertility.

Based on this study’s findings, ideal messengers for vaccination messaging to young adults would be perceived as credible, and, in concordance with many takes on Source Credibility Theory63,64 our study findings suggest emphasis being placed on messengers who are relatable to message recipients, trusted by their communities, and who have expertise ranging from formal credentials to lived experience with COVID-19. These messengers include peers of the same age, ethnicity, and gender with either lived experience or professional credentials. More traditional approaches, such as of having a person of authority (e.g., a government health official), communicate that vaccine information remains valuable to those relying more on expertise to assess source credibility but may fail to reach some young adults due to difficulties associated with relating to and trusting authority figures either generally or specifically as ambassadors of given institutions such as colonial governments.

The qualitative body of literature on communicating about vaccination to young adults is nascent, given that most research exploring vaccine communication has focused on the elderly, parents, or adults as a broad population without disaggregating by age or life phase. Our findings add to this growing body of literature by suggesting that tailoring vaccine information to young adult demographics, and using both loss and gain-framings in ways that are well adapted to social media and partner with trusted community messengers may facilitate swifter uptake of future vaccinations and therefore should be focus of research moving forward. Future research into vaccine message framing and the use of prosocial messaging is needed if public health wants to improve communication and dialog with young adults. Particular emphasis needs to be placed on subgroups of individuals as our findings illustrate the message and messenger cannot be the same even for young adults.

Implications

Given the importance of the 20-something demographic to transmission of some infectious diseases, as well as the young adult phase of life in which many in Canada are making or approaching decisions about vaccination in pregnancy and for young children, improving vaccine communication with young adults should be prioritized. These results suggest that communication to young adults should take advantage of the affordances of platforms they regularly use; for example, via engaging and interactive visually oriented media with immediate cues to action. A multi-pronged approach is likely necessary to ensure the messages and messengers are effective, credible, and relatable to the intended audiences. A promising approach appears to be engaging young adults from diverse communities in ongoing public health advisory groups to help shape communication, bring misinformation to light, and identify appropriate messengers for their demographics. While digital social listening and “AI” technologies may aid in monitoring social media and other public communication platforms for sentiment and misinformation, communities – especially those with lower trust in government – may not be communicating primarily in publicly accessible spaces; therefore, personal relationships of trust between public health and young community members presumably remain important to build and maintain. Young adult public health liaisons or advisors also have the potential to become peer vaccine communicators in their communities.65

Strengths and limitations

Our qualitative approach enabled us to conduct in-depth interviews with young adults, by young adults, on vaccine intentions and hesitancy in Canada, directly eliciting detail on what information sources, messengers, or modes of distribution of information about vaccines should be used by public health to reach this age group. Other strengths of this study include its interdisciplinary approach (integrating epidemiology and public health with information and communication science) and the fact that our purposive sampling, diverse research team, and peer interviewers yielded an ethnically and regionally diverse group of participants who provided a variety of perspectives. A limitation is that our sample nonetheless lacked certain perspectives based on other participant characteristics; to address this we suggest that future research should be led or co-led by Indigenous and other communities underrepresented in these data. As this study’s scope was limited to young adults living in a specific region of Canada (the Island Health region of BC), while findings are likely transferable to other contexts, care should be taken not to assume generalizability to all young adults.

While we expect the underlying principles that messages should be framed specifically to address concerns and build credibility with young adults, as well as be optimized to the communications media they frequent, are widely transferrable, more specific implications such as greater use of digital images may not be applicable in lower-resource settings. Settings where vaccination is not freely accessible may experience more access barriers beyond trust or knowledge, which would not be solved with improved communication. Furthermore, communication devices and channels vary both regionally and culturally, with smartphone prevalence lower in many lower-resource settings (even those with high mobile phone adoption) and different apps dominating different linguistic, cultural, and affluence groups. Caution should be taken to assess a specific setting’s landscape when attempting to tailor vaccine communications, particularly in low- and middle-income countries that may show great promise for mHealth innovations to have great impact, yet experience great disparities in access to apps and social media platforms.66

Future directions

Following on this qualitative, exploratory study, experimental and participatory intervention research exploring the effectiveness of specific recommendations based on these study participants’ perspectives is an important next step. Comparing messaging deliberately constructed using the tactics highlighted here – attention-grabbing statistics, using a combination of positive and negative message framing, messengers tailored to specific communities, and overall optimizing public health’s use of social media’s affordances – against more traditional vaccine messaging would help test and expand on these findings. In addition to action-oriented research building on these findings, our research highlights the need for further exploratory and participatory research with diverse young adult subcommunities identified as mistrustful of general western medical or government information. Even for illnesses that appear to often milder for healthy young adults, potential importance of this age group to controlling infectious disease in the larger population – whether this be minimizing spread of microbes by a highly social age group or as parents making vaccination decisions – justifies additional research identifying effective public health communication strategies with young adults.
