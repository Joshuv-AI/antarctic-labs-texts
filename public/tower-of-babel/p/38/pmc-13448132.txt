Primary Pancreas Ewing Sarcoma with Gastric Invasion: A Case Report
Authors: Su Min Park, Sungjun Hwang, So Yeun Park, Jung Wook Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extraosseous Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a rare malignant tumor with poor prognosis. ES of pancreatic origin is exceedingly rare, with only a few dozen cases documented in the literature. It is a poorly differentiated, aggressive tumor characterized by multiple recurrences and a relatively poor prognosis. Owing to nonspecific imaging findings, it can easily be misdiagnosed as other tumors. Here, we report a case of primary pancreatic ES with direct gastric invasion in a 63-year-old male who presented with melena and summarize previously reported cases of primary pancreatic ES. Radiologists should be aware of the imaging findings of primary pancreatic ES and understand that it may be clinically confused with other tumors because of overlapping imaging features.

INTRODUCTION

The Ewing sarcoma (ES) family of tumors includes ES of the bone and extraosseous ES (1). Approximately 30% of all tumors in this family are extraosseous, with most occurring in the trunk or extremities and rarely in the abdominal organs (2). Among these, the pancreas is an extremely rare site of occurrence, with fewer than 60 cases reported worldwide. We aimed to raise awareness about this diagnosis by reporting a rare case of primary pancreatic ES in a 63-year-old male.

CASE REPORT

A 63-year-old previously healthy male presented with a 1-month history of intermittent melena accompanied by general weakness and abdominal pain. His initial hemoglobin level was 3.5 g/dL, whereas other laboratory tests, including coagulation studies, were within normal limits.

Initial contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT revealed a large lobulated mass filling the lesser sac, inseparable from the body and tail of the pancreas and the gastric lower body, measuring approximately 14 × 10 × 10 cm. The mass showed mild progressive enhancement with internal multifocal low-density lesions, suggesting cystic or necrotic changes. Ulceration was observed in the posterior gastric lower body, and splenic vessel encasement was also present. Several small nodular-enhancing lesions were observed along the left peritoneal wall, suggesting peritoneal seeding (Fig. 1A).

Emergency esophagogastroduodenoscopy was performed for bleeding control, revealing a large subepithelial tumor involving the mid-to-high portion of the posterior gastric body wall with an ulcerated lesion (Fig. 1B). Based on the imaging appearance of a large exophytic mass with necrotic changes and gastric ulceration, a high-risk gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) of gastric origin was initially considered.

On retrospective review of contrast-enhanced CT images, however, the epicenter of the tumor was located within the pancreatic body and tail rather than along the gastric wall. In addition, partial preservation of the gastric mucosal layer was noted, arguing against primary gastric origin. The lesion appeared to displace adjacent structures rather than arise from the stomach, supporting a pancreatic origin over primary gastric malignancy. No associated pancreatic duct dilatation was observed.

PET-CT demonstrated moderate fluorodeoxyglucose uptake (approximately 5.7) in the lobulated mass, with no evidence of distant metastasis (Fig. 1C).

The patient underwent a subtotal gastrectomy and distal pancreatectomy. Final pathology confirmed an ES/primitive neuroectodermal tumor of pancreatic origin, staged as pT4N0Mx (Fig. 1D). Genetic testing confirmed the t(11;22) translocation, known as the EWS–FLI1 fusion, which is a characteristic molecular hallmark of Ewing sarcoma. The patient underwent chemotherapy; however, hepatic metastasis was detected 9 months later. The patient died 21 months after the initial diagnosis.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB No. 2025-11-023).

DISCUSSION

Primary pancreatic ES is an exceptionally rare extraosseous manifestation of the ES family of tumors, characterized by the rearrangement of the EWSR1 gene, most commonly the t(11;22)(q24;q12) translocation producing the EWS–FLI1 fusion protein (3). Extraosseous ES has been described in various solid organs, including the lungs, gallbladder, kidneys, urinary bladder, uterus, and vagina (3), whereas ES of pancreatic origin is exceedingly uncommon, with only isolated cases described worldwide (4567). Among these, gastrointestinal bleeding as the presenting symptom has been documented in only two cases (48), underscoring the rarity of the clinical course observed in our patient.

ES most commonly affects adolescents and young adults with a median age of approximately 20 years (6). Prognosis is influenced by several factors, including age at diagnosis and disease dissemination (6). In our patient, the diagnosis was made at 63 years of age, and the tumor demonstrated direct gastric involvement with suspected peritoneal seeding, suggesting an unfavorable clinical course. The patient subsequently experienced disease progression and died during follow-up.

The imaging characteristics of pancreatic ES are nonspecific and overlap with those of other pancreatic neoplasms, including epithelial and nonepithelial tumors. Previous studies have described pancreatic ES as a large expansile soft-tissue mass frequently containing internal necrotic or cystic components, with enhancement patterns ranging from mild to heterogeneous, depending on tumor cellularity and the extent of necrosis (9). aggressive behavior, including adjacent organ invasion and vascular encasement (79). Adjacent organ invasion and encasement of major vessels such as the celiac axis and common hepatic arteries have also been reported, reflecting the aggressive biological behavior of this tumor (7). When the tumor arises from the pancreatic body or tail, it may expand into and occupy the lesser sac, obscuring its true origin.

Among the differential diagnoses, pancreatic ES with direct gastric involvement can closely mimic a high-risk GIST, which typically presents as an exophytic necrotic mass and may secondarily invade the pancreas through the lesser sac. This overlap contributed to the initial impression of high-risk GIST in the present case. However, pancreatic adenocarcinoma was considered less likely because of the absence of pancreatic duct dilatation, lack of regional lymphadenopathy, and the presence of an expansile growth pattern rather than the infiltrative pattern that is characteristic of adenocarcinoma. Neuroendocrine tumors were also less favored, as the lesion lacked the marked arterial hyperenhancement typical of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors.

In this case, the tumor presented as a large lobulated mass occupying the lesser sac, with internal necrotic components communicating with the gastric lumen, producing an ulcerated lesion that resulted in melena. This appearance strongly suggested a high-risk GIST at initial evaluation. A retrospective review revealed that although the tumor abutted and invaded the gastric wall, the gastric mucosa was relatively preserved and displaced superiorly, subtly favoring a pancreatic origin. Nevertheless, distinguishing pancreatic ES from other pancreatic or gastric neoplasms on the basis of imaging findings alone remains challenging. Because treatment strategies differ substantially, such as systemic chemotherapy for pancreatic ES versus tyrosine kinase inhibitor–based or surgical management for high-risk GIST, histopathological confirmation remains essential for accurate diagnosis.

The recommended management of pancreatic ES involves multimodal therapy, including surgical resection and systemic chemotherapy, similar to the treatment protocols for osseous ES (1). Despite aggressive therapy, pancreatic ES has a high propensity for recurrence and metastatic spread, with frequent liver involvement (5). Therefore, close surveillance after treatment is imperative.

This case emphasizes that, although exceedingly rare, pancreatic ES should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a bulky mass mimicking a gastric subepithelial tumor when the boundary with the pancreas is indistinct. Although pancreatic ES most commonly affects younger adults and frequently involves the pancreatic head (456), this case illustrates that it may also occur in older patients and present with upper gastrointestinal bleeding due to gastric ulceration. Because pancreatic ES can closely mimic high-risk GIST in both clinical and radiologic presentations, careful assessment of the tumor origin and mandatory tissue confirmation are crucial when imaging findings are ambiguous.
