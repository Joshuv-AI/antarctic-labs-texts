NCRAD: Advancing Alzheimer's research through high‐quality biospecimens and data
Authors: Michael C Edler, Kelley Faber, Kaci Lacy, Jacqueline Jackson, Tatiana Foroud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (NCRAD) was established in 2002 to support research on the genetics of Alzheimer's disease. NCRAD quickly became a central resource, banking samples from numerous studies and distributing them to researchers worldwide. As genetic risk variants were identified, NCRAD prepared for functional studies by expanding its collections to include peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), RNA, and biofluids. Over the past decade, NCRAD's extensive repository of plasma, serum, and cerebrospinal fluid was essential to the development of fluid biomarkers. NCRAD's rigorous best practices for sample collection, processing, and distribution ensure biospecimens are of the highest quality for a broad range of experimental approaches. Currently, NCRAD banks samples from 91 studies representing over 135,000 unique, well‐characterized participants, and has distributed over 440,000 aliquots to more than 300 researchers. Enabling Genetic Discovery and Functional Genomics: NCRAD partnerships with numerous research initiatives has facilitated major advances in gene discovery, while also supporting downstream functional studies using induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), transcriptomics, and post mortem brain tissue. INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD) are complex, multifactorial neurodegenerative disorders that pose one of the most urgent global health challenges of this century. 1 Despite decades of research, the biological underpinnings of AD remain only partially understood. A key barrier to progress has been the limited accessibility of well‐characterized, high‐quality biospecimens collected from broadly representative, longitudinally followed cohorts. To address this critical need, the National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (NCRAD) at Indiana University was established through a cooperative agreement with the National Institute on Aging (NIA). NCRAD serves as a national resource, supporting biomarker discovery, genetic research, and therapeutic development.

Since 2002, NCRAD has served as a central hub for the collection, processing, storage, and distribution of biospecimens from numerous NIA‐funded studies. Through standardized operating procedures, rigorous quality assurance (QA), and robust data integration with study partners, NCRAD has enabled researchers to pursue cutting‐edge investigations into the genetic, molecular, and cellular mechanisms underlying ADRD. NCRAD's centralized infrastructure not only promotes reproducibility and scientific rigor but also facilitates collaboration across academic, government, and industry partners, accelerating the pace of discovery.

As NCRAD celebrates its 35th anniversary, this review offers a comprehensive overview of its contributions to ADRD research. We highlight its operational model, QA strategies, and extensive contributions to genetic discovery, biomarker development, and functional genomics research. We also examine key collaborative initiatives, including the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), the ARTFL LEFFTDS Longitudinal Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (ALLFTD) Study, the Alzheimer Biomarker Consortium‐Down Syndrome (ABC‐DS), the Alzheimer Disease Research Centers (ADRCs), and the Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study (LEADS), each of which leverages NCRAD's biobanking infrastructure. Finally, we critically evaluate the current challenges and future directions for biospecimen repositories in advancing the next generation of ADRD diagnostics and therapeutics.

HISTORY

The early years (1990–2002)

NCRAD's origins date back to 1990 when the Cell Bank Core was established within the Indiana ADRC. In its formative years, the repository's primary mission was to support genetic discovery through the recruitment of multiplex families affected by AD, often those with early onset AD, with an emphasis on collecting DNA for groundbreaking genetic linkage studies. NCRAD families contributed to the identification of three genes associated with early onset AD: presenilin 1 (PS1) on chromosome 14, 2 presenilin 2 (PS2) on chromosome 1 3 and the amyloid precursor protein (APP) on chromosome 21. 4 This foundational work established NCRAD's commitment to high‐quality biospecimen collection and long‐term stewardship of samples to support rigorous, data‐driven science.

Evolution and expansion (2002–2008)

In the early 2000s, it was recognized that the genetics of late onset AD (LOAD) was complex, 5 necessitating the recruitment of many multiplex families to successfully delineate the genetic contributions. Recognizing this challenge, the NIA recommended the establishment of a national study through the ADRCs to recruit LOAD families, with their DNA samples centrally banked and made available to the broader research community. In 2002, the NIA transitioned the Cell Repository to a U24 cooperative agreement, formally establishing NCRAD as the national biorepository for AD research. NCRAD's first study was the LOAD Family Study. During this time, NCRAD also established its long‐standing collaboration with ADNI, a partnership that solidified its role as a nationally recognized central biospecimen resource in AD research.

Genomic revolution (2009–2014)

The approaches to gene identification were transformed with the development of genome‐wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays which required large sample sizes for sufficient power to detect the effect of common variants with relatively small individual effects on disease risk. 6 NCRAD became a key partner to the Alzheimer Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) and, subsequently, the Alzheimer Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP)—both major national efforts to detect the effect of rare and common variants on AD susceptibility. As the central biorepository for these projects, NCRAD coordinated the receipt of samples from numerous studies and facilitated their transfer for centralized genomic analyses. NCRAD's role was critical to the success of both genomic initiatives. NCRAD's expansion of specimen banking beyond DNA established the essential infrastructure needed to support functional studies that would elucidate the functional effect of newly identified genomic variants.

A period of unprecedented collaboration (2015–2020)

As the NIA expanded its focus to include both AD and ADRD, NCRAD continued to grow, banking samples from 47 ADRD studies by 2020. These studies span the spectrum of early‐ and late‐onset AD, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), Lewy body dementia (LBD), rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD), Down syndrome, and well‐characterized, longitudinally followed, control or cohort participants, many of whom will complete autopsy.

During this period, NCRAD also expanded its capabilities to meet the needs of the research community. While its initial decade focused solely on banking samples for DNA isolation—often in large quantities to accommodate the available genetic technologies—by 2015, NCRAD expanded its focus to prepare for functional studies essential for understanding the loci and variants which had been identified using genome‐wide genotyping arrays and sequencing approaches. These functional studies required the collection of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from which induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) could be developed, as well as RNA from blood or brain tissue for transcriptomics.

Leading the way into the future (2021‐present)

In recent years, there has been an important shift in the construct of AD from a definition based on clinical symptoms to one focused on the underlying biological processes as defined by biomarkers, both imaging and biofluid, which evaluate beta Amyloid deposition, pathologic Tau, and Neurodegeneration (ATN). 7 This paradigm has recently been formalized through the updated 2023‐2024 NIA‐AA diagnostic criteria, which define AD biologically and introduce a staging system that can be applied across the disease continuum, including asymptomatic individuals. 8 These advances have fueled significant interest in banking of cerebrospinal spinal fluid (CSF) and blood derivatives (plasma and serum), which enable the development and validation of assays for key biomarkers of AD. Recognizing the growing importance of biomarkers to diagnosis, staging, and clinical trial readiness, NCRAD established the Biomarker Assay Laboratory (BAL) to support and accelerate these critical advances, while also providing standardized, longitudinal specimens that align with evolving research frameworks.

ENSURING HIGH‐QUALITY SPECIMENS: RIGOR AND REPRODUCIBILITY

Standardization

High‐quality biospecimens are fundamental to the rigor and reproducibility of ADRD research. As a central biorepository serving more than 90 NIA‐funded studies, NCRAD has developed and maintained a robust infrastructure for QA and quality control (QC) that ensures biospecimen integrity from initial collection and processing through long‐term storage and distribution. NCRAD's commitment to biospecimen excellence is built on comprehensive, study‐specific standard operating procedures (SOPs) that are informed by current best practices described by ISBER, CAP, and ISO20387, ensuring that biospecimens are fit for purpose. These SOPs are regularly reviewed and updated to incorporate the latest advances in laboratory techniques, adapt to evolving regulatory requirements, and integrate feedback from external collaborators.

NCRAD has developed a comprehensive Manual of Procedures (MOP) template specifically tailored for neurodegeneration‐focused studies (Figure 1). This MOP provides detailed and pictorial guidance on specimen collection, processing, storage, and shipment, ensuring standardization across a wide range of research sites. To support consistency in implementation, NCRAD provides detailed training to all site personnel responsible for specimen handling. This training includes manuals, instructional videos, one‐on‐one onboarding sessions, periodic refresher courses, and retraining in response to protocol changes.

Upon receipt at the biorepository, each biospecimen undergoes a series of QC checks to assess volume, labeling accuracy, temperature at arrival, and physical integrity. Deviations are recorded, reviewed, and reconciled through internal tracking systems. DNA and RNA extractions, performed centrally, are evaluated for concentration, purity, and degradation using validated spectrophotometric and fluorometric methods. For CSF and plasma samples, hemolysis scoring and aliquot integrity are monitored. NCRAD also employs laboratory information management systems (LIMS) to track each biospecimen's chain of custody, processing history, and QC data throughout its lifecycle.

Internal audits are conducted regularly by the NCRAD QA team to assess protocol adherence, equipment calibration, freezer monitoring logs, and data accuracy. Additionally, NCRAD participates in external assessments, including inspections and collaborative benchmarking with other NIA‐funded repositories. The implementation of redundancy measures—such as duplicate freezer systems, environmental alarms, backup generators, and 24/7 temperature monitoring—further protects against biospecimen loss or degradation.

Through these systems, NCRAD enables researchers to access biospecimens that meet strict quality criteria, ensuring that findings generated from these materials are reliable and reproducible. Moreover, the use of consistent QA protocols across participating studies enhances data comparability and facilitates integrative analyses across large, heterogeneous cohorts.

As the research community moves toward greater transparency and standardization in pre‐analytical variables, NCRAD's rigorously maintained quality framework serves as a model for centralized repositories. By embedding reproducibility into every step of biospecimen handling, NCRAD supports the generation of robust, replicable insights into the pathogenesis, progression, and treatment of AD/ADRD.

Automation

To address the growing demand for high‐throughput, high‐integrity biospecimen processing, NCRAD has made significant investments in automation across its laboratory and storage infrastructure. Automation not only increases specimen throughput and consistency, but also reduces preanalytical variability, improves traceability, and enables faster, more reliable sample distribution. NCRAD's integrated use of liquid handling robotics, automated nucleic acid extraction platforms, and ultra‐low temperature storage systems supports over 90 NIA‐funded studies while maintaining high standards for specimen quality and consistency.

Liquid handling

Automated liquid handling plays a central role in NCRAD's biobanking workflows, enabling high‐throughput processing of a wide range of biospecimen types with precision and consistency. These systems are used throughout the sample lifecycle—from initial aliquoting of fluid specimens like plasma, serum, urine, and CSF, to preparation of DNA for extraction, quantification, and downstream analysis. Automation reduces hands‐on time and technician variability, allowing NCRAD to process large numbers of samples with greater accuracy and reproducibility. Temperature‐sensitive specimens benefit from integrated cooling features that protect sample integrity during processing, while precise pipetting ensures reliable assay setup and DNA normalization. Even lower‐throughput applications, such as iPSC QC, benefit from automated plating, maintaining consistency across batches. By streamlining complex workflows and minimizing preanalytical variability, liquid handling automation enhances NCRAD's ability to meet the demands of modern, large‐scale Alzheimer's research while maintaining rigorous quality standards.

Nucleic acid extraction

Automation is a key component of NCRAD's approach to nucleic acid processing, allowing for high‐throughput, standardized extraction of both DNA and RNA from a variety of biospecimen types (Figure 1). These automated systems are designed to reduce hands‐on time, increase consistency, and improve overall sample quality—factors that are essential for genetic, transcriptomic, and multi‐omic research in ADRD.

For DNA extraction, NCRAD utilizes magnetic bead–based platforms capable of processing up to 96 specimens in parallel, thereby significantly increasing throughput and reducing turnaround time. Compared to manual methods, automation has led to a marked increase in specimen processing efficiency without the need to expand lab resources. Protocol consolidation has simplified training and improved workflow consistency. Integrated software ensures full specimen traceability. The use of automated protocols has also improved DNA purity—as reflected in higher 260/280 ratios—reducing the need for recollection or cleanup.

For RNA, a semi‐automated system processes whole blood collected in PAXgene RNA tubes. This workflow stabilizes intracellular RNA allowing for flexible processing timelines. The use of standardized extraction kits and consistent processing conditions helps to ensure uniform RNA quality across batches, which is critical for downstream gene expression studies.

Across both DNA and RNA workflows, NCRAD's automation strategy emphasizes not only throughput, but also reproducibility and sample integrity. By minimizing technician variability and optimizing protocols for multiple specimen types, NCRAD can generate high‐quality nucleic acids that support a wide range of modern molecular analyses.

Hamilton BiOS M10: High‐density automated storage and retrieval

NCRAD's cold storage infrastructure is anchored by the Hamilton BiOS M10, an automated ultra‐low temperature storage system designed for high‐density biobanking. The system houses 10 chest freezers maintained at –80°C, with the capacity to store between 1.6 and 4.0 million tubes in SBS‐standard racks. Its robotic arms can retrieve at least 200 tubes per hour, picking and placing samples at –80°C with transport maintained at –20°C, minimizing warming events that could compromise specimen integrity. A custom‐engineered barcode scanner ensures precise inventory control, while redundant compressors, a bulk LN2 tank, and a dedicated generator provide comprehensive emergency backup. This system not only allows NCRAD to support growth within a constrained facility footprint but also accelerates specimen retrieval and shipment timelines, enabling more efficient downstream research.

By integrating automation across liquid handling, DNA/RNA extraction, and long‐term storage, NCRAD ensures specimen consistency, reduces human error, improves laboratory throughput, and strengthens biospecimen quality across the board. These enhancements enable NCRAD to support increasingly complex studies with greater scale, speed, and scientific rigor.

QA and QC

To ensure the highest integrity of distributed samples, NCRAD implements multiple layers of QA and QC (Figure 1). QA processes focus on preventive systems and protocols, while QC involves specific assessments of sample attributes. Together, these practices have been validated by consistently excellent results obtained by individual researchers and core facilities using NCRAD samples.

NCRAD's QA framework focuses on process standardization and risk mitigation across every stage of the biospecimen lifecycle. All samples collected during a participant visit are split and stored in physically independent locations to safeguard against loss due to equipment failure or localized disasters. Environmental monitoring is continuous, with storage units equipped with redundant alarm systems, and a disaster recovery plan ensures readiness in the face of power outages or environmental emergencies.

To enhance traceability and confirm sample identity, NCRAD implemented a custom 96‐SNP fingerprint array using SNP Type Genotyping technology in 2018. This genotyping assay is applied to all DNA samples upon receipt and again before distribution. It serves as a key QA system by confirming longitudinal sample continuity, identifying participants enrolled in multiple studies, and allowing researchers to cross‐reference genotyping results with existing genome‐wide datasets. The array also includes two apolipoprotein E (APOE) SNPs, and NCRAD returns APOE genotypes to contributing studies on a quarterly basis—an added value for researchers banking samples with NCRAD.

Another QA strategy is the creation and distribution of reference pool aliquots for CSF, plasma, and serum. These study‐specific reference pools are generated from residual sample material and used to help researchers detect and correct for assay batch effects, which are common in biomarker studies where samples are analyzed in groups over time. NCRAD creates large volume reference pools from which many reference pool aliquots can be generated. This approach allows large sample requests to use the ideal number of reference pool aliquots to control and minimize assay batch effects without worrying about depleting available reference pool aliquots. Furthermore, for researchers requesting samples at different times from NCRAD, aliquots from the same reference pool can be included to control for batch effects.

Complementing these system‐wide safeguards, NCRAD employs rigorous QC testing at the individual sample level. For DNA, purity is assessed using the Lunatic spectrophotometer, which provides 260/280 ratios within an acceptable range of 1.65 to 2.2 and flags common contaminants such as RNA, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), phenol, or detergents. Double‐stranded DNA concentration is measured using the QuantiFluor fluorescent dye assay, ensuring accurate quantification. RNA samples undergo multiple quality assessments, including 260/280 and 260/230 ratios and RNA Integrity Number (RIN) measurement. NCRAD's average RIN of 8.5 indicates high‐quality RNA suitable for transcriptomic applications. For plasma, serum, and CSF, samples can be evaluated for hemoglobin contamination, a key step in preventing interference in downstream biomarker analyses.

Together, these QA and QC systems form a robust foundation for biospecimen integrity. By embedding quality into both its operational infrastructure and its individual sample assessments, NCRAD ensures that distributed materials meet the highest standards for scientific research. This integrated approach enables reproducible discoveries, reduces technical artifacts, and supports the ADRD research community in advancing precision diagnostics and therapeutic development.

Laboratory information management system

Because of changing study requirements, NCRAD maintains well over a hundred research protocols. The complexity of the protocols, as well as the number of specimens banked in NCRAD, overwhelms many of the smaller laboratory information management systems. Due to the tremendous growth of NCRAD over the past decade, in 2025, NCRAD transitioned from OnCore to OpenSpecimen for managing research protocols and biospecimens.

Open Specimen is an open‐source, web‐based biospecimen management system hosted in the Amazon Web Services (AWS) environment. NCRAD utilizes OpenSpecimen to centralize the management of all protocols and participants and defines expected sample collections based on protocol calendars and study visits. This configuration allows NCRAD to pre‐define expected specimens, and to print specimen labels directly from the system to include in study kits. The NCRAD specimen accessioning team checks in specimens and records any non‐conformance issues (e.g., low volume) directly in OpenSpecimen. Laboratory technicians utilize OpenSpecimen to record all QC measures and to track the processing and storage locations for all specimens, including those that are distributed.

NCRAD adheres to all Indiana University's Information Technology security policies and procedures. These policies comply with the HIPAA Security Rule and align with NIST 800‐53 risk management standards. OpenSpecimen undergoes routine vulnerability scans. Backups of the OpenSpecimen database are completed nightly within Indiana University's managed infrastructure.

BREADTH OF STUDIES BANKING AT NCRAD

NCRAD banks samples from 91 different studies that fall within NCRAD's goal of supporting studies that expand our understanding of dementia. This includes studies focused on early or late onset AD, several of which are family studies. NCRAD has expanded its scope over the past two decades to support studies focused on related dementias such as FTD, Parkinson's disease (PD), RBD, vascular dementia (VD), and Down syndrome. In addition, NCRAD supports a wide range of cohort studies that follow groups of participants with or without dementia as well as high risk cohorts. There are also studies that focus more broadly on aging. Of note, some studies include multiple groups of participants and may be included in more than one of the described groups. The scope and scale of studies banking with NCRAD can vary widely. NCRAD supports studies that consist of a single visit and are conducted at one site; although, increasingly, NCRAD coordinates biospecimen collection and banking for large consortium studies that can have as many as 60 sites, such as ADNI. In addition, studies banking with NCRAD may conduct annual visits that span over a decade. Because of NCRAD's focus on best practices, adherence to standard protocols, and careful QA, samples collected at the baseline visit many years ago can be studied alongside samples collected just a few months ago. This was particularly helpful for the two Frontotemporal Dementia collaborations initially funded by NIA and the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS), The Advancing Research and Treatment in Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (ARTFL) and Longitudinal Evaluation of Familial Frontotemporal Dementia Subjects (LEFFTDS). NCRAD partnered with both consortia to ensure that samples collected by each study utilized the same sample collection, processing, and banking procedures, thereby minimizing any preanalytical variability. Subsequently, in the grant renewal, these two studies joined forces as the ALLFTD study. NCRAD's coordination ensured that all samples that were collected across all phases of these studies could be combined for joint analyses.

A timeline of the growth of sample banking at NCRAD also illustrates the changing focus of ADRD research. As shown in Figure S1, in the early 2000s, most studies banking at NCRAD were focused on genetic analyses and collected samples to obtain DNA. NCRAD has continued to ensure that studies collect a sample for DNA. In 2010, the samples from the Ginkgo Biloba Prevention Trial in Older Individuals/The Ginkgo Evaluation of Memory Study (GEMS) were transferred to NCRAD, marking a new focus on plasma, serum, and CSF. Over the past 15 years, nearly all studies banking at NCRAD are collecting at least one type of sample that can be used for biomarkers.

CROSS‐SECTIONAL AND LONGITUDINAL SAMPLE COLLECTIONS

The scope of samples being collected can vary across studies, depending on the research goal and capabilities at the study sites. Increasingly, most studies collect both DNA and plasma. Several studies are preparing for more functional research and collect PBMCs for future iPSCs and/or blood for RNA isolation to be used for transcriptomics. When possible and feasible, many studies seek to collect CSF, which can be used for a range of biomarker assays. The range of sample types collected at a single visit is another strength of the studies banking at NCRAD. For example, having plasma and CSF from the same participant visit allows for a comparison of biomarker results as well as an evaluation of disease progression across various tissues. NCRAD has banked samples from 71,832 participants who have more than one type of sample collected at a study visit. DNA and plasma are frequently collected in the same visit because both can be obtained from a single EDTA tube. NCRAD has 26,033 participants and 38,026 participant visits with both DNA and plasma banked. There are 1920 participant visits with DNA, plasma, and CSF. If we consider additional combinations of sample types, NCRAD has 9131 participant visits with 5 or more sample types collected. These are typically DNA, plasma, serum, RNA, and CSF, but other sample combinations are also observed.

NCRAD has 34 studies with ongoing or planned longitudinal visits (see Figure 2A). Plasma is the most frequently collected longitudinal sample, and 27 studies are obtaining plasma at multiple visits. Other sample types that are frequently collected longitudinally include serum in 17 studies and CSF in 9 studies. As shown in Figure 2B, NCRAD has these critical samples collected at up to 9 visits. These longitudinal samples have proven to be particularly valuable when assessing progression in fluid biomarkers, an area of increasing importance.

ADVANCING GENETIC RESEARCH IN AD

Facilitating large‐scale genomic studies

In 2008, NCRAD began to bank DNA samples from the well‐characterized ADRC participants. This effort gained momentum and by 2025, there are DNA samples banked at NCRAD from over 50,000 ADRC participants. In partnership with the National Alzheimer Coordinating Center (NACC), each DNA sample was linked to a uniform dataset through the participant's NACCID. Because of the centralization of DNA for the ADRC participants, NCRAD was ready to support the growing National Institutes of Health (NIH) focus on AD genetics. The ADGC served as the first national effort to bring researchers together to work collaboratively and study large numbers of DNA samples using rapidly emerging genomic technologies. The availability of DNA from the ADRCs at NCRAD made them the first samples to be included in genome‐wide SNP arrays. To date, these arrays have been run on over 50,000 ADRC participants, and the data are available at the National Institute on Aging Genetics of Alzheimer's Disease Data Storage Site (NIAGADS) as datasets ADC1‐ADC17. 9

NCRAD's use of standardized material transfer agreements (MTAs) and its ability to rapidly extract DNA and ensure high quality DNA, made it the central repository for the ADGC efforts. To date, 46,047 DNA samples from 16 studies and from 50 different institutions have been transferred through NCRAD as part of the ADGC efforts. Partnered with NCRAD, the ADGC steadily increased the sample sizes included in genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) and demonstrated that common variants of small effect can be detected. 10 , 11 , 12 To date, over 75 variants have now been found to be associated with AD susceptibility. 13 , 14 The ADGC also had sufficient sample sizes to identify associations with rare variants in PLCG2, ABI3, and TREM2 that suggest that microglia‐mediated innate immune response may contribute to AD. 15 As sample sizes increased in non‐European ancestry participants, ADGC analyses have found that the same pathways were implicated in analyses of African American participants, although the specific variants differed in some instances. 16 In total, over 300 publications have utilized data generated by the ADGC, many of which utilized DNA samples handled by NCRAD.

In 2012, the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP) was established to support a national effort to utilize whole exome and then whole genome sequencing to dissect the genetics of AD. 17 NCRAD again served as a central hub for DNA that was to be sequenced at central repositories. To date, NCRAD has processed 25,803 DNA samples from 21 studies and 63 institutions for the ADSP, which has identified numerous novel genes and variants associated with AD risk, including several implicated in immune response and transcriptional regulation. 18 , 19 The ADSP completed analyses of copy number variants as a contributor to disease etiology. 20 More recently, the ADSP analyzed participants from a range of genetic backgrounds and identified DNA variants at APOE, BIN1, PSEN1, and LINC00320 that are associated with AD. 21 In total, over 450 publications have utilized data generated by the ADSP, which relied on NCRAD's rigorous QA and SOPs to efficiently manage sample flow.

Support for functional genomics research: Stem cells

As numerous AD and ADRD risk loci have been identified, research has increasingly shifted toward understanding the biological mechanisms underlying the pathophysiology of AD. While animal models were traditionally the primary tool for functional genomics, their limitations—particularly in modeling complex traits influenced by variants with small effect sizes—have prompted a growing reliance on cellular research models. Among these, iPSCs are of particular interest due to their ability to differentiate into a variety of central and peripheral nervous system cell types. 22 These cell lines enable mechanistic studies of disease‐associated variants in relevant cell types, support high‐throughput drug screening, and allow for personalized modeling of disease phenotypes.

To support this shift towards iPSCs, NCRAD has become a critical resource. Many studies have collected and banked PBMCs at NCRAD. Investigators who receive PBMCs from NCRAD are required to return to NCRAD any derived iPSC line for broader sharing with the research community. This ensures that the lines derived from material obtained from NCRAD are made widely available. Additionally, NCRAD has been designated by the NIA as a recommended repository to accept researcher generated iPSCs and fibroblast lines. As a result, 9 researchers have shared 480 iPSCs and 436 fibroblasts over the past 6 years.

The stem cell collection at NCRAD includes lines derived from individuals with pathogenic variants in key genes associated with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias, such as APP, PSEN1, PSEN2 for familial AD, and MAPT, C9orf72 for frontotemporal dementia and other tauopathies. The repository also includes lines from individuals with sporadic disease and cognitively healthy controls, capturing a broad clinical and genetic spectrum. Importantly, several iPSC lines have been genetically edited to create isogenic pairs, allowing researchers to study the specific effects of disease‐causing mutations in an otherwise identical genetic background. This resource supports investigations into both common and rare mechanisms of neurodegeneration and provides a powerful platform for disease modeling, target validation, and therapeutic development.

One major contributor of PBMCs at NCRAD is ADNI, a 20‐year, longitudinal, multi‐center study that has enrolled individuals meeting a range of criteria: cognitively normal, early mild/mild/late mild cognitive impairment, significant memory concern, and mild AD dementia. ADNI participants contribute a rich set of longitudinal data including neuroimaging, multiple ‐omics platforms, fluid biomarkers, clinical, and cognitive evaluations. In collaboration with the NIH Center for Alzheimer's and Related Dementia (CARD), iPSCs are being generated for 200 ADNI participants using the PBMCs banked at NCRAD. This will include 80 participants with AD, 39 cognitively normal, and 81 with MCI or subjective memory complaints. There is also an overrepresentation of participants of non‐European ancestry given the lack of iPSCs currently available for research. The New York Stem Cell Foundation (NYSCF) is developing these iPSC lines and returning ten vials for each participant to NCRAD, where they are made available to the broader research community to request.

To increase accessibility, NCRAD launched a catalog focused on iPSC and primary fibroblast lines. Catalog data for each line include clinical diagnosis, sex, age of onset (when applicable), age at death, known pathogenic mutations, and NACCID (when relevant). For iPSCs, the catalog also includes cell type source, reprogramming method, passage number, and number of available vials. iPSC lines are listed only after meeting stringent QC criteria. These criteria include confirmation of sterility and absence of mycoplasma; assessment of viability and morphology; SNP fingerprinting to confirm cell line identity; documented karyotype; validated pluripotency; demonstrated capacity for differentiation into ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm lineages; and confirmation of relevant mutations, when applicable. For primary fibroblasts, the catalog includes passage number, guaranteed minimum population doubling level (PDL) remaining, and number of available vials. As with iPSCs, fibroblast lines are listed only after passing all QC criteria, including tests for sterility, mycoplasma, viability after thaw, and confirmation of relevant mutations, when applicable. All iPSC lines also have whole genome sequencing available at NIAGADS. By centralizing the collection, QC, and distribution of iPSC and fibroblast lines, NCRAD enhances reproducibility and accelerates research by reducing variability across laboratories.

Support for functional genomics research: Transcriptomics

NCRAD supports transcriptomic investigations by providing high‐quality RNA extracted from PAXgene‐preserved whole blood. Additionally, frozen post mortem brain tissue is available to researchers, who perform RNA extraction at their own institutions. Blood‐derived RNA enables analysis of systemic and immune‐related gene expression signatures and is particularly useful for exploring peripheral effects of ADRD‐associated genetic variation. 23 All RNA samples extracted by NCRAD meet rigorous QC metrics, including RIN, purity, and concentration, making them suitable for RNA sequencing and other transcriptomic applications. Although peripheral biomarkers show promise, their disease specificity and reproducibility remain under investigation.

Frozen brain tissue provided by NCRAD allows researchers to perform region‐specific RNA extraction and transcriptomic profiling of central nervous system (CNS) tissue. 24 Accompanying metadata—such as post mortem interval, brain region, neuropathological diagnosis, and other clinical variables—support contextualized interpretation of gene expression and splicing patterns in brain regions affected by ADRD. Researchers can use these data to investigate cell‐type–specific transcriptional changes and regulatory variation relevant to disease mechanisms. The primary sources of brain tissue at NCRAD are the NCRAD Family Study, the NIA LOAD Family Study (now broadened to the NIA‐AD Family Based Study), and The Gift of Life Study.

These transcriptomic resources can be combined with genomic data and iPSC models derived from the same participants. While iPSCs offer a valuable platform for functional studies, they may not fully replicate the epigenetic or environmental context of primary tissues. Integrating expression data from blood, brain, and iPSC‐derived models provides a powerful, multi‐tissue approach for interpreting the functional impact of ADRD risk loci. 25

Catalogs

NCRAD has developed catalogs (https://ncrad.iu.edu/access‐samples/catalogs) for the most frequently requested collections: ADRC, Alzheimer's Prevention Initiative ADAD Colombia Trial (API ADAD), API Generations 1 and 2, NCRAD Family Study Brain Tissue, The Gift of Life, Indianapolis‐Ibadan Dementia Study, NIA‐LOAD Family Study, ARTFL/LEFFTDS/4‐Repeat Tauopathy Neuroimaging Initiative (4RTNI‐2), 90+ Study, and the Amyloid Neuroimaging and Genetics Initiative (ANGI). The study‐specific catalogs provide a user‐friendly mechanism to browse, select, and order specimens. When selecting a study‐specific catalog, a default view is presented to the researcher, who can then select additional fields to view and query. To understand the data contained in each field, a data dictionary is available. The catalog data are displayed in tabular form and the user can easily select subsets of the data of interest, which can then be downloaded or submitted as a sample distribution order. The NCRAD catalogs are password‐protected, and the available limited dataset allows researchers to perform their own feasibility studies and identify suitable samples for their research.

The studies banking at NCRAD have a wide disease/research focus and collect an extensive set of variables, which do not overlap substantially between studies. As a result, NCRAD cannot provide a single clinical dataset to accompany all collections; rather, NCRAD receives a relevant minimal dataset for each collection. For many studies, more extensive data are available, and the NCRAD manager helps request these data and answer questions related to catalog queries and/or selecting the most appropriate catalog(s) with relevant data, diagnoses, or biospecimens.

UNIQUE MODEL OF SAMPLE DISTRIBUTION

Sharing of data generated from NCRAD samples

The samples banked by NCRAD are often a limited resource, and it is essential that the data generated by them are made available to the research community. All researchers who receive any type of sample from NCRAD must complete an MTA for outgoing specimens (Figure 1). Included in the MTA is language limiting the distribution of samples to third parties, and language requiring recipients to share results of their analyses with NIH‐approved data sharing resources (i.e., Database of Genotypes and Phenotypes [dbGaP], NIAGADS, Laboratory of Neuroimaging [LONI]). Study‐specific acknowledgement language is included to ensure that the study that originally collected the samples receives appropriate attribution.

NCRAD has distributed samples to over 300 investigators at more than 140 institutions. Data generated from NCRAD samples include genome‐wide SNP arrays, sequencing, transcriptomics, proteomics, biomarkers, and metabolomics. A core principle for NCRAD is to ensure that data generated from samples at NCRAD are placed at the same data repository as the study data. This approach ensures that scientists external to the study can go to one place and learn about the study, the clinical dataset, the available biospecimens and the data generated from these samples. For example, data generated from ADRC samples banked at NCRAD, including APOE genotype and plasma biomarkers, are returned to the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) where all of the accompanying Uniform Data Set (UDS) data are also stored. GWAS and sequence data from the ADRC samples are returned to NIAGADS because of their specialized expertise in the uniform processing and distribution of genomic data. For studies such as ALLFTD, which hosts their clinical and imaging data at LONI, NCRAD works with LONI to coordinate unblinding of NCRAD‐distributed samples, ensuring that data generated from their study samples are in the same place as all other study data. By returning these experimental results to a public data warehouse, these data have been used by an even broader group of investigators, and costly, redundant analyses have been avoided, maximizing the use of the samples for even more research. NCRAD partners with a wide range of data warehouses, but there are five that are most frequently used (Figure 3).

Blinded sample distributions

NCRAD utilizes blinded sample distributions for RNA, plasma, serum, and CSF requests to increase scientific rigor and reproducibility in experiments, and to enforce NCRAD policy that data must be returned for use by the broader research community (Figure 4A). When a researcher receives a blinded sample from NCRAD, they are not able to link the de‐identified sample to any other clinical or genetic data. To obtain the link, data generated from the sample must be returned to an approved data repository and then the sample is unblinded centrally. With the unblinding, the link between the sample and clinical/genetic data is provided to the researcher.

Blinded specimen request process utilized by National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (NCRAD). (A) Overview of biospecimen request process. (B) Key phases of the biospecimen request process with detailed steps provided for each.

When distributing blinded samples, the NCRAD team holds an introductory call with the approved researcher to review the administrative requirements, experimental details, and the process for receiving blinded samples and how unblinding will be completed (Figure 4B). During the call, the design of the experiment is reviewed, with a particular focus on the assay parameters, such as the number of samples per run and the optimal number of reference samples. The NCRAD team discusses with the researchers the optimal number and type of reference pools to include with each run as well as anticipated future requests that may require reference pool aliquots to be reserved. NCRAD discusses with the researcher the possible sources of assay variability (i.e., participant age, sex, disease status). For longitudinal studies, NCRAD recommends including all longitudinal samples from the same individual in the same assay run. Once all major sources of assay variability are identified, NCRAD uses computer algorithms to provide the optimal layout of the samples across the different assay runs, balancing for all key sources of assay variability. The introductory call is a critical component of the NCRAD process and ensures the success of the research experiment. Blinded sample distributions are a unique aspect of Indiana University repositories that is not offered, to our knowledge, by any other biorepository.

INTEGRATED MULTI‐REPOSITORY COLLABORATION AT INDIANA UNIVERSITY

Indiana University has created a repository infrastructure, termed Indiana University Genetics Biobank (IUGB), focused on neurological disorders with closely aligned biospecimen collection and processing procedures that facilitate biospecimen use and data comparison across several domains (Figure 5). In addition to NCRAD, Indiana University is also the site for the NINDS‐funded BioSpecimen Exchange for Neurological Disorders (BioSEND), which banks samples from 62 studies focusing on PD, Huntington's disease (HD), FTD, LBD/dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), traumatic brain injury (TBI), epilepsy, myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), and vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID). Indiana University also leads the biorepository efforts for studies funded by the Michael J. Fox Foundation, including the Parkinson Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI).

Indiana University Neurodegeneration‐Focused Biorepositories.

Having these neurodegeneration‐focused repositories at a single institution ensures that common protocols are used for the collection, processing and distribution of blinded samples, and that participants who are enrolled in multiple studies can be identified. It also maximizes resource efficiency by leveraging shared infrastructure for specimen storage, tracking, and distribution. Researchers can come to a single institution to request samples banked across repositories to address questions related to the sensitivity and specificity of genetic prediction, biomarkers, pathways, and therapeutic development. This consolidated approach creates powerful synergies across ADRD efforts at NIA, NINDS, and the Michael J. Fox Foundation.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Over the past three decades, NCRAD has established itself as a pivotal resource of ADRD research, by enabling broad access to high‐quality biospecimens and implementing rigorous QC measures. The integration of standardized protocols, advanced automation, and commitment to open data sharing has helped drive discoveries in genetic risk, biomarker development, and disease modeling.

NCRAD plays a unique role as an ADRD focused biorepository. Unlike some resources that focus on a particular type of sample, such as brain tissue, NCRAD has placed an emphasis on banking a wide range of sample types that are important for ADRD research. Another area of distinction is the number of studies banking at NCRAD – which currently numbers over 90. NCRAD banks samples from research participants across the ADRD spectrum as well as those at risk and healthy controls. The breadth of sample types, the number of studies included, and the focus on ADRD has made NCRAD one of the largest, if not the largest, ADRD focused biorepositories and a unique resource in the research community.

Despite these contributions, several gaps in knowledge remain. A better understanding of the functional consequences of disease‐associated genetic variants is still needed, particularly in populations that have limited biospecimen resources currently available to support comprehensive research. The biological pathways linking genetic variation to disease phenotypes are not fully delineated, and comprehensive integration of multi‐omic data across tissues and timepoints remains an unmet need. Fluid biomarkers have transformed the landscape of ADRD research, yet questions remain regarding their specificity, sensitivity in preclinical stages, and consistency across analytical platforms.

Addressing these challenges will require broader, longitudinal, and multi‐tissue sample collections, coupled with the use of functional models like iPSCs. Optimized methods to minimize preanalytical variability, standardize assay calibration, and implement reference controls will be necessary for reproducibility in biomarker and functional studies.

The next chapter of biospecimen‐based research must also consider the ethical implications of sample and data use. As technologies enable deeper profiling of individual biology, repositories like NCRAD can lead the development of best practices for reconsent, return of results, and engagement with all participants. At the same time, the growing use of digital health tools, such as wearable medical devices, remote assessments, and electronic health records, presents an opportunity to link biospecimen data with real‐world phenotypic information. Finally, the scale and breadth of NCRAD's resources provide an ideal foundation for applying systems biology and artificial intelligence. By combining multi‐omic, imaging, clinical, and digital health data, machine learning models can help to uncover novel disease subtypes, refine risk predication, and inform personalized treatment strategies.

Looking ahead, NCRAD's infrastructure will need to adapt as ethical standards for biorepository governance continue to evolve. One ongoing challenge is balancing broad data sharing with protecting participant privacy and responding to the emerging expectation to return clinically relevant results when appropriate. Because the return of individual research findings remains legally and logistically complex, NCRAD's flexible consent framework and collaboration with contributing studies help ensure alignment with developing best practices for reconsent and disclosure. As policies advance, NCRAD will continue to monitor this evolving landscape and contribute to efforts that explore responsible, practical approaches for returning results within applicable legal and institutional review board (IRB) frameworks.

Through continued innovation, ethical leadership, and infrastructure designed for collaboration and scale, NCRAD is poised to remain a driving force in the discovery of precision diagnostics and targeted therapeutics for ADRD. The coming decade will depend on repositories like NCRAD not only to support data generation, but to guide how those data are harmonized, interpreted, and translated into meaningful clinical progress.
