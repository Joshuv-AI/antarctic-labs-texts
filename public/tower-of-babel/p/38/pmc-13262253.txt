Varietal differences and process adaptability for precision processing of Lentinula edodes: a systematic evaluation based on volatile and non-volatile flavor compounds
Authors: Ran Zhang, Xinyi Zhao, Ruotong Bao, Caoyu Li, Qiuhui Hu, Yuxi Zhang, Gaoxing Ma, Rui Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLentinula edodes (L. edodes) is a major subject for natural seasoning development due to its variety and umami flavor. However, variety diversity and lack of standardized extraction techniques constrain its precise processing and utilization. Therefore, this study investigated five major L.edodes varieties (Zhejiang 1513, Zhexiang 6, Zhexin 1, Qingke 20, and Qingke 212) from the major production area of Zhejiang province, using different extraction methods (ultrasonic, microwave, and enzymatic hydrolysis) to analyze flavor profile variations and evaluate their processing suitability. The results showed that enzymatic hydrolysis enhanced the release of sweet and umami compounds and key characteristic aromas, ultrasonic treatment promoted the release of organic acids and nucleotides, and microwave treatment facilitated the generation of esters and phenolic compounds; Q20 and Z1513 showed the highest taste and aroma post-enzymatic hydrolysis, respectively. These findings establish a theoretical basis to guide the targeted development of the relevant seasonings.Keywords: Lentinula edodes, Quality evaluation, Non-volatile flavor components, Volatile flavor components, Enzymatic extraction

Introduction

Lentinula edodes (L. edodes), one of the most widely produced and consumed edible fungi globally, is primarily valued for its distinctive flavor profile (Mattar et al., 2018; Sheng et al., 2021). Currently, over 200 L. edodes cultivars have been developed worldwide (Ou et al., 2025), with more than 100 commercial strains in China alone (Li et al., 2008), reflecting the rich genetic diversity of this species. However, current research on the development of natural seasonings using L. edodes as raw material primarily focuses on employing various extraction methods to prepare concentrates or extracts rich in flavor compounds (Du et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2020). While extraction techniques such as ultrasonication (Zhu et al., 2023), microwave treatment (Lu et al., 2022), and enzymatic hydrolysis (Zhang et al., 2024) have been widely studied, their optimization typically relies on a single variety, neglecting the biochemical differences among cultivars (Chen et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023). As a result, extraction methods optimized for one variety often cannot be directly or consistently applied to others, hindering standardized flavor base production and impeding the industry's shift from bulk processing toward precision, targeted utilization.

The processing technology determines the sensory quality of the final product by fundamentally shaping the formation of food flavor (Samarasiri et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2025). Enzymatic hydrolysis-assisted extraction, ultrasound-assisted extraction, and microwave-assisted extraction are currently favored for L. edodes processing due to their enhanced solubility and better retention of sensory and nutritional qualities (Devi et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Microwave-assisted extraction enhances the nutritional value of L. edodes by increasing polysaccharide and polyphenol contents, thereby improving antioxidant activity (Zhou et al., 2022). Enzymatic hydrolysis specifically disrupts the cell wall and flavor precursors, increasing the levels of umami nucleotides and umami amino acids, thereby enhancing the umami intensity and mouthfeel of L. edodes (Chen et al., 2023). Ultrasound-assisted extraction has been applied to edible mushrooms to preserve their sensory and nutritional properties while enhancing flavor release from L. edodes (Tu et al., 2024). These different extraction methods exhibit selectivity in releasing flavor compounds and bioactive components from L. edodes. Therefore, the choice of extraction method should be guided by the desired sensory and nutritional objectives. Existing research primarily focuses on comparing the efficiency of different methods in releasing flavor compounds from a single variety, or analyzing the flavor differences among various cultivars under fixed extraction conditions. Zhu et al. (2022) conducted a comparative study of cellulase, flavorzyme, and complex enzymes in Edible fungi extraction, which demonstrated that the synergistic effect of complex enzymes significantly enhanced the release of flavor compounds. Du et al. (2024) investigated four extraction techniques influence the non-volatile taste component release in a dried strain of L. edodes, and found that high-pressure cooking was the most effective method for promoting the release of 5′-nucleotides. Li et al. (2014) contrasted the non-volatile taste components of five distinct mushroom species. However, few studies have systematically compared the differences in flavor compounds of L. edodes extracts across different extraction methods and cultivars.

This study selected five main cultivated varieties (Zhejiang 1513, Zhexiang 6, Zhexin 1, Qingke 20, and Qingke 212) from a key L. edodes production region in China (Zhejiang) and employed three representative extraction methods(ultrasonication, microwave, and enzymatic hydrolysis) to systematically analyze the differences in volatile and non-volatile flavor compounds in their extracts. The research aims to evaluates how extraction methods affect the flavor in major cultivars, identify key components, and establish cultivar-specific optimal processing conditions. This study establishes a theoretical basis to guide the targeted development of the relevant seasonings.

Materials and methods

Materials

Five varieties of dried L. edodes: Zhejiang Shenxiang 1513 (Z1513), Zhexiang No. 6 (Z6), Zhexin No. 1 (Z1) were obtained from Zhejiang Yuankang Food Co., Ltd., Qingke 20 (Q20), and Qingke 212 (Q212) were purchased from Zhejiang Xiangmanting Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mushrooms were harvested and then dried at 50–65 °C for 24 h by the suppliers. The dried samples were outsourced to Beijing Bangyujin Food Technology Co., Ltd. for pulverization, passed through an 80-mesh sieve, and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Cellulase (30,000 U/g, 40–50 °C, pH 5.0, food grade) was procured from Nanning Pangbo Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. Flavorzyme (50,000 U/g, 53 °C, pH 5.5–6.5, food grade) was procured from Hebei Runbu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; Tetrabutylammonium hydroxide, KH₂PO₄ (chromatography-pure) were procured from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.; 5′-nucleotide standards (5′-CMP, 5′-AMP, 5′-GMP, 5′-IMP, 5′-UMP), organic acids standards (Succinic acid, Malic acid, Citric acid, Tartaric acid) and Soluble sugars standards (Fructose, Glucose, Sucrose, Maltose, Trehalose) were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.; Glacial acetic acid (chromatographic grade) was purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd.; Chromatography-grade methanol and acetonitrile were supplied Tedia, USA.

Preparation of L.edodes extracts

L. edodes powder was extracted using three different extraction methods: ultrasonication (U), microwave (M), and enzymatic hydrolysis (E). For all groups, 5 g of sample was combined with deionized water at a 1:30 (g/mL) solid-to-liquid ratio. Both ultrasound-assisted samples and microwave-assisted samples were adapted from Zhou et al. (2025) with minor modifications. Ultrasound-assisted samples (US): the mixture underwent 3 h of water-bath extraction at 60 °C, followed by 40 min of ultrasonication at 400 W (operating frequency: 40 kHz) and centrifugation at 4000g for 20 min; microwave-assisted samples (MS): after the same 3 h water-bath step at 60 °C, the mixture was treated with 400 W microwave irradiation (operating frequency: 2450 MHz) for 120 s and then centrifuged at 4000g for 20 min. For enzymatic hydrolysis-assisted samples (ES): 1.0% Cellulase (30,000 U/g) and 0.6% Flavorzyme (50,000 U/g) were added to the mixture. After thorough mixing, enzymatic hydrolysis was performed at 50 °C for 3 h in a water bath under the natural pH of the mixture (Poojary et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2024). Subsequently, the mixture was inactivated in a 100 °C water bath for 15 min, cooled, and then centrifuged at 4000g for 20 min.

Organic acid analysis

The analysis of organic acid was extracted according to the method of Nie et al. (2020) with appropriate modifications. Each sample (0.1 mL) was mixed with 4.9 mL of 0.01 M KH₂PO₄ (pH 2.68), followed by a 30-min ultrasonic extraction step. And centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant was membrane-filtered (0.45 μm) prior to HPLC analysis.

HPLC conditions: using a Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm); mobile phase: KH₂PO₄ (0.01 M, pH = 2.68)/methanol (97/3, v/v); Under a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min; detection at 210 nm and injection volume of 20 μL.

5′-Nucleotides analysis

The analysis of 5′-nucleotides was extracted according to the method of Pei et al. (2014). Each 5 mL sample was mixed with 20 mL of ultrapure water. The mixture was boiled for 1 min, allowed to cool, followed by centrifugation at 4000 r/min for 15 min. After two extractions under identical conditions, the combined extracts were concentrated to 10 mL by rotary evaporation. Finally, the supernatant was membrane-filtered (0.45 μm) prior to HPLC analysis. HPLC conditions: a Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm); a mobile phase comprising distilled water, methanol, acetic acid, and tetrabutylammonium hydroxide (894.5:100:5:0.5, v/v); UV detection at 254 nm; column temperature, 25 °C; flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; injection volume, 5 μL.

Soluble sugars analysis

Soluble sugars were extracted according to the method of Du et al. (2024). Each sample (2.5 mL) was mixed with 25 mL of 80% ethanol and incubated in a 60 °C water bath for 30 min. Then centrifugation at 4000 r/min for 15 min. The extraction procedure was repeated twice. The combined supernatants were concentrated by rotary evaporation. The resulting residue was reconstituted to a final volume of 10 mL with a 75% acetonitrile solution. Finally, the supernatant was membrane-filtered (0.22 μm) prior to HPLC analysis. HPLC conditions: COSMOSIL Sugar-D column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm); mobile phase: distilled water/acetonitrile (25:75, v/v); ELSD detectors (Alltech Associate, Deerfield, IL, USA); 99.99% nitrogen as nebulizing gas; column temperature: 55 °C; flow rate, 0.8 mL/min; injection volume, 20 μL.

E-tongue analysis

The L. edodes extract samples were analyzed by ASTREE II tongue analysis system (Alpha M.O.S., Toulouse, France) according to Zan et al. (2025) with minor modifications. The sample (20 mL) was transferred to the sample cup for E-tongue measurement. Each sample was tested four times, with measurement values selected from at least three consistent replicates. The operational parameters were set as follows: 240 s pre-rinsing time, 30 s sample acquisition time, and 6 s post-rinsing time. All measurements were performed at room temperature. The E-Tongue system incorporated a seven-electrode sensor array (AHS, ANS, SCS, CTS, NMS, PKS, CPS) with an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. Designated sensors corresponded to sour (AHS), sweet (ANS), bitter (SCS), salty (CTS), and umami (NMS) tastes, while PKS and CPS served as broad-response sensors.

Free amino acids analysis

The free amino acid (FAAs) content of L. edodes extract samples were analyzed by automatic amino acid analyzer (Amino acid analyzer, L-8900, Tokyo, Japan) following Nie et al. (2020). For each sample, 2 mL was mixed with 2 mL of 10 g/L sulfosalicylic acid and 1 mL of 10 g/L EDTANa₂, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 60 min. A 2 mL aliquot of the mixture was then evaporated to dryness under nitrogen. The dried extract was reconstituted with hydrochloric acid to a final volume of 2 mL. Finally, the supernatant was membrane-filtered (0.22 μm) prior for analysis.

HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis

The VOCs in L. edodes extracts were analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) with GC–MS (Agilent 7890 N GC equipped with 5975 MS Detector). Each sample(5 mL) was transferred into a 20 mL headspace bottle, to which 10 μL of 50 μg/mL 2-octanol internal standard solution was added, and equilibrated in a 60 °C water bath for 15 min. Then, a DVB-CAR-PDMS SPME fiber was inserted into the headspace to collect VOCs over 30 min at the same temperature. After desorption, the fiber was thermally desorbed in the GC injector at 250 °C for 3 min. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an Agilent HP-5 MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm) using helium as carrier gas at a constant flow of 1.0 mL/min. The GC oven temperature program was: 40 °C (3 min), then ramped to 70 °C (3 °C/min), to 180 °C (5 °C/min), and finally to 230 °C (10 °C/min; held 2 min). Operating in EI mode at 70 eV, the mass spectrometer had its quadrupole and ion source temperatures set to 150 °C and 230 °C, respectively. Full-scan data acquisition was performed over a mass range of m/z 35–550 (Lai et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024).

GC-IMS analysis

VOCs in L. edodes extracts were analyzed by GC-IMS (FlavorSpec®, G.A.S., Germany) with an automated headspace sampler. Each sample (1 mL) and distilled water (1 mL) were transferred into a 20 mL headspace bottle. The sample was incubated with agitation at 500 r/min and 60 °C for 20 min, followed by automatic injection of 500 μL of headspace gas in splitless mode at 85 °C. Extracted VOCs were pre-separated by the gas chromatographic column (FS-SE-54-CB-1, 15 m × 0.53 mm, 0.5 μm film thickness) and coupled to IMS. The column was maintained at 60 °C with high-purity nitrogen (99.99%) as the carrier gas. The carrier gas flow rate was set as follows: 2 mL/min (0–2 min), increased linearly to 10 mL/min (2–10 min), then to 100 mL/min (10–20 min), and finally to 150 mL/min (20–30 min). All analyses were performed in triplicate. VOCs were identified by cross-referencing the GC-IMS built-in library and the NIST mass spectral database.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times under consistent conditions. Data were statistically analyzed with Excel 2021 (Microsoft, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (SPSS Inc., USA), and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05, indicating significant differences in the data. Figures and principal component analysis (PCA) were created with Origin 2024 (Origin Lab Co., USA). Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and variable importance in projection (VIP) plots were generated using SIMCA 14.1 software (Umetrics, Sweden). Differential and fingerprint profiles of VOCs were obtained using the integrated LAV software of the GC-IMS system (FlavorSpec®, G.A.S., Germany).

Results and discussion

Organic acids results

Organic acids, which participate in amino acid metabolism and synthesis, significantly contribute to the flavor development of L. edodes (Nie et al., 2019). Table 1 shows that US facilitates the release of organic acids in L. edodes extracts, while enzymatic hydrolysis significantly reduces the content of organic acids in the samples. This may be related to the disruption of cell walls by ultrasonic treatment, which promotes the release and dissolution of certain organic acids, thereby increasing their content (Waghman et al., 2023). Z1513 and Q20 exhibit the highest total organic acid content under all three extraction methods, indicating that these two varieties are naturally rich in organic acids. Among various organic acids, tartaric acid showed the highest content, followed by succinic acid and citric acid. These are the main organic acids in L. edodes, while malic acid had the lowest content. With the exception of malic acid, all of these exhibited high levels in the US and MS, and low levels in the ES. This degradation could be attributed to acid hydrolysis under high temperatures. ES can enhance the production and release of malic acid in L. edodes, contributing to the intensification of umami flavor (Du et al., 2024). Notably, citric acid levels in ES were similar to those in US and MS, owing to its relatively high thermal stability compared with other organic acids (Ding et al., 2017).

5′-Nucleotides results

The flavor quality of L. edodes extracts is strongly influenced by their nucleotide content, which varied significantly depending on the extraction method employed. As shown in Table 2 and Table 3, the US and ES increased total nucleotide content, while MS reduced it. Generally, enzymatic hydrolysis is expected to significantly increase nucleotide content in L. edodes extracts. According to the study by Bao et al. (2023), enzymatic hydrolysis markedly increased the nucleotide levels in S. rugoso-annulata compared to the control. In this study, the ES exhibited slightly lower nucleotide content than the US. This difference may be attributed to the heat-labile nature of nucleotides, which leads to partial degradation during the enzyme inactivation step (Wu et al., 2024). Flavor-active nucleotides, including 5′-GMP, 5′-IMP, 5′-UMP, 5′-CMP, and 5′-AMP, which are prevalent in edible mushrooms and exhibit synergistic interactions with amino acids (Bao et al., 2023; Kawai et al., 2002). Among these, 5′-GMP, 5′-IMP, and 5′-UMP exert pronounced effects on taste perception, contributing dominantly to flavor profiles through their synergistic interactions (Li et al., 2022). It is a key compound responsible for generating vegetable-like and mushroom-like flavors, and among umami nucleotides, 5′-GMP contributes the most pronounced umami taste (Qi et al., 2021). In all samples, the content and TAV value of 5′-GMP were the highest, with its level in the US being significantly higher than in the other groups. 5′-IMP exhibits a pronounced umami taste and acts synergistically with glutamic acid (Chen et al., 2021). In this study, the content and TAV value of 5’-IMP were the lowest among the detected nucleotides. It was difficult to detect in the MS, while only trace amounts were detected in Z1513, Q20, and Q212 in the US; however, enzymatic hydrolysis promoted its release, and 5’-IMP was detected in the enzymolysis solutions of all five varieties. Among these five L. edodes varieties, Q20 exhibited the richest nucleotide content, while Q212 showed the lowest nucleotide content.

Soluble sugars results

The flavor profile of L. edodes extracts is largely determined by the types and contents of soluble sugars, which serve as Maillard reaction precursors, promoting the formation of sweet and caramel notes during processing (Hu et al., 2023). As shown in Table 4, the US sample exhibited the lowest soluble sugar content., whereas the contents in the ES and MS were comparable. The release of active ingredients depends on the degradation of cell walls. Microwave rapidly disrupts the cell wall structure with its strong penetrating power, facilitating the release of soluble sugars from ruptured cells, while its rapid and uniform heating also minimizes degradation by reducing thermal exposure (Zhu et al., 2023). Enzymatic hydrolysis weakens or disrupts the cell wall structure and decomposes complex internal storage compounds, thereby releasing intracellular components like polysaccharides (Tang et al., 2023). Both microwave treatment and enzymatic hydrolysis individually promote the release of soluble sugars. Trehalose and glucose have been identified as the main soluble sugars in L. edodes (Lu et al., 2022). MS samples had the highest content of trehalose, which was consistent with that reported by Wu et al. (2015). The content of fructose was significantly lower in the US, while it was approximately twofold higher in both the MS and ES. Sucrose and maltose exhibited the lowest contents among all soluble sugars, with little variation observed across different varieties. After enzymatic hydrolysis, Z6 showed the highest fructose content, comparatively high levels of glucose and trehalose, and the highest total soluble sugar content, thereby conferring a strong sweet taste characteristic. Q20 showed the lowest soluble sugar content, which aligns with the electronic tongue results below.

E-tongue results

The L. edodes extracts from various varieties and extraction methods were evaluated with an E-tongue and subjected to PCA. Those samples presented higher umami (NMS), saltiness (CTS), and sourness (AHS) taste characteristics, while the responses for bitterness (SCS), sweet (ANS) were comparatively lower. MS and US displayed similar taste profiles with variety-dependent sensor responses. In contrast, ES showed markedly lower sourness (AHS) but higher umami (NMS), saltiness (CTS), and sweetness (ANS), accompanied by a modest increase in bitterness (SCS). These findings are consistent with previous studies on the sensory characteristics of L. edodes extracts (Du et al., 2024).

As shown in Fig. 1A, Q212 exhibited the strongest saltiness, Z1 showed the highest sweetness response, and Z6 had the lowest umami intensity. In the ES group, Z1513 and Q212 presented the highest umami intensity, which may be attributed to the production of variety-specific umami peptides during enzymatic hydrolysis (Chen et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 1B, the same variety subjected to different extraction methods exhibited variety-specific differences in taste profiles. Z1 and Q20 were moderately affected by extraction methods: ultrasound enhanced sourness, while enzymatic hydrolysis promoted umami release. Z6 was the most sensitive to extraction methods: microwave significantly increased sourness, whereas enzymatic hydrolysis resulted in more prominent umami intensity, indicating that extraction methods exert the strongest regulatory effect on the release of taste-active compounds in Z6.

Free amino acids results

The type and content of FAAs play an important role in the taste and flavor of L. edodes extracts (Zheng et al., 2022). Many studies showed that the FAAs in L. edodes extracts were affected by extraction methods and varieties (Du et al., 2024; Li et al., 2022). This study identified 17 amino acids categorized by taste profile: umami (Asp, Glu), sweet (Thr, Ser, Gly, Pro, Ala), bitter (Phe, His, Val, Met, Ile, Leu, Arg), and tasteless (Cys, Tyr, Lys) (Zhu et al., 2023). As shown in Table 5., The highest FAAs content was found under ES. Among the Q20 variety which had the highest FAAs content, the ES group (66.51 mg/g) exhibited levels 2.8 times and 1.6 times higher than those of the US group (24.09 mg/g) and MS group (41.84 mg/g), respectively. This demonstrates that enzymatic hydrolysis is the most effective method for releasing FAAs. This phenomenon may be explained by the cellulase-mediated disruption of cell structure, facilitating macromolecule release, followed by their hydrolysis into free amino acids and small peptides via flavor proteases (Dong et al., 2020), while the short-term high-temperature heating during enzyme inactivation does not adversely affect free amino acid content (Li et al., 2019). The dominant role of Glu among all FAAs is consistent with previous reports. Bao et al. (2023) also found that Glu was the most abundant amino acid in Stropharia rugoso-annulata, contributing significantly to umami taste. The ES group showed a significantly higher content of umami amino acids than the other groups, indicating that enzymatic hydrolysis enhances the umami intensity of the samples and provides abundant precursors for subsequent flavor formation. Similarly, Moerdijk-Poortvliet et al. (2022) demonstrated that enzymatic treatment enhances umami amino acid content, which aligns with our observation that the umami amino acid content in the enzymatic hydrolysis group was significantly higher.

Analysis of EUC and TAV

As an indicator of taste contribution, TAV is derived from the ratio between a compound's concentration and its taste threshold. Generally, a TAV > 1 indicates taste activity, with the numerical value directly reflecting the component's influence, greater the TAV, the stronger its influence on the sensory profile (Jian et al., 2024).

Among the 17 amino acids, Glu exhibited the highest TAV, significantly exceeding that of others (Table S2). Within the sweet amino acids, Thr and Ala contributed the most, while Arg, Val, and His were the primary contributors among bitter amino acids. All these key amino acids reached their maximum TAV in ES group. Based on the TAV results, enzymatic hydrolysis generally enhanced amino acid release across most varieties. Notably, Q20 showed higher FAAs TAVs than other varieties, with Glu having the highest TAV, indicating its pronounced umami potential. The Equivalent Umami Concentration (EUC) quantifies the synergistic enhancement of umami and sweetness resulting from the interaction between flavor nucleotides and FAAs (Liu et al., 2021). Fig. 2 compares the EUC values of samples from different varieties and processing methods. The results show that EUC values varied among varieties and treatments. Most L. edodes extract samples fell into the first-grade EUC range, with some in the second grade. The EUC values of Q20 and Z1513 reached their peak after enzymatic hydrolysis, with Q20 showing a markedly higher EUC value than all other varieties and treatment groups. This further confirms the strong umami characteristics of Q20 and the significant umami-enhancing effect of enzymatic hydrolysis on this variety. In contrast, Z1, Z6 and Q212 varieties achieved their highest EUC values in US group, followed by ES group. Among them, ultrasound treatment most notably increased the EUC value of Q212.

These findings indicate that these compounds form the primary aroma profile of L. edodes. As shown in Fig. 3B, ES samples exhibited the highest overall sensor response values, with the other two groups being significantly and similarly lower. This is consistent with the findings of Ye et al. (2026), who reported that enzymatic hydrolysis significantly enriched the aroma profile of L. edodes, promoted the production of aromatic substances, and imparted sweet and fruity notes. Analysis of GC–MS

To characterize VOCs in L. edodes extracts, VOCs were profiled via SPME-GC–MS, identifying 86 compounds across all samples. The primary compound classes detected were aromatics, alkanes, aldehydes, sulfur compounds, alcohols, and alkenes. As shown in Fig. 4A, the relative composition of VOCs is similar, with sulfur compounds showing the highest relative content, followed by alcohols and aldehydes. These three categories constitute the major aroma compounds. Both extraction methods and mushroom varieties significantly influenced the total yield of VOCs (Fig. 4B-C). ES significantly increased the total content of volatile compounds in L. edodes extracts, particularly enhancing sulfur compounds, alcohols, and phenols. This enhancement is likely attributed to cell wall disruption and the transformation of phenolic substances, which collectively contribute to a more intense and characteristic “mushroom” flavor. MS resulted in a higher than US samples, which notably elevated the levels of alcohols, phenols, and aromatic hydrocarbons. This effect may be due to the heat-induced Maillard reaction between amino compounds and carbonyl compounds, generating various aroma-active substances (Zhang et al., 2023). US was the least effective method for enhancing volatile flavors, yielding substantially lower compound levels than enzymatic hydrolysis, which proved more suitable for this purpose. Regarding varieties, Z1513, Z1, and Q20 showed the highest total aroma content, dominated by elevated levels of the primary flavor compounds-C8 and sulfur substances. This compositional advantage, supported by E-nose analysis, accounts for their more intense flavor profile. C8 and sulfur compounds are the predominant volatile components in L. edodes extracts. Among them, sulfur compounds such as lenthionine (1,2,3,5,6-pentathiepane), 1,2,4-trithiolane, and dimethyl disulfide constitute key aroma contributors (Li et al., 2022). As shown in Table S3, including lenthionine, 1,2,4-trithiolane, carbon disulfide, [1,2,3,4]-tetrathiane, and 1,2,4,5,7,8-hexathionane, increased significantly after enzymatic treatment but were lowest in the US. Lenthionine, the most abundant among them, imparts a distinct sulfurous, garlic-like, and mushroom-like odor to L. edodes aroma (Chen et al., 2023). Lenthionine is primarily synthesized via the γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (γ-GGT) and cysteine sulfoxide lyase (CS lyase)-mediated conversion of its precursors into dithiirane, which subsequently polymerizes to form lanthionine (Liu et al., 2015). During enzymatic treatment, dimethyl disulfide and dimethyl trisulfide levels decreased. This reduction likely contributes to a more balanced overall flavor profile in L. edodes extracts. The C8 compounds, another characteristic group of volatile flavor substances in L. edodes, are primarily represented by alcohols such as 1-octen-3-ol, trans-2-octen-1-ol, and 2-nonanol, which constitute important aroma components (Guo et al., 2024). Among these, 1-octen-3-ol is the most abundant, followed by trans-2-octen-1-ol. These compounds are primarily generated via the enzymatic metabolism of unsaturated fatty acids like linoleic acid (Hadar et al., 1991). 1-Octen-3-ol exhibits a typical mushroom odor and has a relatively low flavor threshold. Its content increases after enzymatic and microwave treatments, contributing significantly to the overall flavor profile.

OAV was determined for each volatile compound to quantify its aroma contribution in the L. edodes extracts (Jiang et al., 2022). As shown in Table S4, 28 VOCs with an OAV > 1 were founded, including alcohols (7), aldehydes (7), aromatic hydrocarbons (5), phenols (1), ketones (2), and sulfur-containing compounds (5). Key compounds with high OAVs were predominantly sulfur compounds, including 1-octen-3-ol, carbon disulfide, dimethyl disulfide, dimethyl trisulfide, 1,2,4-trithiolane, 1,2,4,5-tetrathiane, and lenthionine, were the major contributors to L. edodes extracts aroma. These sulfur compounds, together with 1-octen-3-ol, also showed high VIP scores, confirming their role as both key aroma drivers and differentiation markers.

Analysis of HS-GC-IMS

Compared to MS, IMS offers advantages including faster response, higher sensitivity, and better detection of trace volatile organic compounds (Wang et al., 2020). The flavor profiles of various L. edodes extracts were analyzed using HS-GC-IMS, with concentrations showed in Fig. 6 and Table S5. 68 VOCs were identified, these VOCs were classified as esters (18, 16.5%), alcohols (14, 20.6%), ketones (11, 16.2), aldehydes (10, 14.7%), acids (4, 5.9%), alkenes (3, 4.4%), alkanes (3, 4.4%), and other compounds, including both monomeric and dimeric forms of certain compounds (e.g., Ethanol, 2-Methyl-1-pentanol, Butanal, Isobutyl propionate). Notably, GC-IMS identified more ketones and esters, while GC–MS detected more sulfur compounds, aromatic hydrocarbons, and alkanes. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies on L. edodes (Chen et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2023). This difference may be explained by the lower boiling points of ketones and esters and the higher sensitivity of HS-GC-IMS to highly volatile VOCs (Feng et al., 2022), as well as the fact that GC–MS under vacuum conditions can detect more sulfur compounds and other less volatile analytes (Wang et al., 2020). Fig. 6B shows the relative contents of effective VOCs classes of different L. edodes extracts. Despite they presented fluctuations among the samples, the esters, alcohols were always higher. The content of alcohols and ketones increased after enzymatic treatment, while phenols and esters increased after microwave treatment. US had the most significant impact on aldehydes. These findings are generally consistent with the GC–MS results. Fig. 6A shows the HS-GC-IMS three-dimensional spectrum of Z1 samples treated with three extraction methods, where the X-axis, Y-axis, and Z-axis correspond to drift time, retention time, and signal intensity, respectively. Fig. 6D presents a differential top-view comparison of the HS-GC-IMS 3D topography, showing normalized ion migration times and reaction ion peak (RIP) positions against a blue background. Each point in the diagram represents VOC. Different colors represent varying concentrations: lighter shades (white) indicate lower levels, while darker shades (red) correspond to higher concentrations, with color intensity directly proportional to compound abundance (Jiang et al., 2024). It can be observed that the drift times of most VOCs range from 1.0 to 2.0 ms, while the major volatile flavor compounds are distributed within 200 to 800 s. As shown in Fig. 6D, under the same treatment method, Z1513, Q20, and Q212 exhibit numerous red and blue points, while Z6 consistently shows the fewest such points. Across different treatment methods, samples subjected to enzymatic treatment consistently display more red and blue points, indicating notable variations in VOC levels.

As shown in Fig. 8C, 31 compounds with VIP > 1 were screened. These include eight ester compounds (isobutyl propionate, methyl acetate, γ-hexalactone, butyl propionate, isobutyl acetate, ethyl isobutyrate, ethyl acetate, γ-valerolactone), six aldehyde compounds (heptanal, 2-octenal, butyraldehyde, furfural, isovaleraldehyde, nonanal), and six alcohols (ethanol, tert-butanol, 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol, isopropyl mercaptan, 1-octen-3-ol, 3-octanol), among others. Among them, 2-acetylpyrrole, 5-methyl-2-furanmethanol, furfural, and isobutyl acetate showed higher VIP values. These four volatile organic compounds contribute roasted, smoky, sweet caramel-like, baked bread-like, and soft fruity ester notes, respectively. These components are the primary contributors to the differences among the various samples.

Limitations

Specifically, the water bath temperature for the US and MS groups was set at 60 °C, while the enzymatic hydrolysis temperature for the ES group was 50 °C. This 10 °C temperature difference constitutes a potential confounding variable, as the diffusion, release, and thermal degradation of intracellular compounds are temperature-dependent. Although identical 3-h water bath durations were applied to all groups, the different temperatures may have contributed, at least in part, to the observed differences in flavor compound profiles between the ES group and the US, MS groups. Future studies should decouple the temperature effect from the extraction mechanism. For example, by conducting enzymatic hydrolysis at 60 °C if enzyme stability permits or by including a 50 °C control group for US and MS samples.

Conclusion

This study compared the effects of different extraction methods on the release profiles of non-volatile and volatile flavor compounds from five L. edodes varieties. ES increased soluble sugar and FAAs contents while reducing organic acid and nucleotide levels, thereby enhancing sweet and umami flavors, and key aromas (e.g., lenthionine, 1,2,4-trithiolane, 1-octen-3-ol); US mainly increased organic acid and nucleotide release, reduced soluble sugar content, and had the least effect on enhancing the volatile flavor; MS promoted esters, phenols, and aromatic hydrocarbons. Among the varieties, Q20 and Z1513 exhibited the highest levels of taste and aroma after ES, while US most significantly enhanced umami components such as FAAs and nucleotides in Q212. This work offers a theoretical foundation for the targeted processing and comprehensive utilization of L. edodes. This study establishes a theoretical basis to guide the targeted development of the relevant seasonings.
