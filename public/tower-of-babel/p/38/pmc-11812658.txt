Clinical Comparison of High-resolution and Standard Refractions and Prescriptions
Authors: Dawn Meyer, Martin Rickert, Olivia Reed, Paul Joret, Pete Kollbaum
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SIGNIFICANCERecently, novel refraction technology allows subjective refractions to be performed with a higher-resolution. It is unclear, however, if these benefits are noticed and appreciated by the patient during the examination and after dispensing.PURPOSEThis study investigated benefits and drawbacks of high-resolution refraction technology over standard, specifically in terms of the refraction, glasses prescription, and participant's perceptions of the technology.METHODSSixty progressive-addition-lens wearers (aged 35 to 70 years) and 60 single-vision wearers (18 years or older) were randomized to a high-resolution refraction (Vision-R 800; Essilor Instruments, Dallas, TX; essilorinstrumentsusa.com) and standard refraction in a 2-week crossover dispensing design. Refractive results were converted to M, J0, and J45 and analyzed using multivariate t tests. Bayesian estimation was used to analyze differences between refraction type and age group for subjective outcomes.RESULTSDifferences in refractive error between the two refractions were small, and none differed statistically (P > .05) or clinically (e.g., <0.25 D) in either subgroup. Visual acuities at distance and near were better than 0.00 logMAR; none of the mean differences between the refractions reached statistical or clinical (e.g., <0.10 D) significance. Participants significantly preferred the high-resolution refraction for its quickness and efficiency, improved comfort, and less stress. Bayesian analysis indicated a 76% probability that participants had higher confidence in the high-resolution refraction, 93% probability that they would seek it out for their care, and 94% probability that they would recommend an optometrist using this technology.CONCLUSIONSRefractive and acuity endpoints were similar with the high-resolution and standard refraction. Participants, however, perceived several key benefits of the high-resolution refraction and prescription for their care, the care of their friends/family, and the practice itself.

The phoropter has long been the standard tool for evaluating refractive status and, despite its regular employment, has remained largely unchanged over the last century. The standard phoropter with a rotating bank of lenses is a slight modernization of trial lens refractions.1 When using a manual phoropter, rotating banks of lenses present prescription combinations.2 These practices stack lenses with multiple lens surfaces, which reduces the optical quality3–5 for the patient.

First to market in the early 21st century aiming to streamline and improve refractive outcomes, digital phoropters typically consist of a computerized phoropter head. Innovative technologies boast many features (extended power ranges, wider apertures, prescription programming for efficient direct comparisons, and many others).6–13 Despite their many features, they have yet to be widely incorporated into clinical care.

Although digital phoropters offer innovations for the patient, they remain reliant on a rotating bank of lenses in 0.25-D steps, despite the human visual system being sensitive to changes around 0.12 D.14,15 However, obstacles to standard refraction, such as sequence, the repeatability and reproducibility (having only 80% agreement within ±0.25 D),15 and the associated anxiety of the process, demonstrate a need for further innovation.16,17

Recently, digital refraction technology combining a continuous variable power optical module phoropter6 (Vision-R 800; Essilor Instruments, Dallas, TX; essilorinstrumentsusa.com), a software allowing semi-automated and algorithm assisted refractions, enabled to achieve higher-resolution in subjective refraction (0.01 D steps and 1 degree axis orientations).15–18 Digital surfacing technology in lenses allows practitioners to order ophthalmic lenses without being restricted to traditional 0.25D power steps. It is unclear, however, if these conjoined technologies offer benefits that are noticeable by the patient or would be widely adopted by clinicians.

For simplicity of terminology, the technologies under investigation will be referred to as “high-resolution” and “standard,” although it is important to clarify that this investigation uses the “high resolution” term merely to differentiate the technologies and the smaller (e.g., 0.01 D) dioptric increments available to order, not to imply that the technology provides higher-resolution visual acuity or is necessarily better. This study, however, investigated potential benefits or drawbacks of using and prescribing from high-resolution phoropter technology over the standard. Specifically, this study explored three primary areas of interest: the refraction, glasses prescription resulting from the refraction, and participant's perceptions.

METHODS

Overall Visit Schedule

This was a 2-week crossover dispensing study, with a habitual spectacle lens washout between spectacle pairs (Fig. 1). This research was reviewed by an ethical review board (Sterling IRB ID 7604) and conforms with the principles for the protection of human subjects. Visits were nominally at the same time of day to minimize variability.

Schematic diagram of the two-treatment, two-period crossover study design. Values in the right-hand column show the total number (n) of participants who completed each visit and the number participants in the SV- and PAL-wearing subgroups. In addition, the sample size for the standard (light gray) and high-resolution (light green) refraction at each visit is listed next to the respective filled text box. PAL = progressive addition lens; SV = single vision.

Visit 1

After informed consent, participants provided demographic information and habitual prescription information.

Participants meeting all inclusion criteria were randomized to experience a standard or high-resolution refraction, each performed by the same clinician. A final prescription was generated, and subjective feedback was obtained.

Participants were asked to choose a frame and fitting measurements taken (Essilor's Eye-Ruler 2; Essilor Instruments, Dallas, TX; essilorinstrumentsusa.com).

Visit 2

After arrival and verification of the assembled glasses, participants were provided their study glasses. Glasses were dispensed by an experienced optician, and in-office self-report questionnaires and distance (4 m) logMAR Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) visual acuity were completed (chart luminance, 120 to 200 cd/m2).19 Near visual acuity was measured using a Sloan near vision chart at 40 cm (chart luminance, 120 to 200 cd/m2). Participants were required to wear the study glasses at least 6 hours daily.

Visits 3 and 4

After 2 weeks of wear, participants returned for another visit where they completed questionnaires and objective vision testing. Participants were then switched to the alternate refraction, and all testing procedures were repeated. Lenses were ordered in an identical frame, and the 2-week wear process was repeated.

Visit 5

After the second 2-week wear period, participants returned for a final visit where they provided subjective feedback.

Summary of Study Outcome Measures

The refraction and logMAR visual acuity from the high-resolution and standard processes were compared. Subjective measures were evaluated after 2 weeks of wear for each prescription.

Information about wear time, adaptation, and real-world situational (quality of vision while walking downstairs, when driving during the day, etc.) preferences for glasses were queried (“Please describe your preference for each of the following attributes with your glasses.”) using a 5-point Likert scale with a neutral position and semantic labels “Strongly prefer” and “Prefer” for each of the refraction/glasses types.

Participants

Sixty progressive-addition-lenses wearers and 60 single-vision wearers were enrolled. Individuals were eligible if they (1) were at least 18 years of age, (2) had no previous diagnosis of any binocular vision problems, (3) had no formal training in the eye care field, (4) had wearable glasses with a prescription younger than 2 years and had worn their current glasses at least 6 hours daily over the last 2 weeks, and (5) were willing to continue wearing glasses at least 6 hours daily over the course of the study. To qualify for inclusion in the group of progressive-addition-lens wearers, an individual had to be between 35 and 70 years of age and be currently adapted to progressive addition lenses.

The single vision–wearing group ranged from 18 to 45 years of age (mean ± standard deviation [SD], 22.88 ± 6.38 years), and the progressive addition lens–wearing group ranged from 36 to 70 years of age (56.65 ± 6.57 years); the combined group had a mean ± SD age of 39.77 ± 18.14 years. Overall, 76% of participants identified as female. In the single vision–wearing group, 73% of participants identified as female, whereas 79% of participants in the progressive addition lens–wearing group identified as female.

Equipment

Phoropters

The high-resolution phoropter (Vision-R 800; Essilor Instruments, Dallas, TX; essilorinstrumentsusa.com) contains a motorized tunable lens module, allowing instantaneous changes of spherical lens powers from −20.00 to +20.00 D, along with cylinder lens powers from +8.00 to −8.00 D in 0.01-D steps at 1-degree axis orientations, while the patient views through a 2-cm aperture.6

Standard refractions used calibrated manual Reichert phoropters (Reichert, Buffalo, NY; reichert.com) with spherical powers available between −19.00 and +16.75 D in 0.25-D steps, cylinder from 0.00 to −6.00 D in 0.25-D steps, and the axis in 5-degree steps while the participant viewed through a 1.5-cm-diameter aperture.2

Refraction Procedures

Both refractions were performed with the starting point of the participant's autorefraction. The high-resolution instrument's native (“Smart Refraction”) algorithm was used to obtain the refraction output.16–18 On-screen instructions guided the investigator through participant setup/alignment, measurement of pupillary distance, and adjustment of the vertex distance20 before the sphere, cylinder, and axis checks. The distance testing concluded with a binocular balance. All prescriptions were converted from testing distance (4 m) to infinity after measurement, using the built-in conversion process in conjunction with the built-in vertex alignment camera system.20

In the standard refraction, participants were presented a 20/40 Snellen line, and plus spherical power was added until the line was no longer readable. From there, up to −1.00 D was added to the lens in the phoropter until maximum acuity was attained. Participants were asked to view a 20/30 Snellen line while a Jackson cross-cylinder procedure was performed to determine the cylinder correction and corresponding visual acuity. The prescription was refined in a bracket procedure wherein more minus power was added only if participant visual acuity improved. An alternate-occlusion balance was performed while viewing a 20/30 then 20/25 visual acuity line.

For both refractions, if the participant required a near add, +3.00 D was added to the distance refraction, and a binocular cross-cylinder procedure was performed using the instrument native test. From this outcome, Snellen visual acuity at near was measured. The add was refined using a binocular bracketing procedure. If the clinical opinion of the investigator deemed further refinement to the distance or near refraction necessary to produce a wearable glasses prescription, it was performed.

Spectacles

Spectacle lenses were manufactured from either the standard or high-resolution refraction (in 0.25- or 0.01- D steps, respectfully). Single vision–wearing participants received Stylis (1.67 index), single-vision Essilor f-360 lenses with a Crizal Sapphire 360° antireflective coating (Essilor of America, Inc., Dallas, TX; essilor.com/us-en/). Similarly, progressive addition lens–wearing participants received Varilux X design lenses (Essilor of America, Inc., Dallas, TX, essilor.com/us-en/) of the same material and coatings. Standard lens manufacture variability (mean ± SD) from prescribed was 0.03 ± 0.05 D sphere and 0.01 ± 0.07 D cylinder. Similarly, the manufacturing variability (mean ± SD) from prescribed for the high-resolution lenses was 0.02 ± 0.04 D sphere and 0.03 ± 0.07 D cylinder, both well within American National Standards Institute (ANSI, Washington, DC) standards.21 Magnitude of lens manufacturing repeatability aligned with that seen for accuracy relative to prescription.

Sample Size

We first estimated a total sample size of 90 subjects would allow us to detect a small22 standardized effect of d = 0.3 in the paired mean difference between continuous ratings for the high-resolution and standard refractions with 80% power at the two-sided 5% level of significance.22,23 We then confirmed that, for the within-subgroup analyses involving categorical responses, 45 subjects per group were sufficient to detect a medium effect size with approximately 92% power. The final, post-screening sample size was increased to n = 120 to allow for an attrition rate of 25%.

Overview

Study data were managed using REDCap.24,25 All analyses were performed using R version 3.5.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; www.r-project.org) and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Although not a primary comparison, habitual lens information is included to provide context when comparing technologies.

Refractive Error

Clinically obtained refractive error was converted to Cartesian power vector coordinates (M, J0, and J45),26,27 and differences were summarized descriptively. A multivariate Hotelling T2 test was used to test whether the power vector coordinates for the refraction technologies differed statistically.

logMAR Visual Acuity

The logMAR visual acuity data for each refraction were analyzed using linear mixed-effects (LME) regression with separate models fit to the near and distance data by restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Each LME model included fixed-effects terms for refraction method, participant group, and the refraction-participant group interaction, as well as participant-specific random intercepts.

Subjective Preference

All categorical measures were depicted graphically in terms of percentages of participants endorsing each response category. For descriptive purposes, we report a point estimate for each response proportion along with an exact binomial 95% confidence interval (CI) to quantify the precision of the estimate. We augment the exact binomial result with additional information using Bayesian methods that yielded estimates of the probability (i.e., likelihood) of the participants' preferences for, confidence in, and perceived benefits of each refraction technology. Exact and Bayesian binomial tests were performed after excluding counts for the neutral option and summing counts for categories on either side of the neutral. Nonparametric Friedman rank sum tests with pairwise post hoc tests were used to analyze symptom frequency ratings for habitual, standard, and high-resolution refraction glasses.

Absence Due to COVID-19

There were three main data-related issues due to the COVID-19 global pandemic lockdown. First, because the pandemic prevented many participants from attending their final visit, an electronic survey was used to collect final subjective questionnaire responses from 38 participants. Post hoc analyses indicated no significant difference between measures collected in-office versus those remotely. Thus, all in-office and remote data were pooled in the analyses presented here. Second, differences in the final sample size of the two study groups (n = 49 vs. n = 39 for single-vision and progressive-addition-lens wearers, respectively) were related to the recruitment process at the beginning of the study rather than differential dropout during the study. Third, statistical comparisons between response proportions for the refraction technologies in the full sample remain appropriately powered. However, for subgroup analyses comparing refraction methods within each of the single vision– and progressive addition lens–wearing subgroups, some caution is advised because the implied power falls under the targeted level of 80% when the effect size, h, is equal to 0.425 or less. To aid the reader, any time an undertarget effect size was obtained, this effect size is noted along with the result to allow the reader to make their own determination. However, these subgroup analyses are included to help the reader understand potential opportunities and limitations of these technologies.

RESULTS

Refractive Dioptric Outcomes

The endpoint of the high-resolution refraction for both the right and left eyes (mean ± SD, M = −2.87 ± 2.71, J0 = −0.09 ± 0.43, J45 = 0.05 ± 0.32) occupied the same dioptric space as the standard refraction (M = −3.15 ± 2.80, J0 = −0.10 ± 0.45, J45 = 0.07 ± 0.29) and habitual lenses (M = −2.93 ± 2.65, J0 = −0.04 ± 0.39, J45 = 0.06 ± 0.24) for the single-vision wearers, as well as the progressive-addition-lens wearers (high-resolution refraction, mean ± SD, M = −1.32 ± 2.49, J0 = 0.13 ± 0.36, J45 = −0.02 ± 0.36; standard refraction, mean ± SD, M = −1.41 ± 2.58, J0 = 0.13 ± 0.38, J45 = 0.01 ± 0.22; and habitual lenses M = −1.55 ± 2.51, J0 = 0.06 ± 0.33, J45 = 0.02 ± 0.29).

Pairwise agreement between habitual, standard, and high-resolution refractions was assessed by calculating the power component (M, J0, J45) differences within each participant using both right and left eye data. As expected, minor differences were apparent because of increased resolution in the high-resolution refractions (i.e., fixed, dioptric step size of 0.01 vs. 0.25 D). However, robust one-sample Hotelling T2 tests found no significant differences from the origin (0,0,0) for M, J0, and J45. Furthermore, the clinically small observed differences fell well within the limits of agreement previously reported for refractions (e.g., 0.12 D with 95% limits of agreement of −0.90 to +0.65 D).28

Visual Acuity with Refractive Endpoint

The logMAR binocular visual acuities comparing habitual, standard, and high-resolution refractions were better than 0.00 at distance (4 m) (mean ± SD, −0.13 ± 0.01, −0.14 ± 0.07, and − 0.13 ± 0.07, respectfully) and near (40 cm) (−0.10 ± 0.08, −0.09 ± 0.08, and − 0.09 ± 0.07, respectfully). Because the correlation of visual acuity between right and left eyes was strong, only data from the right eye are reported for remaining analyses. The estimated LME regression coefficients for refraction method, participant group, and the interaction were not statistically significant. Thus, there was no evidence for an association between these factors and logMAR binocular acuity.

Vision Preference with Glasses

All participants reported wearing the lenses 6 hours or more daily.

The single vision–wearing group preferred the standard over the high-resolution refraction on 16 of the 18 attributes queried regarding their lenses. The percentage difference across all attributes, however, was small %Δ = 8.39 (95% CI, 5.26 to 11.52). The attributes related to quality of vision when looking across the room, perception of adaptation speed, ease of adaptation, and overall preference had the largest differences. For example, for perceived speed of adaptation, more single-vision wearers preferred the standard refraction over high-resolution refraction, that is, 59.5% (95% CI, 43.3 to 74.4%) versus 40.5% (95% CI, 25.6 to 56.7%), respectively; Bayesian estimation indicated that the probability of preference for the standard lenses was 0.96.

Progressive-addition-lens wearers, however, preferred the high-resolution refraction for 17 of the 18 attributes queried regarding their lenses, although, across all attributes, the percentage difference was small %Δ = 6.55 (95% CI, 3.61 to 9.49). The attributes related to vision quality across the room and mobile phone use, vision clarity, ease of distance to near transitions, and overall preference had the largest percentage differences. For example, for vision quality with mobile phone use, more progressive-addition-lens wearers preferred the high-resolution refraction over the standard, that is, 70.0% (95% CI, 45.7 to 88.1%) versus 30.0% (95% CI, 11.9 to 54.3%), respectively; Bayesian estimation indicated that the probability of preference for the high-resolution refraction was 0.99.

In summary, when pooling the progressive-addition-lens and single-vision wearers, there were no differences in the participant's preference of standard or high-resolution refraction on the different attributes queried. While looking at the groups individually, single-vision and progressive-addition-lens wearers each had attributes in which they preferred one refraction type over another; however, the percentage differences were less than 10%.

Frequency of Visual Symptoms

The overall pattern of symptom frequency for headaches, ghosting, eyestrain, and dizziness with the spectacle lenses was similar across habitual, standard, and high-resolution refraction prescription glasses equipment, with most participants experiencing the symptoms sometimes, rarely (modal eyestrain response), or never (modal response for headaches, ghosting, and dizziness). Although rank sum tests found no statistical evidence for differences among habitual, standard, and high-resolution refraction glasses for headaches, ghosting, or dizziness, the omnibus test for eyestrain was statistically significant (Friedman χ22 = 11.1, P = .004). Results of follow-up pairwise comparisons showed that eyestrain ratings were significantly lower for the standard and high-resolution refraction in comparison with the habitual glasses among progressive-addition-lens wearers only. A Bayesian multilevel model was fit to the ordinal ratings aggregated across all symptoms to compare population-level effects of the glasses, and although marginal means for symptoms frequency were lower for the standard and high-resolution refraction, there was no evidence to support a difference in symptom frequency. Overall, single-vision and progressive-addition-lens wearers did not experience a difference in visual symptoms between the two technologies for the tested lenses.

Participant Perceptions of Technology

Speed and Efficiency

There were consistently strong preferences for the high-resolution refraction over the standard refraction in terms of participant perception of both efficiency and speed (Fig. 3). At the individual level, responses between speed and efficiency preferences exhibited strong positive associations with Spearman rank-order correlations of 0.91, 0.61, and 0.76 for single-vision wearers, progressive-addition-lens wearers, and all participants, respectively.

For the preference of refraction speed, more participants preferred the high-resolution refraction over the standard, that is, 82.9% (95% CI, 66.3 to 93.4%), 92.6% (95% CI, 77.7 to 99.1%), and 87.1% (95% CI, 76.1 to 94.3%) for single-vision wearers, progressive-addition-lens wearers, and the full sample, respectively. For the preference of refraction efficiency, more participants preferred the high-resolution refraction over the standard, that is, 77.1% (95% CI, 46.0 to 83.5%), 66.7% (95% CI, 46.0 to 83.4%), and 72.6% (95% CI, 59.8 to 83.1%) for single-vision wearers, progressive-addition-lens wearers, and the full sample, respectively. Although the effect size h = 0.34 for progressive-addition-lens wearers fell below threshold for 80% power, Bayesian estimation indicated that the probability of preference for the high-resolution refraction ranged from 0.96 to >0.99. In summary, both the single-vision and progressive-addition-lens wearers preferred the high-resolution refraction over the standard when considering the speed and efficiency of the test.

Refraction Preference

Participants rated their preference between the refractions on perceived quality, confidence (in the result provided), accuracy, decision stress level, and comfort level; the percentage of participants who endorsed each preference response category for (a) single-vision wearers (n = 49), (b) progressive-addition-lens wearers (n = 39), and (c) all participants (n = 88) is shown in Fig. 4. Within each study group (as well as the full sample), Bayesian analysis for quality, stress, comfort, and overall satisfaction showed that the likelihood of preference for the high-resolution refraction is more than 0.5 by probabilities 0.92, 0.96, >0.99, and 0.92, respectively. For accuracy, the likelihood of preference for the high-resolution refraction greater than 0.5 was 0.92 in progressive-addition-lens wearers, but only 0.38 in single-vision wearers. In summary, participants preferred the high-resolution refraction for its perceived quality, stress, comfort, and overall satisfaction.

Confidence with Refraction and Glasses

Among the 111 participants who rated both technologies, 55 (49.5%; 95% CI, 39.9 to 59.2%) reported having the same confidence level with both standard and high-resolution refractions. Of the remaining 56 participants, 30 (53.6%; 95% CI, 39.7 to 67.0%) endorsed a higher level of confidence with the high-resolution refraction and glasses compared with 26 (46.4%; 95% CI, 33.0 to 60.3%) who reported higher confidence with the standard refraction and glasses. Although the effect size h = 0.14 for the difference of proportions fell below threshold for 80% power, Bayesian estimation showed that the likelihood of confidence in refraction and glasses was higher for the high-resolution solution by a probability of 0.77 compared with the standard one.

Perceived Benefits of High-resolution Refraction Technology to Clinical Practice

Most participants endorsed “Agree” and “Strongly Agree” when asked if the high-resolution refraction provided better eye care than standard and if it enhanced the skills of the optometrist (Fig. 5). Regarding better eye care, the combined agreement percentages were 79.6 (95% CI, 65.6 to 89.8%), 71.2 (95% CI, 55.1 to 85.0%), and 78.4% (95% CI, 69.3 to 86.5%) for single-vision wearers, progressive-addition-lens wearers, and all participants, respectively. Although the effect size h = 0.44 for progressive-addition-lens wearers fell slightly below threshold for 80% power, Bayesian analysis determined that the likelihood of the agreement proportions being more than 0.5 was >0.99 in all three groups.

DISCUSSION

Both the high-resolution and standard refraction techniques were able to successfully determine the refractive error of participants and yielded wearable glasses.

Refraction/Algorithm

As anticipated by the differing resolutions of the two refraction techniques and by the typical variability of refractions within an eye and between examiners,29 small differences exist in the refraction outcomes. For example, in all participants, at least one parameter (M, J0, J45) differed by 0.12 D or more in at least one eye between the high-resolution and standard refractions. However, these differences were neither statistically nor clinically significant. Although, dioptrically, the prescriptions occupied the same space, there were some interesting findings. For instance, the high-resolution refraction algorithm found that participants subjectively appreciated some level of astigmatism correction in all but one prescription. Many of these levels of astigmatism were smaller than the typical 0.25 D, which indicates a potential opportunity for practitioners to correct their patient's vision more optimally. This finding combined with the fact that ease of adaptation with the high-resolution prescription was good suggests another potential opportunity to more fully correct participant's vision.

Entering visual acuities within the study were good, and accordingly, outcomes of either refraction technique remained quite good; thus, the standard and high-resolution refractions provided appropriate refractions.

The novel algorithm successfully determined a prescription in all participants, regardless of refractive level and pupil size, among others. All prescriptions were well tolerated, and virtually all (109/113) prescriptions were prescribed entirely from the algorithm without practitioner adjustment. The four prescriptions in which the practitioner made modifications were all due to the practitioner wanting to prescribe slightly larger add powers than the algorithm found or the practitioner decision to not prescribe very small or nonstandard levels of cylinder. In everyday practice, however, a practitioner's judgment on the final prescription could take into consideration habitual lens information before finalizing a prescription.

Spectacle Lenses

Overall, participants found spectacle lenses from both refractions highly acceptable and were able to adapt to lens wear. Because logMAR visual acuity is known to be insensitive to small refractive differences,30,31 quality measures of vision with the lenses were acquired regarding various daily activities participants performed over a 2-week wear period.

Subjectively, the spectacle lenses from both refractions were highly acceptable, yet differences were noticed between single vision– and progressive addition lens–wearing groups. Single vision–wearing participants generally preferred the standard refraction glasses for adaptation quickness and looking at distance across the room. This second attribute could be a result of a small yet nonsignificant difference between the subject's habitual and the high-resolution prescription. This finding highlights an opportunity for optometrists to refine a patient's prescription based on their subjective feedback and history after the high-resolution refraction.

Alternatively, progressive addition lens–wearing participants preferred the high-resolution refraction technology for smartphone use. We hypothesize that the high-resolution refraction afforded participants better visual quality because there was less minus in their overall distance prescription or adding a slight astigmatic correction further enhanced their near visual acuity. Because people spend an estimated 3 hours per day using a smartphone,32 there is significant opportunity for high-resolution technology to enhance the vision of our patient's daily lives.

Participant Perceptions of Technology

Participants significantly preferred the high-resolution refraction for its quickness and efficiency, improved comfort, and less stress during the high-resolution refraction. Participants indicated no difference in perceived quality of the refractions but did indicate higher confidence in the high-resolution refraction. Results also indicated stronger interest and likelihood of “seeking out” (74%) a provider with the technology and recommending it (77%) compared with standard refraction. In addition, 78% of participants said that the high-resolution technology provided better eye care than the standard, and 65% said that it enhanced the skills of their provider. No other digital technology was evaluated in this investigation, but some of the same benefits may be expected from other digital technologies that, which allow refraction, prescription, and the ordering of lenses up to 0.01 D.

There is a learning curve associated with the new technology, however. For example, the algorithm led refraction is performed in a different way than what is commonly performed. The practitioner can “bypass” the automated refraction process and perform the steps as normally; however, this leads to typical resolution refractions and not those of higher resolution. Furthermore, although the timing of the refractions is not explored here, the participants felt that the refractions were more efficient with the high-resolution technology. However, like the use of any new technology, any new user may need to commit time to learning to navigate the high-resolution phoropter and refraction process. Furthermore, although many participants were overall quite receptive to this type of technology, it, of course, may not be a great fit for all patients. Some patients may perceive the high-resolution refraction to be a bit less personal than the standard refraction (e.g., practitioner's physical distance or focus on the unit display).

It is important to acknowledge that one limitation to this study is the presence of a placebo effect. With our focus on exploring the refraction/prescribing process as would occur in a clinical setting, participants were inherently unmasked to the refraction they experienced. Second, although it had face validity, the questionnaire used was not previously otherwise validated; thus, there is no knowledge of its repeatability in quantifying attributes in a person's daily life. Lastly, there was approximately a 1.50-D difference in refractive error between the single-vision and progressive-addition-lens study groups. We believe that this reflects the distribution of lens wearers (e.g., younger myopic eyes need to wear glasses earlier), but this may have had an effect on subject outcomes.

SUMMARY

In summary, the high-resolution technology evaluated in the current study yielded accurate refraction results through a process with potential benefits from both the patient and practitioner. Likewise, when higher-resolution refractions are prescribed in lenses, key advantages remain for wearers, which may have a positive impact on their daily lives. Given these advantages, incorporating this type of high-resolution technology into clinical practices warrants consideration.
