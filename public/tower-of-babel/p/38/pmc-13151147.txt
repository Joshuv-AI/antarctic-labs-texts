Metformin attenuates HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain by restoring Sirt3-mediated mitophagy
Authors: Yuanyuan Fang, Mengqiu Deng, Kesheng Huang, Honghao Song, Yan Chu, Huawei Wei, Dashuang Chen, Yutong Yang, Chaofeng Han, Hongbin Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Varicella-zoster virus reactivation causes neuroinflammation and post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN), in which the mechanism needs to be further explored. Given that Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection induces a similar neuropathological process, a corresponding model was established for this study. Here we reported that Sirt3 expression decreased in spinal dorsal horn and microglia upon HSV-1 infection-induced neuropathic pain model, which was accompanied by increased mitochondria ROS and disrupted mitophagy. Sirt3 deficiency inhibited the anti-viral innate immunity responses, increased viral loads and mitochondrial ROS production, disrupted mitophagy both in vivo and in vitro. Sirt3 knockdown replicated the cellular responses, while Sirt3 overexpression enhanced the antiviral innate immunity in microglia. Mechanism research revealed that HSV-1 infection disrupted CREB-mediated Sirt3 transcription. AMPK agonist metformin treatment could increase CREB-mediated Sirt3 transcription and enhance anti-viral innate immunity both in vitro and in vivo. In sum, our study reveals the inhibition of CREB-mediated Sirt3 transcription might be the pathology of HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain, which can be blocked by metformin.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02841-z.

Introduction

PHN is a chronic neuropathic pain and pruritus syndrome that persists for more than three months after healing of varicella‑zoster vesicles and impairs patients’ quality of life [1, 2]. The clinical course of PHN follows well‑defined stages: primary varicella infection, viral latency in sensory ganglia, reactivation as herpes zoster with a dermatomal vesicular eruption, and the subsequent persistence of neuropathic pain after rash resolution. Population‑based studies estimate the risk of PHN at approximately 5–32.5%. Incidence increases markedly with advancing age, a pattern commonly attributed to age‑related declines in immunity (immunosenescence), which reduce control of varicella‑zoster virus reactivation [3, 4]. The development of PHN results from reactivation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV) in sensory ganglia. Immunosuppression permits viral reactivation; the reactivated virus then undergoes anterograde axonal transport to the corresponding dermatome via sensory nerve pathways, producing the characteristic painful vesicular eruptions of that dermatome [5]. To evade host defenses, herpesviruses deploy sophisticated mechanisms that subvert mitochondrial function, since mitochondria are critical hubs for innate immune signaling and cellular bioenergetics [6]. Recent research shows that herpesvirus infection induces specific mitochondrial remodeling, characterized by reduced mitochondrial volume and a shift to a more spherical shape, which impairs antiviral defenses [7]. The limited effectiveness of existing therapies underscores the inadequacy of current treatment approaches and highlights the need for deeper mechanistic understanding [8, 9]. VZV’s strict species-specific neurotropism, which precludes the development of physiologically relevant animal models, remains a major impediment to VZV research. VZV and HSV-1 both belong to the human neurotropic alpha-herpesvirus family and establish latency in peripheral ganglia following primary infection. Given that HSV-1 can simulate natural viral transmission and is more convenient to maintain, this feature led to its selection for our animal model. Consequently, most preclinical studies employ HSV‑1 infection in rodent models to recapitulate the neuropathology and chronic pain phenotypes associated with PHN [10, 11]. To investigate the pathogenesis of PHN, we established a murine model via intradermal inoculation of HSV-1 into the left tibial skin surface [11]. This model of viral neuropathy partially recapitulates the pathological features of human PHN, while differences in latency establishment and clinical progression between HSV-1 and VZV are acknowledged.

Mitochondria act as central hubs of innate immunity, orchestrating inflammatory responses to infection and acute injury and coordinating antimicrobial defenses against viral and bacterial pathogens [12]. Mitophagy, a selective form of macroautophagy, targets damaged mitochondria for lysosomal degradation via autophagosomal encapsulation [13]. Mitochondrial oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and mitophagic flux are tightly interconnected: mitophagy mitigates ROS and maintains redox homeostasis by removing dysfunctional mitochondria, whereas excess ROS induce mitochondrial membrane potential collapse and oxidative modification of proteins, lipids and nucleic acids. Impaired mitophagy causes accumulation of defective mitochondria, reduced electron transport chain efficiency and decreased ATP production, further driving ROS overproduction and bioenergetic failure [14, 15]. Mitophagy is a critical immunomodulatory mechanism in this context, preserving mitochondrial homeostasis by selectively removing dysfunctional organelles and modulating inflammatory signaling [16–18]. Mitophagy coordinates the survival and metabolic programs essential for memory T cell formation; its dysfunction compromises T cell–mediated antiviral immunity [19]. Cells of the central nervous system are particularly susceptible to mitochondrial dysfunction, and mitophagy maintains mitochondrial homeostasis under stress, thereby limiting release of proinflammatory cytokines [20]. Herpesvirus infection disrupts mitophagic flux and provokes mitochondrial damage, whereas mitophagy is essential for coordinating antiviral immune responses and attenuating neuroinflammation [21]. The precise modulatory role of mitophagy in herpesvirus-induced Postherpetic neuralgia pathogenesis remains to be elucidated.

The sirtuin family, particularly the mitochondrial isoforms SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5, functions as central metabolic regulators, with SIRT3 serving as the principal guardian of mitochondrial integrity and homeostasis [22]. SIRT3 is an NAD+-dependent deacetylase that dynamically regulates the acetylation of mitochondrial metabolic proteins, thereby preserving mitochondrial structural and functional integrity by modulating key processes such as pH homeostasis, membrane potential and fission–fusion dynamics [23, 24]. SIRT3 promotes mitophagy by activating the PINK1/Parkin-LC3B-autophagosomal complex [25–27]. SIRT3 epigenetically suppresses transcription of hepatitis B virus (HBV) covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) via cooperative interaction with histone methyltransferase(s), providing a mechanistic rationale for developing SIRT3 activators as prophylactic or therapeutic agents against HBV infection [28]. The deacetylase activity of SIRT3 is indispensable for suppressing viral replication [29]. However, the mechanisms by which SIRT3‑mediated inhibition of herpesvirus replication might attenuate PHN pathogenesis remain uncharacterized.

Beyond its ability to decrease blood sugar, metformin, the first-line medication for type 2 diabetes mellitus, has a variety of biological effects, such as strong anti-inflammatory reactions, metabolic reprogramming, and mitochondrial cytoprotection [30, 31]. Metformin activates a SIRT3–AMPK axis: SIRT3 preserves mitochondrial proteostasis via NAD+-dependent deacetylation of metabolic enzymes, while AMPK senses cellular energy to coordinate adaptive metabolic responses [32]. By coordinating mitochondrial biogenesis, fission dynamics, and mitophagic flux, AMPK serves as a principal metabolic regulator that sustains mitochondrial quality and homeostasis [33, 34]. SIRT3 enhances phosphorylation of AMPK, thereby promoting macroautophagy via multiple mechanisms, including direct activation of core autophagy‑initiating proteins such as Beclin1 [35, 36]. Metformin, a widely used hypoglycemic agent, activates AMPK and upregulates SIRT3, yielding cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects via enhanced mitophagy and inhibition of mTOR signaling. In viral infection models, it activates RIG-I-like receptor-mediated innate immunity, suppresses viral replication and structural protein synthesis, and reduces viral titers. By promoting mitophagy of dysfunctional mitochondria, depleting cellular energy reserves, inhibiting viral protein synthesis, and attenuating oxidative stress, metformin limits replication of diverse viruses and may therefore have therapeutic potential in PHN [37–39]. Given SIRT3’s dual role in mitochondrial proteostasis and antiviral defense, this study systematically examines its pathophysiological involvement in HSV‑1‑induced neuropathic pain via modulation of mitophagic flux. We further validate metformin as a therapeutic agent that restores mitochondrial homeostasis through the SIRT3–AMPK signaling axis, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for mitochondrial-targeted analgesic strategies in neuropathic pain.

Materials and methods

Mice

Male C57BL/6J wild‑type mice (weighing 20–25 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center, Naval Medical University (Shanghai, China). Sirt3 knockout (Sirt3-/-) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Animals were maintained under specific pathogen‑free (SPF) conditions at 24 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 5% relative humidity, on a 12‑h light/dark cycle, with food and water available ad libitum. All procedures were performed in accordance with China’s Regulations on the Management of Laboratory Animals and the guidelines of AAALAC International. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Naval Medical University (Approval No. SYXK[Hu]2022‑0011).

HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain model

An HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain mouse model was established using strain 7401 H. To minimize potential confounding effects of fluctuating estrogen levels on neuroinflammation and pain signaling, only male mice were used; this design is supported by previous literature [40]. Male C57BL/6J mice had hair removed from the left tibial skin surface followed by chemical depilation. The tibial skin surface was scarified with a 27-gauge needle at 3-day intervals and topically inoculated with 10 µL HSV-1 suspension (TCID50=10−6.57/0.1mL). Heat-inactivated viral suspension (60 °C, 1 h) was given to control groups; inactivation was verified by plaque assay, which revealed no cytopathic effects [11]. Our experimental results confirm that inactivated virus inoculation does not induce chronic pain in mice, with no significant differences observed in thermal or mechanical pain sensitivity compared to the PBS group (Sup Fig. 1A and B).

Viral load within spinal cord tissue was measured by quantitative PCR (qPCR). The thymidine kinase (TK) gene is highly conserved in HSV‑1 and correlates strongly with viral titer; therefore it is commonly used as a reliable marker for quantifying HSV‑1 viral load [41, 42]. Amplification targeted the HSV-1 thymidine kinase (TK) gene, using the following primers: Forward: 5′-ATACCGACGATATGCGACCT-3′,Reverse: 5′-TTATTGCCGTCATAGCGCGG-3′. Amplification products were analyzed by Cq.

This study employed 8-week-old male C57BL/6J and Sirt3-/- mice to assess metformin’s effects. Mice of both genotypes were randomly allocated into two groups (n = 7 per group): one receiving vehicle (drinking water) and the other receiving metformin (5 mg/mL in water; estimated daily dose ~ 1000 mg/kg/day) concomitant with HSV-1 infection. Metformin administration continued for 24 consecutive days.

Measurement of mechanical and thermal pain thresholds

Thresholds were evaluated in a double‑blind manner in a quiet room between 09:00 and 15:00. Before testing began, mice were allowed 30 min to acclimate to the testing environment.

Von Frey filaments (North Coast Medical) were used to assess mechanical sensitivity in HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain model. Filaments weighing 0.07 g (low threshold) and 0.4 g (high threshold) were applied perpendicularly to the ipsilateral hindpaw plantar surface until they bent and were held in place for 5 s. Each filament was tested 10 times, with 5‑minute intervals between stimuli. The number of positive responses (paw withdrawal or licking) was recorded to calculate the response rate (positive responses / total stimuli × 100%) [43].

An electronic von Frey filament (Stoelting, USA) was applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface of the left hindpaw as an alternative method to measure mechanical allodynia. Withdrawal thresholds were measured in grams (g).

Thermal Hyperalgesia Assessment: Mice were placed in transparent plexiglass chambers (25 cm × 15 cm × 10 cm). A radiant heat source (set at 50% intensity) was focused on the plantar surface of the left hind paw using a plantar analgesia meter (Model 37360, Ugo Basile). Paw withdrawal latency was recorded from the onset of the heat stimulus until withdrawal or licking behavior. To prevent tissue damage, a 20-second cutoff was enforced. Each paw was tested five times at 5-minute intervals, and the mean latency for each paw was calculated and used as the final readout.

BV-2 cell and primary microglia culture

High-glucose DMEM mixed with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was used to cultivate the BV-2 microglial cell line (Quicell Biosciences, Shanghai, China). The culture media was changed every day until 80% confluence was achieved, and the cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Mice from postnatal days 1–2 were used to isolate primary microglia. Brains were dissected in ice-cold PBS after being surface sterilized in 70% ethanol. Sterile microscopy was used to remove the meninges and vasculature. After being diced (less than 1 mm3), the cortical tissue was digested with 0.125% trypsin (at 37 °C for 15 min) and neutralized with full medium (DMEM/F12 + 10% FBS). Pellets were reconstituted and seeded in T25 flasks following centrifugation (200 × g, 5 min). Every four days, the medium was changed while the cultures were kept at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Day 14 involved centrifuging the microglia (200 ×g, 5 min), resuspending them in serum-free media, and agitating the flask (180 rpm, 37 °C, 2 h) to separate them. In order to obtain > 95% pure microglia (defined as CD11b⁺IBA1⁺ dual-positive cells), the suspension was plated in 24-well plates (30 min adhesion) and then the media was completely replaced.

Western blotting

Spinal cord dorsal horn tissues or cell pellets were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer (supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail), followed by ultrasonication (3 × 10-s pulses) and centrifugation (12,000×g, 15 min, 4 °C). Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg/lane) were resolved on 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies (overnight, 4 °C) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (2 h, RT). Immunoreactive bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and quantified densitometrically using ImageJ software.

Anti-Sirt3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology [CST], #5490), Anti-IBA-1 (1:200; CST, #17198), Anti-phospho-CREB (Ser133) (1:1000; CST, #9198), Anti-CREB (1:1000; Proteintech, 12208-1-AP), Anti-H3 (1:1000; Proteintech, 17168-1-AP), Anti-AMPKα (1:1000; CST, #2532), Anti-phospho-AMPKα (Thr172) (1:1000; CST, #2535), Anti-β-actin (1:50,000; Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig).

qRT -PCR

Total RNA was isolated from dorsal horn spinal cord tissue, dorsal root ganglia, or BV-2 cells using Freezol reagent (Accurate Biology, FC1001). RNA purity (A260/A280: 1.8–2.0) and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA underwent reverse transcription into cDNA with the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme, R333) under cycling conditions of 50 °C for 15 min followed by 85 °C for 5 s. Synthesized cDNA was stored at -20 °C. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) reactions (20 µL) contained 2 µL cDNA, 0.4 µM each primer (Supplementary Table 1), 10 µL ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711), and nuclease-free ddH₂O. β-Actin served as the endogenous reference gene, and relative expression levels were determined via the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Immunofluorescence

Mouse spinal cord lumbar enlargement tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-µm-thick slices. Following antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) in a pressure cooker (121 °C for 15 min), tissue sections were blocked with 3% H₂O₂ and then with 10% goat serum to prevent nonspecific binding. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies and then probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal visualization was performed using diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining of the nuclei. Finally, histological images were acquired using bright-field microscopy.

Anti-Sirt3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology [CST], #5490), Anti-IBA-1 (1:200; CST, #17198), anti-NeuN antibody (1:200, Abcam, #ab177487), anti-GFAP antibody (1:200, Abcam, #ab7260).

Transfection

Sirt3 knockdown and overexpression were achieved using siRNA (synthesized by OBIO Biotechnology; sequences in Supplementary Table 2) and pCDH-Sirt3 plasmid (details in Supplementary Table 3), respectively. BV-2 microglial cells seeded at 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL were transfected at 30–50% confluency with jetPRIME® reagent (Polyplus). For siRNA-mediated knockdown, 20 nM siRNA complexed with jetPRIME (1:2 w/w ratio) was incubated for 6 h before medium replacement; for plasmid overexpression, 2 µg/mL plasmid was complexed with jetPRIME (1 µg:2 µL ratio) for 8 h. Post-transfection analyses included qRT-PCR at 24 h and Western blot at 48 h, with non-targeting scrambled siRNA and empty vector (EV) transfections as parallel negative controls.

Transcriptomic RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the lumbar spinal cord enlargement of mice, with three biological replicates per group (HSV‑1–infected and control). Sequencing libraries were constructed after poly(A) enrichment followed by mRNA fragmentation. The fragmented mRNA was reverse-transcribed into double‑stranded cDNA, which was then end‑repaired, A‑tailed, and ligated to adapters. cDNA fragments of approximately 200 bp were size‑selected and PCR‑amplified to generate the final libraries. After quality assessment, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform. Bioinformatic analyses, including read mapping, quantification, and differential expression analysis, were performed. The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the SRA under accession number PRJNA1049613.

Transmission electron microscopy

After transcardial perfusion with PBS and 2.5% glutaraldehyde, lumbar spinal cord samples (1 mm³) were post-fixed in glutaraldehyde (4 °C, 4 h) followed by 1% osmium tetroxide (2 h). Tissues were dehydrated through an ethanol series, embedded in epoxy resin, and polymerized (60 °C, 48 h). To evaluate mitochondrial ultrastructure and myelin integrity, ultrathin slices (60 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate/lead citrate and photographed using a TEM (Hitachi HT7800, 80 kV).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation(Ch-IP)

BV-2 cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and chromatin was sheared (200–500 bp fragments) by sonication. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4°C using anti-CREB antibody (Proteintech #12208-1-AP) or IgG control, followed by Protein A/G magnetic bead capture. After sequential washes, DNA-protein complexes were eluted, reverse-crosslinked, and purified. P-CREB binding to the Sirt3 promoter was quantified by qPCR using specific primers (F: 5’-ACAGCGTCAACTCCCACT-3’; R: 5’-ATCCGTTTCTTCACATTAGG-3’) and expressed as %Input.

Mitochondria potential detection by JC-1 staining

After trypsinization, the cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of complete media and treated with JC-1 working solution (37 °C, 20 min, dark). Positive controls received 1µM CCCP. After washing with assay buffer, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (monomers: 488/530nm; aggregates: 585/590nm). The red/green fluorescence ratio (FlowJo) was used to represent the mitochondrial membrane potential.

Reactive oxygen species(ROS)detection

Cells were loaded with 10 µM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium (37 °C, 20 min, 5% CO₂) with periodic shaking. After PBS washes, cells were detached, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS. ROS levels were quantified by flow cytometry (excitation/emission: 488/525 nm; ≥10,000 events/sample). Positive controls received 1 µM CCCP.

Statistical analysis

To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were independently repeated in triplicate. Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 (USA). Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.05), while variance homogeneity was evaluated via Bartlett’s test (P > 0.05). Parametric data are reported as mean ± SEM. For two-group comparisons, unpaired Student’s t-tests were employed; multi-group analyses utilized one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc tests following significant main effects. Longitudinal mechanical withdrawal thresholds were compared using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05.

Results

Decreased expression of Sirt3 in HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain model

HSV-1 (strain 7401 H) was inoculated intradermally into the skin over the left tibia to establish a mouse model of HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain (Fig. 1A). Neuropathic pain progression was assessed quantitatively using two established sensory modalities: thermal hyperalgesia by the Hargreaves test (Fig. 1B) and mechanical allodynia with von Frey filaments (Fig. 1C). The model reproduced the chronic-phase pathology seen in clinical PHN, showing acute nociceptive responses at 7 days post-infection (7 dpi) that subsided between 7 and 14 dpi and progressed to chronic neuropathic pain by 24 dpi. Based on the dynamics of pain progression, the chronic phase timepoint was thus conclusively determined at 24 dpi. Virological confirmation was established through four lines of evidence: positive viral DNA in the spinal dorsal horn by gel electrophoresis (Fig. 1D); elevated TK gene copy number (Fig. 1E); and upregulated expression of the viral genes ICP0 and gB measured via qPCR (Fig. 1F); detection of HSV-1 immunofluorescence in the dorsal horn of the mouse spinal cord (Sup Fig. 1C), collectively demonstrating active HSV-1 replication. HSV-1 infection triggers the antiviral innate immunity responses in mice, accompanied by significantly elevated transcription of type I interferons (IFN-I, comprising IFN-α and IFN-β) (Fig. 1G), and induces neuroinflammation with significantly elevated transcription of inflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α and IL-6) (Fig. 1H). In dorsal root ganglia (DRG), qPCR analyses showed simultaneous elevation of HSV-1 TK DNA (Sup Fig. 1D) and upregulated transcription of proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α (Sup Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that retrograde axonal transport mediated viral invasion of DRG, followed by activation of the neuroinflammatory cascade. The crucial role of microglia as neural axis immunological effectors during viral pathogenesis was quantitatively confirmed by Western blot and immunofluorescence investigations, which also showed microglial activation indicated by spinal dorsal horn IBA-1 overexpression (Fig. 1K, L, O). Transmission electron microscopy revealed ultrastructural neuropathology in infected groups, characterized by mitochondrial edema accompanied by cristae disarray (Fig. 1I) and myelin sheath decompaction (Fig. 1J), indicative of HSV-1-induced axonal damage. While mitochondrially-localized Sirt4/Sirt5 remained unaltered (Sup Fig. 1L), the mitochondrial deacetylase Sirt3 notably displayed significant downregulation at both transcriptional (Fig. 1N) and protein levels (Fig. 1M, O, P). Moreover, the partial co-localization of SIRT3 and HSV-1 in mouse spinal cord (Sup Fig. L-N) further supports the notion that SIRT3 downregulation may be associated with HSV-1 infection. These results suggest that decreased Sirt3 expression might be involved in the mitochondrial dysfunction of HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain pathology.

Sirt3 deficiency enhances HSV-1 viral load and exacerbates neuroinflammation

To further explore the function and mechanism of Sirt3 in HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain, the Sirt3-/- HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain murine model was successfully established with validated knockout efficiency through Western blot analysis (Fig. 2A) and genomic DNA agarose gel electrophoresis(Sup Fig. 1F). Behavioral assessments revealed significantly exacerbated thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 2B) and mechanical allodynia (Fig. 2C) in Sirt3-/- group compared to Sirt3+/- group post HSV-1 infection. Ultrastructural analysis via transmission electron microscopy revealed aggravated mitochondrial cristae disorganization (Fig. 2D) and axonal demyelination (Fig. 2E) in Sirt3-/- mice. Virological quantification showed suppressed IFN-I transcription (Fig. 2F), along with increased HSV-1 TK DNA load (Fig. 2G), upregulated expression of HSV-1 genes ICP0 and gB (Fig. 2H), and enhanced proinflammatory cytokine (TNF-α, IL-6) expression (Fig. 2I). Immunofluorescence co-localization confirmed predominant Sirt3 expression within IBA-1⁺ microglia (Fig. 2J, K, L), indicating cell-type-specific regulatory mechanisms in PHN progression. The spinal dorsal horn showed increased expression of the microglial marker IBA-1 (Fig. 2A, J) but constant astrocytic GFAP levels (Fig. 2K) according to WB and immunofluorescence analysis. 302 differentially expressed genes (145 upregulated and 157 downregulated, Sup Fig. 1G) were found by genome-wide transcriptomic profiling. Functional enrichment analysis (GO/KEGG) revealed compromised innate immune responses (Sup Fig. 1H) and dysregulated phagosome pathways (Sup Fig. 1I). All of these results show that microglial Sirt3 downregulation significantly worsens HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain pathogenesis by impairing antiviral immunity and increasing neuroinflammation. These findings suggest that Sirt3 deficiency decreases antiviral innate immunity and promotes HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain after HSV-1 infection.

HSV-1 Infection induces mitochondria dysfunction and inhibits Sirt3 transcription in microglia

To elucidate microglial roles in HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain pathogenesis, we found that HSV-1 infection of BV-2 cells led to: elevated expression of type I interferon (Fig. 3A), increased HSV-1 TK DNA load (Fig. 3B), upregulation of ICP0 and gB (Fig. 3C), and enhanced expression of the proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α (Fig. 3D). This was accompanied by downregulation of Sirt3 at both the transcriptional and protein levels (Fig. 3E, F). P62 serves as a selective autophagy receptor that undergoes lysosomal degradation via autophagic flux; its pathological accumulation signifies impaired autophagic clearance [44]. A hallmark of autophagy induction is the lipidation of LC3-I to form LC3-II, which incorporates into the growing autophagosomal membrane [45]. As a key regulator of autophagosome initiation, decreased Beclin 1 expression indicates impaired autophagosome formation [46]. HSV-1 infection impaired mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 3G) and induced mitophagy dysfunction characterized by P62 accumulation, decreased Beclin1 expression, elevated LC3II/LC3I ratio (Fig. 3H, I). Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation showed predominant nuclear localization of P-CREB/CREB, with HSV-1 reducing CREB levels (Fig. 3J). CREB and Sirt3 expression in human spinal cord tissues were found to be positively correlated by GEPIA database analysis (Fig. 3K). Reduced P-CREB binding efficiency at the Sirt3 gene promoter after infection was verified by ChIP-qPCR (Fig. 3L). These results suggest that the virus infection disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressed Sirt3 expression via the CREB-mediated Sirt3 transcription. Although HSV-1 infection decreased the binding of CREB to the Sirt3 promoter, its transcriptional regulation of Sirt3 may be modulated by other factors, such as HDAC2, which requires further validation.

Sirt3 overexpression increases viral innate immunity and suppresses HSV-1 replication by restoring mitophagy

Sirt3 overexpression increases viral innate immunity and suppresses HSV-1 replication by restoring mitophagy

To further validate the therapeutic potential of Sirt3 in treating HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain, we overexpressed Sirt3 in BV-2 cells (with overexpression efficiency verified in Sup Fig. 2G, H). This manipulation significantly upregulated type I interferon expression (Fig. 5A), reduced the HSV-1 TK DNA load (Fig. 5B), downregulated the expression of HSV-1 genes ICP0 and gB (Fig. 5C), and decreased proinflammatory cytokine transcription (Fig. 5D). Concurrently, Sirt3 overexpression maintained mitochondrial membrane potential stability (Figs. 5E-I), attenuated ROS accumulation (Figs. 5J, K), and improved mitophagic function as evidenced by reduced P62 accumulation, increased Beclin-1 expression, and elevated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (Fig. 5L, M). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed enhanced Tom20-LC3 colocalization in Sirt3-overexpressing cells (Sup Fig. 2I-M), indicating improved mitophagosome formation efficiency. These findings indicate that Sirt3 overexpression suppresses HSV-1 replication and alleviates neuroinflammation by restoring mitophagic flux and enhancing antiviral immune responses.

Metformin enhances antiviral immunity by regulating mitophagy via the Sirt3-AMPK pathway

To explore the antiviral mechanism of metformin, we pretreated microglia with metformin prior to HSV-1 infection and assessed cellular changes. Mitochondrial functional assays demonstrated that metformin increased Sirt3 protein expression (Fig. 6H), maintained mitochondrial membrane potential stability (Figs. 6A-E) and reduced ROS accumulation (Figs. 6F, G). Concurrently, metformin restored mitophagic function, evidenced by enhanced P62 degradation, elevated Beclin-1 expression, and increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (Fig. 6H, I), along with strengthened Tom20-LC3 colocalization (Sup Fig. 3A-E). Metformin maintains mitochondrial homeostasis af ter viral infection through upregulation of Sirt3 expression. Pretreatment with metformin (0.5-1 mmol/mL) upregulated type I interferons (Fig. 6J), suppressed HSV-1 TK DNA expression in microglia (Fig. 6K), downregulated the expression of HSV-1 genes ICP0 and gB (Fig. 6L), and reduced the levels of proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6M). Metformin may upregulate the expression of the transcription factor CREB (Fig. 6N), which in turn promotes the transcription of Sirt3, thereby enhancing Sirt3 expression. Furthermore, while metformin increased P-AMPK protein levels, this effect was completely abolished upon Sirt3 knockdown (Fig. 6P). These data demonstrate that the AMPK signaling critically regulates both mitophagy enhancement and antiviral immune potentiation.

Metformin alleviates HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain by upregulating Sirt3 to enhance mitophagy and antiviral innate immunity

This study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which metformin exerts its therapeutic effects on HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain through its regulatory association with Sirt3. Oral metformin treatment dramatically reduced thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 7A) and plantar allodynia (Figs. 7B, C) in wild-type mice. Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated that metformin promoted mitophagosome formation in the spinal dorsal horn (Figs. 7D, E; red arrows indicate mitophagosomes) and attenuated axonal demyelination (Figs. 7F, G), while inhibiting microglial activation (reduced IBA-1 expression, Sup Fig. 4A-D). However, GFAP expression remained unchanged (Sup Fig. 4E, F), indicating a potential lack of effect of metformin on astrocytic activation. This was accompanied by increased interferon transcription (Fig. 7H), decreased HSV-1 viral load (Fig. 7I), downregulated the expression of HSV-1 genes ICP0 and gB (Fig. 7J), and decreased expression of proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 7K, L). Metformin upregulated Sirt3 expression in WT mice (Sup Fig. 4G, H), suggesting that this enhancement may underlie its therapeutic effect against HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain. Importantly, these therapeutic effects were completely abolished in Sirt3-/- mice, as evidenced that metformin showed no significant alleviation of pain behaviors (Figs. 7M-O), viral clearance (Sup Fig. 4I), inflammatory suppression (Sup Fig. 4J), IFN-I expression (Sup Fig. 4K), mitophagy and demyelination pathology improvement (Figs. 7P-S), or microglial activation (Sup Fig. 4L, M). HSV-1 infection downregulates Sirt3 expression in host cells, whereas metformin treatment counteracts this effect and enhances Sirt3 levels. The metformin‑mediated upregulation of Sirt3 likely suppresses HSV-1 replication and spread through mechanisms that include improved mitochondrial function, enhanced autophagic activity, and modulated antiviral immune responses, thereby providing important experimental evidence supporting the potential therapeutic use of metformin in antiviral strategies. These results conclusively demonstrate that metformin alleviates HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain through Sirt3-dependent enhancement of mitophagic flux, potentiation of antiviral innate immunity, and attenuation of neuroinflammation, thereby providing a mechanistic foundation for its clinical translation.

Discussion

The risk of postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) increases markedly with age, reaching approximately 12.5% in individuals older than 50 years, and an estimated 500,000 zoster recurrences per year in the United States alone may progress to PHN, whose persistent pain substantially impairs quality of life [47–49]. PHN is initiated by ganglionic reactivation of VZV, leading to degeneration of sensory fibers and central sensitization in the spinal dorsal horn, so that even mild tactile stimuli can evoke allodynia; contributory mechanisms include loss of inhibitory interneurons, upregulation of sodium and calcium channels with resulting neuronal hyperexcitability, and potentiation of NMDA receptor–dependent excitatory signaling [47–49]. Because VZV is human specific, we used an HSV-1 hindlimb infection model in mice to reproduce key features of PHN: HSV-1 invades the CNS via retrograde axonal transport and, following reactivation from latency, drives renewed replication, neuroinflammation, and chronic pain that stabilizes around day 24 [10, 50, 51]. It should be noted that detectable HSV-1 replication in this model makes it representative of a sustained, virus-associated neuropathological state rather than the post-latent clinical phenotype of PHN; differences in latency establishment and clinical course between HSV-1 and VZV, and the unresolved question of whether microglia can sustain productive HSV-1 infection, warrant careful consideration and further experimental study [52]. Consistent with prior reports that HSV-1 impairs mitophagy, TEM revealed pronounced mitochondrial structural damage in the spinal cord after infection [21]. In this study we build on these observations by identifying Sirt3 as a critical mediator of PHN pathogenesis and by evaluating metformin as a potential therapeutic modulator of this pathway.

Innate IFN-I responses constitute the first line of defense against HSV‑1, with cytosolic DNA sensing by cGAS activating the cGAS–STING–IRF3 axis to drive interferon production [51, 53–55]. Consistent with this, our qPCR data show robust induction of interferon expression following HSV‑1 infection, indicative of innate immune activation. HSV‑1 also perturbs mitochondrial homeostasis, causing mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) collapse, ROS accumulation, impaired mitophagy, and consequent microglial activation and pro‑inflammatory cytokine release [53, 56, 57]. In both in vivo and in vitro models, we observed HSV‑1‑induced mitophagy disruption, MMP loss, and increased ROS. Mechanistically, CREB signaling emerged as a regulator of mitochondrial gene expression: ERK‑CREB activation upregulated Sirt3 and promoted mitophagy, and transcription factor prediction together with ChIP assays implicated CREB as a likely transcriptional activator of Sirt3. Notably, HSV‑1 infection reduced CREB binding at the Sirt3 promoter, which may underlie decreased Sirt3 expression. Direct validation using CREB gain‑ and loss‑of‑function approaches is warranted to establish causality and clarify how CREB–Sirt3–AMPK signaling modulates mitophagy in virus‑associated neuropathic pain.

Sirt3, a mitochondrial deacetylase that modulates MAVS, preserves mitochondrial homeostasis and enhances innate antiviral responses [58, 59]. Sirt3 overexpression maintains mitochondrial function, reduces oxidative stress, stabilizes MMP, and prevents mitochondrial apoptosis [60]. Given the central role of mitochondria in neuropathic pain [61], we found that HSV-1 infection downregulated Sirt3 in the spinal dorsal horn, causing mitophagy impairment (p62 accumulation, decreased LC3-II/LC3-I), reduced IFN-α/β production, increased viral load, and exacerbated pain and neuroinflammation. Sirt3 knockout further aggravated MMP collapse, ROS accumulation, and axonal demyelination, supporting a protective role for Sirt3 in mitochondrial stability and antiviral immunity. Importantly, Sirt3 colocalized with the microglial marker IBA-1, and Sirt3 overexpression rescued microglial antiviral function (lower viral load, higher IFN-β), implicating microglia as a key cellular target through which Sirt3 mitigates PHN.

Previous studies show metformin activates AMPK, improves mitochondrial function, promotes autophagy/mitophagy, and lowers ROS and inflammation; it has demonstrated efficacy in several neuropathic and neurodegenerative contexts [43, 62, 63].

Metformin alleviates HSV‑1–induced neuropathic pain via a Sirt3‑dependent pathway: in wild‑type mice it suppressed viral replication, restored mitophagy and reduced pain, whereas these effects were abolished in Sirt3‑deficient mice. Metformin enhances CREB phosphorylation and promotes p‑CREB binding to the Sirt3 promoter, upregulating Sirt3 and concomitantly increasing AMPK phosphorylation in a Sirt3‑dependent manner, thereby synergistically promoting mitophagy and clearance of damaged mitochondria. By inhibiting mitochondrial complex I, metformin lowers the ATP/AMP ratio to activate AMPK and restore energy homeostasis, while suppressing mTOR to impede viral protein synthesis and reactivation [39, 64]. Although prior work has emphasized metformin’s activity against RNA viruses, this study provides, to our knowledge, the first evidence of immunoenhancement against the DNA virus HSV‑1 and establishes AMPK‑driven mitophagy as critical for antiviral immunity in this context [65]. Notably, the analgesic mechanism in HSV‑1–induced neuropathy—Sirt3‑dependent restoration of mitophagy—differs from metformin’s actions in conventional neuropathic pain models that rely mainly on AMPK activation and anti‑inflammation [43, 66]. A limitation of this study is the prophylactic dosing used in vivo and in vitro; future work should assess metformin’s therapeutic efficacy when administered after pain onset.

Limitations of this study include the exclusive use of male mice to avoid estrogen-mediated effects, so validation in female models will be required to rule out sex differences [67]. Concurrent administration of metformin with HSV-1 limits clinical extrapolation; the efficacy of delayed (post-onset) metformin treatment should be evaluated. Additionally, our Sirt3 data are derived from constitutive knockout mice, so contributions from cell types other than microglia cannot be excluded—future work using microglia-specific conditional Sirt3 knockout mice is needed to identify the causal cellular source(s).

Given metformin’s established safety and cost-effectiveness, these findings support its translational potential for prevention and treatment of HSV-1- induced neuropathic pain. We provide the first evidence that Sirt3 is central to HSV-1-induced neuropathic pain and show that metformin restores mitophagy and enhances antiviral immunity via the CREB–Sirt3–AMPK axis, revealing new therapeutic targets and candidate treatments for this condition.
