Clinical Evaluation of Three KRS Families and Cellular Analysis of Distinct ATP13A2 Mutations Reveal Different Levels of Iron Accumulation
Authors: Ezgi Erterek, Benan Temizci, Şeyma Tekgül, Bilal Çakır, A. Nazlı Başak, Murat Gültekin, Zuhal Yapıcı, Arzu Karabay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKufor‐Rakeb Syndrome (KRS) is a rare neurodegenerative disease caused by homozygous mutations in the ATP13A2 gene. The ATP13A2 protein, found in lysosomal and late‐endosomal membranes, performs cellular functions such as iron‐chelating agent transport and intracellular iron homeostasis. Mutations in ATP13A2 can lead to intracellular iron accumulation; however, whether KRS caused by an ATP13A2 mutation falls under Neurodegeneration with Brain Iron Accumulation disorders has long been debated. The most fundamental reason is that magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) cannot identify iron deposits in the basal ganglia in all KRS cases. We hypothesize that analyzing iron deposition at the cellular level could be more sensitive in detecting varying levels of iron accumulation associated with different ATP13A2 mutations, and it may be more useful when conventional MRI fails to detect iron, yields inconclusive results, or cannot be performed. We identified two new ATP13A2 mutations (p.Leu518_Thr519del, and p.Leu939Pro) in this study and comparatively investigated the impacts of three distinct ATP13A2 mutations (p.Pro474fs, p.Leu518_Thr519del, and p.Leu939Pro) using KRS patients' primary fibroblasts and MCF7 cells overexpressing these mutated ATP13A2 proteins to analyze if these different mutations of ATP13A2 can cause differing levels of iron accumulation. Following the detection of iron deposits via Prussian blue staining and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, the cell viability was assessed via MTT assay to ascertain the impact of iron accumulation. Each type of ATP13A2 mutation led to iron accumulation; however, frameshift and deletion mutations resulted in more iron accumulation than the missense mutation. In addition, the transient overexpression of the wild‐type ATP13A2 attenuated the cell death caused by iron accumulation. This study demonstrated that different types of ATP13A2 mutations are related to varying levels of iron accumulation and provided an explanation for the inconsistent perspectives on the association of KRS with iron accumulation.

Introduction

Kufor‐Rakeb Syndrome (KRS), an early‐onset atypical Parkinson's disease (PD), is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner (Schneider et al. 2010). Although KRS is characterized by progressive PD symptoms, it also shares clinical findings with hereditary spastic paraparesis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis in addition to PD (Al‐Din et al. 1994; Bras et al. 2012; Estrada‐Cuzcano et al. 2017; Kaul et al. 2016). As these diseases have similar symptoms, it could potentially be difficult to identify KRS patients based on phenotypic symptoms only. In this regard, molecular studies are critical for precisely diagnosing and differentiating these disorders. KRS is associated with mutations in the ATP13A2 gene, which encodes the ATP13A2 protein, a member of the P5‐type ATPase family, having 1180 amino acids (Spataro et al. 2019). ATP13A2 is a multi‐pass transmembrane protein with 10 transmembrane domains and three functional domains, including the actuator domain (A‐domain), ATPase and nucleotide‐binding domain (N‐domain), and catalytic phosphorylation domain (P‐domain) (da Fonseca and Outeiro 2014).

ATP13A2 protein is mainly localized to lysosomal and late‐endosomal membranes (Ramonet et al. 2012) and participates in the transport of cations such as iron, zinc, manganese, and cadmium (Tan et al. 2011) or cation‐chelating agents (Gaboriau et al. 2004; Veen et al. 2020) to maintain cellular cation homeostasis. Since ATP13A2 maintains intracellular iron homeostasis by transporting iron chelators (Rinaldi et al. 2015), mutations in ATP13A2 resulting in loss of ATP13A2 protein function may disrupt intracellular iron homeostasis and cause iron accumulation. In one study, a compound heterozygous mutation of ATP13A2 associated with KRS was found to cause iron accumulation in the patient's basal ganglia (Brüggemann et al. 2010). Furthermore, in other studies, frameshift mutations of the ATP13A2 gene, causing a truncated form of the protein, led to iron accumulation in the brains of patients (Kırımtay et al. 2021; Martino et al. 2015; Schneider et al. 2010). In addition, evidence of iron accumulation in the basal ganglia has been discovered in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of KRS patients, particularly those caused by frameshift mutations in the ATP13A2 gene (Eiberg et al. 2012; Kırımtay et al. 2021; Martino et al. 2015; Schneider et al. 2010). Kruer and colleagues detected iron accumulation in MRI scans of KRS patients with two different missense mutations (Kruer et al. 2012). Although these studies have proven that KRS, which is associated with loss of ATP13A2 function, can be classified in the Neurodegeneration with Brain Accumulation (NBIA) disease group, there is a dichotomy in the literature since iron accumulation has not always been reported in other KRS‐related clinical research (Table 1). Contrary to the above‐mentioned studies, there are also many cases without evidence of iron accumulation. For example, there are independent studies in which no iron accumulation was found in the brains of patients harboring frameshift mutations (Inzelberg et al. 2018; Park et al. 2011) and missense mutations (Erro et al. 2019; Park et al. 2011) based on MRI of KRS patients.

Iron deposition within cells must reach a particular threshold to recognize iron accumulation at the macro level using MRI (Lehéricy et al. 2020). Therefore, the reason for different outcomes obtained from the MRI performed in studies in the literature could be attributed to the MRI's limited sensitivity at low levels of iron accumulation. Even the age of patients at the time of receiving MRI is a concern, since iron accumulation may not be observed in MR images collected at early ages of patients (Martino et al. 2015), making it uncertain whether the relevant mutations cause iron accumulation. As a result, investigations at the cellular level to identify whether different types of ATP13A2 mutations lead to intracellular iron accumulation or differences in the quantity of intracellular iron accumulation caused by different ATP13A2 mutations may yield more conclusive results.

For these reasons, the objective of this study was to comparatively analyze the effects of different KRS mutations on iron accumulation at the cellular level, aiming not only to enable earlier detection but also to offer an alternative method for assessing iron accumulation to evaluate the progression of the disease when MRI analysis is not feasible and biochemical parameters do not correlate with brain iron accumulation.

In this study, we comparatively examined possible intracellular iron accumulation status within three KRS patients having distinct mutations in their ATP13A2 gene: a de novo deletion mutation (c.1552_1557delCTCACT:p.Leu518_Thr519del) identified in this study, a missense mutation (c.2816T>C:p.Leu939Pro) (previously linked to Parkinson's disease, Kablan et al. 2023) newly detected in a KRS patient in this study, as well as the frameshift mutation (c.1422_1423del:p.Pro474fs), which was previously reported by us and shown to cause iron accumulation by cellular analysis in our prior investigation (Kırımtay et al. 2021). We found that these three different types of mutations in the ATP13A2 gene caused iron accumulation in both patient fibroblasts and MCF7 cells overexpressing mutant ATP13A2 proteins. However, frameshift and deletion mutations had a more drastic effect on iron accumulation than the missense mutation. We also observed the cytotoxic effects of iron deposition in cells after iron treatment and found that cells expressing ATP13A2 with a missense mutation were less sensitive to iron treatment. Overall, these results suggest that different ATP13A2 mutations associated with KRS cause varying levels of iron accumulation, which can be more sensitively detected at the cellular level than by MRI.

Methods

Experimental Design and Pre‐Registration

The study was not pre‐registered and no publicly accessible protocol was deposited prior to conducting the experiments. The primary endpoint of the study is that the three different ATP13A2 mutations identified in different KRS patients have different effects on intracellular iron accumulation. Data points that failed predefined quality control criteria, including insufficient signal detection or technical assay failure, were excluded from the analysis when applicable. No blinding procedures were applied during experimental procedures or data analysis. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on previous studies using similar experimental approaches and common practice in cell‐based experimental studies (Kırımtay et al. 2021; Tiryakiler et al. 2025).

Consent and Approval

Research protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Istanbul Technical University, and the participants provided informed consent.

Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) and Variant Calling

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the MagNa Pure Compact System (Roche, Switzerland), followed by whole exome sequencing (WES) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Macrogen, Korea). The raw data was analyzed using the SEQ platform (Genomize, Turkey) for bioinformatics evaluation, including alignment to the reference genome (hg19/GRCh37), variant calling, and annotation. Noncoding and synonymous variants, excluding those in splice regions, were filtered out. All rare variants (with a MAF < 0.01) in disease‐associated genes listed in the OMIM database were evaluated.

Tissue and Cell Culture

Skin samples from a healthy individual and KRS patients were obtained by punch biopsy and immediately processed to obtain fibroblast cultures. Skin samples were dissociated with accutase, and dissociated fibroblasts were cultured in phenol red‐free Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Capricorn‐Scientific, Germany) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Capricorn‐Scientific, Germany) at 37°C. Fibroblast cells were used between passages 2 and 10.

MCF7 (RRID:CVCL_0031) and HEK293T cell lines (RRID:CVCL_0063) were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2 with DMEM (Gibco, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplied with 10% FBS (Gibco, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cells were passaged by trypsinization (Gibco, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) when they reached approximately 70% confluency, and they were used between passages 12 and 22. The cell lines used in this study are not listed as commonly misidentified cell lines by the International Cell Line Authentication Committee (ICLAC). Cell line authentication was not performed in this study. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Cloning of Wild‐Type and Mutant cDNAs of ATP13A2

Wild‐type (wt) and mutant ATP13A2 cDNAs were cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+)/myc‐His A expression vector (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA). wt‐ATP13A2 cDNA (Transcript variant 1) was obtained from HEK293T cells, and the cDNA sequence was amplified with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using Q5 High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Then, the wt‐ATP13A2 cDNA was inserted into the pcDNA3.1(+)/myc‐His A vector via EcoRI/XbaI restriction sites. Missense (MS) (c.2816T>C) and deletion (del) (c.1552_1557delCTCACT) mutations were introduced into wt‐ATP13A2 cDNA in the expression vector with the help of the Q5 Site‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Additionally, cDNA of ATP13A2 with the frameshift (FS) mutation (c.1422_1423delTG) was obtained from the patient fibroblasts total RNA using Oligo d(T)23 VN primer and ProtoScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Q5 High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase was used to amplify the FS‐ATP13A2 cDNA sequence obtained from the patient fibroblasts. This cDNA was cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+)/myc‐His A vector via EcoRI/XbaI restriction sites. The primers used in PCR and site‐directed mutagenesis are listed in Table S1.

Transfection

For overexpression control, MCF7 cells were seeded into 12‐well plates at a density of 0.3 × 106 cells/well. The next day, cells were transfected with 1.5 μg mock (pcDNA3.1 myc/his A), wt‐ATP13A2, FS‐ATP13A2, del‐ATP13A2, or MS‐ATP13A2 plasmids using PEI (polyethylenimine) at the 1:3 (w/w) (DNA:PEI) ratio.

For Prussian blue staining, MCF7 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well in 6‐well plates. After 24 h, the cells were transfected with 3 μg wt‐ATP13A2, FS‐ATP13A2, del‐ATP13A2, or MS‐ATP13A2 plasmids using PEI at a 1:3 (w/w) (DNA:PEI) ratio.

For the MTT assay, 3 × 105 MCF7 cells were seeded on 6‐well plates. The next day, the cells were transfected with 3 μg wt‐ATP13A2, FS‐ATP13A2, del‐ATP13A2, or MS‐ATP13A2 plasmids with PEI.

For transfection of patient fibroblasts, the Amaxa Nucleofector II was used with the O‐17 program. 1 × 106 patient fibroblast cells were precipitated at 250 × g for 7 min for each experimental and control group. After that, 10 μg wt‐ATP13A2 plasmid or mock plasmid (pcDNA 3.1 myc/hisA) and 100 μL Buffer 2 (5 mM KCl, 15 mM MgCl2, 140 mM Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, and 15 mM HEPES; pH: 7.2) were added to the cells. The mixture was transferred to the nucleofection cuvette, and the O‐17 program was run. After nucleofection, 900 μL DMEM was added. The cells were seeded in 100 mm cell culture dishes within 5 mL DMEM.

Protein Isolation and Western Blot Analysis

To analyze stress signaling proteins expression levels and detect overexpressed wt‐ATP13A2 protein in fibroblasts, RIPA buffer (0.05 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 0.15 M NaCl, 1% Triton X‐100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 20 mM EDTA, 1% trasylol) (Santa Cruz), protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) were used to lyse the cells. After centrifugation at 12000 × g for 25 min at 4°C, supernatants were collected for total protein concentration measurement using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Science, IL, USA). Then, 50 μg of total proteins were run on 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% milk powder in TBS‐T. Then, the membranes were incubated with Myc‐Tag (9B11) mouse mAb (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2276, RRID:AB_331783), NRF2 rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 12721, RRID:AB_2715528), Phospho‐eIF2 alpha (Ser51) rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 3398, RRID:AB_2096481), β‐Actin (8H10D10) mouse mAb (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 3700, RRID:AB_2242334), and β‐Actin (13E5) rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 4970, RRID:AB_2223172) overnight at 4°C. After washing steps with TBS‐T, membranes were incubated with Anti‐mouse IgG (H + L) (DyLight 800 4X PEG Conjugate) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 5257, RRID:AB_10693543), Anti‐mouse IgG (H+L) (DyLight 680 Conjugate) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 5470, RRID:AB_10696895), and Anti‐rabbit IgG (H+L) (DyLight 800 Conjugate) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 5151, RRID:AB_10697505) at room temperature for 1 h and were washed with TBS‐T. Visualization was performed using the Licor Odyssey CLx Near‐Infrared Fluorescence Imaging System. Densitometric analyses were performed using Image Studio Lite (RRID:SCR_013715) (Version 5.2) and Adobe Photoshop CS6 (RRID:SCR_014199) software.

RNA Isolation and Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Fibroblast cells were seeded at 0.5 × 106 in 100 mm cell culture dishes. When their density reached the optimum confluency, the cells were harvested and total RNA was isolated using the NucleoSpin RNA kit (Macherey‐Nagel, Germany Cat# 740955). After that, 500 ng total RNA was reverse transcribed using a random primer mix and ProtoScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA Cat# E6560). Obtained cDNAs were used for real‐time PCR. Human ATP13A2 primers (Forward: 5′‐CATACCGGTCAAGTCCTACCC‐3′ and Reverse: 5′‐GCACAGGGCGTACCAGTAGT‐3′) and probe (UPL#5; Cat# 04685004001), which were purchased from Macrogen and Universal Probe Library (Roche, RRID:SCR_010279), were used in real‐time PCR. Human HMOX1 primers (Forward: 5′‐TGAACTCCCTGGAGATGACTC‐3′ and Reverse: 5′‐AGCTCCTGCAACTCCTCAAA‐3′), human FPN primers (Forward: 5′‐CCCCAGCTCTAGCTGTGAAA‐3′ and Reverse: 5′‐CAGGGGTTTTGGCTCAGTAT‐3′), and probe (UPL#13; Cat# 04685121001) were used to detect mRNA expression levels of HMOX1 and FPN. Also, the human beta‐actin probe (Roche Cat# 05046165001) was used as housekeeping control to analyze the relative expression of ATP13A2 mRNA levels. LightCycler 480 Probe Master (Roche Cat# 04707494001) qRT‐PCR kit and Roche LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche, RRID:SCR_018495) were used to perform the reactions. Relative expressions were analyzed using the ΔΔCT method (Schmittgen and Livak 2008).

Immunocytochemistry

Immunocytochemistry was performed to detect overexpression of ATP13A2 proteins with fluorescently labeled antibodies via confocal microscopy. Coverslips were covered with 1 mL of poly‐L‐lysine for 1 h at 37°C, and they were washed with ethanol and dried under UV light. Subsequently, MCF7 cells were seeded on coverslips in 12‐well plates at a density of 0.3 × 106 cells/well. After 24 h, MCF7 cells were transfected with wt or mutant ATP13A2 constructs. Then, 48 h after transfection, cells were fixed for 15 min using 4% paraformaldehyde. Then, coverslips were washed with 1X PBS 3 times. Cells were blocked with a blocking buffer (3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin and 0.1% (v/v) Triton X‐100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, MCF7 cells were treated with Myc‐Tag (9B11) Mouse mAb (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2276, RRID:AB_331783) at a dilution of 1:200 at 4°C overnight. After incubation, coverslips were washed with 1X PBS 3 times. Then, MCF7 cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594 Conjugate Anti‐mouse IgG (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 8890, RRID:AB_2714182) and BODIPY FL phallacidin (#B607, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at dilutions of 1:250 and 1:70, respectively. This incubation was performed at room temperature for 1 h. During washing after incubation, DAPI (at a dilution of 1:1000) was applied to the cells for 10 min at room temperature. After washing with 1X PBS, ProLong Diamond Antifade Mounting Medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was added onto slides, and coverslips were placed onto the mounting medium. Prepared coverslips were visualized by the TCS SP2 SE Confocal Microscope (Leica, Germany) using a 63X oil immersion objective. All images were captured in a single focal plane using Leica Application Suite X (LASX) (RRID:SCR_013673) (Version 3.5.1) software.

Prussian Blue Staining

Prussian blue staining has been used in many studies to investigate intracellular iron accumulation (Janic et al. 2008; Kırımtay et al. 2021; Leekha et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2016; Neubert et al. 2015; Wan et al. 2019). In order to comparatively analyze the iron accumulation based on the mutations by Prussian blue staining, healthy and patients' fibroblasts were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in 6‐well plates. After 48 h, fibroblasts were fixed with 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde. After three washing steps with 1X PBS, 10% potassium ferrocyanide (K4Fe(CN)6.3H2O) and 20% hydrogen chloride (HCl) were mixed in equal volumes, and this mixture was applied to fixed fibroblasts for 30 min. The cells were washed three times with dH2O, and nuclear fast red (Kernechtrot) solution was applied as a counterstain for 10 min. Then, the cells were washed again with dH2O three times. Subsequently, images of fibroblasts were obtained using the ZEISS Axiocam 305 color Camera on the ZEISS Axiovert 5 Biological Phase Fluorescence Inverted Microscope with 63× magnification.

For staining of MCF7 cells, they were first transfected with wt‐ATP13A2, FS‐ATP13A2, del‐ATP13A2, or MS‐ATP13A2 constructs as explained in the transfection section. On the next day of transfection, MCF7 cells were treated with 250 μM FeCl3‐6H2O for 24 h. After fixation, the same protocol was used to observe iron accumulation.

Analysis of Prussian blue staining was performed using similar methods outlined by López‐Aguirre et al. (2025). For the analysis of iron accumulation, the Fiji (RRID:SCR_002285) software was utilized to count the puncta (deposited iron). Firstly, the images were converted into binary format with a threshold of 1, following conversion to grayscale (8‐bit). Then, the puncta were detected and quantified using the Analyze Particle plugin of Fiji software using size interval of 30–150 (pixel2) and circularity of 0.00–1.00. The total number of puncta was divided by the number of cells in each image to quantify the mean number of puncta detected per cell.

MTT Assay

To analyze fibroblasts' viability, the healthy control and patients' fibroblasts were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 96‐well plates. The next day, the cells were treated with different concentrations of FeCl3‐6H2O (0–1000 μmol) for 24 h. Then, 10 μL of MTT reagent (Millipore, USA) (5 mg/mL prepared with 1X PBS) was added to each well. After the plate was incubated at 37°C for 4 h, the media was taken out, and the formazan salts were dissolved in 200 μL DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). Absorbance measurements were obtained at 570 nm and 655 nm (reference filter) using a BIO‐RAD Benchmark Plus microplate reader (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, USA).

To analyze the direct effects of ATP13A2 mutations on cell viability, MCF7 cells were transfected with FS‐ATP13A2, del‐ATP13A2, or MS‐ATP13A2 mutant constructs, and wt‐ATP13A2 for control. After 24 h of transfection, each group of cells was replated, and 1 × 104 transfected MCF7 cells were seeded on 96‐well plates with 500 μmol FeCl3‐6H2O. After 24 h of treatment, 10 μL MTT reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 4 h. DMEM was removed from each well, and formazan salts were dissolved in 200 μL of DMSO. The absorbance measurements were performed with the same wavelengths as in fibroblasts.

To understand the rescue effect of wt‐ATP13A2 in patient fibroblasts, wt‐ATP13A2 protein was overexpressed in patient fibroblasts. After 24 h of transfection, the cells were trypsinized and counted to perform an MTT assay. The cells were replated at a density of 1 × 104 per well in 96‐well plates with increasing concentrations of FeCl3‐6H2O (0–1000 μmol). After 24 h, an MTT assay was performed.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP‐MS)

Control and patient‐derived fibroblast cells were detached by trypsinization and washed three times with 1× PBS. Cell pellets were collected to obtain a final mass of approximately 100 mg for each sample, and residual liquid was allowed to evaporate under a laminar flow hood. Then, pellets were digested in 10 mL of 5.00% (v/v) nitric acid (HNO3) solution. Samples were incubated in an ultrasonic water bath (frequency: 40 Hz) at 80°C for half an hour. The resulting solutions were then filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate (CA filter) syringe filter and fed into the ICP‐MS system, as previously described (Badran et al. 2018).

Thermo Scientific iCAP RQplus ICP‐MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used for all measurements. The sample intake system consisted of a Peltier‐cooled (3°C), compartmentalized cyclonic spray chamber, a micro mist nozzle, and a quartz torch with a removable quartz injector with a 2.5 mm inner diameter. The instrument was operated in kinetic energy separation (KED) mode using pure helium (He) collision gas in a collision/reaction chamber (CRC). A summary of all instrument parameters is given in Table 2. The Thermo Scientific iSC‐65 Automated Sampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used for sample collection. The data acquisition and processing were controlled using the Thermo Scientific Qtegra Intelligent Scientific Data Solution (ISDS) Software version 2.18.5492.239 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; RRID:SCR_002798). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe test prior to parametric analyses. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test. Comparisons among multiple independent groups were conducted using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test where appropriate. All statistical tests were two‐tailed. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For datasets normalized to control values, statistical analyses were performed on normalized data. No statistical tests for outliers were performed. Data points were excluded only if they failed predefined technical quality control criteria. Where indicated, data were normalized to the corresponding control group to allow comparison between independent experiments and to reduce inter‐experimental variability. Normalization procedures were consistent with standard practice for relative expression and viability assays and did not preclude the use of parametric statistical analyses. Full reports of all statistical analyses are presented in Table S4.

Results

Clinical Findings

Case 1 (shown with an arrow in Figure 1A) is a 43‐year‐old male patient who was born of a first‐degree cousin marriage, and is the second of 3 children in the family. An older sister is healthy, and a younger sister has similar neurological findings.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans and pedigrees of the KRS patients. The pedigrees (A) and axial (B‐left) and sagittal (B‐right) T2‐weighted MR images of the families of Case 1 (II‐1) with frameshift (FS) mutation, Case 2 (II‐2) with deletion (del) mutation, and Case 3 (II‐2) with missense (MS) mutation are depicted.

The patient's physical and mental development was normal until the age of 10 years (Table 3). After this age, a speech disorder with involuntary movements of his tongue was observed. After 12 years of age, he had difficulty walking because of involuntary twisting movements of the foot, and he underwent different treatments (including bromocriptine 10 mg/day and biperiden 2 mg/day) with a pre‐diagnosis of juvenile parkinsonism between the ages of 14 and 25. However, he did not benefit, and progression continued. After the age of 28, he could no longer stand and walk and became wheelchair dependent after the age of 30. His mental activity gradually slowed down over time, and additionally, anger attacks started to be seen. In the neurological examination, it was possible to establish meaningful verbal communication despite the severe dysarthria. In the cranial nerve examination, the fundus was normal, upward gaze was limited in eye movements, and there was no nystagmus. He had severe dysarthria (nasal speech), dysphagia, and sialorrhea. Motor examination revealed paraparesis of the lower extremities together with slight spasticity. There was a tendency toward a pes equinovarus posture in his foot. In addition, the muscle volume had slightly decreased in the distal parts of the lower extremities. Extrapyramidal system examination revealed hypomimia, bradykinesia, risus sardonicus, and cogwheel rigidity in the wrist and elbows. Semi‐flexion posture was predominant in the upper extremities. Deep tendon reflexes were generally brisk, and bilaterally Babinski's sign was positive. He could not stand alone and walk, but could crawl. In the laboratory investigations, serum and urine copper, ceruloplasmin, peripheral smear, and congenital metabolic screening tests were within normal limits. Electroneuromyography (EMG) showed mixed‐type polyneuropathy in which motor and sensory fibers were affected. Cranial MRI performed at the age of 36 showed extensive, severe cerebral and cerebellar atrophy. Although the basal ganglia appeared to be hypointense on T2‐weighted MR images, it was considered necessary to perform an additional quantitative analysis (QSM) since there was not enough hypointense dark color as seen in diseases with neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA). The 31‐year‐old sister's symptoms began after the age of 20 with gait instability. Over time, she developed slowness of movement and speech impairment. Neurological examination revealed hypomimia, bradykinesia, and intermittent oromandibular dystonia, identified as risus sardonicus. Additionally, cogwheel rigidity was observed in both upper extremities, and postural instability was noted. Clinically, she was diagnosed with early‐onset parkinsonism and mild dystonia. The ATP13A2 mutation identified in her brother was also detected in her.

We investigated if the other mutations identified in this study caused a decrease in ATP13A2 mRNA expression as well as the FS mutation. We performed qPCR using mRNA pools from healthy control and patient fibroblasts to evaluate the changes in the mRNA expression levels of ATP13A2 having three distinct mutations (Figure 3).

Intracellular Iron Accumulation Varies Depending on the ATP13A2 Mutation Types

To compare the effects of different mutation types of ATP13A2 on iron accumulation, Prussian blue staining was used to identify intracellular iron levels in FS‐fibroblasts, del‐fibroblasts, and MS‐fibroblasts obtained from patients; Case 1, Case 2, and Case 3, respectively (Figure 4A). In the FS‐fibroblasts and del‐fibroblasts, iron deposition was observed intensely, whereas it was less in the MS‐fibroblasts. Additionally, almost no iron accumulation was detected in the control fibroblasts. A quantitative analysis of iron deposition in fibroblasts with different types of mutations revealed a significant difference in deposited iron levels in FS‐fibroblasts, del‐fibroblasts, and MS‐fibroblasts compared to control fibroblasts (Figure 4B). The amount of iron deposits in MS‐fibroblasts was significantly lower than in FS‐fibroblasts and del‐fibroblasts. The results showed that while all three types of mutations led to iron accumulation, the missense mutation led to less iron accumulation than the other types of mutations.

Mutations in ATP13A2 Lead to a Decrease in Cell Viability Under Iron Treatment Conditions

After demonstrating different levels of iron accumulation in patient fibroblasts having different ATP13A2 mutations, we performed an MTT assay to determine if having different kinds of ATP13A2 mutations influences cell viability differently based on varying levels of iron accumulation. For this aim, control and patients' fibroblasts were treated with increasing concentrations of FeCl3‐6H2O (0–1000 μM) to observe the effect of iron accumulation on cytotoxicity. Since we observed the most prominent difference at 1000 μM FeCl3‐6H2O (Figure 7A), we compared the cell viability of fibroblasts at this concentration (Figure 7B). We analyzed these fibroblasts' cell viability results by normalizing them to untreated conditions because each fibroblast had different physiological characteristics due to the presence of different mutations. The MTT results indicated that the viability of fibroblast cells harboring each of the three kinds of ATP13A2 mutations was significantly reduced compared to untreated control fibroblasts, while no significant change was observed in the viability of control fibroblasts upon iron overload. Moreover, the reduction in cell viability following iron treatment was greater in FS‐ and del‐fibroblasts than in MS‐fibroblasts (Figure 7B), which is consistent with the accumulated iron levels observed in patient‐derived fibroblasts (Figure 4B).

To show that the decrease in cell viability of patient fibroblasts was directly caused by the mutations in ATP13A2, we performed an MTT assay with MCF7 cells overexpressing wt or these three different types of mutant ATP13A2 proteins. The cell viability of MCF7 cells overexpressing wt or mutant ATP13A2 proteins was analyzed in the presence of FeCl3‐6H2O. Since MCF7 cells had different properties than fibroblasts, we first determined the appropriate iron dosage that produced the maximum cytotoxic difference (Figure 7C). Since the viability‐reducing effect of FS mutation in the patient fibroblasts was known to be drastic (Figure 7A), we used FS‐ATP13A2‐overexpressing MCF7 cells to compare with wt‐ATP13A2‐overexpressing cells. The difference in cytotoxic effects between wt‐ATP13A2 and FS‐ATP13A2‐expressing MCF7 cells was greater at 500 μM than at 1000 μM (Figure 7C). Hence, we decided to compare the cell viability of MCF7 cells overexpressing wt or mutant ATP13A2 proteins upon treatment with 500 μM FeCl3‐6H2O. We found that FS‐ATP13A2 and del‐ATP13A2 proteins decreased the cell viability of MCF7 cells when subjected to FeCl3‐6H2O treatment (Figure 7D). We observed the most dramatic decrease in cell viability in FS‐ATP13A2 overexpressing MCF7 cells, whereas MS‐ATP13A2 overexpression resulted in the least cytotoxic effect. Consistent with patient fibroblasts, we showed that the presence of each of the three different ATP13A2 mutations increased the sensitivity of the cells to iron treatment. These results indicated that FS‐ATP13A2 and del‐ATP13A2 reduced cell viability more dramatically than MS‐ATP13A2.

Fibroblasts Harboring Different Types of ATP13A2 Mutations Exhibit Variable Iron‐Mediated Stress Responses

After identifying that different types of ATP13A2 mutations resulted in variable degrees of intracellular iron accumulation and differentially affected the cell viability depending on the cells' ability to tolerate iron, we next investigated key components of cellular iron homeostasis and stress response pathways in control and patient‐derived fibroblasts (Figure 8). First, we investigated the expression level of p‐eIF2α, as it is a marker for PERK (protein kinase RNA‐like endoplasmic reticulum kinase) activation. We found that p‐eIF2α was significantly increased only in FS‐fibroblasts compared to control fibroblasts (Figure 8A). We then evaluated total NRF2 expression levels in fibroblasts and observed a substantial increase only in FS‐fibroblasts, with no significant alterations identified in del‐ or MS‐fibroblasts compared to controls (Figure 8B). These results indicated that the FS‐fibroblasts have a more pronounced stress response, characterized by activation of the PERK–eIF2α–NRF2 signaling axis, whereas fibroblasts harboring either deletion or missense mutations do not induce a comparable level of stress pathway activation.

Wt‐ATP13A2 Increased the Cell Viability of Patient Fibroblast

Thus far, we have shown how mutant ATP13A2 proteins caused the iron deposition and decreased cell viability in a toxicity‐related manner. After that, we designed another experiment to determine whether wt‐ATP13A2 could increase the cell viability of patient fibroblasts. To demonstrate the rescue effect of wt‐ATP13A2 in patient fibroblasts, we overexpressed the wt‐ATP13A2 protein in FS‐fibroblasts. FS‐fibroblasts were chosen to investigate this effect due to their significant decrease in cell viability, as shown in Figure 7B. Additionally, the FS‐ATP13A2 protein was not expressed in FS‐fibroblasts since its mRNA was degraded (Figure 3A). Therefore, only the wt‐ATP13A2 protein would be expressed in patient FS‐fibroblasts upon forced expression of the wt‐ATP13A2. After confirming overexpression of the wt‐ATP13A2 protein through western blot analysis (Figure 9A), patient fibroblasts expressing mock or wt‐ATP13A2 proteins were treated with varying concentrations of FeCl3‐6H2O (0 μM, 250 μM, 500 μM, and 1000 μM) to assess their viability using MTT assay. The viability of fibroblasts decreased proportionally with increasing concentrations of FeCl3‐6H2O, as shown in Figure 9B. Despite this, patient fibroblasts overexpressing wt‐ATP13A2 protein exhibited a smaller reduction in cell viability. This comparison showed that wt‐ATP13A2 protein overexpression partially alleviated patient fibroblast viability by as much as 10%. The results indicated that overexpression of wt‐ATP13A2 in FS‐fibroblasts reduced the decrease in cell viability when exposed to iron, most likely due to a reduction in iron accumulation within the cells.

Discussion

Since the ATP13A2 protein plays a role in regulating cation transportation, it aids in the maintenance of iron homeostasis in cells (Rinaldi et al. 2015). Hence, loss of function mutations in ATP13A2 disrupt iron balance. Although iron is necessary as a cofactor for many biochemical processes, it can also be harmful to cells in high concentrations, acting as a catalyst for free radical reactions. Therefore, cellular iron homeostasis must be strictly regulated (Cairo et al. 2006). Iron accumulation can often be observed in various neurodegenerative disorders (Ndayisaba et al. 2019), particularly those classified as neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA), where early abnormal iron deposition occurs in the basal ganglia (Hogarth 2015). The classification of Kufor‐Rakeb syndrome (KRS), which is caused by mutations in ATP13A2, as a form of NBIA remains a subject of debate. In the literature, iron accumulation in the basal ganglia on MRI has been documented in some KRS patients (Brüggemann et al. 2010; Kırımtay et al. 2021; Martino et al. 2015; Schneider et al. 2010), but not in others (Erro et al. 2019; Fong et al. 2011; Santoro et al. 2011), probably due to MRI's inadequate sensitivity. Therefore, cellular studies may provide a more reliable approach to recognize iron accumulation in KRS.

In this study, we examined iron accumulation at macroanatomical and cellular levels in three KRS patients carrying distinct ATP13A2 mutations: a previously reported frameshift (p.Ala475Phefs520X, Case 1), a novel de novo deletion (p.Leu518_Thr519del, Case 2), and a missense mutation (p.Leu939Pro, Case 3). While the missense variant has been previously linked to Parkinson's disease (Kablan et al. 2023), this is the first study to associate it with KRS.

When the clinical symptoms of the patients were examined, the most important difference was observed in the age of onset of the disease. While juvenile onset of the disease was observed in Case 1 and Case 2, which harbored the frameshift or deletion mutations, respectively, late onset of the disease was observed in Case 3, who harbored the missense mutation (Table 3). When we examined the iron accumulation levels, we found that frameshift and deletion mutations in ATP13A2 caused more iron accumulation than the missense mutation (Figures 4 and 5C,D). It is unclear whether the iron accumulation seen in neurodegenerative diseases is a primary event or a secondary effect. However, iron accumulation is detected only in certain parts of the brain, independent of aging, in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's disease or Alzheimer's disease. This suggests that iron release plays an important role in the pathogenesis of common neurodegenerative diseases (Levi et al. 2024). In this regard, our findings have shown that different levels of iron accumulation caused by various types of ATP13A2 mutations are correlated with the age of onset of the disease. Thus, it has become readily apparent why Case 3, harboring the missense mutation, shows disease symptoms at a later age than Case 1 and Case 2.

Despite its late onset, the disease progressed rapidly in Case 3, harboring the missense mutation, and the patient became wheelchair‐bound within 2 years. In contrast, Cases 1 and 2 were wheelchair‐bound after a lengthy period, namely 20 years for Case 1 and 14 years for Case 2 (Table 3). Although the disease appears to progress faster in Case 3 than in the other cases, it should not be overlooked that aging plays a substantial role in the progression and escalation of neurodegenerative disorders (Mayne et al. 2020). Therefore, when comparing the effects of different types of ATP13A2 mutations in disease, it is more accurate to compare the time it takes for symptoms to appear rather than the severity observed after the disease has begun. Our clinical observations regarding the ages at which Cases 1, 2, and 3 became wheelchair‐bound (30, 25, and 45, respectively) seem to be consistent with the mutation type‐dependent effects and, hence, the duration required for accumulation of iron. The findings suggest that the missense mutation in Case 3 initiates clinical symptoms later than the frameshift and deletion mutations of Cases 1 and 2, respectively, but progresses rapidly.

Furthermore, when the MRI results of the cases were examined to compare the levels of atrophy caused by different types of mutations, severe cerebral and cerebellar atrophy were detected in Case 1 harboring the frameshift mutation, whereas moderate cerebral and cerebellar atrophy were detected in Case 3 harboring the missense mutation (Figure 1B). These observations also support the findings that the missense mutation in the ATP13A2 gene has relatively less severe effects than the frameshift mutation. On the other hand, normal findings were observed in the MRI performed when Case 2 was 16 years old (Figure 1B). However, a follow‐up MRI could not be performed to enable quantitative analyses, such as volumetric assessment and evaluation of iron accumulation due to the DBS procedure performed on the patient. We also analyzed several biochemical parameters related to iron metabolism in these cases and their affected sisters (Table S3). However, in NBIA disorders, peripheral blood iron levels, total iron‐binding capacity, and ferritin concentrations do not correlate with brain iron accumulation. Only neuroferritinopathy and aceruloplasminemia are directly associated with systemic iron metabolism, leading to alterations in systemic iron parameters and making these measurements diagnostically relevant. In contrast, for the remaining NBIA disorders, including KRS, these peripheral iron assessments have no practical diagnostic value (Dusek et al. 2012), and conventional MRI often fails to detect iron accumulation in the early stages of the disease. Therefore, under such circumstances, in vitro cellular analyses of iron accumulation may provide an effective approach to better understand the effects and progression of the disease.

Our earlier study indicated that iron accumulation caused by ATP13A2 deficiency can lead to cell death (Kırımtay et al. 2021). Although the ATP13A2 deletion and missense mutations analyzed in this study did not affect the expression level of ATP13A2 mRNA (Figure 3), Prussian blue staining and ICP‐MS analysis demonstrated that these mutant proteins were unable to perform their function, as the presence of these mutations caused iron accumulation (Figures 4, 5, and 6B). In this regard, we performed MTT assays to compare the effects of different levels of iron accumulation caused by three different types of ATP13A2 mutations on cell viability. We found that the presence of all three types of ATP13A2 mutations rendered cells more sensitive to iron treatment; however, cells with the missense mutation were more resistant to iron overload than those with frameshift and deletion mutations (Figure 7). This cellular finding was consistent with the MRI findings in Cases 1 (Figure 1B and Uygun et al. 2025) and 3, which may explain the more severe brain atrophy observed in Case 1. The frameshift mutation in Case 1 causes intracellular iron accumulation and subsequent cell death at a faster pace than the missense mutation in Case 3, and therefore more severe brain atrophy may have been observed in Case 1. In addition, the deletion mutation was as sensitive to iron as cells with the frameshift mutation, and the reason for the severe symptoms observed in Case 2, whose MRI could not be repeated, was identified.

The ATP13A2 protein, a P5‐type ATPase composed of 1180 amino acids, contains 10 transmembrane (TM) helices spanning around residues 250 to 1180. The regions encompassing TM4‐TM6 and TM8‐TM10 are critical for metal coordination. ATP13A2 requires three functional domains for its ATP‐dependent ion transport activity: the actuator domain (around residues 350‐450), the phosphorylation domain (around residues 550‐650), and the nucleotide‐binding domain (around residues 700‐850) (van Veen et al. 2014). Only the first four TM helices (TM1–TM4) are present in the plasmid‐based expression construct of FS‐ATP13A2, which is not subjected to nonsense‐mediated decay and produces the truncated protein (Figure 5B). However, this truncated version lacks the crucial phosphorylation and nucleotide‐binding domains required for ATPase activity and metal transport. Since a frameshift mutation in the ATP13A2 gene leads to ATP13A2 deficiency in fibroblast cells (Kırımtay et al. 2021), mutation effect assessments based on the overexpression of FS‐ATP13A2 constructs in MCF‐7 cells did not replicate the cellular phenotype observed in fibroblasts. Nevertheless, FS‐ATP13A2 overexpression served as a negative control in this study, as the recombinant FS‐ATP13A2 protein is non‐functional. This experimental design enabled us to directly assess whether the truncated, non‐functional ATP13A2 protein has any residual or dominant‐negative effect on iron metabolism, particularly under iron overload conditions. The results obtained from MCF‐7 cells (Figures 5C,D and 7D) were similar to those from fibroblast cells (Figures 4 and 7B), indicating that expression of the truncated FS‐ATP13A2 protein has no dominant‐negative effect on iron metabolism. Importantly, the most critical aspect of our study was the work conducted using patient‐derived fibroblast cells. The findings from overexpressing mutant proteins in MCF‐7 cells mirrored those from the fibroblast experiments, further validating our conclusions.

Growing evidence indicates that ferroptosis and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress are interrelated processes involved in the development of various neurodegenerative disorders. An imbalance in intracellular iron homeostasis can lead to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn activate multiple stress‐related signaling pathways, including ER stress. If these stress responses fail to elicit an adequate adaptive program, cells may undergo ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death triggered by an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants, ultimately resulting in excessive ROS production (Guo et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2024, 2025). One of the key proteins involved in the crosstalk between ferroptosis and ER stress is PERK, which phosphorylates both eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) and nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (NRF2) (Mazzolini and Touriol 2025; Tao et al. 2025). Upon iron accumulation–mediated PERK activation, phosphorylation of eIF2α suppresses global protein synthesis and may contribute to cell death by inducing stress‐responsive genes (Mazzolini and Touriol 2025). In parallel, phosphorylation of NRF2 enhances its stability and promotes its nuclear translocation, where it induces the transcription of antioxidant genes, including heme oxygenase‐1 (HMOX1) and ferroportin (FPN) (Yan et al. 2023). In addition to iron accumulation investigations, we investigated important stress‐related proteins in patient‐derived fibroblasts. We found that although FS‐fibroblasts exhibited considerably higher expression levels of NRF2 and p‐eIF2α compared with del‐ or MS‐fibroblasts, the mRNA levels of HMOX1 and FPN were significantly greater in del‐ and MS‐fibroblasts than in FS‐fibroblasts (Figure 8). Although this finding appears counterintuitive at first, it is consistent with our findings related to iron accumulation in patient‐derived fibroblasts (Figures 4, 6, and 7B). Considering that HMOX‐1 and ferroportin are adaptive iron‐homeostatic responses that limit intracellular labile iron and protect against oxidative stress and ferroptosis (Harada et al. 2011), FS‐fibroblasts may experience a prolonged iron‐induced stress resulting in ferroptosis through inhibiting transcription of antioxidant genes (Reichard et al. 2007; Xie et al. 2023). Although the PERK‐eIF2α‐NRF2 axis is activated, the considerably lower expression of ferroptosis‐suppressive genes suggests that adaptive responses are insufficient, making these cells more vulnerable to ferroptotic cell death. In contrast, del‐ and MS‐fibroblasts exhibited greater activation of these antioxidant and iron‐handling genes, suggesting that compensatory defensive mechanisms may remain active. This increased antioxidant response is consistent with lower intracellular iron accumulation in del‐ and MS‐fibroblasts compared to FS‐fibroblasts, indicating that activation of these protective pathways may help minimize iron overload. HMOX1 and FPN can be activated by stress‐responsive mechanisms other than PERK‐eIF2α‐NRF2 signaling (Medina et al. 2020; Alam et al. 2004; Marques et al. 2025), which might explain their higher expression in del‐ and MS‐fibroblasts without overexpression in NRF2. However, further research is needed to understand the precise molecular pathways driving these mutation‐specific changes in iron‐induced stress responses.

Given that Case 1's frameshift mutation (c.G1426T, c.1429_1430insAAA, and c.1422_1423delTG) in the ATP13A2 gene causes protein deficiency (Kırımtay et al. 2021), it is not surprising that the cells harboring this mutation accumulate excessive amount of iron (Figures 4 and 5) and consequently are very sensitive to iron overload (Figure 7). In contrast, both Case 2's deletion (c.1552_1557delCTCACT; p.Leu518_Thr519del) and Case 3's missense (c.2816T>C; p.Leu939Pro) mutations are located within the conserved region of the ATP13A2 protein (Figure 2A, Case 2–3, right panels). However, both types of ATP13A2 mutations exhibited different impacts on iron accumulation, which might be explained by the position of the mutation relative to the protein's functional domains. The conserved region affected by the deletion mutation of Case 2 is located in the phosphorylation domain required for the activity of P‐type ATPases (Figure 2B), resulting in the deletion of amino acids immediately adjacent to the autophosphorylation motif (DKTGT) (Figure 2A, Case 2, right panel). Previous studies, it was shown that the ATP13A2 protein was auto‐phosphorylated via the aspartate (Asp; D) amino acid in the conserved autophosphorylation motif, and this phosphorylation was determined to be critical for the protein's activity (Holemans et al. 2015; Martin et al. 2015). Furthermore, it was also discovered that the p.Thr517IIe mutation found in this motif inhibits the autophosphorylation of the protein (as shown in Figure 2A, Case 2, right panel), causing the protein to lose its function (Estrada‐Cuzcano et al. 2017). Given that the mutation of Case 2 could alter the three‐dimensional structure of the autophosphorylation motif by causing the loss of amino acids adjacent to the relevant region, it is possible to think that autophosphorylation of ATP13A2 could be impaired. The ability of ATP13A2 to carry out all of its functions, including iron transport, depends on its ability to undergo autophosphorylation (Covy et al. 2012; Gitler et al. 2009; Park et al. 2014; Rinaldi et al. 2015; Schmidt et al. 2009; Tan et al. 2011). Therefore, the ATP13A2 protein's function may be completely abolished by the presence of this deletion mutation, which may explain why the deletion mutation in Case 2 has similar effects as the frameshift mutation causing protein deficiency in Case 1.

Case 3's missense mutation corresponds to the 5th transmembrane region of the ATP13A2 protein and results in the substitution of leucine amino acid at position 939 with proline (Figure 2B). Transmembrane domains are composed of hydrophobic alpha helix structures. Amino acids like methionine, alanine, leucine, glutamate, and lysine are mostly found in α‐helical structures, while proline and glycine have a lower propensity to form helices (Skipper 2005). A previous study reported that missense mutations in the transmembrane regions of the ATP13A2 protein caused the proteins to be retained in the endoplasmic reticulum, thereby preventing its proper trafficking to the lysosome or cytoplasmic membranes, and impairing its function (Podhajska et al. 2012). Considering that Case 3's missense mutation replaces leucine, which is compatible with the alpha helix structure, with the rigid amino acid proline, it is reasonable to predict that this mutation will disrupt the alpha helix structure in the transmembrane domain, and affect proper folding of the ATP13A2 protein. Even though we initially thought that the presence of this missense mutation may impede the delivery of the relevant protein to the locations where it will perform its function, our data showed no significant change in the expression level of p‐eIF2α in MS‐fibroblasts (Figure 8A), suggesting that this mutation may not induce a pronounced protein misfolding–related ER stress response. Although the missense mutation in Case 3 leads to iron accumulation, the extent of accumulation is lower than that observed with the frameshift and deletion mutations in Cases 1 and 2. One possible explanation is that this mutation does not directly affect a core functional domain of ATP13A2; however, further studies are required to establish a definitive mechanism.

Finally, we assessed the rescue effect of wt‐ATP13A2 on cell viability under iron treatment in fibroblasts from the patient carrying the frameshift mutation, which we identified to cause the most drastic iron accumulation among the three ATP13A2 mutations. Despite the low efficiency of the transient transfection, we performed in fibroblast cells (Figure 9A), we determined that patient fibroblast cells expressing wt‐ATP13A2 were 10% less sensitive to iron treatment than patient fibroblast cells transfected with a mock vector (Figure 9B,C). This finding supports the loss of function of the ATP13A2 protein plays an active role in iron‐induced cell death observed in patient fibroblasts. Furthermore, this finding showed that overexpression of wt‐ATP13A2 can restore intracellular iron homeostasis in patient cells, implying that gene therapy could play a significant role in the treatment of incurable hereditary neurodegenerative illnesses such as KRS.

In conclusion, our research demonstrates that three types of mutations in the ATP13A2 gene result in varying degrees of iron deposition and different impacts on cell viability. Furthermore, the detection of iron accumulation that could not be detected on MRI by our in vitro studies, as well as the fact that our experimental findings are directly parallel to clinical findings, demonstrated the importance of molecular studies in understanding the effects of mutations causing neurodegenerative diseases with slow progression.
