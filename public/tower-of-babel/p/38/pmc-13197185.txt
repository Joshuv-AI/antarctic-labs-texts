Electronic health record-based prediction models for dementia detection: a systematic review of model performance and quality
Authors: Alicia Lu, Velandai Srikanth, Sarah Westworth, Yue-Guang Baey, Chris Moran, Richard Beare, Kristy Siostrom, Nadine Andrew, Taya Collyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLeveraging routine electronic health records (EHR) for dementia detection is a growing field, but quality and clinical utility of existing models are unclear. This systematic review aimed to evaluate performance, methodological quality, and risk of bias of EHR-based dementia prediction models.Materials and MethodsWe systematically searched Medline, EMBASE, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and ACM from conception until July 2024. All studies and grey literature describing development or validation of probabilistic prediction models using EHR data for dementia detection were included. Risk of bias was assessed using PROBAST.ResultsFifty-six studies (434 prediction models, 155 external validations) were included. Most models were prognostic (66%), used US data (71%), relied solely on structured data, and 47 (11%) were externally validated. Modeled outcomes were extremely heterogeneous: gold-standard clinical criteria were used in 17 models (4%), with others reliant on diagnostic codes for case ascertainment. Discriminative metrics were frequently reported (82% of models), but calibration was rarely assessed (16%). All models were judged high risk of bias, driven by poor outcome definition, inadequate handling of missing data, and potential overfitting.DiscussionOur review highlights significant issues with methodological rigor and reporting transparency in existing EHR dementia prediction models. Ambiguous outcomes, flawed case ascertainment, and incomplete performance reporting, all limit clinical usefulness. Overall, model performance was difficult to assess and compare across studies due to incomplete reporting.ConclusionElectronic health record-based dementia prediction is still in its infancy. Methodological rigor and interdisciplinary collaboration are essential to meet clinical needs and achieve real-world impact.Keywords: electronic health records, dementia, predictive learning models, machine learning, diagnosis

Background and significance

Dementia is a major global health issue.1 Timely and accurate identification is challenging as early changes may be subtle and there is no single objective diagnostic test. Diagnosis is a complex clinical process based on DSM-5 criteria,2 and involves detailed history-taking supported by cognitive testing and other investigations (Figure 1). Many people remain undiagnosed or are diagnosed late,3 impeding access to appropriate care pathways. These challenges have prompted interest in tools that can improve dementia identification and support better care. Prediction models, which predict health-related outcomes from patterns in variables known as “predictors”4 are increasingly being developed to support disease detection. In dementia, models can be diagnostic (detecting unrecognized dementia) or prognostic (estimating future risk).5

Much prediction modeling work in dementia has focused on imaging, biomarkers, cognitive assessment, or cohort study data that are not routinely available.6–8 While valuable in research contexts, these approaches are often difficult to scale in real-world practice due to cost, invasiveness, or resource demands. By contrast, electronic health record (EHR) data generated through routine medical care provide a pragmatic and widely available data source for prediction modeling.9 Electronic health records capture rich longitudinal information across both structured (eg, coded data such as diagnosis codes or prescriptions) and unstructured text (eg, clinical notes, imaging reports).10

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have created new opportunities for prediction modeling with EHRs. Machine learning and deep learning may be well suited to the complexity of high-dimensional EHR data, while natural language processing and transformer models may detect early clues of cognitive or functional decline in clinical notes.11 However, it remains unclear whether AI approaches offer meaningful gains in model performance over simpler statistical methods, or whether their added complexity introduces challenges such as reduced interpretability and greater risk of model overfitting.

The performance, methodological quality, and risk of bias of current EHR-based dementia prediction models are poorly understood. Existing reviews have been limited, either lacking systematic appraisal of methodological quality12 or not capturing the breadth of interdisciplinary literature.13 To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review to provide a comprehensive overview of EHR-based dementia prediction models and critically appraise their performance, methodological quality, and risk of bias. We also examined variability in performance by data type (structured or unstructured data), model type (diagnostic or prognostic), and modeling approach (statistical or AI).

Methods

This review follows the TRIPOD-SRMA (Transparent Reporting of Multivariable Prediction Models for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis Tailored for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) guidelines.14 The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024578155).

Eligibility

We included peer-reviewed studies and grey literature that developed or validated prediction models for identifying dementia (diagnostic) or predicting future dementia (prognostic), using EHR data as the primary data source. We relied on authors’ classification of data sources as EHR, acknowledging that EHRs may contain administrative elements. For this review, we considered EHR data to include routinely collected clinical information recorded during care (eg, diagnosis codes, medications, laboratory results, vital signs, clinical notes, encounter history, and problem lists). We excluded studies that (1) lacked an explicit predictive aim (eg, phenotype characterization or predictor identification only), (2) focused on conversion from mild cognitive impairment or dementia progression, (3) relied primarily on non-EHR data (eg, imaging datasets, recruited cohorts) with minimal EHR input, or (4) reported deterministic rule-based models. Deterministic approaches (eg, “if-then” rule systems) were excluded as they are poorly suited to the complexity of dementia and the high-dimensional nature of EHR data.

Literature search and study selection process

We searched Medline, EMBASE, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and ACM from inception to July 15, 2024, without filters. Search strings were developed in Medline and translated for other databases. Three concepts (dementia detection, prediction models, EHR) were combined using “AND,”, with “OR” applied within concepts to account for heterogeneous terminology. To capture emerging work, one author (A.L.) conducted a grey literature search using Google Scholar and preprint servers (arXiv, medRxiv, and bioRxiv) from inception to September 24, 2024, without date or filter restrictions. For each source, screening ceased once 50 consecutive records yielded no eligible studies. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary File A.

Records were deduplicated and imported into Covidence.15 Two reviewers (A.L., S.W.) independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts, with 97% agreement at title/abstract screening and 92.5% at full-text screening. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (T.C.). Corresponding authors were contacted when required to determine eligibility, and forward/backward citation tracking was undertaken for all included studies.

Data extraction and measures of interest

The data extraction form was informed by CHARMS16 and TRIPOD+AI (Supplementary File B).17 Two reviewers from a team of three (A.L., S.W., and Y.-G.B.) extracted data independently for each study, resolving differences by consensus. Extracted items included study characteristics, data source and setting, modeling approach, participants, predictors, outcomes, and model performance. Modeling approaches were classified as statistical, machine learning, deep learning, or hybrid/ensemble. We recorded whether models used predictors derived from structured data, unstructured data, or both. For model outcomes, we recorded both the stated prediction task and operational outcome.

Model performance was extracted as reported, focusing on discrimination (commonly reported as the area under the receiver operating characteristic [AUROC] curve or C-index) and calibration (typically assessed with calibration plots, slopes, or intercepts).18 Where studies developed multiple models using different predictors, methods, or outcomes, each model was extracted separately. Where available, we recorded model performance at 3 levels: apparent (development dataset), internal validation (resampling within the development dataset), and external validation (independent dataset).

Synthesis of results

Due to heterogeneity in performance metrics, we did not conduct formal meta-analysis. We synthesized results narratively and summarized performance descriptively, presenting summary statistics (means and 95% CIs) where reported and calculable. We compared model performance across subgroups defined by data type (structured, unstructured, both), model type (diagnostic, prognostic), and modeling approach (statistical, machine learning, deep learning).

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers per study (of A.L., S.W., or Y.-G.B.) using the PROBAST tool.19 It assesses risk of bias across 4 domains (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis) via 20 signaling questions. Rating differences were resolved by consensus.

Results

We identified 11 206 studies through database searches, 22 through grey literature sources, and 1 through citation tracking.20 After deduplication, 8312 abstracts were screened and 226 full-text articles assessed. Fifty-six studies met inclusion criteria, describing 434 prediction models and 155 external validations (Figure 2).

Model use and outcomes

Outcome definition and prediction task

Overall, 35 out of 54 model development studies (63%) did not clearly describe the outcome or prediction task (Supplementary File C, Table S1), making it difficult to determine whether a recorded dementia diagnosis was being used to indicate diagnosed dementia, undiagnosed but existing dementia, or future dementia risk (Figure 1). The prediction task was often described broadly (“detection” or “prediction”), while the operational outcome was a recorded diagnosis, without specifying the clinical timepoint or use case. In 6 studies, identical predictors were used to develop both diagnostic and prognostic models, differing only in prediction horizon (time period at which the outcome is predicted).21–26 Prediction horizons for prognostic models ranged from 1 day27 to 10 years,28 although most were under 5 years (169/285 models, 59%).

Reference standard

The method for ascertaining dementia status was not reported for 41 models (9%) across 5 model development studies (9%).29–33 Only 4 studies (7%) describing 17 models (4%) used diagnosis by established clinical criteria as the reference standard.27,34–36 Most studies relied on dementia administrative codes, dementia medication codes, or rule-based criteria combining codes with cognitive test scores or health-care utilization measures. Two studies (4%) describing 5 models (1%) used specialist clinic diagnostic codes as surrogate for clinical diagnosis.37,38 The remainder relied on manual chart review (5 studies, 9.3%)39–43 or a machine learning classifier (1 study, 1.9%)44 as reference standard.

Data used for model development

Data source and settings

Datasets integrating primary, secondary, and tertiary care were used to develop 191 models (44%). Another 120 models (28%) used hospital datasets in secondary and tertiary care, often from academic institutions or large health systems. Primary care datasets were used to develop 90 models (21%). Only 12 models (3%), all from a single study, used community or home-based care data. Full details are provided in Supplementary File C, Table S2.

Data type

Most models were developed using structured EHR data only (347 models, 80%). Forty-six models used unstructured data (11%), and 41 models (9%) used both. Structured predictors frequently included demographics (age, sex, ethnicity), clinical information (medical conditions, procedures, medications, laboratory values, vital signs), and health-care utilization measures. Unstructured predictors were derived from clinical text such as clinic letters and progress notes. These ranged from simple keyword lists to more complex representations such as text embeddings or topic features derived through extensive feature engineering.

Missing data

Missing data handling and assumptions about missingness were not reported for 321 models (75%). When reported, indicator methods (creation of binary variables indicating missingness) were most common (46 models, 11%), followed by single imputation (41 models, 9%) and complete case analysis (14 models, 3%). More than one approach was used in 10 models (2%). Supplementary File C, Table S3 provides a summary of approaches.

Modeling approach

Statistical methods (eg, regression) were used for 129 models (30%), machine learning for 193 models (45%), and deep learning for 96 models (22%). Combined methods were used for 10 models (2%) by averaging prediction probabilities from different models26 or using ensemble majority vote.45 The modeling approach was not reported for one model.41 A full list of approaches is provided in Supplementary File C, Table S4.

Model evaluation

Level of performance reporting

Apparent performance was reported for 21 models (5%) across 8 studies (14%), and was the only performance reported for 11 of the models (3%). Internal validation performance was reported for 366 models (84%) across 38 studies (68%), and both internal and external validation performance for 39 models (9%) across 3 studies (5%). Four studies (7%) reported external validation only; in 2 of these, models were developed but only external validation results were presented. No study reported all 3 levels of performance. In 3 studies (5%), performance reporting was difficult to interpret due to unclear model counts46 or incomplete results.43,47 See Supplementary File C, Table S5 for details.

Internal validation

A total of 420 models (97%) underwent internal validation. Only one method of internal validation was used for 247 models (58.8%), while 2 methods were used for 173 models (41.2%). Hold-out validation was most common (357 models, 85%), which includes train/test split of the development dataset, followed by cross-validation (223 models, 53%). Bootstrapping was used in only 10 models (2.3%), all from the same study.27 Additionally, 3 models (1%) from 2 studies were validated against manual chart review of a small stratified sample of previously unlabeled controls.39,40 Supplementary File C, Table S6 summarizes internal validation methods used.

Performance metrics

Performance reporting was highly variable. Discrimination was most frequently reported, with AUROC reported for 352 models (81%) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) reported for 50 models (12%). Calibration was reported for 69 models (16%) across 9 studies, most commonly using calibration plots (20 models, 5%).

Classification metrics were also inconsistently presented. Sensitivity was most common (211 models, 49%), followed by F1 score for 157 models (36%), positive predictive value (PPV) for 116 models (27%), specificity for 107 models (25%), model accuracy for 66 models (15%), and negative predictive value (NPV) for 19 models (4%). Risk thresholds used to derive these metrics were rarely specified (57 models, 13%). When reported, a priori thresholds were used for 45 models (10.4%) across 6 studies, and these were typically set at 0.5 with little justification or stakeholder input. As calibration and classification metrics were inconsistently reported, pooled comparison was not feasible. Discriminative performance, as measured by AUROC, was the most consistently reported metric and was therefore used to summarize model performance. Risk of bias assessment

All 434 models (100%) were rated as high overall risk of bias using PROBAST (Figure 4A). This was primarily driven by the analysis domain (389 models, 90%) and outcome domain (355 models, 82%). In the analysis domain, common reasons for high risk of bias included inadequate handling of missing data and failure to account for overfitting or optimism, often due to small sample sizes relative to the number of predictors, or inadequate internal validation. In the outcome domain, the main source of bias came from inappropriate outcome determination, as overreliance on diagnostic codes was likely to have introduced misclassification bias.48 Risk of bias in the participant domain was also common (280 models, 65%), largely due to widespread use of non-nested case-control designs. Reasons contributing to high risk of bias are detailed in Supplementary File C, Table S10. The predictor domain was almost always judged “unclear” (420 models, 97%) as studies rarely reported whether predictors were assessed without knowledge of outcome status. Across domains, many signaling questions could not be answered due to incomplete reporting.

Discussion

In this systematic review of EHR-based prediction models for dementia detection or prediction, we found a rapidly growing but methodologically inconsistent body of research. Across 434 developed models and 155 external validations, key limitations included unclear prediction aims, unreliable case ascertainment methods, incomplete performance reporting, and high risk of bias. These collectively limit the interpretability and clinical usefulness of current models.

Systematic reviews on dementia prediction have been published since our formal literature search was performed, but none have focused specifically on EHR-based dementia prediction models. Other reviews examined different data modalities,8,49 prediction tasks,50,51 or specific modeling approaches such as natural language processing.52,53 Where comparable, some general findings aligned with ours. Reviews that applied PROBAST consistently identified a high risk of bias across most models,8,51,54,55 and shared our specific concerns in the analysis and outcome domains. Limited external validation was repeatedly noted,8,49–52 alongside incomplete performance reporting, especially with regard to calibration.8,50,52 Importantly, very few reviews explicitly examine the reference standards used within such models, with only 2 EHR-focused reviews highlighting the overreliance on imperfect proxies such as diagnostic codes.50,52 Additionally, in contrast to reviews exploring specialized data modalities (eg, neuro-imaging, cognitive tests, biomarkers), our review focused exclusively on models that use routinely collected EHR data, including structured and unstructured textual data, which enables rapid and scalable dementia detection without requiring additional diagnostic assessments. Clinical notes may be particularly informative for dementia, as key diagnostic features such as functional and behavioral changes are often documented in text rather than in structured fields. By providing a comprehensive and detailed evaluation of reference standards, methodology, reporting quality, and bias for EHR-based dementia prediction models, we identify critical gaps that must be addressed to advance the potential of EHR data for real-world dementia detection.

A key finding of our review was ambiguity in how outcomes were defined relative to the intended prediction task. For prediction models to be clinically useful, both the health outcome and the model’s use case must be specified from the start.17,56 This is especially important in dementia, which lacks a discrete onset and can be diagnosed at multiple points along the disease trajectory. Although many studies listed a recorded diagnosis as the operational outcome, this same label can represent different prediction tasks, including identifying those with clinically diagnosed dementia for prevalence estimation,35 detecting those with undiagnosed dementia,34 or finding those at risk of future dementia for risk reduction (Figure 1).27 When the intended task is not explicitly stated, model outputs are difficult to interpret, and clinicians cannot determine whether a model is relevant to their practice, thus widening the translational gap between development and implementation.57 Clear problem definition at the outset, ideally with clinical stakeholder input, would better align model outcomes with the clinical question.

Clarity of the prediction task must be matched by accuracy in how dementia status is defined. The reliability of the reference standard is central to model accuracy,58 especially in dementia prediction where no standard diagnostic test exists. Only 4 studies used established clinical diagnostic criteria. Most relied on diagnostic codes, either alone or combined with other structured data, and few justified their chosen reference standard or acknowledged its limitations. Diagnostic codes are readily available in EHR datasets, but are prone to misclassification59 and may not reflect true dementia status, especially if derived from administrative data or nonspecialist settings (Figure 5). Reported PPVs vary widely across datasets (9%-100%), meaning some coded cases may not meet clinical diagnostic criteria.60–62 A few studies tried to improve reliability by only using codes from specialist clinics,37,38 but this approach was uncommon. Diagnostic codes also have variable sensitivity (21%-86%) as dementia is under-recorded and under-recognized, so absence of a code does not reliably indicate absence of dementia.60,63

A related issue is how the “non-dementia” comparator group is defined. Many studies implicitly assumed that individuals without a recorded diagnosis were dementia-free. However, given the known under-recognition and under-recording of dementia in community and hospital settings,64,65 this can misclassify the negative class and undermine the model’s ability to discriminate between “dementia” and “non-dementia” groups. Clear justification of both case and comparator definitions is therefore needed to support the development of dementia prediction models that are accurate and clinically useful.

We also found notable reporting inconsistencies, consistent with other prediction model reviews.66,67 Despite most studies being published after the release of the TRIPOD guidelines,17 adherence to reporting recommendations was low and evaluation practices were highly variable. Most studies reported discrimination metrics, most commonly AUROC, which represents the probability that a model assigns a higher risk to an individual with dementia compared with one without (Figure 6A).68 However, discrimination alone does not indicate whether predicted risks are accurate. Calibration, which assesses how closely predicted risks align with observed outcomes (Figure 6B),69 was rarely reported. Reliance on discrimination metrics alone is problematic, as a model may correctly rank individuals, but still systematically overestimate or underestimate absolute risk.70 This is particularly important in low-prevalence conditions like dementia, where high AUROC values can be misleading as models may appear to perform well simply by predicting the majority class.71 Clinically useful models require both good discrimination and good calibration to support decision-making.72

(B) Calibration: how well predicted risks match observed outcomes within groups of individuals with similar predicted risk. Classification metrics, which evaluate the model’s classification performance after applying a risk threshold (Figure 6C), were also inconsistently reported. Risk thresholds used were rarely specified, and even when reported, few studies provided clinical justification. Threshold selection is inherently dependent on the clinical context,73 as tradeoffs between false positives and false negatives can have important implications for end-users. For broad case-finding or screening, sensitivity may be prioritized to minimize missed dementia cases, whereas in referral triage or specialist assessment pathways, a higher PPV may be preferable to avoid unnecessary investigations and anxiety. When studies fail to justify or report their thresholds, it becomes difficult to interpret model performance or assess clinical applicability. Clinical utility analysis, such as net benefit or decision curve analysis, remains underused and could be valuable in guiding threshold choice and providing more insight into clinical utility.74

Despite the increasing volume of EHR-based dementia prediction models, only 2 have progressed to formal impact assessment. eRADAR is a 31-predictor logistic regression model that detects people with unrecognized dementia using structured EHR data,75 while the Passive Digital Marker model is a random forest model developed to flag Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias 1-3 years before routine documentation using EHR prescription data, diagnostic codes, and medical notes.76 To our knowledge, no models have yet been implemented in clinical practice. Although this may partly reflect how new the field is, limited external validation is also a likely contributor, as most models were not tested across different health systems or time periods, despite known variation in coding practices, EHR data structures, and population characteristics.77,78 This creates uncertainty about model performance outside of the development setting. Lack of model transparency further limits model evaluation and adoption, as many studies do not publish model coefficients/code, or propose multiple candidate models without identifying which ones warrant further validation. This makes it challenging for others to build on prior work. Additionally, while strengthening external validation and reporting are necessary first steps, clinical adoption of these models also depends on their acceptability to clinicians and people with dementia, integration within existing workflows, and the ability to monitor and update them over time. These require collaboration between model developers, clinical teams, and health services early in the model development process, rather than after model completion.

Implications and future directions

Our findings highlight several priorities for advancing the clinical utility of EHR-based dementia detection models. First, modeling approaches should be selected on the basis of a clearly defined clinical question and health-care setting rather than methodological novelty. In this review, performance did not differ substantially across statistical, machine learning, and deep learning approaches, consistent with other reviews.79 Improving data quality, outcome definition, and overall model evaluation may therefore have greater real-world impact than pursuing additional model complexity. Second, consensus is needed on what constitutes a valid reference standard for dementia in EHR contexts, recognizing that dementia is often under-recorded and lacks a single diagnostic test. Third, EHR data itself carries both promise and challenge for dementia prediction modeling. Structured data are readily available at scale, but may miss the subtle cognitive, functional, or behavioral changes that characterize dementia. Unstructured data (eg, clinical notes) can capture this nuance but may require greater methodological complexity in data processing. Recent advances in natural language processing offer new opportunities to extract meaningful features from free text, so a key priority moving forward is to utilize both data types in ways that capture dementia’s clinical complexity without compromising model interpretability and reproducibility.

Fourth, improved reporting is essential to support transparency, model reproducibility, and methodological rigor. Several well-established resources are already available to guide researchers. Reporting frameworks like TRIPOD/TRIPOD+AI for prediction model development,14,17 along with medical AI-specific assessment tools such as the IJMEDI Medical AI checklist,80 provide detailed guidance on key elements to be considered in model development and validation. Additionally, although PROBAST is a risk of bias assessment tool, its accompanying guidance provides valuable information on common methodological pitfalls during model development, including outcome definition, analysis, and handling of missing data.19

Finally, stronger interdisciplinary collaboration is required throughout model development as no single discipline holds all the necessary expertise to develop rigorous and clinically relevant models. Clinicians and people with dementia are suited to defining the clinical problem and risk thresholds, statisticians to ensuring methodological rigor, data scientists or machine learning engineers to supporting the use of advanced computational or AI approaches, and health services to guiding clinical workflow integration. Without early collaboration, models risk becoming technically robust but clinically unusable.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this review is that it provides an extensive and in-depth overview of the current landscape in dementia prediction modeling using EHR data. Our search strategy was comprehensive, covering both published peer-reviewed studies and grey literature. Our use of the CHARMS and PROBAST frameworks also provided a structured and detailed approach to data extraction and risk of bias assessment. Independent dual screening and extraction by multiple reviewers reduced the risk of selection bias. However, some limitations should be noted. First, heterogeneity in model design and performance reporting precluded formal meta-analysis. Consequently, we summarized model performance using mean AUROC, acknowledging that it does not fully reflect overall model performance. Subgroup differences in mean AUROC should also be interpreted cautiously. Second, our data synthesis was heavily reliant on what was published. Last, some PROBAST judgments may be subjective given the poor reporting across studies.

Conclusion

Despite growing interest in using EHR data for dementia prediction and detection, most existing models suffer from methodological limitations, unclear clinical intent, and poor reporting, which limits readiness for clinical implementation. Improving model transparency, clearly defining intended use and outcomes, and prioritizing external validation are essential next steps. As the field incorporates increasingly complex modeling techniques, interdisciplinary collaboration and end-user involvement will be critical to ensure that future models are both technically robust and clinically meaningful.
