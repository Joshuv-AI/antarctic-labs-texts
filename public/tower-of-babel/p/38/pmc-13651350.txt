From parallel to aligned: a United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE®) mapping and assessment-tagging framework for medical education
Authors: Robert M Badeau, Thea Mallini, Jennifer Laury, Vincent VanBuren, Danielle Dickey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical students often experience a “parallel curriculum,” which is perceived as a real misalignment between what is taught in the formal institutional curriculum and, for licensing, the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE®) content that they must master independently. Background

In many medical schools, students perceive that two curricula exist in “parallel;” the institutional curriculum that is formally taught and assessed, and the 2025 USMLE® Content Outline [1] that students must master independently and is self-directed [2, 3]. This perception may increase student apprehension in choosing their study planning. For example, they may be required to study a large amount of mandatory content for their courses that is unrelated to the USMLE® Content Outline, and the additional workload may have a negative impact on performance and their overall wellbeing [2–5]. This perceived duality highlights the value of linking the USMLE® Content Outline directly to the institutional curriculum, especially to learning objectives and assessments. Doing so offers a benchmark to understand where USMLE® Content Outline topics are addressed and for faculty to realign their course instructional design to bridge coverage and sequence gaps and reduce redundancy. Providing these linkages through visualization, such as in dashboards, allows curriculum committees and administrators to evaluate alignment and make intentional choices to strengthen connections between institutional objectives, the USMLE® Content Outline, and assessments [6, 7].

Curriculum mapping plays a major role in curriculum development and planning and, overall, in the quality improvement process by forming relational linkages among institutional program objectives, course-level learning objectives, session-level learning objectives while allowing alignment with frameworks such as the AAMC Keywords and the USMLE® Content Outline [6–10]. Visualizing these relational linkages may increase positive faculty and student perceptions of curricular alignment and intentionality. This alignment is facilitated by “tagging” curricular content, such as assessments, to the USMLE® Content Outline and AAMC Keywords, that allows for aggregated data summation, curation, and visualization.

Although not directly intended to reduce the perception of a “parallel” curriculum and lacking an intentional coding system, approaches to map the USMLE® Content Outline to curricular elements exist. Dexter et al. developed an approach that deconstructed the USMLE® Content Outline into Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms for use in their database, while Yoder et al. demonstrated how student-generated learning objectives could be mapped against the USMLE® Content Outline [11, 12]. In these studies, the USMLE® Content Outline is not formally coded or presented at a granular level. This limits the standardization, depth, and utility in categorizing institutional USMLE® Content Outline content and setting USMLE® Content Outline benchmarks for curricular content development. The USMLE® Content Outline is granular at multiple levels that can create complexity, especially in MeSH term searching.

Currently, coded AAMC Keywords offer a solution to standardizing curriculum content through tagging, however, these Keywords offer broad and simple medical and basic science terms that are not granular and require additional searching to find medical content definitions and are not directly connected to the USMLE® Content Outline. With an effective curricular map that is aligned to the USMLE® Content Outline, faculty and students can easily understand how the institution’s curriculum aligns with the USMLE® Content Outline. Here, we describe how these codes are operationalized and show their role in bridging institutional learning objectives with the USMLE® Content Outline to align in-house curricular design to the USMLE® Content Outline. We created a machine-readable, granular, hierarchical coding library that was built from the USMLE® Content Outline. We have used these USMLE® Content Outline codes to tag or link preclerkship session learning objectives and exam questions. Prior work has mapped curricular content to the USMLE® Content Outline: Dexter et al. deconstructed the outline into Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, and Yoder et al. mapped student-generated learning objectives against it [11, 12]. The distinct contribution of the present work is the granularity and machine-readability of the coding rather than the absence of prior mapping efforts; to our knowledge, no prior approach has preserved the full parent-child hierarchy of the outline as a directly assignable code library.

Methods

USMLE® content outline

As our reference, we used the 2025 USMLE® Content Outline [1]: https://www.usmle.org/sites/default/files/2022-01/USMLE_Content_Outline_0.pdf.

Code library

We created a hierarchical coding library by a top-down, left-to-right method in transferring data from the 2025 USMLE® Content Outline to an Office 365 Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The major Content Topic was the parent and from each bullet point downward, we assigned “2.1” as the first code. If there was another bullet point that had more than one description, then we created “2.1.1” etc. For “normal” processes, we created our in-house code as “1.0.” This was machine-readable and granular.

Curriculum linking to session learning objectives

We used ChatGPT (GPT-5, OpenAI) to propose USMLE® Content Outline code matches for preclerkship session learning objectives. This model was selected as the generative AI tool available to the curriculum mapping team at the time the mapping was performed. The structured prompt provided to the model was: “I am a curriculum analyst at a medical school. I need to map the USMLE® Content Outline codes to session learning objectives. Using the master code library provided (USMLE Codes Library worksheet), assign all USMLE® Content Outline codes that are linked to each session learning objective in column C of the course worksheet. Return each session learning objective with its matched code(s) and the code description. Where a session learning objective maps to more than one domain, list all applicable codes.” The master code library and the course session learning objectives were supplied to the model as structured worksheet inputs. Mapping was performed iteratively per course: the model’s initial output was reviewed, and where a session learning objective was unmapped or mapped to an implausible code, the objective was re-submitted with the relevant outline section for refinement. Because the model is proprietary and non-deterministic, identical prompts may produce minor variation in output across runs; all final mappings were therefore subject to human verification (described below) rather than accepted as generated.

Exam tagging

Using the ExamSoft Enterprise Portal (www.examsoft.com), in “Category Management” in the “ADMIN” drop-down menu, we created a USMLE Codes category. We created 18 categories for each USMLE® Content Outline title. These categories were then available upon editing an exam question.

Mapping verification and scope

All AI-proposed code assignments underwent a two-step expert verification before entry into the database. In the first step, a curriculum specialist holding a terminal degree in medicine reviewed each proposed code against the master code library and the source USMLE® Content Outline, comparing the assigned code and its parent-child position to the conceptual content of the session learning objective. Where the model proposed a code that did not match the objective’s content, or omitted an applicable code, the specialist corrected the assignment manually, and objectives mapping to more than one domain were verified for each applicable code. In the second step, the relevant course directors reviewed the verified mappings for their respective courses, confirming conceptual fit within the context of the course’s instructional design and resolving any remaining ambiguous assignments by reference to the outline text and their subject-matter expertise.

Disagreements between steps were reconciled by discussion between the curriculum specialist and the course director. The verification relied on this expert review process rather than on a quantified inter-rater agreement statistic, which was not formally recorded in this proof-of-concept phase; we identify this as a limitation below and therefore describe the mappings as expert-verified rather than reporting a numerical accuracy rate.

The work reported here was conducted in the preclerkship phase at a single institution. Session learning objective mapping was performed across preclerkship organ-system and foundational courses, and exam tagging was operationalized in the cardiovascular course as the initial demonstration. The figures presented illustrate representative output from this mapping and tagging process rather than aggregate coverage statistics, which were not yet compiled at the time of this report.

Discussion

Here, we show the operationalization of the USMLE® Content Outline through a machine-readable, hierarchical coding library. We show how these codes can be mapped to session-level learning objectives and to exam questions.

This innovation’s utility comes through data collection and visualization. A challenge that we have at our medical institution is that students perceive that our curriculum is “disjointed,” and this reduces confidence that they will be prepared for their USMLE® Step 1 exam. Students use third-party resources for studying and may not attend lectures and may not reach their potential on in-house exams leading to dissatisfaction. To provide a solution, we collect session-level learning objectives from our courses each academic year and have linked USMLE® Content Outline codes to these. We are also mapping these USMLE® Content Outline codes to our in-house exams. We have started to do semantic mapping with these codes and session-level learning objectives to visualize the coverage of the USMLE® Content Outline in our curriculum. For transparency and intentionality, we intend to share these dashboards with faculty and students.

According to the USMLE® Step 1 Content Outline and Specifications, musculoskeletal, skin, and subcutaneous tissue account for approximately 8–12% of total questions, and the gastrointestinal system accounts for 6–10% [1]. It is possible to create dashboards of respectively covered content from the USMLE® Content Outline “tags” that we have mapped to our session-level learning objectives and to assessment questions that support our exam blueprinting initiatives. Although we are in the process of creating semantic mapping visualizations and dashboards for these benchmarks, we can now communicate with curriculum stakeholders that we are effectively mapping our preclerkship curriculum to the USMLE® Content Outline. In addition, the machine-readability of the code data allows generative artificial intelligence platforms to align both curricular inventory and the content outline, with the possibility to create custom dashboards for the curriculum as a whole or cross-sectionally at each course.

Furthermore, curricular relationships are easily understood by faculty and students with this standardized USMLE® Content Outline coding. A limitation to prior studies is in the USMLE® Content Outline data structure, because it has presented a challenge for curriculum mappers, and limited their success in demonstrating how well their curricula align with it. By coding and linking the USMLE® Content Outline to session-level learning objectives or exam questions directly, these relationships are explicit. Prior studies did not address direct linkages of session-level learning objectives to the USMLE® Content Outline (Dexter et al., Yoder) [11, 12]

The high-granularity coding that this method provides is, to our knowledge, distinct from prior approaches in its preservation of the full outline hierarchy as a directly assignable code set, and could in principle support multi-institutional benchmarking and a common coding framework analogous to the existing AAMC Keyword library. We present this multi-institutional potential as anticipated value rather than a demonstrated outcome; no cross-institutional data were collected in the present work. A limitation in the AAMC Keyword library is its lack of granularity, which can reduce clarity for faculty and other stakeholders. However, the AAMC Keyword library can provide information on the USMLE® Step 1 discipline specifications (United States Medical Licensing Examination [USMLE], n.d.). For example, 45 to 55% of the USMLE® Step 1 discipline content is pathology (United States Medical Licensing Examination [USMLE], n.d.). This can be mapped to keyword K-064 pathophysiology, and when mapped to session-level objectives and to assessment questions, curricular coverage can be determined.

While the present study focused on building the USMLE® Content Outline coding library, the system is designed to be adaptable. Institutions can use this method by session-level mapping, in assessment tagging, in gap and redundancy analysis, in multi-institutional benchmarking, and integration with existing frameworks. In session-level mapping, faculty can assign the USMLE® Content Outline codes directly to course and session objectives, creating a relational map between institutional objectives and the USMLE® Content Outline. For example, a session on allergic asthma could be coded to both RS (Respiratory System: asthma, obstructive airway disease) and IS (Immune System: hypersensitivity reactions). With assessment tagging, exam questions can be linked to the USMLE® Content Outline codes, allowing institutions to generate item banks that align both with their curriculum and USMLE® emphasis. This enables performance dashboards that display how students perform on specific USMLE® Content Outline domains. In gap and redundancy analysis, institutions can compare which domains are overrepresented (e.g., Cardiovascular, Gastrointestinal) and which are underrepresented (e.g., Social Sciences, Patient Safety). This provides an evidence-based approach to refining curricular balance and constructive alignment. With multi-institutional benchmarking, because the code library is standardized and machine-readable, two or more schools could compare their mappings to the same USMLE® Content Outline domains. This creates opportunities for shared benchmarking, collaborative research, and shared curricular innovation across institutions. In integration with existing frameworks, the USMLE® Content Outline code library can be layered alongside the AAMC Keyword Library or institutional program objectives creating a unified database that supports our efforts in aligning our curriculum to the USMLE® Content Outline and may reduce the perception of a “parallel curriculum.”

Limitations

This work has several limitations. First, the coding library was constructed by a single coder through a top-down, left-to-right parse of the outline; ambiguous coding decisions, such as where to terminate a parent-child branch or how to place content spanning multiple systems, were resolved by the coder’s judgment against the outline text rather than by independent dual coding, and a second-coder reliability check of the library structure was not performed. Second, the study was conducted at a single institution and was limited to the preclerkship phase; clerkship content and external-institution applicability remain untested. Third, although AI-generated mappings were verified through a two-step expert process involving a curriculum specialist with a terminal medical degree followed by course director review, a quantified measure of agreement between AI-proposed and human-assigned codes (such as an accepted/corrected/rejected ratio or a second-reviewer agreement statistic) was not recorded, so we describe the mappings as expert-verified rather than reporting an accuracy rate. Fourth, the generative model used is proprietary and non-deterministic, which constrains exact reproducibility of the automated mapping step; the human verification layer is intended to mitigate, but does not eliminate, this constraint. The mappings reported here were generated using GPT-5; the curriculum mapping workflow has subsequently transitioned to Anthropic Claude Opus (versions 4.6 through 4.8) for ongoing curriculum cycles, and a systematic comparison of model performance on this mapping task remains an area for future work. Finally, the dashboards, semantic-mapping visualizations, and exam-blueprinting analytics described are in development; no perception, alignment, performance, or multi-institutional outcome data are reported here, and the corresponding statements should be read as rationale and anticipated value rather than demonstrated outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a granular, machine‑readable USMLE® Content Outline code library operationalizes the 2025 USMLE® Content Outline for curriculum mapping and assessment. The approach is designed to be adaptable across courses and institutions and is intended to lay the groundwork for future multi‑institutional benchmarking, improved transparency for learners and faculty, and scalable analytics and visualizations that connect objectives and assessments. Validation of these anticipated applications, including alignment and performance outcomes, is the subject of ongoing work.
