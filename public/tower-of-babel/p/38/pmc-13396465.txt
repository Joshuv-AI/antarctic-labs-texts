Methylation-based ctDNA monitoring in metastatic breast cancer during CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy
Authors: Mitchell J Elliott, Jesús Fuentes-Antrás, Sasha C Main, Aaron Dou, Elizabeth Shah, Caroline Weipert, Geethika Yalamanchili, Taylor Bird, June Roh, Celeste Yu, Michelle B Nadler, Eitan Amir, Hal K Berman, Lillian L Siu, Philippe L Bedard, David W Cescon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCirculating tumor DNA (ctDNA) enables minimally invasive treatment monitoring, yet the clinical validity of longitudinal ctDNA surveillance in metastatic breast cancer remains incompletely defined. Here we show that methylation-based tumor fraction (mTF), a tissue-agnostic epigenomic measure of ctDNA, closely mirrors radiographic response and clinical outcomes in 57 patients with estrogen receptor-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer receiving endocrine therapy with a CDK4/6 inhibitor across 350 serial plasma timepoints. Baseline ctDNA is detectable in 94.7% of patients. Clearance of mTF on treatment is associated with prolonged time to treatment discontinuation (HR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.07-0.41; p < 0.0001). A rise in mTF (molecular progression) precedes treatment discontinuation by a median of 5.8 months and outperforms mutation-based tracking, with resistance-associated alterations in ESR1 and RB1 frequently emerging at molecular progression. These findings support prospective evaluation of mTF-guided treatment strategies.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Breast cancer

Introduction

Estrogen receptor-positive, HER2-negative (ER+/HER2−) breast cancer is the most common clinical receptor subtype1. Despite advances in screening and curative-intent therapy, many patients present with de novo metastatic disease and a sizable proportion of those treated for early-stage breast cancer will develop incurable recurrent metastatic disease2. Standard initial treatment for ER+/HER2− metastatic breast cancer (mBC) includes endocrine therapy and a CDK4/6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i)3–5.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), the tumor-derived component of cell-free DNA (cfDNA), is a blood-based biomarker that offers a minimally invasive method for tumor genotyping and treatment response monitoring6–8. While baseline ctDNA genotyping and ctDNA levels have some prognostic value in patients receiving endocrine therapy + CDK4/6i, the validity of longitudinal ctDNA monitoring in this population remains incompletely defined9–13. Many studies lack concurrent radiographic assessments, limiting the ability to correlate ctDNA dynamics with standard tumor response measurements. In addition, patients without radiographically evaluable disease by standard Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version (v) 1.1 are frequently excluded from clinical trials, which limits the generalizability of findings to this common disease presentation14. Additionally, most literature to date has used panel-based single nucleotide variant (SNV) ctDNA genotyping assays which vary in sensitivity and specificity15,16. These assays can produce false negatives when ctDNA levels fall below the detection threshold (typically 0.1–0.3%), and may be confounded by clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP)-related variants misclassified as tumor-derived, hindering accurate monitoring of on-treatment ctDNA dynamics17.

To address these challenges, we evaluated the clinical validity of a tumor-agnostic, highly sensitive (limit of detection 95, LoD = 0.005% in advanced breast cancer; limit of quantification 95, LoQ95: 0.01%) epigenomic ctDNA assay (Guardant Reveal), which provides a methylation-based tumor fraction (mTF), in patients with ER+/HER2− mBC treated with endocrine therapy and a CDK4/6i. We evaluated its role in baseline prognostication and assessed concordance with radiographic response, on treatment ctDNA dynamics, as well as the timing of emerging resistance alterations detected using a comprehensive genotyping ctDNA test (Guardant360 Liquid). Ultimately, this work explored how using such an assay for longitudinal ctDNA surveillance could support prospective evaluation, optimize clinical response assessment, and inform future trial designs in ER+/HER2− mBC.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 58 participants were enrolled between May 1st, 2018 and December 1st, 2023 (median follow-up: 33.9 months, range: 1.6–75.9). Of these, 57 participants were included in the final cohort (one participant did not proceed onto CDK4/6i treatment and was excluded). The median age at diagnosis of stage IV disease was 59.3 years (range: 38.0–88.2). Nearly all participants were female (56/57), post-menopausal (46/57, 80.7%) and had invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC, 45/57, 78.9%). The most common endocrine therapy partner was an aromatase inhibitor (letrozole; 39/57, 68.4%) and most participants received palbociclib (41/57, 71.9%), followed by ribociclib (14/57, 24.6%), and abemaciclib (2/57, 3.5%). Discontinuation of CDK4/6i with continuation of endocrine therapy was rare (4/57 patients) and all instances were related to the theoretical concerns about complications related to COVID-19 during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. Measurable disease by RECIST v1.1 was present in 35/57 (61.4%) with 5/57 (8.8%) having bone-only, non-RECIST measurable disease. Sample availability and sequencing status

All available timepoints were sequenced (n = 350; median 6 per patient, range, 2–15). Most samples were collected while receiving endocrine therapy + CDK4/6i (330/350, 94.3%); some participants consented to additional collections following progression on this therapy, which were included. All samples were successfully sequenced using the methylation assay; however, five samples failed NGS-based genotyping (753 gene panel; 99% success rate).

Baseline ctDNA detection and association with outcome

ctDNA was detected by methylation in 54/57 (94.7%) of participants at baseline and in 276/350 (79%) of all samples tested. Clinical characteristics of participants without mTF detected at baseline are summarized (Supplementary Table 1) but were generally participants with low volume metastatic disease. One of these participants experienced clinical progression in the follow up period at which time ctDNA was detected via a rising mTF (outlined below). The median mTF at baseline was 1.23% (range: 0–51.3%) and across all timepoints in detectable samples was 0.45% (range: 0.00046–79.9%). Higher baseline mTF was associated with death during the follow up period (Supplementary Fig. 1C; Wilcoxon rank-sum, p = 0.00013), the use of abemaciclib (Supplementary Fig. 1B; Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.011), and the use of fulvestrant (Supplementary Fig. 1D; Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.026). The association between mTF and other baseline clinical variables of interest are illustrated (Supplementary Fig. 1E–K).

Somatic alterations in genes of interest detected in the baseline sample are summarized (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1). The most common somatic alterations were in PIK3CA (38%) and TP53 (30%), as expected. Given the small sample size and number of events, the association between curated resistance alterations at baseline and outcome was not evaluated.

An association between higher baseline mTF (defined relative to the cohort median with participants having undetectable ctDNA by this method considered separately) and shorter time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) approached but did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1B; p = 0.058), whereas a significant association with overall survival was observed (Fig. 1C; p = 0.0023). Participants with a mTF that was undetectable or below the cohort median had the best TTD and OS outcomes. For those who had available panel-based sequencing, baseline maxVAF was not prognostic of TTD (Supplementary Fig. 1L; p = 0.14) but was prognostic of OS (Supplementary Fig. 1M; p = 0.011).

ctDNA dynamics and association with outcome

An exploratory analysis of multiple thresholds of mTF decrease and their association with TTD and OS was performed (Table 2). A consistent trend of larger mTF decrease from baseline was associated with improved outcomes. Landmark analyses were performed at 2–4, 4–6, and 6–8 months to evaluate whether a ≥50% decline in mTF was associated with clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table 2). Across all intervals, a decline in mTF was associated with favorable trends in both TTD and OS. The associations were most pronounced at later timepoints (4–6 and 6–8 months) consistent with the median time to ctDNA clearance, as discussed below (Supplementary Table 2). The association was not observed between a ≥50% decline in maxVAF and TTD or OS (Supplementary Table 2).

ctDNA clearance and association with outcome

The association between ctDNA clearance at any timepoint and outcome were explored using both mTF and maxVAF. There was a strong positive correlation between mTF and maxVAF in all available timepoints (Supplementary Fig. 2A; r2 = 0.8, Pearson p < 0.0001). ctDNA clearance on therapy, as measured by mTF, was observed in 18 participants with a median time to clearance of 7.3 months (range: 0.7–30.6 months; Fig. 2A). mTF clearance was associated with the use of an aromatase inhibitor (Fig. 2B; OR: 6.4, 95% CI: 1.52–44.36; p = 0.024), likely representing a more endocrine-sensitive disease state. The presence of visceral disease, a baseline mTF above the median, RECIST-measurable disease, and pre-menopausal status were associated with mTF persistence on therapy (Fig. 2B). There were non-significant trends towards higher baseline mTF in participants with RECIST-measurable disease (p = 0.09; Supplementary Fig. 2B) and in those with persistent mTF (p = 0.15; Supplementary Fig. 2C).

Participants who had mTF clearance at any timepoint had a significantly longer TTD (Fig. 2C; HR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.07–0.41; p < 0.0001) and OS (Fig. 2D; HR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.42; p = 0.0019). Time-dependent Cox proportional hazards modeling was performed to evaluate the association between ctDNA clearance and TTD; this approach accounted for covariates that vary over time, thereby minimizing immortal time bias and enabling a more accurate assessment of biomarker effect. In this analysis, mTF clearance was also strongly associated with improved TTD (HR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.10–0.53, p < 0.0001). OS was not evaluated given possible confounding effects in subsequent lines of therapy and a lack of corresponding ctDNA dynamics.

In contrast to mTF, longitudinal assessment of maxVAF demonstrated persistence of low-level somatic variants in many timepoints despite treatment response (Supplementary Fig. 2D and Supplementary Data 2). When evaluating individual variants, many persisted at low levels independent of treatment, suggestive of possible CHIP-related origin despite being labeled as somatic (Supplementary Data 2). ctDNA clearance, measured by the maxVAF, occurred in five participants; there were non-significant trends for improved TTD (Supplementary Fig. 2E; HR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.06–1.1; p = 0.068) and OS (Supplementary Fig. 2F; HR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.16–1.06; p = 0.066) for those who had clearance by this method.

Exploring metrics of ctDNA response and progression

An exploratory analysis of ctDNA dynamics at serial timepoints and radiographic outcomes was performed. Matched radiographic evaluations and evaluable ctDNA timepoints are summarized (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Using a strict definition that only considered matched radiographic restaging events and plasma timepoints collected within 30 days, there were 175 evaluable events, of which 110 were evaluable by RECIST v1.1 criteria (Supplementary Fig. 4A).

In evaluable participants, exploratory thresholding changes in mTF over time improved performance of longitudinal monitoring with respect to radiographic changes in all participants (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Data 3) and in those with RECIST-measurable disease (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Negative predictive value (NPV) and sensitivity (SN) were higher than positive predictive value (PPV) and specificity (SP) when measuring mTF and maxVAF, suggesting that individuals with ctDNA-defined response were unlikely to have radiographic progression in the absence of mPD regardless of the method used. However, specificity and PPV were lower as mTF/maxVAF often rose prior to radiographic progression, corresponding to a molecular lead time (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 4B). PPV and SP were better for changes in mTF compared to changes in maxVAF, which may reflect spurious contributors to maxVAF such as CHIP (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Given the presence of fluctuating detection in patients with lower levels of ctDNA as determined by the methylation-based assay (mTF), an analytical floor was considered and definitions of response and progression were explored with progression defined as rising above 0.1% (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 4B). In general, this improved sensitivity and NPV across evaluated definitions (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 4B).

An exploratory definition of molecular response (≥50% decrease in mTF from baseline) and molecular progression (<50% decrease in mTF from baseline and/or a rise in mTF above an absolute mTF of 0.1%) was evaluated, which had a NPV of 99.2% and SN of 95.8% versus radiologist assessment; one event of non-RECIST defined radiographic progression occurred in the radiographically evaluable participants in the absence of a rise in mTF (breast lesion increased from 11 × 9 mm to 13 × 13 mm). The above definition of molecular response and progression had an NPV of 100% and SN of 100% for progressive disease by RECIST v1.1 criteria.

Overall response rate (ORR) was generally concordant with matched plasma timepoints and the measured change in mTF from baseline; participants with more favorable radiographic response had a more marked decline in mTF from baseline while participants with lesser response experienced intermediate declines in mTF (Fig. 4B). Most participants with primary progressive disease did not have a decrease in mTF ≥50% or had a persistent rise in mTF at matched radiographic assessments (Fig. 4B). One participant experienced primary progressive disease despite an initial ≥50% decrease in mTF compared to baseline. However, the mTF measured at radiographic progression increased relative to a prior on-treatment timepoint, highlighting both the value of serial monitoring and the limitations of interpreting a later on-treatment or progression sample in isolation without considering earlier on-treatment dynamics (Fig. 4C; denoted as ** in Fig. 4B). Another participant had a progressive rise in mTF at their matched best radiographic response assessment of RECIST-measurable lesions (Fig. 4D, denoted as *** in Fig. 4B; −76% decrease in baseline tumor measurements). This participant went on to have RECIST-defined progressive disease at their subsequent radiographic assessment with the emergence of new lesions. Few patients had samples analyzed post progression (Supplementary Data 4).

Molecular progression, ctDNA lead time, and emergence of resistance alterations

Next, we sought to characterize the temporal dynamics of acquired genomic alterations associated with CDK4/6i resistance in relation to the rise in mTF (molecular progression). Acquired genomic alterations in this cohort are highlighted (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Data 5). Consistent with previous data, gain of function alterations in ESR1 were detected in 45% of participants while loss of function alterations in RB1 occurred in 32% (Fig. 5A). Examples illustrating the interval between ctDNA molecular progression (mTF rise) to genomic progression (the emergence of an acquired alteration putatively associated with treatment resistance) are highlighted (Fig. 5B). Consistent with the reported sensitivity of the two tests, the mTF rose prior to the detection of CDK4/6i resistance alterations, including all (7/7) evaluable cases with acquired ESR1 mutations. The median lead time from mTF rise (mPD) to discontinuation of CDK4/6i was 5.8 months (range: 0.7–29.2 months).

Discussion

Here, we present data demonstrating the clinical validity of longitudinal ctDNA monitoring data in patients with ER+/HER2− mBC receiving endocrine therapy and a CDK4/6-inhibitor. With a median follow-up of almost three years, this real-world cohort revealed several key insights about the prognostic and predictive value of methylation-based ctDNA detection, clearance, and molecular progression, as well as its relationship with radiographic outcomes and emergent resistance alterations in mBC. Consistent with the high sensitivity of methylation-based approaches, ctDNA was detected in 94.7% of patients at baseline; participants without detectable ctDNA at baseline typically had low-volume or indolent disease with no deaths occurring in this group in follow-up. Baseline genomic alterations were consistent with those published in secondary analyses of large clinical trials with similar populations. It is notable that baseline mTF was prognostic of TTD and OS in this cohort. While no predefined cutoff was used to classify those with high or low risk based on the baseline mTF, these data are hypothesis-generating and could permit refinement of patient selection for investigational interventions, such as treatment escalation. In addition, the association between higher baseline mTF and adverse clinical features, including visceral disease and fulvestrant use (likely representing a second-line or an endocrine-resistant setting), further highlighting its potential role as a stratification biomarker that reflects clinical behavior and disease biology in this setting.

A ≥50% decrease in mTF early on treatment or clearance at any timepoint was associated with favorable clinical outcomes, which is consistent with previous data using this assay across other cancer types18. Landmark analyses demonstrated that a ≥50% decline in mTF within 8 months of treatment initiation was associated with favorable TTD and OS, with the strongest effects observed in the later treatment windows. Meanwhile, patients with an increase in mTF in the first 2–4 months progressed quickly, highlighting the potential utility of monitoring both early on-therapy (to identify early progressors) and on active treatment to further stratify molecular response and detection of subsequent molecular progression. In contrast to the results with mTF, no association was observed between a ≥50% decline in maxVAF and either endpoint, underscoring the accuracy of mTF-based dynamics in capturing clinically relevant treatment responses. Clearance of mTF was associated with the use of letrozole as the endocrine partner suggesting that this is more likely to occur in an endocrine-sensitive setting.

Compared with tumor-agnostic, mutation-based ctDNA assays that rely on tracking putative somatic variants, a methylation-based approach enabled tumor fraction quantification, which was independent of the mutational status of a patient’s cancer and not confounded by CHIP variants. This approach may provide a more broadly applicable strategy for longitudinal disease monitoring, especially in tumor types with low somatic mutation rates.

Given the repeated measure at variable intervals, incorporating time-dependent covariates into the survival analysis was essential to minimize the risk of immortality bias. Time-dependent modeling provided a framework to capture the dynamic relationship between mTF clearance and patient outcomes, thereby strengthening the validity of clinical interpretations.

While selecting for those who are experiencing the best response to therapy and ongoing response to therapy are obvious correlates of improved clinical outcomes, these metrics go beyond what is routinely available in the clinic for prognostication. While serial response assessment is currently performed using cross-sectional imaging, few patients experienced complete radiographic response to therapy, and CR can be rarely declared in the presence of bone or lymph node metastases. Molecular response assessment via mTF may provide complementary prognostic information, enabling more personalized discussions regarding prognosis, expectations, and future treatment planning. While our observations could support the potential value of a ‘treat-to-molecular-complete-response’ strategy, it is important to note that similar efforts targeting radiographic complete response in breast cancer, particularly with cytotoxic therapies, have not demonstrated consistent improvements in clinical outcomes19. Prospective trials of adaptive escalation or de-escalation strategies evaluating a biomarker-selected approach to test such approaches are required.

Our study was designed so that serial timepoints were collected on therapy with the goal of evaluating concordance between imaging and both initial molecular response and eventual progression. While not every restaging timepoint had a matched ctDNA assessment, there were 175 evaluable events, of which 110 were evaluable by RECIST v1.1. A key finding from this analysis was that patients with and without measurable disease by RECIST criteria have consistent and measurable responses as assessed through longitudinal ctDNA monitoring. While many clinical trials, particularly early phase studies, limit accrual to participants with radiographically measurable or evaluable disease, the development of a liquid biopsy-defined response could improve patient and biologic representation in clinical trials20.

Longitudinal analysis demonstrated that ctDNA monitoring had a high NPV and sensitivity for radiographic progression; improvements in PPV and specificity were seen with serial measurement of the mTF versus the maxVAF of the somatic variants in each sample. In evaluable participants, a mTF rise preceded radiographic progression, nominating this as a reliable marker of mPD. The sensitivity of this metric is consistent with observations from clinical trials evaluating single gene alterations and also provides a generalizable marker that is agnostic to specific tumor somatic variants or mode of acquired resistance21,22. It is notable that ctDNA clearance and imaging concordance of serial monitoring was improved by measuring mTF versus somatic maxVAF. This is likely attributable, in part, to the improved sensitivity and quantification provided by mTF, as well as a potential subset of CHIP variants being misclassified as somatic. Stable VAFs despite response to treatment and persistence at low levels of detection were suggestive of this phenomenon. Together, these underscore the superior specificity of tumor fraction quantification using the methylation based approach. A high NPV may provide opportunity for novel approaches to monitoring patient response to therapy, including blood-first monitoring, which could reduce the frequency of radiographic monitoring in participants with a defined molecular response. Confirmatory radiographic assessment early after treatment initiation will likely remain necessary given the fluctuation in mTF early on therapy.

Interestingly, when evaluating the mTF in longitudinal samples with low-level ctDNA detection, there was fluctuation in positivity (from undetectable to detectable mTF) at levels near the assay lower limit of detection (LoD95%: 0.005% for advanced breast cancer), in the absence of radiographic progression. The LoD for this assay has been previously established through analytical validation studies, including breast cancer-specific performance characteristics18,23. However, variability at levels near the LoD in this clinical dataset may reflect biological fluctuation, pre-analytical factors, or assay-related noise, and therefore warrants cautious interpretation. It remains unclear whether these low-level signals are related to the cyclical administration of CDK4/6i, other patient- or tumor-specific factors, or technical variability. Further characterization of these findings in both advanced- and early-stage disease will be important for future studies. Applying a definition of molecular progression incorporating both a percentage and absolute value threshold yielded considerable improvement in sensitivity for radiographic progression.

Finally, this study provided insight into the timing of emergence of acquired resistance alterations relative to molecular progression by mTF. In most cases, the rise in mTF occurred prior to the emergence of CDK4/6i resistance mutations, consistent with the increased sensitivity of the methylation-based component of the assay as compared to the sensitivity for any specific mutation, and the subclonal nature of many resistance mutations (i.e. ESR1). A median lead time from mTF rise to treatment discontinuation of 5.8 months may offer a longer window for clinical intervention over that of NGS-based mutation surveillance. It is notable that the frequency of sample collection and cadence of disease progression likely had considerable influence on this lead time and that prospective studies are needed to further explore ideal sampling frequency and timing in this setting. Nevertheless, these data are consistent with the results from PADA-1 and SERENA-6 (which only included patients who were stable after 6 months of AI + CDK4/6i) but highlight the benefits of mTF monitoring, which offers earlier, more precise detection across the entire population (beyond ESR1-specific surveillance), with subsequent use of a broad NGS guiding genotype-matched therapy for clinical trial design21,22. Future studies could test the impact of monitoring using mTF, potentially decreasing the frequency of imaging in those with an mTF-defined molecular response. This clinical approach could reduce the burden of time toxicity and scan-related anxiety, while also simultaneously reducing the demand for imaging infrastructure and broader health system resources.

Several additional approaches to tumor fraction quantification have been described, including alternative methylation-based platforms (both commercial and laboratory-developed), mutation-tracking strategies focused on recurrent SNVs, and sWGS-based methods such as ichorCNA. While these assays have demonstrated clinical validity in selected contexts, they differ in pre-analytical handling requirements, input, analytic sensitivity and dynamic range, and no prospective head-to-head comparisons have been performed to determine relative performance or clinical interchangeability across platforms. Future studies would benefit from this approach to nominate ideal candidates for future prospective validation; our results suggest that sensitivity in the range of the assay used here may be important.

Our study has several limitations. First, this is a relatively small cohort of participants, which may limit representation of the patient population and less common patterns of molecular response that might exist; larger retrospective analyses of later-stage clinical trials would provide additional insight into these findings. Next, many of the participants were recruited to this study during the COVID-19 pandemic where institutional policy moved towards decentralized blood collection and remote visits, resulting in missed blood sample collections. The estimated lead time from mTF rise to treatment discontinuation is likely influenced by the frequency and timing of blood collection and imaging in this real-world cohort and may differ under prospective surveillance schedules with standardized sampling intervals. In addition to larger studies, those with more regular blood collections are required to definitively evaluate associations observed. This is also a retrospective analysis performed on a single-center cohort, which could contain bias including study recruitment, clinical practice for treatment discontinuation, radiologist patterns of interpretation, and follow up frequency. Future studies would benefit from prospective multicenter cohorts to corroborate clinical utility, especially in the setting of potential changes to the standard of care including genotype-directed therapies. Furthermore, establishing the utility of blood-first monitoring requires well-designed, prospective trials to ensure the approach is safe and acceptable for patients before clear guidance for broad application could be developed.

In conclusion, longitudinal surveillance with mTF in patients with ER+/HER2− mBC provides important insight into treatment response and the development of resistance with improved association with long-term outcomes compared to tumor-agnostic mutation-based methods (maxVAF). mTF dynamics closely mirror radiographic response and resistance to ET + CDK4/6i with a molecular lead time to treatment discontinuation of nearly 6 months, often prior to resistance mutation detection by genotyping. These data support the evaluation of clinical utility of prospective mTF analysis as a tool to guide approaches for optimizing first-line therapy, timing of repeat genotyping, and personalized treatment in the setting of molecular progression prior to clinical progression.

Methods

Patient recruitment

Participants provided informed consent for serial blood collection at the time of their standard-of-care blood work, in accordance with the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS2) and approved by the University Health Network (UHN) Research Ethics Board (REB#: 23-5123). Samples were collected and banked under the Liquid Biopsy Evaluation and Repository Development at Princess Margaret (LIBERATE) protocol (NCT03702309). Patients diagnosed with ER+/HER2− mBC receiving primary treatment at Princess Margaret Cancer Centre were enrolled prospectively from 2018 onwards. Standard-of-care follow-up, therapy selection, dose modifications, frequency of radiographic assessments, and adverse event management were determined by treating physicians. Discontinuation of CDK4/6i was at the discretion of the treating physician, based on clinical or radiographic disease progression, treatment-related adverse events, and participant preference.

Cohort identification

All participants who consented to participate in the study and continued onto standard ET + CDK4/6i were included in this analysis. All available timepoints (n = 350) were sent for ctDNA sequencing including baseline, on treatment, and at progression.

Patient treatment and clinical follow up

Choice of CDK4/6i and endocrine therapy partner was determined by the treating medical oncologist in collaboration with each participant in accordance with the available local standard of care and the patients’ comorbidities, preferences, and other clinical factors. The frequency of visits, radiographic assessments, and treatment modifications were made by the treating medical oncologist and were abstracted from the electronic medical record. Routine use of serum tumor markers was not performed, aligned with the local standard of care.

Radiographic staging evaluation

Baseline and standard clinical follow up staging CT scans were performed and collected. Frequency of CT staging was determined by the treating physician. Results of ctDNA analyses were not available for treatment decision-making, given the retrospective nature of this study. Radiographic evaluation of response to therapy was recorded in two ways for all CT scans that occurred in the follow up period: 1) per clinical radiologist assessment and 2) per RECIST v1.1 criteria14. Radiologist assessment was summarized as improving (imp.), stable (stab.), or worsening (wors.). RECIST v1.1 responses were categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD)14.

Clinical specimens

Serial blood samples (Streck blood collection tubes) were collected prior to initiation of therapy and in follow-up associated with routine clinical visits, which were timed to be in close proximity to routine restaging radiographic assessments (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Additional samples were collected at C1D15 in most patients. Blood samples were drawn, double-spun, separated into plasma and buffy coat, aliquoted, and stored at –80 °C until analysis. Approximately 4 mL of plasma for each timepoint was provided to Guardant Health for analysis. The assay provider was blinded to clinical outcomes at the time of sequencing and analysis.

Clinical variables

All participants were required to have stage IV disease according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition prognostic staging for breast cancer. Sites of metastatic disease at baseline were identified through review of baseline radiographic staging assessments. In cases of recurrent breast cancer, prior treatments and the interval from initial diagnosis to recurrence were collected. Estrogen receptor and HER2 status were assessed using each participant’s clinical diagnostic biopsy (if available) or original surgical specimens in the case of recurrent disease, following ASCO/CAP guidelines as reported clinically by institutional pathologists24,25. The most recent specimen prior to study entry was used to assign receptor subtype status for each participant. Patient characteristics, including age at diagnosis of metastatic disease, sex, systemic therapies [(neo)adjuvant, palliative chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and/or endocrine therapy], endocrine therapy combination partners, specific CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib, abemaciclib), surgeries, and radiation treatments, were obtained through manual review of electronic health records.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from initiation of ET + CDK4/6i until the date of death. Time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) was defined as the time from initiation of ET + CDK4/6i to discontinuation of therapy for any cause. Patients without events were censored at their last follow up visit and their status was recorded. Treatment discontinuation and survival outcomes were last updated on October 31st, 2024.

ctDNA analysis for monitoring

Up to 30 ng of cfDNA, extracted from ~4 mL of plasma, was partitioned based on its methylation state, then repaired, ligated with custom molecular barcodes, and amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The libraries were enriched for a significant portion (~15 Mb) of the DNA methylome, covering over 20,000 epigenomic regions that are differentially methylated in cancer, along with methylation control regions18,23,26,27. The enriched libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Sequencing data were analyzed with Guardant Reveal using Guardant Health’s proprietary bioinformatics pipeline for ctDNA detection based on quantification on predefined epigenomic signals. The bioinformatics algorithm classified each sample as either ctDNA detected (positive) or not detected (negative) according to a predefined statistical likelihood threshold18,23,26,27. A tumor fraction (TF), based on methylation (referred to as mTF), was estimated by normalizing molecule counts in cancer-specific differentially methylated regions (DMRs) against matched control regions within each sample. mTF was calculated at each timepoint by averaging the cancer-specific DMRs with the highest scores.

The limit of detection (LoD = 0.005% in advanced breast cancer), limit of quantification (LoQ = 0.01%), and accuracy of the mTF were previously established in a separate set of breast, lung, colorectal, and other cancer samples and approved by CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments)26–28.

ctDNA analysis for genotyping

Up to 30 ng of cfDNA was extracted from ~4 mL of plasma, then repaired, ligated with custom molecular barcodes, and amplified using PCR. The enriched libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Genomic variants in over 700 genes were assessed from ctDNA using Guardant360 Liquid, including SNVs/indels, gene-level copy number data, rearrangements, and were generated using a proprietary bioinformatics pipeline (Guardant Health). The maximum variant allele fraction (maxVAF), representing the non-synonymous somatic variant with the highest copy number corrected variant allele fraction, was used as a surrogate for tumor fraction for the genotyping portion of the assay. Somatic, germline, and CHIP status were annotated by the laboratory and were used as such for all genomic analyses. CHIP annotation was performed using a proprietary bioinformatics algorithm assessing variant-level metrics related to fragmentomics, methylation patterns, and variant allele frequencies, as well as metadata features aggregated from over 250,000 clinical samples and public databases such as COSMIC and GnomAD29,30.

Defining metrics of ctDNA response and progression

Definitions of ctDNA response and progression, based on both mTF and maxVAF, were assessed in relation to radiographic outcomes. Molecular response was calculated as the percent change from baseline with response defined as a percent decrease from baseline; molecular progression was defined by a value above this with or without an absolute rise in mTF or maxVAF greater than 0.1% to account for stochastic changes seen at low levels (mTF 0.001% to 0.1%). ctDNA clearance was defined as the conversion of a previously detectable mTF or maxVAF to 0% while on treatment. Radiographic outcomes were categorized as true positive when a ctDNA rise corresponded with radiographic evidence of disease progression or enlargement, true negative when a ctDNA decrease below the predefined threshold corresponded with stable disease or radiographic response, false positive when a ctDNA rise occurred despite radiographic stability or response, and false negative when no ctDNA rise above the threshold was observed despite radiographic evidence of disease progression. The sensitivity (SN), specificity (SP), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and C-index were explored for each definition of ctDNA response, by mTF and maxVAF, in relation to the adjudicated radiographic change at each matched timepoint and were calculated in the usual fashion.

Statistical analysis

Predefined analyses included assessment of baseline rate of ctDNA detection (clinical sensitivity), evaluation of ctDNA detection through each participant’s clinical course, and exploratory characterization of the prognostic association with ctDNA detection and clinical outcomes. Concordance between concurrent matched liquid biopsy timepoints and radiographic assessments were considered if assessments performed within 30 days of each other. Definitions of ctDNA response and progression are outlined in the manuscript. These were not predefined and should be considered exploratory.

TTD and OS rates were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier product-limit method. Association between TTD and percent mTF decrease was assessed using a cox-proportional hazard model. Time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models were also constructed to assess the association between ctDNA clearance and clinical outcomes. Follow-up for each patient was structured using a start-stop interval approach based on serial blood collection timepoints, allowing for incorporation of covariates that change over time and minimizing the risk of immortal time bias. ctDNA clearance status was modeled as a time-dependent covariate, coded as 0 prior to first clearance and 1 thereafter. Events were assigned at the interval corresponding to treatment discontinuation (TTD) or death (OS). Models were fit with clustering by patient identifier to account for intra-patient correlation arising from repeated intervals. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated to quantify associations between ctDNA clearance and each clinical endpoint.

Lead time was calculated as the interval between the first sample with ctDNA detected and/or rising (after reaching a nadir) to the date of treatment discontinuation. Statistical analyses were performed using PRISM GraphPad (v9.0) and R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Oncoplots and swimmer plots were created using R-studio with coding assistance from ChatGPT (OpenAI), as well as cBioPortal. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided p-value < 0.05.

CDK4/6-inhibitor resistance alterations

A defined list of known endocrine therapy and CDK4/6i resistance mechanisms were curated based on the available literature. These include somatic alterations in: RB1 (copy number loss, loss of function mutations), ESR1 (gain of function mutations, fusions), PTEN (copy number loss, loss of function mutations), MET activation (SNV, indel, copy number amplification), EGFR (copy number amplification), CCNE1/2 (copy number amplification), MYC activating alterations, CDK4 (copy number amplification), CDK6 (copy number amplification), FAT1 loss of function (copy number loss, loss of function mutations), AKT1 (copy number gain, gain of function mutations), KRAS/HRAS/NRAS (copy number gain, gain of function mutations), FGFR1/2 (copy number gain, gain of function mutations, fusions), HER2 (copy number gain, gain of function mutations), AURKA (copy number amplification), and CDKN2A/B/C (copy number loss, loss of function mutations).
