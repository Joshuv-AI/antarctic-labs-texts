Associations of relative muscular strength with blood pressure changes and incident hypertension: a prospective cohort study in Korea
Authors: Jae Ho Park, Joong-Yeon Lim, Hyun-Young Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a chronic condition that increases the risk of incident cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality. This study investigates the association between relative handgrip strength (rHGS), blood pressure (BP) changes, and incident hypertension risk.MethodsData from 25,103 hypertension-free participants from nationwide Korean cohorts at baseline were analyzed. Participants were categorized into sex-specific rHGS tertiles (low, mid, and high). Multiple linear regression models assessed the rHGS associations with annual BP changes, whereas multiple Cox proportional hazard regression models estimated incident hypertension risk.ResultsOver 105,833.68 person-years of follow-up, 3,401 (13.55%) participants developed hypertension. Every 0.1-unit increase in rHGS was associated with an annual decrease of 0.04 mmHg in systolic BP (SBP) and 0.02 mmHg in diastolic BP (DBP) among women. Women with mid and high rHGS had a 15% and 23% lower risk of hypertension, respectively, than those with low rHGS. In men, every 0.1-unit increase in rHGS was associated with an annual decrease of 0.02 mmHg in SBP; however, no significant association was observed between rHGS levels and incident hypertension risk.ConclusionsHigher muscular strength may protect against hypertension, particularly in women. However, the observed annual SBP reduction with increasing rHGS in men and women suggests that higher muscular strength may improve BP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25091-z.

Background

Hypertension, or high blood pressure (BP), is a chronic condition that increases the risk of incident cardiovascular disease (CVD), CVD-specific and all-cause mortality [1, 2]. The World Health Organization reports that while hypertension prevalence among adults aged 30–79 years has declined in the European region from 45% to 37%, it has risen in the Western Pacific region from 24% to 28% over the last 3 decades [3]. In Korea, a Western Pacific country, hypertension prevalence among adults aged 65 years or older has increased from 49% to 59% in men and from 62% to 66% in women over the last 2 decades [4]. Furthermore, each 10 mmHg increase in systolic BP (SBP) is associated with a 49% and 44% higher risk of CVD and stroke, respectively, among adults without prior CVD or antihypertensive medication use [5]. These trends highlight the growing need for effective hypertension prevention and BP management strategies, even among normotensive individuals.

Regular aerobic-related physical activity (PA) is traditionally recommended for hypertension prevention and treatment. The European Society of Hypertension advises at least 150 min/week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise or 75 min/week of vigorous-intensity aerobic exercise to lower BP [6]. Resistance training (RT) is also recommended for additional health benefits, including improved muscular fitness, lipid profile, insulin sensitivity, and blood glucose regulation [7]. Interest in the antihypertensive effect of regular RT is growing, particularly following a recent meta-analysis reporting significant SBP and diastolic BP (DBP) reductions of 4.55 and 3.04 mmHg, respectively [8]. Starting in 2024, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) recommends incorporating low- to moderate-intensity dynamic or isometric RT (2–3 days/week) to improve BP and reduce CVD risk [9]. However, few longitudinal studies have examined the long-term effects of RT on incident hypertension risk beyond its BP-lowering effect. Although a study in an Australian cohort found that regular RT was associated with a significantly reduced risk of hypertension [10], a recent sex-disaggregated analysis in a Korean cohort reported that engaging in RT for ≥ 3 days/week for at least 6 months significantly reduced hypertension risk only in women [11].

Increased muscular strength, a major outcome of long-term RT, can be efficiently assessed using handgrip strength (HGS) testing in research and clinical settings [12, 13]. Although few studies have directly examined the association between regular RT and hypertension risk, several cross-sectional studies have investigated the relationships between HGS, BP status, and hypertension risk. Relative HGS (rHGS), defined as absolute HGS divided by body weight or body mass index (BMI), has been inversely associated with SBP and hypertension risk in both sexes [14–16]. However, another study reported that higher rHGS was significantly related to elevated DBP in both sexes and an increased risk of hypertension in men [17]. These conflicting findings indicate that the potential antihypertensive effects of higher muscular strength remain unclear. Moreover, given the cross-sectional nature of previous studies, further prospective studies are necessary to establish a causal relationship between muscular strength and incident hypertension. Although recent studies have reported an inverse association between HGS and incident hypertension risk in a European population [18, 19], future research is needed to determine potential differences among ethnicities and races. Given that a dose-response association was observed between SBP and CVD risk [5], further longitudinal studies are essential to examine the relationship between muscular strength levels and annual BP changes.

Therefore, this study examines the association between rHGS and incident hypertension in large nationwide Korean cohorts. Furthermore, it investigates the relationship between rHGS and annual changes in SBP and DBP.

Methods

Study participants

This study analyzed data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) conducted by the National Institute of Health, Korea. KoGES is a consortium project of six prospective Korean cohorts designed to investigate genetic and environmental factors contributing to non-communicable diseases, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cancer, and CVD [20]. Among the KoGES cohorts, baseline data from the Health Examinee (HEXA) cohort (2004–2013) were used, which included 173,195 urban residents aged 40–79 years, along with follow-up data (2012–2016). Additionally, data from the seventh wave (2013–2014) of the Ansan and Ansung cohort were included, comprising 5,906 participants aged 51–82 years residing in these areas. Since HGS was first measured in the seventh wave, this wave was designated as the baseline. Participants underwent biennial follow-ups until the eleventh wave (2021–2022). Face-to-face surveys and physical examinations were conducted by trained medical staff for all participants. Detailed information on these cohorts has been previously reported [20].

Of the 179,101 participants, individuals were excluded based on the following criteria: a clinical history of CVD or any cancer (n = 11,919), missing BP data (n = 31,261), missing HGS data (n = 87,103), missing covariate data (n = 1,183), or hypertension diagnosis at baseline (n = 22,532). After exclusions, 25,103 participants (17,403 women) remained for the final analysis (Fig. 1). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institute of Health, Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (Approval No. KDCA-2024-02-12-P-01).

Measurement of rHGS

By trained healthcare providers, HGS was measured using a digital grip strength dynamometer (T.K.K. 5401; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). During the measurement, participants stood upright with their feet hip-width apart, looking forward with fully extended elbows. The dynamometer was held by the testing hand in a neutral position (not flexed or extended), with the index finger at a 90° flexion. The participants squeezed the grip continuously, exerting maximum force for at least 3 s, with approximately 60-s intervals between trials. HGS was recorded as the mean of two trials per hand, and the highest value (in kg) from either hand was used as the absolute HGS. rHGS was calculated by dividing the absolute HGS (kg) by BMI (kg/m2), following previously described methods [15, 21]. Participants were categorized into sex-specific rHGS tertiles (low, mid, and high).

Definition of incident hypertension

Incident hypertension was defined as the first occurrence of hypertension during any follow-up period, based on the following criteria: SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported use of antihypertensive medication, or a physician’s diagnosis of hypertension. BP was measured by trained healthcare providers following standardized protocols. SBP and DBP were calculated as the average of two readings from the arm with the higher SBP after the participant had rested for 5 min in a seated position. For baseline comparisons, participants were categorized into two groups based on new-onset hypertension during the follow-up period: “non-hypertensive” (without hypertension) and “hypertensive” (with hypertension). Annual BP progression was defined as the difference between the last measured and baseline BP values, divided by the number of follow-up years (mmHg/year).

Covariates

The analysis included sociodemographic and health-related factors, including age, sex, educational level, drinking and smoking habits, total leisure-time PA (PA time), BMI, waist circumference (WC), BP, prehypertension, diabetes mellitus, and laboratory parameters. Educational level was categorized as elementary school or lower, middle or high school, or college or higher. Drinking and smoking habits were classified as “never,” “former,” or “current.” PA time was defined as the total time (min/week) spent engaging in moderate-intensity leisure-time PA in a typical week. Moderate-intensity leisure-time PA was defined as sports participation or exercise that induces sweating. RT regularity was defined as participation on at least 1 day per week. RT refers to any training program involving muscle contraction against external resistance, such as body weight, weight machines, barbells, or dumbbells.

Anthropometric measurements, including height, body weight, and WC, were taken by trained healthcare providers using standardized protocols. BMI was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared (kg/m2). After 8 h of overnight fasting, blood samples were collected for biochemical assays, measuring total cholesterol (T-Chol), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and creatinine levels. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the following formula, with creatinine expressed in mg/dL [22]: eGFR (mL/min per 1.73 m2) = 175 × (creatinine)–1.154 × (age)–0.203 × (0.742, if female). Prehypertension was defined as an SBP of 120–139 mmHg or a DBP of 80–89 mmHg without antihypertensive medication. Diabetes mellitus was identified based on a previous physician diagnosis, current use of antidiabetic medications, including insulin and oral hypoglycemic agents, FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, or glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%. Detailed information on the biochemical analyses has been previously reported [20].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, United States). Participants’ baseline characteristics are summarized using descriptive statistics, with continuous variables presented as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages (%). Chi-square tests assessed intergroup differences in educational level, drinking and smoking habits, RT regularity, and the prevalence of prehypertension and diabetes mellitus. Independent t-tests were conducted to compare age, PA time, HGS, rHGS, BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, T-Chol, HDL-C, TG, FBG, creatinine, and eGFR between groups.

Multiple Cox proportional hazard regression models were applied to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident hypertension. Multiple linear regression models were used to assess the association between rHGS and annual BP progression. Adjustments were made for age, sex, drinking and smoking habits, educational level, T-Chol, BP, eGFR, PA time, RT regularity, and diabetes mellitus in both analyses. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex were conducted to examine the association between higher rHGS and incident hypertension, considering age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), educational level (≤ middle school vs. ≥ high school), current drinking habits (yes vs. no), smoking status (never vs. ever), PA time level (< 150 vs. ≥ 150 min/week), prehypertension status (yes vs. no), and diabetes mellitus status (yes vs. no). The p-value for interaction was estimated to assess the consistency of the relationships across subgroups.

To assess the robustness of the findings, two sensitivity analyses were performed in which rHGS was recalculated as absolute HGS divided by body weight instead of BMI. First, HRs for new-onset hypertension were re-estimated according to sex-specific tertiles of body weight–adjusted rHGS. Second, associations between each 0.1-unit increment in body weight–adjusted rHGS and the annual change in BP for each sex were examined. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 25,103 participants (17,403 women) were included in this study. The mean follow-up period was 4.22 ± 1.25 years (range: 1.33–8.51 years) with 105,833.68 person-years of follow-up. During this period, 3,401 participants (13.55%) developed hypertension. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants. Men were older than women on average and had a higher prevalence of higher educational attainment (≥ college), current drinking and smoking, prehypertension, and diabetes mellitus. Men also exhibited significantly higher PA time, HGS, rHGS, BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, TG, FBG, and creatinine. In contrast, women had significantly higher T-Chol, HDL-C, and eGFR than men.

Table 3 presents the associations between rHGS levels and annual BP changes for each sex. Higher rHGS was associated with lower annual SBP progression in both sexes, even after adjusting for covariates. However, annual DBP progression was inversely associated with rHGS only in women. In women, each 0.1-unit increase in rHGS was associated with an annual reduction of 0.04 mmHg in SBP (p < 0.0001) and 0.02 mmHg in DBP (p < 0.05) after adjustment for covariates. In men, each 0.1-unit increase in rHGS was associated with an annual reduction of 0.02 mmHg in SBP after adjustment for covariates (p < 0.01).

Subgroup analyses were conducted separately for men and women to determine whether the association between higher rHGS and reduced hypertension risk remained consistent across different subgroups, including age, educational level, current drinking habits, smoking status, PA time, prehypertension status, and diabetes mellitus status. In men, the significance of the relationship between high rHGS and hypertension risk reduction differed in the two subgroups (Additional file 2). The protective effect of high rHGS was significant only in male participants who did not drink (p < 0.05) or who had no prehypertension (p < 0.05). A significant interaction was observed only with prehypertension status (p for interaction < 0.001). In women, the significance of the association between high rHGS and hypertension risk reduction also differed in some subgroups (Additional file 3). The protective effect of high rHGS against the development of hypertension was significant only in female participants aged < 65 years (p < 0.0001), those with a high school education or higher (p < 0.001), those who had never smoked (p < 0.001), and those without diabetes mellitus (p < 0.001). No significant interactions were observed across all subgroups except for prehypertension status (p for interaction < 0.001). However, high rHGS remained protective against hypertension in prehypertensive and non-prehypertensive women.

In the sensitivity analyses using body weight–adjusted rHGS instead of BMI-adjusted rHGS, the sex-specific patterns observed in Tables 2 and 3 remained consistent. Women with mid and high rHGS had an 11% (p < 0.05) and 25% (p < 0.0001) lower risk of hypertension, respectively, than those with low rHGS; however, no significant association was observed between rHGS and incident hypertension risk in men (Additional file 4). Every 0.1-unit increase in rHGS was associated with an annual decrease of 0.09 mmHg in SBP (p < 0.001) and 0.04 mmHg in DBP (p < 0.05) among women; however, the same increase was associated only with an annual decrease of 0.07 mmHg in SBP among men (p < 0.01) (Additional file 5).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that higher relative muscular strength may help prevent hypertension, particularly in women. In women, mid and high rHGS were associated with a 15% and 23% lower risk of incident hypertension, respectively, than in those with low rHGS. Additionally, each 0.1-unit increase in rHGS was associated with an annual reduction of 0.04 mmHg in SBP and 0.02 mmHg in DBP. In the normotensive subgroup without prehypertension, high rHGS provided a significant protective benefit against hypertension in both sexes. Furthermore, given the 0.02 mmHg reduction in SBP annually per 0.1-unit increase in rHGS in men, improving relative muscular strength appears beneficial for BP management in men and women. These findings support the recommendation to enhance muscular strength through exercise training, such as long-term RT, as a strategy to regulate BP and prevent hypertension.

In our study, the association between rHGS levels and BP changes was more significant for SBP in both sexes. Elevated SBP is a critical risk factor for CVD. Although each 10 mmHg increase in SBP is associated with a 49% higher CVD risk, a 30% higher ischemic stroke risk, and a 68% higher intracerebral hemorrhage risk, SBP increases linearly with age in both sexes [5, 23]. Hypertension onset at a younger age is also linked to a higher risk of incident CVD and all-cause mortality [24]. These findings highlight the growing need for BP management strategies, even in normotensive individuals. Regular aerobic exercise has traditionally been recommended for BP reduction [6]; however, the BP-lowering effects of RT and higher muscular strength, a major outcome of RT, remain debated. Previous cross-sectional studies in U.S. adults reported an inverse association between SBP, rHGS, and muscle quality, defined as absolute HGS divided by arm lean soft tissue mass [14, 25]. Similarly, a Korean cross-sectional study found that weak HGS was strongly associated with elevated pulse pressure, calculated as the difference between SBP and DBP [26]. However, another U.S. adult study found no association between rHGS and SBP in both sexes [17]. Given the cross-sectional nature of these previous studies, we investigated whether higher relative muscular strength was inversely associated with annual BP changes. Our findings suggest that rHGS is inversely associated with the annual SBP and DBP progression in women, and with SBP progression in men, even after adjusting for covariates, including total PA time. These results support the significant causal association between higher relative muscular strength and BP-lowering effects in the Korean population. As dynamic and isometric RT, the two primary forms of RT, have significant BP-lowering effects [8], regular participation is recommended to improve BP regulation and enhance muscular strength.

As of 2024, the ESC has recommended performing low- or moderate-intensity RT (2–3 days/week) to improve BP status [9]. However, few prospective studies have examined the associations between long-term RT or higher muscular strength and the risk of incident hypertension beyond their BP-lowering effect. Although recent cross-sectional studies have reported an inverse correlation between rHGS and hypertension risk in both sexes in Korean [15, 27] and Chinese populations [16], further prospective studies are needed to establish causality. Similarly, a recent study in Australia found that performing RT more than 1 day/week decreased the risk of incident hypertension by 19% [10]. Additional research is required to verify the potential differences across sex, ethnicity, and race. Our findings indicate that maintaining a higher rHGS reduces the risk of incident hypertension in women significantly but not in men, even after adjusting for covariates. This result aligns with those of previous studies. A Chinese cross-sectional study reported that a higher rHGS was significantly associated with a lower hypertension risk in women but not in men [28]. Similarly, a recent Korean longitudinal study found that incorporating the RT into a PA routine of individuals who met the current guidelines (≥ 150 min/week of moderate-intensity PA) further reduced incident hypertension risk by 35% in women but not in men [29]. Another recent Korean cross-sectional study reported that performing RT for ≥ 3 days/week for at least 6 months significantly lowered hypertension risk only in women but not in men [11]. A U.S. study also found that high relative whole-body muscular strength was not significantly associated with incident hypertension risk in males after adjusting for covariates [30]. In contrast, in both sexes, the risk of incident hypertension was inversely associated with absolute HGS in European populations [18, 19] and with rHGS in Chinese populations [31]. Given these differing findings, sex-based differences in the association between regular RT, muscular strength, and hypertension risk require further investigation. However, considering that no studies, including ours, have reported an increased hypertension risk from RT or higher muscular strength in either men or women, regular RT remains recommended to enhance musculoskeletal fitness and overall health in both sexes.

Recent meta-analytical evidence indicates that dynamic and isometric RT significantly reduce SBP regardless of baseline BP status, including normal BP, prehypertension, and hypertension [8]. However, to our knowledge, few prospective cohort studies have accounted for baseline BP status when investigating the association between long-term RT, higher muscular strength, and hypertension risk in initially non-hypertensive individuals. In the subgroup analysis of this study, high rHGS significantly reduced the risk of developing hypertension by 24% in normotensive men but not in prehypertensive men (Additional file 2). Similarly, in women, the preventive effect of high rHGS on the incidence of hypertension was greater in normotensive females than in those with prehypertension (28% vs. 15%), with a significant interaction observed (Additional file 3). Although no significant interaction was found in women, the antihypertensive effect of high rHGS remained significant in those younger than 65 years and those without diabetes mellitus. There is no direct evidence on whether this effect differs between premenopausal and postmenopausal women. The progression of menopause is strongly associated with an increased risk of hypertension [32], which may offset its preventive benefits. The age-specific protective effect in younger women may be attributed to estrogen’s cardioprotective mechanisms, including enhanced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, and reduced oxidative stress and vascular inflammation [33], which may interact synergistically with preserved muscle strength to amplify antihypertensive benefits. Therefore, to effectively reduce the risk of hypertension, men and women should maintain sufficient muscular strength through regular RT, beginning early in adulthood when BP is still within the normotensive range. Among men, the antihypertensive effect of high rHGS was observed only in those who did not drink. A recent dose–response meta-analysis reported that women exhibited no increase in the risk of hypertension with alcohol intake up to 12 g/day, whereas men showed a positive linear association across the entire exposure range, including levels below 12 g/day [34]. In men, alcohol-related hypertension risk is heightened by testosterone-driven activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and oxidative stress, whereas in women, estrogen confers cardiovascular protection [35]. Although limited studies have directly investigated the interaction between RT and alcohol consumption in relation to antihypertensive effects, even light drinking in men may counteract the potential benefits of regular RT. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that RT performed at moderate intensity (60–80% of one-repetition maximum), three times per week, for at least 10 weeks maximizes the BP-lowering effects [36]. Our findings and prior studies support the recommendation to maintain high muscular strength through regular RT for hypertension prevention and BP management.

Although the mechanisms linking high muscular strength through regular RT to BP regulation and incident hypertension risk remain unclear, vascular effects of RT have been proposed as a potential explanation. Recent studies suggest regular dynamic RT improves BP status by enhancing endothelial NO-mediated vasodilation, increasing limb vascular conductance, and reducing total peripheral resistance (TPR) [37–39]. Additionally, recent meta-analytical evidence indicates that long-term isometric RT significantly lowers TPR by improving autonomic vasomotor control without affecting cardiac output [40]. A sex-based difference in the antihypertensive effect of high rHGS was observed in this study. Increased NO production, inhibition of the RAAS and endothelin-1, and modulation of sympathetic activity are representative estrogen-mediated vascular protective mechanisms [41], which may underlie this sex-based difference. This difference may also be explained by the markedly greater muscular strength in men than in women, in absolute HGS and rHGS. Given that high-intensity RT is more effective at increasing muscular strength than low-intensity RT in young, well-trained individuals [42] and older adults [43], a higher proportion of men may have improved rHGS through high-intensity RT. However, since high-intensity RT and the concurrent Valsalva maneuver may increase central arterial stiffness by chronically elevating sympathetic activity and BP during RT [39], the antihypertensive effect of high muscular strength may have been less pronounced in men than in women. Given the lack of participants’ training intensity data in this observational study, further randomized controlled trials are needed to explore the potential mechanisms linking the vascular effects of dynamic or isometric RT, considering training intensity as a major factor influencing muscular fitness and BP regulation.

A key strength of this study is the use of large, nationwide cohorts representative of the general Korean population aged 40–81 years. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to examine the relationship between relative muscular strength, BP changes, and incident hypertension in the Korean population. These findings may provide further evidence for hypertension prevention, particularly in this population. Despite these strengths, some limitations should be noted. First, the sample included only Korean participants, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Second, although HGS was used as an indicator of muscular strength because of its reliability and ease of assessment, it may not fully reflect whole-body muscular strength. Third, although RT regularity was adjusted for, detailed training parameters, including frequency, intensity, and duration, were unavailable in the present cohorts; therefore, they could not be accounted for. Lastly, we could not account for other potential confounders, such as dietary habits and psychological stress.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that higher relative muscular strength may help prevent hypertension, particularly in women. High rHGS provided a significant protective benefit in normotensive individuals without prehypertension in both sexes. Additionally, as rHGS increased, the annual SBP progression reduced proportionally in men and women, indicating that higher muscular strength may contribute to BP improvement. Collectively, these findings support the promotion of regular RT to enhance muscular strength, optimize BP management, and reduce the risk of hypertension.
