Effects of Genistein on Inflammasome Induced Pancreatic β‐Cell Apoptosis by Dexamethasone
Authors: Nattinee Jitprawet, Jatuporn Sujjitjoon, Petcharee Maneethorn, Namoiy Semprasert, Suwattanee Kooptiwut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSteroid‐induced diabetes is a well‐known side effect of prolonged glucocorticoid (GC) treatment. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress has been identified as one of the mechanisms underlying GC‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis. Recent studies suggest a connection between ER stress and the NLRP3 inflammasome, which promotes cell apoptosis. Genistein, a phytoestrogen, has demonstrated protective effects against pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis induced by toxic agents. This study aims to investigate whether genistein protects against dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis by reducing ER stress and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This study demonstrates that dexamethasone induces pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis by increasing cPARP protein expression. Dexamethasone upregulates SERCA, IRE1‐α, and sXBP expressions, while increasing pJNK and Bax and decreasing Bcl‐2 protein expressions. Furthermore, dexamethasone upregulates TXNIP, a link protein between ER stress and the NLRP3 inflammasome, and increases NLRP3 and ASC, components of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to increase IL‐1β expressions. Co‐treatment of dexamethasone with genistein decreases cPARP expression, reduces SERCA, IRE1‐α, sXBP, pJNK, and Bax, and induces Bcl‐2 expressions. Co‐treatment significantly decreases TXNIP, NLRP3, ASC, and IL‐1β expressions. Similar results were observed in pancreatic islets for cPARP, NLRP3, and IL‐1β. This study demonstrates that genistein protects against dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis by decreasing ER stress and the NLRP3 inflammasome.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disease that causes high blood glucose levels. Incidences of diabetic patients have significantly increased every year worldwide [1]. This disease generates health concerns globally as high glucose levels can damage macro and micro blood vessels over time [2]. Genetic polymorphism, environmental and lifestyle factors, and drug use are all factors that can cause diabetes [3]. Common drug‐induced diabetes is caused by steroids [4], with the duration, potency, and total dose serving as predictive factors for steroid‐induced diabetes (SID) [5]. SID occurs in 20%–50% of patients receiving long‐term glucocorticoid therapy, depending on the dose and duration of treatment [6, 7]. SID not only worsens outcomes in patients with pre‐existing metabolic disease but also increases morbidity in individuals treated for conditions such as autoimmune diseases, cancer, and organ transplantation. At present, there are no targeted preventive strategies for SID, and standard antidiabetic regimens are often used empirically. Thus, there is a compelling need to explore agents that could protect β‐cell function or mitigate glucocorticoid‐induced metabolic stress.

The mechanisms of steroid‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis have been previously explored [4]. ER stress has been identified as a mechanism by which glucocorticoid (GC) drugs induce apoptosis in pancreatic β‐cells [8]. Linssen et al. (2011) demonstrates that prednisolone activates Inositol‐requiring transmembrane kinase/endoribonuclease 1 alpha (IRE‐1α), leading to the splicing of X‐box binding protein‐1 (sXBP1) [8]. sXBP1, downstream of the IRE‐1α signaling pathway, subsequently stimulates Jun N‐terminal kinases (JNK), thereby triggering pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis [9]. Another GC drug, dexamethasone (Dex), induces apoptosis in pancreatic β‐cells by upregulating the expression of thioredoxin‐interacting protein (TXNIP) in both mouse pancreatic beta cell lines (MIN6) and insulinoma (INS‐1) cell lines [10, 11]. Elevated TXNIP expressions activate NOD‐like receptor‐pyrin‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [12]. Activated NLRP3 recruits adaptor apoptosis‐associated speck‐like proteins (ASC), which contain a C‐terminal caspase‐recruitment domain (CARD) [13]. CARD then recruits procaspase‐1, which is cleaved to form caspase‐1 within the NLRP3 inflammasome [14]. Caspase‐1 then cleaves proinflammatory cytokines pro‐IL‐1β and pro‐IL‐18 into their active forms Interleukin 1 beta (IL‐1β) and Interleukin 18 (IL‐18), ultimately inducing cell death [15]. Whether dexamethasone induces pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis via ER stress‐induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation remains to be elucidated.

Genistein, an isoflavone recognized for its diverse health benefits, has shown promising therapeutic implications in the treatment of diabetes mellitus [16]. Studies conducted on both humans and animals have demonstrated its protective effects on pancreatic β‐cells. Human studies have shown that genistein supplementation leads to reduced fasting glucose levels, improved glucose tolerance, and ameliorated insulin resistance in postmenopausal women [17]. Similarly, animal studies have demonstrated that dietary supplements of genistein significantly lower fasting plasma glucose levels and elevate plasma insulin levels in rats [18]. These findings suggest a potential protective role for genistein in preserving pancreatic β‐cell functions in diabetic conditions [19]. Previous research has proposed that genistein enhances both the function and proliferation of pancreatic β‐cells, involving mechanisms such as insulin exocytosis induced by the activation of the cyclic adenosine 3′,5′‐monophosphate/ Protein kinase A (cAMP/PKA) pathway [20]. Notably, genistein has also demonstrated an ability to prevent cytokine production in high‐fat‐diet‐induced ovariectomized diabetic rats [21]. Thus, genistein might be a candidate substance for the prevention of dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis.

Together, it is possible that dexamethasone activates both ER stress and NLRP3 inflammasomes, inducing pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis. Genistein might protect against dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis. Using a combination of in vitro and ex vivo models, this study investigates this mechanistic niche, determining whether genistein offers protection against this condition by reducing ER stress and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This study might offer an alternative for reducing the incidences of hyperglycemia induced by high doses of steroids.

Materials and Methods

Animal Model

Male Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) outbred, aged 8–12‐week‐old, mice (28–35 g) were obtained from the National Laboratory Animal Center, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. The animal experimentation protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand (approval no: SI‐ACUP 001/2559). The entire experiment followed the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. All mice were acclimatized for 7 days prior to the initiation of experimental procedures. The mice were fed ad libitum and maintained in a 12‐h light/dark cycle environment at 25 ± 2°C and 60% humidity.

Mouse Pancreatic Islet Isolation and Culture

After sacrifice, pancreatic islets were isolated via collagenase digestion and histopaque gradient. Briefly, pancreases were infused with collagenase‐P (91%–98% purity, Roche, Switzerland) and digested at 37°C. Islets were separated using histopaque gradient (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) and hand‐picked under a stereomicroscope. Isolated islets were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 Medium supplemented with heated‐activated fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, and streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO2. The culture medium was changed every 2 days. A total of 300–500 islets were cultured with or without 0.1 µM dexamethasone in the presence or absence of 10 µM genistein for 7 days.

INS‐1 Cell Culture

INS‐1 cells were purchased from Merck Life Science (Darmstadt, Germany). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in humidified air containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was changed every 2 days. The cells were cultured in medium with or without 0.1 µM dexamethasone (≥99% purity, Sigma–Aldrich, USA), and in the presence or absence of 10 µM genistein (≥98% purity, Sigma–Aldrich, USA). All experiments performed in the study followed relevant guidelines and regulations.

Dosage Information

Genistein was used at 10 µM, a concentration previously shown to be non‐toxic and biologically active in pancreatic β‐cell lines such as INS‐1. At this dose, genistein enhances insulin secretion and protects against oxidative stress and apoptosis without impairing cell viability [20, 22]. Higher concentrations (>50 µM) have been associated with pro‐apoptotic and cytotoxic effects.

Dexamethasone was applied at 1 µM, a well‐established concentration for inducing β‐cell apoptosis and dysfunction in vitro through oxidative stress, ER stress, and suppression of survival pathways [23, 24]. Both doses were selected based on literature and preliminary viability testing to ensure relevance and safety for mechanistic investigation.

Analysis of Cell Apoptosis by Annexin V‐FITC/PI Staining

INS‐1 cells were briefly treated with 0.1 µM dexamethasone in the presence or absence of 10 µM genistein for 72 h. Then, cells were collected and stained with propidium iodine (PI), and cell apoptosis was detected using an FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The apoptotic cells were then analyzed using a FAC Sort Flow Cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Immunoblot Analysis

INS‐1 cells and mouse pancreases were lysed in a radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA buffer) (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA) and quantified for total protein using a Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce Biotechnology). Total protein extracts from mouse pancreatic islets and INS‐1 cells were subjected to electrophoresis on 8%–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) gels. Protein samples, prepared in both 4x reducing and non‐reducing dry formulations, underwent denaturation through incubation at 95°C for 5 min. Electrophoresis was carried out for 90–140 min to separate proteins based on molecular weight. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Subsequent to this transfer, membranes underwent blocking using either 5% non‐fat skimmed milk or a 3%–5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution in 1xTBST for one hour to prevent non‐specific binding. Probing of the membranes was conducted using a panel of primary antibodies, including anti‐cPARP anti‐SERCA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti‐IRE1α (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti‐XBP1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti‐p‐JNK (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti‐Bax (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti‐Bcl‐2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti‐TXNIP (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), anti‐NLRP3 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, UK), anti‐ASC (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), anti‐caspase1 (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), anti‐IL‐1β (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) and anti‐β actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA). After the primary antibody incubation, membranes underwent a rigorous triple wash with 1xTBST. Subsequently, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system. Quantification of band intensities was performed using Image J software, ensuring meticulous and quantitative analysis of the acquired Western blot data.

Immunofluorescent Staining and Confocal Microscopy

INS‐1 832/13, a rat insulinoma cell line, was seeded in a 6‐well plate containing sterile coverslips for 2 days. The cells were then treated with either dexamethasone or genistein, or with both, for 48 h. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 containing phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the cells were probed with primary antibodies against IL‐1β (1:50; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, UK), and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After thorough washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with a mixture containing Alexa488‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (1:1000; Invitrogen, California, USA) antibody in the presence of Hoechst33342 for nuclear staining (Invitrogen, California, USA). The slides were mounted with an anti‐fade medium (Invitrogen, California, USA), and images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 800, Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Statistical Analysis

Data were collected from at least three independent experiments. Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). The differences between the groups of results were determined by one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genistein Attenuates Dexamethasone‐Induced Pancreatic β‐cell Apoptosis

To investigate whether genistein protects against dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis, INS‐1 cells were treated with dexamethasone and/or genistein, and apoptosis was measured using Annexin V/PI assay. Dexamethasone significantly increased apoptosis, which was markedly reduced by co‐treatment with genistein (Figure 1A,B). Genistein alone did not have any effect on pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis.

To confirm these findings, cleaved PARP (cPARP), a key apoptotic marker, was analyzed. Dexamethasone elevated cPARP expressions, while co‐treatment with genistein significantly reduced it in both INS‐1 cells and mouse islets (Figure 1B,C). Genistein alone did not alter cPARP levels, indicating its protective effect occurs specifically under apoptotic stress.

Genistein Decreased Dexamethasone‐Induced ER Stress by Reducing the IRE‐1α Pathway

Dexamethasone has been reported to induce endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress via upregulation of IRE‐1α. To investigate whether genistein mitigates this effect, the expression of sarcoendoplasmic reticulum Ca2⁺ ATPase (SERCA) and IRE‐1α was assessed in INS‐1 cells under experimental conditions. Dexamethasone significantly down‐regulated SERCA and up‐regulated IRE‐1α expression, whereas co‐treatment with genistein restored SERCA levels and attenuated IRE‐1α upregulation in both INS‐1 cells and mouse pancreatic islets (Figures 1D and 2A,B). Genistein alone had no effect on either protein.

To confirm downstream activation of the IRE‐1α pathway, the expression of spliced X‐box binding protein 1 (sXBP1), a pro‐apoptotic mediator, was evaluated. Dexamethasone markedly increased sXBP1 expression, while co‐treatment with genistein reduced it. These findings indicate that genistein attenuates dexamethasone‐induced ER stress by modulating the IRE‐1α/sXBP1 signaling axis.

Genistein Decreased pJNK and Bax but Increased Bcl2

IRE‐1α is known to activate JNK, promoting apoptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction. To determine whether genistein attenuates dexamethasone‐induced JNK activation and mitochondrial apoptotic signaling, expressions of phosphorylated JNK (pJNK), Bax, and Bcl2 were evaluated by immunoblotting. Dexamethasone increased pJNK and Bax levels while reducing Bcl2 expression. Co‐treatment with genistein significantly reversed these effects (Figures 2D and 3A,B). Genistein alone had no impact on pJNK, Bax, or Bcl2 expressions relative to the control, indicating its effects are specific to stress‐induced conditions.

Genistein Suppressed Dexamethasone‐Induced TXNIP and NLRP3 Inflammasome

IRE‐1α activation is linked to NLRP3 inflammasome formation through upregulation of TXNIP, a key mediator [11]. To assess whether genistein attenuates dexamethasone‐induced activation of this pathway, expressions of TXNIP, NLRP3, and apoptosis‐associated speck‐like protein (ASC) were evaluated in INS‐1 cells and mouse pancreatic islets. Dexamethasone significantly increased the expression of all three proteins, while co‐treatment with genistein restored their levels to those observed under control conditions (Figures 3C,D and 4A–C). Genistein alone had no effect. These results indicate that genistein suppresses dexamethasone‐induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation by downregulating TXNIP and its downstream effectors.

Genistein Suppressed Dexamethasone‐Induced IL‐1β Expression

The NLRP3 inflammasome promotes caspase‐1 activation and subsequent IL‐1β secretion [25], which can induce apoptosis via IL‐1β receptor signaling. To assess whether genistein modulates this pathway, caspase‐1 and IL‐1β expressions were measured by immunoblotting. Dexamethasone significantly increased both proteins, while co‐treatment with genistein reduced their expressions to near control levels (Figures 4, 5). Genistein alone had no effect.

Immunofluorescence analysis of IL‐1β in INS‐1 cells further confirmed these findings. Dexamethasone increased intracellular cleaved IL‐1β staining, which was reversed by genistein co‐treatment (Figure 5B). These results suggest that genistein inhibits dexamethasone‐induced IL‐1β production by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that dexamethasone induces ER stress through the IRE‐1α pathway, leading to apoptosis. Our findings align with previous research indicating that dexamethasone increases ER stress in mouse osteoblasts [26]. Additionally, prednisolone induces pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis by stimulating IRE1α [27]. Upon activation, IRE1α mediates the splicing of unspliced X‐box binding protein‐1 (uXBP1) to produce sXBP1 [28, 29]. This sXBP1 induction leads to protein degradation and apoptosis [30]. IRS1 deficiency protects β‐cells against ER stress‐induced apoptosis by modulating sXBP‐1 stability and protein translation [30]. Moreover, previous studies have shown that IRE‐1α induces JNK phosphorylation, resulting in cell apoptosis via mitochondrial dysfunction [9]. Our results corroborate this concept, as dexamethasone increases pJNK and Bax protein expression but decreases Bcl2 expression. This is consistent with previous findings showing that ER stress activates JNK phosphorylation in mouse embryonic fibroblast cells [9].

Increased Bax promotes pore formation on mitochondrial membranes [31, 32]. After pore formation, cytochrome c is released from mitochondria [33, 34]. Cytosolic cytochrome c activates caspase 9, which then activates cleaved pro‐caspase 3 to cleave caspase 3 [35]. Activated caspase 3 then cleaves poly‐ADP‐ribose polymerase (PARP) to form cPARP [36, 37]. Therefore, cPARP is an apoptotic marker for cellular apoptosis [38]. This study shows that mouse pancreatic islets cultured with dexamethasone increase cPARP. This result indicates that dexamethasone induces pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis.

The present study demonstrates that dexamethasone not only activates IRE1α but also induces TXNIP protein expression in both INS‐1 and mouse pancreatic islets. Our findings are consistent with the conclusions of Lerner et al. (2011), stating that ER stress inducers up‐regulate TXNIP protein expression in pancreatic β‐cells of Akita mice [11]. Our study found similar evidence that dexamethasone promotes TXNIP and NLRP3 expressions in both INS‐1 and mouse pancreatic islets. This is supported by Lerner et al. (2011), finding that high glucose increases apoptosis by increasing the interaction between TXNIP and NLRP3 inflammasome [11]. Furthermore, it is supported by Tseng et al. (2016) showing that high glucose increases both TXNIP and NLRP3 inflammasome in INS‐1 and U937 monocyte [39].

Dixit et al. (2013) has been reported that NLRP3 recruits an adaptor protein, ASC, and assembles it as an NLRP3 inflammasome [40]. After NLRP3 inflammasome formation, procaspase1 is activated into a cleaved caspase 1 [41, 42] which induces pro‐interleukin (IL)‐1β to mature IL‐1β [43]. Our study supports this notion by demonstrating that dexamethasone up‐regulates NLRP3, ASC, caspase 1, and IL‐1 β. These results are also consistent with Szpigel (2018), showing that palmitate promotes NLRP3 inflammasome and IL‐1β secretion in THP‐1 monocyte cells [44]. Livers of high‐fat diet‐fed mice show upregulated s NLRP3 inflammasome and IL‐1β expression [15]. Therefore, the present study demonstrates that dexamethasone induces pancreatic β‐cells apoptosis through the NLRP3 inflammasome.

This study demonstrates that genistein rescues pancreatic β‐cells from apoptotic pathways induced by dexamethasone by attenuating ER stress. When combined with dexamethasone, genistein upregulates SERCA and Bcl2 but downregulates IRE‐1α, pJNK, and Bax. Several previous studies have shown that genistein has anti‐ER stress effects [45, 46]. Previous research also indicates that estrogen increases SERCA protein expression in glucotoxicity‐induced pancreatic β‐cell death [47]. Additionally, genistein downregulates GRP78 and IRE1α protein expression in mammary tumors of female Sprague‐Dawley rats [46]. Moreover, Park et al. (2016) demonstrates that genistein significantly reduces sXBP1 mRNA expression in high‐fat diet (HFD) mice and remarkably reduces pJNK mRNA expression in the brains of HFD‐fed mice [45]. Furthermore, genistein significantly protects against Bax protein expression in Tat‐induced neurotoxicity [48].

This study further examines whether genistein protects pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis against dexamethasone‐induced NLRP3 inflammasome. The results demonstrate that genistein, when administered with dexamethasone, reduces the expression of TXNIP, NLRP3, ASC, caspase1, cPARP, and IL‐1β. These findings are consistent with previous research. For instance, Wu et al. (2021) shows that genistein reduces H2O2‐induced TXNIP and NLRP3 mRNA and protein expressions in human umbilical vein endothelial cells [49]. Additionally, a study using the human monocytic cell line THP‐1 found that genistein decreases lipopolysaccharides (LPS)‐induced NLRP3 protein expressions in a dose‐dependent manner [50]. Furthermore, the present study also determines the effect of genistein on ASC expression, finding that genistein decreases LPS‐induced IL‐1β protein expression in a dose‐dependent manner in THP‐1 [51]. Genistein also inhibits IL‐1β‐stimulated NO production and enzyme activity in human chondrocytes [52]. Similarly, co‐treatment of genistein with LPS decreases IL‐1β and IL‐18 in RAW 264.7 cells [53]. All evidence supports the present study's conclusion that genistein protects against dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis by decreasing TXNIP, NLRP3 inflammasome, and proinflammatory cytokine expressions.

In conclusion, this study reveals that dexamethasone induces pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis through both ER stress and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways. However, when co‐cultured with genistein, dexamethasone‐induced ER stress and NLRP3 inflammasome activation are ameliorated, thereby protecting pancreatic β‐cells from apoptosis induced by dexamethasone (Figure 6). These findings suggest genistein may serve as a potential therapeutic agent against glucocorticoid‐induced β‐cell damage. Moreover, genistein has antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and insulinotropic properties, which serve as an adjunct therapy in patients undergoing long‐term glucocorticoid treatment. Its ability to preserve β‐cell viability and insulin secretion under dexamethasone‐induced stress suggests the potential for prophylactic use in high‐risk individuals. Genistein has shown good safety profiles in human studies related to metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes [54]. However, several limitations should be considered. The study has been limited to in vitro and ex vivo models, lacking in vivo validation and human islet data. Future research should focus on in vivo models, human β‐cell studies, and clinical trials to validate genistein's therapeutic potential and safety.

The possible mechanism of genistein‐mediated protection against dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis. Genistein decreases the activation of IRE‐1α, resulting in the reduction of sXBP1, JNK, TXNIP, NLRP3, and IL‐1β expression. Genistein inhibits dexamethasone‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis.
