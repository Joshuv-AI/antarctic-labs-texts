Association between high-risk drinking and cardiovascular health based on Life’s Essential 8: analysis using 2016–2021 Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data
Authors: Sookja Choi, Yunmi Kim, Jiyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAlcohol consumption above recommended limits has been associated with increased cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in observational studies. In particular, little is known about the association between high-risk drinking and cardiovascular health (CVH), as assessed by the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) health metrics, in the context of community-based population datasets. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the relationship between high-risk drinking and CVH status using data from the 2016–2021 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES).DesignCross-sectional secondary analysis study.SettingThe 2016–2021 KNHANES.ParticipantsThis analysis included 18 500 adults aged 19 years or older.Primary and secondary outcome measuresHigh-risk drinking was defined as consuming seven or more drinks (or five cans of beer) in one sitting for men, and five or more drinks (or three cans of beer) for women, at least once a month. The frequency of high-risk drinking was categorised as follows: ‘rarely or never’, ‘less than once per month’, ‘at least once per month’, ‘once a week’ or ‘nearly daily’. We calculated an LE8 score for each of eight composite metrics, with each metric ranging from 0 to 100. LE8 total scores were categorised as 0–49, 50–79 and 80–100, representing low, moderate and high CVH, respectively. We used weighted log-linear regression models to analyse the relationship between high-risk drinking and CVH.ResultsNearly daily or weekly high-risk drinking was reported by 37.3% of men and 14.7% of women. The mean CVH score of groups with more frequent high-risk drinking decreased linearly in both sexes. Using the ‘nearly daily’ drinking group as the reference group, the exponential coefficients (exp(B)) showed that the total CVH score increased progressively as the frequency of high-risk drinking decreased in both men and women. Compared with the ‘nearly daily’ group, the total CVH score was 5% higher in the ‘once a week’ group (exp(B)=1.05, p<0.001) and 14% higher in the ‘rarely or never’ group (exp(B)=1.14, p<0.001) for men. Similar trends were observed for female participants (once a week: exp(B)=1.04, p<0.001; rarely or never: exp(B)=1.13, p<0.001).ConclusionsThis study found that high-risk drinking was negatively associated with ideal CVH in Korean adults, and this association showed sex differences. Interventions targeting high-risk drinking may be more effective than focusing on overall alcohol consumption.

Introduction

Among various lifestyle factors contributing to cardiovascular disease (CVD), alcohol consumption has been extensively studied as a modifiable risk factor and is associated with multiple adverse cardiovascular outcomes.15 Meanwhile, WHO6 has identified alcohol abuse as a major public health concern, emphasising its role in premature morbidity and mortality.

Regardless of the evidence, it remains that the relationship between alcohol consumption and cardiovascular health (CVH) is rather convoluted. While heavy and binge drinking are associated with increased cardiovascular risks, several observational studies have reported a nonlinear, J-shaped association between alcohol intake and CVD, suggesting that light-to-moderate consumption may be linked to a lower risk of myocardial infarction and chronic CVD.713 This points to a lack of clarity, particularly in high-risk drinking populations, as to the extent to which alcohol consumption affects CVH through intermediate measures.14 Most existing studies have focused on either overall alcohol consumption or moderate drinking patterns, leaving a gap in our understanding of the specific cardiovascular implications of sustained high-risk drinking behaviours.81519

High-risk drinking, typically characterised by heavy alcohol intake and frequent high-risk drinking episodes, is associated with structural and functional changes in the cardiovascular system, including diastolic and systolic ventricular dysfunction, atrial dysfunction, chronic arrhythmias and coronary heart diseases (eg, angina and myocardial infarction).20 In younger populations, high-risk drinking has been identified as a significant predictor of future CVD risk.14

To better assess CVH, the American Heart Association (AHA) recently introduced the Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) in 2022, comprising the following eight component CVH metrics: diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health, body mass index (BMI), blood lipids, blood glucose and blood pressure.21 This indicator is an update of Life’s Simple 7, which was proposed by the AHA to monitor CVH,22 with the new indicator now including sleeping. Numerous epidemiological studies have shown that the newly introduced LE8 metric is associated with lower rates of cardiovascular events, reduced all-cause mortality and a reduced incidence of non-CVDs such as cancer.2326 However, limited research has examined how high-risk drinking is associated with these CVH metrics collectively, with most studies instead focusing on individual risk factors such as hypertension or cholesterol levels.14 27 28 Furthermore, previous studies have extensively examined the effects of alcohol consumption on CVD but have very scarcely focused on the association between high-risk drinking and intermediate CVH measures. In addition, most existing studies have been conducted in Western populations, particularly in the USA and Europe, which may limit the understanding of the unique sociocultural and behavioural patterns associated with drinking and CVH in the Korean population. Therefore, population-specific studies are required to further clarify the association between high-risk drinking and CVH indicators in the Korean context.

The roles of sex and age in alcohol-related CVH also remain underexplored. Over the past decade, drinking patterns by sex have shifted, with high-risk drinking rates increasing among women but remaining relatively stable among men.29 30 Despite the growing concerns regarding alcohol-related health risks in women, many studies investigating high-risk drinking and cardiovascular parameters have not adequately examined the potential interaction effects by sex.10 14 Given that alcohol metabolism and cardiovascular responses differ between men and women, further research is required to elucidate the sex-specific associations of high-risk drinking.

Moreover, although the LE8 provides a comprehensive framework for assessing CVH, the associations between high-risk drinking and individual LE8 components have not been thoroughly examined. Most studies using LE8 have evaluated either the overall LE8 composite score or examined individual components within the LE8 framework,31 32 rather than assessing how behavioural factors such as high-risk drinking may be jointly associated with multiple LE8 components. Identifying the LE8 factors most vulnerable to high-risk drinking will provide valuable insights for the development of targeted intervention strategies.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to examine the relationship between high-risk drinking and CVH using data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES). The LE8 framework was integrated with the aim of providing more comprehensive insights into the mechanisms through which high-risk drinking is associated with CVH. Specifically, this study aimed to (1) examine sex differences in the association between high-risk drinking and LE8 metrics, (2) explore how these associations vary across different age groups and (3) analyse the impact of high-risk drinking on each individual LE8 component. The hope was to afford invested stakeholders more nuanced evidence on alcohol-related CVH risk and help inform more effective public health interventions.

Methods

Data source and study population

We used data from the KNHANES conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), which provides objective and standardised national health data. KNHANES is a nationally representative, cross-sectional database consisting of three main components: a health interview, a medical examination and a nutrition survey. The survey assesses health status, health behaviours and disease conditions through direct physical examinations, clinical and laboratory tests and structured interviews.

KNHANES employs a multistage, stratified probability sampling design to generate nationally representative estimates of the Korean population. From 2016 to 2021, a total of 27 536 adults aged ≥19 years or older who had consumed alcohol within the past year were included. Among them, we excluded 9036 participants who had missing data on key variables (including CVH metrics or covariates). In total, 18 500 adults aged ≥19 years (9127 men and 9373 women) were included in the final analysis (figure 1).

Selection of study population.

Patient and public involvement

This research did not involve patients or the public in its design, implementation, reporting, or dissemination.

Variables

Definition of CVH

CVH was assessed based on the LE8 criteria established by the AHA. The CVH metric includes four health behaviours (physical activity, diet, nicotine exposure and sleep health) and four health factors (BMI, blood lipids, blood glucose and blood pressure).21 We calculated LE8 scores for each measure in the KNHANES data that had previously been published.33 34 Each LE8 metric is assigned a score ranging from 0 to 100; higher scores indicate better CVH status. The composite LE8 score is derived by averaging the individual scores across the eight components. Participants were categorised into three groups based on their LE8 score21: low (0–49), moderate (50–79) and high (≥ 80) CVH.

Dietary quality was assessed using the Health Eating Index (HEI) specific to the Korean population (KHEI). The KHEI is calculated using dietary recall data, 24-hour dietary recalls and food frequency questionnaires, all of which are based on KNHANES data.35 Although the KHEI is uniquely based on Korean dietary guidelines, its scoring methodology aligns with the US HEI framework, enabling meaningful evaluation of dietary quality. Previous studies have shown that KHEI and HEI-2015 scores demonstrate similar trends across sex and age groups within their respective populations.36 Physical activity levels were evaluated using the self-reported Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, which measures activity across occupational, leisure time and transport-related domains. Vigorous activities were given twice the weight of moderate activities. Nicotine exposure was determined using self-reported questionnaire regarding current and past smoking habits, including duration, electronic cigarette usage and exposure to indoor secondhand smoke at home. Sleep health was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire to determine average daily sleep time. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m2) and was categorised according to Asia-Pacific regional criteria.37 Blood lipid levels were classified according to non-high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, which was determined by subtracting HDL cholesterol from total cholesterol (measured in mg/dL). Blood glucose was assessed by measuring fasting blood glucose levels (in mg/dL) or HbA1c levels (in %) and by reporting diabetes history. Blood pressure levels were measured using a properly sized cuff. Information on the use of antihypertensive medication was collected through a self-administered questionnaire.

High-risk drinking

WHO defined high-risk alcohol consumption as an alcohol consumption of 61 g or more among men, or 41 g or more among women, on a given day.38 In this study, alcohol consumption levels were assessed using data obtained from a self-report questionnaire that included health and behavioural interviews. To minimise potential lifelong abstainer and sick-quitter biases, analyses were limited to current drinkers, defined as individuals who reported alcohol consumption within the past year. For the purposes of this research, high-risk drinking was defined as having consumed seven or more drinks (or five cans of beer) for men and five or more drinks (or three cans of beer) for women on at least one occasion during a month. This is based on the criteria of high-risk drinking suggested by the KDCA, which in turn is a modification of the standard of WHO to suit South Koreans, considering the average amount of alcohol consumed per person.3941 There were five response options for the frequency of high-risk drinking, as described herein: ‘rarely or never’, ‘less than once per month’, ‘at least once per month’, ‘once a week’ or ‘nearly daily’.

Covariates

The sociodemographic variables included in our analysis were age category in years (19–39, 40–64 or over 65), sex (men or women), residential area (urban or rural), educational attainment, income, marital status, occupation, living alone (yes or no) and women’s menopausal status. These variables were selected as potential confounders because previous studies have consistently shown their associations with both alcohol consumption and cardiovascular risk factors in the Korean adult population.22 42

Income was classified into quartiles according to the mean monthly household income, which was calculated by dividing household income by the square root of the number of people in the household. Educational attainment was categorised into three groups (middle or lower, high school, or university or higher), marital status was classified as ‘married’, ‘single’ or ‘others’ which included separated, widowed and divorced. Occupation was categorised as manual (agriculture, forestry, fishery, technician and labour workers), non-manual (professional, clerical, sales and service workers) and other (unemployed, including housewives and students). Menopause was classified by the health interview, with participants responding yes or no to having no menstruation during the past 12 months.

Statistical analysis

To obtain nationally representative estimates, all analyses accounted for the complex sampling design, including sample weights, clustering and stratification. The Rao-Scott χ2 test was used to compare baseline characteristics across high-risk drinking frequency categories for categorical variables, and the data were presented as frequencies and weighted percentages. Differences in mean CVH scores among the drinking groups were evaluated using analysis of variance, followed by post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons.

We employed weighted linear regression models to analyse the relationship between CVH scores and high-risk drinking status. For variables with a skewed distribution, the data were log-transformed to satisfy the normality assumption. We subsequently back-transformed all results using the exponential function to express the estimated effects as multiplicative changes in the geometric mean. We estimated SEs using the Taylor series linearisation method to ensure that population-based estimates were accurate.

All covariates were included as categorical variables. All analyses were performed using SAS (V.9.4; SAS Institute), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of <0.05.

For men, the proportion of daily high-risk drinking was higher among those who were middle aged, lived in rural areas, had lower education levels, had lower incomes, worked in manual jobs and were separated, widowed and divorced. The mean (SE) of the total LE8 scores was 64.7 (0.18). 13.1% of men were also more likely to have a poorer CVH status (table 1).

Female participants tended to show similar patterns of high-risk drinking to those of male participants, but the proportion of those who engaged in high-risk drinking daily was significantly higher, especially among younger participants, those who lived alone, those who had not had menopause and those who had a low CVH score. The mean (SE) for women on the total LE8 scores was 74.22 (0.17) (table 2).

Figure 2 shows the mean CVH scores for different categories of high-risk drinking, adjusted for age in men and for age and menopausal status in women. The mean CVH scores decreased linearly as the frequency of high-risk drinking increased for both sexes. In men, the differences in mean CVH scores across drinking categories were highly significant (F=94.84, p<0.001). Post hoc analysis revealed that the three groups with the lowest drinking frequencies did not differ significantly from each other but had significantly higher scores than the ‘once a week’ group. Furthermore, the ‘once a week’ group scored significantly higher than the ‘nearly daily’ group. Group differences were also statistically significant for women (F=54.69, p<0.001). The log-linear regression analysis revealed a significant inverse association between the frequency of high-risk drinking and the total CVH score in both sexes (table 3). Using the ‘nearly daily’ drinking group as the reference, the exponential coefficients (exp(B)) showed that the total CVH score increased progressively as the frequency of high-risk drinking decreased. Compared with the ‘nearly daily’ group, the total CVH score was 5% higher in the ‘once a week’ group (exp(B)=1.05, p<0.001) and was 14% higher in the ‘rarely or never’ group (exp(B)=1.14, p<0.001) for male participants. Similar results were observed. The ‘once a week’ group exhibited a 7% higher total score (exp(B)=1.07, p<0.001), while the ‘rarely or never’ group showed a 13% higher total CVH score (exp(B)=1.13, p<0.001) compared with the reference group.

Analysis of individual CVH metrics revealed that behavioural factors (diet, nicotine exposure and sleep health) were more strongly influenced by drinking frequency than biological factors (blood glucose and blood lipids) (table 4). A lower frequency of high-risk drinking was significantly associated with improved CVH scores across most categories, particularly with regard to nicotine exposure and diet. Compared with the reference group, men who rarely or never engaged in high-risk drinking exhibited a 238% higher nicotine exposure metric score (exp(B)=3.38, p<0.001) and a 68% higher diet score (exp(B)=1.68, p<0.001). Frequent high-risk drinking was also associated with lower sleep health scores (exp(B)=1.11, p<0.001 for the ‘at least once per month’ group). Notably, blood pressure metrics were significantly better in the ‘less than once per month’ group (exp(B)=1.34, p<0.001) than in the reference group. Among women, those in the ‘rarely or never’ group had a 314% higher nicotine exposure metric score (exp(B)=4.14, p<0.001) and a 114% higher diet score (exp(B)=2.14, p<0.001) than those who drank almost daily. There were also progressive associations between high-risk drinking and blood pressure and sleep health, while blood lipids and blood glucose metrics showed less pronounced or non-significant associations across most drinking categories in both sexes.

Discussion

This community-based population study aimed to examine the relationship between high-risk drinking and CVH using data from the KNHANES. In this study, a graded association was observed between the frequency of high-risk drinking and CVH; specifically, higher frequencies of high-risk drinking were associated with poorer LE8 profiles and a lower likelihood of achieving ideal CVH in both men and women. This result is consistent with findings from previous studies, which reported that high-risk drinking was negatively associated with heart disease.7 17 43 Existing scientific knowledge, such as the J-curve theory, suggests that moderate drinking can have a protective effect on the cardiovascular system.43 44 Regardless of this past evidence on the potential positive outcomes of drinking, this study focused on the risks associated with high-risk drinking, which has been more rarely explored, specifically demonstrating its negative health effects. Furthermore, since this study was conducted using data from the Korean population, it may be inferred that it reflects the characteristics of Korean drinking culture, where people drink for social interaction purposes and drink large amounts at once rather than drinking little by little.45 Thus, for the purposes of improving CVH in South Korea, interventions for dealing with high-risk drinking frequency may be more important than those for the simple act of drinking.

Among the individual CVH metric categories, our findings showed that, in both men and women, a higher frequency of high-risk drinking was associated with poorer adherence to a healthy diet. These results are consistent with evidence from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2000, which reported an inverse relationship between alcohol consumption patterns and diet quality.46 These findings suggest that high-risk drinking does not occur in isolation but rather tends to cluster with other unhealthy lifestyle behaviours, particularly poorer diet quality.47

In our sample, a sex-specific pattern was observed in the association between high-risk drinking frequency and physical activity. Among men, less frequent high-risk drinking (once a week and at least once per month) was significantly associated with higher odds of meeting physical activity criteria compared with nearly daily high-risk drinking, whereas no such association was found in women. A prospective cohort study reported that low levels of physical activity are related to the occurrence of alcohol use disorders.48 Given that these findings are derived from a cross-sectional analysis and show sex-specific patterns that differ from parts of the existing literature, future prospective studies are warranted to confirm these associations.

Ideal blood pressure and smoking status were negatively associated with high-risk drinking. In the United Kingdom, smoking prevalence appears to increase with alcohol consumption.49 Sleep health, assessed by daily sleep duration, was significantly associated with the frequency of high-risk drinking. This finding is consistent with evidence from longitudinal studies demonstrating a relationship between alcohol consumption and subsequent sleep quality.50 However, compared with smoking, the magnitude of the association between high-risk drinking frequency and sleep health was relatively modest, suggesting that sleep health may be less strongly associated with high-risk drinking than other behaviours such as smoking.

BMI was significantly associated with the frequency of high-risk drinking only when the differences in drinking frequency were substantial, and this pattern was observed in both men and women. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that BMI is more strongly related to the amount of alcohol consumed per occasion rather than drinking frequency alone.51

In the present study, the blood lipid component, assessed using high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, was not significantly associated with the frequency of high-risk drinking in either men or women. These findings differ from those of previous studies, which reported an association between binge drinking and total cholesterol in men but not in women.14 Furthermore, higher levels of alcohol consumption have been associated with increases in blood triglycerides and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol.28 This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in lipid markers examined and alcohol exposure metrics, as prior studies primarily focused on alcohol quantity or binge drinking episodes, whereas the present analysis evaluated drinking frequency and used HDL cholesterol as the sole indicator of blood lipids within the LE8 framework.

In the present study, no significant association was observed between the frequency of high-risk drinking and blood glucose levels. This finding differs from those of the Northern Swedish Cohort Study, which reported that binge drinking and total alcohol consumption from adolescence to mid-adulthood were associated with elevated fasting plasma glucose in women.52 This discrepancy may be explained by differences in study design and exposure assessment, as the Swedish study employed a longitudinal approach capturing cumulative alcohol exposure over several decades, whereas the present study assessed drinking frequency at a single time point. In addition, prior studies focused on alcohol quantity and binge drinking, which may exert more pronounced metabolic effects on glucose regulation than drinking frequency alone.

In the present study, the frequency of high-risk drinking was significantly associated with higher blood pressure in both men and women, with a notably stronger association observed in men. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence linking repeated heavy or binge drinking to elevations in blood pressure and other cardiovascular risk metrics. For example, analysis of the NHANES 2011–2014 data demonstrated that repeated binge drinking was associated with increased blood pressure levels among young adults.14 Systematic review and meta-analysis findings further indicate that alcohol consumption confers a greater risk of hypertension in men compared with women.27 Mechanistically, experimental and clinical studies suggest that alcohol-induced hypertension may result from sympathetic nervous system activation, endothelial dysfunction and alterations in vascular reactivity, which may be more pronounced with frequent heavy drinking.53 Taken together, these findings support the notion that high-risk drinking frequency contributes to elevated blood pressure, particularly in men, and underscore the importance of addressing alcohol behaviours in hypertension prevention efforts.

We analysed the relationship between high-risk drinking patterns and CVH according to sex to address the shortcomings of prior studies. Among men, the prevalence of high-risk drinking was highest in the 40–64-year age group and was particularly elevated among residents of rural areas, those with lower education or income, individuals in manual occupations and those who were separated, widowed or divorced, highlighting the need for tailored interventions targeting socially vulnerable populations. Among women, higher rates of high-risk drinking were observed in those who were younger, unmarried, living in rural areas, with a high school education (compared with middle school or college and above), belonging to the second-lowest income quartile (Q2), engaged in manual occupations, living alone or separated, widowed or divorced. In addition, lower CVH was associated with high-risk drinking, highlighting the need to strengthen health education and preventive policies targeting alcohol consumption among women. These findings highlight that in South Korea, men have a high overall high-risk drinking rate,42 whereas in women, high-risk drinking is more strongly associated with specific age group54 and reproductive health status.55 Women’s cardiovascular systems may be more sensitive to drinking than men’s owing to the influence of oestrogen56; therefore, future sex-based analyses and intervention strategies are needed.

This study has several strengths. Because the study used data from a large-scale, population-based survey (KNHANES), the findings are considered to have high representativeness and reliability. The comprehensive assessment of CVH using LE8 provides valuable insights, and the analysis of high-risk drinking by sex and individual CVH components offers specific evidence that can inform targeted interventions.

Future research should clarify the causal relationship between high-risk drinking and CVH through longitudinal studies and evaluate the effectiveness of alcohol intervention programmes by examining their impact on LE8 scores. In addition, in-depth qualitative research on high-risk drinking trends among women and younger age groups, as well as comparative studies across different cultural contexts, may further elucidate the relationship between LE8 and drinking behaviours.

This study also has several limitations. First, owing to its cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be inferred, and the observed associations may be subject to reverse causation. In addition, alcohol consumption was self-reported, which may have introduced recall bias, and some LE8 components (eg, dietary habits) lacked detailed assessment to confirm accuracy. Furthermore, the evaluation of multiple outcomes, particularly in sex-stratified analyses and subgroup analyses of individual LE8 components, increases the possibility that some statistically significant findings may reflect chance rather than true associations, and results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Although LE8 has recently been expanded to Life’s Crucial 9 with the inclusion of mental health, the present study focused on LE8 when examining its association with high-risk drinking, as depression-related variables in the KNHANES were assessed only in selected survey years (2016, 2018 and 2020) within the 2016–2021 cycle, precluding consistent analysis across all years. Consequently, the inability to incorporate mental health into the CVH assessment and examine its relationship with high-risk drinking should be considered an additional limitation. Future studies that more comprehensively integrate mental health may help further clarify its role in CVH.

Conclusions

In this community-based study of Korean adults, higher frequencies of high-risk drinking were associated with poorer CVH, as assessed by LE8, with sex-specific patterns observed. Men in middle age and socially vulnerable groups, and younger, unmarried or premenopausal women, were particularly affected. These findings highlight the importance of targeting high-risk drinking frequency, rather than alcohol consumption alone, in interventions to improve CVH. Given the cross-sectional design and self-reported data, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm causal relationships and evaluate the effectiveness of tailored prevention strategies.
