Massive Solitary Liver Metastasis From a Locally Recurrent Basal Cell Carcinoma Treated With Stereotactic Body Radiation Therapy: A Rare Manifestation of a Common Disease
Authors: Ian J Gerard, Marcus Butler, Erika A Martinez, Maxwell Sauder, Tae Kyoung Kim, Mame Daro Faye, Aisling M Glynn, Zaid Saeed Kamil, Laura A Dawson
Published: 2026

Introduction

Metastatic basal cell carcinoma (mBCC) is a rare evolution of BCC, occurring in only approximately 0.0028% to 0.55% of cases.1 The most frequent sites of spread in mBCC are the regional lymph nodes (53%), lung (33%), bone (20%), or liver (4%).1 Hematogenous spread is associated with significantly worse outcomes.2 Despite recent novel treatment options, the prognosis for mBCC remains poor with a median overall survival (OS) of 17.1 months.2 While there is limited evidence surrounding mBCC, some risk factors have been identified: diameter of primary lesion ≥4 cm, head/neck location, and a depth of invasion beyond fat.3 As with primary BCC, mBCC is often driven by mutations or loss of heterozygosity in the PTCH1 gene.3

Surgery remains the primary option in mBCC if technically feasible and medically reasonable. When systemic therapy is indicated, hedgehog pathway inhibitors (HHI) are the mainstay of treatment, with vismodegib and sonidegib having shown objective response rates (ORR) of 30% and 17.4% in the ERIVANCE4 and BOLT5 trials, respectively. When patients progress or cannot tolerate HHIs, immune checkpoint inhibitors, specifically cemiplimab, are often used as the next line of therapy (on-label in the USA, off-label in Canada), and have demonstrated an ORR of 21% in mBCC with durable responses extending beyond 1 year in most responders.6

Radiation therapy (RT) is often used in the management of mBCC with curative, adjuvant, and/or palliative intents and can provide excellent local control (LC), functional sparing, and symptom relief with a minimal disruption in quality of life.7 Given the rarity of mBCC, there have been no large-scale or randomized studies evaluating the efficacy of RT as a primary management modality. Stereotactic body RT (SBRT) uses highly conformal and high doses of photon RT in 3 to 5 fractions as a means for an ablative intent treatment. It is a common technique used in oligometastases in solid tumors, with well-described LC and outcome benefits.8 Unfortunately, there is limited evidence on the use of SBRT in primary or mBCC, and the rationale for its use is based on extrapolation from other solid tumor sites and is limited to several case reports and series.9,10 Adding to this extremely limited body of evidence, we report on mBCC following a local recurrence of a BCC of the neck with a massive solitary liver metastasis treated with liver SBRT.

Case Presentation

A previously fit 64-year-old man with a known diagnosis of a right-sided neck and shoulder 2 cm basaloid BCC treated with a wide local excision (WLE) followed by adjuvant RT for a positive margin in 2016 presented for evaluation. At that time, the patient had an abdominal ultrasound for an unrelated reason that demonstrated mild hepatomegaly without any focal hepatic lesions or liver disease. In June 2021, the patient had a biopsy-proven local recurrence that was treated with a WLE and right neck dissection of levels 1b, III, IV, and V. Pathology revealed a 3-cm basaloid BCC involving the dermis and subcutaneous adipose tissue with negative margins (0.8 cm deep and 1.5 cm peripheral) with 0/16 lymph nodes positive for carcinoma. The patient received adjuvant re-irradiation, 50 Gy in 20 fractions to the primary surgical bed in October 2021. As part of the workup for this local recurrence, prior to his WLE, he underwent staging with computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. This demonstrated a large left-sided liver lesion that was characterized as a hemangioma after evaluation with both ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

In 2023, the patient developed liver-associated abdominal pain. Repeat imaging demonstrated substantial growth of the liver lesion (+237%) from 10.8 × 6.3 × 8.4 cm3 (Fig. 1A) to 18.2 × 9.2 × 11.5 cm3 (Fig. 1B). The lesion had progressed into the caudate and right lobes, surrounding the gallbladder, and causing mass effect on the stomach. most consistent with an infiltrative neoplasm. The lesion was biopsied, and pathology showed BCC with basaloid features, consistent with his primary diagnosis. He began vismodegib in October 2023 with a modest transient response (−23%) to 15.0 × 8.6 × 11.5 cm3 (Fig. 1C), in February 2024, followed by progression (+44%) in June 2024, measuring 15.2 × 9.6 × 14.6 cm3 (Fig. 1D). A repeat biopsy reconfirmed BCC with basaloid features with molecular profiling revealing both PTCH1 and TP53 mutations.

After discussion at a multidisciplinary tumor board, the patient was referred to radiation oncology for an opinion regarding SBRT. Other local therapies, including surgery and interventional radiology locoregional therapies (transarterial chemoembolization [TACE], transarterial radioembolization [TARE], and radiofrequency ablation), were discussed but not preferred given the large tumor size and risks of toxicity and the desire to restart systemic therapy as quickly as possible.

His primary symptom was hepatic-associated liver pain, worse with inspiration, movement, and palpation. His past medical history was significant for chronic neck pain from his previous WLE neck surgeries. He had no history of underlying liver or cardiovascular disease and took no regular medication. Physical examination revealed a tender liver on deep palpation, with no stigmata of liver disease or ascites. Bloodwork revealed elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), grade 1 transaminitis, and platelets at the lower limit of normal without other abnormalities (Table 1), all stable over the prior year.

During SBRT planning, he could not tolerate abdominal compression or breath-hold immobilization due to hepatic pain. A 4D-CT was used to evaluate hepatic motion. The gross tumor volume (GTV) and the clinical target volume (CTV) were the visible liver tumor without an additional margin. On the baseline exhale planning CT scan, an internal target volume based on liver motion observed on 4DCT was created as an inferior and anterior expansion of 13 mm and 8 mm, respectively, and an isotropic 5 mm margin was used for the planning target volume. The prescribed dose was 27.5 Gy in 5 fractions, to be delivered every other day. More than 99% of the planning target volume received 27.5 Gy, with the tumor (GTV/CTV) receiving a Dmin of 29 Gy (Fig. 2). Clinical goals and normal tissue dose constraints were met (Table 2). His vismodegib was held 5 days before and after his treatment, and he received 1 mg dexamethasone for the first 2 days of SBRT—to reduce the risk of treatment-related edema—and an antiemetic 1-hour prior to each SBRT treatment to prevent nausea. A proton pump inhibitor was prescribed to reduce the risk of gastritis and ulcers for 3 months after SBRT. SBRT was tolerated with mild fatigue and nausea during treatment and the development of mild muscular abdominal wall pain.

Following SBRT, there was a radiological response with a reduction in size of the liver mass (−49%) to 13.2 × 9.7 × 8.5 cm3 (Fig. 1E) on MRI at 2 months and (−52%) 11.0 × 8.4 × 11.0 cm3 (Fig. 1F) on CT at 3 months. He remained relatively well with decreased hepatic pain and improved abdominal wall pain. His Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was 1, and he continued vesmodogib. At 5 and 6 months post-SBRT, there was a mixed response with radiologic regression of the liver lesion on MRI, now measuring 10.0 × 8.0 × 12.5 cm3 (Fig. 1G) (−8% from previous MRI), but further progression to 10.5 × 8.2 × 13.5 cm3 (Fig. 1H) on CT (+14% from previous CT), respectively. He had clinical resolution of his hepatic pain but continued abdominal wall discomfort. Hematologically, at 2 months post-SBRT, the patient had an initial substantial decrease in LDH and developed mild lymphopenia. By 6 months post-SBRT, he developed mild asymptomatic thrombocytopenia and anemia without the need for transfusions; however, the LDH began to rise once again (Table 1). Following this, his systemic therapy was changed to cempiblimab. At 8 months post-SBRT (1 month on cemiplimab), there was further radiologic progression on MRI to 12.7 × 8.4 × 13.5 cm3 (+19%, Fig. 1I).

At his last follow-up, 11 months post-SBRT (4 months on cemiplimab), repeat MRI showed stability of the lesion without further growth or new metastases and no hepatic or abdominal wall pain. He had an ECOG performance status of 2 with worsening chronic neck pain as his primary issue. Unfortunately, the patient recently requested medical aid in dying (MAID) due to his neck pain and has stopped all systemic therapy.

After review of all imaging by an expert hepatobiliary radiologist (TKK), the primary area of progression was along the right and superior peripheral portions of the mass, where radiation was limited due to organ at risk (OAR) dose concerns.

Discussion

This case highlights the complexity and aggressiveness of mBCC and the need for better therapeutic options for this uncommon and challenging situation. When considering local therapies for liver metastases, there are multiple therapeutic modalities, each with specific advantages and disadvantages. TARE enables selective delivery of high-dose internal radiation with yttrium-90 microspheres. It is particularly useful for multifocal or bilobar, liver-dominant disease, but requires invasive angiographic planning and is limited by hepatopulmonary shunting and underlying liver dysfunction. TACE exploits the arterial supply of hepatic tumors to deliver locoregional chemotherapy and ischemia, offering disease control in unresectable, liver-dominant metastases, although efficacy may be reduced in hypovascular tumors. TARE and TACE are generally not recommended for such large lesions. Radiofrequency ablation achieves excellent LC for very small (<3-5cm), well-localized lesions and is minimally invasive, but is limited by tumor size, proximity to vessels or bile ducts, and the heat-sink effect. For this patient, many of these treatment options were discussed, but ultimately, SBRT was recommended as the preferred locoregional therapy to balance the potential benefits and risks and to facilitate a short systemic therapy interruption.

While SBRT did not provide a complete response, it can play a role in the management of patients with liver metastases when systemic therapy or other local therapies are not suitable, especially for liver metastases that are smaller, when higher doses may be delivered safely. In this patient, SBRT led to an initial partial radiologic response with improvement in hepatic pain and a decrease in LDH. The patient experienced abdominal wall pain following treatment which was possibly secondary to toxicity from SBRT, as his tumor-related abdominal pain had improved. Unfortunately, the tumor response was only sustained for 5 months before radiologic progression, but with a change in systemic therapy, the growth had again stabilized before the patient requested MAID.

This case was particularly challenging due to the large size of the liver metastasis abutting the stomach that limited the dose of SBRT that could be safely delivered. SBRT was favored over a conventional or moderately hypofractionated approach for several reasons: (1) desire to restart systemic therapy as quickly as possible; (2) desire to escalate the dose within the target (GTV) while having a sharp dose gradient outside the target near the organs at risk (specifically the stomach and the healthy liver); and (3) patient factors including preference for minimal hospital visits due to socioeconomic challenges. He was not eligible for adaptive RT (ART) due to claustrophobia and difficulties lying flat (for MR-based ART) and unsuitability for breath-hold motion management (a requirement for CT-based ART at our institution at this time).

Extrapolating from prior reports and studies using SBRT for liver metastases, SBRT doses with a biologically effective dose (BED10) of at least 75 Gy are associated with improved LC, and the dose-response relationship increases significantly when able to increase the BED10 to > 80 Gy and >100 Gy, especially in radioresistant malignancies.11 In this case, the BED10 of 27.5 Gy in 5 fractions comes to approximately 43 Gy, so it is not surprising that LC was not durable. Simultaneously, it is encouraging that such a large BCC metastasis, a histology rarely treated with SBRT, partially responded.

Hepatic metastases from mBCC are extremely rare, accounting for only 1% to 4% of all mBCC reported cases or approximately 0.00003% of all BCCs.1 In 2013, a review of all case reports on the management of mBCC between 1981 and 2011 found only 5 cases of liver-specific metastases.12 Among reports of the management of mBCC with liver metastases, treatment is often poorly described as “palliative local therapy”13 or “radiotherapy and chemotherapy,”14 chemotherapy management alone,15,16 or no treatment.17 Furthermore, due to its rarity, identifying this disease on imaging poses a major challenge to radiologists, as it can grow slowly and exhibit features of other benign processes.18 This phenomenon was also seen in this case, as the lesion was initially favored to be a large hemangioma rather than mBCC before it reached a point of accelerated growth and invasion into peripheral hepatic structures which delayed intervention.

SBRT is not currently being studied in any clinical trials for mBCC; however, the idea of high-dose/high–dose-rate RT is being evaluated in a phase 2 clinical trial for primary BCC using FLASH-RT, an RT delivery technique that delivers high doses of radiation in ultra-short time frames, typically under 200 ms. In this clinical trial, participants are randomized to receive either FLASH-RT of 22 Gy in 1 fraction (for small lesions) or 30 Gy in 5 fractions (for larger lesions) or standard external beam radiation therapy.19 This study will provide important safety and efficacy data for BCC and high-dose RT. Other technological advances also have the potential to improve the therapeutic ratio for such patients, including ART and spatially fractionated RT. Due to the rarity of mBCC, management of these patients is challenging due to the limited quality of evidence and availability of therapies, and multidisciplinary decision-making is recommended.

Outside of HHIs and cemiplimab, systemic therapy options are sparse and often limited to clinical trials. Limited work in a small phase 2 basket trial evaluating 16 patients with mBCC found that dual anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 blockade (ipilimumab and nivolumab) provided an ORR of 31% (95% CI, 19%-50%) with the 12-month OS of 75% (95% CI, 57%-100%) and median progression-free survival of 9.3 months (95% CI, 3.3–NA).20 Similar to many spheres of oncology, immunotherapy appears to show promise as an important tool in the management of mBCC in the future; combining immunotherapy with SBRT for mBCC may be synergistic and should be explored, as even in this case, after starting cemiplimab, the treated liver lesion stabilized and may suggest a synergistic effect. Given the rarity of this presentation, it is likely that personalized treatment decision-making will be required, perhaps extrapolating from other cancers with similar genomics or phenotypes.

Conclusion

We present a challenging case of liver SBRT for mBCC that resulted in a partial response for several months with subjective improvement in hepatic pain and a decrease in the size of the hepatic metastasis before progression and eventual stabilization following a change in systemic therapy to cemiplimab. The lack of initial sustained LC was likely due to the inability to deliver higher SBRT doses due to the massive volume of the metastasis and proximity to critical organs. Liver metastases from mBCC are an exceedingly rare presentation with a sparsity of reported local and systemic treatment strategies with further need for description in the literature. Liver SBRT is a safe therapy that may be considered in these patients, especially when systemic therapy is not feasible or is associated with a high risk of toxicity.
