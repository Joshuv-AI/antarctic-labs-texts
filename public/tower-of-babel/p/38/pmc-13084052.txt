Drug target mining and in silico screening of Tibetan plant metabolites for potential alleviation of Oroya fever, a neglected tropical disease
Authors: Zarrin Basharat, Afsheen Raza, Hanan A Ogaly, Fatimah A M Al-Zahrani, Ufaq Saleem, Calvin R Wei, Ibrar Ahmed, Madiha Islam, Sara Jadoon, Seil Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBartonella bacilliformis is the causative agent of a neglected tropical disease, the Oroya fever or Carrion’s disease. Current treatment options for B. bacilliformis infections are limited, and the emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic interventions. This study aimed to identify novel inhibitors from Tibetan medicinal plant metabolites against B. bacilliformis, a causative agent of Oroya fever, utilizing a multi-pronged computational approach. We employed pan-genomics and hierarchal subtractive proteomics approach to filter drug targets and then predicted binding affinities of compounds with riboflavin synthase target using AutoDock Vina, DiffDock, and GNINA. The stability of the top inhibitors was confirmed through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations in GROMACS, while MM/GBSA and ADMET profiling assessed pharmacokinetic and toxicity properties. The analysis identified Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside, Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside, and Hirtusneanoside as promising inhibitors. MD simulations demonstrated stable binding with RMSD within 3 Å, and the candidates showed no skin sensitization, AMES mutagenicity, or hepatotoxicity. Physiologically-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling estimated compound distribution within the human body and revealed slightly higher absorption in pregnant and steatosis conditions, but overall absorption rates were similar across scenarios. Cyclodextrin complexation, particularly with SBE-β-CD indicates potential for enhanced solubility of these ligands. Since Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside exhibited off-target effects, the Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside and Hirtusneanoside are recommended for further investigation. Introduction

Carrion’s disease or Oroya fever is caused by Bartonella bacilliformis and is considered a neglected tropical disease1. It primarily affects populations living in certain regions of South America, particularly in Peru, Ecuador, and Colombia but cases are also reported globally2. To date, almost 30 Bartonella spp. have been discovered, with most of them being zoonotic3. Among these species, three are particularly well known for causing severe infections in humans. One of them, Bartonella henselae, the causative agent of cat-scratch disease, typically leads to mild lymphadenopathy and fever following a cat bite4. Bartonella quintana, transmitted by lice, is responsible for trench fever, often referred to as five-day fever, while B. bacilliformis is the causative agent of Carrion’s disease5. Female phlebotomine sand flies, primarily Lutzomyia verrucarum, serve as the vectors responsible for transmitting the disease6. The disease has a biphasic nature: the acute phase (Oroya fever), often affecting children, presents with non-specific symptoms such as anemia, fever, jaundice, malaise, headache, and anorexia7,8. It is characterized by the bacterial invasion of erythrocytes, leading to massive hemolysis and severe anemia9. The pathogen adheres to and penetrates red blood cells using surface adhesins, where it multiplies intracellularly and disrupts membrane integrity. This results in impaired oxygen transport, immunosuppression, and increased susceptibility to secondary infections like salmonellosis, contributing to the high mortality (40–90%) observed in untreated cases10. Prompt antibiotic therapy curtails progression to chronic verruga peruana (vascular skin disease) stage but without timely treatment, progression between phases may result in life-threatening complications such as cardiopulmonary failure11,12.

Although ciprofloxacin, rifampicin, and erythromycin have been used with success against this infection, reports of treatment failures and relapses have emerged13. Antibiotic-resistant strains of B. bacilliformis, with mutations in DNA gyrase, rpoB, and 23S rRNA have been reported13,14. These findings suggest that there is a potential risk of developing antibiotic-resistant strains of B. bacilliformis in clinical settings15 and underscores the urgent need to explore novel therapeutic scaffolds. In this context, several essential oils and phytocompounds, including cinnamaldehyde (cinnamon bark), carvacrol (oregano), and eugenol (clove bud), have demonstrated potent activity against Bartonella spp. at very low Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) values, highlighting their promise as alternative or adjunctive therapeutic candidates16,17. There is currently no published evidence directly evaluating phytocompounds, plant-derived oils, or botanical extracts specifically against B. bacilliformis. Owing to its strict intracellular lifestyle and fastidious growth requirements, studies exploring natural product activity against this species remain very limited. Traditional Tibetan medicine (TTM) offers a unique repertoire of medicinal plants with long-standing ethnopharmacological use against infectious and inflammatory conditions18. These phytochemicals represent an underexplored resource for novel drug discovery against B. bacilliformis.

To accelerate this search, computer-aided drug design (CADD) offers a systematic and swift approach to drug discovery, by integrating molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) predictions to identify and validate potential inhibitors19–21. Using in silico tools, we can also determine off-target prediction of the compounds22, which can shed light on compound mechanism of action23. This enables the prioritization of natural compounds with both strong binding affinity and favorable pharmacokinetic profiles24.

In this context, the present study is the first to integrate ethnopharmacological knowledge from TTM with a comprehensive in silico pipeline to identify potential inhibitors of B. bacilliformis riboflavin synthase. By combining molecular docking, MD simulations, ADMET profiling, and off-target prediction, this work advances beyond prior in silico studies by prioritizing TTM-derived natural scaffolds with both pathogen-specific binding stability and favorable pharmacokinetic characteristics. Although riboflavin synthase has been explored as a drug target in several bacterial pathogens using in silico approaches, existing studies have largely focused on synthetic inhibitors or drug-repurposing libraries and have rarely addressed Bartonella species. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no computational or experimental studies have systematically investigated plant derived phytochemicals against B. bacilliformis, particularly those informed by traditional medicinal systems. This represents a significant gap, given the unique intracellular lifestyle of the pathogen and the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies for Carrion’s disease. The systematic approach adapted in this study provides new mechanistic insights and establishes a computational framework for natural-product-based lead discovery against B. bacilliformis.

Material and methods

Core proteome extraction

B. bacilliformis proteomes (n = 17), representing a diverse set of strains, were retrieved from the BV_BRC database (https://www.bv-brc.org/view/Taxonomy/774#view_tab=phylogeny; retrieved 10 February 2024)25. These proteomes were used to extract the conserved or core protein elements across the species. The core proteome, representing the set of genes present in all examined strains, provides valuable insights into the fundamental genetic makeup essential for the species survival and adaptation. The Bacterial Pan Genome Analysis (BPGA) 26 was employed to identify and extract the core proteins shared among the various strains. Clustering of protein sequences based on sequence similarity and orthology was done with an application of a 70% similarity threshold, which ensured the inclusion of only highly conserved genes in the core genome27. The resulting core genome dataset served as the foundation for downstream analyses, including phylogenetic reconstruction and functional annotation, providing a framework for understanding the evolutionary relationships and adaptive strategies of different B. bacilliformis strains.

Drug target identification

Implementing a subtractive proteomics approach, filtering of proteins to identify potential therapeutic targets specific to the pathogen was implemented. The core proteins were subjected to CD-HIT28, a sequence clustering tool, to reduce redundancy within the dataset. Sequences with high similarity were grouped, thereby simplifying subsequent analyses. The refined proteome was then cross-referenced with the Database of Essential Genes (DEG)29 to pinpoint essential genes vital for the survival and growth of B. bacilliformis. Targeting essential genes increases the likelihood of developing effective drugs. Further refinement involved comparing the essential genes identified with the Core Essential Genes (CEG) database30, emphasizing those commonly essential across various bacterial species. This step ensures that the selected targets are not only vital for B. bacilliformis but are also potential pan-bacterial drug targets. To mitigate the risk of unintended effects on the human microbiome, the remaining protein candidates were compared against databases representing the human gut flora (n = 84) according to previously described parameters31. Proteins with significant similarities were excluded, minimizing the impact on the symbiotic gut microbial community. Finally, the candidate proteins underwent comparison with the human proteome database. Proteins with similarities to human proteins were excluded to ensure specificity in targeting B. bacilliformis without affecting host cellular functions. The left over sequences were mapped to the DrugBank v532and homologs with hits in the database were considered as therapeutic targets (Supplementary Table S1). These were further prioritized using SPIDER, a stacking-based ensemble learning framework for accurate prediction of druggable proteins (http://pmlabstack.pythonanywhere.com/SPIDER)33. Candidates with positive class assignment and probability > 0.95 were selected for high-confidence essentiality. These were subjected to subcellular localization prediction via CELLO34, prioritizing cytoplasmic proteins for improved small-molecule accessibility over inner membrane or periplasmic ones. Physicochemical properties, including theoretical pI, molecular weight, instability index, and aliphatic index were calculated using Expasy ProtParam tool35. Aliphatic index measures relative volume occupied by aliphatic side chains (Ala, Val, Ile, Leu), where higher values indicate greater rigidity and heat tolerance36.

Molecular docking analysis

Total 52 TTM plant metabolites (Sowa rigpa compounds) were identified from the documented flora, including Anaphalis contorta, Juniperus squamata, Rumex nepalensis, Bergenia purpurascens, Gentiana algida, Hypecoum leptocarpum, Meconopsis horridula, Meconopsis paniculata, Usnea longissima, Usnea articulata, and Usnea hirta, as reported in existing literature37–40 (Supplementary Table S2). The 3D structures of these compounds were obtained from PubChem and subsequently downloaded as SDF files. The 3D structure of selected target (Riboflavin synthase) was downloaded from AlphaFold database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/Q9REI0)41. The predicted model exhibited a high confidence level, with an average predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score of 95.75, indicating excellent structural reliability. Swiss-Model Assessment (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/assess/) was employed to evaluate the stereochemical quality of the structure, including Ramachandran plot statistics.

Structural docking was carried out for the Sowa rigpa compounds through PyRx (https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/), utilizing the AutoDock interface42. To facilitate virtual screening, these structures were then prepared in PDBQT format, ensuring compatibility with AutoDock tools. Ligands, represented as small molecules and stored in PDB or SDF formats, were also imported. Following this, a grid box was defined around the active site of the target protein, configuring grid parameters such as dimensions and spacing for subsequent docking simulations. Parameters were: exhaustiveness = 8; center_x = 0.2429; center_y = -0.985009391747; center_z = 0.288906392051; size_x = 25.0; size_y = 29.1038751174; size_z = 27.9108709024. After docking, the ligand–protein binding poses and affinities were analyzed in PyRx, while structural visualizations were performed using PyMOL43, PLIP44 and Maestro (Schrödinger LLC). Subsequently, docking scores were evaluated to prioritize ligands based on predicted binding affinities. Potential hit compounds were also assessed by DiffDock to assess binding affinities45. The DiffDock diffusion model refines the positioning and orientation of the small molecule around the target protein. The resulting binding poses are then passed to a confidence model, which evaluates and ranks each pose. To facilitate comparisons across different ligands, the binding poses were scored using GNINA46, which incorporates both convolutional neural network (CNN) modules and the Vinardo score function47. The top three docking poses for each ligand in DiffDock were scored with the GNINA model and ranked according to the Vinardo score. The CNN scores and confidence metrics were compared with the binding scores obtained from AutoDock to assess consistency and predictive accuracy of the docking.

Off-target analysis of compounds

Macromolecular targets of the best scoring molecules in humans were validated using SWISS target prediction tool (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/; retrieved 18 February 2024)48. Using a ligand-based approach, this tool leverages the 2D and 3D similarity between a query molecule and the known ligands of a diverse array of protein targets. This method facilitates the rationalization of both favorable and unfavorable side effects, as well as the prediction of off-target interactions, ensuring feasibility of the compound in humans.

ADMET

Top scoring compounds were subjected to ADMET prediction using pkCSM49 and ADMET Predictor v10.4.0.050. The pkCSM is a web-based tool that employs machine-learning models to predict ADMET properties, while ADMET Predictor utilizes the Extended Clearance Classification System (ECCS) to predict clearance mechanisms51. The ECCS framework classifies compounds into categories based on permeability, ionization, and molecular weight, distinguishing whether clearance is primarily through metabolism, renal excretion, or transporter-mediated uptake. The S + Mechanistic Clearance Classification further refines this by identifying the rate-determining processes in clearance, improving predictions of compound disposition in the body52.

Cyclodextrin compelxation

To improve oral bioavailability of compounds with suboptimal absorption, FormulationAI (https://formulationai.computpharm.org/; accessed March 2025) was used to assess cyclodextrin complexation with eight cyclodextrins, including Dimethyl-β-cyclodextrin (DMCD), Gamma-Cyclodextrin (γ-CD), Alpha-Cyclodextrin (α-CD), Beta-Cyclodextrin (β-CD), Trimethyl-β-cyclodextrin (TMCD), Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (Hp-β-CD), Randomly Methylated-β-cyclodextrin (RMCD), Sulfobutylether-β-cyclodextrin (SBE-β-CD) 53. SMILES were input for each ligand and complexation free energy (ΔG, kJ/mol) was obtained for analysis.

Pharmacokinetic simulation

Pharmacokinetic simulation was done using the GastroPlus 9.3.254. It integrates various physiological, physicochemical, and biopharmaceutical factors to simulate the pharmacokinetic behavior of compounds. Simulations were based on a 100 mg oral dose under fasted human physiology, applying the Advanced Compartmental Absorption and Transit (ACAT) model with optimized LogD models (SA/V 6.1) 39. Demographic parameters included individuals aged between 30 to 60 years, with a specific consideration for pregnant individuals within 30–40 year range. Rest of the parameters were as described before55. Pregnancy and health conditions such as steatosis (fatty liver) and moderate renal infection were also separately factored into the simulation, recognizing their potential impact on drug metabolism and clearance. Pregnancy is characterized by substantial changes in plasma volume, gastrointestinal transit, hepatic enzyme activity, and renal clearance, all of which can markedly influence systemic drug exposure56. Given that B. bacilliformis infection also affects individuals across reproductive age groups, evaluating pharmacokinetics under pregnancy-associated physiological conditions provides insight into potential exposure variability. Renal impairment was included because altered renal function is a major determinant of drug clearance, particularly for compounds or metabolites eliminated through renal pathways57. Physiological based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) simulation under renal impairment enables evaluation of changes in systemic exposure arising from reduced glomerular filtration, altered tubular secretion, and potential accumulation of parent compounds or metabolites. Moreover, acute or chronic renal dysfunction may arise secondary to severe infectious diseases or as a comorbidity in affected populations58, making this scenario relevant for assessing dosing robustness and safety margins. Steatosis was modelled as a representative hepatic dysfunction state to examine the sensitivity of compound disposition to altered liver physiology. As hepatic metabolism is a primary determinant of drug clearance, PBPK simulation under steatotic conditions allows assessment of potential changes in bioavailability and exposure that may arise in metabolically compromised hosts59. The simulation duration was set for 24 h, and a population of 50 individuals was taken into account to assess variability in drug responses among different subjects.

MD simulation

MD simulation was carried out using GROMACS60. Protein and ligand structures were prepared to generate GROMACS-compatible topology files. Protein topologies were generated with the CHARMM27 force field, while ligand parameters were obtained from the APCYPE. Each protein–ligand complex was solvated in a cubic box using the TIP3P water model, ensuring a minimum distance of 1.0 nm between the solute and the box edge. Counterions were added to neutralize the system. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 5000 steps, after which equilibration was conducted under constant volume (NVT) and constant pressure (NPT) ensembles to stabilize the system 39 for 100 ps. A 2 fs integration step was employed, with all bonds constrained using the LINear Constraint Solver (LINCS) algorithm. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, and van der Waals and Coulomb cut-offs were set at 1.2 nm. The production phase was run for 100 ns with the CHARMM27 force field, maintaining a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 bar. Snapshots of the trajectory were saved every 100 ps (1000 frames).The obtained data was subjected to analyses encompassing root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), hydrogen bond analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), radius of gyration (Rg) and molecular mechanics/poisson-boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA). Python v 3.10 was used for plotting RMSD/RMSF graphs, box-plots, PCA scatter plots, and time resolved SASA heatmaps.

Drug target analysis

A comprehensive drug target analysis was conducted on core proteome sequences. Following CD-HIT clustering, a refined set of 1,012 representative sequences was obtained. Subsequent screening for essential proteins identified 696 sequences in the DEG database and 625 in the CEG database, highlighting proteins conserved as essential across diverse bacterial species. Integration of the DEG and CEG results yielded a common set of 613 essential sequences.

To account for potential host-related interactions, 247 sequences showing similarity to the human proteome were excluded from further analysis, leaving 80 sequences after comparison with the gut proteome. From these, a subset of 20 sequences was identified as potential drug targets, prioritized based on multiple criteria, including their essentiality and lack of interaction with human proteins (Supplementary Table S1). These were further ranked for suitability using SPIDER scores and sub-cellular localization. Proteins predicted to be membrane-associated were excluded due to their limited usefulness for docking and increased structural complexity, as membrane proteins pose challenges in crystallization61 and face bottlenecks in drug development pipelines62. Additionally, targets predicted to be unstable (instability index > 40) were removed. Cytoplasmic proteins with SPIDER score > 0.95 were retained.

Among the excluded targets, the DHA2 family efflux MFS transporter permease subunit and ubiquinol oxidase subunit II were removed due to their inner membrane localization. Although MFS efflux pumps play a central role in multidrug resistance63, they are widely regarded as challenging drug targets because of their broad substrate specificity and high mutational plasticity64, which compromise inhibitor selectivity and long-term efficacy. Ubiquinol oxidase subunit II, a component of the bacterial respiratory chain, is also embedded in the membrane. This limits drug accessibility and the presence of homologous respiratory proteins in host mitochondria raises concerns regarding potential off-target toxicity65.

Two homologs of uracil-DNA glycosylase were also excluded. One displayed the lowest SPIDER druggability score and was classified as a negative target, while the second exhibited dual subcellular localization and a DNA repair function associated with functional redundancy. Inhibition of DNA glycosylases has been shown to be therapeutically challenging due to the presence of compensatory repair enzymes, such as Tag glycosylase, which can restore DNA repair activity and reduce inhibitor effectiveness66. Several cytoplasmic enzymes, including 5-(carboxyamino)imidazole ribonucleotide synthase, 4-hydroxythreonine-4-phosphate dehydrogenase (PdxA), UDP-3-O-acyl-N-acetylglucosamine deacetylase, and holo-ACP synthase were excluded based on predicted instability index because they are prone to rapid in vivo degradation, which prevents sustained inhibitor binding and reproducible pharmacology. Consistent with this, previous studies have reported limited inhibitor engagement with holo-ACP synthase, attributed to the conformational dynamics associated with the phosphopantetheine transfer process. Nine proteins were identified as suitable candidates based on their predicted druggability and stability (Table 2).

Riboflavin synthase had suitable molecular weight, exhibits stability, and ranked highest in SPIDER-based druggability assessment, supporting its selection as the final target. In contrast to several other retained enzymes that participate in central metabolic or DNA repair pathways with potential functional redundancy, riboflavin synthase represents a bottleneck enzyme with limited compensatory alternatives. It plays a pivotal role in the biosynthesis of riboflavin (vitamin B2), an essential cofactor in numerous enzymatic reactions67. Riboflavin is indispensable for energy metabolism, primarily in the form of flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). Specifically, riboflavin synthase catalyzes the terminal step in riboflavin biosynthesis, converting 6,7-dimethyl-8-ribityllumazine into riboflavin68. This pathway is characteristic of microorganisms, fungi, and plants, whereas mammals lack the ability to synthesize riboflavin and must acquire it through dietary intake69. Targeting riboflavin synthase as a drug target in bacteria represents a promising avenue for developing antimicrobial agents since bacteria must synthesize riboflavin de novo and mammals lack this capability. Hence, inhibiting riboflavin synthesis can selectively target bacterial pathogens70,71. Inhibitors specifically designed to block riboflavin synthase can exert bacteriostatic or bactericidal effects, thereby impairing the survival of pathogenic bacteria. Such compounds hold potential for treating a broad spectrum of infections, including those caused by antibiotic-resistant strains72.

3D structure of this enzyme was available in the AlphaFold database and exhibited a remarkable Ramachandran score, with 98.5% of residues located in favored regions (Supplementary Fig. S1). This indicates excellent stereochemistry and strong structural reliability. The Ramachandran plot evaluates backbone dihedral angles (ϕ and ψ) in a protein structure, and a high percentage of residues in favored regions reflects a well-folded and stable conformation73. Additionally, the structural model derived from Alphafold provided pLDDT ranging from 0 to 10074,75. A pLDDT score above 90 indicates a high level of confidence in the predicted model. For riboflavin synthase of B. bacilliformis, the majority of protein residues demonstrated a pLDDT greater than 90, suggesting a robust and reliable structural prediction. Additional Swiss-Model quality metrics further supported this assessment of model reliability. The MolProbity score of 0.71 indicated excellent overall geometry, as values closer to zero reflect fewer structural issues such as steric clashes or bond angle deviations. The clash score of 0.62 suggests a very low number of steric overlaps between atoms, which is a strong indicator of a physically realistic structure. Furthermore, the QMEANDisCo global score of 0.81 ± 0.06 reflected good agreement between the model and typical structural features observed in high-resolution experimental protein structures, as scores above 0.7 generally indicate high model quality. Together, these metrics reinforce the reliability and accuracy of the predicted 3D structure of riboflavin synthase.

Off-target analysis of compounds

Macromolecular targets of prioritized compounds were searched in humans for determining their off-target potential. This was to identify if the compounds would bind human protein as well. ADMET

ADMET prediction suggested that kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside has a tendency toward supersaturation, while the probability of blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration was low. It was classified as ECCS Class 4 (Renal), indicating that it is likely to be eliminated via the kidneys. Transporter analysis showed that it did not inhibit most transporters, but is predicted to be a substrate for organic ion transporting polypeptides (OATP1B1, OATP1B3), Organic Anion Transporter 3 (OAT3), P-glycoprotein (Pgp), and Breast Cancer Resistance Protein (BCRP). The predicted clearance values and fractional unbound plasma (Fup) percentages suggest moderate to high values, indicating potential efficient elimination. Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside was also predicted to have a tendency to supersaturate, and the likelihood of BBB penetration was low. The RMSD did not exceed 3 Å for any of the compounds, suggesting that the simulated structures maintained a relatively close resemblance to their initial conformations (Fig. 4A). The boxplot of RMSD values showed that the Hirtusneanoside complex exhibited a median RMSD of approximately 2.5 Å, whereas Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside and Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside displayed lower median RMSD values of 2.0 Å and 2.2 Å, respectively (Fig. 4B). The ligand interaction with residues (within a 4 Å distance) fluctuated over the course of trajectory along with changes in bond formation (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Discussion

The pan-proteome analysis provided a comprehensive view of the diversity and shared features among the B. bacilliformis strains, highlighting both core and accessory components that shape their unique biological characteristics. Power-fit and exponential curve equations suggest that as more proteomes are added to the pan-proteome analysis, the total number of protein families increase but the rate of increase differs77. The power-fit curve also indicated a slower, gradual increase, while the exponential curve suggested a more rapid increase initially, followed by a decreasing rate as more proteomes were added. The negative exponent in the exponential fit suggests a diminishing core proteome size as more proteomes are included. This aligns with the concept that the core proteome becomes more defined and reduces in size with additional proteomes78. Furthermore, strain-specific protein losses may reflect distinct evolutionary trajectories or selective pressures acting on individual strains79.

Subtractive proteomics proved to be a pivotal approach in drug design when applied to B. bacilliformis, the causative agent of Carrion’s disease. Through a comparative analysis of the proteomic data from the pathogen and its host, unique bacterial proteins crucial for survival were pinpointed as potential drug targets specific to B. bacilliformis. This approach minimizes the risk of adverse effects on the host 80. Within this framework, riboflavin synthase was selected as a promising candidate, given its role in riboflavin biosynthesis. Humans obtain riboflavin from the diet rather than de novo synthesis81, making riboflavin synthase a selective bacterial target. Subtractive proteomics confirmed it as a non-homologous, essential enzyme, highlighting its drug target potential. The enzyme shows a high-affinity ligand binding site that accommodates substrate analogs and riboflavin, indicating good ligandability for small molecule ligand design. Its selection as a drug target was further supported by previous studies where it has been investigated in various pathogenic bacteria, demonstrating broad-spectrum potential for antimicrobial intervention. Riboflavin synthase was targeted in Leptospira kmetyi, with inhibitors screened from the ZINC database82. In Fusobacterium nucleatum, cyanobactins, streptomycins, and marine bacterial secondary metabolites were evaluated against riboflavin synthase83. For Campylobacter spp., the NPASS natural product library was screened against riboflavin synthase of Campylobacter coli72, and Campylobacter hyointestinali84.

By targeting this specific enzyme in B. bacilliformis, we aimed to identify antimicrobial agents from TTM that effectively combat bacterial infections while sparing the host from unintended consequences. TTM boasts a rich repository of medicinal plants with historical use in the Himalayan region85. The selection of TTM-derived plant metabolites in this study was informed by both ethnopharmacological knowledge and their long-standing application in managing infectious and systemic diseases86. TTM plants, including species such as Juniperus squamata, Meconopsis horridula, and Usnea spp., have traditionally been employed to treat infections, inflammatory disorders, and blood-related conditions. The known therapeutic effects of several plants in our dataset (e.g., Bergenia purpurascens, Usnea sp., Meconopsis spp., and Gentiana algida) suggest that their metabolites could act on pathophysiological features of B. bacilliformis infection, which progresses from acute Oroya fever (with hemolytic anemia and endothelial invasion) to a chronic eruptive phase, verruga peruana (with angiogenic skin lesions).

Many Tibetan plants are rich in bioactive constituents such as flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, polysaccharides, and glycosides 87, which are known to exert antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and even anti-angiogenic effects. Among the screened flora, some plants had documented antimicrobial activity while inclusion of others was to assess their potential as novel leads. Anaphalis contorta and Juniperus squamata have reported anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective activities88,89. Anaphalis contorta has antibacterial and antifungal effects (with sesquiterpenes such as E-caryophyllene and β-himachalene contributing to inhibition of human pathogens), consistent with its traditional use in wound healing and antisepsis90,91. Juniperus squamata essential oil has demonstrated antimicrobial activity, largely due to bioactive terpenoids while Bergenia purpurascens exhibits antioxidant, hepatoprotective properties 89and antimicrobial activities mainly due to phenolic compounds such as procyanidins and galloylepicatechin derivatives. Usnea spp. are recognized for anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and wound-healing activities 92. We hypothesized that by combining the ethnopharmacological relevance of these plants with a computational screening pipeline (molecular docking, ADMET profiling, and MD simulations), we could identify potential therapeutic leads against B. bacilliformis with limited existing drug discovery efforts. Given the structural and functional conservation of key bacterial enzymes such as flavoproteins, it is plausible that bioactive compounds with activity against related Gram-negative bacteria may also inhibit Bartonella sp. enzyme. This is in line with emerging natural product-based discovery strategies for neglected tropical diseases, where mechanistic rationale often substitutes for direct ethnopharmacological precedent. Collectively, these traditional uses indicate that these plants could potentially address multiple pathological aspects of B. bacilliformis infection, including inflammation, blood disorders, and microbial proliferation.

In total, 52 metabolites were docked against riboflavin synthase, and the top-ranking ligands were included Kaempferol derivatives and Hirtusneanoside. ASN76, GLY83, GLN182 made interactions across all complexes, highlighting their role as anchoring hotspots. Overall, Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside emerged as the most promising candidate due to its extensive and diverse interaction network, while Hirtusneanoside offered a strong but more specialized binding mode through highly stable H-bonds and aromatic stacking.

Kaempferol derivatives are known to exhibit pharmacological activities relevant to inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic disorders, and infection93. Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside has previously demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and enzyme-modulating effects, including inhibition of 5-lipoxygenase and COX-1, which contributes to reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines and tissue damage in asthma models. It has also shown anticancer activity via TGF-β/ALK5 pathway blockade94. Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside shows similar anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, suggesting potential cytoprotective and immunomodulatory effects consistent with other kaempferol glycosides88,89. Hirtusneanoside, a depsidone from Usnea hirta has documented antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities, supporting its traditional use against infections and oxidative stress-related pathologies92.

The prioritized metabolites did not strictly conform to Ro5 criteria (Supplementary Table S2). However, many plant-derived metabolites naturally exhibit high molecular weight, elevated lipophilicity, and multiple polar functional groups, often exceeding Ro5 thresholds. Some plant metabolites have complex structures with multiple sugar groups or fused ring systems, contributing to their higher molecular weight. A typical example is ginsenoside Rb1 with a weight of 1108 Da95. Paclitaxel, a diterpenoid derived from the Pacific yew tree (Taxus brevifolia) is a potent anticancer agent 96and has molecular weight exceeding 850 Da. Other examples include the immunosuppressant Cyclosporine (molecular weight > 1200 Daltons)97, anticoagulant Heparin (highly polar molecule with many hydrogen bond donors and acceptors)98 and antiretroviral Ritonavir (high lipophilicity; ClogP value)99, exceeding the Ro5 limit of 5. This suggests that Ro5 can be a valuable tool in drug discovery but is not the only factor to consider. Other factors, such as the drug target site, metabolism, and formulation, can also play a significant role in its oral bioavailability100. Their ability to act as substrates for key transporters could further facilitate absorption. These findings highlight the need to reconsider the strict applicability of Lipinski’s Ro5 when evaluating natural products, particularly those derived from plants. The distinctive structural complexity of plant metabolites often challenges conventional drug design paradigms, necessitating a more nuanced framework for assessing their therapeutic value. Moreover, the use of optimized delivery strategies, such as higher dosing regimens or advanced formulations including liposomes and nanoparticles, could further improve the pharmacological impact of these molecules101.

In addition to this, we searched for similarities between the compounds and known human protein binders to identify whether these inhibitors might also bind to human proteins, potentially causing side effects. Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside showed some potential for binding to human family A G protein-coupled receptors and hydrolase (Acetylcholinesterase). Linked family A GPCR receptors are involved in regulating various functions like blood pressure, muscle contraction, and stress response102. Binding to these receptors could potentially affect these processes, while acetylcholinesterase plays a role in nerve impulse transmission and interaction with this enzyme could have neurological effects103. The other two inhibitors did not show significant binding with human proteins. These binding probabilities are estimates and may not reflect actual binding and further research work in this direction is warranted. Additionally, some degree of interaction with human proteins and modulation of associated pathways might be necessary for the compound to have beneficial effects. Nevertheless, these observations represent preliminary insights on the potential off-target effects of these inhibitors. Further studies are crucial to determine its safety and effectiveness in a human context.

MD simulation results highlighted the stability of all three compounds throughout the simulation. The majority of residue movements remained within a 3 Å range. The Rg, measuring structural compactness, showed similar values for Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside, Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside, and Hirtusneanoside, indicating near-identical structural compactness. Hirtusneanoside demonstrated the strongest and most consistent hydrogen-bonding capacity, followed by Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside, while Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside showed weaker hydrogen-bonding interactions. This highlights the prominent role of polar binding in stabilizing Hirtusneanoside, which showed the greatest solvent exposure and a diverse conformational landscape. The thermodynamic analysis based on MM/GBSA profiles indicated overall favorable interactions for all three compounds. Ligands contributed negatively to the overall energy, suggesting favorable interactions with Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside showing highest affinity, underlining its potential as a favorable ligand for riboflavin synthase.

The distinctive attributes uncovered through off-target analysis, MD simulation and thermodynamic analyses of these compounds present both opportunities and challenges for drug design. Particularly, the potential binding of Kaempferol 3-O-gentiobioside to receptors associated with crucial physiological parameters like blood pressure regulation, muscle contraction, and stress response, presents a dual outcome for therapeutic development. While such interactions underscore the compound efficacy and safety profile, they may also restrict its applicability in individuals with conditions directly influenced by these pathways (for instance hypertension or cardiovascular disorders). However, leveraging the information obtained from MD simulations and thermodynamic analyses can still aid in the rational design of derivatives or analogs with improved binding affinity, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties. This iterative process of structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis and optimization is essential for maximizing the therapeutic potential of these compounds while minimizing undesirable off-target effects.

PBPK simulations were performed under pregnancy, renal impairment, and steatosis, in addition to normal physiology, to evaluate how physiological variability affects drug pharmacokinetics. Inter-individual variability increased substantially, with the coefficient of variation rising from 25% under normal conditions to 55% under these stressors. Pregnancy simulations, accounted for 30–50% increase in plasma volume, ~ 40% elevation in glomerular filtration rate, and predicted 25–35% reductions in flavonoid AUC. Renal impairment approximately doubled systemic exposure due to reduced clearance, while hepatic steatosis decreased fractional absorption by 15–25%, through impaired CYP-mediated metabolism. These scenarios serve as exploratory sensitivity analyses to inform future experimental validation and dose-optimization strategies rather than as direct clinical predictors. Pregnancy modelling reflected known physiological changes leading to reduced predicted phytochemical exposure. Simulations of renal impairment indicated a need for dose adjustment, while steatosis modelling revealed altered absorption and exposure. Overall, disease and pregnancy states showed substantially greater pharmacokinetic variability than normal physiology, supporting prioritization of riboflavin synthase inhibitors with wide therapeutic margins.

A key strength of this study lies in the integrative computational approach, which combined docking, ADMET predictions, MD simulations, thermodynamic analyses, and off-target profiling. This multilayered strategy provided a robust evaluation of candidate compounds and revealed stable conformations and favorable interactions, underscoring their potential as drug candidates. Additionally, the ethnopharmacological relevance of Tibetan medicinal plants strengthens the rationale for exploring these scaffolds, as they have long been associated with antimicrobial use. The identification of unique conformational changes, such as those in Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside, further offers opportunities for designing novel analogs with distinct mechanisms of action.

The primary limitation of this study is that it relies exclusively on in silico analyses and in spite of the comprehensive computational workflow employed, the absence of experimental validation remains a key constraint. Hence, the predicted inhibitory activity and pharmacokinetic behaviour of the shortlisted phytocompounds requires confirmation through biochemical, cellular, and in vivo investigations. Future validation efforts should begin with in vitro enzymatic assays to evaluate inhibition of B. bacilliformis riboflavin synthase. Binding affinities and kinetic parameters may then be quantified using established biophysical techniques, including surface plasmon resonance (SPR), isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), and thermal shift assays104. In vitro cellular assays employing B. bacilliformis infection models would allow assessment of antibacterial efficacy and intracellular activity, consistent with its obligate intracellular lifestyle. Cytotoxicity testing in mammalian cell lines will be essential to establish host selectivity and safety, while validated lead compounds may ultimately be evaluated in suitable in vivo infection models to characterize pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and toxicity105.

Several prioritized compounds deviated from Lipinski’s Ro5 but this does not completely preclude them from being a viable drug candidate as the success of a drug candidate depends on a multitude of factors, including its pharmacological activity, safety profile, and therapeutic efficacy, rather than strict adherence to Lipinski’s Ro5. There are numerous examples of drugs that violate one or more of Lipinski’s rules97–99 but still exhibit favorable pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties. Pharmacokinetic evaluation is therefore essential, as it provides critical insights into drug behavior under diverse physiological and pathological conditions, ultimately aiding in the optimization of formulations and dosing strategies. Scaffold hopping strategies offer promising avenues for further development in this regard106. Understanding SAR enables the modification of non-essential regions while preserving crucial interaction sites, enhancing drug-like properties. The unique conformational changes observed in Kaempferol 3-(6''-p-coumarylglucoside)-7-glucoside provide an opportunity to design compounds with distinct modes of action. Bioisosteric replacement, mimicking physicochemical properties, and hybridization with features from other drug-like molecules could yield compounds with improved characteristics. However, challenges include the need for bioavailability optimization through prodrug design or formulation development, addressing potential toxicity and side effects associated with Ro5 deviation, and considering the synthetic feasibility of modified scaffolds for practical drug development. Rigorous toxicity studies are crucial to assess the safety profile of these non-traditional drug candidates. The findings emphasize the necessity of a comprehensive evaluation, taking into account multiple factors that influence the ligand performance across various physiological conditions. Such insights are invaluable for drug development and given the escalating threat of antibiotic resistance, the need for novel antimicrobial agents is pressing. Although challenges like potential off-target effects, high molecular weight, deviation from Ro5 and resistance development necessitate careful consideration, the targeted inhibition of riboflavin synthase emerges as a rational and selective strategy in the battle against bacterial infections. Ongoing research in this domain holds considerable promise for the development of highly effective and specific antibacterial drugs.

Conclusion

The pan-proteome analysis of B. bacilliformis revealed dynamics within protein families, with the pan-proteome appearing nearly closed and the core proteome exhibiting an exponential decrease. These observations shed light on the stability and evolution of protein families within the studied proteomes. Subtractive proteomics unveiled potential drug targets and virtual screening was used to identify drug targets. Screening of TTM compounds against selected target riboflavin synthase showed that although top-scoring compounds did not fulfill the traditional drug-likeness criteria but their favorable binding features present opportunities for innovative drug design. The subtle differences in their pharmacokinetic profiles may influence their use in specific therapeutic applications, and further optimization may be necessary to enhance their overall drug-like properties. Cyclodextrin complexation offers a promising strategy for improving solubility, stability, and bioavailability. By coupling with cyclodextrins, such as SBE-β-CD, which is specifically designed to improve water solubility and reduce toxicity, these compounds can benefit from enhanced complexation efficiency, leading to improved dissolution rates and better oral absorption. However, experimental validation, such as in vitro and in vivo studies, is crucial to confirm these predictions and guide further drug development efforts.
