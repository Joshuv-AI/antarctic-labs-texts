Memantine, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, and quality‐of‐care outcomes among patients with cognitive impairment: an emulated trial design
Authors: Erin L Ferguson, Jingxuan Wang, Justin S White, Katherine Possin, Anna Chodos, Fan Xia, Eva Raphael, Alexander K Smith, M Maria Glymour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe evaluated the effect of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) and memantine on non‐cognitive outcomes by implementing emulated trials in electronic health records.METHODSIndividuals diagnosed with cognitive impairment who initiated AChEIs or memantine were matched to up to five non‐initiators. Associations of medication initiation with 11 non‐cognitive outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. We calculated e‐values for each hazard ratio (HR) and compared their magnitude to socioeconomic predictors of memantine and AChEI use in a US‐based cohort.RESULTSAChEI initiation was associated with nine of 11 adverse outcomes. For example, the HR for antipsychotic use = 1.67 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.51 to 1.84) and depression HR = 1.86 (1.67 to 2.06). Results for memantine were similar. E‐values varied from 1.45 to 2.11 for memantine and 1.40 to 2.44 for AChEIs. Many potential confounders had odds ratios (ORs) > 1.4 for initiation, including marital status (ORs = 1.36 to 6.67).DISCUSSIONMemantine and AChEIs were associated with adverse outcomes, and residual confounding was unlikely to fully account for all the observed associations.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cholinesterase inhibitors, dementia, electronic health records, memantine, quality of life, target trial emulation

BACKGROUND

Primary care providers (PCPs) make 65% of all cognitive impairment diagnoses and prescribe the majority of anti‐dementia medications. 1 , 2 PCPs commonly prescribe one or both of two US Food and Drug Administration‐approved drug classes: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) and/or memantine. Despite providing only modest gains in cognition, 3 , 4 these medications remain common, with between 30% and 60% of individuals with cognitive impairment receiving a prescription at some point. 1 , 5 Because of their regular use, it is necessary to evaluate whether they have other effects on patients.

There is minimal evidence that AChEIs improve quality of life when used alone, though some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) showed small functional status benefits 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 and evidence for negative psychiatric effects. 10 There is minimal evidence that memantine monotherapy improves quality of life or functional status. 11 When used together, dual therapy may modestly improve quality of life. 12 , 13 Prior trials have limited generalizability. 14 , 15 , 16 Observational studies can address key limitations of RCTs, but they are susceptible to common biases, specifically immortal time bias and prevalent user bias. A recent review found that 57% of studies evaluating medication effectiveness using claims‐based data had immortal time bias and 44% had prevalent user bias. 17

Emulating target trials helps avoid immortal time and prevalent user biases by designing observational studies to mimic hypothetical RCTs for the same research question, that is, an emulated target trial. By aligning critical elements of study design (e.g., definition of baseline and the study population), observational data can come close to estimating an intent‐to‐treat (ITT) effect mirroring an RCT result – that is, the effect of being assigned to initiate a medication (either via randomization in an RCT or by receiving a prescription in the emulated trial). 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Although emulated target trials avoid certain common biases, they rely on the typical assumptions for causal inference, most pertinently the assumption that, after controlling for the covariates, whether a patient receives a treatment is unrelated to the outcome they would otherwise have experienced (exchangeability). To our knowledge, no observational study has applied this framework to evaluate anti‐dementia medications. This work also serves to evaluate the effectiveness of anti‐dementia medications against a large panel of non‐cognitive outcomes using electronic health records (EHRs).

By emulating target trials, we estimated the effect of initiating memantine or AChEIs on quality‐of‐care (QoC) outcomes using EHRs from a California healthcare system. Given limited measured covariates in EHRs, we additionally evaluated the likelihood of residual confounding using the All of Us Research Program with more comprehensive data.

METHODS

Study population

Our cohort comprised adults aged 55 and older who received a first diagnosis of cognitive impairment from a PCP within the University of California (UC) medical system from 2012 to 2024. EHRs were available from the UC Data Warehouse, which included diagnoses, medications, and encounters from six UC campuses (Davis [UCD], San Francisco [UCSF], Los Angeles [UCLA], Irvine [UCI], Riverside [UCR], and San Diego [UCSD]). 22 We defined primary care departments as departments that were both (1) mapped as an outpatient department in the EHR system and (2) included a term indicative of primary care in the department name (e.g., “Family Medicine”). This definition included 471 departments in internal medicine, family medicine, geriatric medicine, and executive health.

Of 61,539 individuals first diagnosed with cognitive impairment in primary care, we excluded 7543 (12.3%) younger than 55 years at diagnosis. To ensure appropriate matching with non‐initiators, we excluded individuals who were prescribed memantine (n = 4430, 7.2%) or AChEIs (n = 7993, 13.0%) prior to first cognitive impairment diagnosis. These exclusions resulted in an analytic sample of 49,566 for memantine and 46,003 for AChEIs. Birth month was not available in this dataset, so we used a birthdate of July 2 for age calculations.

Target trial emulation

To implement the emulated trial framework, 18 , 23 we first specified components of two theoretical RCTs for anti‐dementia medications and quality of life (Table 1), such as treatment groups, eligibility criteria, and the follow‐up period. While observational data do not have randomization or placebo control groups, we were able to align components of our observational study as closely as possible to this hypothetical RCT. For example, our exposure definition (initiation vs non‐initiation), baseline (matched initiation date), and the estimand (comparing individuals assigned to initiate vs those not assigned to initiate) are comparable to our hypothetical RCT. We emulated separate trials for memantine and AChEI initiation. Our interpretation assumes that nearly everyone in the observational data who receives a prescription for a medication at least initiates that medication, although that person may subsequently cease use.

QoC outcomes

We identified 11 outcomes associated with quality of life and mortality 24 , 25 , 26 : (1) incident prescription orders (antipsychotics and benzodiazepines), (2) healthcare utilization (emergency department [ED] visits or hospitalizations), (3) Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT)‐defined incident geriatric outcomes (incident delirium, depression, falls, hip fractures, fecal incontinence, and urinary incontinence), or (4) linked California death records (Table S1). Antipsychotics and benzodiazepines were included as outcomes as we believed anti‐dementia medications could influence these outcomes through adverse events or improvement of behavioral symptoms of dementia. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30

Initiation of anti‐dementia medications and creation of cohorts

We created trials separately for anti‐dementia medication type and outcome. We identified first‐time initiators of memantine or AChEIs (donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine) during the study period from prescription orders. Using incidence density sampling, we matched each initiator with up to five individuals who had not yet initiated the anti‐dementia medication of interest based on birth year (distance matched, with a maximum 5‐year difference), most recent cognitive impairment diagnosis (memory loss, mild cognitive impairment [MCI], Alzheimer's disease and related dementias [ADRD]; Table S1), and time (i.e., non‐initiators were active patients in the UC system at time of initiator's first prescription). Patients were considered active in the UC system from time of first to last clinical record. For trials of incident outcomes (prescriptions and geriatric outcomes), we removed participants with prevalent prescription use or diagnoses from the eligible sample of initiators and non‐initiators. Non‐initiators could be matches for multiple initiators (on different initiation dates), and initiators could serve as controls prior to their first initiation of an anti‐dementia medication. Follow‐up time began for both initiators and non‐initiators at the matched initiation date (defined from here as baseline; Figure S1). After matching and restricting samples for patients without each outcome at baseline, we had 18 distinct cohorts for each medication and outcome, as (1) models for ED visits, hospitalizations, and death were evaluated in the same cohort and (2) models of incident outcomes removed patients with prevalent outcomes from the study population (two exposures and eight incident outcomes implies 16 distinct cohorts).

Covariates

We identified eight covariates, including race (Asian, Black, other, unknown, White), ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, unknown), sex (male or female), other anti‐dementia medication use (i.e., for a memantine trial, AChEI use), UC site (UCD, UCI, UCLA, UCR, UCSD, and UCSF), count of UC healthcare encounters in the year prior to baseline, count of prescription orders in the year prior to baseline, indicator for having a visit with a neurologist in the year prior to baseline, history of sensory impairments (Table S1), history of mobility problems, 31 and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). CCI was calculated using the comorbidity package in R (version 1.1.0). 32

Inverse probability of treatment weights

We modeled the probability of anti‐dementia medication initiation in a logistic regression from covariates excluding matched factors. For initiators, stabilized weights were calculated as the proportion of initiators in the cohort divided by their predicted probability of initiation given covariates (i.e., their propensity score). For non‐initiators, stabilized weights were calculated as the proportion of non‐initiators in the cohort divided by one minus their propensity score (1 − PS). Analyses were restricted to individuals with propensity scores from the first and 99th percentiles. If an initiator was dropped, all that initiator's matched non‐initiators were also dropped.

Statistical analysis

We evaluated IP‐weighted Cox proportional hazard models for each QoC outcome, censored at the earliest of death, a lapse in healthcare utilization of half a year (including visits and prescription orders), or end of study period (December 13, 2024). All outcome models were adjusted for covariates, matched factors (age [years], cognitive impairment diagnosis [memory loss, MCI, and ADRD], and trial year [continuous]), and deciles of propensity score. Robust standard errors were used. As we leveraged a matched cohort, our estimates can be interpreted as the effect of initiating anti‐dementia medications among individuals who initiate, rather than the estimated effect for the whole population.

We considered four secondary analyses. First, we considered changes in treatment assignment in per‐protocol analyses, censoring at the first 60‐day lapse in anti‐dementia medication use (i.e., 60 days after the end date on their medication order). Non‐initiators were censored when they initiated the anti‐dementia drug of interest. Second, we stratified models by cognitive impairment diagnosis. Third, we limited the eligible sample to patients diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease – the primary indication for both medications – creating new matched samples. 33 , 34 Fourth, we evaluated the potential for effect modification by concurrent use of the other anti‐dementia medication.

Bias analysis

EHRs are susceptible to residual confounding because few social history variables are available. 35 , 36 , 37 To assess the extent to which our results could be biased by residual confounding, we calculated e‐values for each statistically significant ITT estimate using the EValue R package (version 4.1.3). The e‐value is defined as the magnitude of an effect an unmeasured confounder would need to have on anti‐dementia medication initiation and the outcome to fully account for the observed association. 38

We then identified predictors of memantine or AChEI initiation in a sample of 5247 All of Us participants with cognitive impairment. The All of Us Research Program, which enrolls individuals living in the United States through over 340 sites, includes a sociodemographic and health behavior survey at enrollment, with linked EHR data. We fit logistic regressions to predict ever receiving a prescription of memantine or AChEIs. Our primary model included eight predictors not available in UC EHRs, in addition to age (years, defined as date of first prescription for initiators and date of survey for non‐initiators), sex (male, female, other/unknown), and race (non‐Hispanic Asian, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, other, missing): education (less than high school, high school, some college, college or above, missing), smoking status (smoker, non‐smoker, missing), alcohol use (yes, no, missing), income (less than USD 10 thousand, USD 10 to 25 thousand, 25 to 35 thousand, 35 to 75 thousand, more than 75 thousand, missing), marital status (married, living with partner, divorced, separated, widowed, never married, missing), general health (excellent, very good, good, fair, poor, missing), birth country (outside USA, USA, missing), health insurance coverage (yes, no, missing). Our secondary model was intended to assess whether the association of these sociodemographic covariates might be captured by the clinical measures in EHRs; to do this, we included all predictors from the primary model, plus 13 overlapping covariates with UC EHR: healthcare utilization and self‐reported indicators for history of comorbidities at survey (cancer, hypertension, asthma, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease, dementia, heart failure, stroke, congestive heart failure, heart attack, diabetes, obesity). We estimated both models with and without adjustment for ever use of the other anti‐dementia medication. By comparing the strength of these predictors to our calculated e‐values, we evaluated whether any of the sociodemographic variables measured in All of Us would be likely to have biased our emulated trial estimates.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2).

Data availability

Access to the clinical records used in this project is restricted for patient privacy. Individuals affiliated with the UC system can find information on how to request access to the UC Data Warehouse through their institution.

Results

Sample characteristics

From the eligible populations of 49,566 for memantine and 46,003 for AChEIs, there were 6061 initiators of memantine (8.2%) and 9784 initiators of AChEIs (21.3%). Propensity score distributions for memantine and AChEI initiators substantially overlapped with those for matched non‐initiators (Figure S2). After dropping individuals with extreme propensity scores, matched pairs differed in birth year by a maximum of 0.006 years (Table S2).

Initiators of memantine had fewer prescription orders (24.9 vs 33.6) and encounters (25.6 vs 35.9) in the year prior to baseline compared to matched non‐initiators (Table 2). Memantine initiators were more likely to also be taking AChEIs at or before the time of memantine initiation compared to controls (69.6% vs 38.6%). Initiators of AChEIs had similar patterns, with fewer prescriptions and encounters prior to baseline compared to non‐initiators (Table 3). After weighting, covariate balance improved for both memantine (Table S3) and AChEI cohorts (Table S4).

Falls were the least common outcome (about seven falls per 1000 person‐years). In the memantine cohort, death was the most common outcome (110.67 deaths per 1000 person‐years), while emergency visits were the most common outcome in the AChEI cohort (111.85 cases per 1000 person‐years; Table S5).

Memantine initiation and QoC

Memantine initiation was associated with increased hazard of all outcomes except death and falls (Figure 1), including receiving an antipsychotic prescription (HR = 1.60, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.45 to 1.75), emergency room visits (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.08 to 1.30), and depression (HR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.26 to 1.64). Estimates were consistent when stratified by most recent cognitive impairment diagnosis, except memantine initiation was associated with decreased hazard of death among those with ADRD (HR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.87 to 1.01) but increased hazard of death among those with memory loss (HR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.17 to 1.66; Table S6). Estimates for memantine initiation were similar in rematched samples of patients with AD (Figure S3).

Intent‐to‐treat hazard ratios for the association between AChEI initiation or memantine initiation and 11 quality‐of‐care outcomes. Models used inverse probability of treatment weight‐stabilized weights with trimming of extreme propensity scores and were adjusted for race, ethnicity, sex, other anti‐dementia medication use, University of California site, count of encounters in the year prior to baseline, count of prescriptions in the year prior to baseline, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), prior neurology visit, history of sensory impairment, history of mobility problems, age, most recent cognitive impairment diagnosis, trial year, and deciles of propensity score.

There was a qualitative interaction by concurrent AChEI use for the association between memantine initiation and death. Among individuals concurrently or previously using an AChEI, memantine initiation was associated with decreased risk of death (HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.78 to 0.91). For individuals without concurrent AChEI use, memantine initiation was associated with increased risk of death (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.26; Figure S4).

By the end of study follow‐up, 11.1% of non‐initiators had initiated memantine (Table S7). In per‐protocol models, memantine was associated with increased hazard of adverse QoC outcomes, except for death (Figure S5). Memantine initiation was strongly associated with decreased hazard of death when censoring at deviation from assigned treatment group (HR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.60 to 0.78).

AChEI initiation and QoC

AChEI initiation was associated with increased hazard of antipsychotic initiation (HR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.51 to 1.84), emergency visits (HR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.14 to 1.34), and depression (HR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.67 to 2.06; Figure 1). AChEIs were not associated with death overall (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.92 to 1.05) but were associated with decreased mortality in the ADRD population (HR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.79 to 0.95) and increased mortality in the memory loss population (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.24 to 1.51; Table S8). AChEI initiation was also associated with decreased risk of death in a cohort restricted to patients with AD (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.66 to 0.94; Figure S3). There was no effect modification by concurrent memantine use (Figure S6).

By end of follow‐up, 15.6% of non‐initiators had received a prescription for an AChEI (Table S5). In per‐protocol models, the magnitude of the associations between AChEI use, falls (HR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.42 to 3.21), and depression (HR = 2.43, 95% CI: 2.15 to 2.76) were larger than in models that did not account for changes in treatment. Additionally, AChEI use was associated with reductions in mortality (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.51 to 0.66).

Bias analysis

E‐values for the estimated effects of memantine initiation ranged from 1.45 to 2.11, and e‐values for AChEIs ranged from 1.40 to 2.44 (Table S9). The largest e‐value for AChEIs corresponded to falls, while the largest e‐value for memantine corresponded to antipsychotics.

Among 5247 patients with cognitive impairment in the All of Us cohort, 371 (7.1%) ever initiated memantine and 858 (16.4%) ever initiated AChEIs. Marital status predicted memantine and AChEIs, with odds ratios for some marital status variables larger than 1.4 or less than 0.71. For example, divorced individuals had 0.55 times the odds of memantine initiation (95% CI: 0.39 to 0.77) and 0.65 times the odds of AChEI initiation (95% CI: 0.0.52 to 0.82; Table 4) compared to married individuals. Education also predicted memantine initiation (Table S10).

DISCUSSION

In these emulated target trials estimating the effect of initiation of memantine or AChEIs on QoC outcomes and mortality, initiation of either medication was associated with increased hazard of all outcomes except for death. Initiation of either medication had the strongest magnitude of association with prescriptions for antipsychotics or benzodiazepines, emergency room visits, depression, and delirium. Concurrent AChEI use qualitatively modified the relationship between memantine initiation and mortality such that memantine initiation among AChEI users was associated with lower mortality. Our bias analysis using data from All of Us found that residual confounding by sociodemographic factors may account for the weak associations of memantine or AchEIs with hospitalizations, but residual confounding by these factors would be unlikely to explain the stronger associations. In summary, we found that initiation of memantine or AChEIs was associated with adverse geriatric outcomes.

Prior research showed no effect or small benefits of memantine 11 or AChEIs 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 on quality of life and functional status. As this prior work was based on RCTs, differences in duration of follow‐up, real‐world medication use, or confounding may explain differences between the present study and prior literature. For example, many trials include only 6 months of follow‐up. However, our work is consistent with a meta‐analysis of RCTs finding that AChEIs are associated with increased risk of psychiatric adverse events, including depression. 10 Prior observational studies reported mixed associations between anti‐dementia medication use and non‐cognitive outcomes, though many studies may be affected by prevalent user or immortal time bias. 39 , 40 , 41 Our work improves upon these prior studies by using an emulated target trial design less prone to bias and investigating other patient‐centered outcomes. Our estimates were consistent with adverse events identified in RCTs and post‐approval studies: dizziness, suicidal ideation, and urinary incontinence for memantine 33 , 42 and urinary incontinence, gastrointestinal issues, hallucinations, and depression for AChEIs. 34 , 43

Receiving a prescription for an anti‐dementia medication may prompt individuals to receive more healthcare to address side effects. During these interactions, other, unrelated conditions may be recognized and treated. 44 While we adjusted for baseline healthcare utilization, there may still be differences between groups after medication initiation. As RCTs and cohorts with regularly scheduled follow‐up are less susceptible to this bias by following up with both treatment groups equally, it is plausible that this bias could account for some of the difference between our estimates and prior work.

Our bias analysis in the All of Us cohort indicated that residual confounding by sociodemographic factors unavailable in EHRs is unlikely to fully explain our primary results but is not a comprehensive quantitative bias analysis. 45 The results from this bias analysis should be interpreted as an indication of the plausibility of confounding rather than conclusive estimates of the confounding. It is likely that confounding by marital status and education exists in our models using EHRs, and it explains estimates closer to the null, such as the estimate for hospitalization. It is less likely that confounding by either of these factors fully explains stronger associations, such as the relationship between anti‐dementia medication use and antipsychotics, but we cannot exclude that possibility.

To our knowledge, this is the first application of the emulated target trial design to anti‐dementia medications. By using this framework, we avoided immortal time and prevalent user biases. 18 , 23 We addressed an important data limitation of EHRs by augmenting our analyses with sociodemographic predictors of anti‐dementia medication use in the All of Us cohort. Our sample was large and generally more racially and ethnically diverse than other studies on dementia care (29% to 30% non‐White, 10% Hispanic).

We excluded individuals with prescriptions prior to cognitive impairment diagnosis (7% to 13% of the sample) because no good matches could be identified for such initiators. However, individuals sometimes receive anti‐dementia prescriptions prior to initial diagnosis, and our results would not generalize to this population with better baseline cognitive function. Additionally, we could not fully account for the severity of cognitive impairment without data on memory assessments. As we only have access to prescription orders and not dispensing or utilization data, we are likely underestimating the true effect of these anti‐dementia medications. We may have missed important predictors of prescription use in our bias analysis, specifically caregiver support and social isolation. The All of Us data are vulnerable to substantial selection bias, suggesting caution in interpretation of the bias analysis. 46 Despite our bias analysis, we cannot eliminate the possibility of residual confounding by factors we did not assess.

We found that anti‐dementia medications were associated with increased risk of negative geriatric outcomes, especially antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, healthcare utilization, depression, and delirium. For memantine, these associations were modified by concurrent AChEI use. Clinicians may want to weigh the risk of these adverse outcomes against the potential for cognitive or caregiver benefit when prescribing memantine or AChEIs. Future work should consider leveraging clinical notes to identify other meaningful patient‐centered outcomes in older adults, like nursing home admissions. The emulated target trial framework will be valuable for real‐world evaluation of anti‐amyloid medications, such as lecanemab, as take‐up of these medications expands.

DIVERSITY, EQUITY, AND INCLUSION STATEMENT

Equitable representation of racial and ethnic groups is a priority in our approach to ADRD research. Rather than use a single UC site, we opted to use data from across the UC system to capture diversity in race and ethnicity across the patient population served by different UC sites. We did not stratify by racial and ethnic group as we did not have sufficient sample size to be powered to detect a meaningful point estimate by strata. We believe future work with larger sample sizes should prioritize investigating effect modification by racial and ethnic identity.
