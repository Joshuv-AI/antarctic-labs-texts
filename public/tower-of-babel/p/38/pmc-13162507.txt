Rethinking traditional emergency department design to improve flow and patient - staff satisfaction: A future proof design for 24 h operations
Authors: Davinia Coopman, Bernard Meyers, Jan Schrijvers, Soufia Cherif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency departments (EDs) face increasing pressure due to rising patient volumes, evolving care models, and limited physical space. EDs can be organized according to different structural and operational models. Some systems allocate patients based on specific pathologies, while others prioritize triage acuity levels, as commonly implemented in regions such as Scotland and Wales. A third model - predominantly used in the United States - organizes care delivery into “pods” that combine mixed pathologies and varying levels of acuity within the same unit. While this mixed pod-based model is widespread in the United States, it remains uncommon in most European emergency care settings.ObjectiveThis article describes the redesign of the emergency department at AZ Oostende and evaluates the implementation of a modular, unit-based organizational model aimed at improving patient flow, workload distribution, and adaptability without major physical expansion.MethodsFollowing the 2025 centralization of all emergency services at AZ Oostende, the ED layout and operational model were redesigned. Traditional pathology-based zoning was replaced with three mixed-pathology care units staffed by decentralized, co-located teams. Multifunctional treatment rooms and real-time patient allocation were introduced to allow flexible capacity management during daily and seasonal fluctuations in demand.ResultsEarly observations indicate improvements in operational performance, including shorter patient throughput times, reduced crowding scores, higher staff and patient satisfaction, and more balanced staff workloads.ConclusionBy integrating architectural redesign with operational changes, the new modular ED model provides a resilient and adaptable framework for emergency care. This approach improves patient flow, team collaboration, and capacity management while maintaining continuous 24-hour operations and may serve as a future-oriented template for European emergency departments.Keywords: Emergency department design, Patient flow, Staff well-being, Modular architecture, Unit-based model

Emergency department (ED) design is under increasing pressure due to a growing constellation of demands: increasing patient volumes, evolving care delivery models, shifting policy landscapes, within dwindling budgets [1, 2]. Modern EDs must now optimize for flexibility, surge capacity, efficiency, technology integration, patient experience, and staff well-being - often all at once [1, 3]. This complexity calls for a fundamental rethinking of how we approach ED infrastructure and workflow design [2].

At hospitals Oostende, situated in a coastal Belgian middle sized city, the recent centralization of all emergency services at one campus represented an ambitious and timely response to these pressures. Completed in February 2025, the project consolidated the entire emergency care at the AZ Damiaan campus, serving an annual caseload of over 50.000 patients. With peak daily volumes reaching up to 200 patients, the ED had to be rethought not only to handle increased demands but also to be implemented without interrupting day-to-day operations [4, 5].

In Belgium, many EDs are still designed around pathology-specific zones, where patients are directed to care areas based on the presenting complaint: internal medicine, surgery, orthopaedics, and so on. While seeming logical from a clinical organization point of view, this traditional approach often results in structural inefficiencies and contributes to operational bottlenecks [1, 5, 6].

A key concern is imbalance in workload and cognitive demand. Physicians in zones like internal medicine are often faced with a disproportionately high intellectual burden, managing complex diagnostic cases in high density [3, 5]. This not only affects provider performance and well-being but also undermines equitable workload distribution across the team. Nurses too, are affected by these uneven dynamics. All these elements leading to a slower flow [3].

This structural rigidity is especially problematic given seasonal variation in case mix. In AZ Oostende, patient presentations fluctuate significantly: winter brings infectious disease surges, while the summer tourist season leads to increased trauma, dehydration, and heat-related illness [4]. Fixed zones do not allow the department to reallocate capacity fluidly in response to such changing demands [3, 5]. In addition, the need to manage significant rise in patient volumes within a largely unchanged spatial footprint was impossible. Simply.

expanding square meters was not feasible [2]. Therefore, the solution was a smarter design and workflow architecture, with minimal physical enlargement. To address these constraints, we implemented a modular, unit-based ED model, organized around three mixed-pathology care units (Fig. 1) [2]. Rather than rigidly separating patients by complaint, diagnosis or specialty, each unit is equipped and staffed to handle a broad range of conditions and acuity levels. This model allows the department to dynamically allocate patients based on real-time factors such as workload, staffing, and unit availability [1, 3].

Unit-based ED model, AZ Oostende

Each care unit is staffed by a decentralized team, with nurses and physicians co-located in dedicated unit offices [3]. This spatial and organizational alignment fosters more direct communication, faster decision-making, and stronger team cohesion [2, 3]. Patient allocation is managed actively: triage nurses and an allocated triage physician assess the clinical urgency and current unit capacity to ensure balanced patient distribution [4, 5]. For instance, when a Mobile Emergency Group (MUG) team is out handling a critical patient, the system adjusts patient flow to other units to avoid pressure buildup [4].

A core enabler of this model is the implementation of multifunctional treatment rooms [1, 3]. Every treatment space includes monitoring capabilities, oxygen outlets, procedural lighting, and the ability to manage a range of clinical needs. By equipping all treatment rooms equally and using mobile procedure carts rather than fixed-function rooms (e.g., dedicated “suture rooms”), the ED can flexibly deliver any type of care in any location [1, 2]. This reduces unnecessary patient transfers, supports workflow standardization, and optimizes throughput. Importantly, this modular architecture also facilitates future scalability. If demand increases, it is far simpler and more cost-effective to add a new unit than to expand or reconfigure fixed specialty zones [2]. This flexibility makes the design resilient and future-proof, adaptable to nighttime downsizing, evolving clinical practice and growth [2, 3]. Since the implementation of the new design, early data and qualitative feedback suggest meaningful improvements despite the increased number of patients. Key outcomes include among others shorter overall patient throughput times, reduced door-to-imaging intervals, higher patient satisfaction ratings (Fig. 2), lower CEDOCS (Crowding Emergency Department Occupancy Score) values (Fig. 3.) While these improvements coincided with other initiatives, such as the introduction of medical triage and flow managers, the unit-based model appears to play a central role [2, 3]. Another improvement was the expansion of our medical staffing to ensure that all units are adequately staffed. This increase in staffing was also accompanied by a transition from a triage-based emergency department to a full-service–oriented emergency department. Staff consistently report better collaboration, a more balanced workload, and improved operational flow. The increased flexibility also enhanced the ED’s resilience during unexpected surges and high-occupancy periods [2, 3, 5].

Transforming a high-volume ED while maintaining continuous operations presents multiple logistical and cultural challenges [4]. At AZ Oostende, phased implementation, clear internal communication, and tight coordination with clinical leadership were crucial [4, 5]. The transition to decentralized teamwork required training and cultural adaptation. The success of real-time patient distribution depends on situational awareness and rapid communication among triage staff and unit teams [3, 5]. These elements require ongoing education and feedback loops to remain effective [3].

The redesigned emergency department at AZ Oostende demonstrates a forward-looking, resilient, and patient-centered approach to emergency care [2]. By moving away from outdated pathology-specific zoning and embracing modular, mixed-pathology units, the department has improved operational efficiency, team dynamics, and adaptability while handling increased patient volumes [2, 3]. This flexible infrastructure allows for scalable growth, ensuring that future expansions can occur unit by unit, rather than through costly and disruptive renovations [2]. As emergency medicine is to evolve, the Oostende model offers a compelling, future-proof blueprint for the next generation of ED design [1–3, 6]. Given the widespread problem of long waiting times in many EDs and seasonal variation, there is a strong case made for reconsidering ED architecture as a structural driver of patient flow [2, 5]. We believe that similar models can contribute meaningfully to reducing delays and improving the overall patient experience, not only through operational strategies, but by rethinking the very space in which emergency care is delivered [1–3].
