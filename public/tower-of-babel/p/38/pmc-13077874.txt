Generative AI adoption and ethical perceptions: a comparative study of medical and non-medical researchers in Chinese universities
Authors: Janak L. Pathak, Kaiyue Zhang, Hao Jiang, Yuxin Zhang, Wanying Zhang, Haiyan Wang, Yongxin Mo, Fengping Liu, Zhijun Tian, Lihong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe integration of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) into academia presents profound ethical challenges to research integrity. Disciplinary norms likely shape engagement with this technology, yet a comparative analysis between medical and non-medical research cultures is lacking. This study examines how this critical divide influences the adoption of GenAI, the development of ethically sensitive behaviors, and perceptions of oversight in Chinese universities.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was administered to 5,731 active researchers (including postdoctoral fellows and doctoral, graduate, and undergraduate students) from 48 universities, categorized as medical (n = 1,935) or non-medical researchers (n = 3,796). The instrument measured the frequency of GenAI adoption, integrity awareness, self-reported behaviors (including data fabrication/falsification), and ethical attitudes. Analyses used the chi-square test and independent-samples t-tests.ResultsNon-medical researchers reported higher GenAI adoption (57.0% vs. 45.6%, p<.001), while medical researchers showed greater integrity awareness (83.1% vs. 76.1%, p<.001). Critically, non-medical researchers reported using GenAI for data fabrication/falsification more frequently (27.0% vs. 10.9%, p < .001). The medical researchers reported greater efficiency benefits (79.8% vs. 57.1%, p < .001) but held stricter views on the use of unverified AI-generated text (75.2% vs. 65.3%, p < .001). Non-medical researchers expressed greater confidence in detecting misconduct (71.7% vs. 58.8%, p<.001).ConclusionsThere are two very different approaches to using AI in research, and this creates a key ethical challenge. Medical researchers are more cautious, working within strict rules but having less confidence in current oversight, which makes patient safety their top concern. In contrast, researchers in other fields often adopt AI more readily and report engaging in riskier practices. Because of this major divide, a single, universal policy for AI ethics is inadequate. Instead, our findings point to the value of distinct guidelines tailored to each context: for medical research, the focus must be on transparency and protecting patients; for non-medical fields, the priorities should be tracking where content comes from and establishing realistic oversight. This flexible, principle-based strategy is essential for protecting the integrity of research across all disciplines.

Introduction

The proliferation of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) represents a transformative and disruptive force in academia, offering unprecedented tools for accelerating scientific discovery and scholarly communication while simultaneously posing fundamental threats to the core ethical principles that underpin research integrity [1–3]. Research integrity, encompassing the adherence to values of honesty, accountability, transparency, and rigor throughout the research process, is the bedrock of scientific credibility, public trust, and the self-correcting nature of science [4–6]. GenAI directly and profoundly challenges these tenets by blurring concepts of authorship and originality, enabling sophisticated fabrication of text, data, and imagery, and complicating traditional lines of accountability and oversight [7–9].

Current ethical debates and institutional responses, such as publisher policies on AI disclosure, often proceed from a position of presumed uniformity [10, 11]. However, the ethical implications and practical adoption of GenAI are not uniform; they are critically mediated by disciplinary context, epistemological foundations, and methodological practices [12, 13]. A significant and ethically meaningful divide likely exists between medical research and other academic fields. Medical research operates within a uniquely stringent and historically grounded ethical ecosystem. It is governed by foundational international codes, such as the Declaration of Helsinki, national regulations, and oversight by institutional review boards (IRBs) [14–16], and for clinician-researchers and professional licensing bodies. This framework is driven by patient safety imperatives and subjects research to intense multi-level scrutiny [16]. The stakes of misconduct are perceived as uniquely high, involving direct potential harm to human participants and public health. Consequently, ethics training is often more formalized and integrated into medical research curricula [17].

In contrast, while equally committed to intellectual honesty, non-medical disciplines (e.g., humanities, engineering, social sciences) may operate under different normative pressures, incentive structures, methodological vulnerabilities (e.g., reliance on textual argument rather than reproducible experiments), and cultural understandings of authorship and plagiarism [10, 18]. The perception of harm from misconduct may be more diffuse, relating to the erosion of scholarly trust rather than immediate physical risk.

Despite its importance for developing nuanced governance, a direct comparative bioethics analysis of how medical and non-medical researchers perceive, adopt, and ethically navigate GenAI is lacking. Most discussions remain generic or siloed within single fields. This study addresses this critical gap by empirically investigating the differential adoption, self-reported behaviors, and ethical perceptions of GenAI between these two broad groups within Chinese universities. By highlighting this dichotomy, we aim to provide an evidence-based argument for moving beyond unitary ethical policies towards the development of nuanced, discipline-sensitive frameworks that can more effectively safeguard integrity while promoting responsible innovation.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional online survey of academic professionals actively engaged in research at Chinese universities and institutes over a 12-week period (November 18, 2024 – February 4, 2025). The questionnaire was developed by integrating a theoretical framework for analyzing ethical challenges in science (informed by the work of Resnik, D.B. on research integrity and technology adoption) with validated instruments from Lund University’s Department of Medical Ethics [12, 13].

The target population, researchers (including postdoctoral fellows and doctaral, graduate, and undergraduate students) involved in ongoing research, was recruited using a multi-pronged strategy. This included professional association mailing lists, direct email outreach to department heads at approximately 60 major universities, and advertisements within discipline-specific forums on WeChat, a ubiquitous Chinese social media and professional networking platform.

Individuals who accessed the survey link were presented with a detailed information sheet and provided informed consent. Participation required meeting two inclusion criteria: (1) current affiliation with a Chinese university or research institute, and (2) active involvement in research as a principal investigator, collaborator, or doctoral candidate.

Due to the open and multi-channel recruitment strategy (inclusive of snowball sampling), a formal response rate could not be calculated, as a definitive denominator of all invited individuals was unattainable. An initial pool of 6,740 survey accesses was recorded. Data quality controls were applied to identify and remove non-serious responses, duplicates, and logically inconsistent submissions.

Survey instrument development and measures

The survey instrument was developed through a multi-stage, rigorous process:

Literature review and item generation: A comprehensive review identified key constructs: GenAI adoption, research integrity knowledge, ethical attitudes, and behavioral intentions. Items were adapted from established research misconduct instruments and tailored to the GenAI context based on emerging ethical frameworks [12, 13].

Translation and cultural adaptation: The English-language draft was translated into Mandarin Chinese by two independent bilingual researchers. A third researcher synthesized the translations. The Chinese version was then back-translated into English to check for conceptual equivalence. A panel of five senior academics from medical and non-medical fields reviewed the draft for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness.

Pilot testing: The refined survey was piloted with 50 researchers (25 medical, 25 non-medical). Cognitive debriefing interviews were conducted to assess participants' interpretations of the items. Internal consistency (Cronbach's α) was calculated for the attitude scales. Minor adjustments to wording were made based on feedback.

The final survey instrument assessed five primary domains (Table 1). Domain 1 captured demographics and professional background, including discipline, academic role, research experience, and institutional type. Domain 2, GenAI adoption and familiarity, measured the frequency of use across various research tasks using a 5-point scale from “never” to “always”; a binary “frequent use” variable was derived by combining “often” and “always” responses. Domain 3 evaluated the understanding of research integrity through self-reported knowledge of core concepts and ratings of their importance. Domain 4 gathered data on self-reported GenAI-assisted research behaviors via specific binary (yes/no) items; to ensure clarity and minimize bias, sensitive questions were phrased precisely, for example, one key item stated: “in your research, have you ever used generative AI to generate, alter, or fabricate experimental or survey data (including images, figures, or datasets) with the intent to misrepresent findings?” Other behaviors covered included text drafting, idea generation, and proofreading. Finally, domain 5 measured ethical attitudes and perceptions using 5-point Likert scales, capturing perceived benefits, ethical judgments, accountability, oversight perceptions, and descriptive norms related to misconduct; this domain demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.809).

Ethical considerations

No financial or material incentives were offered for participation in the survey. This study received ethical approval from the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University ethics committee (approval no: GCXYJ2025021402). The first page of the online survey presented comprehensive informed consent information. Participation was voluntary and anonymous; no personally identifiable information was collected. Implied consent was obtained by proceeding with the survey after reading the information sheet.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were first calculated to summarize the sample characteristics and key study variables. The primary analysis compared outcomes between the medical and non-medical researcher groups.

For categorical outcomes (e.g., proportion of frequent GenAI users, percentage reporting specific behaviors), group differences were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) tests. For continuous outcomes (i.e., composite scores for GenAI-assisted behaviors and ethical attitudes), group differences were evaluated using independent samples t-tests. Prior to conducting t-tests, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were examined. Homogeneity of variance was formally tested using Levene’s test. For the composite ethical attitude score, variances were unequal (F = 287.5, p < .001); therefore, the t-test results, which do not assume equal variances, are reported. For all inferential analyses, a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

After applying quality controls, 5,731 valid and complete responses were retained for analysis, representing a completion rate of 85.0% from the initial 6,740 accesses. For the primary analysis of this manuscript, participants were categorized into two mutually exclusive groups based on their self-reported primary discipline. The first group, designated as medical researchers (n = 1,935, 33.8% of the sample), included researchers from clinical medicine, basic medical sciences, dentistry, pharmacy, nursing, and public health. The second group, designated as non-medical researchers (n = 3,796, 66.2%), was an aggregated category comprising researchers from the humanities (e.g., philosophy, history, literature), social sciences (e.g., economics, law, education, management), natural sciences (e.g., physics, chemistry, biology), engineering, and agriculture.

The demographic and professional characteristics of the final sample, divided by primary category, are presented in Table 2. The sample was nearly gender-balanced overall, with a slightly higher proportion of females in both groups. The non-medical group included a diverse range of disciplines, with engineering and social sciences as the largest sub-components.

Ethical attitudes and perceptions of oversight

Attitudinal differences were complex and revealing (Table 5). A far greater proportion of medical researchers (79.8%) endorsed the efficiency benefits of GenAI compared to non-medical researchers (57.1%). Interestingly, non-medical researchers (71.7%) expressed significantly greater confidence than medical researchers (58.8%) in detecting AI-related misconduct at their institutions. The most striking difference was in perceived detectability: non-medical researchers expressed 12.9% greater confidence that AI-related misconduct would be easily detected at their institution.

Composite score comparisons

Independent-samples t-tests on composite scores supported the narrative of distinct profiles and were corrected for unequal variances where necessary (Table 6). The mean score for GenAI-assisted research behaviors was significantly higher for non-medical researchers than for medical researchers (M = 2.71, SD = 1.35 vs. M = 2.49, SD = 1.22), t(5729) = 5.89, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.17. Conversely, the mean composite ethical attitude score was significantly lower for medical researchers (M = 26.50, SD = 4.21) compared to non-medical researchers (M = 27.42, SD = 5.55). Due to a significant Levene’s test indicating unequal variances (F = 287.5, p < .001), the adjusted t-statistic is reported: t(4562.7) = 6.98, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.19. This indicates that despite their higher formal awareness and stricter views on text, their overall attitudinal posture was relatively more permissive.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed, comparative bioethics mapping of the landscape of GenAI use among academics in China, highlighting a profound and significant dichotomy between medical and non-medical researchers. The findings extend beyond mere adoption rates to reveal contrasting ethical vulnerabilities, perceptions of oversight, and potential points of tension between efficiency gains and integrity norms.

The most striking divergence observed in our study is the markedly higher self-report of GenAI-involved data fabrication or falsification among non-medical researchers (27.0%) compared to their medical counterparts (10.9%). The most straightforward interpretation of this disparity is that it reflects a genuine, discipline-shaped difference in ethical vulnerability. Medical research operates within a robust governance ecosystem characterized by exacting IRB protocols, a historical awareness of scandals linked to patient harm, and a culture in which data fraud carries direct legal and professional accountability [16, 17]. This institutional infrastructure plausibly serves as a more potent deterrent. Conversely, the higher rate in non-medical fields may stem from differing norms regarding data construction, potentially lower perceived auditability in non-experimental research, and incentive structures where the tangible consequences of fraud are perceived as less immediate [19–21]. However, this interpretive tension must be explicitly acknowledged. The lower self-report among medical researchers could also be influenced by a heightened sensitivity to social desirability bias or fear-based underreporting. Even within an anonymous survey, researchers operating in a field with severe real-world accountability may perceive greater subconscious or conscious risk in acknowledging misconduct. Therefore, while the disparity likely signals a real differential in ethical risk profiles shaped by disciplinary context, the absolute self-reported rates must be interpreted with caution. They may not fully capture the underlying behavior in either group, underscoring the inherent challenge of measuring sensitive practices across diverse research cultures.

The medical researcher profile presents a nuanced and potentially precarious ethical picture: high formal integrity awareness coupled with the strongest perception of utility and the most permissive overall attitude scores. This suggests a pronounced ethics-utility tension. The intense pressure for productivity in clinical and translational research [17], driven by funding cycles and promotion metrics, may create an environment in which the efficiency gains of GenAI are valued so highly that they strain adherence to precautionary principles. Their significantly lower confidence in detecting AI-assisted misconduct is ethically telling. It may reflect a more realistic, perhaps jaded, appraisal of the difficulty in uncovering sophisticated fraud within complex, multi-center clinical trials or bioinformatic datasets [14]. This perceived oversight weakness represents a critical systemic vulnerability that could be exploited by bad actors.

The stark divergence in perceived detectability is a cornerstone finding for ethics governance. Non-medical researchers’ relative optimism about institutional oversight may lead to complacency among researchers and administrators, creating a false sense of security. The medical researchers’ pessimism may be more realistic, but it could erode trust in the system, and needs to be addressed through transparent and robust verification mechanisms.

From a bioethics and policy perspective, these results argue decisively against the feasibility and effectiveness of a one-size-fits-all approach to AI governance in academia. For medical research, ethical guidelines could usefully extend beyond simplistic authorship disclosure to emphasize: (1) Transparency and provenance: Clear documentation of how GenAI was used in data analysis, including prompts and validation steps. (2) Vigilance against algorithmic bias: Proactive steps to audit AI tools for biases that could exacerbate health disparities when used in research. (3) Preservation of the patient safety paradigm: This refers to the foundational bioethical principle that the welfare of the research participant is paramount. It requires a rigorous assessment of how AI-assisted research processes affect the risk-benefit calculus for human participants, in line with the spirit of the Declaration of Helsinki [14–16].

For non-medical research, ethical frameworks must prioritize different interventions: (1) Combating fabrication and plagiarism: intensive education on the provenance of AI-generated text and data, coupled with the development and adoption of reliable detection tools tailored to non-STEM outputs. (2) Addressing distinct incentive structures: academic institutions must re-evaluate promotion and funding metrics that may inadvertently reward volume over veracity in fields where GenAI can mass-produce plausible but superficial work.

A common, reinforcing imperative for both domains is the unequivocal reinforcement of ultimate human accountability. The broad agreement on shared co-author responsibility is a positive foundation that must be codified in journal submission systems, institutional policies, and training programs.

Limitations and future research

This study has limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference about how disciplinary norms shape behavior. Self-reported data on sensitive topics, even when anonymized and carefully worded, are susceptible to bias. In addition, the high rate of reported ‘fabrication/falsification’ among non-medical researchers may partly reflect interpretive variation: in some disciplines (e.g., computer science, engineering, quantitative social sciences), generating synthetic or simulated data is a routine and ethically legitimate practice, which could have influenced how respondents understood the survey item. The aggregation of highly diverse fields into a single ‘non-medical’ category obscures important intra-group variations; this was a necessary analytical trade-off for the primary comparison, but should be unpacked in future work. The Chinese context, with its unique research assessment systems and cultural factors, may limit the direct generalizability of findings to other national settings. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs to track behavioral evolution, qualitative methods to explore the motivations behind the observed differences (especially the high rate of data fabrication reports), and comparative studies across different national research ethics governance regimes. A critical next step will be interdisciplinary efforts to develop and validate effective, discipline-specific tools for detecting AI-generated content and data manipulation.

Conclusion

This large-scale comparative study demonstrates that medical and non-medical researchers occupy distinct ethical landscapes shaped by their disciplinary epistemologies, norms, and perceived risks regarding Generative AI. Medical researchers, while socialized within a strong bioethics framework, navigate a tension between high utility and cautious oversight, revealing a specific vulnerability. Non-medical researchers are more prolific adopters but exhibit higher rates of self-reported ethically questionable behaviors, coupled with an overconfidence in oversight that masks significant risk.

Safeguarding research integrity in the age of AI, therefore, suggests the need for policy responses that are not uniform but rather principle-based and context-sensitive. We suggest considering the parallel development of two complementary streams of ethical guidelines, along with the integration of GenAI literacy into research-related teaching and training modules, and the establishment of verification standards: one stream tailored to the patient-centric, high-stakes, and heavily regulated environment of medical research; another adapted to another adapted to the diverse methodological, normative, and incentive landscapes of non-medical scholarship. Such a nuanced, evidence-based, and ethically-grounded approach may be key to harnessing the innovative potential of GenAI while steadfastly upholding the foundational values of scholarly integrity across all academic disciplines.
