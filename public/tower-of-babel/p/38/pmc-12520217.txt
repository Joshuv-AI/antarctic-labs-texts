Portal cavernoma cholangiopathy: A systematic review of current understanding, clinical significance, and management
Authors: Elisa Pinto, Laure Elkrief, Virginia Hernández-Gea, Alberto Busatto, Kymentie Ferdinande, Alberto Zanetto, Akash Shukla, Marco Senzolo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Portal cavernoma cholangiopathy (PCC) is a rare condition characterized by abnormalities of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts and the gallbladder, resulting from long-standing chronic portal vein thrombosis (cPVT). These vascular changes often occur in the setting of non-cirrhotic, non-neoplastic portal hypertension. Its prevalence among cPVT patients ranges from 4% to 92%, with an unweighted average of 45.2%, increasing to 67.0% in studies using magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). Although most patients remain asymptomatic, about 25% develop symptoms. Diagnosis mainly depends on imaging, with MRCP being the most accurate choice. The classification proposed by Llop and colleagues provides a useful framework by integrating anatomical involvement with the severity of biliary disease. However, combining this classification with the pathophysiological one could support a more personalized treatment approach. Management depends on symptom presentation. Asymptomatic patients are managed conservatively. Symptomatic cases may require endoscopic interventions; plastic stents are most commonly used, although they have around a 30% risk of hemobilia and a high re-intervention rate of ~50%. Interventional radiology techniques, particularly portal vein recanalization with transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), exhibit high technical success rates (median 91%) and patency rates (median 81.8%). Surgical portosystemic shunts remain a key option for a subgroup of patients, with about 65% achieving symptom resolution after decompression alone, whereas liver transplantation is rarely indicated. Given the rarity of this condition and the limited quality of existing studies, we conducted this systematic review to provide a comprehensive overview of the current literature and to promote a more standardized approach to managing PCC. This review aims to fill knowledge gaps and enhance consistency in treatment strategies for this complex condition.

INTRODUCTION

Portal cavernoma cholangiopathy (PCC) is a condition characterized by abnormalities in the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts and gallbladder due to chronic portal hypertension of prolonged duration in chronic non-cirrhotic/non-neoplastic portal vein thrombosis (cPVT) and portal cavernoma (PC).1–3 These abnormalities, identified in over 80%–100% of patients with cPVT, are thought to be the result of the compression and distortion of bile ducts by collateral veins, although mechanistic studies are lacking. While most patients remain asymptomatic, some may present with portal hypertension (PH), whereas others develop biliary complications.4–7 Diagnosis combines imaging techniques to capture vascular and biliary system changes. Ultrasound (US) and computed tomography (CT) are commonly used initially, while magnetic resonance imaging, combining angiography (MRA) and cholangiography (MRCP), provides a detailed, non-invasive evaluation of both the biliary and vascular structures, making it particularly useful for diagnosis. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a therapeutic tool; it could also add diagnostic information, especially valuable in cases requiring stenting for biliary decompression.8–10

Treatment strategies are guided by symptom severity. 11 Asymptomatic patients are typically managed conservatively with regular monitoring, while symptomatic cases may require endoscopic stenting to relieve biliary obstruction. For patients with severe or refractory symptoms, surgical options, including shunt procedures with or without hepatic-jejunostomy, if feasible, or liver transplantation, may be considered.

While increasing the number of PCC cases being managed, the available literature on this topic must be updated. It predominantly consists of case reports and case series, underscoring a significant gap in up-to-date research and data in the current literature. This indicates an urgent need for further studies and updated reviews to enhance our understanding and management of this condition.

Therefore, this systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current literature on PCC and highlight the unmet needs that still characterize this condition. We will examine the available evidence on its definition, epidemiology, classification, diagnostic approaches, disease progression, and treatment strategies.

METHODS

This study was conducted following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.

Search strategy and study selection

Data for our systematic review were retrieved exclusively from PubMed and Embase using the MeSH terms ((portal biliopathy) OR (portal cholangiopathy) OR (pseudosclerosing cholangitis) OR (pseudolcholangiocaRcinoma) OR (portal hypertensive cholangiopathy) OR (cholangiopathy associated with portal hypertension) OR (Portal cavernoma-associated cholangiopathy)) AND ((“2003/01/01”[Date – MeSH]: “2025/02/28”[Date – MeSH])) and relevant citations were included in the preparation of the manuscript. We did not include grey literature.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria and data extraction

Only publications written in English were included. We considered only studies involving non-cirrhotic patients. More criteria were applied based on the topic (Figure 1). Eligibility criteria for study inclusion were defined a priori, and after removal of duplicate citations 2 independent reviewers (Elisa Pinto and Alberto Busatto) screened titles, abstracts, and full texts to determine study eligibility. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Eligible studies were independently evaluated by 2 investigators, who collected the required data using predefined extraction forms. These included the nomenclature, the type of radiological lesions, the imaging modalities used for diagnosis, whether a biopsy was performed, the classification employed, the clinical presentation and course, the treatment used and its complications, and the outcomes.

It is essential to highlight that each of these studies focuses on different aims, and we extrapolated collateral data that we were interested in.

NOMENCLATURE AND DEFINITIONS

The concept of PCC has evolved since it was first described by Fraser and Brown in 1944. 12 Despite sporadic reports over the following decades, 13 its recognition grew in the late 1970s with further studies on common bile duct (CBD) varices. 14 Key advancements included Williams’s demonstration of choledochal varices in 1982 15 and the introduction of endoscopic management techniques, such as biliary stenting, in 1993. 16

Over time, varying terms were used for the same condition, often based on presumed pathogenesis, PH, or similar disorders (Figure 2). Terms like “pseudosclerosing cholangitis” 4 and “pseudocholangiocarcinoma sign”17,18 were used to describe biliary abnormalities that mimic primary sclerosing cholangitis or cholangiocarcinoma, despite not representing either condition. In addition, terms like “cholangiopathy associated with portal hypertension” 19 and “portal hypertensive biliopathy” 20 were introduced to describe biliary alterations in patients with PH. On the other hand, “Portal cavernoma-associated cholangiopathy” 21 was used to highlight the association with the presence of a PC.

Timeline showing the evolution of PCC nomenclature and definitions over time. Abbreviations: PB, portal biliopathy; PCC, portal cavernoma cholangiopathy.

However, these terms did not include a precise diagnostic definition. The first definition was the one of Sarin and Bhatia, 22 who coined “portal biliopathy” to describe biliary and gallbladder wall abnormalities in patients with PH, although this lacked specificity. Later, Dhiman et al., 23 in 2007, defined “portal hypertensive biliopathy” as abnormalities of the entire biliary tree, including both intrahepatic and extrahepatic ducts and the gallbladder in patients with PH. In 2014, Dhiman et al. 24 questioned the necessity of PH in defining PCC. They proposed “portal cavernoma cholangiopathy” to describe extrahepatic biliary abnormalities, with or without intrahepatic involvement, in non-cirrhotic patients with longstanding PVT and no alternative biliary disease.

Despite the 2014 consensus, 24 a review of studies from the past decade shows that “PCC” is not the only term used (53.8%), and “portal biliopathy” and other terms continue to appear in the literature. This highlights the ongoing need for further uniformity in terminology in this field. In this review, we have chosen to use the term PCC, as it is the only term agreed upon after the only consensus nomenclature available so far, and as the term endorsed by the recent European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) clinical practice guidelines on vascular liver diseases (in press), which recommend this term.

ANATOMY AND PATHOGENESIS

When the portal vein becomes obstructed, a network of collateral veins often forms to bypass the blockage, resulting in a portal cavernoma. These collateral vessels can develop around the extrahepatic bile ducts, gallbladder, and cystic duct, potentially compressing the bile ducts and impairing bile flow.

An important contributing mechanism in PCC pathophysiology is the particular vulnerability of the common bile duct to extrinsic compression by the collateral venous network formed during cavernous transformation. Two critical venous networks (Figure 3), the epi-choledochal venous plexus of Saint (which encircles the bile duct) and the paracholedochal plexus of Petren (which runs alongside the CBD), become dilated in response to portal vein obstruction. These plexuses and other peribiliary collaterals are hypothesized to exert pressure on the bile ducts, potentially contributing to strictures and segmental dilations frequently observed in cholangiographic imaging of PCC patients. 25

In addition to mechanical compression, a reduction in blood flow due to altered vascular supply may also play a role.26,27 The portal vein, via its small peribiliary branches, is a key source of bile duct perfusion. 28 When the portal vein is replaced by collateral vessels, this compromised blood flow can lead to ischemia-induced necrosis and fibrosis of the bile ducts, further exacerbating biliary strictures.24,25,29,30

As discussed in the following chapters, 3 types of PCC have been proposed based on the presumed predominant mechanism: varicoid (primarily compressive), fibrotic (primarily ischemic), and mixed. Nevertheless, these pathophysiological classifications remain hypothetical and require validation through mechanistic studies. 1

Moreover, histopathological findings in PCC often reveal ischemic injury, fibrosis, and chronic inflammation.31,32

EPIDEMIOLOGY

The incidence of PCC varies across studies and populations. According to the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA), it has been reported in ~0.5%–1% of patients with cPVT. 33

Although we were able to extract meaningful prevalence data from the existing literature, it is essential to highlight that large-scale, population-based epidemiological studies are lacking. The absence of robust prevalence studies not only limits our understanding of the burden of PCC but also poses a significant challenge to fully characterizing the natural history of the disease, as discussed in subsequent chapters.

Retrospective studies involving patients with cPVT report widely variable prevalence estimates of PCC, mainly due to methodological heterogeneity. These variations are influenced by factors such as the imaging modality employed, the presence or absence of symptoms, and the timing of diagnosis. Moreover, the reported prevalence of PCC is highly dependent on the diagnostic modality. PCC often remains undiagnosed in asymptomatic patients unless high-resolution imaging techniques like MRCP or ERCP are utilized—modalities significantly more sensitive than ultrasound or CT in detecting subtle biliary changes. Indeed, ERCP or MRCP studies have documented PCC-related abnormalities in 80%–100% of patients with cPVT.4–7

In our review of studies including ≥50 patients with portal vein thrombosis (Table 1), we calculated the proportion of patients with PCC among those with cPVT for each study. We then derived the average prevalence across these studies, which was 45.2%, with a broad range from 4% to 92%. Notably, when limiting the analysis to studies using MRCP or ERCP, the average reported prevalence rose to 67.0% (range 31.7%–92%). The median age of presentation was 36 years, with a male predominance of 63%.

DIAGNOSIS

Accurate diagnosis of PCC relies on imaging studies capable of detecting both cPVT and the characteristic biliary tract abnormalities.

As illustrated in Figure 4, PCC-associated lesions include both biliary and vascular abnormalities. Biliary findings include acute CBD angulation (“kinking”) and scalloped or wavy duct contours on MRCP due to venous compression. 45 Vascular abnormalities include pericholecystic varices in 62% of cases (28%–100%), paracholedochal varices in 95% (80%–100%), and pericholedochal varices in 79% (68%–90%).

Our systematic analysis identified 21 studies evaluating PCC diagnosis. However, these studies exhibit notable heterogeneity in design, with limited consistency in reporting diagnostic modalities or applying a standardized classification of lesions.

Reported radiological features include CBD stenosis or strictures [62.6% (2%–100%)], intrahepatic dilation [71.6% (42%–100%)], angulation [51.1% (3%–100%)], CBD narrowing [39.9% (27.8%–52%)], and irregular contour [75.65% (55%–100%)]. Despite these observations, only a minority of studies explicitly classify these abnormalities within a uniform diagnostic framework. Furthermore, it is important to note that the wide percentage ranges reflect the significant heterogeneity among the studies.

To explore variability across study designs, we conducted a secondary analysis of non-treatment-focused studies (Figure 5). For each study, we calculated the proportion of patients with stenosis or CBD stones relative to the total number of patients included, and then averaged these proportions across all studies. This analysis showed an average stenosis rate of 80% and a CBD stone rate of 33.8% in studies, no-treatment focused,1,2,9,21,31,32,36,39–41,46,47,48 and an average stenosis rate of 66.4% in studies considering both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients.1,9,32,38,47

Imaging modalities

US is commonly used as the first-line imaging modality and is also used for follow-up. However, its sensitivity is limited with a 54% discrepancy between US and ERCP findings. 8 CT provides more detailed anatomical information, particularly in assessing the extent of the thrombosis, presence, and type of collateral circulation and liver parenchyma. The sensitivity and specificity for biliary tract evaluation are comparable between CT and MRI, 9 but both are less accurate than MRCP.10,38 MRCP, often combined with MR angiography, is considered the preferred non-invasive modality for PCC diagnosis. In PCC-focused studies, 85%–90% of diagnoses were made using MRI-based techniques. MRCP matches ERCP in diagnostic accuracy but avoids the procedural risks of invasive intervention. Although ERCP remains essential in therapeutic studies (used in 100% of such cases), its role as a purely diagnostic tool is more limited. Based on available data, ERCP was used solely for diagnostic purposes in ~28% of cases.

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) has gained attention in recent years, offering high-resolution imaging capable of detecting early or subtle changes not easily visualized by other imaging techniques.48,49

Histopathology

Liver biopsy plays a limited but significant role in the evaluation of PCC, primarily to exclude alternative diagnoses. However, it is rarely performed in individuals with severe disease due to the high risk of bleeding complications associated with PH and peribiliary varices. When performed, histological features commonly described in PCC include dilated multiple portal venous channels (72%), thickening of the hepatic artery (70%), aberrant vascular channels around the portal tracts (54%), elastosis of the portal veins (50%), and bile ductular reaction (44%). 31

Shin et al. 1 categorized PCC into 3 types: varicoid, fibrotic, and mixed. Varicoid irregularities present as smooth indentations, suggesting dilation of the paracholedochal plexus (cavernous transformation). In the fibrotic type, smooth localized strictures are observed at the level of common bile duct wall thickening. The thickened part of the wall exhibits delayed increased enhancement on dynamic 3D gradient echo images during MRCP, indicating an ischemic etiology. In the mixed type, irregular wall contour and thickening are evident, but the thickened wall does not show delayed increased enhancement, suggesting a non-ischemic or inflammatory cause.

Llop et al. 38 proposed the only classification system based on the severity of the disease, comprising 3 grades that correlate with clinical decision-making.

Premkumar and Dhiman 30 recently proposed a new clinical classification that divides patients into symptomatic and asymptomatic groups. Symptomatic patients are further classified based on the presence of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis, cholangitis or choledocholithiasis, vascular compression, and strictures categorized as fibrotic types.

Despite the availability of these systems, our literature review revealed limited adoption. Among reviewed studies, only 7 explicitly reported using a classification system: 3 employed the Chandra et al. classification,31,34,51 only 1 employed the Llop et al. system, 40 and 3 employed both.39,52,53

According to the 2014 consensus, 24 the classification proposed by Llop et al. 38 should be preferentially used, as it is based on disease severity (a criterion helpful for therapeutic decisions) and has been prospectively validated. However, systems like those of Shin et al. 1 and Chandra et al., 50 which incorporate pathophysiological mechanisms and anatomical patterns, can provide complementary insights, especially in differentiating the nature of biliary abnormalities. Combining a severity-based system with one that reflects underlying mechanisms may offer a more comprehensive framework, improve clinical evaluation, and support more personalized, evidence-based treatments.

NATURAL HISTORY

The 2014 consensus already highlighted the limited availability of data on the progression and natural history of PCC, largely due to the absence of prospective studies on this subject.

The only 2 prospective studies that provide significant data regarding the natural history of the disease and offer partial answers to these questions are those by Llop et al. 38 and Sarma et al. 40

In the Spanish study, 38 of 67 patients with cPVT, PCC appeared to be largely non-progressive. Only one patient with grades I–II lesions progressed to grade III over 43 months of follow-up. The main predictor of symptom development was stricture severity: grade III strictures showed a 41% positive and 100% negative predictive value for symptoms. However, the relatively limited follow-up raises uncertainty about whether grade III strictures may become symptomatic over longer periods. In the more recent Indian study, 40 the only risk factor for the development of PCC in patients with cPVT without cirrhosis was non-patency of the superior mesenteric vein. Neither age at diagnosis nor duration of treatment significantly influenced PCC development.

Although Shukla et al. 54 reported median times of 7 years from initial symptoms and 12 years from the first variceal bleed to symptomatic PCC, the exact timing of PCC onset remains unclear. Many patients have cPVT and subclinical biliary abnormalities that are detected only through targeted imaging, and the impact of PVT evolution on PCC is poorly defined.

CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

PCC presents a broad clinical spectrum (Figure 6), influenced by factors such as underlying predisposing conditions, age, disease severity, duration, and the presence of CBD and gallbladder stones. 20 However, most cases are asymptomatic, with ~65%–95% of patients not exhibiting symptoms related to biliary obstruction, although they often show biochemical abnormalities, such as elevated alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase levels. Although patients may be asymptomatic, cholangiography imaging usually reveals early changes, such as bile duct irregularities and varices. Based on studies not focused on treatment,1,2,9,21,31,32,36,39–41,47,48 the average of symptomatic patients is ~25%, with reported rates ranging from 7% to 43%. The most common symptoms include jaundice (75%), cholangitis (60%), and abdominal pain (60%). However, it is often difficult to determine whether these manifestations result from biliary obstruction or are secondary to liver dysfunction associated with PH. Esophageal and gastric varices occur in 68.3%, similar to patients with cPVT without PCC. These findings underscore 2 key unanswered questions: (i) What are the risk factors for the development of PCC in patients with cPVT? and (ii) What are the risk factors for symptomatic presentation?

TREATMENT

Current management strategies for PC are empirically driven, with substantial inter-center variation. No prospective studies have determined the optimal therapeutic approach—whether endoscopic, radiological, or surgical—nor established the ideal sequence of interventions.

Treatment for PCC is generally reserved for symptomatic patients with biliary complications and/or PH manifestations. In asymptomatic cases with only mild biochemical changes, they are typically managed conservatively through regular monitoring. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) may help alleviate mild symptoms and prevent recurrent cholangitis by reducing cholestasis, but the exact mechanism behind this effect is unknown, and no randomized controlled trials or large observational studies have specifically examined its role in PCC.20,51,55,56

For most symptomatic patients, portal decompression via portosystemic shunts (PSS)—either surgical or radiological—represents the only intervention capable of modifying the natural course of the disease. Both surgical and radiological PSS are the preferred approaches for mitigating elevated portal pressure in individuals suffering from cPVT and the development of PC.

However, surgery is only feasible in patients with shuntable veins and without liver dysfunction. 24

Importantly, in cases where ischemic injury to the biliary system is involved or calculi are present, mere portal decompression is often insufficient. These patients often require repeated endoscopic and surgical procedures to manage persistent or recurrent biliary tract obstruction. Such procedures may be necessary repeatedly over time, contributing to a high lifetime procedural burden. 11

Endoscopic treatment can be effective for symptomatic PCC, but it carries risks. Hemobilia is one of the most common complications.3,57 Among the total number of symptomatic PCC patients who underwent endoscopic treatment included in case series and case reports in the last 30 years in the literature, hemobilia occurred in 35 procedures out of 310 analyzed. In more than 90% of cases, bleeding happened after balloon swiping or stent replacement and was usually self-limiting or treated endoscopically in the same sitting with no need for hemodynamic resuscitation or repeated procedures. The risk primarily arises from venous collaterals around the bile duct, which can be mistaken for stones; therefore, balloon catheters are generally preferred over baskets for stone extraction.

Cholangitis is another frequent complication, likely related to recurrent biliary obstruction, the presence of stones, or altered biliary anatomy. Endoscopic ultrasound can aid in procedural planning by delineating the location of collateral vessels. 49 Although rare, serious events such as porto-biliary fistulas have been reported 57 and are usually managed successfully with covered stents and balloon tamponade. Importantly, no procedure-related mortality has been reported, although some patients did die from unrelated or disease-related complications, such as cholangitis or cirrhosis progression.

Interventional radiological procedures

Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt

Over the past 20 years, there has been a notable rise in the use of transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), which has shifted attention toward less invasive, faster, and safer methods that offer comparable success rates with fewer major adverse events.58,59

Over 2 decades of literature on intrahepatic portosystemic shunt with portal vein recanalization (PVR-TIPS) in cases of chronic non-cirrhotic PVT consists only of case reports and series, with no prospective studies identified. When considering only patients with portal cavernoma, pooled data from multiple studies report a median technical success rate (number of patients who underwent TIPS without subsequent procedures or severe complications/total patients who underwent TIPS) of 91% (range 73%–100%) and a median stent patency rate of 81.8% (range 69.5%–100%). However, not all studies reported this data, primarily because their focus was on assessing technical feasibility, clinical improvement, and major post-procedural complications, which occurred with low incidence. It is also possible that some patients were excluded due to anatomical limitations, which may have introduced a potential selection bias that contributed to the high reported success and patency rates.

Moreover, only 2 scientific studies addressed whether patients with bile duct dilatation underwent endoscopic drainage before liver puncture. This likely reflects 2 key factors: (i) the rarity of clinically significant portal biliopathy in patients with cPVT; and (ii) a tendency among authors to limit the scope of analysis to procedural outcomes, rather than biliary pathology.

In cases of extensive portal vein thrombosis,60,61 where TIPS placement is unfeasible due to the absence of a clear landing zone, the trans-splenic approach or trans-mesenteric routes can facilitate the procedure. By advancing a guidewire through the splenic or mesenteric vein into the portal system, interventional radiologists can target the portal vein from the hepatic vein more accurately, enabling precise intrahepatic puncture and improving the technical success of TIPS in challenging anatomy. 62 In a retrospective cohort analysis of 39 adult non-cirrhotic patients with chronic cPVT, cavernoma and mesenteric/splenic thrombosis, Knight et al. demonstrated the feasibility of TIPS placement using trans-splenic, trans-mesenteric, and trans-portal accesses for recanalization. The procedure was successful in all patients, exhibiting an acceptable adverse event profile, significant clinical improvement, and a 36-month patency rate of 81%.

Importantly, none of the available studies specifically evaluated interventional radiology approaches in patients with PCC. This underscores a clear need for prospective studies focusing on this patient group to better determine the role, effectiveness, and safety of PVR-TIPS and related techniques in managing PCC.

A direct intrahepatic portocaval shunt (DIPS) is a variation of TIPS, in which the stent is deployed through a gunshot approach, connecting the IVC and the recanalized portal vein. 63 This technique appears to be safe and a potential lifesaving alternative to TIPS.

Surgical management

Shunt

Although various PSS techniques are available to decompress the portal system and recanalize the portal vein, their specific role in managing PCC remains unclear, especially in pediatric patients.

Among available techniques, recently, the mesenteric-left portal vein bypass (Meso-Rex operation) has been recommended by the Baveno consensus as curative surgery, and it should be performed in pediatric patients in the early stage of cPVT.64,65 Modifications of the standard meso-Rex shunt have now been attempted, particularly when the standard procedure cannot be performed due to technical challenges or to extend the feasibility to adults. 66

The Proximal Spleno-Renal Shunt (PSRS) is a standardized procedure typically conducted subsequent to splenectomy. It has a satisfactory rate of success with minimum morbidity and no mortality, as described by Agarwal et al. 67 in 2010, who published prospective data about 39 patients with symptomatic PCC who underwent proximal splenorenal shunt as first-line surgery. Postoperative complications were limited, with no instances of mortality observed. Over a mean follow-up period of 32.2 months, all patients remained asymptomatic. Decompressive shunt surgery alone achieved resolution of biliary obstruction in 24 out of 37 patients (64.9%), while in the remaining 13 patients (35.1%), it facilitated a safe second-stage biliary decompression procedure. However, in pediatric patients, some surgeons opt for a lateral-to-lateral splenorenal shunt to preserve the spleen. 68 If the splenic vein is deemed unsuitable, alternative shunts, although less frequently performed, may be considered, including the meso-gonadal vein shunt, meso-renal shunt, right porto-ovarian shunt, or a shunt between a portal varix and the cava.58,69,70

Our review (Table 4) highlights the historical significance of surgical intervention. 59 When technically feasible, PSSs have been the first-line choice, used in over 70% of cases. Splenorenal and mesocaval REX shunts in pediatric patients have shown high rates of symptom resolution during follow-up. The rates of complications remain low, confirming the efficacy and safety of these procedures, but only when performed in highly specialized centers. The remaining 30% of cases involved non-portosystemic shunts (NPSSs), which are discussed in the following section.

The debate remains open due to a lack of data on whether the creation of a PSS for causes other than portal biliopathy has a protective effect against the condition itself. A recent meta-analysis by Yamoto et al. 88 compared the surgical outcomes of 257 children with cPVT who underwent either meso-Rex bypass (MRB, n=131) or PSS (n=126), finding no significant differences in terms of efficacy or complications and mortality.

Notably, the potential development of PCC was not mentioned. This omission could be due to an insufficiently long follow-up period or the possibility that the shunt itself has a protective effect by restoring portal flow. 88

The scarcity of well-conducted studies in this field underscores the need for future multicenter research and investigations focused on long-term outcomes.

Other surgical procedures

When shuntable veins are unavailable, surgical options for PCC include direct bilioenteric bypass without prior PSS, splenectomy with devascularization, and, in rare cases, liver transplantation. 54 Direct bypass is technically feasible but carries a high risk of intraoperative bleeding due to dense venous collaterals and should be performed by experienced surgeons. Success rates are acceptable, as reported by Cellich 89 et al., Vibert et al., 8 and Ochoa et al. 90 who described successful Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomies in patients lacking shuntable veins, with manageable complications such as bleeding, fistula, and the need for percutaneous cholangioscopies. Nevertheless, the perioperative and postoperative morbidity in patients with cPVT undergoing abdominal surgery remains high. 91

Splenectomy with devascularization may reduce portal pressure and alter the natural course of PCC, although strong evidence remains limited, and most of the studies have been conducted only in Asia.37,44,92 A study by Anwar et al. 44 found no difference in outcomes between patients undergoing proximal splenorenal shunt and those treated with devascularization, suggesting comparable efficacy. Similarly, Das et al. 43 identified PCC as the leading surgical indication, with splenectomy and devascularization being the second most common surgical option after shunt procedures.

Liver transplantation remains a last-resort option, with only a few cases reported over 2 decades. The indications included secondary biliary cirrhosis, recurrent cholangitis despite endoscopic treatment, and repeated gastrointestinal bleeding.71,72

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, PCC encompasses a variety of biliary abnormalities, most commonly linked to cPVT. The clinical presentation ranges widely, from incidental findings on imaging to biliary obstruction, recurrent cholangitis, or complications related to PH. Due to this variability, a tailored, step-by-step management approach is practical, and patients should ideally be referred to specialized centers with expertise in hepatobiliary and PH care.

Although the 2014 consensus recommends using the Llop et al. classification, we recognized the added value of combining it with other frameworks, such as the anatomical and the pathogenetic classification. These complementary perspectives have direct implications for the likelihood and type of intervention, especially when considering options like stenting. Therefore, this combination offers an important insight into the type and extent of biliary involvement, which is crucial for guiding clinical decisions.

The resulting framework guides decision-making, ranging from conservative management for asymptomatic cases to endoscopic or percutaneous interventions for symptomatic patients. Notably, TIPS is increasingly seen as a preferred option in suitable candidates, especially when less invasive approaches fail or portal decompression is needed. More invasive options, such as surgical decompression or bilioenteric anastomosis, may be considered in refractory cases, although supporting evidence remains limited.

Conversely, liver transplantation is still an option for selected cases of secondary cirrhosis, refractory complications, or uncontrollable PH, as long as adequate physiological portal inflow can be maintained.

Importantly, most available studies are retrospective and single-center, with varied inclusion criteria and outcome definitions. This highlights a significant unmet need for standardized diagnostic criteria, prospective multicenter research, and evidence-based guidelines to better define treatment indications and enhance patient outcomes.
