Biogenic nanohydroxyapatite derived from Channa striata fish bones using alkaline hydrolysis and calcination
Authors: Nurdiana Dewi, Meirina Gartika, Dikdik Kurnia, Dwi Gustiono, Nendar Herdianto, Riesma Tasomara, Winda Rianti, Nuning Aisah, Bambang Triwibowo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanohydroxyapatite (nHA) derived from natural resources has emerged as a sustainable alternative to synthetic nHA for biomedical applications. In this study, nHA was synthesized from Channa striata fish bones using a modified two-step alkaline hydrolysis followed by calcination at 550 °C. The structural and physicochemical properties of the material were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller analysis, particle size analysis (PSA), and thermogravimetric analysis. The results confirmed the formation of phase-pure crystalline hydroxyapatite with characteristic phosphate, hydroxyl, and carbonate functional groups. The Ca/P ratio (1.71) was close to the stoichiometric value, indicating high compositional purity. Morphological analysis revealed rod-shaped nanoparticles with an average size of ~ 60 nm, while PSA indicated a broader size distribution due to particle agglomeration. The applied calcination temperature effectively removed organic components while preserving nanoscale structural features. In addition, antibacterial activity against Streptococcus mutans was demonstrated, suggesting potential for dental applications. These findings indicate that the proposed synthesis approach enables the production of phase-pure biogenic nHA with controlled nanoscale characteristics under moderate calcination conditions, highlighting Channa striata fish bones as a sustainable and cost-effective precursor for biomedical and dental materials.

Introduction

Nanohydroxyapatite (nHA) is a bioceramic material that has attracted considerable interest in the dental and biomedical fields due to its excellent biocompatibility, remineralization capacity, and close structural resemblance to natural bone and enamel1,2. Recent studies have further demonstrated the potential of nHA in advanced biomedical applications, including drug delivery systems, bone tissue engineering, and antibacterial materials, highlighting its multifunctional properties and clinical relevance3–5. In dental applications, nHA has been explored for enamel remineralization and prevention of bacterial colonization6. Unlike fluoride, which primarily forms a protective fluorapatite layer, nHA actively promotes enamel repair by remineralizing demineralized regions and improving surface microhardness, making it a promising alternative for caries prevention and management7,8.

The use of natural sources for nHA synthesis has gained increasing interest due to its sustainability and cost-effectiveness compared to synthesis routes9. Fish bones, in particular, are rich in calcium and phosphate, the main constituents of nHA10,11. Various fish species, including salmon, tuna, tilapia, and sardine, have been successfully used as precursors for nHA synthesis12–15. Among these, Channa striata (snakehead fish) is abundant in Southeast Asia and widely cultivated for food and medicinal purposes16–18. Its processing generates significant byproducts, such as bones and other residues, which, if not properly managed, may pose environmental concerns but also represent valuable resources for high-value biomaterials19,20.

The physicochemical properties of nHA, including crystallinity, particle size, morphology, and Ca/P ratio, play a critical role in determining its performance in specific applications. Controlling these properties through optimized synthesis conditions is essential to enhance functional performance. These properties are strongly influenced by both the biological source and the synthesis conditions, particularly pre-treatment strategies and calcination parameters. Although nHA has been synthesized from Channa striata bones using alkaline hydrolysis and calcination, challenges remain in achieving phase-pure nHA with controlled nanoscale morphology and optimal crystallinity12,13,21. Variations in mineral composition and organic content among different species, as well as differences in processing conditions, can significantly affect the final properties of nHA, emphasizing the need for optimized synthesis approaches22.

Conventional calcination is commonly employed to remove organic components and enhance crystallinity in bone-derived materials. However, high calcination temperatures (700–1000 °C) often lead to increased energy consumption, particle coarsening, and the potential for phase transformation into secondary calcium phosphate phases, such as tricalcium phosphate (TCP). Excessively high calcination temperatures can also lead to grain growth and increased particle agglomeration. These limitations may compromise the nanoscale characteristics required for biomedical and dental applications21,23,24.

Recent studies have highlighted significant advances in hydroxyapatite-based nanocomposites, including improvements in mechanical properties, biological performance, and synthesis strategies. These studies emphasize the importance of controlling synthesis parameters to tailor crystallinity, morphology, and surface properties for improved biomedical performance25–27. Despite these advances, limited attention has been given to integrating multi-step pretreatment strategies for fish-bone-derived hydroxyapatite, particularly to achieve phase-pure nanohydroxyapatite with controlled nanoscale morphology under moderate calcination conditions. To address these challenges, this study proposes a modified synthesis approach combining two-step alkaline hydrolysis with moderate-temperature calcination at 550 °C. The calcination temperature of 550 °C was selected as a moderate thermal treatment condition based on previous studies reporting that calcination in the range of 500–600 °C is sufficient to remove residual organic matter while preserving the nHA phase in fish-bone-derived-materials13,14,28. The pre-treatment strategy is designed to enhance the removal of organic components prior to thermal processing, thereby improving calcination efficiency and enabling better control over crystallization behavior. By integrating optimized pretreatment and controlled thermal conditions, this approach aims to produce phase-pure nHA with nanoscale morphology, reduced agglomeration, and a Ca/P ratio close to the stoichiometric value, without requiring high-temperature calcination21,23.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the synthesis of nHA derived from Channa striata fish bones using a two-step alkaline hydrolysis–calcination approach and to evaluate its structural and physicochemical properties. The findings of this work provide insight into how process optimization and material-specific characteristics can be leveraged to achieve phase-pure nanohydroxyapatite with controlled nanoscale features at moderate calcination temperature, offering an effective and sustainable route for producing high-quality biogenic nHA for dental and biomedical applications.

Results

Functional group analysis

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was utilized to identify the functional groups present in HA powders of both uncalcined and calcined at 550 °C, as shown in Fig. 1. The spectrum shows peaks/intensity (transmittance) at specific wavenumbers associated with the functional groups (ions) contained within the sample. The strongest peaks bands in the wavenumber range of 1030–1045 cm⁻¹ are attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration (v3) of the phosphate ions (PO43−), and the sharp absorption band present at around 565 cm⁻¹ and 604 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the bending vibration (v4) of the phosphate ions (PO43−). Additionally, the stretching vibration of the OH− ions is detected at approximately 3443 cm⁻¹. It was also observed that the weak adsorption band at 1630–1637 cm⁻¹ and the broad band at 3100–3500 cm⁻¹ indicate surface-adsorbed water (bending vibration of H2O) and crystal water (stretching vibration of H2O). Moreover, the band around 1430–1472 cm⁻¹ with a very low-intensity peak, is associated with the vibration of CO32−. Hydroxyapatite is widely known for its tunable lattice, which permits various ionic substitutions, including carbonate ions (CO3)2−. There are three recognized types of substitution carbonates in hydroxyapatite based on replacement sites in the apatite lattice: at the hydroxide (A-type), at the phosphate ( B-type), or at both sites (A-B type)29–31. Type A apatite displays a single distinct band at 879 cm⁻¹, whereas Type B shows a strong band at 873 cm⁻¹. Type A apatite presents a clear doublet at 1535 and 1463 cm⁻¹, while type B apatite exhibits a comparable feature in the range of 1456–1422 cm⁻¹. In comparison, type AB apatite produces a more complex spectrum, with main bands and shoulders appearing at approximately 1416, 1452, 1470, 1500, 1545, and 1568 cm⁻¹32. Type B shows the strongest IR bands of the CO32− ion at about 1455, 1410, and 875 cm⁻¹33. The spectral features indicate B-type carbonate substitutions. In another study, the strongest IR bands of the CO32− type B at about 1455, 1410, and 875 cm⁻¹33. Therefore, based on the previous study, the HA powder indicates B-type carbonate substitutions. These results are in good agreement with the carbonates identified in biological fish bone34. Given that carbonates are part of the bone structure, the incorporation of CO₃²⁻ is likely to increase bioactivity rather than pose a potential issue14.

Phase and crystallinity analysis

As shown in Fig. 2, the peaks were sharp, indicating that nHA compounds/phases were affected by the removal of organic components. No other compounds or phases, such as tricalcium phosphate (TCP), are visible. According to Gibson et al.’s report, the nHA phase is transformed to the β-tricalcium phosphate (β–TCP) phase at a temperature range of 710–740 °C if the produced nHA is deficient in calcium35. Therefore, the calcined nHA powder at 550 °C remains a pure HA phase in the present study.

The crystallite size of uncalcined and calcined nHA was calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation (Eq. (1)). The Debye–Scherrer equation is an outstanding tool for determining crystallite size in nanocrystals, but it is limited for micron crystals36. The crystallite size was analyzed along the 002 plane of hydroxyapatite at around 2θ = 25.80°, as this plane corresponds to the c-axis length and yields an isolated peak. In this study, the crystallite sizes of uncalcined and calcined HA along the 002 diffraction plane were determined to be 29.47 and 30.46 nm, respectively (Table 3). The selection of the (002) reflection plane is based on several considerations. First, the (002) plane is aligned with the c-axis of the hexagonal crystal structure of hydroxyapatite, making it representative of the crystal growth direction36. Second, this reflection typically appears as a sharp, intense, and well-isolated peak, minimizing interference with other diffraction peaks and allowing for more reliable analysis. Third, the (002) peak is sensitive to structural disorder, making it suitable for evaluating changes in the material’s crystallinity.

Morphology and surface element composition of the HA powder

Figure 4 shows scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of Channa striata fish bone nHA powder samples synthesized via the alkaline hydrolysis method (Fig. 4a and b, and 4c) and alkaline hydrolysis technique followed by the calcination method at 550 °C (Fig. 4d and e, and 4f). Figure 4a, b and c, and 4d are SEM images showing that the HA powder particles are spherical and form agglomerates of varying sizes. The particle sizes of these powders range from the nanoscale (Fig. 4b and e) to the micrometer scale (Fig. 4a and d). Figure 4c and f are TEM images showing that the single nHA particles synthesized via the alkaline hydrolysis technique and the alkaline hydrolysis technique followed by calcination have a rod-like shape, with a width (or diameter) and length of approximately 20–80 nm, with an average size of ~ 60 nm, respectively. Moreover, nHA synthesized via the alkaline hydrolysis method has a larger width (Fig. 4b and c) than nHA synthesized via the alkaline hydrolysis method followed by the calcination method (Fig. 4e and f). The reduction in the paticle width of nHA is thought to be due to a decrease in organic material as the temperature increases to 550 °C. There is no organic material, and only the inorganic material remains, namely, HA, as proven by the TGA characterization results below and the XRD characterization results above.

White arrows in Fig. 4b, c and e, and 4f point to individual nanoscale HA particles (nHA). The size of the individual nHA particles after the calcination process is reduced, as seen in Fig. 4c (FESEM) and 4f (HRTEM). This reduction in the size of individual nHA particles results in a smaller agglomerate particle size.

Particle size analysis

Figure 6 shows the distribution of the size of the group (agglomerate) of nHA particles derived from the bones of Channa striata before and after calcining at 550 °C. In the sample before (not) calcination, the range of particle sizes varied from 700 to 2500 nm, and the number of particles measuring between 1000 and 1600 nm was the greatest. While the size distribution of the nHA particles that had been calcined at 550 °C ranged from approximately 60 to 1600 nm, the number of particles measuring between 300 nm and 600 nm was the greatest. The results of this particle size analysis (PSA) characterization show that calcination at 550 °C reduces the agglomeration of nHA particles, which is thought to be due to the loss of inorganic material that covers and binds between particles, as shown by the results of the TGA characterization, where inorganic material is no longer present starting at a temperature of approximately 530 °C. Variations in particle size from tens to thousands of nanometers were also observed in the SEM characterization results, where the increase in particle size was caused by agglomeration between individual nHA particles during the growth and bone formation process.

Discussion

nHA can be obtained by chemical synthesis or by extraction from biological sources36,37. Compared with synthetic routes, biogenic nHA derived from animal bones offers advantages such as compositional similarity to natural bone, cost-effectiveness, and enhanced biocompatibility9. Organic matter removal from bone is typically achieved through alkaline treatment with NaOH, which hydrolyzes organic components, leaving calcium phosphate, which is subsequently washed and filtered14. To ensure complete elimination of organic residues, calcination is performed, producing a purified nHA material23.

The role of the two-step alkaline hydrolysis process can be interpreted in terms of its contribution to material purity, crystallinity, and morphology. The alkaline treatment facilitates the removal of organic components from the fish bone matrix, including collagen, proteins, and lipids, yielding a cleaner precursor, as evidenced by FTIR and elemental analysis. This improved precursor condition supports the formation of phase-pure hydroxyapatite during calcination14,37.

While the crystallinity is mainly governed by the calcination process, as evidenced by the sharp XRD peaks, the prior removal of organic matter contributes to a more uniform crystal formation. However, conventional methods often employ temperatures above 700 °C, which may lead to particle coarsening and phase transformation into secondary calcium phosphate phases, such as tricalcium phosphate (TCP)21,23. In contrast, the present study demonstrates that calcination at 550 °C is sufficient to achieve phase-pure hydroxyapatite. Thermogravimetric analysis confirms that the major mass loss associated with organic decomposition occurs below this temperature, indicating that higher thermal input is unnecessary (Fig. 9).

Schematic illustration of the synthesis process of nanohydroxyapatite (nHA) from Channa striata fish bone, showing the sequential steps of pre-treatment, two-step alkaline hydrolysis for organic removal, and subsequent calcination at 550 °C, leading to the formation of phase-pure nHA.

The combined effect of two-step alkaline hydrolysis with moderate-temperature calcination at 550 °C provides several advantages. First, efficient removal of organic matter prior to calcination improves crystallization behavior and phase purity13. Second, limiting the calcination temperature helps preserve nanoscale morphology and minimizes particle agglomeration14,23. Third, the absence of secondary phases, such as TCP, confirms that the applied conditions are suitable for maintaining phase stability28. These findings highlight the importance of process integration in controlling final material properties, which is crucial for dental and biomedical applications38.

Compared with previous studies, the present method demonstrates improved control over physicochemical characteristics at relatively lower calcination temperatures. For example, Herpandi et al. reported that higher calcination temperatures (900 °C) may induce partial phase transformation of nHA into TCP, whereas the current study maintains a stable nHA phase21. Similarly, studies by Surya et al. and Mohd Pu’ad et al. reported nanoscale nHA but required higher thermal treatment or different processing routes14,38. The present results indicate that optimizing pre-treatment can reduce thermal requirements without compromising crystallinity or structural integrity.

Uncalcined nHA from Channa striata fish bone resulted in a slightly larger particle size than nHA obtained from tilapia fish scales (24.6 nm)39. In contrast, nHA synthesized from devilfish (Loricariidae) bone and calcined at 550 °C had a particle size of 28.62 nm, which is close to that of the calcined nHA in this study40. The crystallinity of uncalcined nHA was 31.74%, increasing to 39.18% after calcination at 550 °C. The calcination process induced nucleation and crystal growth of nHA, as indicated by the narrowing of the diffraction peaks, suggesting higher crystallinity and larger crystallite sizes. This improvement, reflected by narrower diffraction peaks, indicates enhanced nucleation and crystal growth. The increase in crystallinity is consistent with previous findings on porcine bone-derived nHA and exceeds the values reported for Channa striata nHA synthesized via precipitation methods41,42.

SEM and TEM analyses revealed that the synthesized nHA exhibited a rod-like morphology, which is a typical characteristic of biogenic nHA43. Previous studies have attributed the transition from needle-like to rod-like morphology to the substitution of CO₃²⁻ for PO₄³⁻ and Na⁺ for Ca²⁺44. The PSA characterization results show a range of HA particle sizes from 60 to 1600 nm, with a dominant number of micron-sized particles. TEM results confirm that the primary HA particles have an average size of approximately 60 nm, while PSA detects a broader size distribution dominated by larger agglomerates, with only a minor fraction of particles around 60 nm. TEM results further explain that micro-sized HA particles are formed (large) due to the agglomeration of individual nano-sized HA particles (nHA).

BET analysis revealed a high surface area, which may enhance bioactivity and support potential applications in biomedical and dental materials45,46. The surface area of nHA derived from the bones of Channa striata shows a much higher value compared to other fish species reported in the literature in recent years, namely before and after calcination at a temperature of 550 °C, which is 82 m²/g and 62 m²/g. The surface area value of tilapia fish reported by PV Nam et al. is 32.83 m²/g and González et al. is 2.29 m²/g47,48. Furthermore, the surface area of nHA Carp fish (Cyprinidae) reported by Muhammad et al. is 37.38 m²/g49. The decrease in the surface area value at a temperature of 550 °C indicates the growth of nHA crystals that are getting bigger from a radius of 16.46 nm to 21.71 nm, which is in accordance with the increase in crystal size in the XRD data.

The Ca/P ratio of the synthesized nHA (1.71) is close to the stoichiometric value of 1.67 for natural bone minerals, indicating high compositional accuracy and phase purity50. Variations in Ca/P ratio reported in previous studies are often associated with differences in synthesis methods and processing conditions. Biological sources such as fish scales and animal bones also generally produce nonstoichiometric nHA due to the incorporation of carbonate groups and trace ions (HPO₄²⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Sr²⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, and F⁻) in the crystal lattice10. Elemental analysis by EDS confirmed high purity with minimal contaminants. Importantly, the heavy metal content (0.0% Cd, 0.1% Hg, 0.2% As, and 0.0% Pb) remained below the permissible threshold set by ISO 13779-6, underscoring the suitability of Channa striata as a safe precursor for biomedical applications51.

As shown in Table 5, most previous studies require higher calcination temperatures (≥ 600 °C) to achieve comparable crystallinity, which often results in particle coarsening or agglomeration. In contrast, the present study demonstrates that the combination of two-step alkaline hydrolysis and moderate calcination at 550 °C is sufficient to produce phase-pure nHA with controlled nanoscale morphology and a Ca/P ratio close to the stoichiometric value.

In addition to its physicochemical characteristics, the biological functionality of nHA, particularly its antibacterial activity, is a key factor determining its potential application in dental materials6. This study showed that nHA has optimal antibacterial activity at moderate concentrations (5–10%), with no significant improvement or even reduced efficacy at higher concentrations. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that nanohydroxyapatite exhibits optimal antibacterial activity at moderate concentrations. Dewi et al. observed a similar trend against Streptococcus mutans, which is further supported by recent studies highlighting the non-linear dose-response behavior of hydroxyapatite-based materials5,52.

This phenomenon may be attributed to nanoparticle aggregation and reduced dispersion at higher concentrations, which limits effective interaction with bacterial cells53. The antibacterial activity of nHA is likely related to its nanoscale size and high surface area, which enhance interaction with bacterial cell membranes and may lead to membrane disruption. Additionally, ionic interactions involving calcium and phosphate species may interfere with bacterial metabolism, while particle adhesion to the cell surface can inhibit bacterial growth and colonization6.

Despite the comprehensive physicochemical characterization, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. This study did not include detailed biological evaluations, such as cytocompatibility, biofilm inhibition, or in vivo assessment, which are essential for fully assessing the clinical applicability of the synthesized nanohydroxyapatite. Therefore, future studies should focus on evaluating the material’s biocompatibility, antibiofilm activity, and long-term stability, as well as its performance in more clinically relevant models to further validate its potential for dental and biomedical applications.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a sustainable approach for producing nHA from Channa striata fish bones using a modified two-step alkaline hydrolysis followed by calcination at 550 °C. The applied synthesis route effectively removed organic components while preserving the nHA phase and nanoscale morphology. Structural and compositional analyses confirmed the formation of phase-pure nHA with rod-shaped nanoparticles and a Ca/P ratio close to the stoichiometric value. The moderate calcination temperature enabled the production of crystalline nHA while limiting particle agglomeration and maintaining nanoscale structural characteristics. Compared with conventional high-temperature calcination methods, the proposed approach demonstrates that phase-pure biogenic nHA can be successfully produced at a moderate calcination temperature. In addition, a preliminary antibacterial evaluation against Streptococcus mutans showed that the synthesized nHA exhibits inhibitory activity, suggesting its potential as an antibacterial biomaterial for dental applications. These findings highlight the potential of Channa striata bone waste as an environmentally sustainable and cost-effective precursor for biomedical and dental material applications.

Methods

Sample collection

The Channa striata fish bones were sourced from a Channa striata cracker factory in Banjarmasin, Indonesia. All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade.

Preparation of the fish bones

A wooden hammer and a bladed cutter were used to delicately remove the fish bones from the collected samples. The bones were carefully boiled at approximately 100 °C in 2 L of distilled water to remove any leftover flesh or skin. To eliminate any remaining proteins, lipids, oils, or other organic contaminants, the cleaned bones were boiled for 1 h after being submerged in a 500 mL solution containing 2% sodium hydroxide (NaOH, HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) and 10 mL of acetone (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). To remove any remaining moisture, the treated bones were cleaned and dried in an oven set to 100 °C for 3 h. Finally, a mortar and pestle were used to grind the dried fish bones into a fine powder.

Synthesis of nHA

The alkaline hydrolysis method in this study was adapted with slight modifications from the research of Surya et al.14. Similarly, the calcination process was modified from the study conducted by Bee et al.28. The modifications mainly involved applying a two-step alkaline hydrolysis process with varying NaOH concentrations, followed by moderate-temperature calcination at 550 °C. The procedure is summarized and illustrated in Fig. 9. Two steps of alkaline hydrolysis were applied to the prepared fish bones. They were first heated to 70 °C, agitated for 5 h at 400 rpm, and submerged in a 5% NaOH solution (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) at a 1:14 (w/v, FB: NaOH) ratio. After the treated bones were filtered, the precipitates were carefully cleaned with distilled water and dried for two hours at 100 °C.

The second phase involved heating the dried precipitate to 100 °C for 1 h after treatment with a 50% NaOH solution at a 1:6 (w/v) precipitate: NaOH solution ratio. After the resulting white solution was filtered, the filtrate was rinsed with distilled water until it was clear. Following the suspension of the resulting white precipitate in 200 milliliters of distilled water, 0.1 M phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was added to adjust the pH to neutral (pH 7). After an hour of constant stirring, the mixture was filtered. To obtain nHA powder, the finished product was sieved after drying for 2 h at 100 °C in an oven. The samples were subsequently placed in a Carbolite CWF 1100 furnace (Germany) and calcined at 550 °C. Over 2 h, the heating process was conducted at a controlled rate of 5 °C/min. After calcination at 550 °C for 2 h, the sample was allowed to cool naturally inside the furnace.

Characterization of nHA powder

The synthesized nHA was characterized via multiple analytical techniques to assess its structural and compositional properties.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The presence of nHA was verified, and functional groups were identified via Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. A Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS-10 was used to acquire the spectra via a transmission approach with KBr added at the BRIN. At room temperature, measurements were made between 4000 and 500 cm⁻¹, with a resolution of ± 4 cm⁻¹ and a scan frequency of 16 times.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

An X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) equipped with Cu Kα1 radiation at BRIN was used to analyze the crystalline phases and the purity of the nHA powder. To identify the phase, the acquired diffraction patterns were compared with reference data from the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD) No. 98-016-9498. Data acquisition was carried out with a step width of 0.01° and over the 2θ range of 10° to 75°.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis

The powder BET surface area, pore size, and pore volume were measured via an N₂ adsorption‒desorption analyzer (Quantachrome, Quadsorbs evo) at BRIN. A 10 mg sample was introduced into the system and pretreated to remove moisture before N₂ adsorption at -196 °C. Two measurements were conducted, and the results are presented as the statistical average.

Scanning electron microscopy-energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)

A field emission-scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) Apreo 2 S (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operating at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV was used to examine the microstructures and morphologies of the nHA crystals. The local chemical composition was examined by integrating FE-SEM with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Both measurement tools are at the BRIN.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) analysis

Images of the nanostructure were captured via a Talos F200X transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated at 200 kV. The powder particle morphology was examined, and particle size was determined using ImageJ.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

The thermal degradation properties of the powders were examined via TGA (TGA Absys Evo Setaram Simultaneous TG DTA) at BRIN. With a heating rate of 5 °C·min⁻¹ and a nitrogen flow rate of 70 ml·min⁻¹, a 20 mg sample was heated from room temperature to 700 °C in a nitrogen environment.

Particle size analysis

The dynamic light scattering (DLS) method (CILAS NANO DS Dual Scattering) at BRIN was used to measure the particle size of the nHA powder.

Antibacterial susceptibility testing

Prior to the antibacterial assay, the samples were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. The sterile nHA was dispersed in sterile distilled water to obtain concentrations of 5%, 10%, and 20% (w/v).

The antimicrobial efficacy of nanohydroxyapatite was assessed against Streptococcus mutans (ATCC 25175) obtained from the Biomedical Laboratory, Faculty of Dentistry, Universitas Lambung Mangkurat, Indonesia, using the disk diffusion technique. The bacterial suspension was first prepared by inoculating Streptococcus mutans in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth and standardized to a 0.5 McFarland.

A 50 µL aliquot of the standardized inoculum was uniformly distributed across the Mueller-Hinton (MH) agar surface using a sterile L-spreader. Sterilized Whatman No. 1 filter paper disks (6 mm diameter) were impregnated with 20 µL of the experimental nHA (5%, 10%, and 20%). These disks, along with negative (sterile distilled water), were placed on the inoculated media (4 disks per plate). The inoculated plates were then refrigerated at 4 °C for 3 h to enhance diffusion of the antimicrobial agents, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h under anaerobic conditions to observe the inhibition zone. Following incubation, the inhibition zones, including the diameter of the paper disc, were measured in millimeters. The presence of clear zones indicated antibacterial activity, reflecting nHA’s ability to inhibit bacterial growth.
