Targeting Apolipoprotein E4 rescues photoreceptor degeneration by inhibiting the disease-associated microglia activation in experimental retinal detachment
Authors: Xiaomeng Li, Niannian Fan, Yuanye Yan, Mingxin Ren, Haomin Wu, Kai Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Retinal detachment (RD) is a prevalent ocular disorder that leads to photoreceptor death and irreversible visual impairment. Following RD, microglia—the resident immune cells of the retina—become activated and participate in regulating inflammatory responses and tissue repair processes. A distinct microglial subtype, disease-associated microglia (DAM) emerges in stressed neuronal microenvironments. However, its specific contribution to photoreceptor degeneration remains poorly understood. Apolipoprotein E (ApoE), a major lipoprotein predominantly expressed in brain and ocular myeloid cells, has been implicated in modulating neurodegeneration within the central nervous system through influencing DAM activation. In this study, we employed an experimental mouse model of RD and observed upregulation of ApoE and DAM-related markers at three days following RD induction. Genetic deletion of ApoE significantly attenuated photoreceptor loss and suppressed neuroinflammatory responses after RD, accompanied by reduced DAM activation. Furthermore, modulation of the ApoE-Galectin-3 axis reduced TUNEL-positive cells and inhibited TLR4-dependent inflammatory cascades post-RD. Using humanized ApoE allele mice, we further elucidated that the ApoE4 isoform significantly downregulated DAM-associated markers (including Galectin-3, Spp-1 and Gpnmb), promoted photoreceptor survival, and attenuated retinal inflammation. In contrast, ApoE2 and ApoE3 conferred no protection benefit compared to wild-type mice after RD. Our findings indicate that ApoE-mediated DAM activation exacerbates photoreceptor degeneration after RD insult. Both ApoE deficiency and ApoE4 expression potentially mitigated RD-induced photoreceptor death and ameliorated neuroinflammatory pathways via suppression of DAM activation. Collectively, our study highlights ApoE4 as a promising therapeutic target for modulating microglial cells to promote neuronal survival in photoreceptor degeneration conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03762-x.

Introduction

Retinal detachment (RD) is a common cause of photoreceptor loss in fundus diseases [1]. The detached retinal neuroepithelium fails to attain nutritional support from the separated retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and choroidal vessels, leading to irreversible photoreceptor death [2]. Among the various types of RD, rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) has the highest annual incidence, reported at approximately 13 cases per 100,000 individuals [3]. Although surgical intervention can achieve successful anatomical reattachment, the photoreceptors continue to degenerate, ultimately resulting in permanent vision dysfunction [4]. Therefore, genetic or pharmacologic interventions targeting photoreceptor death are essential for treating RRD and restoring vision function.

Visual function defects following RD are consistently attributed to photoreceptor death, however, the underlying mechanisms leading to photoreceptor death in RD have not been fully elucidated. Previous neuroprotection studies have primarily focused on inhibiting cell death pathways. Nonetheless, clinically, inhibition of a single checkpoint may not directly promote photoreceptor survival [1, 5]. Regardless of the complex causes of retinal degeneration, early intervention targeting the inflammatory cascade is crucial for modulating the degenerative process [6]. Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), have emerged as an important cell type prominently involved in retinal neuroinflammation [7, 8].

During CNS disease processes, the interaction between microglial cells and neurons is sophisticated and controversial. In healthy tissues, microglia play a positive role in maintaining homeostasis through immune monitoring, clearance, and tissue repair [9, 10]. Nevertheless, microglia under diseased conditions exhibit cytotoxicity and inflammatory properties by releasing chemokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1, resulting in the disruption of the neuronal microenvironment, which triggers an inflammatory cascade that ultimately exacerbates the disease [11, 12].

Consequently, this intricate nature of microglia is recapitulated in retinal degenerative diseases, where they exhibit a pronounced “double-edged sword” effect. On one hand, activated microglia can play a critical protective role by migrating to sites of injury and restricting neuronal death through specific molecular programs [13]. On the other hand, dysregulated microglial activity is a key driver of pathology. When microglial inflammatory signaling becomes uncontrolled, such as through forced activation of the MyD88 pathway, it shifts the balance toward cytotoxicity, compromising photoreceptor survival and exacerbating degeneration [14]. These studies underscore that the outcome of microglial activation is not intrinsically beneficial or detrimental but is determined by precise control of their functional state [15]. Therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating rather than broadly suppressing microglia hold significant promise.

In this context, the “disease-associated microglia” (DAM) phenotype is of central importance. Initially identified in Alzheimer’s disease [16], DAM arises from the transition of homeostatic microglia at lesion sites and has been extensively characterized across various CNS pathologies. This phenotype is defined by a marked downregulation of homeostatic markers (e.g., Tmem119, Cx3cr1, Hexb) coupled with upregulation of genes involved in lipid metabolism, phagocytosis, and immune regulation, such as ApoE, Lgals3, Cstb, and Cd68. Further studies revealed that Apolipoprotein E (ApoE), a major lipoprotein in brain myeloid cells, is a crucial factor for DAM transformation [17]. In particular, ApoE-driven DAM has a potential effect on late-onset AD; one of its subtypes, ApoE4, accelerates the deposition of amyloid in the brain and exacerbates neuronal degeneration [18]. In retinal diseases, however, expression of humanized ApoE4 has been shown to alleviate DAM activation and protect retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) in a glaucoma model [19]. ApoE4 was also shown to decrease age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and photoreceptor damage in mice [20, 21]. Nevertheless, the contradictory roles of ApoE4 in AD versus retinal diseases remain poorly understood, and how ApoE4 contributes to DAM modulation and photoreceptor death in RD remains unclear.

In this study, we utilized subretinal injection of sodium hyaluronate to induce experimental RD in mice. We also employed ApoE knockout mice and humanized ApoE4 mice to elucidate the characteristics of microglia activation and the role of ApoE-mediated DAM in photoreceptor degeneration following RD. Our findings demonstrated significant DAM activation after RD. ApoE depletion significantly enhanced photoreceptor survival in a DAM-dependent manner. We further confirmed that ApoE4 inhibits microglial transition into the DAM phenotype and thereby preserves photoreceptors. Targeting ApoE4 also relieved retinal neuroinflammation stress via the Galectin-3 signaling pathway after RD. Taken together, our findings reveal that ApoE4 suppresses photoreceptor degeneration by inhibiting DAM activation, and show that the ApoE-Galectin-3 signaling axis may facilitate neuroprotective strategies for RD and other photoreceptor degeneration diseases.

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Institute Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the USTC and conducted in strict adherence to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Male C57BL/6 J mice were provided by the First Affiliated Hospital of USTC, while male ApoE−/−, LGALS3−/−, homozygous ApoE2, ApoE3, ApoE4, and Cx3cr1CreERT2;Ai14 mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. Mice were 6–12 weeks of age and weighed 20–25 g at the beginning of each experiment. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water. Only the right eyes of mice were subjected to surgical procedures, and the contralateral left eyes served as controls.

Experimental RD model

RD surgery was performed as previously described [22]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). The eyelashes were shortened, and the pupils were dilated with 0.5% tropicamide and 0.5% epinephrine (Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan). Under an ophthalmic microscope, a 30-gauge needle was used to create a self-sealing scleral incision 1.0 mm posterior to the limbus. The bevel of the needle was kept upward to avoid accidental injury to the lens. Through the scleral incision, a small area of peripheral retina was gently detached from the RPE using a 32-gauge blunt needle. Afterwards, 1.5 μL of sodium hyaluronate (10 mg/mL; LG Life Sciences Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) was slowly injected into subretinal space until the entire retina was detached from underlying RPE (Fig. 1A-C). Finally, ofloxacin antibiotic ointment (Dikeluo, Siqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China) was applied to the scleral wound, and mice were placed on a heating pad to prevent hypothermia. To avoid potential ocular infections, levofloxacin eye drops were applied three times a day for three days after RD surgery. Microglia isolation, enrichment and single-cell RNA sequencing

Mice were sacrificed by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) and eyes were enucleated. Retinal tissues were dissected and rinsed three times in cold PBS to remove surface contaminants, and then placed in a sterile RNase-free culture dish on ice containing calcium- and magnesium-free PBS. Retinal tissues were then minced into 0.5 mm2 pieces, washed again with PBS to remove residual blood and fatty tissue, and subjected to enzymatic dissociation.

Tissue dissociation was performed by incubating the fragments in pre-warmed dissociation solution at 37 °C in a shaking water bath (10 rpm) for 20 min. The digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The tissue digest was gently triturated 5–10 times using a Pasteur pipette and sequentially filtered through stacked 70 μm and 30 μm cell strainers. The single-cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL PBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Red blood cells were lysed by adding 1 mL of RBC lysis buffer (MACS, 130–094–183, 10 ×) and incubating on ice for 10 min. After lysis, cells were washed once with PBS/0.04% BSA. Dead cells were then removed using the Miltenyi Dead Cell Removal Kit (MACS 130–090–101) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following two additional washes with PBS/0.04% BSA, the final cell pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of the same buffer. Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion using a Countess II Automated Cell Counter, confirming a viability > 85% for all samples. Cell concentration was adjusted to 700–1200 cells/μL for downstream processing.

To ensure sufficient capture of retinal microglia for downstream single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis, a FACS-based enrichment strategy was employed prior to library construction. Briefly, cells were resuspended in ice-cold FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA). Cells were then incubated with fluorescently conjugated antibodies against mouse CD11b and CD45 for 30 min on ice in the dark. Microglia were identified and sorted as live, single cells within the CD11b+ CD45low population based on forward and side scatter properties. A minimum of 6,000 sorted microglia cells were collected into a tube containing complete culture medium. An aliquot of the pre-sort, unsorted "whole retinal suspension" was preserved separately on ice.

Following sorting, cell counts and viability were re-assessed for both the sorted microglial fraction and the reserved whole retinal suspension. To generate an optimized input for scRNA-seq that preserved native cellular interactions while enhancing microglial representation, we reconstituted the cell mixture by combining the sorted microglia with the microglia-depleted retinal cell suspension at a defined ratio of 4:6 (microglia: all other retinal cells). This final enriched suspension was gently mixed and prepared for sequencing.

The enriched single-cell suspensions were loaded onto a 10X Chromium Controller (10X Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Chromium Single-Cell 3’ Reagent Kit (V4). Subsequent cDNA amplification and library construction steps were performed following the standard 10X Genomics workflow. The final libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp paired-end reads) by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China), with a target depth of at least 20,000 reads per cell.

Genetic lineage tracing of microglia

To distinguish resident microglia from infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages within the Iba-1 positive myeloid population, we employed a lineage tracing strategy using Cx3cr1CreERT2;Ai14 mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.). Tamoxifen was dissolved in corn oil to a concentration of 20 mg/mL. Adult mice (6–8 weeks) were administered tamoxifen (75 mg/kg per day, MedChemExpress, HY-13757A) via intraperitoneal injection for 5 consecutive days. Afterwards, mice were maintained for 4 weeks to allow complete turnover of unlabeled peripheral monocytes, ensuring that any tdTomato-labeled cells were derived from the long-lived resident microglial pool. Subsequent to this period, RD surgery was performed.

Western blotting

In brief, a buffer containing 50 mmol/L Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, protease inhibitors (Roche, #11,679,498,001, Mannheim, Germany), and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, #P0044) was utilized to homogenize retinal tissues. The samples were electrophoresed through 8%−12% Bis–Tris gels and then electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The following primary and secondary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-cleaved caspase3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9661, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Galectin-3 (Proteintech, #14,979–1-AP, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Spp-1 (Abcam, #ab218237, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Gpnmb (Abcam, #ab317325, 1:1000), rabbit anti-TLR4 (Proteintech, #19,811–1-AP, 1:1000), rabbit anti-NLRP3 (Abcam, #ab263899, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Gasdermin D (Abcam, #ab209845, 1:1000), rabbit anti-β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, #4970, 1:1000), rabbit anti-β-tubulin (Proteintech, #10,094–1-AP, 1:1000), and HRP-goat anti-rabbit (Proteintech, #RGAR001, 1:5000). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Amersham, UK), and signal intensity was quantified with ImageJ 1.54 software.

Immunohistochemistry and TUNEL labeling

For retinal flatmount immunofluorescence, mice underwent transcardiac perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Eyes were enucleated and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 60 min at room temperature. Under a microscope, retinas were carefully dissected, flattened with four radial cuts, and washed in PBS. Tissues were then permeabilized and blocked in a solution containing 0.5% Triton X-100 and 5% goat serum for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, retinas were incubated with primary antibodies rabbit anti-Iba-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #17,198, 1:200) for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by extensive washing with PBS and incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI for 2 h at room temperature. After final washes, the retinas were flat-mounted on slides with an anti-fade medium, coverslipped, sealed, and imaged using confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM800).

Retinal sections were performed as previously described [23]. Briefly, mice were sacrificed and subjected to transcardiac perfusion with 4% PFA. The eyeballs were enucleated and dehydrated in 20% and 40% sucrose solutions sequentially at 4 °C. Subsequently, the eyes were fully embedded in Tissue OCT-Freeze Medium (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan) and frozen at −20 °C. Sections were cut at a thickness of 10 μm.

For immunofluorescence, retinal sections were first blocked with 10% goat serum and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 2 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-ApoE (Invitrogen, #16H22L18, 1:500), rabbit anti-Galectin-3 (Proteintech, #14,979–1-AP, 1:300), rabbit anti-Iba-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #17,198, 1:200), and rabbit anti-P2ry12 (Invitrogen, #702,516, 1:500). After incubation, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit, Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-rabbit and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit. Finally, the sections were washed again, coverslipped with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI, and stored in slide boxes. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) was performed as previously described [23]. Retinal sections were examined under fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss Axio Imager 2) or confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM800).

Histopathological retinal outer nuclear layer thickness assessment

Mice were sacrificed and the eyeballs were removed 7 days after RD and fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. From each eyeball, paraffin-embedded retinal Sects. (10 μm thick) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin as previously described [23]. Four sections per eye were randomly selected by masked observers, and the retinal tissue structure was examined under a light microscope. Ten loci per section were measured and calculated (starting from the optic nerve, 5 loci on the left and 5 loci on the right), with each locus spaced 200 μm apart. To control for variations in retinal sectioning angle and tissue processing, we used the inner nuclear layer (INL) thickness as an internal reference, as it remains relatively stable in the acute phase of RD. The outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness was then normalized to account for these technical variations. Briefly, within the same eye, the ratio of ONL thickness to total retinal thickness was calculated for both the detached and the attached regions (contralateral control). The final normalized ONL thickness was expressed as the fold change of this ratio in the detached region relative to the attached region. To sum up, the ONL thickness was normalized using the formula: (ONL detached/total retina detached)/(ONL attached/total retina attached), as described previously [23–25].

Transmission electron microscopy

Photoreceptor death induced by RD was further evaluated with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to delineate morphological features associated with apoptotic or necrotic cell death. Briefly, mice were sacrificed 3 days after RD, and the eyeballs were enucleated. The corneas and lenses were removed, and the retinas were gently separated from the underlying RPE and immersed in 4% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 24 h. Afterwards, the retinas were dissected into small pieces, fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 mol/L sodium phosphate buffer, dehydrated, and embedded in Eponate resin. Ultrathin sections were stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate and then examined under a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1200EX, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Approximately 200 photoreceptors per retinal sample were evaluated. Cells displaying cellular shrinkage and nuclear condensation were classified as apoptotic, whereas those exhibiting cellular and organellar swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and nuclear disintegration were defined as necrotic or pyroptotic.

Electroretinogram

Retinal function was assessed by electroretinogram (ERG) after overnight dark adaptation. Recordings were performed at baseline and 7 days post-RD. All procedures were conducted under dim red light. Briefly, mice were anesthetized, and pupils were dilated as previously described [23]. A gold wire loop electrode was placed on the corneal surface, with a reference electrode positioned subcutaneously on the forehead and a ground electrode attached to the tail. Photic stimulation was delivered via a Ganzfeld integrating sphere using standardized flash intensities of 0.1 cd·s/m2 and 1.0 cd·s/m2, respectively. The evoked responses were amplified through a preamplifier with a bandpass filter set at 0.2–300 Hz. The a-wave amplitude was measured from the pre-stimulus baseline to the trough of the initial negative deflection, and the b-wave amplitude was measured from the a-wave trough to the peak of the subsequent positive deflection.

Statistical analyses

At least three independent experiments were performed for each study. All data are presented as the mean ± SD. An unpaired t-test was used to analyze differences between two groups, and one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was applied for comparisons among three or more groups. Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Homeostatic microglia adopt a disease-associated phenotype upon retinal detachment

To elucidate the subtype differences of microglia in normal retinas versus degenerated retinas, we investigated the expression of homeostatic and DAM-related genes in an RD model. Retinal neuroepithelial detachment was induced by injecting sodium hyaluronate into the subretinal space of wild-type mouse eyes (Fig. 1A) [23, 26]. Fundus photography and HE staining of retinal sections confirmed persistent separation of the retina from the RPE layer 14 days after modeling (Fig. 1B, C). TUNEL staining and western blotting of cleaved caspase3 revealed significant photoreceptor death in the ONL 3 days after RD (Fig. 1D-G). Accordingly, we defined day 3 as the time point of peak photoreceptor degeneration, consistent with previous studies [27]. At this time point, retinal samples were harvested and microglia were enriched for scRNA-seq (Fig. 1H). The uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plot displayed 14 cell clusters, which were annotated based on the expression of marker genes respectively (Fig. 1I). Volcano plot analysis identified ApoE, LGALS3, Spp-1, and Gpnmb as among the most significantly upregulated genes in the microglial cluster (Fig. 1J). Moreover, heatmap showed upregulation of DAM-related genes (e.g., ApoE, LGALS3, Spp-1) and downregulation of homeostatic genes (e.g., Cx3cr1, Tmem119) in microglia from detached retinas (Fig. 1K). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated significant enrichment for terms related to “oxidative phosphorylation”, “Alzheimer disease”, “cytokine activity”, “respiratory chain” and “cytosolic small ribosomal subunit” (Fig. 1L). These findings suggest a critical involvement of disease-associated microglial dysfunction in subsequent neuroinflammatory injury following RD.

Given that Iba-1 represents a pan-myeloid marker, we employed genetic lineage tracing in Cx3cr1CreERT2;Ai14 mice to delineate the contribution of monocyte-derived macrophages to photoreceptor degeneration in our RD model (Fig. 2A). Quantitative analysis across the ganglion cell layer (GCL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), and subretinal space (SRS) revealed that the majority (> 90%) of Iba-1+ myeloid cells in the detached retina were tdTomato+ (Fig. 2B-E), indicating their origin from the long-lived, resident microglial population. In contrast, Iba-1+ tdTomato− infiltrating cells were barely detected. We further performed a comparative immunohistochemical analysis of Iba-1 and the homeostatic microglial marker P2ry12 in retinal sections. This analysis showed that Iba-1 staining provided a more robust signal for identifying activated microglia in our model, as P2ry12 expression is known to be downregulated upon activation. Three days post-RD, Iba-1 immunoreactivity was markedly increased in the ONL and SRS (Figure S3A-C). ApoE upregulation in microglia of RD models and in the vitreous of RRD patients

Studies of the CNS have identified ApoE as a key marker of DAM, playing a regulatory role in microglial function and neurodegenerative processes [28]. Our results also demonstrated transcriptional upregulation of the ApoE gene in microglia in the RD model. To further investigate ApoE expression at the protein level during RD, we analyzed human vitreous samples from patients with early-stage RRD and control patients (with VMT and MH) (Table 1). ELISA revealed that the median concentration of ApoE was significantly higher in RRD vitreous than in control vitreous (Fig. 3A). We next performed Western blotting to evaluate the expression of ApoE and several downstream DAM markers in detached retinas. In neurodegenerative diseases, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) acts as a critical downstream effector of Galectin-3, contributing to neuroinflammatory cascades and playing a key role in the release of inflammatory cytokines from microglia and macrophages [31, 32]. Recent studies have also reported pyroptotic microglial impairment and subsequent inflammatory responses in RD models [33, 34]. These findings prompted us to investigate microglial inflammation levels by detecting TLR4-driven inflammasome pathway following ApoE or Galectin-3 intervention. Interestingly, Western blot analysis showed that inhibition of the ApoE-Galectin-3 axis significantly downregulated the expression of TLR4, NLRP3, and pore-forming protein, N-terminal fragment of Gasdermin D (Gsdmd-N) in mouse retinas (Fig. 4J-M) (Fig. S5C-F). These data imply that deficiency in ApoE or Galectin-3 deterred retinal inflammatory cascade in experimental RD.

Humanized ApoE4 confers protection against RD-induced photoreceptor degeneration and neuroinflammation

Unlike mice expressing a single invariant ApoE isoform, humans exhibit a triallelic polymorphism at the ApoE locus, giving rise to three primary protein isoforms: ApoE2, ApoE3, and ApoE4. ApoE3 is the most common subtype, while ApoE4 represents a major genetic risk factor of Alzheimer's disease [35, 36]. In contrast, the ApoE4 allele has been reported to exert neuroprotective effects in models of glaucoma and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [19, 37]. To explore how ApoE4 orchestrates the fate of photoreceptors in RD, we first analyzed human vitreous samples from primary RRD patients. Compared with control vitreous (from patients with MH or VMT), ApoE4 levels and the ratio of ApoE4/total ApoE were significantly lower in RRD patients (Fig. S5G). There are conflicting reports in the literature regarding the effect of microglia depletion on photoreceptor degeneration. Strikingly, microglial depletion with PLX3397 abolished the protective phenotype observed in both hApoE4 and ApoE−/− mice. Compared to genotype-matched controls on a regular diet, PLX3397-treated mice exhibited exacerbated retinal structural damage (Fig. 9E, H) (Fig. S5O, Q), increased photoreceptor loss (Fig. 9F, I) (Fig. S5P, R), and significantly diminished a-wave and b-wave amplitudes in ERG recordings (Fig. 9G, J-M) (Fig. S5S, T). At the molecular level, western blot analysis revealed a marked intensification of cleaved caspase-3 in PLX3397-treated hApoE4 and ApoE−/− mice (Fig. 9N, O) (Fig. S5I, K). These data demonstrate that the presence of microglia is required for the neuroprotective effect of ApoE4 in RD, as their depletion completely reverses the structural, functional, and molecular benefits.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of DAM in an experimental RD model. Following RD induction, microglia transitioned from homeostatic state to a neurodegenerative phenotype characterized by upregulation of ApoE, LGALS3, and other neurotoxic cytokines—a response also reported in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and glaucoma [16, 17, 19]. Thus, despite differences in etiology and lesion sites, RD-induced photoreceptor degeneration elicits a DAM-like microglial response similar to that in other CNS pathologies. We further identified ApoE as one of the most significantly upregulated genes in retinal microglia after RD. Genetic deletion of ApoE drastically suppressed DAM activation, improved ONL thickness, and reduced photoreceptor loss. Subsequent inhibition of Galectin-3, a key downstream effector of ApoE, also attenuated retinal structural disruption and inflammatory cascades.

A key finding of our study is that the ApoE4 allele, a well-established genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease [40], conferred microglial homeostasis and neuroprotection comparable to that achieved through ApoE depletion in RD. On the contrary, microglia in ApoE2 and ApoE3 mice failed to maintain homeostatic balance, leading to sustained photoreceptor degeneration. Depletion of microglia readily neutralized the protective effect associated with ApoE4 expression. Collectively, our findings suggest that modulating ApoE4 signaling may alleviate retinal DAM activation and mitigate photoreceptor death following RD injury (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Schematic diagram illustrating the protective effect of ApoE deficiency and ApoE4 expression on microglial homeostasis and photoreceptor loss in RD-induced retinal disorders

Multiple cell death pathways, including apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy, have been extensively reported to orchestrate photoreceptor degeneration after RD [41–44], Inhibition of a single checkpoint—such as caspase3—has been shown to lead to alternative receptor-interacting protein 1 (RIP1)-mediated necroptosis activation [2]. These studies imply that cell death pathways may engage in crosstalk and compensate for one another in retinal injury. Consequently, there is currently no therapeutic strategy that completely prevents photoreceptor death, highlighting that early intervention in retinal inflammatory processes may represent a more promising neuroprotective approach [45, 46]. Microglia, as the resident immune cells in the retina, play a critical role in neuroinflammation under pathological conditions [47]. However, the specific role of microglial activation and phenotypic switching in RD-induced photoreceptor degeneration remains incompletely understood.

The accurate profiling of microglial responses hinges on the choice of appropriate cellular markers. While P2ry12 is often selected as a specific microglial marker in the murine retina [8, 33], our study revealed its limitation in the context of acute RD. At 3 days post-injury, very few P2ry12-positive cells were detected in the ONL and subretinal space, which are the primary sites of photoreceptor degeneration. In contrast, numerous Iba-1-positive cells were readily observed at these lesion sites. This disparity is explained by the distinct biological properties of these markers: P2ry12 is a canonical homeostatic marker whose expression is rapidly downregulated upon microglial activation [48, 49], whereas Iba-1 is a pan-myeloid marker stably expressed across both resting and activated states. Consequently, Iba-1 provides a more complete mapping of the total myeloid response. Moreover, to further confirm the origin of the Iba-1-positive cells, we employed Cx3cr1CreERT2;Ai14 mice to distinguish resident microglia from infiltrating monocytes. The near-complete overlap of Iba-1 immunoreactivity with tdTomato-labeled cells demonstrated that the majority of responding myeloid cells were derived from the resident microglial pool. Thus, the use of Iba-1, validated by direct lineage tracing, enabled a comprehensive and accurate assessment of microglial activation and distribution in our experimental RD model.

DAM, also entitled as “neurodegenerative microglia (MGnD)”, has been widely reported as an activated microglial subtype with enhanced capacity for phagocytosing debris and secreting cytokines. DAM differentiation occurs through a sequential two-step process. The transition from step 1 to step 2 DAM is dependent on the Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells 2 (Trem2), whereas the initial shift from homeostatic microglia to step 1 DAM is Trem2-independent; the mediators of this first transition remain largely unknown [50]. In the rhodopsin knockout mouse model, inhibition of excessive DAM activation potentially protects against photoreceptor degeneration [51]. In contrast, Trem2 deficiency in the N-methyl-N-nitrosourea (MNU)-induced retinal degeneration model diminished DAM activation and exacerbated photoreceptor loss [52]. Here, we provide compelling evidence that inhibition of DAM transition significantly reduced neurodegenerative microglial responses, suppressed retinal inflammation, and rescued retinal disorder in RD mice. Our findings align with several recent studies indicating that blockade of DAM transition exerts neuroprotective effects in retinal diseases [19, 51]. However, one study reported that Trem2 deficiency downregulated DAM markers and accelerated photoreceptor death after RD [53]. A potential explanation is that Trem2 deficiency may restrain step 2 DAM transition, leading to accumulation of pro-inflammatory step 1 DAM and consequently deteriorate retinal damage. Together with current evidence, our findings suggest that DAM may play context-dependent roles in different retinal disorders, and the regulatory mechanisms and functions of DAM in neurodegenerative diseases require further investigation.

ApoE and Trem2 are both signature factors of DAM. As a major lipid metabolism mediator in the CNS, ApoE has previously been reported to be upregulated in the aqueous humor and retinal tissues of glaucoma patients [54–56]. Earlier studies showed that ApoE depletion ameliorates the neurodegeneration level in an AMD model [57]. Furthermore, the ApoE4 genotype was found to strengthen both retinal vasculature and neuronal structure in ageing mice [21, 58]. Consistent with these reports, our data suggest a protective role for both ApoE deficiency and humanized ApoE4 in maintaining microglial homeostasis and preserving photoreceptors.

It should be noted that ApoE4 is a major genetic risk factor of Alzheimer’s disease [59]. Several studies have reported that ApoE4 facilitates abnormal lipid accumulation and exacerbates neuronal loss in the brain [40, 60]. While prior studies in aging or degenerative retinal disorders consistently report ApoE4-induced pathology [61, 62], our work using an acute RD model demonstrates a protective function, wherein humanized ApoE4 mitigates microglia-mediated photoreceptor degeneration. This apparent discrepancy highlights that ApoE4’s impact is not universal but shaped by key experimental and biological variables.

The divergence may stems from three principal factors. First, the disease model's underlying pathology and temporal dynamics differ fundamentally. Chronic models involve cumulative metabolic and synaptic stress over months, where ApoE4 exacerbates damage. In contrast, acute RD triggers a rapid inflammatory wave, wherein ApoE4 may serve to modulate the initial microglial response, preventing excessive collateral injury, a beneficial role that is obscured in slower pathologies. Second, the primary cellular targets and mechanisms vary. Previous work implicates ApoE4 in direct synaptic toxicity and Müller cell dysfunction, while our model highlights its role in regulating the acute innate immune response via microglia. ApoE4’s involvement in lipid signaling may thus yield opposite outcomes: detrimental in chronic neuroinflammation yet beneficial in acute injury by promoting a more regulated microglial phenotype. Third, the temporal window of analysis is critical. Our observed protection is acute; over extended time, this early beneficial signaling might evolve into chronic inflammation, converging with deleterious outcomes seen in other models. Therefore, the function of ApoE4 necessitates precisely tailored therapeutic strategies: inhibiting ApoE4 may be warranted in chronic neurodegeneration, whereas preserving or mimicking its function could be beneficial during acute CNS injury.

Consistent with previous research [19], our findings indicate that Galectin-3 is potentially regulated by ApoE and the ApoE4 allele in retinal microglia. Galectin-3 is widely recognized as a pro-inflammatory cytokine in numerous neuroinflammatory eye diseases [56, 63, 64]. To probe its function, we employed TD139, a high-affinity, selective small-molecule inhibitor that acts by binding directly to the carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) of Galectin-3. This binding competitively blocks Galectin-3’s interaction with endogenous glycoprotein ligands, thereby inhibiting its activity [65]. Here, we found that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of Galectin-3 led to a significant downregulation not only of its own expression but also of classical DAM markers such as Spp-1 and Gpnmb [66]. As noted, this result suggests that Galectin-3 inhibition likely disrupts a pathogenic feed-forward signaling loop, extending beyond merely blocking its extracellular lectin activity. We propose the following mechanistic model: following RD, Galectin-3 is induced within the ApoE-enriched microenvironment and functions as a central amplifier of the microglial inflammatory response. Secreted Galectin-3 can engage cell surface receptors (e.g., TLR4, MARCKS) on microglia in an autocrine/paracrine manner [31, 67], thereby activating downstream pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as NF-κB and STAT3 [68, 69]. These activated factors, in turn, can bind to the promoter regions and drive the transcription of LGALS3, Spp-1, and Gpnmb, establishing a self-reinforcing cycle that perpetuates and exacerbates the DAM phenotype and photoreceptor toxicity. This model is supported by prior studies in other chronic diseases where Galectin-3 has been shown to sustain its own expression alongside that of related inflammatory mediators [68, 70]. Consequently, the therapeutic efficacy of Galectin-3 deficiency may stem from its capacity to interrupt this vicious cycle at its core, leading to a coordinated collapse of the entire pathogenic gene program. This feed-forward mechanism implies that Galectin-3 may not serve as a passive biomarker but as a master regulator of pathological microglial activation in RD, which explains the profound protective effects observed following its genetic or pharmacological inhibition.

By binding to the TLR4, Galectin-3 stimulates microglial activation, enhances phagocytic activity, and aggravates detrimental inflammatory cascades [71]. Additionally, TLR4 serves as an endogenous checkpoint for pyroptosis, triggering inflammasome assembly and downstream cytokine secretion [72]. Recent studies have also identified pyroptotic microglial activation as an important pathogenic mechanism in retinal disorders [33, 34]. These findings led us to further investigate whether pyroptosis-related inflammatory responses operate through an ApoE-Galectin-3-dependent pathway. Interestingly, our results suggest that both Galectin-3 deficiency and ApoE4 expression significantly reduced TLR4-mediated pyroptotic inflammation, as evidenced by robust suppression of NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and gasdermin D (Gsdmd) cleavage in TD139-treated, ApoE4-expressing, and ApoE-deficient mice. Furthermore, targeting of both Galectin-3 and ApoE simultaneously did not confer additional anti-inflammatory or photoreceptor protective effects compared to individual interference. Collectively, these data support the conclusion that Galectin-3 acts as a critical downstream effector of ApoE in driving DAM-like microglial activation following RD. Targeting ApoE-Galectin-3 may represent a promising neuroprotective strategy for RD and other neurodegenerative ocular diseases.

PLX3397 is a CSF1R inhibitor and a specific microglia-depleting agent. Prior studies found that PLX3397 gavage effectively eliminated 85–90% of microglia in the brain and retinal tissue of mice [12, 73]. We administered PLX3397 in this study to further determine whether the protective effects of ApoE knockout or humanized ApoE4 expression are strictly dependent on the presence of microglia. In lineage-traced mice, this depletion specifically targeted tdTomato+ resident microglia without creating a niche for Iba-1+ tdTomato− monocyte-derived macrophage infiltration, and most critically, the depletion of microglia significantly reversed the photoreceptor protection conferred by either ApoE knockout or hApoE4 expression. Therefore, these results provide direct genetic evidence that the beneficial outcomes of modulating the ApoE pathway are microglia-dependent. The reversal of protection upon microglial elimination indicates that in our acute RD model, ApoE deficiency or hApoE4 expression does not merely remove the detrimental DAM-related signal but actively promotes a microglial state that is essential for tissue resilience. The loss of protection with PLX3397 is due to the removal of these beneficial effector cells themselves.

Our study has several limitations. First, we used global ApoE knockout mice because ApoE-floxed mice were not accessible in our region, which precluded cell type-specific deletion of ApoE. Second, it is important to note that our acute RD model operates within a short therapeutic window, whereas in clinical practice, diagnosis and intervention for RD patients are sometimes delayed beyond this early phase. Third, although human vitreous samples were collected and analyzed, further studies are warranted to examine the distribution and expression of ApoE isoforms in retinal tissues from RD patients using donor eyeballs.

In summary, our study demonstrates that the ApoE-Galectin-3 signaling pathway is activated in retinal microglia following experimental RD. We highlight that modulation of ApoE significantly ameliorates RD-induced DAM transition, photoreceptor loss, and retinal inflammation. We further reveal that the ApoE4 allele—commonly known as a risk factor in Alzheimer’s disease—confers protection against neurodegenerative microglial activation and improves photoreceptor survival. Targeting the ApoE-Galectin-3 signaling axis may represent a promising neuroprotective strategy for RD and other photoreceptor degenerative disorders.
