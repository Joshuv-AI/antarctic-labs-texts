Mesenchymal stromal cells-loaded 3D radially aligned composite scaffold with potentiated paracrine signaling for sequential bone regeneration
Authors: Lei Fang, Min He, Tao Zhang, Bowen Gong, Li Ruan, Jichuan Qiu, Jiajia Xue, Feng Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Critical-sized bone defects remain a major clinical challenge because they lack the intrinsic capacity to heal and cannot orchestrate the sequential processes of inflammation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis required for regeneration. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) offer potent paracrine signaling, yet therapeutic efficacy is constrained by poor survival in the early inflammatory milieu and the intrinsic plasticity of MSCs, which leads to attenuation of paracrine activity as inflammation resolves, limiting sustained support across all regenerative phases. Here, we present a 3D radially aligned nanofiber platform comprising hydroxyapatite (HAp)-incorporated fibers with a GelMA hydrogel encapsulating bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs). The radial architecture promotes early centripetal cell infiltration, GelMA preserves encapsulated BMSCs viability and residency to extend paracrine signaling, and HAp provides durable osteoconductive cues while amplifying encapsulated BMSC paracrine output, thereby expediting the immune-angiogenic-osteogenic transition. In vitro metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed that upregulation of glycerophospholipid metabolism supports early BMSC proliferation, while activation of PI3K/Akt signaling drives osteogenic commitment. In rat subcutaneous implant and critical-sized cranial defect model, the composite scaffold attenuated early inflammation and markedly enhanced bone regeneration. Overall, this paracrine-enhancing platform integrates radial topography, sustained paracrine amplification, and persistent osteoconductive support to achieve temporally coordinated regulation of cranial bone repair with high translational potential.

Introduction

Bone defects, particularly those exceeding the critical size after trauma or tumor resection, remain major clinical challenges owing to the limited intrinsic regenerative capacity of bone tissue [1,2] and disruption of the highly coordinated sequence of inflammation, angiogenesis, osteogenesis, and biomineralization [[3], [4], [5]]. Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-based therapies are increasingly applied in clinical practice [6]. The 2025 International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy (ISCT) guidelines further standardize clinical identification, emphasizing the term stromal rather than stem and underscoring paracrine activity rather than engraftment-driven differentiation as the principal therapeutic mechanism [7,8]. These paracrine programs regulate immune responses, promote vascular regeneration, and enhance osteogenic differentiation [9]. However, their efficacy in vivo is constrained by two interrelated factors: poor MSC survival in the early inflammatory milieu [10] and the inherent plasticity of paracrine programs, which rapidly decline as inflammation resolves [11,12].

To address these challenges, diverse biomaterial strategies have been developed to integrate MSCs with scaffolds, aiming to preserve viability, enhance retention, and modulate local microenvironments [13,14]. Hydrogels such as GelMA [15,16], alginate [17], hyaluronic acid [18,19], and fibrin [20] are widely used carriers, yet rapid degradation and limited mechanical strength compromise their long-term performance [21]. Electrospun nanofibers composed of polymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) and poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) [22,23] recapitulate extracellular matrix (ECM) architecture and offer improved mechanical support, but conventional two-dimensional mats lack the three-dimensional (3D) architecture necessary to sustain MSC retention [24]. 3D printing and bioprinting provide structural complexity, yet prolonged cell encapsulation during fabrication imposes stress that diminishes MSC functionality [25]. Critically, most existing platforms primarily protect MSCs from inflammatory damage but fail to sustain paracrine activity against cellular plasticity, limiting their ability to orchestrate bone regeneration across sequential healing stages. This underscores the urgent need for clinically translatable composite scaffolds that integrate multiple inductive cues to sustain long-term MSC paracrine function and thereby drive continuous bone regeneration.

Nanofiber scaffolds fabricated by electrospinning recapitulate ECM architecture and hold considerable clinical promise [26,27]. When engineered into intrinsically 3D architectures, they provide topographical cues that regulate cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation [28]. Incorporation of bioactive components further enables these scaffolds to support proliferation, differentiation, and biomineralization over extended periods, thereby offering stage-spanning control of bone regeneration [26,29,30]. Hydroxyapatite (HAp), a mineral analogue of native bone, has consistently been shown to elicit sustained osteogenic effects when integrated into nanofiber scaffolds [[31], [32], [33]]. For hydrogels, GelMA, a photocrosslinkable gelatin derivative, combines biocompatibility, tunable mechanics, and abundant adhesion motifs, making MSC-loaded GelMA systems attractive for clinical translation [34]. To counteract diminished paracrine activity, several strategies have been investigated, including hypoxic preconditioning [35,36] or stimulation with growth factors such as BMP-2 [37]. However, hypoxic exposure provides only transient enhancement, while exogenous growth factors suffer from intrinsic instability. Notably, HAp has also been reported to potentiate MSC paracrine signaling, for example by augmenting VEGF secretion, thereby enhancing both angiogenic and osteogenic responses [38,39]. Building on this evidence, integrating 3D nanofibrous structure, HAp, GelMA microenvironment with MSCs could provide robust structural and mechanical support while sustaining and amplifying MSC paracrine activity, thus enabling continuous regulation of the healing process [11,40].

Here, we present a paracrine-enhancing, 3D radially aligned composite scaffold composed of HAp-incorporated polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibers and GelMA hydrogel encapsulating bone marrow-derived MSCs (PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) (Scheme 1). The PCL/HAp framework, fabricated by electrospinning and gas foaming, forms a highly porous, radially oriented architecture that promotes centripetal cell migration and provides sustained osteoconductive ionic cues. Encapsulation of BMSCs within GelMA preserves cellular viability and retention, while HAp synergistically amplifies BMSC paracrine signaling across immunomodulatory, angiogenic, and osteogenic axes, thereby supporting the full continuum of bone healing. Designed from a clinical perspective, the platform leverages clinically relevant components (PCL, HAp, GelMA, and MSCs) and scalable fabrication approaches (electrospinning and gas foaming), offering a manufacturing-ready scaffold with strong translational potential.Scheme 1Schematic diagram of the MSC-encapsulated 3D Radially Aligned Composite Scaffold with Paracrine Potentiation for Temporally Coordinated Bone Regeneration.Scheme 1

Results

Construction and characterization of 3D composite scaffolds

The 3D composite scaffold was constructed by integrating a 3D PCL/HAp nanofiber scaffold with a BMSCs-encapsulated GelMA hydrogel. The radially aligned PCL/HAp nanofiber scaffold was fabricated by thermally welding aligned nanofiber membranes perpendicular to the fiber orientation, followed by a gas-foaming process (Fig. 1A) [28]. To fabricate the 3D PCL/HAp scaffold, 2D aligned nanofiber membranes were electrospun from a PCL solution containing 50 wt% HAp relative to PCL (Fig. 1B–S1). This loading ratio, consistent with prior studies and representing a high loading ratio stably achieved under the current fabrication and gas-foaming process conditions [41,42], increased the solution viscosity and reduced the average fiber diameter from 520 ± 274 nm (PCL nanofibers) to 490 ± 170 nm (PCL/HAp nanofibers) (Fig. S2) [43]. FTIR spectra confirmed HAp incorporation, with characteristic P-O (603 cm−1), OH− (631 cm−1), and PO43− (1040 cm−1) vibration bands observed alongside PCL peaks at 1726, 1180, and 2867 cm−1 (Fig. S3A). The XRD patterns show that the PCL/HAp composite scaffold retains the characteristic diffraction peaks of both PCL and HAp, with no new peaks or significant peak shifts observed, indicating that HAp was successfully incorporated and the composite process did not disrupt the crystal structures of the individual components (Fig. S3B). Elemental mapping further revealed uniform Ca and P distribution in PCL/HAp nanofibers (Fig. S4). SEM imaging of the radially aligned 3D scaffold confirmed an interconnected porous architecture with large pores (500 ± 286 μm, Fig. 1B, Fig. S5), which facilitated hydrogel perfusion and directional cell infiltration. Crosslinking time dictates hydrogel network density, thereby determining its stiffness, degradation, and cell infiltration. To identify an optimal balance among these properties, 5% (w/v) GelMA hydrogels were photocrosslinked (405 nm light) for 10, 20, or 30 s and systematically evaluated. SEM revealed a uniform microporous structure across all curing conditions (Fig. S7), with an average pore size of 60.5 ± 13.1 μm for the 30 s condition (Fig. S8), facilitating effective cell attachment and nutrient exchange. Swelling and degradation tests showed that longer curing times led to reduced swelling ratios (44%, 38%, and 35% for 10, 20, and 30 s after 24 h, respectively) and slowed degradation (mass loss of 80.3%, 60.0%, and 47.6% after 16 d; Fig. S9). Given that the typical evaluation period for bone regeneration is generally two months, all groups achieved timely degradation to permit tissue ingrowth. Mechanical testing showed curing-time-dependent reinforcement, with compressive strength increasing from 63.5 kPa (10 s) to 233.2 kPa (30 s) and modulus from 3.2 to 8.7 kPa, thereby providing mechanical properties suitable for an early regenerative tissue or cell-adaptive microenvironment (Fig. S10). For cytocompatibility evaluation, BMSCs were first transfected with a GFP-expressing lentivirus at an optimized multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, yielding high transduction efficiency within 24 h (Fig. S11). The resulting GFP-labeled BMSCs were encapsulated in GelMA via in situ photocrosslinking. Live/dead staining revealed that the cells successfully adhered, proliferated, and formed interconnected networks within the 30s cured hydrogel by day 5 across all groups, with the 30 s hydrogel maintaining comparable cytocompatibility (Fig. S12). Thus, the 30 s condition, balancing degradability, mechanics, and cytocompatibility, was selected for subsequent experiments.

The composite scaffold was then generated by immersing the 3D PCL/HAp framework into the BMSCs-loaded GelMA precursor, followed by in situ polymerization. SEM imaging of the composite scaffold confirmed uniform GelMA filling without compromising pore size or radial integrity (Fig. 1B). As the in situ polymerization could potentially alter the overall scaffold properties, mechanical analysis was performed. The composite scaffold exhibited a comparable swelling ratio, whereas degradation proceeded more rapidly, with 65% mass loss by day 16 compared with 51.2% for GelMA alone (Fig. 1C and D). This accelerated degradation was likely due to reduced crosslinking efficiency during integration. Mechanically, the composite scaffold exhibited a 1.56-fold increase in compressive strength and a 1.39-fold increase in compressive modulus relative to GelMA (Fig. 1E-G). Incorporation of BMSCs slightly reduced these properties, presumably due to space occupation and network disruption. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the composite scaffold achieves appropriate mechanical robustness, controlled degradation, and sustained cell viability, establishing a microenvironment favorable for harnessing BMSC paracrine signaling. Given that the compressive properties remain within the kPa range, this system is particularly suited for non-load-bearing applications, such as cancellous bone defects and cranial bone repair, where biological regulation and guided regeneration are more critical than high load-bearing capacity. The ion release curves showed that both PCL/HAp and PCL/HAp-GelMA composites exhibited a sustained release of Ca2+ and PO43− ions with prolonged immersion time, and the GelMA coating did not significantly hinder ion release, which enhanced the long-term bioactivity and osteoinductive potential of the material (Fig. 1H).

Enhanced paracrine signaling via HAp-Incorporated scaffolds

Enhancing the paracrine function of encapsulated BMSCs is central to this scaffold-mediated bone repair, as secreted factors orchestrate immune modulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis [13,44]. We first evaluated cell viability within the composite scaffold, a prerequisite for sustained paracrine activity. Live/dead fluorescence imaging at days 1, 3, 5, and 14 revealed progressive cellular spreading and interconnectivity, culminating in extensive network formation by day 14, confirming excellent cytocompatibility of the composite scaffold (Fig. 2A). To investigate scaffold-dependent modulation of paracrine activity, three groups were compared: PCL-BMSCs, with BMSCs seeded on a PCL scaffold; PCL-GelMA/BMSCs, where BMSCs were encapsulated in GelMA hydrogel of PCL-GelMA scaffold; PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs, where BMSCs were encapsulated in GelMA hydrogel of PCL/HAp-GelMA scaffold. At day 3, the baseline secretion levels in PCL-BMSCs were TGF-β 163.06 pg/mL, PGE2 218.92 pg/mL, VEGF 570.95 pg/mL, HGF 458.48 pg/mL, and BMP-2 7.87 ng/mL. Relative to this control, TGF-β secretion increased to 1.54-fold (p < 0.05) in PCL-GelMA/BMSCs and 1.84-fold (p < 0.01) in PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs, while PGE2 (218 pg/mL) rose to 1.48-fold (p < 0.01) and 1.81-fold (p < 0.0001), respectively. This early upregulation indicates a transition toward a pro-resolving, regenerative immune milieu. For angiogenic cues, VEGF exhibited a modest elevation with PCL-GelMA/BMSCs (1.38-fold, ns) and a significant increase with PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs (1.57-fold, p < 0.01), consistent with early priming rather than full angiogenic activation. In contrast, HGF and BMP-2 showed no significant differences, suggesting that osteogenic drivers were not yet dominant. Together, the day 3 profile highlights stage-specific paracrine activity favoring immunomodulation and early angiogenesis.

By day 7, the baseline secretion levels in PCL-BMSCs shifted to TGF-β 272.02 pg/mL, PGE2 413.99 pg/mL, VEGF 1104.37 pg/mL, HGF 851.55 pg/mL, and BMP-2 19.07 ng/mL. The paracrine signature shifted toward robust angiogenic and osteogenic activity. Although TGF-β and PGE2 secretion in the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group remained significantly higher than PCL controls (1.73-fold, p < 0.01 and 1.52-fold, p < 0.05, respectively), both declined relative to their day 3 levels. Similarly, VEGF secretion remained comparable increase relative to day 3 levels. In contrast, HGF and BMP-2 were markedly upregulated. In the PCL-GelMA/BMSCs group, HGF rose to 1.72-fold (p < 0.001) and BMP-2 to 1.27-fold (p < 0.05). Notably, HAp incorporation further amplified these effects, elevating HGF to 2.44-fold (p < 0.0001) and BMP-2 to 1.92-fold (p < 0.0001). Thus, by day 7, angiogenic and osteogenic mediators (HGF, BMP-2) became predominant, with the HAp-containing scaffold exerting the strongest stimulatory effect.

To further elucidate the molecular basis of the paracrine response, transcriptional profiles of relevant genes were analyzed by qPCR (Fig. 2C). At day 3, early activation of a pro-osteogenic program was evident. Compared with the PCL-BMSCs group, TGFB1 and the PGE2-synthesis–related gene PTGS2 mRNA were upregulated in the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group by 2.62-fold (p < 0.01) and 2.17-fold (p < 0.05), respectively. Notably, BMP-2 mRNA expression showed a striking induction, with a 5.23-fold increase (p < 0.001) in PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs and a 2.41-fold increase (p < 0.01) in PCL-GelMA/BMSCs. By day 7, this transcriptional program was reinforced and expanded. PTGS2, VEGFA, and HGF mRNA were significantly elevated in both PCL-GelMA/BMSCs and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffolds, whereas TGFB1 returned to baseline. HAp incorporation exerted the strongest stimulatory effect, particularly on HGF (5.47-fold, p < 0.001), while BMP2 expression remained elevated in PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs (4.94-fold, p < 0.01). Notably, the pronounced BMP-2 mRNA upregulation at day 3 preceded the peak of its protein secretion at day 7, consistent with the temporal sequence of transcription-translation coupling in protein biosynthesis.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that GelMA provides a supportive baseline microenvironment that preserves encapsulated BMSC paracrine activity, while HAp incorporation amplifies and diversifies these signals. Together, they coordinate immunomodulatory, angiogenic, and osteogenic programs in a sequential and sustained manner, underscoring scaffold-mediated potentiation as the driver of effective bone regeneration.

Immunomodulation and pro-angiogenesis modulation

Given the scaffold's ability to enhance the paracrine profile of BMSCs, particularly the early upregulation of immunomodulatory factors, we next investigated its effect on macrophage polarization, a critical determinant of a pro-regenerative microenvironment [44,45]. To induce a pro-inflammatory phenotype, RAW 264.7 macrophages were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and subsequently cultured with extracts from different scaffolds (PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) prepared by incubation in medium for 3 d. Immunofluorescence staining indicated that scaffolds without encapsulated BMSCs had minimal impact, whereas the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group markedly suppressed the M1 marker CD86 and enhanced the M2 marker CD206 (Fig. 3A-C). This transition toward an anti-inflammatory, pro-regenerative phenotype was further validated by qPCR, which showed downregulation of M1-associated genes TNF-α (0.85-fold) and NOS2 (0.81-fold), alongside upregulation of M2-associated genes CD163 (1.63-fold) and MRC1 (2.60-fold), relative to the PCL group (Fig. 3D). Building on the elevated secretion of angiogenic factors such as VEGF and HGF, we next assessed scaffold-induced angiogenesis, as vascularization is essential for sustaining cell viability and promoting osteogenesis during bone repair. A HUVEC tube formation assay was conducted on Matrigel using extracts collected after 7 d of scaffold incubation. All groups containing HAp, GelMA, or BMSCs promoted tubule formation compared with controls (Fig. 3E). Notably, extracts from the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffold elicited the most pronounced effect, increasing tube numbers by 48.7% relative to the Blank group (Fig. 3F). The potent angiogenic response likely arises from the synergy of complementary mechanisms: bioactive RGD-containing peptides released from degrading GelMA matrix [46,47], ionic cues (e.g., Ca2+) released from HAp [48,49], and angiogenic factors secreted by BMSCs, all amplified within the composite microenvironment.

Regulation of BMSCs behavior: migration, proliferation, and differentiation

Effective recruitment of endogenous progenitor cells to the defect site is a prerequisite for successful in situ osteogenic differentiation [50,51]. To simulate this process in vitro, GFP-labeled BMSCs were encapsulated in GelMA gels, and scaffolds from different groups (PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) were positioned within the gels to establish a gel–scaffold migration model that allowed directional migration. After 3 d, confocal microscopy revealed scaffold composition-dependent migration, with the highest density observed in PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs groups (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis indicated that 698 ± 22 cells migrated into the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffold, 1.3-fold increase than the PCL group (Fig. 4B). To further confirm the pro-migratory activity, a Transwell migration assay was performed. Scaffolds were placed in the lower chamber and BMSCs were seeded in the upper chamber. After 24 h, migrated cells on the underside of the membrane were stained, imaged, and quantified. Migration was promoted by individual components (HAp, GelMA, or BMSCs), whereas the integrated composite scaffold induced the strongest effect, increasing migrated cells by 3.1-fold relative to the Blank group (Fig. S13A and S13B). Following initial recruitment, local expansion through proliferation is essential for matrix deposition. To avoid confounding effects of pre-encapsulated cells, BMSC proliferation was evaluated on cell-free PCL, PCL/HAp, and PCL/HAp-GelMA scaffolds for 5 d. All groups supported robust proliferation (Fig. S14) with PCL/HAp-GelMA showing the highest growth rates, indicating the addition of GelMA was particularly favorable for this process. Cytoskeletal staining further revealed that cells elongated and aligned along the radial fiber orientation, indicating topographical guidance of organized cell growth (Fig. S15).

The therapeutic efficacy of a scaffold eventually depends on directing recruited cells toward osteogenic differentiation. Osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs on different scaffolds (Blank, PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) was evaluated by ALP staining at day 7 and ARS staining at day 14. The PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffolds exhibited markedly higher ALP activity and calcium deposition compared with the Blank and PCL groups (Fig. 4C–S16). In PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs, ALP activity was 1.4-fold higher than in PCL and 1.2-fold higher than in PCL/HAp, while ARS-positive mineralization was 2.0-fold and 1.4-fold higher, respectively (Fig. 4D and E).

Immunofluorescence staining further assessed the osteogenic markers including OPN, RUNX-2, and OCN. The fluorescent images revealed that cells exhibited an elongated, oriented morphology along the radial nanofibers (Fig. 4F). Semi-quantitative analysis confirmed that OPN, RUNX-2, and OCN expression levels in PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group were 1.7-, 3.2-, and 2.3-fold relative to PCL group, respectively. To corroborate this pro-osteogenic efficacy at the transcriptional level, we analyzed pivotal osteogenic markers, including RUNX-2, an early transcription factor crucial for osteoblast differentiation; ALP, OPN, OCN, and COL-1, key matrix proteins indicative of bone formation; and BMP-2, an important osteoinductive growth factor. A clear trend was observed: as bioactive components (HAp, GelMA, and BMSCs) were incorporated into the PCL scaffold, the mRNA expression of these markers progressively increased, with the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group showing the highest expression for all analyzed genes (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the combined presence of HAp, GelMA, and BMSCs synergistically enhances BMSC recruitment, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation, thereby orchestrating a coordinated regenerative cascade from initial migration to matrix mineralization at both protein and gene expression levels.

Mechanistic insights: metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis

Building on the proliferation and differentiation readouts, we performed untargeted metabolomics to resolve scaffold-driven alterations in the baseline metabolic network. BMSCs were cultured with or without PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffolds for 7 d in proliferation medium, a design that isolated material-level metabolic influences while minimizing confounding from osteogenic supplements.

The circular plot of metabolite category composition (Fig. 5A) outlined the overall distribution, with major classes including Benzene and substituted derivatives (17.38%), Amino acids and their metabolites (15.40%), Heterocyclic compounds (10.59%), Organic acids and their derivatives (9.53%), Glycerophospholipids (GP, 8.54%), Aldehydes, ketones, and esters (6.55%), Fatty acyls (FA, 5.79%). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) revealed clear group separation (Fig. 5B), indicating distinct scaffold-induced metabolic reprogramming. Volcano plot analysis (Fig. 5C) identified 1551 detected metabolites, among which 42 were significantly altered (20 upregulated, 22 downregulated) between the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs and Blank groups. Differential metabolite analysis further highlighted scaffold-responsive signatures. Heatmap clustering (Fig. 5D) showed widespread upregulation of GP and FA-related metabolites, including phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and phosphatidylinositol (PI). The radar plot of the top 20 VIP-ranked differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) (Fig. S17) confirmed that the majority belonged to GP subclasses. Global correlation heatmaps (Fig. S18) further depicted overall correlation patterns among DEMs, highlighting extensive associations within lipid-related metabolites. To resolve key relationships, pairwise correlation analysis visualized by circos chord plots (Fig. 5E) revealed strong intra- and inter-class correlations, particularly among GP, FA, and organic acid metabolites, which was further validated by Pearson correlation analysis of differentially correlated metabolites (Fig. S19). Together, these findings suggest that the observed remodeling of glycerophospholipid metabolism arises from both altered membrane lipid turnover and phosphate-related signaling modulated by HAp degradation, collectively supporting bone regeneration.

These molecular mechanisms are highly consistent with their osteogenic effects at the cellular levels, providing a solid theoretical basis for their application in bone tissue engineering.

Modulation of the in vivo local immune microenvironment

The early immune response following implantation is a key determinant of bone regeneration, dictating the transition from acute inflammation to a pro-regenerative phase. Building on the in vitro findings that the composite scaffold enhanced BMSC secretion of immunomodulatory factors, we next examined its ability to modulate the local immune milieu in vivo. Scaffolds were implanted subcutaneously in rats for 5 d, followed by histological and immunohistochemical assessment.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed good biocompatibility across all groups, with no evidence of acute inflammatory infiltration in the PCL, PCL/HAp, or PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs implants (Fig. 7A). Notably, the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffold exhibited a particularly mild and well-organized cellular response surrounding the implant. To further dissect macrophage dynamics, immunofluorescence staining was performed on day 5 (Fig. 7B). While macrophages were recruited around all implants, their polarization profile differed markedly. CD86 expression (M1 marker) was reduced to 20.3% in the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group compared with 34.3% in PCL and 31.7% in PCL/HAp (Fig. 7C and D), indicating attenuated pro-inflammatory activity. Conversely, CD206 expression (M2 marker) was highest in the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group (32.3%), representing an 8.1-fold and 1.3-fold increase over PCL and PCL/HAp, respectively. This polarization shift highlights a scaffold-driven transition toward an M2-dominant, anti-inflammatory, pro-regenerative macrophage phenotype, mediated by BMSC paracrine signaling. Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed these findings. Expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β was significantly reduced in the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs group, reaching only 67.7% and 62.4% of the levels in PCL and PCL/HAp scaffolds, respectively (Fig. 7E and F). In contrast, IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, was elevated 1.7- and 1.2-fold compared with the PCL and PCL/HAp groups, respectively (Fig. 7G). In vivo osteogenic potential in a rat cranial defect model

To assess scaffold efficacy in a clinically relevant setting, a critical-sized rat cranial defect model was employed. After 8 weeks of implantation, bone regeneration was examined by Micro-CT and histological analyses to determine the combined effects of scaffold structure, osteoinductivity, and BMSC paracrine signaling. As depicted in the 3D reconstructions (Fig. 8A), new bone formation occurred in all groups, progressing from the defect margins toward the center. This healing pattern indicates that osteogenesis was predominantly mediated by migration and differentiation of host-derived cells rather than direct ossification by transplanted BMSCs. Compared with the Blank group, the PCL/HAp scaffold promoted greater bone formation, primarily attributable to its 3D topographical guidance that facilitates cell infiltration and tissue ingrowth, together with the osteoconductive contribution of HAp. Moreover, regenerated bone in this group displayed a more mature trabecular structure, with increased trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, 0.16 mm) and reduced trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, 0.12 mm) compared to both PCL and PCL/HAp scaffolds. Conclusion

In summary, we developed a 3D radially aligned composite scaffold (PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) that addresses the multifaceted challenge of critical-sized bone defect repair by coordinating sequential healing stages. The scaffold integrates radially aligned PCL/HAp nanofibers that provide early topographical guidance, GelMA hydrogels that preserve BMSC viability and retention, and HAp that delivers persistent osteoconductive cues while amplifying the paracrine activity of encapsulated BMSCs. This paracrine enhancement increased the secretion of anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and osteogenic factors, reprogrammed the immune microenvironment toward a pro-regenerative phenotype, and promoted BMSC migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation in vitro. Multi-omics analyses further implicated glycerophospholipid metabolism in early proliferative responses and PI3K/Akt signaling in osteogenic commitment. In a rat critical-sized cranial defect, the composite scaffold mitigated early inflammation and markedly accelerated the formation of mature, contiguous bone. Together, these findings establish a clinically relevant strategy in which material-driven amplification of MSC paracrine signaling is integrated with stage-appropriate structural and osteoconductive cues to achieve temporally coordinated regulation and promote comprehensive cranial bone regeneration.

Preparation and characterization of the 3D PCL/HAp scaffold

Aligned 2D PCL/HAp nanofiber membranes were fabricated via electrospinning a TFE solution containing 10% (w/v) PCL, 5% (w/v) HAp, and 0.5% (w/v) Pluronic F-127. The process was conducted at 18 kV with a rotating drum collector (1700 rpm). The membranes were then treated with oxygen plasma (30 s) to enhance hydrophilicity. To create the 3D architecture, membranes were cut, thermally fixed perpendicular to the fiber direction, and immersed in 1 M NaBH4 for 3 h to induce gas foaming, followed by washing and freeze-drying for 12 h. The morphology and pore size of the resulting 3D scaffold framework were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, S-4800, Japan).

Fabrication and characterization of the PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSC composite scaffold

BMSCs, isolated from rat femurs, were cultured in α-MEM (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin). To visualize and track the cells in subsequent experiments, BMSCs were transduced by incubation with a GFP-PURO lentivirus at a concentration of 106 pU/mL for 24 h. Transduction efficiency was confirmed by laser scanning confocal microscope (CLSM), and the resulting GFP-labeled BMSCs were harvested and cryopreserved for future use.

For the 3D Composite Scaffold Assembly and Characterization, a 5% (w/v) GelMA in PBS solution containing 0.25% LAP photoinitiator was prepared, and GFP-BMSCs were suspended within it at a density of 106 cells/mL. The PCL/HAp scaffold frameworks were then placed in molds, infused with the BMSC-loaded GelMA solution, and photocrosslinked (405 nm, 30 s) to form the final composite scaffolds. The key physicochemical properties of these composite scaffolds and their components were then characterized. Swelling behavior and degradation rates were determined by monitoring weight changes in PBS at 37 °C. Degradation rates were determined by monitoring mass loss over 16 d. Compressive properties were tested using a universal testing machine with a 10 N load cell, with the modulus calculated from the initial linear region (0–10% strain) of the stress-strain curve. Finally, the viability and distribution of the encapsulated BMSCs were evaluated at days 1, 3, 5, and 14 by staining with Calcein-AM (live cells) and Propidium Iodide (dead cells) and imaging via CLSM.

Paracrine signaling analysis of BMSCs in scaffold

To evaluate the paracrine activity of BMSCs, three experimental groups were established: PCL (control), PCL-GelMA/BMSCs, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs. For each group, 1 × 104 BMSCs were encapsulated within the respective scaffold constructs. Different scaffolds were cultured for 3 and 7 d, after which the culture supernatants were collected. The supernatants were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to remove cell debris. The concentrations of secreted factors (TGF-β, PGE2, VEGF, HGF, and BMP-2) in the resulting supernatants were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To account for potential differences in cell numbers, the total protein content in each parallel sample was determined using a BCA assay. The final ELISA results were normalized to the total protein content and expressed as fold change relative to the control group.

For gene expression analysis, BMSCs were cultured on the same three scaffold groups in parallel. At day 3 and day 7, total RNA was extracted from the cells using the FastPure Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Reverse transcription was performed to synthesize cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme). Subsequently, quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) to detect the expression of genes encoding for the secreted factors: TGFB1, PTGS2 (encoding for a key PGE2 synthesis enzyme), VEGFA, HGF, and BMP2. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH. The relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and expressed as fold change relative to the control group. All primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

In vitro macrophage polarization and angiogenesis

For in vitro immunomodulation investigation, extracts were first prepared by incubating the different scaffold groups (PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) in α-MEM for 3 d, and the resulting media were collected and filtered. RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured to 70-80% confluence and then polarized into the M1 phenotype using lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) for 24 h. After polarization, the culture medium was replaced with the prepared conditioned media.

For immunofluorescence analysis, after 1 day of culture in conditioned media, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 10% goat serum. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD86 and CD206, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI staining. Images were captured using a wide-field fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany), and the positive fluorescent area was semi-quantified using ImageJ. For gene expression analysis, total RNA was extracted from macrophages cultured in parallel. Reverse transcription and RT-qPCR were performed to detect the expression of inflammation-related genes (TNF-α, NOS2, CD163, and MRC1), using GAPDH as an internal control. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

For in vitro angiogenesis investigation, conditioned media were first prepared by incubating the different scaffolds (Blank, PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) in DMEM for 5 d. HUVECs (1 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded onto Matrigel-coated plates and then cultured in the prepared conditioned media. After 4 h of incubation, the cells were stained with Calcein-AM, and the formation of tubular networks was observed and quantified using a wide-field fluorescence microscope.

In vitro BMSC migration assay

To simulate the migration of cells from surrounding tissue, GFP-labeled BMSCs were resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL in GelMA hydrogels. After the cell fusion rate reached 90%, a 5 mm hole was created in the center of each gel, into which scaffolds from the different groups (PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) were positioned. After 3 d, the migration of GFP-BMSCs towards the center of the scaffold was observed using a CLSM after fixation and DAPI staining. The number of migrated cells was quantified using ImageJ.

To further corroborate the pro-migratory effect of the scaffolds, a Transwell migration assay was conducted. Scaffolds (PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) were incubated with culture medium in the lower chamber, and BMSCs (1 × 104 cells) were seeded in the upper chamber of 24-well Transwell (Corning, USA). After 24 h, migrated cells on the lower surface of the membrane were stained with 0.1% crystal violet and counted. Bright-field images were acquired using an optical microscope, and cell counting was performed using ImageJ.

In vitro cell proliferation assay

To evaluate the proliferative support of the scaffolds, BMSCs viabilities on different 3D scaffolds (PCL, PCL/HAp, and PCL/HAp-GelMA) were assessed using a CCK-8 assay on days 1, 3, and 5. To visualize cell morphology and alignment, BMSCs cultured for 5 d were stained with DAPI and Alexa Fluor 488-phalloidin for F-actin and imaged by CLSM.

In vitro osteogenic differentiation assay

To assess the osteogenic potential of the scaffolds, BMSCs were cultured on Tissue Culture Plastic (TCP) and scaffolds from all experimental groups (PCL, PCL/HAp, PCL/HAp-GelMA, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs). The cells were first cultured in growth medium for 3 d. Subsequently, all groups were switched to an osteogenic differentiation medium and cultured for an additional 7 and 14 d. At day 7 and 14, ALP activity and calcium deposition were detected using a BCIP/NBT ALP staining kit and a calcium staining kit, respectively, and the results were semi-quantified using ImageJ. After 14 d of differentiation, immunofluorescence staining for ALP, RUNX2, and OCN was performed. In parallel, total RNA was extracted, and RT-qPCR was used to detect the expression of osteogenesis-related genes (RUNX-2, COL-1, ALP, OPN, BMP-2, and OCN), using GAPDH as the internal reference. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

To evaluate the metabolic regulation of BMSCs on different scaffolds, BMSCs were cultured with or without PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffolds for 7 d in proliferation medium. After removing the culture medium and washing with PBS, cells were scraped from the scaffolds. For metabolite extraction, cell samples were thawed on ice, and pre-chilled 80% methanol-water solution containing an internal standard was added. The mixtures were vortexed to ensure complete suspension. Three freeze-thaw cycles were performed, each consisting of rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen, followed by thawing on ice, to achieve thorough cell lysis. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and incubated at -20 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins. After a final centrifugation step (12,000 rpm, 3 min, 4 °C), 200 μL of the final supernatant was transferred to an LC-MS vial for analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with a UHPLC system. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) using a gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution was as follows: 0-1 min, 95% A; 1-12 min, 95% to 5% A; 12-14 min, 5% A; 14-14.1 min, 5% to 95% A; 14.1-16 min, 95% A. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes over a mass range of 70-1050 m/z.

Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed using the XCMS package in R for peak alignment, peak picking, and metabolite annotation. The resulting data matrix was imported into R for statistical analysis. DEMs between the Blank and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs groups were identified based on a p-value <0.05 and were visualized using a volcano plot. The relationships between samples and DEMs were visualized using a hierarchical clustering heatmap. To explore the interactions between different metabolite categories, Pearson correlation analysis was performed, and the results were visualized using a correlation heatmap, a chord diagram, and a network diagram. Finally, to identify the most significantly impacted biological pathways, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the set of identified DEMs.

Transcriptomic analysis and western blotting

To assess signaling pathways activated during osteogenic differentiation, RNA-seq analysis was performed on BMSCs cultured on blank plates and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffolds after 14 d of differentiation. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Beyotime, China), and RNA-seq libraries were prepared using NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit. Sequencing was performed on the HiseqX-ten platform. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis were performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Western blotting was performed on BMSCs cultured on blank plates and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs scaffolds after 14 d of differentiation to examine the expression levels of PI3K, phosphorylated PI3K, Akt, and phosphorylated Akt during osteogenic differentiation. Cells were gently rinsed twice with pre-chilled PBS, followed by removal of residual PBS. An appropriate amount of lysis buffer was added, and cells were scraped and collected together with the buffer. The lysates were pipetted thoroughly and incubated on ice for 15 min to ensure complete lysis. The non-denatured protein supernatant was obtained and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein samples were mixed with 5 × loading buffer at the appropriate ratio and denatured by boiling. Equal amounts of total protein were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels (5% stacking gel, 10% separating gel) and electrophoresed. The separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes under constant current (200 mA) for 1.5 h. Membranes were blocked with 3% skim milk, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After a brief rinse and five washes with 1 × TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:20,000) at room temperature for 1 h. Following additional washes, protein bands were visualized using ECL Plus reagents and quantified using ImageJ.

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University approved the experiments, ensuring compliance with ethical guidelines. Male rats (n = 3 per group for each study) were utilized. To evaluate the local immune response, scaffolds from three groups (PCL, PCL/HAp, and PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs) were implanted into subcutaneous pockets on the dorsum of the rats.

After 5 d, the implants with their surrounding tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. For general inflammation assessment, sections underwent H&E staining. To assess macrophage polarization, sections were analyzed by immunofluorescence staining for the M1 marker CD86 and the M2 marker CD206, with DAPI counterstaining. Images were captured via confocal laser scanning microscopy, and the M1/M2 ratios were semi-quantified using ImageJ. To evaluate local cytokine expression, sections underwent immunohistochemistry for the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Staining was visualized using DAB, and the positive area was semi-quantified using ImageJ.

In vivo rat cranial defect model and analysis

All animal experiments described in this study were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (ID: WCHSIRB-D2022-194). Male Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks old, n = 3 per group) were utilized. The rats were anesthetized, and a sagittal incision was made on the scalp to expose the calvarium. A critical-sized defect (5 mm in diameter) was created in the parietal bone using a trephine bur under constant saline irrigation. The animals were randomly divided into three groups, and the defects were implanted with scaffolds from the following groups: (i) Blank, (ii) PCL/HAp, and (iii) PCL/HAp-GelMA/BMSCs.

After 8 weeks, the rats were euthanized, and the calvaria were harvested and fixed. The harvested specimens were scanned using a high-resolution micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) system to evaluate bone regeneration. The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the original 5 mm defect area. Three-dimensional reconstruction was performed, and quantitative analysis was conducted to determine the bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). Following Micro-CT scanning, the specimens were decalcified in EDTA, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Coronal sections (5 μm thickness) were prepared and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome for qualitative evaluation of new bone formation and collagen deposition, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments included at least three parallel samples, and data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. For comparisons between the two groups, a t-test was employed. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by post-hoc analysis when appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The level of significance was indicated as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.
