Promoting equity for children and adolescents through implementation processes in health and social care: a scoping review
Authors: Michelle Gooey, Heather Morris, Rebecca Madill, Callie Walsh-Bailey, Ahlia Griffiths, Melissa Savaglio, Claire Blewitt, Amanda O’Connor, Sara Holton, Helia Khalesi, Sofia Grage-Moore, Maria Fernandez, Echezona E. Ezeanolue, Allison Metz, Ana Baumann, Helen Skouteris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the many existing implementation methods, theories, and frameworks, implementation science is insufficiently closing the research-to-practice gap. Bridging this gap is critical to achieving healthy and thriving communities globally, and requires implementation processes that promote equity and reach diverse populations, especially those most in need. The aim of this scoping review was to identify, thematically analyse, categorise, and provide examples of implementation processes described in equity-focused literature, with a focus on health and social care interventions for children and adolescents from birth to 18 years of age.MethodsA comprehensive search of literature was undertaken across six electronic databases (Medline, Embase, Cinahl, Scopus, PsycInfo, and Proquest) and Google Scholar, seeking peer-reviewed articles published in English between January 2018 and April 2024. All articles were double-screened at abstract and full text stage. Implementation processes were identified then thematically analysed.ResultsA total of 14,374 articles were screened; 80 articles (59 health, 21 social care) were included in the analysis. Seven main themes were identified: (1) foster trusting relationships; (2) dismantle top-down approaches and redistribute power; (3) invest with intention; (4) respect and value community-defined evidence; (5) tailor according to context; (6) broaden perspectives beyond practice norms; and (7) use strengths- and value-based approaches. Twenty-six sub-themes and 140 implementation processes were identified, including examples of how these can be operationalised in practice.ConclusionThis is the first review of equity-focused implementation processes in health and social care. By offering practical examples of the identified implementation processes, the review synthesises findings from published literature into tangible actions directly relevant to implementation practice. However, while these processes show promise, further research is required as their effectiveness remains largely untested and most evidence is from high-income countries. To promote health and social care equity for all children and adolescents, the evidence-base for effective equitable implementation work must be strengthened by, and reflective of, globally diverse voices and experiences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-026-03176-z.

Background

Despite significant investment by governments and philanthropic organisations internationally to promote child health and wellbeing, many interventions are not adopted or sustained in practice [1]. Subsequently, most of the child-related targets of the 2030 United Nations Sustainable Development Goals are unlikely to be met [2]. Closing the gap between scientific knowledge and its application in practice, known as the research-to-practice gap, is fundamental to achieving healthy, thriving communities.

Implementation science aims to bridge this gap [3]; however, it has not always been successful in ensuring interventions are equitably adopted and sustained. Despite the global call for a more explicit focus on equity to redress social inequities and structural barriers to optimal health is intensifying [4–7], key actions for equitable implementation, such as the processes for embedding equity in interventions, are poorly understood [8]. Given this emerging and complex topic, and the predominantly descriptive and qualitative nature of the existing literature on equitable implementation, a scoping review was warranted. Whilst other scoping reviews have explored equity in implementation research, they have mostly focused on specific frameworks [9] or diseases [10].

The overall aim of this scoping review was to identify, thematically analyse, categorise, and provide examples of implementation processes described in equity-focused health and social care interventions.

Equity considerations are critical at all life stages. This review focused specifically on children and adolescents up to 18 years old because they are a key priority population internationally, vulnerable to injustices and inequities [2, 11]. Inequities experienced in childhood and adolescence can also have significant and long-lasting impact on subsequent health, development and overall wellbeing [12].

Studies across both health and social care were included strategically. Social care is defined as personal care, protection, social support and/or practical assistance [13, 14], that, for example, may be offered to children or adolescents at risk of, or already experiencing, social, emotional or physical ability challenges. Social care is often delivered by allied health practitioners, such as social workers and psychologists, and is crucial for promoting health equity [5].

Methods

The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic

Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [15].

Search strategy

Six electronic databases were searched: Medline, Embase, Cinahl, Scopus, PsycInfo, and Proquest (including the Education Collection and Education Database). Supported by an academic librarian, a combination of key words and subject headings were adapted for each search, plus adjustments to account for the main disciplinary focus of each database (see Supplementary material 1). The database searches were conducted on 24 April 2024. Specific health and social care Google Scholar searches were conducted on 11 July 2024 and the first 300 ‘hits’ reviewed.

Eligibility criteria

Articles were included if they described an intervention that: (1) explicitly sought to address health and/or social care equity for children and adolescents aged between birth and 18 years of age; (2) had been completed/undertaken (i.e., were not merely planned, recommended, or proposed); and (3) aimed to improve a health-related and/or social care-related outcome. The review included peer-reviewed articles published in English, between January 2018 and April 2024. Articles were excluded if they were not peer-reviewed, or were a protocol, description of a proposed study, in a book chapter or a conference proceeding.

Screening

All identified articles were imported into Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/). Both title/abstract and full-text were double-screened by MG, HM, SGM, HK, RM, SH, and HS. Conflicts were resolved through consensus by two or three researchers.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted in NVivo and transposed to an Excel spreadsheet. Three researchers (SH, SGM, MS) extracted study characteristics including country, study design, discipline, age of children/adolescents, source of inequity (e.g., ethnicity, socioeconomic, gender etc.). As each article had an explicit focus on equity, sources of inequity were readily identifiable. One researcher (MG) extracted relevant implementation processes and examples of how each implementation process could be operationalised in practice from each article. Twenty percent of studies were double extracted and categorised by researcher MS, with differences resolved through consensus.

Data analysis

A hybrid thematic analysis approach was employed. Six elements for equitable implementation proposed by Metz et al. [16] provided a framework for the initial deductive coding of the data: 1. Building trusting relationships; 2. Dismantling power structures; 3. Prioritizing investments and decision-making to advance equity; 4. Applying critical perspectives; 5. Valuing community-defined evidence; and 6. Ensuring adaptation and cultural responsiveness. Prior to formal coding, all included articles were read in full to facilitate familiarization with the data. To determine deductive themes, HM, SGM, RM, MG, CWB first conducted a thematic analysis on a limited number of articles to explore how the implementation processes related to the Metz et al. elements [16]. Formal extraction and coding of all articles were then conducted systematically by MG using NVivo (version 14); data were exported to a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and a Mural workspace (https://www.mural.co/) to refine codes. Given the explicit focus on equitable implementation processes in practice, the original six Metz et al. elements [16] were reworded for clarity and/or to address subtle distinctions that required a more precise or expanded definition of these elements. The refined elements were treated as deductive themes and added to a codebook, facilitating deeper inductive analysis. As the coding progressed, a new theme was identified. This seventh theme captured data that did not fit within the existing themes and was also added to the code book. Sub-themes were then identified inductively, capturing more specific, nuanced categories within each of the seven themes. MG facilitated several peer debriefings and collaborative coding sessions with HM, SGM, SH, AG, RM, MS, CWB, AM and HS throughout this process. During these sessions, discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus. The inductively-derived sub-themes were added to the codebook.

Study characteristics

Of the 80 health and social care articles, 74% percent were health focused (n = 59) and 26% were social care focused (n = 21). The vast majority of articles reported studies conducted in high-income countries (n = 69, 86%) and of these, 81% (n = 56) were conducted in the United States.

Eight different study types were included, the most common were mixed-method studies (n = 23, 29%). Most studies implemented interventions which addressed racial inequities (n = 53, 66%), followed by socioeconomic disadvantage (n = 42, 53%); over half of the studies (n = 46, 58%) recognised that inequity arises from more than one cause, reflecting an intersectional perspective. Theme 1: foster trusting relationships

This theme reflects three sub-themes and 19 processes focused on the importance of trusting relationships to support implementation (Table 2), particularly in the context of traditionally hierarchical relationships, such as researcher and participant, or practitioner and the child/adolescent receiving care. The theme highlights the broader aspects of fostering trusting relationships, recognising the role that the environment plays in this process. It also outlines several phases of trust development (e.g., engage, build, and sustain), whilst acknowledging the boundaries between these phases are not well defined.

Theme 2: dismantle top-down approaches and redistribute power

This theme centres on the collaboration, leadership, and empowerment of community/end users to support equitable implementation. It includes five sub-themes and 21 processes (Table 3). Invoking the phrase ‘nothing about us, without us’, this theme emphasises a move away from the traditional, hierarchical “top-down” approach to research or care provision to one that is driven “bottom-up” – that is, inclusive of, and empowering for children, adolescents and their communities.

Theme 3: invest with intention

This theme is focused on purposeful investment in equitable implementation processes in health and social care and includes four sub-themes and 21 processes (Table 4). It also describes investment in a community, population or group of people, where the expected return on investment is not primarily financial, but rather growing commodities such as knowledge, social capital and/or human capital.

Theme 4: respect and value community-defined evidence

Ten processes that recognise the value of community evidence were identified across three sub-themes (Table 5). This theme highlights engagement with community to develop evidence that is defined and shaped by the community themselves, and increasing their capacity to develop and use such knowledge in a meaningful, not tokenistic, way.

Theme 5: tailor according to context

This theme highlights how the context of any given intervention will influence the way it is implemented (Table 6). Twenty-five processes were identified across three sub-themes. Here, the concept of tailoring relates to the implementation context, for example, the physical environment, the geographical location, the culture of the community in which the researchers are working, and in the case of health and social care provision, the needs of the individual receiving that care. A core value of this theme is that end users are served in ways that meet their needs.

Theme 6: broaden perspectives and approaches beyond practice norms

For this theme, 28 implementation processes that encourage action beyond standard practice were identified across 5 sub-themes (Table 7). This theme has broad applicability; it covers processes relevant to individuals, practitioners, organisations, communities and policy makers. The underlying premise is the need to question “the status quo” and take a broader, more holistic view of health and social care provision.

Theme 7: use strengths- and values-based approaches

This theme is composed of three sub-themes and encompasses 16 processes (Table 8), emphasising ways for recognising and building upon the unique capabilities, resources, and cultural assets within communities and/or among end users. It also underscores efforts to ensure that implementation actions are guided by, and reflect, what matters most to those who are the focus of interventions, and fosters approaches that are both authentic and responsive to their health and social care needs.

Discussion

In the context of increasing calls for a more explicit focus on equity in implementation science, the aim of this scoping review was to identify, categorise, and provide examples of implementation processes that have been described in equity-focused literature across health and social care for children and adolescents aged from birth to 18 years. Seven themes, 26 sub-themes and 140 processes with examples were identified from 80 peer-reviewed articles.

Findings expand the six essential elements of equitable implementation proposed by Metz and colleagues [16] by refining the title descriptors to align more closely with implementation processes identified in the review. The new theme Use of Strength- and Values-Based Approaches emphasises how the way information is presented can shape the attitudes and behaviours of its audience. For example, it shows how clinicians and practitioners can move away from deficit-based framing, which is common in both health care and social care [25, 26], towards a strengths-based approach. Deficit-based framing reinforces negative stereotypes and implicit biases, whilst ignoring strengths and assets of marginalized groups [27]. This theme also aligns with the concept of values-driven implementation, which connects the explicit statement of values with improved implementation outcomes [28].

Despite implementation research and implementation practice being critical and complementary components of implementation science [29], there is a persistent gap between academic implementation research and “real-world” implementation practice [30]. Academic researchers tend to operate within disciplinary boundaries which encourages siloed knowledge gatekeeping (such as discipline-specific journals and conferences), resulting in evidence that is poorly disseminated or not presented in ways that can be easily applied in real-world settings. Drawing on published literature relating to both practice and research, the findings of this review provide tangible guidance to those seeking to operationalise the themes, subthemes and processes into actions, by providing specific practice-based examples for each implementation process.

A key finding of this review is the predominance of equity-focused implementation research and practice studies conducted in high-income, Western countries such as the United States, Canada, Sweden, and Australia. While this may partly reflect the exclusion of non-English language publications for this review, it also aligns with previous observations of the limited geographic and cultural scope of implementation science [31]. This finding highlights a significant gap in the literature and suggests the presence of an inward-looking “echo chamber” within implementation science. To advance equity meaningfully in implementation practice and research in health and social care, there is an urgent need to include and amplify underrepresented voices, incorporate diverse forms of knowledge, and embrace decolonised perspectives [32]. Greater attention must be given to equity-focused implementation research and practice conducted in low- and middle-income countries to ensure that implementation science is informed by global insights.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this review is its identification of implementation processes, offering insight into how implementers could embed equity considerations into health and social care practice. The broad and systematic search across six discipline-specific academic databases provides a comprehensive overview of strategies documented in the published literature.

While many processes may be applicable to more than one theme or sub-theme, each was assigned to a single category to maintain analytical clarity and parsimony. Importantly, conclusions on the effectiveness of identified processes are out of scope for this review, and the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that these approaches will definitively lead to more equitable outcomes. Rather, they reflect the current state of emerging areas of scientific inquiry and point to promising directions for future research. Even though the review focuses on children and adolescents, many of the identified processes may be applicable to adult populations, particularly in settings such as primary care or community health. This could also be an area for future research. Another area for further investigation could be an exploration of processes commonly linked to specific types of inequities.

Conclusion

This review identified implementation processes and corresponding practice examples which may contribute to ensuring equitable health and social care for children and adolescents. However, their effectiveness remains largely untested. Future research should focus on rigorous evaluation using robust metrics and longitudinal designs, expanding beyond high-income Western contexts, develop evidence-based frameworks that provide guidance beyond the “what we need to do” to the “how” and ensure equity is a forethought not an afterthought in intervention planning, design, implementation and evaluation. Building capacity among implementation researchers and practitioners, especially in under-resourced settings, and fostering meaningful involvement of diverse populations experiencing the greatest disadvantage is critical to advancing a more global, inclusive, and equitable implementation science.
