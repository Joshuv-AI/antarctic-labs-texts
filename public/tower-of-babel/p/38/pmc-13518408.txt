Immunomodulatory Effects of Bifidobacterium bifidum BGN4 in Cyclophosphamide-induced Immunosuppressed Mice via Activation of NK Cells and Macrophages
Authors: Seungil Kim, Ju Hye Song, Sang-Hyuk Yoo, Ji Yeon Yoo, Ji-Eun Eom, Kyung Min Lim, Gun-Dong Kim, Hee Soon Shin, Kyung Bae Lee, Jong Ik Jeon, Keon Heo, So-Young Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The immune system is a highly coordinated network that defends the host against pathogens and maintains physiological homeostasis. Probiotics have emerged as promising immunoregulatory agents; however, the mechanisms by which they modulate innate and adaptive immune responses remain incompletely understood. In this study, we investigated the immunomodulatory potential of Bifidobacterium bifidum BGN4 using in vitro assays and a cyclophosphamide (CP)-induced immunosuppressed mouse model. In vitro, B. bifidum BGN4 treatment markedly enhanced natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity against YAC-1 lymphoma target cells and activated macrophages, as evidenced by elevated nitric oxide production, increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), and upregulated expression of phagocytosis-related genes (Marco, Msr1, and Cd14). These effects were accompanied by activation of MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways in macrophages. In vivo, oral administration of B. bifidum BGN4 significantly mitigated CP-induced reductions in body weight, spleen and thymus indices, and leukocyte counts. Moreover, B. bifidum BGN4 enhanced splenocyte proliferation under both basal and mitogen-stimulated conditions and improved NK cell cytotoxic activity in immunosuppressed mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that B. bifidum BGN4 promotes both innate and adaptive immune activation, effectively counteracting CP-induced immunosuppression. This probiotic strain represents a promising functional food ingredient for immune enhancement and recovery.

Introduction

The mammalian immune system is composed of a dynamic network of cells, tissues, and molecular mediators that orchestrate defense against pathogens and maintain immune homeostasis. It comprises two independent branches: innate and adaptive immunity. Innate immunity serves as the first line of defense, providing rapid and nonspecific responses through immune effector cells such as macrophages, neutrophil, and natural killer (NK) cells [1, 2]. These cells recognize conserved microbial components through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and subsequently trigger inflammatory and cytotoxic responses [3].

NK cells are pivotal components of innate immunity that provide the first line of defense against viral infections and malignant transformation [4, 5]. Their cytotoxic function relies on the secretion of perforin and granzymes that induce apoptosis in target cells, as well as the production of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α that shape downstream adaptive responses [6, 7]. The maintenance of NK cell activity is therefore essential for effective immune surveillance and early host protection. Meanwhile, macrophages serve as the primary coordinators of innate immunity, bridging early pathogen recognition and downstream adaptive responses. Their activation through PRRs, such as TLR2 and TLR4, initiates canonical MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways that lead to the production of pro-inflammatory mediators including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [8, 9]. These cytokines are crucial not only for direct antimicrobial defense but also for recruiting and activating lymphocytes, thereby linking innate and adaptive immunity [10]. Adaptive immunity, characterized by antigen specificity and memory, is mediated by B and T lymphocytes [11]. Upon activation, B cells differentiate into plasma cells and secrete immunoglobulins, whereas T helper cells release cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ to facilitate B cell activation and cytotoxic T cell responses [12–14]. Coordination between innate and adaptive mechanisms ensures effective pathogen clearance and long-term immune protection. Dysregulation of this balance, however, leads to immune suppression or chronic inflammation, conditions often associated with infectious diseases, aging, and chemotherapy [15–17].

Cyclophosphamide (CP) is a potent alkylating agent widely used in cancer chemotherapy and autoimmune disease treatment [18]. Despite its therapeutic efficacy, CP induces severe immunosuppression characterized by myelosuppression, lymphopenia, and atrophy of lymphoid organs such as the thymus and spleen. These adverse effects markedly compromise both innate and adaptive immune defense, increasing susceptibility to infections and delaying immune recovery [19]. Accordingly, the CP-induced immunosuppression model has been widely adopted to evaluate immune-enhancing interventions, including probiotics, functional foods, and herbal medicines [20].

Probiotics, defined as “live microorganisms that confer health benefits on the host when administered in adequate amounts,” have been extensively studied for their immunomodulatory potential. Numerous Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains have been shown to enhance immune defense by regulating both innate and adaptive immune cell functions [21]. These effects include the activation of macrophages and NK cells, stimulation of cytokine secretion, and promotion of immunoglobulin synthesis [22–24]. Several studies have demonstrated that probiotic supplementation can restore immune competence under immunosuppressive or stress conditions [25, 26].

Bifidobacterium bifidum BGN4 (BGN4), originally isolated from healthy Korean infants, has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory and gut barrier-protective effects through the regulation of intestinal epithelial cytokines and PRRs signaling [27]. However, its systemic immunomodulatory potential, particularly its ability to enhance innate immune effector cells such as NK cells and macrophages under immunosuppressive conditions, remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to comprehensively investigate the immune-enhancing effects of BGN4 using both in vitro and in vivo models, with a focus on NK cell cytotoxicity, macrophage activation via MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, and overall immune recovery in a CP-induced immunosuppressed mouse model. These findings may provide scientific evidence for the application of BGN4 as a functional food ingredient for immune enhancement under immunocompromised conditions.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of BGN4

Lyophilized BGN4 strain powder was obtained from the BIFIDO Inc. (Gangwon, Republic of Korea). Bifidobacterium bifidum BGN4 (Accession No. KCCM 12754P) was deposited at the Korean Culture Center of Microorganisms (KCCM, Seoul, Republic of Korea) on June 24, 2020. The viable bacterial count was determined using a standard plate cultivation method and expressed as colony-forming units (CFU). The BGN4 powder was stored at 4 ℃ until use. For experimental procedures, the powder was suspended and serially diluted in sterile PBS. The identity and purity of BGN4 were confirmed by sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene prior to use.

NK Cell Cytotoxicity Assay

The effect of BGN4 on NK cell cytotoxic function was evaluated using both splenocyte cultures (in vitro) and immunosuppressed mice (in vivo). For the in vitro assay, spleens were collected from 5-week-old BALB/c mice, and single-cell suspensions were prepared in RPMI-1640 medium (Welgene, Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Welgene). Splenocytes (5 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with BGN4 suspensions (105–10⁷ CFU) for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. YAC-1 lymphoma cells (ATCC, Rockville, MD) were labeled with CellTrace Violet (10 µM, 15 min)(Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and used as target cells. Effector (splenocyte) and target (YAC-1) cells were co-cultured at an effector-to-target cell (E:T) ratio of 10:1 for 4 h. After incubation, cells were stained with 7-aminoactinomycin D (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and the percentage of dead target cells was quantified by Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Science, Indianapolis, IN). Furthermore, to evaluate the effect of BGN4 on NK cell cytotoxicity in a mouse model, splenocytes were isolated from the spleens of BGN4-treated mice and co-cultured with YAC-1 cells at a 10:1 or 20:1 E:T ratio for 4 h. Dead YAC-1 cells were quantified using the same method as in the in vitro assay.

Macrophage Activation and Proliferation Assay

For in vitro assays, peritoneal macrophages were elicited by intraperitoneal injection of 3% Brewer thioglycollate medium into 5-week-old BALB/c mice. After 72 h, peritoneal exudate cells were harvested, centrifuged, and filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer. Cells (1 × 105/well) were seeded in 96-well plates in RPMI-1640 medium (Welgene) supplemented with 10% FBS (Welgene) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Welgene) and treated with BGN4 (105–10⁷ CFU) for 24 h. Nitric oxide (NO) levels were quantified using the Griess (Thermo Fisher Scientific) reaction at 540 nm using an Epoch Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT). Cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) were measured using mouse ELISA kits (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). For in vivo evaluation of macrophage function, peritoneal macrophages were collected from each experimental group (Naive, CP, BGN4, and RG) by peritoneal lavage. Macrophage proliferative activity was assessed using the water-soluble tetrazolium-1 (WST-1) assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 48 h. Following incubation, WST-1 reagent (DoGEN, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was added to the culture medium at a 1:10 (v/v) ratio. After a further 30 min incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Epoch Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments).

Western Blot

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 10⁶ cells/well) and cultured in DMEM (Welgene) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Welgene) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Welgene) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. The cells were then treated with BGN4 for 30 min, and LPS (1 µg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a positive control. Total cellular proteins were extracted using lysis buffer containing a protease–phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and protein concentrations were determined with a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For analysis of nuclear proteins such as NF-κB p65, nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were separately prepared using NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or 5% BSA for phosphorylated proteins and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-p38, p38, p-ERK, ERK, p-JNK, JNK, p65, IκBα, Lamin B1 and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA). After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature, and protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensity was quantified using Fusion software (Vilber, France).

Animal and Treatment

Four-week-old male BALB/c mice (Orient Bio Inc., Seongnam, Korea) were acclimated for 1 week and housed under controlled specific-pathogen-free conditions (24 ± 2 °C, 60% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle). Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) naive control (n = 7), (2) cyclophosphamide (CP)(Sigma-Aldrich) injection only (n = 7), (3) BGN4 supplementation with CP injection at 10⁷ CFU/mouse/day (n = 7), (4) red ginseng (RG, 200 mg/kg) supplementation with CP injection (positive control) (n = 7). The naïve control groups received 200 μL of sterile PBS. RG has been widely commercialized in Korea as a health functional food with immune-enhancing properties, and its immunostimulatory effects have been demonstrated in CP-induced immunosuppressed mouse models [28]. Accordingly, RG (containing total ginsenosides Rg1, Rb1, and Rg3 at 16.5 mg/g) was kindly provided by Huons Foodience Co., Ltd. (Chuncheon, Republic of Korea) and used as a positive control in this study. To induce immunosuppression, CP (100 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally on days 25 [23, 29]. Oral administration of BGN4 or RG was performed once daily for 27 consecutive days (days 1–27). On day 28, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized for sample collection. Blood and organs (Spleen and thymus) were harvested for hematological and immunological analyses. The relative weights of the spleen and thymus were calculated as organ indices, expressed as the ratio of organ weight (mg) to body weight (g). All animal procedures were approved by and performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines of the Korea Food Research Institute (approval number: KFRI-M-24036). Whole-blood samples were analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (ADVIA 2120i; Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Erlangen, Germany) with species-specific (mouse) software setting to determine hematopoietic parameters including total leukocyte and lymphocyte counts.

Flow Cytometry

Splenocytes were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD3 and CD335 (NKp46) (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) following Fc receptor blocking with anti-CD16/CD32 (BD Biosciences). Data were acquired using a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences).

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using an AccuPrep Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). cDNA was synthesized from purified RNA with the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (Toyobo) to measure the expression of target genes. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with Gapdh serving as the internal reference gene. The primer sequences used were as follows: IL-1β, 5′-GCAACTGTTCCTGAACTCAACT-3′ and 5′-ATCTTTTGGGGTCCGTCAACT-3′; IL-6, 5′-TAGTCCTTCCTACCCCAATTTCC-3′ and 5′-TTGGTCCTTAGCCACTCCTTC-3′; Marco, 5′-GCACAGAAGACAGAGCCGAT-3′ and 5′-AGTGATCCATTGCCACAGCA-3′; Cd14, 5′-CTCTGTCCTTAAAGCGGCTTAC-3′ and 5′-GTTGCGGAGGTTCAAGATGTT-3′; Msr1, 5′-TGAACGAGAGGATGCTGACTG-3′ and 5′-TGTCATTGAACGTGCGTCAAA-3′; Perforin1, 5′-ACACAGTAGAGTGTCGCATGTAC-3′ and 5′-GTGGAGCTGTTAAAGTTGCGGG.

-3′; Granzyme B, 5′-CAGGAGAAGACCCAGCAAGTCA-3′ and 5′-CTCACAGCTCTAGTCCTCTTGG-3′; Gapdh, 5′-CATCACTGCCACCCAGAAGACTG-3′ and 5′-ATGCCAGTGAGCTTCCCGTTCAG-3.

Splenocyte Proliferation Assay

Splenocyte proliferation was evaluated using a WST-1 assay. Spleens were aseptically removed from CP-treated mice, homogenized, and subjected to erythrocyte lysis. The isolated splenocytes were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁶ cells/well in RPMI-1640 medium (Welgene) supplemented with 10% FBS (Welgene) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Welgene). Cells were stimulated with either 1 μg/mL concanavalin A (ConA) (Sigma-Aldrich) or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Sigma-Aldrich) to assess T- and B-cell mitogenic responses, respectively, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 48 h. Following incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and WST-1 reagent (DoGEN) was added to the culture medium at a 1:10 (v/v) ratio. After a further 30 min incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Epoch Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test or Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

BGN4 Stimulates NK Cell Cytotoxicity in vitro

To evaluate whether BGN4 can influence NK cell activity, splenocytes containing NK cells were co-cultured with YAC-1 lymphoma cells, a NK-sensitive target cell line used to assess cytotoxic function. Splenic NK cell cytotoxicity against YAC-1 lymphoma target cells was markedly enhanced following treatment with BGN4 (Fig. 1A). Compared with PBS-treated group, which exhibited relatively low basal cytotoxicity, BGN4 stimulation resulted in a clear and statistically significant increase in NK-mediated lysis of target cells (Fig. 1A). At 10⁷ CFU/mL, BGN4 treatment increased NK cell cytotoxicity to 10.98 ± 1.37% vs. 6.42 ± 1.15% in the control group (p < 0.01), corresponding to an approximately 1.7-fold elevation. BGN4 Activates Macrophage Function

To assess the effect of BGN4 on macrophage activity, peritoneal macrophages were treated with BGN4 and analyzed for NO production, cytokine secretion, and expression of phagocytosis-related genes. Macrophages treated with BGN4 showed markedly increased NO production compared with PBS control group, indicating an enhanced activation state (Fig. 1B). The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were also significantly elevated in the culture supernatants following BGN4 treatment (Fig. 1C). In addition, the expression of Marco, Cd14, and Msr1 genes was noticeably upregulated compared with PBS control group (Fig. 1D). These genes are associated with macrophage recognition and scavenging functions, and their increased expression reflects the activation of macrophage immune responses [30, 31]. Western blot analysis revealed that BGN4 treatment markedly enhanced the phosphorylation of MAPK pathway components, including p38, ERK, and JNK (Fig. 2A and B). Compared to the control group, macrophages treated with BGN4 exhibited elevated levels of phosphorylated p38 (p-p38), p-ERK, and p-JNK, indicating activation of MAPK signaling cascades. Concomitantly, BGN4 activated the NF-κB signaling pathway, as demonstrated by increased phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 and degradation of IκBα (Fig. 2C and D). The decrease in IκBα levels indicates proteasomal degradation, which permits the release and nuclear translocation of p65. Furthermore, analysis of nuclear extracts confirmed elevated levels of p65 in the nucleus, supporting activation of NF-κB–dependent transcription. BGN4 Prevents CP-induced Body Weight Loss and Organ Atrophy

To establish an immunocompromised mouse model, CP was administered prior to BGN4 supplementation. CP treatment resulted in a pronounced reduction in body weight and a substantial decrease in immune organ indices, confirming successful induction of immunosuppression (Fig. 3). Oral administration of BGN4 effectively mitigated these CP-induced reductions. Mice receiving BGN4 maintained body weights comparable to those of the positive-control (RG) group, indicating that probiotic supplementation alleviated the systemic wasting effects associated with CP treatment (Fig. 3A). The thymus and spleen are essential lymphoid organs responsible for T cell maturation and immune surveillance, and their relative organ weights—normalized to body weight—are widely used as indices of overall immune status [32, 33]. In the CP-treated group, the relative weights of the thymus and spleen were markedly reduced, reflecting atrophy of both primary and secondary lymphoid organs (Fig. 3B and C). BGN4 Improves Hematological and Cellular Immune Parameters

CP treatment resulted in marked leukopenia and lymphopenia (Fig. 4A and B), characteristic features of chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression. Oral administration of BGN4 significantly improved these hematological parameters, supporting hematopoietic recovery under immunosuppressive stress. To further assess the cellular immune response, splenocyte proliferation assays were performed using ConA and LPS as mitogen for T and B cells, respectively (Fig. 4C and D). CP treatment caused distinct changes in proliferative responses depending on the mitogen used: ConA-stimulated T cell proliferation showed a slight increase compared with the naive group, whereas LPS-stimulated B cell proliferation was notably decreased, indicating a selective impairment of B cell activity. In contrast, splenocytes isolated from BGN4-treated mice exhibited significantly higher proliferation under both stimulation conditions compared with the CP group. BGN4 supplementation enhanced ConA-induced proliferation beyond the level observed in CP-treated mice and effectively improved the suppressed LPS-induced proliferation. In addition, splenocytes from BGN4-treated mice showed increased proliferation even in the absence of mitogenic stimulation compared with the CP group (Fig. 4E). Consistent with these results, analysis of cytokine production in splenocyte culture supernatants revealed that CP treatment significantly reduced IL-2 levels, whereas BGN4 supplementation maintained IL-2 production to near control levels (Fig. 4F). BGN4 Enhances NK Cell Function in vivo

The effect of BGN4 on NK cell function was further evaluated in CP-induced immunosuppressed mice. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the proportion of CD3⁻CD335⁺ NK cells in the spleen was markedly reduced by CP treatment compared with the naive control, confirming suppression of NK cell populations under immunosuppressive conditions (Fig. 5A). Oral administration of BGN4 prevented this decrease, and the NK cell population increased in BGN4 treatment groups (Fig. 5A). In parallel, NK cell cytotoxicity against YAC-1 target cells was also reduced in the CP group, indicating functional impairment of innate cytotoxic responses (Fig. 5B and C). Splenocytes isolated from BGN4-treated mice exhibited significantly higher NK-mediated lysis of target cells compared with CP-treated controls (Fig. 5B and C). The NK activity in the BGN4 group was comparable to that of the RG positive-control group, indicating that BGN4 effectively enhanced NK effector function in vivo. In addition to cytotoxic function, the expression of cytolytic effector molecules was analyzed to evaluate NK cell activation status. CP treatment tended to reduce Perforin1 and Granzyme B mRNA expression levels in splenocytes compared with the naive group (Fig. 5D). Perforin1 expression was significantly increased in BGN4-treated mice compared with the CP group, while Granzyme B expression showed an increasing trend that did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5D). BGN4 Enhances Macrophage Function in vivo

To assess whether BGN4 enhances macrophage activation in vivo, peritoneal macrophages were isolated from CP-induced immunosuppressed mice and analyzed for proliferation, NO production, and gene expression related to inflammation and phagocytic activity. CP treatment did not significantly affect macrophage proliferation or NO release, and no clear differences were observed among BGN4- or RG-treated groups (Fig. 6A and B). mRNA expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 was significantly reduced in the CP group compared with the naive control (Fig. 6C). In the BGN4-treated group, the decreased expression of both IL-1β and IL-6 was significantly recovered compared with the CP group (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, genes associated with macrophage phagocytic function (Marco, Cd14, Msr1) were significantly downregulated following CP treatment (Fig. 6D). The expression of Marco, Cd14, and Msr1 was upregulated in BGN4-treated mice relative to the CP group, indicating recovery of macrophage recognition and scavenging capacity (Fig. 6D). Discussion

The present study demonstrates that BGN4 exerts broad immunomodulatory effects in a CP-induced immunosuppressed mouse model. BGN4 supplementation effectively mitigated CP-induced reductions in body weight, immune organ mass, and hematological parameters, indicating recovery of systemic immune competence. Functional assays revealed that BGN4 enhanced both innate and adaptive immune activities, as evidenced by increased NK cell cytotoxicity, improved splenocyte proliferation, and upregulation of cytokine production. In macrophage, BGN4 promoted NO release, pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and activation of MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, reflecting strengthened innate immune signaling. Collectively, these results indicate that BGN4 prevents immune function compromised by CP through coordinated enhancement of NK cell and macrophage, supporting its potential as an immune-protective probiotic strain.

Probiotic-mediated regulation of NK cell activity has been increasingly recognized as a key mechanism linking microbial stimulation to enhanced host defense. Several probiotic strains have been evaluated for their NK cell-enhancing effects in CP-induced immunosuppression models. For instance, Lactobacillus casei HY7213 enhanced NK cell activity and cytotoxic T cell function in CP-treated mice [35], and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum KLDS1.0318 improved NK cell activity and macrophage phagocytosis alongside increased IL-2, IL-6, and IFN-γ levels [36]. These effects often through dendritic cell–derived IL-12 and IL-15 signaling [37, 38]. However, these studies did not examine the intracellular signaling mechanisms underlying macrophage activation. More recently, Limosilactobacillus reuteri KBL346 was shown to activate macrophages via TLR-mediated NF-κB and MAPK pathways and enhance NK cell activity in CP-treated mice [39], but this strain belongs to the Lactobacillus lineage and the study did not assess cytolytic effector molecule expression in NK cells. In contrast, BGN4 is among the few Bifidobacterium strains demonstrated to simultaneously enhance NK cell cytotoxicity and activate MAPK/NF-κB signaling in macrophages within a CP-induced immunosuppression model. Together, these data and prior studies suggest that probiotics such as BGN4 exert their immunomodulatory effects not by directly stimulating cytotoxicity, but by restoring cytokine and receptor signaling environments conducive to NK cell activation. This interpretation underscores the potential of BGN4 as a supportive immunobiotic for reestablishing innate immune readiness in immunocompromised hosts.

Accumulating evidence indicates that probiotics exert significant regulatory effects on macrophage function, influencing both inflammatory and tissue-repair pathways [40, 41]. Several Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus strains have been shown to restore macrophage activity in models of immune suppression by engaging pattern-recognition receptors such as TLR2 and TLR4 and activating downstream MAPK and NF-κB signaling [42, 43]. Recent studies have identified specific signaling pathways through which probiotic strains activate macrophages under immunosuppressive conditions. Lactococcus lactis OTG1204 was shown to activate macrophages through TLR2/NF-κB/MAPK/AP-1 pathways in CP-treated mice and modulate gut microbiota composition [44]; however, as a Lactococcus strain, its relevance to Bifidobacterium-mediated immunity is limited. BGN4 extends these findings by demonstrating coordinated activation of both MAPK (p38, ERK, JNK) and NF-κB pathways alongside upregulation of scavenger receptor genes (Marco, Cd14, Msr1), providing a more comprehensive mechanistic profile for Bifidobacterium-mediated macrophage activation. This dual activation of inflammatory signaling and phagocytic receptor expression distinguishes BGN4 from other probiotic strains and supports its potential as a Bifidobacterium-based immunobiotic candidate.

Probiotic surface components such as lipoteichoic acids and peptidoglycan fragments are known to stimulate immune signaling pathways, suggesting that BGN4 may provide structural MAMP cues that mimic pathogen-associated stimulation while avoiding excessive inflammation [45, 46]. This mode of activation may enable macrophages to regain their cytokine-producing and antigen-presenting capabilities without triggering a systemic inflammatory cascade — an essential feature for safe immunorestoration. Furthermore, the observed upregulation of Marco, Cd14, and Msr1 implies that BGN4 promotes recovery of the macrophage scavenging system, which plays a critical role in debris clearance and maintenance of tissue homeostasis [40, 41]. Enhanced expression of these receptors is often associated with efficient resolution of inflammation and balanced immune tone. Therefore, BGN4 may facilitate a dual process of immune reactivation and resolution by enhancing macrophage competence through both inflammatory and regulatory pathways. Overall, these findings position BGN4 as a probiotic capable of reprogramming macrophage signaling under immunosuppressive conditions. By reinstating cytokine production, phagocytic receptor expression, and MAPK–NF-κB activity, BGN4 appears to re-establish a balanced macrophage phenotype that supports host defense and immune homeostasis. Future work dissecting specific receptor interactions and downstream transcriptional regulators will help clarify how this strain fine-tunes macrophage-driven immune restoration.

The immunomodulatory capacity of probiotics is increasingly understood as a systemic phenomenon involving the coordinated activation of multiple immune compartments [47]. Rather than acting on a single effector population, probiotic-derived signals appear to restore communication between innate and adaptive immune circuits that are disrupted during immunosuppression [35, 48]. In the present study, BGN4 supplementation simultaneously improved macrophage activation, NK cell cytotoxicity, and splenocyte proliferation, indicating that this strain orchestrates a broad reactivation of immune functionality. Such concerted enhancement of innate and adaptive responses suggests that BGN4 acts as a “network modulator” of immune homeostasis rather than a simple immunostimulant. Mechanistically, these integrated effects likely result from the convergence of multiple immune-regulatory signals rather than the isolated activation of a single pathway [49, 50]. In BGN4-treated mice, improvement of macrophage and NK cell function implies reactivation of both receptor-mediated and metabolite-driven communication routes. Instead of reiterating specific MAPK or NF-κB events described above, this integrated response can be viewed as a higher-order recalibration of immune signaling networks.

Probiotic-derived structural components, including peptidoglycans and exopolysaccharides, may act as microbial cues that synchronize receptor-mediated cytokine responses among macrophages, NK cells, and lymphocytes, whereas microbial metabolites such as lactates contribute to hematopoietic and metabolic reprogramming of immune cells [51–53]. These coordinated actions create a systemic signaling milieu that supports immune cell renewal and balanced activation. Thus, B. bifidum BGN4 appears to function not merely as a local immunostimulant but as a system-level modulator that maintains cross-talk among innate and adaptive immune compartments, leading to durable recovery of immune homeostasis.

Although intestinal microbiota composition was not assessed in this study, it is plausible that BGN4 colonization or transient persistence in the gut contributed to systemic immune recovery by re-establishing mucosal–systemic immune connectivity. This hypothesis is supported by prior findings that oral probiotic administration enhances thymic and splenic immune functions through gut-associated lymphoid tissue-mediated signaling [54, 55]. Taken together, these observations suggest that BGN4 supports immune competence via an integrated mechanism involving receptor-mediated signaling, cytokine network activation, and gut-derived metabolic modulation. By synchronizing the activities of macrophages, NK cells, and lymphocytes, BGN4 appears to recalibrate immune homeostasis and reinforce systemic defense under immunocompromised conditions. Future multi-omics and microbiome-integrated studies will be instrumental in defining the inter-organ signaling networks underlying the immunoprotective effects of BGN4.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study demonstrates that BGN4 exerts broad immunoprotective effects in a CP-induced immunosuppressed mouse model. Oral administration of BGN4 ameliorated CP-induced reductions in body weight, lymphoid organ indices, and hematological parameters, reflecting recovery of systemic immune competence. Functional analyses revealed that BGN4 enhanced both innate and adaptive immune responses by enhancing NK cell cytotoxicity, promoting macrophage activation, and improving splenocyte proliferation and cytokine production. These findings suggest that BGN4 contributes to immune recovery through coordinated enhancement of multiple immune cell functions and normalization of cytokine-mediated communication networks. Although the specific bacterial components or metabolites responsible for receptor-mediated immune activation were not identified in this study, the observed cellular responses suggest that BGN4 may interact with pattern-recognition or metabolic signaling pathways similar to those described for other immunobiotic strains. Future studies aimed at isolating the active immunomodulatory molecules and identifying their corresponding host receptors will be crucial to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying BGN4-mediated immune protection. Collectively, our results highlight BGN4 as a promising immunobiotic candidate for supporting immune resilience and recovery under immunosuppressive conditions.
