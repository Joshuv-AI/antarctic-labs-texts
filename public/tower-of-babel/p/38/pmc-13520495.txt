Inferior vena cava respiratory variability changes during jet ventilation in rigid bronchoscopy: a prospective self-controlled study
Authors: Mingyuan Yang, Yuxue Yao, Hong Li, Qinghao Cheng, Guyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInferior vena cava (IVC) respiratory variability is widely used to assess volume status and fluid responsiveness, but its interpretation depends on the physiological context. Introduction

Rigid bronchoscopy continues to play a central role in the diagnosis and interventional management of central airway disorders. Because the procedure is performed in a shared and partially open airway, jet ventilation is commonly used to maintain oxygenation while preserving procedural access [1, 2]. Compared with conventional closed-circuit positive-pressure ventilation, however, jet ventilation generates a distinct physiological environment, particularly in terms of airway openness and pressure transmission [3, 4]. Such differences may influence the behavior of dynamic hemodynamic variables, including ultrasound-derived indices.

Respiratory variation of the inferior vena cava (IVC) is widely used in perioperative and critical care practice as a bedside index related to venous return, preload dependence, and fluid responsiveness [5, 6]. However, its interpretation is highly dependent on the physiological context in which it is measured. Earlier studies suggested that IVC variability could help predict fluid responsiveness in selected mechanically ventilated patients [7], whereas subsequent investigations in spontaneously breathing conditions have shown more heterogeneous and less reproducible results [8, 9]. More recent reviews had further emphasized that the reliability of IVC-derived indices was strongly influenced by ventilatory mode, respiratory mechanics, and underlying cardiopulmonary conditions [8, 10, 11].

These considerations are particularly relevant during rigid bronchoscopy under jet ventilation. Although IVC collapsibility and distensibility have been widely studied in spontaneous breathing and conventional mechanical ventilation, corresponding data in the open jet ventilation setting remain limited. As a result, the clinical meaning of IVC-derived indices under jet ventilation has not been clearly defined.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective self-controlled observational study to characterize changes in IVC respiratory variability during the transition from mask-assisted positive-pressure (MAPP) ventilation during induction to stable intraoperative jet ventilation during rigid bronchoscopy. Rather than evaluating fluid responsiveness, this study focused on whether IVC-derived indices behave differently across two clinically distinct ventilation conditions, thereby informing the interpretation of IVC ultrasound during open jet ventilation.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a single-center, prospective, self-controlled observational study conducted in adult patients undergoing rigid bronchoscopy under general anesthesia. The study was designed to evaluate within-patient changes in IVC respiratory variability across predefined perioperative ventilation stages, with particular focus on the transition from MAPP ventilation during induction to stable intraoperative jet ventilation. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Emergency General Hospital, Beijing, China (approval No. KY202627), and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on March 24, 2026 (registration No. ChiCTR2600121550). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives before enrollment. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational studies.

Participants

Consecutive adult patients scheduled to undergo rigid bronchoscopy under general anesthesia at our institution were prospectively screened for eligibility. Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II or III; (3) planned rigid bronchoscopy under general anesthesia with expected completion of the predefined intraoperative measurement protocol; and (4) an adequate IVC acoustic window allowing repeated ultrasonographic assessment.

Patients were excluded if they had conditions likely to substantially interfere with interpretation of IVC derived respiratory variability or venous return physiology, including severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or emphysema, clinically significant pulmonary hypertension, moderate to severe tricuspid regurgitation, relevant right heart dysfunction, marked baseline volume derangement, recent large volume fluid loading, or poor quality IVC ultrasonographic images. Patients were also excluded if intraoperative vasoactive support was required or if the ventilation strategy had to be changed for safety reasons, thereby preventing completion of the predefined study protocol.

Patients who met the eligibility criteria but could not complete the protocol because of intraoperative instability, protocol interruption, or incomplete image acquisition were withdrawn from analysis. A total of 60 consecutive eligible patients were ultimately enrolled and included in the final analysis.

Anesthesia and ventilation management

All patients fasted for 8 h for solids and 2 h for clear fluids before anesthesia. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), heart rate (HR), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), and bispectral index monitoring (BIS). T0 was defined as the awake baseline stage before induction. General anesthesia was induced with propofol 2 mg/kg, remifentanil 1 µg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg, and was maintained using a standardized total intravenous anesthesia regimen with propofol 4–6 mg/kg/h and remifentanil 0.15–0.25 µg/kg/min. General anesthesia was delivered using a standard anesthesia workstation (Mindray, Shenzhen, China).

After induction, MAPP ventilation was applied before insertion of the rigid bronchoscope. During this stage, ventilation was delivered through a tight-fitting facemask with 100% oxygen. No external positive end-expiratory pressure was applied, and no clinically relevant mask leak was observed during the measurement period. T1 was defined as the closed MAPP ventilation condition after completion of anesthetic induction and before rigid bronchoscope insertion. MAPP ventilation was maintained under stable conditions for 2 min before ultrasonographic and hemodynamic measurements were obtained. Ventilation was performed in volume-controlled mode, with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg, a respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, and an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio (I: E) of 1:1. Peak airway pressure during MAPP ventilation was not included as a predefined recorded variable in the study protocol.

Rigid bronchoscopy was then performed according to routine clinical practice. Jet ventilation was delivered using the TwinStream jet ventilator (Carl Reiner GMBH, Vienna, Austria) through the dedicated jet ventilation port of the rigid bronchoscope, with the airway remaining partially open to the atmosphere. According to the study protocol, normal frequency jet ventilation (NFJV) was used with 100% oxygen, a driving pressure of 0.9 bar, a frequency of 18 breaths/min, and with I: E of 1:1. The driving pressure of 0.9 bar was selected according to our institutional routine practice for adult rigid bronchoscopy and was kept constant across patients to provide a standardized ventilation condition for within-patient comparison. The pressure setting was not individualized. T2 was defined as the stable intraoperative open jet ventilation stage. Measurements at T2 were obtained approximately 20 min after rigid bronchoscope insertion and initiation of jet ventilation, after the predefined jet ventilation settings had been resumed and stabilized under clinically steady conditions before repeat ultrasonographic and physiologic measurements were recorded.

Study time points and data collection

Data collection was performed at the three predefined perioperative time points (T0, T1, and T2), as described above. At each time point, IVC ultrasonography and hemodynamic parameters were recorded. Hemodynamic parameters included HR, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and SpO₂. IVC ultrasonography measurements included maximum IVC diameter (IVCmax) and minimum IVC diameter (IVCmin), obtained in three consecutive respiratory cycles and averaged for analysis. Perioperative variables, including total procedure duration and cumulative fluid volume, were recorded at the end of the procedure.

Ultrasonographic measurements

IVC ultrasonography was performed using a commercially available ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with a phased-array probe. And it was performed by trained anesthesiology physicians using M-mode imaging in the subxiphoid long-axis view. The IVC was visualized near its junction with the right atrium, and measurements were obtained at a standardized site approximately 2 cm caudal to the hepatic vein confluence whenever image quality allowed. The same anatomical measurement site was used for repeated assessments in each patient whenever possible. IVCmax and IVCmin were identified from the M-mode tracing over consecutive respiratory cycles, and the average values were used for analysis. Because T0 represented an awake spontaneous breathing condition, the IVC collapsibility index (cIVC) was used. In contrast, T1 and T2 represented mechanically assisted ventilation conditions with positive pressure ventilation, under which the IVC distensibility index (dIVC) was more appropriate for assessing respiratory variation.

The following derived variables were calculated: IVC diameter variation (ΔIVC) = IVCmax − IVCmin; cIVC = (IVCmax − IVCmin) / IVCmax × 100%; and dIVC = (IVCmax − IVCmin) / IVCmin × 100%. The change in dIVC (ΔdIVC) = dIVC at T2 − dIVC at T1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the paired change in dIVC between T1 and T2 (ΔdIVC).

Secondary outcomes were paired changes between T1 and T2 in IVCmin, IVCmax, ΔIVC, HR, MAP, and SpO₂.

Exploratory variables included baseline variables, perioperative contextual variables, as well as their associations and potential effects with ΔdIVC. Baseline covariates considered in exploratory analyses included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ASA physical status, baseline HR, baseline MAP, baseline SpO2, baseline IVC parameters, and baseline cIVC at T0. Common comorbidities of interest included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease. Perioperative variables included cumulative fluid volume and total procedure duration.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated based on preliminary observations and expert assessment during protocol development. Because previous studies directly evaluating dIVC changes during open jet ventilation for rigid bronchoscopy were unavailable, a pragmatic target sample size was selected. Given the paired within-patient design, approximately 30 patients were considered sufficient to detect a clinically meaningful change in dIVC between ventilation stages. To improve precision, account for potential measurement variability, and allow exploratory covariate analyses, the target sample size was expanded to 60 patients.

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and median (Q1- Q3) for skewed data. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentage). For paired comparisons between T1 and T2, normality of paired differences was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables with normally distributed paired differences were compared using the paired-samples t test, whereas variables with non-normally distributed paired differences were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Exploratory univariable associations between continuous baseline variables and ΔdIVC were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. Associations of categorical variables with ΔdIVC were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. A multivariable linear regression model was constructed with ΔdIVC as the dependent variable. The multivariable model included age, sex, BMI, ASA physical status, baseline cIVC, cumulative fluid volume, and procedure duration. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons; therefore, secondary and exploratory analyses were interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 85 patients were screened for eligibility. Sixteen patients were excluded due to predefined exclusion criteria, and 9 patients were excluded due to inadequate ultrasound image quality. Finally, 60 patients were included in the analysis. The study workflow and assessment time points are shown in Fig. 1. Thirty-two patients (53.33%) were male and 28 (46.67%) were female. Twenty-four patients (40.00%) were classified as ASA physical status II and 36 (60.0%) as ASA physical status III. The prevalences of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease were 16.67%, 25.0%, and 8.33%, respectively.

Discussion

In this prospective self-controlled study, a marked change in IVC-derived respiratory variability was observed when ventilation was transitioned from MAPP to intraoperative jet ventilation. Specifically, dIVC decreased substantially at T2, accompanied by an increase in IVCmin, a smaller rise in IVCmax, and a reduction in absolute diameter variation. In contrast, HR and MAP remained largely unchanged. This within-patient shift provides the basis for interpreting subsequent IVC findings in relation to ventilation mode rather than volume status alone.

The clinical implication of these findings is primarily cautionary. The results do not suggest that dIVC should be used as a stand-alone guide for fluid therapy during jet ventilation. Instead, they indicate that a low dIVC obtained under open jet ventilation should not be interpreted alone as evidence of adequate preload or absence of fluid responsiveness. During rigid bronchoscopy, IVC ultrasound should be interpreted together with arterial pressure trends, procedural context, ventilation mode, and echocardiographic assessment of cardiac function when available.

Respiratory variation in IVC diameter has been reported to predict fluid responsiveness in selected mechanically ventilated patients, particularly under controlled ventilation and appropriate tidal volume conditions [5, 12]. However, many studies had shown that its diagnostic performance was not universal and was influenced by ventilatory settings, respiratory effort, cardiac function, and measurement conditions. In spontaneously breathing patients, the performance of IVC collapsibility had been more variable, and several studies havd recommended cautious interpretation [8, 9]. The controlled crossover study by Juhl-Olsen et al. further supports this caution, showing that IVC collapsibility was not a valid measure of preload changes during triggered positive-pressure ventilation [13]. Although that ventilation setting differs from open jet ventilation, it reinforces the broader principle that IVC-derived indices may lose their conventional preload-related meaning when respiratory mechanics and pressure transmission differ from standard assumptions [11, 13–15].

These results add to existing evidence by examining IVC-derived respiratory variability in a procedural setting that has not been well characterized. Rigid bronchoscopy with open jet ventilation differs from conventional closed ventilation because airway pressure may dissipate through the open airway and bronchoscope system. In this context, the present within-patient data provide clinically relevant evidence that conventional assumptions regarding IVC-derived indices may not be directly transferable.

During MAPP ventilation, gas is delivered within a relatively closed system, producing cyclic increases in intrathoracic pressure that contribute to variation in venous return and IVC diameter [16, 17]. In contrast, jet ventilation introduces gas into a partially open airway, allowing partial pressure dissipation and modifying the transmission of airway pressure to the thoracic cavity [1, 18, 19]. From a physiological perspective, the decrease in dIVC during open jet ventilation is most plausibly related to altered transmission of airway pressure to the thoracic cavity and the resulting change in heart-lung interaction. However, because intrathoracic pressure, airway pressure during jet ventilation, airway leak, transpulmonary pressure, respiratory system compliance, and right heart function were not directly measured, the present study cannot quantify the relative contribution of these mechanisms.

The relatively high dIVC values observed at T1 also require careful interpretation. T1 was measured shortly after induction under closed mask-assisted volume-controlled ventilation. At this stage, anesthetic-induced vasodilation, preoperative fasting, absence of external PEEP, and positive-pressure insufflation may have contributed to a smaller IVCmin and greater respiratory diameter variation. This combination would result in higher dIVC values under the T1 measurement condition. Therefore, the high T1 values should be interpreted as reflecting the peri-induction ventilatory and physiological context rather than as direct evidence of severe hypovolemia.

Exploratory analyses did not identify baseline or perioperative variables that were significantly associated with ΔdIVC. While this may indicate that the observed change is primarily related to the ventilatory transition itself, caution is warranted. The study was not powered to detect predictors of ΔdIVC, and unmeasured factors, including airway leak, airway geometry, respiratory system compliance, and right ventricular loading conditions, may have contributed to the observed variability.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center observational study with a modest sample size, and the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Second, peak airway pressure during MAPP ventilation, airway pressure during jet ventilation, airway leak, transpulmonary pressure, respiratory system compliance, and direct intrathoracic pressure were not systematically measured. Therefore, the proposed explanation involving altered pressure transmission and heart-lung interaction remains speculative. Third, cardiac output, central venous pressure, right heart function, stroke volume response to fluid challenge, and postoperative outcomes were not assessed. Consequently, the present study cannot determine whether the observed dIVC changes correspond to true changes in preload, fluid responsiveness, or clinically meaningful hemodynamic outcomes. Fourth, the driving pressure of 0.9 bar during jet ventilation was standardized rather than individualized. Although this improved comparability for the within-patient analysis, it may limit generalizability, and the adequacy of ventilation under this setting could not be objectively verified. Fifth, the size of the rigid bronchoscope was selected according to clinical need and was not completely uniform, usually ranging between sizes 10 and 12. Differences in bronchoscope size and airway leak may have influenced airway openness and pressure dissipation. Finally, the findings apply specifically to the transition from MAPP ventilation to normal-frequency jet ventilation during rigid bronchoscopy and should not be generalized to other jet ventilation modes or patient populations without further validation. These limitations restrict mechanistic and clinical interpretation, but they do not negate the primary within-patient observation that dIVC changed markedly after transition to open jet ventilation. Further studies incorporating direct respiratory mechanics and hemodynamic measurements are needed to determine the clinical utility of IVC ultrasound in this setting.

Conclusion

In summary, IVC-derived respiratory variability changed markedly after transition from MAPP ventilation to NFJV during rigid bronchoscopy. This observation suggests that IVC-derived indices are sensitive to ventilation mode and airway openness, and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of intravascular volume status or fluid responsiveness in this setting. Conventional IVC thresholds derived from spontaneous breathing or closed positive-pressure ventilation should therefore be applied with caution during open jet ventilation. Further studies incorporating direct respiratory mechanics and hemodynamic measurements are needed to clarify the physiological mechanisms and clinical utility of IVC ultrasound in this setting.
