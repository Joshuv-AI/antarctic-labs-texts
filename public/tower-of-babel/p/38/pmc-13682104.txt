Cyclophosphamide-induced animal models of ovarian dysfunction: from molecular mechanisms and modeling strategies to validation criteria
Authors: Yanxi Wang, Jixing Cao, Zheng Gong, Saihua Ma, Yuge Yan, Tian Yang, Rongfan Wu, Puyu Hu, Liping Yang, Xiaoyan Zou, Tian Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prevalence of ovarian dysfunction is rising annually and trending toward younger populations, significantly compromising the quality of life and reproductive potential of women in their prime childbearing years. Given the multifactorial etiology and complex pathogenesis, ovarian dysfunction requires robust animal models to elucidate underlying mechanisms and evaluate therapeutic efficacy. Due to its well-documented gonadotoxicity, cyclophosphamide (CTX) has become a cornerstone in establishing standardized models of ovarian dysfunction. This review provides a systematic summary of the core mechanisms underlying CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction, with an emphasis on their interconnected regulatory network encompassing oxidative stress, DNA damage and defective repair, apoptosis, cellular senescence, immune-inflammatory responses, autophagy, and ferroptosis. Furthermore, it delineates modeling protocols, encompassing animal species selection, dosing regimens, validation criteria, and outcome evaluation. Several critical limitations in current modeling practices have been identified, including significant inter-study variability in dosing regimens, the indiscriminate application of uniform doses across distinct disease stages, and a dearth of standardized benchmarks for both model establishment and outcome evaluation. Future studies should clarify the mechanistic crosstalk in CTX-induced ovarian injury and identify potential therapeutic targets. Moreover, it is essential to delineate the dose-response relationships, establishment criteria, and evaluation metrics specifically tailored to DOR, POI, and POF. Establishing standardized, graded modeling protocols is critical to providing a reliable framework for the clinical translation of basic research findings.

Introduction

Ovarian dysfunction poses a significant public health challenge within reproductive medicine, encompassing a clinical spectrum that is primarily characterized by diminished ovarian reserve (DOR), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), and premature ovarian failure (POF) [1]. Epidemiological data reveal that the incidence of ovarian dysfunction is increasing annually, with a discernible shift toward earlier onset in younger cohorts. Specifically, the prevalence of DOR among women of reproductive age is 38.4%, whereas global estimates for POI and POF are approximately 3.5% and 1%–3%, respectively [2–4]. Clinically, these conditions are characterized by a decline in both oocyte quantity and quality, coupled with hormonal imbalances and menstrual irregularities. Beyond impaired fertility, patients frequently experience perimenopausal vasomotor symptoms, such as hot flashes and night sweats. Furthermore, the long-term sequelae of ovarian dysfunction encompass an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, and neurological disorders, which collectively compromise the reproductive health and future fertility of women in their childbearing age [5, 6]. While current research suggests that the pathogenesis of ovarian dysfunction is driven by a multifaceted interplay of genetic, immunological, and environmental factors, the precise molecular underpinnings remain elusive [7, 8]. This knowledge gap hampers the development and clinical translation of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Clinically, ovarian dysfunction encompasses a spectrum of interrelated conditions, including DOR, POI, and POF. DOR is characterized by a reduction in oocyte quantity and/or quality, leading to decreased fertility. It is typically identified by abnormal ovarian reserve test results, such as AMH < 1.1 ng/mL, AFC < 5–7, and/or basal FSH ≥ 10 IU/L [9]. Notably, DOR carries no age restriction and may occur in women both younger and older than 40 years [6]. In contrast, POI is defined as ovarian dysfunction occurring before age 40, presenting with oligo/amenorrhea for at least 4 months and FSH > 25 IU/L on two occasions more than 4 weeks apart [10]. The term “insufficiency” is preferred over “failure” because ovarian function in these women is not invariably and irreversibly lost—indeed, approximately 50% of patients may exhibit intermittent follicular activity [11]. POF more specifically denotes the end-stage of this continuum, characterized by permanent cessation of ovarian function before age 40, with FSH levels typically exceeding 40 IU/L [6]. Although DOR, POI, and POF share some overlapping risk factors, current evidence does not support DOR as an obligate precursor to POI, and they have distinct clinical management strategies [6]. These three conditions are thus clinically and diagnostically distinct, with each defined by specific criteria and clinical features, yet they may be sequentially recapitulated in CTX-induced models through dose-dependent ovarian insult.

An ideal animal model serves not only as a cornerstone for mechanistic research but also as an indispensable tool for evaluating clinical interventions and their therapeutic efficacy. Currently, iatrogenic agents commonly employed to establish animal models of ovarian dysfunction primarily include cyclophosphamide (CTX), cisplatin (DDP), Tripterygium wilfordii polyglycosides (TWP), and cyclophosphamide in combination with busulfan (CTX + BUS). Among these, CTX, CTX + BUS, and DDP are administered via intraperitoneal injection, whereas TWP is typically delivered by intragastric gavage. These models predominantly utilize female rodents, specifically various strains of mice and rats. This review specifically focuses on animal models of ovarian dysfunction induced by CTX; a comprehensive comparison between CTX-mediated modeling and other pharmaceutical approaches is summarized in Table 1.

Among the various iatrogenic models of ovarian dysfunction summarized in Table 1, extensive evidence demonstrates that CTX modeling offers operational simplicity, a controllable dosing regimen, and a high success rate [15, 43]. These features ensure reliable efficacy, establishing CTX as one of the most widely used induction agents in reproductive research [44–45]. Compared with other pharmaceutical agents, CTX provides exceptional flexibility in dosing regimens. Through the strategic modulation of dosage, frequency, and duration, it can effectively recapitulate the entire clinical spectrum of ovarian dysfunction, including DOR, POI, and POF phenotypes. Furthermore, as a widely utilized chemotherapeutic agent, CTX-induced animal models offer exceptional clinical fidelity, effectively recapitulating the pathological manifestations and disease progression observed in patients of reproductive age.

Conversely, although the combination of CTX and BUS achieves a more profound depletion of the primordial follicle pool, it is associated with heightened systemic toxicity and requires stringent dose control to minimize animal mortality. DDP is predominantly utilized to induce acute ovarian insult; however, its application in establishing stable, chronic POF models remains a challenge that requires further methodological optimization. TWP is particularly effective for simulating the TCM “Kidney Deficiency” syndrome, thereby facilitating integrated disease-syndrome studies in integrative medicine; however, batch-to-batch variability in its phytochemical constituents may compromise model reproducibility and reliability. Therefore, CTX-induced modeling demonstrates broader applicability and serves as an ideal tool for investigating the graded severity and dynamic pathological characteristics of ovarian function decline. Nevertheless, the extent to which existing literature has systematically and comprehensively evaluated CTX-specific modeling remains limited.

While recent reviews have addressed POI/POF modeling from various perspectives, their scopes remain either overly broad or pathway-restricted. Specifically, some literature offers panoramic overviews of diverse induction methods—encompassing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, genetic modifications, and metabolic stressors—yet falls short of a granular, protocol-specific examination dedicated exclusively to CTX [43, 46]. Others focus intensively on individual mechanisms such as cellular senescence, oxidative stress, or inflammation, without synthesizing their pathological crosstalk [45, 47]. Meanwhile, therapy-centric reviews emphasize pharmacological countermeasures, offering limited guidance on the optimization of model establishment [48]. Thus, despite these valuable contributions, a systematic synthesis that integrates both the mechanistic network and the methodological landscape specifically for CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction has remained noticeably absent.

To address these gaps, the current review provides a systematic synthesis of recent research progress, molecular mechanisms of injury, and modeling methodologies for CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction, thus serving as a foundational resource for future investigations in reproductive medicine.

Mechanisms of CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction

As a widely prescribed alkylating antineoplastic agent, CTX is extensively documented to exert profound gonadotoxicity in females. It triggers a cascade of follicular depletion—primordial, growing, and antral follicles—while simultaneously accelerating atresia and disrupting systemic endocrine balance. These cumulative insults severely impair the ovarian reserve, manifesting as clinical ovarian dysfunction [49, 50]. The mechanisms underlying CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction are inherently multifaceted and intricately interconnected, functioning as an integrated pathogenic network rather than a solitary molecular pathway. These pathways encompass oxidative stress, DNA damage and defective repair, apoptosis, cellular senescence, immuno-inflammatory responses, autophagy, and ferroptosis (Fig. 1). The following section systematically delineates the current research landscape and synthesizes current insights into these underlying mechanisms.

Oxidative stress

Oxidative stress manifests as a state of redox disequilibrium, arising from an imbalance between the production of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the scavenging capacity of antioxidant defense systems. The aberrant accumulation of ROS induces profound oxidative damage on cellular organelles, proteins, and DNA, exacerbating follicular atresia and eroding the ovarian reserve, ultimately leading to ovarian dysfunction [51, 52]. By triggering oxidative injury within the follicular niche—specifically in oocytes and granulosa cells—CTX effectively recapitulates clinicopathological phenotypes, thereby facilitating the reliable establishment of animal models that closely mimic human disease progression [53, 54]. In a study utilizing a CTX-induced DOR model, BALB/c mice exhibited a significant reduction in serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity along with elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations, reflecting a diminished superoxide radical scavenging capacity [55]. Consistent with these systemic findings, an independent study utilizing a CTX-induced POI model in C57BL/6 mice demonstrated diminished levels of SOD and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), concomitant with elevated MDA accumulation directly within ovarian tissues. Notably, this biochemical imbalance was accompanied by markedly enhanced ROS fluorescence signals localized predominantly in the granulosa cells [56]. Moreover, similar shifts in oxidative biomarkers have been observed in ovarian tissues within CTX-induced POF models [57]. These findings underscore the ubiquity of this mechanism across various stages of ovarian insufficiency, from diminished reserve to overt failure.

The excessive production and accumulation of ROS not only compromise the functional integrity of oocytes and granulosa cells but also perturb intercellular communication and impair steroidogenesis, ultimately accelerating the attrition of the ovarian reserve [58]. Studies have demonstrated that oocyte quality is significantly compromised in CTX-induced murine models, as evidenced by increased meiotic spindle and chromosomal abnormality scores, along with ovarian atrophy and fibrotic alterations [59]. In addition, direct exposure of oocytes to CTX and its primary metabolite, acrolein, has been shown to elevate intracellular ROS fluorescence intensity, which provides direct evidence of the oxidative insult mediated by these agents [59]. Intraperitoneal administration of CTX in a murine POI model elicited a robust oxidative stress response, as manifested by significantly elevated ROS levels in granulosa cells, diminished serum SOD and GSH-Px activities, and increased MDA content. Concomitantly, the granulosa cells exhibited severe mitochondrial impairment, which triggered a pathological cascade of mitophagy and pyroptosis, ultimately driving the decline in ovarian function [60].

DNA damage and defective repair

The accrual of DNA damage and the failure of repair mechanisms trigger cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, thereby disrupting normal folliculogenesis and eroding the ovarian reserve [61]. As a bifunctional alkylating agent, CTX generates active metabolites capable of forming both intrastrand and interstrand DNA cross-links, thereby inducing profound DNA damage and disrupting genomic integrity [62]. Upon sensing DNA lesions, cells orchestrate a sophisticated DNA damage response (DDR) network. This signaling cascade, mediated by p53, Chk1, and Chk2, acts to either facilitate DNA repair or enforce cell cycle arrest to maintain genomic integrity [63]. Upon successful completion of DNA repair, cell cycle arrest is relieved, and normal cellular functions, such as proliferation, are restored to homeostasis. However, when DNA lesions are irreparable or exceed the cell’s intrinsic repair capacity, the cell is directed toward programmed cell death pathways [50]. In a murine model of CTX-induced POF, the drug drives ovarian failure through two convergent mechanisms. First, it directly induces DNA damage in dormant oocytes, as evidenced by the accumulation of γH2AX, p-DNA-PK, and cleaved-PARP, subsequently triggering TAp63-mediated apoptosis. Second, CTX induces nuclear AKT phosphorylation while downregulating FOXO3a, thereby forcing dormant oocytes out of quiescence and driving aberrant follicular activation. The synergy between these two pathways culminates in the irreversible depletion of the ovarian reserve [64]. Furthermore, CTX has been shown to induce PUMA/NOXA-mediated oocyte apoptosis in response to DNA damage, while concurrently hyperactivating the PI3K/PTEN/AKT/FOXO3a signaling pathway. These molecular events collectively precipitate premature primordial follicle activation, exacerbate granulosa cell apoptosis, and accelerate the atresia of growing follicles, ultimately leading to a marked impairment of ovarian function [49].

Apoptosis

Apoptosis, a fundamental mode of programmed cell death, occurs throughout the stages of folliculogenesis and ovulation, playing a critical role in maintaining ovarian homeostasis [65]. Within the ovary, apoptosis manifests predominantly as follicular atresia. Under physiological conditions, this process maintains intrafollicular homeostasis and ovarian cyclicity by eliminating defective or compromised cells [66]. In pathological conditions such as oxidative stress and chemotherapy, apoptosis is aberrantly hyperactivated, precipitating premature follicular atresia and the accelerated exhaustion of the ovarian reserve, eventually resulting in a truncated reproductive lifespan [67, 68]. Studies have demonstrated that CTX induces apoptosis in multiple cell types within the ovary, thereby impairing ovarian function through these synergistic pathological effects [69, 70].

The elevation of aberrant oocyte apoptosis serves as the most direct indicator of ovarian reserve depletion [71]. Primordial follicles constitute the finite, non-renewable reservoir within the ovary, harboring the fundamental pool of immature oocytes. Studies have demonstrated that CTX treatment results in the near-total ablation of the primordial follicle pool in wild-type adult mice, leading to massive oocyte loss. However, the genetic deletion of the pro-apoptotic gene PUMA confers protection, preserving primordial follicles and maintaining fertility following treatment [72]. These findings collectively suggest that CTX-induced DNA damage activates the PUMA-mediated apoptotic pathway, thereby triggering oocyte death and the subsequent depletion of the ovarian reserve [72]. CTX and its primary active metabolite, 4-hydroperoxycyclophosphamide (4-HC), elicit γH2AX upregulation in primordial oocytes, thereby activating the ATR-CHK1/CHK2 signaling cascade. This pathway promotes the activation of TAp63, an oocyte-specific DNA damage response protein, which subsequently induces the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and triggers the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. These molecular events culminate in the generation of cleaved PARP, ultimately leading to oocyte apoptosis and the irreversible depletion of the ovarian reserve [73].

Beyond the oocyte, granulosa cells are also pivotal targets of CTX-induced apoptosis. As the indispensable supporting cells within the follicle, granulosa cells provide essential metabolic substrates to the oocyte, synthesizing steroid hormones and regulating follicular development through bidirectional signaling crosstalk [74]. Granulosa cell apoptosis precipitates follicular atresia, which subsequently impairs follicular growth, maturation, and ovulation. This cascade not only compromises oocyte development but also further exacerbates the attrition of the ovarian reserve [75]. CTX and its active metabolite 4-HC attenuate EZH2 expression and deplete H3K27me3 levels in ovarian granulosa cells, leading to the loss of H3K27me3-mediated repressive marks at the promoters of pro-apoptotic gene promoters. This epigenetic derepression aberrantly activates a pro-apoptotic transcriptional program, ultimately driving granulosa cell death [76]. CTX also attenuates PI3K/AKT signaling activity in granulosa cells, precipitating a pro-apoptotic shift in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and the subsequent activation of caspase-3, collectively culminating in granulosa cell apoptosis [77]. Another study confirmed that CTX induces DNA double-strand breaks in both oocytes and granulosa cells. However, apoptosis markers such as TUNEL and cleaved-PARP were predominantly detected in granulosa cells of growing follicles, suggesting that CTX primarily leads to follicular depletion and ovarian insufficiency by inducing granulosa cell apoptosis [78]. Furthermore, knockout of Bak and Bax in granulosa cells significantly reduces CTX-induced follicle loss and improves fertility outcomes, further confirming that inhibiting granulosa cell apoptosis confers ovarian protection .

In addition to oocytes and granulosa cells, ovarian stem cells are profoundly vulnerable to CTX-induced apoptosis. Characterized by their intrinsic self-renewal and differentiation potential, these cells represent highly promising therapeutic targets for counteracting chemotherapy-induced ovarian injury [74]. CTX and its active metabolite 4-HC trigger apoptosis in this subpopulation through both direct and indirect pathways. This mechanism depletes the ovarian stem cell pool, compromises the primordial follicle reservoir, and ultimately accelerates the decline of ovarian function. It has been reported that CTX directly induces an abrupt surge in intracellular ROS levels within ovarian stem cells, precipitating DNA damage that subsequently activates the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. This cascade modulates the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio to trigger the key executioner, caspase-3, ultimately driving ovarian stem cell apoptosis [79]. Another study has further delineated the upstream regulatory mechanism of CTX-induced ovarian stem cell depletion. Specifically, CTX treatment suppresses Hedgehog signaling activity, thereby compromising the antioxidant defense capacity of these cells. This disruption exacerbates ROS accumulation and triggers the dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential, ultimately activating the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway [80].

Cellular senescence

Cellular senescence refers to the stable and irreversible exit of cells from the cell cycle into a permanent state of proliferative arrest, serving as a critical defense mechanism against diverse endogenous and exogenous stressors [47]. Cellular senescence is characterized by the secretion of a diverse repertoire of pro-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and proteases, a multifaceted secretome collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [81]. Current research indicates that oxidative stress and inflammation induce ovarian cellular senescence by perturbing the follicular microenvironment. Senescent cells subsequently exacerbate this microenvironmental imbalance through the SASP, establishing a self-perpetuating feedback loop that drives the onset and progression of ovarian dysfunction [82, 83]. Both granulosa cells derived from CTX-induced POI mice and CTX-treated human granulosa cells exhibited significantly elevated levels of cellular senescence hallmarks, including p16, p21, p53, and γH2AX. These molecular changes were accompanied by a marked attrition of primordial and primary follicles and an increased ratio of growing to dormant follicles, reflecting an accelerated recruitment of the ovarian reserve [47]. In the CTX-induced POF model, CTX treatment induces ROS accumulation in granulosa cells and activates p53, which upregulates the expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase SIAH1. This, in turn, promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of the telomere-protective protein TRF2, leading to the loss of telomere protection and ultimately inducing granulosa cell senescence [84].

Immune-inflammatory response

Immune-inflammatory responses also play an essential role in CTX-induced diminished ovarian reserve. Chronic low-grade inflammation is considered a key contributor to impaired follicular development and is fundamentally involved in the pathological processes of various ovarian dysfunctions [85]. Studies utilizing RNA sequencing on ovarian tissue from both chemotherapy-induced and age-related DOR mouse models have revealed that the shared pathogenic mechanisms underlying these two DOR subtypes are fundamentally associated with immune-inflammatory responses [86]. Both DOR models exhibited a significant reduction in the infiltration of regulatory T (Treg) cells, coupled with the increased recruitment of M0 macrophages, resting natural killer (NK) cells, and CD4+ T cells. These findings suggest a profound dysregulation of the local ovarian immune landscape [86]. CTX also activates the p38 MAPK/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway, upregulating the expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, which induce chronic inflammation and oxidative damage in granulosa cells. This signaling activation impairs the proliferative capacity of these cells, subsequently disrupting follicular architecture, accelerating atresia, and depleting the recruitable follicle pool [87]. Another study corroborated the presence of a local inflammatory response and the pro-inflammatory activation of granulosa cells within the CTX-induced POF mouse model. Moreover, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell transplantation significantly attenuated this inflammatory response and apoptosis in granulosa cells through a paracrine mechanism, thereby restoring ovarian function [88].

Autophagy

Autophagy serves as a fundamental self-protective mechanism through which cells counter adverse survival conditions, such as nutrient deprivation and oxidative stress. By degrading damaged organelles and misfolded proteins via the lysosomal pathway, this catabolic process maintains intracellular homeostasis and ensures cellular proteostasis under stress [89]. Evidence suggests that basal autophagy is essential for maintaining physiological oocyte development, whereas the aberrant activation or suppression of this process leads to cellular dysfunction and triggers autophagic cell death [90, 91]. CTX and 4-HC have been shown to induce pathological hypoxia and subsequent autophagic cell death in ovarian granulosa cells. Mechanistically, upregulated HIF-1α activates its target gene BNIP3, which then binds to Bcl-2. This competitive displacement liberates Beclin-1 from the Bcl-2/Beclin-1 complex, thereby initiating the autophagic cascade. This cascade induces mitochondrial damage and autophagosome accumulation, ultimately driving granulosa cell death and accelerating primordial follicle depletion [92]. CTX also activates the MAPK signaling pathway in granulosa cells, elevating the phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2, c-Jun, and c-Fos. This activation is accompanied by the upregulation of autophagy-related proteins Beclin-1, ATG5, and LC3-II, alongside the downregulation of p62, which collectively compromises ovarian function [93].

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death that is intricately involved in the pathogenesis of various ovarian dysfunctions [94]. CTX induces ferroptosis in ovarian granulosa cells by disrupting iron metabolism, leading to iron overload and triggering overwhelming lipid peroxidation. Consequently, this process results in impaired follicular development, ovarian stromal fibrosis, and the deterioration of ovarian function [95, 96]. CTX upregulates heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) expression in granulosa cells, leading to intracellular iron overload. This process subsequently triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by elevated mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). The aggravated oxidative stress further increases total and lipid ROS levels while suppressing GPX4 activity, resulting in substantial intracellular accumulation of lipid peroxides and ultimately inducing ferroptosis in granulosa cells [97]. Another study indicates that CTX impairs the NRF2-mediated antioxidant defense system in granulosa cells, leading to the excessive accumulation of ROS. This ROS overload triggers aberrant autophagy, which subsequently promotes intracellular iron overload and compromises cellular peroxide-scavenging capacity. Consequently, lipid peroxides accumulate massively within the cells, ultimately inducing ferroptosis in granulosa cells [98]. Furthermore, a comparative study of granulosa cells from women with normal ovarian reserve, DOR, and advanced maternal age found that GPX4 expression was significantly downregulated in both the DOR and aging groups. Subsequent experiments confirmed that CTX suppresses GPX4 expression, inducing typical mitochondrial alterations characteristic of ferroptosis, which subsequently drives the depletion of follicular reserve and the decline in oocyte competence [99].

Mechanistic integration

Although the aforementioned pathways have been delineated separately, they do not function in isolation. In vivo, CTX-induced ovarian damage is orchestrated by intricate crosstalk and reciprocal feedback loops among these molecular events. This section summarizes the principal synergistic interactions and regulatory networks that collectively amplify the initial gonadotoxic insult.

The interplay between oxidative stress and DNA damage represents a primary amplification loop in CTX-induced ovarian toxicity. As demonstrated in POF models, excessive mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) accumulation not only directly induces ovarian DNA lesions but also synergizes with TRPM2-mediated intracellular Ca²⁺ overload to activate the AMPK/p53 axis [100]. This cooperative signaling cascade simultaneously drives proliferative arrest and amplifies the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, indicating that oxidative injury and genotoxic stress mutually reinforce each other to govern granulosa cell fate.

Notably, DNA damage does not uniformly commit cells to apoptosis; it can also trigger cellular senescence, creating a parallel pathological route. In CTX-treated ovaries, the DNA damage marker γH2AX is markedly upregulated in tandem with the senescence effectors p16 and p21 [47]. This bifurcation suggests that the severity of DNA lesions and the cellular context govern whether granulosa cells undergo apoptotic elimination or enter a persistent senescent state, both of which contribute to follicular depletion, albeit through divergent temporal dynamics.

Beyond classical apoptosis, oxidative stress orchestrates ferroptosis, a recently identified iron-dependent cell death pathway in CTX injury. Activation of the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis serves as a critical brake on this process; the disruption of which leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxides and labile iron, ultimately triggering ferroptotic granulosa cell death [101]. The concomitant activation of both apoptosis and ferroptosis cascades upon Nrf2 exhaustion highlights the overlapping upstream molecular switches governing different death modalities. This implies that oxidative stress simultaneously drives parallel death pathways that converge to synergistically exacerbate ovarian injury.

Most importantly, the pathological network extends beyond intrinsic cellular events to encompass immune-inflammatory crosstalk. CTX activates the p53/NF-κB axis to drive M1 macrophage polarization, and these pro-inflammatory macrophages in turn secrete factors that elevate ROS levels in adjacent granulosa cells, thereby instigating p53/p21/p38 MAPK-mediated senescence [102]. This paracrine loop exemplifies a higher-order synergy wherein immune activation amplifies oxidative stress, which then perpetuates ovarian dysfunction independent of sustained drug exposure.

In summary, CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction arises from a highly interconnected pathological network. Oxidative stress serves as a central hub, driving DNA damage, apoptosis, senescence, and ferroptosis, while dysregulated autophagy and immune-inflammatory responses further amplify the initial insult. The interplay among these mechanisms creates a self-reinforcing cycle, ultimately driving follicular depletion and functional decline.

Establishment of the CTX-induced ovarian dysfunction animal models

Due to its potent gonadotoxicity, CTX has been extensively utilized to establish pathological models that faithfully recapitulate the pathological features and progressive continuum observed in human ovarian injury. Moreover, its brief induction period and high reproducibility have established it as a cornerstone agent for modeling animal models of ovarian dysfunction [103, 104]. The following section systematically reviews and analyzes CTX modeling methodologies through four primary lenses: animal species selection, routes of administration, dosing regimens, and the criteria for model validation and outcome evaluation.

Animal species selection

Rodents, specifically rats and mice, are widely utilized in biomedical research due to their well-characterized, cost-effective, physiological similarity to humans, and remarkable pharmacological responsiveness, which facilitate the reliable replication of clinical disease models [105, 106]. Female rodents exhibit predictable estrous cyclicity and possess a hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis highly homologous to that of humans. Characterized by well-characterized genetic backgrounds and a rapid reproductive turnover, these animals serve as the quintessential in vivo models for mechanistic research on ovarian dysfunction. For model establishment, 6-8-week-old sexually mature female mice (typically C57BL/6 or BALB/c strains) and 8-10-week-old female rats (commonly Sprague-Dawley or Wistar strains) are widely preferred to ensure experimental standardization [107–110].

Route of administration

During model establishment, CTX is typically administered via two primary systemic routes: intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) and tail vein injection (i.v.) [111, 112]. As a prodrug, CTX exerts its cytotoxic effects only after undergoing bioactivation by hepatic microsomal enzymes into its active alkylating metabolite, phosphoramide mustard [113]. Intraperitoneal injection facilitates a gradual and sustained absorption phase, effectively recapitulating the prolonged systemic exposure profile characteristic of chemotherapeutic agents in vivo. Additionally, this route affords the distinct advantages of procedural simplicity, rapid execution, and minimized handling-induced stress, rendering it ideal for large-scale and prolonged dosing regimens [114]. In contrast, tail vein injection remains technically demanding, particularly in C57BL/6 mice due to their dark tail pigmentation and indistinct vascular anatomy. Repeated venipuncture markedly increases the risk of local complications, including phlebitis, thrombosis, or even procedural mortality [115–117]. Moreover, comparative studies between intraperitoneal and tail vein injections of CTX reveal negligible differences in pharmacodynamic responses and metabolic activation levels [118]. Consequently, intraperitoneal injection has become a widely used and well-established route for establishing CTX-induced animal models.

Dosage regimen

During animal modeling, dosing regimens are highly contingent upon the target pathological phenotype, the specific animal species and strain, and the chosen route of administration. Currently, the dosing protocols for CTX-induced models of ovarian dysfunction can be broadly categorized into three paradigms: a single high-dose intraperitoneal injection, multiple consecutive low-dose intraperitoneal injections, and the combined administration of CTX and BUS (Fig. 2). In the first category, DOR, POI, and POF models are established via a single high-dose intraperitoneal injection. Typically, the dosages range from 75 to 90 mg/kg for DOR [119, 120], 90–120 mg/kg for POI [60, 126], and 100–150 mg/kg for POF [133, 139, 140]. The administered dose increases with the severity of ovarian impairment. This protocol is straightforward, offering a rapid induction period, well-defined modeling timelines, and high experimental reproducibility, while inducing potent acute gonadotoxic effects. The second category features consecutive, low-dose intraperitoneal administration, primarily utilized to establish POI and POF models. Specific protocols comprise: (i) a fixed-dose regimen, involving daily administrations of 50–70 mg/kg for 14–15 consecutive days [19, 123, 134]; (ii) a loading-plus-maintenance regimen, characterized by an initial loading dose of 50–200 mg/kg followed by daily maintenance doses of 5–12 mg/kg for 12–15 consecutive days [108, 128, 129]. This protocol effectively mimics clinical administration patterns, closely reproducing the progressive deterioration of ovarian function following repeated chemotherapy cycles. Consequently, it offers enhanced translational relevance for constructing chronic injury models, albeit at the expense of extended experimental durations and an elevated risk of cumulative animal mortality. The third category comprises a single intraperitoneal injection of CTX combined with BUS. This approach is primarily used to establish POI models, albeit occasionally applied to DOR and POF models. However, the dosages employed exhibit substantial variation in ovarian dysfunction models. A common regimen involves 60–120 mg/kg of CTX combined with 6–30 mg/kg of BUS [21, 25, 125]. While this protocol facilitates exhaustive follicular depletion, it is associated with profound systemic toxicity and an elevated risk of animal mortality.

However, current dosing regimens for establishing models of ovarian dysfunction exhibit substantial methodological ambiguity. For instance, within the single high-dose paradigm in C57BL/6 mice, 90 mg/kg of CTX has been employed to establish both DOR and POI models, underscoring a pronounced phenotypic divergence at identical dosages [60, 120]. Regarding the corresponding multiple low-dose regimen in SD/Wistar rats, a priming dose of 50 mg/kg followed by 8 mg/kg for 14 consecutive days has been interchangeably applied to both POI and POF models, highlighting the significant dosimetric overlap across the ovarian failure spectrum [127, 135]. This phenomenon, characterized by identical modeling protocols receiving disparate designations, reflects the current lack of a unified consensus for characterizing CTX-induced ovarian impairment.

Reflecting the dose-dependent nature of CTX-induced gonadotoxicity, the induction dosages for modeling DOR, POI, and POF phenotypes should exhibit a graded escalation to accurately reflect the increasing severity of ovarian impairment in this pharmacological model. However, the data in Table 2 reveal an anomalous discrepancy wherein the dosages employed for POF models are occasionally lower than those for POI. For example, in BALB/c mice, a single intraperitoneal injection of 120 mg/kg CTX has been used for POI modeling, whereas a lower dose of 100 mg/kg was utilized to establish POF [126, 139], reflecting an inverted dose-response relationship in current literature.

We propose that this dose-severity discordance is driven by a combination of subjective factors and objective confounding variables. On the one hand, subjectivity plays a dominant role: in the BALB/c example above, where animal ages (6–8 weeks) and observation windows (28 days) were largely comparable across studies, the assignment of a lower dose to the more severe POF label strongly suggests that disease nomenclature is frequently tailored to specific research aims rather than being derived from objective, quantitative assessments of ovarian insult. On the other hand, substantial heterogeneity in experimental conditions across different studies significantly exacerbates this confusion. As detailed in Table 2, animal species and strains (C57BL/6 vs. BALB/c vs. SD rats), ages (ranging from 5 to 8 weeks), post- injection observation windows (spanning from 7 to 35 days), and evaluation criteria (varying from simple hormonal assays to comprehensive fertility tests) diverge markedly across the literature. For instance, the identical dual-dose regimen of 50 mg/kg combined with 8 mg/kg in SD rats yielded disparate disease labels (POI vs. POF), which may be partially attributable to differences in animal ages (6–8 weeks vs. 5 weeks) and observation endpoints (15 days vs. 14 days post-CTX) across the cited studies [127, 141, 142]. Thus, while researchers’ subjective choices contribute fundamentally to the current nomenclature chaos, the lack of standardized animal characteristics and procedural parameters critically hinders direct cross-study comparisons, collectively contributing to the observed dose-severity inversion.

In summary, the absence of standardized dosing protocols, intertwined with substantial heterogeneities in animal characteristics, observation windows, and evaluation criteria, inevitably blurs the diagnostic boundaries among DOR, POI, and POF. This operational ambiguity poses formidable challenges to reproducible model construction and critically undermines cross-study comparability. This situation creates a two-fold conundrum: differing protocols for the same model hinder direct comparisons, while identical protocols assigned to different models obscure the precise correspondence between pathological characteristics and nomenclature. Therefore, the establishment of a unified modeling framework predicated on objective pathophysiological indicators is an urgent imperative to advance the standardization and reproducibility of animal models in ovarian dysfunction research.

Criteria for model validation and outcome evaluation

The hallmarks of ovarian dysfunction primarily encompass menstrual cycle irregularities, the depletion of follicles across all developmental stages, accelerated follicular atresia, endocrine dysregulation, and subsequent infertility. Based on these hallmarks, the evaluation criteria for model validation are categorized into five dimensions: estrous cycle patterns, ovarian histomorphology, sex hormone profiles, behavioral assessment, and reproductive capacity (Fig. 3). Since rodents possess a regular estrous cycle regulated by an intact HPG axis rather than a menstrual cycle, the estrous cycle is routinely monitored as a reliable proxy for clinical menstrual observation [148, 149]. Disruption of estrous cyclicity is widely recognized as a pivotal phenotypic hallmark for validating successful model establishment [150]. Ovarian histomorphometric analysis, including ovarian weight and quantitative follicle counts across various developmental stages, provides a direct and reliable assessment of chemotherapy-induced gonadotoxicity [151]. Animal models of ovarian dysfunction typically exhibit ovarian atrophy characterized by reduced weight. Histopathologically, the successful establishment of the model is evidenced by a significant reduction in primordial, growing, and antral follicles, alongside a surge in atretic follicles. Furthermore, sex hormone levels serve as critical indicators of ovarian reserve, notably including Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH), Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH), Luteinizing Hormone (LH), and Estradiol (E2). In pathological models, these hormonal alterations are typically characterized by reduced AMH and E2 levels alongside elevated FSH and LH concentrations, with the magnitude of hormonal aberrations directly correlating with the severity of ovarian dysfunction [152, 153]. General behavioral indicators encompass the animal’s integumentary condition, locomotor activity, dietary intake, and responsiveness to external stimuli. Additionally, assessments of neurobehavioral status, sleep patterns, and excretory status provide critical insights into the systemic impact of the modeling protocol [154]. Although these indicators do not directly reflect ovarian function, they offer a comprehensive view of the systemic impact of CTX. Their abnormal manifestations thus provide critical ancillary support for the successful induction of the disease phenotype. Furthermore, as ovarian dysfunction fundamentally compromises fertility, evaluating litter size following timed mating serves as an ultimate functional readout reflecting the cumulative severity of reproductive impairment.

While specific evaluation panels vary across the literature, the integration of estrous cyclicity, ovarian histomorphology, and critical hormonal markers (FSH, AMH, E2) constitutes a universally recognized core matrix. This triad serves as the foundational reference for standardizing modeling criteria and validating model outcomes. In contrast, general behavioral assessments and fertility outcomes serve primarily as ancillary indicators; their inclusion remains discretionary, allowing researchers to refine their evaluation panels according to the specific objectives of the experimental design.

Summary and future perspectives

Owing to its potent gonadotoxicity, CTX induces multifaceted ovarian damage that closely recapitulates the core pathological features of DOR, POI, and POF. Consequently, it has become a widely accepted inductive agent for establishing animal models and facilitating in-depth mechanistic research in ovarian dysfunction. At the molecular level, CTX orchestrates a synergistic pathological network wherein oxidative stress and DNA damage converge with cellular senescence, apoptosis, immune-inflammatory responses, autophagy, and ferroptosis to collectively dismantle ovarian function. Currently, the methodological landscape of model establishment is dominated by three primary induction paradigms: acute high-dose administration, chronic repeated low-dose regimens, and the synergistic combination of CTX and BUS. For model validation, the integration of estrous cyclicity, ovarian histomorphology, and serum hormonal profiles constitutes a tripartite core framework for defining modeling success and evaluating experimental efficacy. Despite extensive research, unified modeling standards remain elusive, resulting in ambiguous boundaries between dosing regimens across different pathological stages. This dose-severity inconsistency, coupled with the variable selection of assessment metrics, complicates the objective validation of models. Consequently, the lack of a consensus-driven framework poses a major barrier to reproducing and comparing findings across different research groups.

Building upon these critical insights, future research should advance along two primary directions to enhance the rigor and translational value of ovarian dysfunction models. First, to conduct an in-depth analysis of the multi-mechanistic interaction network underlying CTX-induced ovarian injury, thereby identifying novel targets for precision interventions. While the preceding sections have delineated the intricate crosstalk among oxidative stress, DNA damage, and distinct cell death modalities, emerging evidence suggests that additional layers of complexity—such as the underexplored interplay between mitophagy and ferroptosis, as well as the spatiotemporal remodeling of the immune-inflammatory microenvironment—may further dictate the architecture of this injury network [155, 156]. Further studies are warranted to define the roles of these emerging mechanisms within the injury network and to decipher their synergistic cascading relationships. To this end, single-cell transcriptomics and spatial multi-omics could be leveraged to systematically delineate the intercellular molecular communication networks among oocytes, granulosa cells, and ovarian stem cells following CTX exposure. Such approaches would help transcend the traditional focus on isolated cell types or linear pathways, pinpointing the master molecular nodes that govern the quiescence-activation homeostasis of the primordial follicle pool, thereby providing a definitive theoretical basis for developing multi-target ovarian protective agents.

Second, standardized and graded modeling protocols and evaluation criteria should be developed through coordinated, consensus-building efforts. Given the multiple interconnected variables involved in modeling—including strain, age, and dosing parameters—a single-center study can not adequately cover the entire experimental matrix. Multi-institutional collaborative networks should be established to cross-compare the susceptibility and injury landscapes to standard CTX regimens across different strains (e.g., C57BL/6 vs. BALB/c) and across animals at distinct sexual maturity stages (e.g., pre-pubertal early maturity vs. fully mature adul thood ). Building on this, a multi-parametric weighted scoring model could be developed to integrate multiple diagnostic dimensions—estrous cycle dynamics, endocrine profiles (e.g., AMH/FSH ratios), morphological indices (primordial follicle atresia), and definitive fertility outcomes (pregnancy and live birth rates). This standardized framework would allow for the rigorous differentiation among DOR, POI, and POF stages based on their respective dose thresholds and phenotypic windows. Ultimately, by integrating the aforementioned collaborative validation data, we aim to establish a modular, tiered modeling guideline encompassing the ‘animal strain—dosing regimen—evaluation timepoint—disease severity’ paradigm, which will substantially enhance cross-study comparability and the efficiency of preclinical data translation.
