5 G new radio fiber-wireless converged systems by injection locking multi-optical carrier into directly-modulated lasers
Authors: Hai-Han Lu, Hsiao-Mei Lin, Chia-Peng Wang, Stotaw Talbachew Hayle, Chung-Yi Li, Xu-Hong Huang, Yu-Yao Bai, Kelper Okram, Jia-Ming Lu, Yu-Chen Chung, Wei-Wen Hsu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The integration of fiber-optical wireless convergence with fifth generation new radio is crucial in building high-performance access networks. This approach not only provides high-transmission-rates but also ensures broad coverage, which is vital for future networks. Here we report fifth generation new radio fiber-wireless converged systems by injection locking multi-optical carrier into directly-modulated lasers. Data rates of 10 Gb/s, 20 Gb/s, and 40 Gb/s are achieved by direct modulation on directly-modulated lasers using 16-quadrature amplitude modulation-orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing signal. Through 25-km single-mode fiber, 1.5-km optical wireless, and 12-/22-/33-m millimeter-wave/sub-terahertz wireless integrated-media, 10-Gb/s/20-GHz, 20-Gb/s/60-GHz, and 40-Gb/s/100-GHz signals are transmitted with acceptably low bit error rates and error vector magnitudes, as well as distinct constellations. The successful transport of fifth generation new radio millimeter-wave and sub-terahertz signals at different carrier frequencies through fiber-wireless convergence demonstrates the potential of the system to meet the evolving requirement of next-generation communications.

Introduction

High-transmission-rate access networks are critical to facilitate widespread adoption and seamless operation of various advanced technologies. Advancements such as the Internet of Things, mixed reality, and ultra-high-definition video streaming heavily rely on high transmission capacity1–5. To address the increasing demand for these technologies, it’s essential to ensure high transmission rate not only in fiber backbone networks but also in last-mile connections reaching individual users and devices. One promising approach to achieve the required transmission rate is through the integration of various technologies, particularly the integration of fiber optics, optical wireless, and radio-frequency (RF) wireless communications. The realization of fifth generation (5 G) fiber-wireless converged systems thus holds great promise in enhancing high transmission rate. As the 5 G technologies continue to develop, the exploration of millimeter-wave (MMW) and sub-terahertz (THz) bands for 5 G communications opens up new possibilities for achieving higher transmission rate compared to traditional sub-6 GHz frequencies6,7. MMW and sub-THz frequencies offer high transmission rate but have limited range due to higher atmospheric absorption. Therefore, they are more suitable for short-range wireless communication such as localized outdoor environments. Optical wireless communication has developed as a potential one to resolve the limitation of short-range 5 G wireless communication. To achieve high transmission rate over long distances, 5 G new radio (NR) fiber-wireless converged systems that simultaneously transmit MMW and sub-THz signals are developed8–10. The integration of fiber-optical wireless convergence with 5 G NR extension enables high transmission rate and covers wide service area.

Previous study demonstrated the construction of a 5 G MMW analog radio-over-fiber system operating at 28.2 and 35.3 GHz by injection locking an optical carrier into a distributed feedback (DFB) laser with direct modulation11. However, the technique of injection locking a DFB laser with direct modulation is simpler than injection locking multiple optical carriers into directly-modulated lasers (DMLs). In addition, a 64-quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) discrete multitone analog radio-over-fiber distribution system working at 37.5 GHz was successfully built12. Nevertheless, it required a complex MMW generation structure using a dual-stage active demultiplexer. Moreover, operating at lower frequencies of 28.2, 35.3 and 37.5 GHz achieved lower data rates compared to 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems operating at higher frequencies of 60 and 100 GHz. Demonstrating a 100-Gb/s multicarrier THz wireless transport system employing a gain-switched laser comb source was also shown to be practicable13. Establishing a 25-Gb/s orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) 60-GHz radio-over-fiber transport system using a gain-switched DFB laser was presented to be practicable14. Erecting an optical heterodyne analog radio-over-fiber link employing a gain-switched laser optical frequency comb source was shown to be feasible15. Nonetheless, elaborated gain-switched laser optical frequency comb sources are needed. Furthermore, none of them are connected to 5 G signals through optical wireless communications. Integrating 5 G signals with optical wireless communications can extend the coverage limitations of 5 G communications.

In the demonstration, we present a 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged system by injection locking multi-optical carrier into DMLs. Compared with external modulation based on Mach-Zehnder modulators or in-phase/quadrature modulators, 10, 20, and 40 Gb/s different data rates are implemented through direct modulation of lasers using 16-QAM-OFDM signal, providing a way with low-cost and low-complexity. A DFB laser diode (LD) is modulated directly by an RF signal to produce multi-optical carrier with specific carrier spacing. After being enhanced by an optical carrier noise (OCNR) enhancement device and selected by an optical bandpass filter, an optical comb source with 7 optical carriers is produced. Through light injection locking into the DMLs, 10, 20, and 40 Gb/s 16-QAM-OFDM signals are applied to three of the optical carriers. Since an unmodulated optical carrier performs as an optical local oscillator (LO), 5 G NR communications at MMW and THz frequency range are creatively generated. An optical comb source, primarily comprising a DFB LD with multi-optical carrier for injection locking into DMLs, is used in 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems. The proposed method is attractive since it avoids the requirement for costly components such as gain-switched discrete mode laser16 and mode-locked InAs quantum dot laser17, sophisticated electro-optic optical frequency comb-based supercontinuum source18, complicated micro-resonator-based optical comb source19, and costly and complex cascaded phase modulator with intensity modulator20. By addressing the key concern of simplifying the optical comb source, the proposed approach that employs a DFB LD with multi-optical carrier for injection locking into DMLs offers a practical and cost-effective solution for deploying 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems.

The realization of such a 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged system poses significant challenges, particularly for high total transmission rate and required transmission performance. MMW and sub-THz signals over a 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged system at 20, 60, and 100 GHz different carrier frequencies are successfully achieved. A high total transmission rate of 70 Gb/s is achieved, including 10 Gb/s at 20-GHz MMW frequency, 20 Gb/s at 60-GHz MMW frequency, and 40 Gb/s at 100-GHz sub-THz frequency. In actual 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems, different data rates need to be transmitted at different carrier frequencies to optimize spectrum efficiency. Higher carrier frequencies carry higher data rates, while lower carrier frequencies carry lower data rates21. Through 25 km single-mode fiber (SMF), 1.5 km optical wireless, and 12/22/33 m 5 G NR wireless cross-media, 10-Gb/s/20-GHz, 20-Gb/s/60-GHz, and 40-Gb/s/100-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signals are distributed with satisfactorily low bit error rates (BERs) and error vector magnitudes (EVMs), as well as distinct constellations. By injection locking into DMLs, the aggregate transmission rate is substantially increased. By using triplet lenses, the reach of optical wireless links is greatly extended. It is significant as it marks the successful application of injection locking into DMLs to achieve a 70-Gb/s high aggregate transmission rate, and the successful application of using triplet lenses to establish a 1.5-km optical wireless communication. Laser beam, when transmitting over long distances, makes it challenging to completely couple into the fiber ferrule input22,23. Triplet lens at the reception end addresses this issue by acting as a laser beam narrowing device. It effectively narrows laser beam size and ensures maximum coupling into fiber ferrule input. By overcoming challenges associated with laser beam spreading and optimizing coupling efficiency, triplet lenses enable reliable communication over long distances. Achieving low BERs and EVMs, along with clear constellation diagrams, at an aggregate transmission rate of 70 Gb/s highlights the effectiveness of 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems. The successful transport of MMW and sub-THz signals over fiber-wireless convergence exemplifies the advanced capabilities required for 5 G NR. It highlights the system’s ability to meet high total data rates and large coverage requirements.

Measured BERs of 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems under different conditions

Figure 3a–c show the measured BERs against different PD input powers, using 16-QAM-OFDM signal at 10, 20, and 40 Gb/s different data rates. Under the conditions through cascaded-media, including 25 km SMF, 1.5 km optical wireless, and 12/22/33 m MMW/sub-THz wireless, at appropriate PD input powers, the measured BERs are below the 3.8 × 10−3 forward error correction (FEC) limit (FEC 7%). Appropriate PD input powers are crucial for achieving BERs below the FEC limit. For 20 GHz MMW signal transmission using 16-QAM-OFDM signal at 10 Gb/s data rate (Fig. 3a), lowest BERs of 3.7 × 10−4 (12 m link), 6.6 × 10−4 (22 m link), and 1.2 × 10−3 (33 m link) are achieved at 2.1, 2.4, and 2.7 dBm high-speed PD input powers. As the high-speed PD input power increases, the BER first decreases to reach optimal values for 12 m, 22 m, and 33 m wireless links. However, after exceeding the optimal high-speed PD input power, further increasing the high-speed PD input power will lead to saturation of MMW signal output power. Once the MMW signal power is saturated, additional high-speed PD input power will not lead to an improvement in BER performance28,29. In fact, it causes BER performance degradation. Providing an optimal high-speed PD input power is crucial to achieve the lowest BER. Furthermore, for 60 GHz MMW and 100 GHz sub-THz signals transmission using 16-QAM-OFDM signal at 20 Gb/s (Fig. 3b) and 40 Gb/s (Fig. 3c) data rates, lowest BERs of 4.5 × 10−4 and 6.3 × 10−4 (12 m link), 7.7 × 10−4 and 8.6 × 10−4 (22 m link), as well as 2.5 × 10−3 and 3.8 × 10−3 (33 m link) are achieved at 4.3 and 6.3 dBm, 4.4 and 6.5 dBm, as well as 4.7 and 6.7 dBm ultra-fast PD, and UTC-PD input powers. As the input power of ultra-fast PD/UTC-PD increases, the BER initially decreases. However, this trend continues only to a certain optimal input power level. Beyond the optimal input power, further increases in power can cause the BER to rise again. For the constellations, through 25 km SMF, 1.5 km optical wireless, and 33 m MMW/sub-THz wireless transmissions, distinct constellations are observed at 10, 20, and 40 Gb/s data rates. The chirp generated by direct modulation in DML is related to optical signal’s phase deviation. Injection-locked DML offers the advantage of chirp reduction, thereby improving signal quality30. Methods

5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems

Fig. 6 illustrates the architecture of 5 G NR fiber-wireless converged systems employing injection locking of multi-optical carrier into DMLs, using 20-, 60-, and 100-GHz frequencies as carrier frequencies, across diverse-media including 25 km SMF, 1.5 km optical wireless, and 12/22/33 m MMW/sub-THz wireless. A 20-GHz optical comb source generates seven optical carriers spaced 20 GHz apart. One optical carrier (λ1) from the output port 1 of wavelength selective switch remains unmodulated and serves as an optical LO. Three optical carriers from the output port 2 (λ2), 4 (λ4), and 6 (λ6) of wavelength selective switch are attenuated by variable optical attenuators, polarization-adjusted by polarization controllers, and injection-locked into DMLs (DML1, DML2, and DML3) through optical circulators. For data modulation, 10, 20, and 40 Gb/s single-sideband 16-QAM-OFDM signals from OFDM transmitters modulate the DML1, DML2, and DML3, respectively. The 20-GHz/60-GHz/100-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signal has parameters of 512 fast Fourier transform size, 128/256/512 subcarriers, and 10 G samples per second (GSa/s). 128/256/512 subcarriers and 10 GSa/s sampling rate result in a subcarrier spacing of 10/(128/256/512) GHz. With 128/256/512 data subcarriers, the signal bandwidth becomes 128/256/512 × 10/(128/256/512) GHz = 10/10/10 GHz. The total number of bits transmitted per symbol is 128/256/512 × 4 = 512/1024/2048. The bit per sample rate can be calculated by dividing the total number of bits by the total number of samples: (512/1024/2048)/512 = 1/2/4 bit/sample. Digital-to-analog conversion transmits samples at 10 GSa/s rate. The data rate can be calculated by multiplying the bit per sample rate with the transmission rate: 1/2/4 × 10 = 10/20/40 Gb/s. To know more clearly, a table (Table 1) lists the 16-QAM-OFDM parameters, signal bandwidth and data rate calculation. Injection locking DML1, DML2, and DML3 locked at λ2, λ4, and λ6 are thereby modulated with 16-QAM-OFDM at 10, 20, and 40 Gb/s different data rates. Optical carriers from the injection-locked DML1, DML2, and DML3 are spaced 20 GHz, 60 GHz, and 100 GHz, respectively, apart from the optical LO. One unmodulated optical LO and three modulated and injection-locked optical carriers and are combined by a 4 × 1 optical coupler. The combined optical carriers travel through an EDFA. A variable optical attenuator is located at the start of 25 km SMF transmission followed by 1.5 km optical wireless link to optimize the optical power inputted into the optical fiber with free space. Four optical carriers are transmitted through a combination of 25 km SMF transport and 1.5 km optical wireless link using triplet lenses. Triplet lenses, which consist of three lenses (convex, concave, and convex lenses), are used to facilitate the transmission of laser beams between the links. A pair of triplet lenses is used to emit laser beam from fiber ferrule output to free space and focus laser beam from free space to fiber ferrule input. Since the laser beam goes through 1.5 km free space, it is a big challenge to completely focus the laser beam into the fiber ferrule input. The main challenge lies in ensuring that the laser beam is efficiently focused through the 1.5 km optical wireless link. A triplet lens at the reception side serves as an effective optical component to narrow the laser beam diameter and focus it precisely into the fiber ferrule, especially over a challenging 1.5 km optical wireless link. Employing a 1.5-km optical wireless link to support 5 G signal transmission can substantially reduce the energy consumption associated with the deployment of 5 G base stations. This is primarily because of the potential reduction in the number of required 5 G base stations and associated power-hungry components, such as power amplifiers (PAs). Over a distance of 1.5 km optical wireless, the optical carriers are divided by an optical splitter, adapted by a variable optical attenuator, and detected by a 25-GHz high-speed PD, a 70-GHz ultra-fast PD, and a UTC-PD operating at 75–110 GHz frequencies, respectively. Unlike traditional PD uses both electrons and holes as active carriers, UTC-PDs utilize only electrons. The depletion region is designed to support only electron flow. Electrons have higher mobility than holes, meaning they can travel through the semiconductor material faster31. UTC-PD is therefore capable of detecting signals in the sub-THz range. This makes UTC-PD suitable for applications that require ultra-fast signal processing such as high-speed communication systems. At the outputs of high-speed PD, ultra-fast PD and UTC-PD, 10-Gb/s/20-GHz, 20-Gb/s/60-GHz and 40-Gb/s/100-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM electrical signals are produced through optical beating effect. These MMW/sub-THz electrical signals are separately boosted using three different PAs that operate over specific frequency ranges. PA-1 boosts signal within the 17–24 GHz frequency range, PA-2 boosts signal within the 50–75 GHz frequency range, and PA-3 boosts signal within the 75–110 GHz frequency range. The amplified signals are then transmitted wirelessly using horn antennas operating in K-band at 18–26.5 GHz, V-band at 50–75 GHz, and W-band at 75–110 GHz frequencies. The MMW/sub-THz wireless link is achieved by a set of PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) lenses operating in MMW/sub-THz frequencies32,33. After wireless link at distances of 12, 22, and 33 m, these MMW/sub-THz signals are down-converted into intermediate frequencies utilizing separate mixers with 10, 25, and 30 GHz electrical LOs. Down-conversion uses frequency multipliers with factors of 1, 2, and 3. After down-conversion, the center frequencies of these 16-QAM-OFDM signals are 10 GHz (20–10 × 1, 60–25 × 2, 100–30 × 3). The down-converted 16-QAM-OFDM signals with 10, 20, and 40 Gb/s data rates occupy 10 GHz bandwidth. Afterwards, the down-converted signals are boosted by three separate low noise amplifiers working at 10 MHz to 26.5 GHz frequencies.
