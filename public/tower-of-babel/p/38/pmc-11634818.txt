Dietary Anthocyanin Intake, Genetic Risk, and Incident Ulcerative Colitis: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Sishen Sun, Danshu Wang, Lintao Dan, Tian Fu, Jie Chen, Yao Zhang, Jing Sun, Duowu Zou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSummary evidence from animal experiments indicates that anthocyanin supplements can contribute to intestinal health. Nevertheless, no evidence has linked dietary anthocyanins to the prevention potential against inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in humans. We leveraged data from 188,044 IBD‐free individuals (mean age 59 years; 55.2% females) from the prospective cohort UK Biobank. The anthocyanin intake was estimated using dietary information from validated 24 h dietary recalls. Incident IBD was ascertained via national health‐related records. Genetic susceptibility to Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) was estimated by polygenic risk scores and further categorized into low‐ and high‐risk groups by median value. The Cox proportional regression model was applied to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). During the mean follow‐up of 9.7 years, we documented 255 CD and 606 UC. We found that compared with participants with the lowest quartiles of anthocyanin intake, those in the highest quartiles were associated with 24% (95% CI 6%–38%, p = 0.012; p‐trend = 0.003) and 35% (95% CI 16%–49%, p = 0.001; p‐trend < 0.001) reduced risk of IBD and UC, respectively. The inverse associations were stronger (p‐interaction = 0.022) among individuals with a high genetic risk of UC. We did not observe a significant association between anthocyanin intake and CD (p‐trend = 0.536). Higher dietary anthocyanin intake was associated with reduced risk of IBD and UC, but not CD. Genetic factors may modify the influence of dietary anthocyanin on UC susceptibility, and possible mechanisms need to be further elucidated in the future.Keywords: cohort study, flavonoid, genetic risk

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a lifelong gastrointestinal disease with increasing incidence and prevalence rates globally (Kaplan and Windsor 2021). IBD has two major subtypes, Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), both posing a significant burden on patients' quality of life and public health expenditure (Kaplan and Windsor 2021).

Diet plays a critical role in the etiology of IBD (Levine et al. 2020). Previous studies have found that fruit and vegetable intake is inversely associated with the incidence of IBD and have suggested that fruits and vegetables have the potential to prevent IBD (Levine et al. 2020). These encourage further exploration to elucidate which dietary constituents are responsible for this association. Anthocyanins are a sub‐class of the flavonoid which is widely present in fruits and vegetables. Summary evidence of animal experiments indicated that anthocyanin supplements can contribute to intestinal health and ameliorate colitis phenotypes by modulating the intestinal microbiota, improving the intestinal barrier, and reducing inflammation (Farzaei et al. 2018; Verediano et al. 2021; Zhou, Wang, and Yan 2023). Previous study demonstrated anthocyanin may play a therapeutic role in the development of IBD modulation of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), phosphoinositide‐3‐kinase regulatory subunit 1, and AKT Serine/Threonine Kinase 1 in the EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance pathway (Lin et al. 2021). However, despite the support of experimental and preclinical evidence, there is still a knowledge gap as to whether anthocyanin intake has the potential to prevent IBD in humans.

Additionally, the development of IBD is the result of a combination of genetic and environmental factors (Fisher et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2015). For example, anthocyanins have significant anti‐inflammatory effects by up‐regulating the expression of genes encoding anti‐inflammatory proteins (e.g., interleukin 10 [IL‐10], transforming growth factor‐β, etc.), thereby attenuating intestinal inflammatory responses (Verediano et al. 2021). This anti‐inflammatory effect of anthocyanins may be more pronounced in individuals carrying IBD susceptibility genes, as their immune system may be more likely to be activated, leading to a more severe inflammatory response (Fisher et al. 2008). Therefore, clarifying the effects of anthocyanin intake on different genetically susceptible populations will facilitate both precise prevention strategies and underlying mechanisms.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective cohort study to investigate the association between anthocyanin and incident IBD. We further examined the interaction between genetic susceptibility and anthocyanin in the associations.

Methods

Study Population

This study leveraged data from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort of 500,000 participants across the UK. Between 2006 and 2010, participants were recruited and underwent a series of information and biospecimen collection processes. More details were described elsewhere (Sudlow et al. 2015). The North West–Haydock Research Ethics Committee granted ethical approval to use the UK Biobank database (REC reference: 16/NW/0274). All participants signed an electronic consent at assessment centers.

In this study, we included individuals with available 24 h dietary recalls (n = 210,948) and excluded participants (1) reporting a nontypical diet or incredible energy intake (defined as < 800 or > 4200 kcal/day for males, < 600 or > 3500 kcal/days for females) (n = 19,883); (2) with dietary anthocyanins intake outside the normal distribution (n = 642) based on the Rosner's generalized extreme studentized deviate test (Rosner 1983); and (3) with a diagnosis of IBD before baseline (n = 2378). We included 188,045 individuals in the primary analysis (Figure 1). In the genetic‐related analyses, 155,709 participants were left, after the exclusion of 8329 participants who were not of Caucasian ethnicity and 24,007 without genetic information. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline.

Flowchart of the study.

Dietary Information Assessment

The dietary intake was collected by a web‐based 24 h recall questionnaire (WebQ) including 206 food and 32 beverage items that were commonly consumed in the UK. Participants were invited to complete the WebQ for five rounds from 2009 to 2012. The validity and reproducibility of the questionnaire have been described elsewhere (Beking and Vieira 2011).

Participants will report their daily consumption in the number of portions consumed from each food. We calculated the weight of each food item using portion size recorded in grams of each food from the publication by the Food Standards Agency (Crawley 1988). Then, we estimated the anthocyanin intake by multiplying food intake (in grams) and anthocyanin content in the corresponding food. The anthocyanin content was obtained from two national flavonoid databases, the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) database on flavonoids (Haytowitz, Wu, and Bhagwat 2018) and Phenol‐Explorer (Rothwell et al. 2013). These two databases were previously applied to estimate dietary intakes of flavonoids in the UK population (Beking and Vieira 2011; Ziauddeen et al. 2019). Additionally, a previous study in EPIC found a high correlation (r = 0.99) between energy intake calculated using the European nutrition databases and the USDA database (Iguacel et al. 2022). These previous studies suggest the food and nutrient content of these databases matches well with the diets of Europe, including the British diet.

In the current analysis, we estimated the anthocyanin intake using 36 food items (Table S1) including common sources high in anthocyanins like berries, grapes, and red wine. The mean anthocyanin intake was 52.4 mg/day in our study, which was comparable to the results from the UK National Diet and Nutrition Survey Rolling Programme (45.3 mg/day for 50–64‐year‐olds) (Ziauddeen et al. 2019).

Genetic Risk Profiling

Polygenic risk score (PRS) was utilized to estimate genetic susceptibility to CD and UC, and its construction process was described in detailing our previous study (Sun et al. 2023). Briefly, PRS of CD and UC were constructed based on 48 and 67 independent single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) strongly associated with CD and UC (p < 5 × 10−8, r 2 < 0.001) from a genome‐wide association study of up to 86,640 individuals of European ancestry (6968 UC and 5956 CD) (Liu et al. 2015). Participants were categorized into low and high genetic risk groups according to PRS score by median values.

Ascertainment of Outcome

The outcomes of interest were incident IBD, ascertained via inpatient data and primary care data. Self‐reported diagnosis of IBD in verbal interviews with trained nurses at the assessment center was included to exclude individuals with baseline IBD. The International Classification of Disease (ICD) code was used to identify IBD in inpatient data while read codes were used in primary care data. The accuracy of diagnostic codes and detailed diagnostic code sets to identify IBD has been described elsewhere (Yuan et al. 2023).

Process of Covariates

In the current study, we included self‐reported information including age, sex (female and male), race (White and non‐White), education level (college and below college), smoking status (never, previous or current), physical activity level (adequate and inadequate) from touchscreen questionnaires. The physical activity level was collected by the short form of the International Physical Activity Question and was categorized into adequate and inadequate according to the recommendation of the American Heart Association (Lloyd‐Jones et al. 2010). Body mass index was calculated using height and weight. The Townsend deprivation index was included as an indicator of material and socioeconomic deprivation. Regular use of medication (anti‐inflammatory drugs, proton pump inhibitors, and antibiotics) reported to be associated with IBD was obtained from oral interviews. Dietary variables were collected by 24 h WebQ, including total energy, protein, fat, carbohydrate, sugar, alcohol, vitamin D, vitamin E, vitamin B12, folate, zinc, and dietary fiber. The details of nutrient calculation were published elsewhere (Perez‐Cornago et al. 2021). A modified Alternative Healthy Eating Index (AHEI) was also calculated to represent overall diet quality. Details of the definition and process of the covariates are presented in Table S2. If covariate information was missing or recorded as “unknown,” we imputed the median values for continuous variables or applied the most frequently used category for categorical variables.

Statistical Analysis

In the primary analysis, we first plotted Kaplan–Meier curves to compare the cumulative risk of IBD, CD, and UC among individuals by anthocyanin intake in quartiles during the follow‐up. Cox model was applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with three multivariable‐adjusted models: Model 1 (minimally adjusted model) adjusted for age, sex, and total energy; Model 2 further adjusted for physical activity, TDI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, and AHEI based on the minimally adjusted model; Model 3 (fully adjusted model) further adjusted for protein, fat, carbohydrate, sugar, vitamin D, vitamin E, vitamin B12, folate, zinc, dietary fiber, fruit intake, and vegetable intake based on Model 2. Proportional hazard assumptions were confirmed using a weighted residual method (p > 0.39). To evaluate the effect of unmeasured residual confounding, we calculated E values based on the fully adjusted models (VanderWeele and Ding 2017).

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were applied to visualize the dose–response associations between anthocyanin and IBD. As there are currently no official recommendations for daily intake of anthocyanins in the UK, we relied on the recommendations (50 mg/day) of the Chinese Nutrition Society (2013). The daily intake of anthocyanins was classified as below and above the recommended intake (50 mg/day), which was similar to the mean intake (52.4 mg/day) of the study sample. We calculated the population attributable fractions (PAF) to estimate the proportion of incident IBD cases attributable (if a causal association existed) to anthocyanin intake below the recommended intake (Mansournia and Altman 2018).

To explore the genetic‐diet interaction, we calculated the multiplicative and additive interaction of the genetic risk group and dietary anthocyanin on the risk of CD and UC. Additive interactions were assessed by three indicators: (1) the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI); (2) attributable proportion due to interaction (AP); and (3) the synergy index (SI) (Knol and VanderWeele 2012). Joint associations of genetic risk and dietary anthocyanin were also conducted. Individuals were categorized into eight groups by the combination of genetic risk groups and quartiles of dietary anthocyanin.

In the secondary analysis, we reprocessed the anthocyanins using residual methods and energy density methods to calculate energy‐adjusted anthocyanins and anthocyanin intake per 1000 kcal energy intake (Willett, Howe, and Kushi 1997). We also calculated anthocyanin intake per 1 kg of body weight. These techniques can mitigate the effects of measurement error in data collected using self‐reported dietary assessment instruments and the impact of inter‐individual variation in body weight (Willett, Howe, and Kushi 1997). Additionally, we explored the benefit of six common types of anthocyanin in preventing IBD in both separate and mutually adjusted models. Subgroup analyses were conducted and multiplication interactions were tested in the population by age (< 60, ≥ 60), sex, smoking status, physical activity, and BMI (< 30, ≥ 30 kg/m2). Several sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) excluded incident IBD cases in the first 2‐, 4‐, and 6‐years of follow‐up; (2) limited the analysis to individuals with at least 2 rounds of WebQ; (3) identified incident IBD cases using at least two related medical records; (4) additionally adjusted for Charlson comorbidity index as an indicator of baseline comorbidity and objective health status (Mak et al. 2021); (5) additionally adjusted for C‐reactive protein; (6) additionally adjusted for IBD‐related medication (7) used propensity score matching as an alternative to the multivariable adjustment to eliminate selection bias. All statistical analyses were performed with R 4.1.1. Statistical tests were two‐sided with a significant level of p < 0.05.

Results

Primary Analysis

A total of 188,045 individuals free of IBD at baseline were included in this study (Table 1). The mean (SD) age of the population was 59.0 years old and 55.2% of the participants were female. The mean (SD) intake of anthocyanins was 52.4 mg/day. During the mean follow‐up of 9.7 years, we documented 255 CD and 606 UC. Kaplan–Meier curves showed that higher anthocyanin intake was associated with a lower risk of IBD and UC (Figure S1). In the fully adjusted model (Table 2), the HR (95% CI) comparing extreme quartiles intake of anthocyanin was 0.76 (95% CI 0.62–0.94, p = 0.012; p‐trend = 0.003) for IBD and 0.65 (95% CI 0.51–0.84, p = 0.001; p‐trend < 0.001) for UC. The E values comparing the extreme quartiles were 1.96 for IBD and 2.45 for UC. This implies that the observed associations could be explained by the presence of a substantial unmeasured confounder. We did not observe a significant association between anthocyanin intake and CD (p‐trend = 0.536).

We found that compared to individuals with anthocyanin intake below the recommended 50 mg/day, those with a higher intake have a 16% (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.71–0.98, p = 0.028) and 24% (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.63–0.93, p = 0.006) lower risk of incident IBD and UC, respectively (Table S3). The results of PAF (Table S3) showed that if the observed associations were causal, 11.5% (95% CI 1.6%–21.3%) and 17.0% (95% CI 5.5%–28.4%) of incident IBD and UC cases can be attributable to anthocyanin intake below recommended intake.

Interaction With Genetic Risk

We found that genetic susceptibility to UC significantly modified the associations between dietary anthocyanin and UC (Table 3, p‐interaction = 0.022). In particular, the associations between dietary anthocyanin and UC were stronger in individuals with high genetic risk than those with low genetic risk. Additive interaction demonstrated a significant synergistic effect of high genetic risk and low dietary anthocyanin on the risk of UC (Table S4, SI = 1.25). Joint associations showed that compared to individuals with low genetic risk and the highest quartile intake of dietary anthocyanin, individuals with high genetic risk and the lowest quartile intake of dietary anthocyanin were associated with 263% (95% 138%–455%, p < 0.001) increased risk of UC, respectively (Table S4).

Secondary Analysis

We observed consistent inverse associations of energy‐adjusted anthocyanin (p‐trend = 0.031), anthocyanin per 1000 kcal energy intake (p‐trend = 0.003), and anthocyanin per 1 kg body weight (p‐trend = 0.022) with IBD (Table S5). Similar associations were observed when treating incident UC as the outcome. When exploring the individual effect of different anthocyanins on the risk of IBD (Tables S6 and S7), 5 out of 6 types were inversely related to the risk of IBD and UC (all p‐trend < 0.05) except for dietary pelargonidin intake. However, we did not observe significant associations of any type of anthocyanin with IBD and UC risk after mutually adjusting them in the fully adjusted model.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analysis

In subgroup analyses, we did not observe statistical differences in the associations of anthocyanin with IBD, CD, and UC stratified by age, sex, smoking status, BMI, and, physical activity level (all p‐interaction > 0.05, Tables S8–S10). In the sensitivity analysis (Table S11), the associations were consistent with the primary analysis after excluding incident IBD in the first 2‐, 4‐, or 6‐years of follow‐up, restricting to individuals with at least 2 rounds of 24 h recalls, further adjusting for CCI/IBD‐related medication/CRP, identifying individuals with IBD using at least two medical records only, or restricting to participants with balanced covariates using the propensity score matching method (Table S12).

Discussion

In the current study, we found that anthocyanins were inversely associated with reduced risk of IBD and UC, but not CD. Estimation using PAF indicated that assuming causality, 11.5% and 17.0% of IBD and UC morbidity could be statistically avoided by increasing anthocyanin intake in populations below 50 mg/day. Genetic susceptibility to UC can modify the associations of dietary anthocyanin with UC. We found that inverse associations were only significant among individuals with high genetic risk of UC and detected a synergistic effect of genetic risk and dietary anthocyanin on the development of UC.

Previous evidence from laboratory work has revealed a biological rationale for our findings. Anthocyanin may prevent UC through a number of pathways that are largely based on their anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and gut microbiota‐modulating functions. (1) First, anthocyanins are able to inhibit the production and activity of a variety of cellular inflammatory factors. It is reported that administration of anthocyanin can reduce serum levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines including IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α (Farzaei et al. 2018; Zhou, Wang, and Yan 2023). In addition, anthocyanins can reduce the occurrence of intestinal immune reactions and inflammatory responses by up‐regulating the tight junction proteins between intestinal epithelial cells, enhancing intestinal barrier function, and preventing harmful substances such as bacteria and toxins from penetrating the intestinal wall and entering the body (Verediano et al. 2021). (2) Second, anthocyanin is a potent antioxidant that effectively scavenges the body's metabolic production of harmful free radicals such as hydroxyl radicals, DPPH radicals, and OH radicals (Zhou, Wang, and Yan 2023). These free radicals can damage intestinal cells, leading to intestinal inflammation and damage. By scavenging these free radicals, anthocyanins can protect intestinal cells from damage caused by oxidative stress (Zhou, Wang, and Yan 2023). In vitro experiments have also shown that treatment of anthocyanin increased total antioxidant capacity by inducing the expression of HO‐1 and NQO‐1 and increasing the level of reduced glutathione (Farzaei et al. 2018). (3) Third, anthocyanins may modulate the gut microbiota by promoting the colonization and growth of beneficial bacteria and improving the overall environment of the intestinal tract, thereby alleviating dysbiosis, enhancing intestinal barrier function, and reducing the occurrence of intestinal inflammation (Verediano et al. 2021). A previous study found that the supplementation of dietary blueberries and broccoli that are rich in anthocyanin can significantly reduce IBD‐related gut microbiota such as Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, and Escherichia coli and increase organic acids, such as butyric and propionic acids in the intestinal tract (Paturi et al. 2012). Our study updates the current evidence on the effect of anthocyanins on the development of IBD.

To our knowledge, there was no previous study directly linked to the anthocyanin intake to incident IBD in humans. Two pilot trials investigating the therapeutic role of anthocyanins for UC patients provided indirect evidence to support our findings. One randomized, placebo‐controlled, clinical trial study of consecutive weeks of oral administration of aqueous pomegranate peel extract to 29 patients with UC‐reduced fecal incontinence and the need for antidiarrheal medications (Kamali et al. 2015). The other trials based on an open‐label design found oral supplementation of bilberry extract to 11 patients with mild‐to‐moderate UC can reduce serum levels of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines (Roth et al. 2016). In line with the protective effect of anthocyanin‐rich food extracts in these pilot studies, our study indicated the protective effects of anthocyanin intake on the development of UC in a large cohort of more than 180,000 individuals. In addition, we found that 11.5% and 17.0% of IBD and UC incidence can be avoided (assuming the associations were causal) by increasing the anthocyanin intake above 50 mg/day, equivalent to the content in 30 g blueberries (Vaccinium spp.) (Haytowitz, Wu, and Bhagwat 2018). This may be informative for public health strategy development and clinical decision‐making.

We did not find a significant protective effect against UC for any of the specific anthocyanins in the mutually adjusted model. This may suggest that the protective effect of anthocyanins for UC development is caused by generalized properties such as antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory as mentioned above. Future studies need to further clarify whether different types of anthocyanin supplements have similar effects on the prevention and treatment of UC patients.

Our study also found that the inverse association between dietary anthocyanin and UC was significant in individuals with high genetic risk but disappeared in those with low genetic risk. Results from the calculation of multiplicative and additive interaction and joint association analysis support the critical role genetic susceptibility played in the anthocyanin‐UC associations. Anthocyanins can inhibit the NF‐κB pathway to reduce high levels of inflammation. Meanwhile, previous genome‐wide association analyses revealed ECM1 as the strongest risk loci for UC (Fisher et al. 2008), which can strongly activate NF‐κB (Thompson and Lees 2011). For possible protective effects due to the antioxidant properties of anthocyanins, populations with different genetic predispositions may also benefit differently. A Portuguese study including 436 CD 367 UC, and 434 healthy controls found that polymorphisms in endogenous antioxidant enzymes were associated with higher UC risk (Costa Pereira et al. 2017). This evidence rationalizes the role of genetic risk in influencing the effects of anthocyanins on the development of UC and supports the assumption that for people with a genetic predisposition to UC, their anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant functions are impaired, so anthocyanins are more effective for them. Our results imply that genetic risk can serve as a convenient classification strategy to identify individuals who may benefit from increasing dietary anthocyanin for preventive purposes.

There are some limitations to our study. First, because our cohort study was observational, potential confounders and reverse causality prevented us from proving the causality of findings. However, we obtained consistent results in sensitivity analyses, demonstrating that our conclusions are unlikely to be heavily influenced by the aforementioned biases. In terms of reverse causality, lagged analyses that excluded IBD cases at the initial 2‐, 4‐, and 6‐year follow‐ups suggest that delays in IBD diagnosis are unlikely to influence the main conclusions. For potential confounders, we used multivariate adjustment and propensity scores to reduce bias and obtain consistent results. In addition, we calculated E values to estimate unmeasured confounders. Compared with the extreme quartiles, the E‐value for UC was 2.45. The current meta‐analysis of environmental risk factors for UC did not identify risk factors associated with both dietary anthocyanin intake and UC (2.45 times the risk ratio) (Piovani et al. 2019). Other limitations included the fact that although we used a validated 24 h WebQ, approximately 38% of the study sample had data from only 1 questionnaire. We excluded extreme energy intake and atypical diets to minimize bias in self‐reported dietary information, as recommended by previous UK Biobank studies (Chen et al. 2022). Finally, the vast majority of participants in our study were older than 40 years and white, so caution is warranted in generalizing our findings to other IBD populations.

Conclusions

Our study found that anthocyanin intake was inversely associated with the risk of IBD and UC, but not with the risk of CD. We found that genetic susceptibility to UC significantly affects the effect of anthocyanins on the development of UC. Individuals with a high genetic risk for UC are more likely to benefit from increased dietary anthocyanins. Our study requires validation in further interventional studies and provides a reference for clinical trials on anthocyanin dosages and suitable populations.
