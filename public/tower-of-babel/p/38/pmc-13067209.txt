Factors Associated With Concurrent Benzodiazepine and Opioid Use Following Total Hip and Knee Arthroplasty: A Nationwide Cohort Study
Authors: Manuela Yepes‐Calderón, Rob G. H. H. Nelissen, Marcel L. Bouvy, Liza N. van Steenbergen, Albert Dahan, Frits R. Rosendaal, Maaike G. J. Gademan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeConcurrent use of benzodiazepines and opioids is discouraged due to synergistic adverse effects. However, patients undergoing total hip or knee arthroplasty (THA/TKA) often receive them, particularly in the first 3 postoperative months. We identified factors associated with new outpatient concurrent benzodiazepine‐opioid dispensation following THA/TKA.MethodsIn this nationwide cohort study, we linked the Dutch Arthroplasty Register with the Dutch Foundation for Pharmaceutical Statistics, which provided medication dispensation data. We included all patients undergoing primary elective THA/TKA (2013–2022) who had no preoperative concurrent use in the 6 months pre‐procedure. The primary outcome was ≥ 7 days of a new concurrent benzodiazepine‐opioid dispensation within 90‐day postoperative. Determinants included patient and implant characteristics, and preoperative medication use. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed, adjusted for age, sex, and comorbidity.ResultsAmong 89 139 THA and 76 710 TKA patients, 3756 (4%) and 5571 (7%), respectively, received new postoperative concurrent benzodiazepine‐opioid dispensation within 90‐days postoperative. The main factor associated with such dispensation was preoperative benzodiazepine use (THA: OR 23.5 [95% CI: 21.8–25.3], TKA: OR 22.8 [95% CI: 21.3–24.3]), followed by preoperative antidepressant/anxiolytic use (THA: OR 2.9 [95% CI: 2.6–3.1], TKA: OR 2.5 [95% CI: 2.3–2.7]). Other factors included female sex, current smoking, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) scale III‐IV. Preoperative pain scores, preoperative opioid use, and implant characteristics showed little to no association with the outcome.ConclusionsPreoperative benzodiazepine use was the main factor associated with new outpatient concurrent benzodiazepine‐opioid dispensation after THA/TKA, followed by preoperative antidepressant/anxiolytic use. These results highlighted that careful review of the patient's medication history when planning postoperative pain management could help prevent unsafe co‐prescription.

Introduction

Opioid use is pervasive in orthopaedic surgery, with approximately 75%–90% of patients receiving at least one opioid prescription after hip or knee arthroplasty [1, 2, 3, 4]. Simultaneously, there is growing concern about the rising use of benzodiazepines in the general population [5, 6, 7], especially among elderly women [8, 9], who represent a large proportion of patients undergoing arthroplasty [10].

In 2017, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) released a black box warning against the combination of benzodiazepines and opioids due to multiple reports of increased risk of serious adverse effects such as respiratory depression [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. These concerns have also been echoed by the European Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction (EMCDDA) [16]. In patients who undergo arthroplasty, this drug combination has been associated with increased risk of perioperative falls, subsequent fractures, and revision surgeries [17, 18]. Despite these risks, recent data show that 6% of elective hip arthroplasty and 9% of elective knee arthroplasty received concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid dispensations within the first postoperative year, especially during the first three postoperative months. Orthopaedic surgeons were often the initial prescribers of these medications during the early postoperative phase [1].

Identifying patients at higher risk for concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid use is essential for guiding risk management strategies to reduce unsafe prescribing. Prior studies from the USA and Asia have identified some characteristics such as older age, female sex, and insurance type as potential risk factors [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. However, evidence from other populations, particularly regarding postoperative orthopaedic care, remains limited. This is critical because prescription practices, drug regulation, and public attitudes toward medical and non‐medical use vary across regions [24, 25], and medication use patterns in the arthroplasty population often differ from that in the general population [4].

The aim of this study was to assess which patient, implant, and preoperative medication characteristics are associated with increased odds of outpatient concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid dispensation following primary total hip and knee arthroplasty (THA/TKA) for osteoarthritis. To address this, we used data from two national databases: the Dutch Arthroplasty Register (LROI) and the Dutch Foundation for Pharmaceutical Statistics (SFK) [26, 27].

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Linkage

In this nationwide cohort study, we used data from the LROI and the SKF. The LROI covers all hospitals performing arthroplasties in the Netherlands, with a level of completeness above 98% for primary arthroplasty [26]. LROI registers procedure‐related information and patient demographics at the time of the procedure.

The SFK contains pharmacy dispensation data from > 95% of the outpatient community pharmacies in the Netherlands [27]. The SFK provided pharmacy dispensation data for each patient who underwent arthroplasty from the year before and two years after the procedure, including the Anatomic‐Therapeutic‐Chemical (ATC) code, the dispensation date (in relation to the day of surgery), and the number of medication units dispensed.

A deterministic linkage between the LROI and the SFK was performed based on a combination of the patient's day of birth, sex, and 4‐digit postal code. Approval by a medical research ethics committee was waived by the research review board (non‐WMO‐committee of division 1 of the Leiden University Medical Center; reference number: 2023–050).

Study Population

All patients who underwent primary THA and TKA procedures performed for osteoarthritis between 2013 and 2022 were eligible for inclusion. We included only index procedures, meaning the first eligible arthroplasty during the study period for each patient. Exclusion criteria were: patients < 18 years at the moment of procedure, and patients who could not be uniquely linked to pharmaceutical data. Additionally, linked patients without prescriptions consistent with thromboprophylaxis [28]—defined as at least one prescription of low molecular weight heparin, direct oral anticoagulants, or warfarin between 30 days before and 35 days after the procedure—were excluded due to potential linkage unreliability. Patients who were prevalent concurrent users, defined as having more than one concurrent opioid and benzodiazepine dispensation in the six months prior to the procedure, were also excluded.

Concurrent Opioid and Benzodiazepine Use

Outpatient benzodiazepine and opioid dispensations were identified using ATC codes: N05BA, N05CD, and N05CF for benzodiazepines, and N02A for opioids. We excluded N03AE (Clonazepam) and N07BC (drugs used in opioid dependence) because they mainly reflect treatment for specific conditions (epilepsy and opioid dependence) rather than typical medications used in outpatient postoperative care. The duration of exposure for each dispensation was estimated using the Individualized Dispensing Patterns method [29]. This method considers the number of units dispensed and the interval between consecutive dispensations of the same medication for each patient, accounting for individual medication use patterns and pro re nata prescriptions [30].

Concurrent use was defined as at least 7 consecutive days of overlap between a benzodiazepine and an opioid dispensation in the first 90 days after the procedure (Figure 1). This definition was chosen because a 7‐day overlap has been used in previous studies [31, 32], and represents a pragmatic balance between other reported thresholds, such as 1 day [33], which may capture brief incidental overlaps, and longer thresholds (e.g., 30 days or more [34]), which could miss clinically meaningful concurrent exposure in the postoperative setting. Finally, it reflects durations of concomitant opioid exposure that have previously been associated with adverse clinical outcomes [35].

Study design. Preoperative medication use was evaluated during the six months before surgery. Patient and procedure characteristics were collected on the date of operation. Follow‐up was performed through postoperative day 90. During this period, concurrent use was defined as an overlap of benzodiazepine and opioid use for at least seven days. BDZ, benzodiazepines; PROMs, Patient reported outcome measurements.

Determinants of Interest

We evaluated patient, implant, and medication characteristics as potential determinants of the outcome of interest.

Patient characteristics were extracted at the moment of procedure and included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), current smoking status, Charnley score, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification and socioeconomic status (SES, Figure 1). SES was measured with the standardized neighborhood SES scores from Statistics Netherlands, which are based on average income, education levels, and labor participation [36]. Additionally, we evaluated preoperative patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the EQ‐5D visual analogue scale (EQ‐5D VAS), numeric rating scales (NRS) for pain at rest and during activity, and the Hip and Knee disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Scores (HOOS‐PS and KOOS‐PS). According to LROI protocols, PROMs may be completed at any time within the 180 days prior to arthroplasty; PROMs recorded more than 180 days before arthroplasty were excluded.

Implant characteristics included the year of procedure (≤ 2017, > 2017), the presence of prior surgeries aside from arthroplasty on the same joint, and the type of prosthesis fixation.

Medication characteristics included specific preoperative medication use as well as the weighted Rx‐Risk score. Specific preoperative medication use was assessed as having > 1 dispensation within six months prior to surgery, and included opioids, benzodiazepines, and antidepressants/anxiolytics (ATC codes N06AA*, N06AB*, N06AF*, N06AX*, Figure 1). The weighted Rx‐Risk score is an aggregate measure of comorbidity burden based on the outpatient prescriptions received in the previous year. This score evaluates 46 different comorbidities and assigns weights to them (from −1 to 6) based on their impact in mortality risk [37]. The score ranges from −7 to 67. A person with no comorbidities will have a score of 0, and a person with all comorbidities a score of 60, as some comorbidities are scored as protective the score can drop below 0. We modified the score by excluding the categories “anxiety,” “pain” and “depression,” as these medications (benzodiazepines, opioids and antidepressants/anxiolytics) were evaluated individually. We did not modify the original one‐year lookback period, as the Rx‐Risk score was developed and validated using this time window, and used it to capture underlying comorbidity burden, which generally reflects chronic medication use and is less sensitive to short‐term changes.

Data Analysis

Data analyses and graphs were performed with R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Given their expected different clinical behavior [38], analyses were stratified by THA and TKA. For descriptive statistics, continuous variables are displayed as mean with standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as n (%).

Missing data were assumed to be missing at random and multiple imputation by chained equations was performed. All candidate determinants and the outcome of interest were used in the imputation model. Ten data sets were generated and the results were pooled according to Rubin's rules [39].

To evaluate the relationship between potential determinants and the odds of receiving a concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid dispensation in the first 90 days post‐surgery, we first calculated descriptive statistics for the determinant variables, stratified by whether patients experienced the outcome of interest. Logistic regression analyses were then performed to assess the associations between each determinant and the outcome of interest, adjusting for age, sex, and the modified Rx‐Risk score. Confidence intervals were derived from the model.

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the multivariable logistic regression with (1) adjustment for the year of the procedure to evaluate the influence of potential temporal trends in medication use, (2) extending the definition of concurrent use to 30 days to reduce the effect of short postoperative exposures, and (3) using only complete case data to examine the impact of missing data on our results.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

The LROI provided data on 470 495 elective THA and TKA, of which 89 139 THA and 76 710 TKA met the inclusion criteria and were successfully linked (Figure 2). Patient and procedure characteristics of the linked and unlinked populations were similar, except for sex, with a higher proportion of men in the linked group (Table S1). A detailed quality control of this linkage is available elsewhere [31]. Here, we externally validated the linkage by comparing postoperative opioid dispensing in the linked population with that of the entire arthroplasty population. The prevalence was nearly identical. Missing data ranged from less than 1% for the ASA‐score to 50%–60% for PROMs (Table S2). The patients with missing data about PROMs and patients with complete data were quite comparable, supporting the missing at random assumption (Table S3).

In THA, preoperative benzodiazepine use was the factor most strongly associated with receiving a postoperative concurrent dispensation (OR 23.5, 95% CI 21.8–25.3). Other factors included female sex (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.7–1.9), current smoking (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.5–1.8), ASA classification (OR 2.2, 95% CI 1.9–2.5 III‐IV vs. I) and preoperative antidepressant/anxiolytic use (OR 2.9, 95% CI 2.6–3.1). Higher socioeconomic status was associated with lower odds (OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.6–0.8 per 1 SD increase), as well as an uncemented fixation type (OR 0.8, 95% CI 0.7–0.8) when compared to cemented THA and procedures performed after 2017 (OR 0.9, 95% CI 0.8–0.9) compared to 2013–2017. Preoperative PROMs and the modified Rx‐Risk score showed generally weak associations with the odds of receiving concurrent dispensations, with ORs ranging between 1.0 and 1.2. Age, BMI, Charnley classification, previous procedures in the intervened joint and preoperative opioid use showed no association with the outcome (Table 2).

Similar patterns were observed in patients who underwent TKA. Preoperative benzodiazepine use remained the factor with the strongest association with receiving a postoperative concurrent dispensation (OR 22.8, 95% CI 21.3–24.3). Other factors included female sex (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.5–1.7), current smoking (OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.3–1.5), ASA classification (OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.5 III‐IV vs. I) and prior antidepressant/anxiolytic use (OR 2.5, 95% CI 2.3–2.7). Uncemented prostheses were again associated with lower odds of concurrent use when compared to cemented (OR 0.8, 95% CI 0.7–0.9), as well as procedures performed after 2017 (OR 0.9, 95% CI 0.8–0.9) compared to 2013–2017. Also here, PROMs showed generally weak associations with the odds of receiving concurrent dispensations; as well as BMI, previous procedures in the intervened joint and the modified Rx‐Risk score (OR between 0.9 and 1.1). Age, Charnley classification, SES and preoperative opioid use showed no association with the outcome (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses adjusting for year of procedure, prolonging the definition of concurrent use to > 30 days, and using complete‐case data yielded similar results (Tables S4–S6).

Discussion

In this nationwide study, we found that 4% of patients who underwent THA and 7% of those who underwent TKA received a new outpatient concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid dispensation during the first 3 postoperative months, despite the well‐documented risks of this drug combination [12, 13, 14, 15]. Patients who received preoperative benzodiazepine use had a more than 20‐fold higher odds of concurrent postoperative dispensations than non‐users, and those with previous antidepressant/anxiolytic use had 2‐fold higher odds. Additional factors included female sex, current smoking and ASA III‐IV vs. I. Uncemented prostheses (compared to cemented fixation) and procedures performed after 2017 were associated with lower odds of concurrent use. Preoperative PROMs, including pain scores, and preoperative opioid use showed little to no association with the outcome.

Most studies on factors associated with concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid use in the general population have primarily focused on chronic opioid users as they were considered to be the population at the highest risk [13, 40]. However, we found that among patients who undergo arthroplasty, preoperative benzodiazepine use plays a more significant role. Even more, we did not find that preoperative opioid use was a factor associated with postoperative concurrent use. This difference may reflect the widespread adoption of opioids as postoperative analgesia [3, 4], which increases the likelihood of concurrent exposure among benzodiazepine users.

The magnitude of this association between preoperative benzodiazepine use and postoperative concurrent use needs to be interpreted with caution, as it may partly stem from the fact that preoperative benzodiazepine use can serve as a marker of ongoing benzodiazepine treatment, which may overlap with postoperative opioid dispensing more easily than the simultaneous initiation of both medications after surgery in preoperative benzodiazepine non‐users. However, sensitivity analyses using a more stringent outcome definition that requires 30 days of overlap, intended to minimize the impact of short‐term continuation, produced similar results. Additionally, odds ratios from logistic regression can overestimate the corresponding risk ratios [41].

We also found that patients with preoperative antidepressant/anxiolytics use were at increased odds of postoperative concurrent dispensations. Previous studies in the general population have similarly reported high rates of concurrent use among patients with anxiety disorders [19, 22]. Part of this association may be explained by the fact that antidepressant/anxiolytic users are also more likely to use benzodiazepines as part of the comprehensive treatment of these conditions. Additionally, patients with anxiety and depressive disorders are more likely to experience postoperative insomnia [42] and report higher pain scores after arthroplasty [43], which may increase their likelihood of receiving both benzodiazepines and opioids postoperatively.

Other factors identified in our study, such as female sex [22], smoking [19], and poorer general health status [19] align with findings from general population studies. We also found that patients who received uncemented protheses had a lower concomitant opioid and benzodiazepine use than cemented procedures. Given that patients who receive cemented as well as uncemented prostheses have similar post‐operative pain scores and opioid utilization [44], this finding probably relates to the general recommendation of using cemented prothesis in patients who are frail in order to prevent a periprosthetic fracture in these often patients who have osteoporosis [45]. Furthermore, frailty in patients is associated with benzodiazepine use [46], and since neither osteoporosis nor frailty data were available, we could not correct for these in our analysis. In addition, procedures performed after 2017 were less likely to be followed by postoperative concurrent use, which may reflect temporal changes in prescribing practices, potentially influenced by increasingly prominent safety warnings during this period [11, 16]. However, sensitivity analyses showed that accounting for this temporal trend did not change the factors associated with postoperative concurrent use.

Finally, preoperative PROMs, including high pain scores, were associated with the odds of concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid use, but the associations were relatively weak when compared with other potential determinants. This finding is in disagreement with previous evidence which showed that reported pain and the presence of pain disorders are strong determinants of concurrent use among the general population [19, 21]. However, because preoperative pain is almost universal in patients undergoing arthroplasty for osteoarthritis and pain management is a cornerstone of postoperative care, it is understandable that preoperative pain has less influence on concurrent use in this setting, particularly given the widespread use of opioids for postoperative analgesia. In addition, prior work suggests that the observed increase in postoperative opioid use over time is at least partly independent of changes in pain severity, as average pre‐ and postoperative reported pain scores remained broadly stable during the study period [47, 48, 49] and may instead reflect changes in care pathways and prescribing practices, such as the increasing use of fast‐track protocols with earlier discharge [50].

The factors identified in this study may help identify patients at increased likelihood of receiving a postoperative concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid dispensation. This is clinically important because concurrent use of benzodiazepines and opioids has been associated with serious adverse events, including falls, respiratory depression, and overdose [12, 15, 35]. Prescribers may represent an important target for intervention. In our previous work, we found that most concurrent dispensations were initiated by general practitioners, although orthopedic surgeons contributed to approximately 30% of concurrent dispensations in the early postoperative months [31]. Other studies have similarly reported relatively high rates of concurrent prescribing among orthopedic surgeons [51]. Together, these findings suggest that general practitioners are a key point for intervention to reduce concurrent use, while also highlighting the importance of coordination between primary care and surgical teams in the early postoperative period.

A key strength of this study is the use of nationwide data sources, which allowed the inclusion of a diverse array of patient and procedural characteristics. However, several limitations should be noted. First, although external validation showed that the linked population was representative of the source population regarding medication use, the linked arthroplasties had an underrepresentation of women. Second, we were unable to account for inpatient medication use, which may also contribute to concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid exposure. However, since the average length of stay in the Netherlands after THA and TKA is 2 days [52], patients who had intrahospital exposure would not meet our definition of concurrent use (≥ 7 days), and most safety concerns regarding concurrent use have been raised in the outpatient setting [12, 15, 35]. Next, Postoperative PROMs and diagnostic information on pain disorders were not available in the linked LROI–SFK dataset. Therefore, we could not determine whether postoperative pain scores, unlike preoperative scores, are associated with the concurrent use of benzodiazepines and opioids after surgery. Also, our exposure and outcome measures relied on outpatient pharmacy dispensations rather than confirmed medication intake. While dispensation data serve as a stronger proxy than prescriptions since patients need to pick up the medication, there can still be misclassification and overestimation of concurrent use, for example, due to dispensed opioids not being taken if not needed [53]. Additionally, while this study identified factors associated with concurrent use, its observational design prevents definitive conclusions about causal relationships.

In conclusion, new concurrent benzodiazepine and opioid use was common in the early postoperative period following THA and TKA. Preoperative benzodiazepine use was the strongest factor associated with receiving this medication combination, followed by previous antidepressant/anxiolytic use. Preoperative opioid use, usually considered the main risk factor among the general population, showed among these patients no association with concurrent use. These results emphasize the importance of thoroughly evaluating preoperative medication history when planning postoperative analgesia, to support adherence to safety guidelines, assess the indications supporting continuous benzodiazepine use, and assess whether opioids represent a safe postoperative analgesic option.
