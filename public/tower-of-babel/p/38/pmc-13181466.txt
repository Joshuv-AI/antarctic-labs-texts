Cost-effectiveness and evidence gaps of advanced technologies in lumbar spine surgery: a scoping review
Authors: Andrea Fabregas, Gonzalo F. Del Rio Montesinos, Andrea Vazquez, Nelson I. Molina Lara, David Vázquez Rodríguez, Lancelot Benn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobotic guidance, augmented reality (AR), and emerging artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are increasingly integrated into lumbar spine surgery. While improvements in technical accuracy and perioperative metrics have been widely reported, the economic value and feasibility of adopting these technologies remain uncertain. This scoping review aimed to map and synthesize evidence on cost-effectiveness, economic evaluation approaches, and adoption-related gaps associated with robotic-, AR-, and AI-assisted lumbar spine surgery.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Scopus, PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched from inception through May 2025 for human studies evaluating robotic, AR, or AI technologies in lumbar spine surgery that reported clinical or economic outcomes. Data on study design, technology type, clinical outcomes, complications, and cost-related measures were extracted and synthesized narratively, with emphasis on economic outcomes and implementation considerations.ResultsFourteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Robotic-assisted surgery consistently demonstrated higher pedicle screw accuracy and modest reductions in blood loss and hospital length of stay (LOS) compared with freehand or navigated techniques, although operative time was frequently increased. Economic findings were heterogeneous: modeling studies and select institutional analyses suggested potential cost savings at high surgical volumes, whereas large administrative database studies reported higher hospital charges and increased transfusion rates in robotic cases. AR studies were limited to small case series and narrative reviews, reporting favorable perioperative metrics without formal economic evaluation. AI applications were confined to prognostic modeling, with no studies reporting patient-level clinical or cost outcomes.ConclusionsEvidence supporting the cost-effectiveness of advanced technologies in lumbar spine surgery remains inconsistent and highly context dependent. While robotic systems offer technical advantages, their economic value varies by institutional factors such as surgical volume and efficiency. Standardized economic evaluations and implementation-focused studies are needed to guide value-based adoption of robotic, AR, and AI technologies.

Key findings

• This scoping review found that robotic assisted lumbar spine surgery consistently improves pedicle screw accuracy and modestly reduces blood loss and hospital length of stay. However, reported cost-effectiveness is highly variable and dependent on institutional factors. Augmented reality (AR) and artificial intelligence (AI) technologies lack robust economic evaluations and long-term outcome data, limiting informed adoption.

What is known and what is new?

• Advanced technologies such as robotics, AR, and AI have been shown to improve technical accuracy and perioperative metrics in lumbar spine surgery; however, existing evidence largely focuses on short-term outcomes and provides limited insight into cost-effectiveness or implementation feasibility.

• This manuscript is the first scoping review to systematically map cost-effectiveness evidence and economic evaluation approaches for robotic, AR, and AI technologies in lumbar spine surgery, identifying substantial heterogeneity in reported costs and critical gaps that limit informed adoption.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Adoption of advanced lumbar spine technologies should be guided by institution specific cost-benefit analyses rather than technical performance alone and future research should prioritize standardized economic evaluations, patient reported outcomes and implementation metrics to support value-based decision making.

Introduction

Technological innovation has become a defining feature of modern spine surgery, particularly in the lumbar spine, where precision and safety are critical (1,2). Robotic platforms, augmented reality (AR) systems, and emerging artificial intelligence (AI) tools are increasingly incorporated into surgical workflows with the promise of improving accuracy, reducing radiation exposure, and enhancing intraoperative efficiency (3,4). These technologies build on advances in imaging and navigation, offering surgeons improved visualization and control during pedicle screw placement and lumbar fusion protocols (5,6).

Despite its rapid growth, substantial uncertainty remains regarding the value proposition of these technologies (1). Much of the literature has focused on short-term metrics such as pedicle screw accuracy, operative time, and blood loss, while fewer studies have examined patient-reported outcomes, complication rates, or standardized economic endpoints beyond the early postoperative period (4). In parallel, substantial capital, maintenance, and training costs associated with robotic, AR, and AI platforms underscore the need to understand their economic impact, particularly in resource-limited settings (7). Whether perioperative efficiencies such as reductions in revision rates, length of stay (LOS), or operative inefficiencies translate into sustained cost savings or cost-effectiveness across different institutional contexts remains unclear (8-10).

Robotic arm-guided systems provide pre-planned trajectories and rigid guidance for pedicle screw insertion, while navigation platforms overlay instrument position in three-dimensional space. AR superimposes anatomical information directly within the surgeon’s visual field, and AI holds promise for predictive analytics and workflow optimization (9,10). These technologies aim to enhance accuracy, reduce radiation exposure, and improve operative efficiency. Nevertheless, economic evaluation methodologies and adoption-related considerations for robotic-, AR-, and AI-assisted lumbar spine surgery remain insufficiently characterized (11). This review aims to identify how economic outcomes are currently assessed, where findings are conflicting, and which evidence gaps limit informed, value-based decision-making. A scoping review methodology was selected due to the heterogeneity of technologies, study designs, and outcome reporting, as well as the need to systematically map areas of evidence saturation and deficiency. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2025-1-255/rc).

Methods

Study design and search strategy

A comprehensive search of Scopus, PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science was performed through May 2025. The search strategy incorporated both medical subject headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords combined with Boolean operators related to lumbar spine surgery, robotic-assisted surgery, AR, AI, cost-effectiveness, economic evaluation, and clinical outcomes. Search results were exported to an Excel spreadsheet for data organization, and duplicate records were removed. In addition, reference lists of relevant articles and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. The complete electronic search strategy for PubMed is provided in Appendix 1. A review protocol was developed before the literature search; however, the protocol was not registered in a public repository.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they examined the use of robotic guidance, AR, or AI in lumbar spine surgery and reported either economic outcomes or clinical outcomes relevant to value assessment. Articles were limited to human subjects and published in English. Eligible designs included randomized and non-randomized comparative studies, prospective and retrospective cohorts, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and large database analyses. Exclusion criteria included case reports or series with less than 10 patients, non-clinical studies, cervical or thoracic only procedures, and publications lacking clinical or economic outcome data relevant to value assessment. These criteria were selected to ensure inclusion of studies capable of informing economic value, implementation considerations, or sustained clinical reporting.

Data extraction

Four reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. For each study, data extracted consisted of author, year of publication, study design, sample size, duration of follow-up, reported clinical outcomes, complications, and cost-related or economic measures. Data extraction was performed independently by four reviewers, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, outcome measures, follow-up durations, and economic methodologies, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. Clinical outcomes, economic evaluation approaches, and technology-specific insights were summarized to provide a structured mapping of the available evidence. Table 1 summarizes study characteristics, clinical outcomes, and cost-related findings. Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence was not performed, as this study was conducted as a scoping review in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidance.

Results

The literature search identified 1,162 records, of which 14 studies met the inclusion criteria after screening and full-text review. A PRISMA 2020 flow diagram summarizing the study selection progress is presented in Figure 1. Included study designs comprised of systematic or network meta-analyses (n=6), narrative or scoping reviews (n=3), large administrative database analyses (n=2), retrospective cohort studies (n=2), and randomized controlled trials (n=1). Robotic-assisted technologies were evaluated in 10 studies, AR in four studies, and AI in three studies, but were limited to prognostic modeling without patient-level clinical or economic outcomes. Reported follow-up varied widely, with most studies emphasizing perioperative or short-term outcomes and relatively few providing extended postoperative outcome reporting.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection.

Robotic guidance consistently improved pedicle screw accuracy compared with fluoroscopy-based techniques. In a prospective randomized controlled trial, Han et al. reported outcomes for 234 patients involving 1,116 pedicle screws and found that the robotic group achieved a higher rate of clinically acceptable screw placement than the fluoroscopy group (98.7% vs. 93.5%) (12). A retrospective case-control comparing robot-guided and freehand minimally invasive surgery with a transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) demonstrated a lower pedicle breach rate (0.27% vs. 1.75%, P=0.04) and less blood loss (313.7 vs. 431.6 mL, P=0.019) (13). Comparative analyses of percutaneous pedicle screw fixation for thoracolumbar fractures showed that robot-assisted navigation reduced operative time (89 vs. 117 min, P=0.003), blood loss (66 vs. 138 mL, P=0.001), and fluoroscopy exposures (9.24 vs. 18.48, P=0.002). Screw placement accuracy reached 98.4% in the robotic group vs. 90% in controls (P=0.003) (14).

Meta-analyses corroborated these findings. A 2024 network meta-analysis of five studies reported that robotics decreased hospital LOS by 2.67 days and reduced blood loss by 120 mL relative to fluoroscopy-guided techniques (9). Another meta-analysis of 14 trials (892 patients) found that robotic or navigated techniques doubled the odds of accurate screw placement [odds ratio (OR) =2.66; P=0.01], reduced facet joint violations [risk ratio (RR) =0.09; P<0.01], and decreased major complications (RR =0.31; P=0.02) (15). However, robotics was also associated with increased operative duration, often by 10–40 minutes compared with navigation or freehand (16). Large database studies from the United States (US) reported mixed findings: in 461,965 patients undergoing single-level lumbar fusion, robotic cases had slightly shorter LOS (3.06 vs. 3.13 days, P=0.042) but higher charges and increased rates of postoperative anemia and transfusion (17). Another analysis of 58,270 patients found that robotic surgeries were associated with greater blood loss, anemia, and transfusion requirements, potentially reflecting case complexity and surgical approach rather than technology alone (11).

AR studies were smaller but reported promising perioperative benefits. A narrative review summarizing 12 studies involving 854 patients found that AR reduced pedicle screw misplacement from 9% to 4% and decreased operative time by roughly 20% (18). Proof-of-concept trials using head-mounted displays achieved screw placement accuracy of 98% with a mean deviation of 1.2 vs. 4.5 mm in freehand (P<0.001). Another review of eight studies (712 patients) reported 20% reductions in operative time and 15% reductions in blood loss. No included AR studies reported standardized long-term clinical outcomes or economic analyses.

AI remains an emergent technology in spine surgery. A 2025 scoping review identified 105 AI-focused studies, most using supervised or deep learning for prognostication; only three involved intraoperative assistance (19). None of the identified AI studies reported patient-level clinical outcomes beyond the perioperative period or any cost or economic data, underscoring the early developmental stage of AI applications in lumbar spine surgery. Importantly, the current AI literature in lumbar spine surgery largely reflects predictive modeling and decision support research rather than clinically deployed AI systems integrated into routine surgical care.

Cost-effectiveness

It is important to note that reported hospital charges derived from administrative databases, direct institutional cost estimates, and modeled cost-effectiveness analyses represent distinct economic metrics and are not directly comparable across studies.

Evidence on cost-effectiveness was limited and methodologically heterogeneous. A 2018 cost-effectiveness analysis modeled 1,985 elective cases at a tertiary center and estimated that adopting robotics in 557 thoracolumbar cases could avoid 9.47 revision surgeries and save US $608,546 per year through reduced operating time, shorter LOS, and fewer infections (20). The 2024 network meta-analysis estimated cost savings of US $4,086–4,865 per patient when robotics was compared with computed tomography navigation and US $7,317–9,654 per patient vs. fluoroscopy due to shorter LOS and less blood loss (9). A contemporary cohort from Singapore reported that robot-assisted oblique lumbar interbody fusion saved 50–62 minutes per operation and reduced operating costs by approximately Singapore dollars (SGD) 1,500 per patient; however, LOS did not differ (21). A retrospective cohort study estimating the financial return on investment for a robotic platform found that the net present value became positive after about 2.08 years at a volume of 200 surgeries per year (22). Across studies, cost-effectiveness estimates were highly sensitive to procedural volume, institutional efficiency, and whether capital and maintenance costs were incorporated.

Technology-specific insights

Robotic systems enhance pedicle screw accuracy and may reduce blood loss and LOS, but often prolong operative time, particularly during initial learning curves. Complication rates appear similar to conventional techniques; however, robot-related errors, like aborted trajectories, can increase blood loss and transfusion requirements (23). Long-term outcomes, including fusion rates and sustained patient-reported outcomes, were infrequently reported and showed no consistent superiority over conventional approaches, although select cohorts reported improved Oswestry disability index (ODI) and pain scores at 24 months in the robotic group (22).

AR head-mounted systems provide real-time visualization of anatomical structures and instrument trajectories. Early studies demonstrated high screw accuracy and reductions in operative time and radiation exposure (18). Barriers to adoption include equipment cost, workflow integration, and lack of long-term and economic data, which were inconsistently addressed.

AI applications were primarily limited to outcome prediction and surgical planning. Current models lack prospective validation and economic evaluation (19). No evidence was identified supporting clinical or cost-effectiveness of AI-based systems in lumbar spine surgery.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes evidence on advanced technologies in lumbar spine surgery with a focus on economic value, evidence gaps, and adoption considerations. Robotics has evolved from early guidance frameworks to sophisticated platforms integrating real-time imaging and, in some systems, AI (1,6,24). Across controlled studies and meta-analyses, robotic guidance yields superior screw accuracy and modest reductions in blood loss and LOS compared with freehand or navigated techniques (25). These benefits, however, are counterbalanced by longer operative times and increased upfront costs. Large administrative datasets reveal that robotic cases may incur higher hospital charges and increased transfusion rates, suggesting that the value of robotic assistants is highly context-dependent and may vary according to case complexity, surgeon experience, institutional workflow, and surgical approach (4,17).

Importantly, short-term gains in technical accuracy have not been consistently associated with superior long-term functional outcomes (26,27). Several cohorts report comparable patient-reported outcomes at 1 to 2 years between robotic-assisted and conventional techniques, underscoring the need for caution when extrapolating perioperative advantages to sustained clinical benefit (1,27). The relative scarcity of standardized long-term outcomes further limits definitive conclusions regarding the durability of benefit.

AR demonstrates favorable outcomes in intraoperative orientation and efficiency, particularly in minimally invasive and complex deformity procedures (28,29). Available studies report high technical accuracy and decreased operative time (29); however, these findings are derived from small case series and proof-of-concept studies (30,31). Robust comparative trials, long-term outcome reporting, and formal economic evaluations are notably absent, and the costs associated with AR hardware, software, and training present barriers to widespread adoption.

AI remains at the exploratory stage within lumbar spine surgery. Existing studies focus predominantly on prognostication and decision-making support modeling rather than clinical deployment (32-34). As a result, no evidence currently exists to assess the clinical effectiveness or economic impact of AI-driven interventions. While AI holds potential for personalized stratification, surgical planning, and postoperative planning, these applications require prospective validation before their value can be meaningfully assessed.

From an economic perspective, robotics may become cost-effective under specific conditions, particularly at high surgical volumes where reductions in operative time, complications, or revisions offset acquisition and maintenance costs (9,10). Reported payback periods vary widely depending on purchase price, procedural volume, and local cost structures (8). These findings highlight the importance of institution-specific cost-benefit analyses rather than universal assumptions of value, especially in resource-constrained settings (22).

Limitations of this study

This review is limited by heterogeneity of study designs, outcome measures, and economic methodologies, which limited direct comparison across studies. Articles published only in English were included, which may limit the inclusion of relevant international evidence. Additionally, because this study was conducted as a scoping review, the methodological quality of the included studies was not formally evaluated. As a result, variability in study quality and reporting may influence the interpretation of the reported economic findings and cost-effectiveness estimates.

Conclusions

Robotic and augmented‑reality technologies improve technical accuracy and may reduce blood loss and hospital stay in lumbar spine surgery. Nevertheless, current evidence does not consistently demonstrate sustained clinical benefit or uniform cost-effectiveness. Economic value appears highly dependent on surgical volume, institutional efficiency, and implementation context. AI applications remain nascent, with no available data on clinical or economic outcomes. Future adoption of advanced technology should be guided by rigorous, value-focused evidence rather than technical performance alone.
