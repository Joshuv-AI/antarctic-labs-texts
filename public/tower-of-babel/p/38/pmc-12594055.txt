Inflexibility in depression: Comparative analysis of cognitive, explanatory, coping, and belief flexibility
Authors: Jyotika Singh, Devvarta Kumar, Naren P. Rao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Flexibility, or the ability to adapt to changing circumstances, is crucial for psychological health. Depression is often characterized by inflexibility in various domains.Aim:This cross-sectional study aimed to assess, compare, and examine the correlations between cognitive, explanatory, coping, and belief flexibilities in individuals with major depression.Materials and Methods:A sample of 40 adult patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) was compared with 40 healthy matched controls using the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, Attributional Style Questionnaire, Coping Flexibility Scale, and the Emotional variant of the Bias Against Disconfirmatory Evidence task.Results:Results revealed that individuals with depression exhibited significant coping inflexibility. They also exhibited belief inflexibility when the stimuli were self-referent and negative. However, no cognitive inflexibility was observed. In the clinical group, cognitive flexibility correlated with both positive and negative belief flexibilities, while explanatory and coping flexibility showed no links. In controls, cognitive flexibility was related to negative belief flexibility, and explanatory flexibility was associated with positive belief flexibility. Multiple regression analyses further showed that cognitive flexibility significantly predicted both negative (β = –0.61, P < 0.001) and positive belief flexibility (β = –0.57, P < 0.001) in individuals with depression, suggesting a potential causal link.Conclusion:These findings suggest that specific areas of flexibility are impaired in depression. Furthermore, different correlation patterns in both groups may have important clinical implications.

INTRODUCTION

Understanding the psychological factors associated with depression is imperative for evolving better psychotherapeutic strategies. One line of research in this direction can be to investigate the psychological flexibility of individuals with depression, as it is associated with better adjustment and overall well-being.[12]

Flexibility refers to an individual’s ability to adapt effectively to changes in internal states or external circumstances.[3] This adaptability involves the dynamic ability to shift among various patterns of thinking, interpreting, coping, and belief systems in response to situational demands, thereby encompassing cognitive, emotional, and behavioral domains of functioning. Together, these domains illustrate how flexibility can be conceptualized and assessed using appropriate tools across cognitive, explanatory, coping, and belief dimensions.

There are some indications that individuals with depression exhibit reduced flexibility in these domains. For example, they exhibit a higher propensity to interpret ambiguous information negatively, especially if it relates to their own self,[4] and do not consider multiple perspectives while attributing causes to negative events.[5] Similarly, a lack of flexibility in adjusting coping strategies is seen in depression.[6]

Flexibility may function as a state effect of depression, reflecting changes that occur during the illness[78] or as a causal/maintaining factor that contributes to its persistence. For example, reduced flexibility in interpreting events, with a bias toward negative aspects, may contribute to persistent low mood and heightened threat perception.[9] Besides, lack of flexibility in coping strategies has been linked to difficulties in adjustment.[1]

While a substantial number of studies have explored individual domains of inflexibility in depression, relatively few have examined multiple domains concurrently or investigated how different forms of inflexibility relate to one another within the same sample.[10] This will help with our understanding of the cognitive structure of inflexibility in depression, which can clarify whether these forms of inflexibility reflect similar or distinct processes, and can inform the development of more targeted and effective interventions.

Fresco et al.[10] reported that coping and explanatory flexibility are interrelated and predict depression and anxiety symptoms. Zhu et al.[11] studied cognitive, explanatory, and belief flexibility across depression, schizophrenia, and healthy controls, and found a positive association between cognitive and belief flexibility in the overall sample. However, this relationship was absent when the depression group was analyzed separately, suggesting that negative mood may affect the relationship between various flexibilities. As these findings require further validation, the present study was undertaken to assess whether individuals with depression differ with healthy controls in terms of cognitive, explanatory, coping, and belief-inflexibilities, and whether belief inflexibility is specific to self-referent stimuli or extends to non–self-referent stimuli. Second, we also wished to assess the relationship among cognitive, explanatory, coping, and belief flexibilities in depression, as these links have important implications for intervention design.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Forty-two adult individuals diagnosed with major depressive disorder (MDD) according to DSM-5 were recruited from a tertiary care hospital in India. The participants with any psychiatric comorbidities, personality disorder, psychotic symptoms, or any current or past organic brain disorders were excluded. Forty-two healthy controls having no lifetime history of psychiatric disorders or personality disorders were recruited from the community through snowball sampling and were matched with the clinical group on age, sex, and education. All assessments were done through clinical interview, followed by the regular outdoor practice.

Although a priori sample size estimation was not done, a previous study[12] used a smaller group size (32-37 participants). We recruited 42 participants per group, focusing on depression without comorbidities. Our sample size is consistent with previous research while taking into account both methodological rigor and practical constraints of clinical recruitment.

Description of tools

Modified mini screen[13]

The Modified Mini Screen (MMS) is a 22-item clinician-administered interview to screen for mood disorders, anxiety disorders, and psychotic disorders based on DSM-IV-TR.[14] In the present study, it was used as a screening tool to screen for any mental health difficulties in the control group.

Beck’s depression inventory[15]

The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) is a widely used 21-item self-report measure of depressive symptom severity experienced across the past 2 weeks.

The structured clinical interview for DSM-5 personality disorders[16]

The structured clinical interview for DSM-5 personality disorders (SCID-5-PD) is a semistructured diagnostic interview to assess the 10 DSM-5 personality disorders across clusters A, B, and C. It was used to screen for personality disorders in both clinical and control groups.

Flexibility measures

Wisconsin card sorting test[17]

The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) was used in this study to assess cognitive flexibility. It requires sorting cards according to a particular rule, which has to be figured out based on the examiner’s feedback, and hence requires changing responses according to the feedback. For measuring cognitive flexibility, WCST perseverative errors were taken as an index.[18]

Attributional style questionnaire[19]

Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ) is a self-report inventory for assessing explanatory flexibility, which requires rating of causes of hypothetical positive and negative events as either internal or external, stable or unstable, or specific vs global on a scale of 1 to 7. Explanatory flexibility is calculated as the standard deviation (SD) of the scores on negative items for stable and global dimensions. A higher SD points toward more flexibility.

Coping flexibility scale[20]

The Coping Flexibility Scale (CFS) is a self-report inventory consisting of two 5-item subscales that pertain to adaptive coping (e.g., When a stressful situation has not improved, I try to think of other ways to cope with it) and evaluation coping (e.g., If I feel that I have failed to cope with stress, I change the way in which I deal with stress). It has been validated for use in the Indian context in a large adult sample.[21]

The emotional variant of the bias against disconfirmatory evidence (BADE)

The Emotional Variant of the Bias Against Disconfirmatory Evidence (BADE) task[22] has been commonly used to assess belief flexibility in psychosis by presenting ambiguous scenarios that become clearer with additional information. Participants are required to rate the plausibility of four interpretations: two lures (initially plausible, later less convincing), a true explanation (initially less plausible, later more likely), and an absurd explanation (implausible throughout)—each three times as more information about the scenario emerges. Greater belief flexibility is indicated by adjusting initial ratings as new contradictory information emerges. This study developed an emotional BADE variant—Emotional Bias Against Disconfirmatory Evidence (BADE) task to assess interpretation inflexibility in emotional contexts. The development process involved creating 40 scenarios modeled after the BADE task structure and aligning with the framework used in Everaert et al.[23]

These scenarios were evaluated by five qualified clinical psychologists, who rated each scenario on eight criteria to ensure adherence to the BADE task structure. Scenarios receiving ratings below 7 on more than one criterion or from more than one rater were discarded. For those with lower ratings on a single criterion, expert suggestions were incorporated, and clarifications were sought. Based on this validation process, 24 scenarios were finalized with two scenario types: Disconfirming Negative (initially negative, eventually positive) and Disconfirming Positive (initially positive, eventually negative) (see Appendix 1 for examples). Scenarios were either self-referent (“You are feeling low”) or other-referent (“Raj is feeling low”). The BADE score was computed by subtracting the average of Rating 1 scores for Lure 1 and Lure 2 from the average of Rating 3 scores for Lure 1 and Lure 2. BADE = mean (R3L1 + R3L2) – mean (R1L1 + R1L2). It was referred to as BADE-DN in disconfirming negative scenarios and BADE-DP in disconfirming positive scenarios.

Other measures

Bhatia’s short battery of intelligence[24]

A short battery of intelligence was used to ascertain that the participants’ intellectual ability is not compromised. It consists of Kohs Block Design, which requires design construction using blocks, and Alexander’s Passalong Test, which involves manipulation of blocks within a block to make a design without lifting the blocks.

Digits forward and backward test[25]

The digits forward test was used as a measure of attention, and the digits backward test was used to assess working memory to ensure that results were not confounded by general cognitive impairments.

Procedure

The study reports a part of the research for the PhD program examining flexibility variables in depression. The protocol for the research was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Data were collected from May 2022 to October 2023 (18 months) at a tertiary care hospital in Bengaluru. Each patient was diagnosed by a psychiatrist. The control group was recruited from the community. Written informed consent was taken from each participant, and tools were administered individually to each participant. All tools were administered in English. Assessment was completed in one or two sessions separated by not more than a week’s time.

Statistical analyses

Independent sample t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used wherever appropriate (based on whether or not the data met normality criteria). Spearman correlation was used to measure associations between flexibility indices separately in both groups. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to assess the predictive relationships among flexibility variables with BDI scores and belief flexibility scores (negative and positive) used as dependent variables. Analyses were done using SPSS version 27.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

A total of 2,500 patient files were screened in both outpatient and inpatient setting, of which 2,104 were excluded (language/education barriers: n = 1,400; comorbidities: n = 267; diagnosed personality disorder: n = 250; depression with psychotic features: n = 187). Of the 396 provisionally eligible, 354 were further excluded (SCID-diagnosed personality disorder: n = 20; other diagnoses: n = 30; refusal: n = 122; no response/did not turn up: n = 182). Forty-two patients completed assessments, of whom two were excluded during data cleaning. In the control group, 151 community participants were screened. Of these, 109 were excluded (did not meet inclusion criteria: n = 40; refusal: n = 69). Forty-two completed assessments, of whom two were excluded to match the clinical sample (based on case-to-case matching). Thus, the statistical analyses happened on 40 participants in each group. Explanatory flexibility

As measured through the standard deviation of negative events on stable and global dimensions of the Attributional Style Questionnaire, the clinical and control groups did not differ significantly (U = 700.50, P = 0.33) [Table 2].

Coping flexibility

The control group had higher adaptive coping (t = 2.40; P = 0.01); evaluative coping (t = 4.34; P < 0.001), and total coping (t = 3.36; P = 0.001) scores [Table 2].

Belief flexibility

Flexibility in interpretations was measured through the BADE scores for disconfirming negative and disconfirming positive scenarios, calculated as difference in average of third and average of first rating of both lures (lure one and two). For self-referent disconfirming negative scenarios (BADE-Self-DN), depression group was more inflexible than controls (U = 550; P = 0.01). There were no significant differences between groups on disconfirming positive scenarios [Table 2].

The BADE subvariables positively correlated with each other in both groups [Tables 3 and 4].

Regression analysis

A multiple regression analysis was conducted to examine whether flexibility variables predicted BDI scores within the depression group. The variables cognitive flexibility, explanatory flexibility, coping flexibility, negative belief flexibility, and positive belief flexibility were entered simultaneously in the regression equation, and the BDI severity score was the dependent variable. The overall regression model was not statistically significant.

In a separate model, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine whether cognitive flexibility, explanatory flexibility, and coping flexibility significantly predicted negative belief flexibility. Negative and positive belief flexibility were used as outcome variables based on the theoretical assumption that belief flexibility is influenced by broader cognitive and coping processes.[3]

The overall regression model was statistically significant, F (3, 36) = 7.06, P < 0.001. The model accounted for approximately 37.1% of the variance in negative belief flexibility (R2 = 0.371, adjusted R2 = 0.318).

Among the three predictors, only cognitive flexibility (WCST perseverative errors) emerged as a statistically significant predictor (β = –0.61, P < 0.001). Neither explanatory flexibility (β = –0.106, P = 0.44) nor coping flexibility (β = –0.015, P = 0.914) significantly contributed to the model. Similarly, the model was tested for positive belief flexibility as well. Again, cognitive flexibility emerged as a significant predictor (β = –0.57, P < 0.001) [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

This study assessed whether individuals with depression show greater cognitive, explanatory, coping, and belief inflexibilities compared to healthy controls, and how these inflexibilities relate to each other. Findings were partially consistent with our hypotheses: coping and belief inflexibility were more pronounced in depression, particularly in response to self-referent negative stimuli, while the two groups did not differ in terms of cognitive and explanatory flexibility.

The higher level of coping inflexibility in clinically depressed individuals suggests that they remain stuck with ineffective coping strategies during stress, potentially leading to frustration and other negative emotions. Similarly, Zong et al.[26] found that college students with depressive symptoms (BDI score >14) had lower coping effectiveness than nondepressed peers as measured by the Coping Flexibility Questionnaire.[27] The findings suggest that enhancing coping flexibility could be a valuable target for interventions in patients with depression.

Higher coping inflexibility observed in depression suggests a rigid approach to coping with stress. However, the cognitive flexibility shown in response to neutral stimuli (e.g. WCST cards) indicates that while depressed individuals exhibit flexibility to neutral tasks, they lack similar flexibility when tasks resemble real life situations as seen on the items of Coping Flexibility Scale (CFS). This conclusion is further supported by our findings of nonsignificant correlation between coping and cognitive flexibility.

This pattern supports the hot-cold cognition model,[28] wherein depressed individuals demonstrate intact flexibility on emotionally neutral (cold) tasks such as the WCST, but exhibit significant inflexibility on emotionally salient (hot) tasks like real world coping scenarios and self-referent belief evaluations.

Findings on cognitive flexibility in depression have been inconsistent. Some studies, in line with the present observations have reported no significant group differences[29] whereas others have documented impairments on tasks such as perseverative errors[30] and the Trail Making Test.[31]

Research suggests that deficits are more likely to emerge in older or more severely depressed individuals.[732] The absence of group differences may reflect generally low perseverative errors in both samples, possibly influenced by their relatively high intelligence and education levels. Such variability highlights the need to consider demographic and clinical factors when evaluating cognitive flexibility in depression.

Belief inflexibility for self-referent stimuli emerged as a notable finding, and consistent with our hypothesis. Previous work also links depression severity with greater negative and reduced positive interpretation biases on self-referent tasks.[23] These findings implicate that opinions formed about an event or situation that concerns them are resistant to change. Thus, any negative cognitions about the self (e.g., “people do not like me”) can be defended by individuals with depression even if the evidences indicate otherwise. Such belief inflexibility could be an important maintaining factor for depression as interpreted through Beck’s cognitive theory of depression, which posits that individuals with depression develop negative self-schemas that resist disconfirmation. Cognitive therapies targeting depressive symptoms could focus on challenging self-referent negative biases. In fact a recent study showed that individuals with depression often underestimate their abilities, even though they may possess adequate skills and suggested that instead of focusing solely on skill development it is important to focus on their own view of self.[33] Therefore, exercises that encourage individuals to consider how others might interpret the same situations, especially familiar individuals such as close friends, could reduce the emotional salience of self-referent negativity and foster a broader, more balanced perspective.

Explanatory flexibility, or the ability to vary causal attributions across events, did not differ between depressed and control groups in this study. Similar null findings have been reported elsewhere,[11] though some studies, particularly with college samples, have found reduced explanatory flexibility in depression.[25] Insignificant finding between depression and control groups in explanatory flexibility could be due to the nature of this construct focusing on a general style of responding rather than specific cognitive content. It is also possible that differences in flexibility may emerge only under emotionally charged conditions (e.g., stress or mood induction).[8] This construct perhaps requires further research to investigate aforementioned dimensions.

Correlation patterns offered additional insights into how flexibility domains interact. A strong correlation was seen between cognitive flexibility and both positive and negative belief flexibility in the depression group. Further analyses confirmed that cognitive flexibility was an important predictor of belief inflexibility, suggesting that difficulties in shifting cognitive strategies may contribute to rigidity in updating beliefs. The strong correlation in the clinical group suggests a strong link between cognitive flexibility and maladaptive belief systems. Addressing these intertwined factors could lead to more effective treatment strategies. In controls, explanatory flexibility was linked to positive belief flexibility suggesting more sensitivity to contextual information when revising beliefs.

We did not find a correlation between depression severity and flexibility variables, and none of the flexibility variables emerged as a significant predictor of depression severity. These findings align with some previous findings.[29] Although earlier studies have suggested that fewer symptoms may relate to greater explanatory flexibility,[510] or coping flexibility,[212634] and that severe depression may heighten negative interpretation biases,[2335] such patterns were not evident here. These differences could be attributed to the heterogeneity of samples across studies, highlighting the need for further investigation in this area.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The sample was recruited from a clinical population and control group was matched as closely to the clinical group as possible. No a priori sample size calculation was done, however, the study included a larger sample than in the similar studies carried out earlier. The emotional BADE task included self and other statements together which has not been studied till now. The present study does have limitations; the study used specific tools to assess flexibility and did not include multiple tools to check if findings were consistent across different measures. However, that was not the scope of the study and it could have lengthened the assessment time significantly. The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences. These preliminary findings may help to build theoretical models of flexibility to better understand the interplay among these variables and implications in depression.

CONCLUSION

This study examined multiple flexibility domains in depression. Findings suggest that while coping and self-referent belief inflexibilities are elevated, cognitive and explanatory flexibilities may remain relatively unaffected. Distinct interrelations among domains suggest the need for larger, longitudinal studies to clarify their roles in depression and inform more targeted interventions.
