Characteristics of gut flora in children who go to bed early versus late
Authors: Chunmei Mao, Caiping Xi, Rong Du, Wenting Chen, Na Song, Yuansong Qian, Xueping Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigating the characteristics of the gut flora in children who go to bed early versus late. The study sample consisted of 88 healthy children aged 2–14 years, with an equal number of boys and girls. The researchers collected faecal samples from all participants and sequenced the genome of their gut flora. Findings indicate that beta diversity was statistically significant at the genus level for both the early and late sleeper groups (P = 0.045). Furthermore, alpha diversity indicators, including Simpson’s index (P = 0.0011) and Shannon’s index (P = 0.0013), exhibited higher values at the genus level. The differences observed in terms of species diversity, abundance, and metabolic pathways offer potential avenues for implementing pharmacological interventions aimed at addressing sleep disorders in children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75006-y.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Microbiology, Psychology, Health care

Introduction

Children’s sleep duration is a crucial factor in their growth and developmen1. Research has shown that their sleep duration is influenced by their daily activities, particularly during the school year when they have a more regular routine2. However, during weekends and holidays, their bedtimes and wake-up times tend to be later3. Ensuring that school-aged children get enough sleep has been found to have a positive impact on their learning. A study conducted by Blunden and his team found that children who slept longer performed better in subjects such as grammar, writing, and math4. Another study indicated that following an early to bed, early to rise routine was associated with a lower body mass index (BMI) in children, those who followed a late to bed, late to rise routine had higher BMIs5. Adolescents, too, benefit from developing consistent rest and leisure habits, as it contributes to their overall health and cognitive growth. Fukuda et al. concluded this based on their findings6. In addition, changes in children’s work habits have been linked to changes in their metabolism, as suggested by research conducted by Matricciani and his colleagues7.

The crucial role of the gut microbiota in maintaining human health includes contributions to conditions like obesity, diabetes, and autoimmune diseases8. Three types of gut flora exist: beneficial commensal flora, harmful flora, and conditionally pathogenic bacteria. These various types of flora are mutually limiting and interdependent, collectively determining the gut’s homeostatic motility9. Multiple factors significantly influence the composition of the intestinal flora, including dietary intake, lifestyle choices, infections, diseases, and pharmacological interventions10. Important physiological functions involving the intestinal flora include vitamin synthesis, bile catabolism, and exogenous substance detoxification, all contributing to metabolic stability11. Recent research demonstrated that the gut flora of obese children substantially impacts their glucose tolerance and insulin resistance. However, the exact mechanism of action still remains unclear12. In a study by Guzzardi MA et al.germ-free mice received transplants of feces from 90 5-year-old children, researchers observed the mediation of the gut-cognitive axis in both the donor children and recipient mice by specific metabolites, such as xanthine, hypoxanthine, formic acid, mannose, tyrosine, phenylalanine, and glutamine13.

Research has demonstrated that establishing early bedtimes and mornings can bring numerous advantages for children14. These advantages encompass ensuring sufficient sleep and establishing regular patterns, which are crucial for children’s well-being and educational achievements15. While prior investigations have primarily concentrated on observing children’s sleep patterns and implementing interventions to enhance these patterns, there exists a substantial research gap in examining children’s sleep from a physiological and metabolic viewpoint. Given this, the objective of the current study was to examine the metabolic mechanisms associated with early bedtime and wakefulness in children, with special attention to the composition of their gut microbiota and metabolic pathways. The findings of this investigation may potentially open doors for the development of novel pharmacological interventions targeting sleep patterns.

Objects and methods

Study subjects

A total of 88 healthy children aged 2 to 14 years from Northwest China were included in this study. The sample was split equally between boys and girls. Before participating in the study, all participants underwent a standard assessment conducted by a pediatrician. This assessment included various general characteristics of the subjects, such as birth weight, birth length, daily bowel movement frequency, time spent exercising, number of meals consumed, as well as their current weight and height. Simultaneously, the psychological states of the enrolled children and their parents were professionally assessed to ensure that the relevant indicators from the sleep diary could be recorded objectively and accurately. The purpose of this evaluation was to confirm that the participants had not been ill recently. In addition, none of the children had undergone abdominal surgery in the past six months or taken antibiotics in the four weeks prior to the study. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee and written consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all the children involved in the study.

Methods

Subjects and study design

The current research adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and gained approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Lanzhou University Second Hospital (APPROVAL number: 2022 A-221). This investigation focused on pediatric outpatients and sought to explore the potential relationship among sleep timing, gut microbiota, and metabolites utilizing multiomics data from Children residing in the northwest region of China. In total, a cohort of seemingly healthy children aged 2–14 years were sequentially enrolled from Lanzhou City in Gansu province, situated in Northwest China. Consequently, the enrolled children were disqualified if they had a severe chronic infection, current infection, or had taken antibiotics within the last 30 days prior to participating in this research. Before specimen collection, the parents of the enrolled children underwent training on how to accurately and promptly complete the sleep diary. Following successful completion of this training, all enrolled children were monitored and recorded in the sleep diary for a consecutive period of 14 days.Sleep diary monitoring and recording includes: Time to sleep, Woke up a few times during sleep, How long did you sleep at night?, sleep efficiency(Total time sleeping/total time lying in bed), Number of dreams, Sleep Quality Score (1 is poor, 5 is fair, 10 is excellent), Daytime nap time(min), How long did you sleep at night?, Total time spent in bed at night, Daytime nap time. ‘ The children included in this study were categorized into two groups based on bedtime (early sleep group: prior to 21:30, late sleep group: after 21:30). All biological specimens (including fecal samples and blood) and clinical biochemical markers were gathered following the Standard Operating Procedure at the second affiliated hospital of Lanzhou University in Gansu province. Specifically, the biological samples were accrued, promptly stored at a -80 °C freezer, and then promptly transferred to the designated laboratory on dry ice.

DNA preparation and metagenomics sequencing

The DNA extraction process began by using the phenol/trichloromethane method on samples that had been thawed on ice. Following this, the extracts underwent treatment with DNase-free RNase, and the quality of the DNA was assessed through agarose gel electrophoresis and a Qubit 3.0 fluorimeter. After confirming the concentration and integrity of the total DNA, 500 ng of DNA was then fragmented using ultrasound and size-selected to range from 300 bp to 700 bp using magnet beads. The selected DNA fragments were repaired and ligated with indexed adaptors, and the resulting products were amplified by PCR, hybridized with exon probes, and captured by streptavidin beads. Subsequently, the captured DNA was amplified once more via PCR and circularized to create a single-stranded circular library. This library was then amplified through rolling circle amplification to generate DNA nanoballs, which were loaded onto a flowcell and sequenced using the DNBSEQ Platform.During the sequencing process, raw reads that contained 50% low-quality bases (quality ≤ 20) or more than five ambiguous bases were excluded. The remaining reads were then filtered to remove any host DNA by referencing the human genome as previously described. Metagenomics sequencing was carried out on the DNBSEQ-T1 platform with pair-end libraries and a read length of 150 bp. Quality control measures were implemented, and any adapter sequences or host contamination were eliminated using Fastp16and bowtie217.

Taxonomic and functional metabolic pathways profiling acquisition

Taxonomic profiles were derived from high-quality reads via Metaphlan 3.018, using parameters (- input_type fastq - ignore_viruses - nproc 6) as detailed on the MetaPhlAn3 official page (MetaPhlAn3-The Huttenhower Lab (harvard.edu)). For functional metabolic pathways profiling, HUMAnN 3.019 was utilized, employing settings (-i input_clean_data -o output --threads 10 --memory-use maximum --remove-tempoutput) to effectively and precisely profile the abundance of microbial metabolic pathways along with other molecular functions derived from the metagenomic sequencing data (clean data). This methodology adheres to the specifications offered by the official HUMAnN website (https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/humann).

Alpha- and beta-diversity

Alpha diversity (diversity within a sample, using R 4.0.3 vegan: diversity function (data, index = ‘richness/shannon/Simpson/InSimpson’)) was determined by assessing richness, Shannon index, Simpson index, and Inverse Simpson index, which varied based on taxonomic profiles. Beta diversity (diversity between samples, calculated by R 4.0.3 ape: pcoa function (‘bray_curtis distance’, correction="none”, rn = NULL)) was measured using the Bray-Curtis distance, which also depended on taxonomic profiles. Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA, R 4.0.3 code: adonis function (dist ~ phe, permutations = 9999)) was conducted on gut microbial species/genus abundance profiles to analyze the impact of groups on the gut microbiome.

Statistical analysis

PERMANOVA was used to study the effect of various phenotypes on the gut microbiota. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to analyze the relationship between sleep habits and species. Wilcoxon ranksum test was used to compare the difference of the species, genera, phyla, KEGG pathways between bed-early and bed-late. All statistical analyses were based on R packages (Version 4.2.1). PERMANOVA: vegan, adonis(t(otu1) ~ phe (,1), data = phe, permutations = 999, na.rm = T). Wilcoxon rank-sum test: wilcox.test (as.numeric(pr[i, f1]), as.numeric(pr[i, f2])). Heatmap: pheatmap(cmt, cale = “none”,cluster_row = T, cluster_col = T, display_numbers = pmt). P < 0.05 was considered significant difference.

Results

A sleep diary was maintained for 88 children over a 14-day period, revealing that 44 children went to sleep before 21:30 while the remaining 44 children went to sleep after this time. No significant differences were observed in sex ratio, body mass index, age distribution, birth weight, birth length, age of parents, number of bowel movements per day, eating habits, handwashing practices before meals, geographical distribution, regular exercise, or allergy history(Table 1).

Analysis of the sleep diary indicators revealed significant differences between the early sleep group and the late sleep group. Specifically, the time it takes to fall asleep showed a significant difference (P = 1e-06), as did the frequency of waking up during the night (P = 0.015). Additionally, sleep efficiency exhibited a significant difference (P = 9.9e-06), and sleep quality also showed a notable difference (P = 0.00016). In contrast, the total minutes of sleep, the number of dreams, and the duration of naps (in minutes) displayed less pronounced differences.(Fig. 1).

Moreover, CAG_83 fungi levels were raised in the early bedtime group (P = 0.048)(Fig. 3). On the level of phylum/genus, Verrucomicrobia (P = 0.00024) and Akkermansia (P = 0.00024) were more common in the group that went to sleep early. Additionally, Holdemania had a higher abundance value (P = 0.0077) in the group with an early bedtime. Furthermore, Firmuicutes_unclassified abundance was also greater in the group that went to sleep early (P = 0.022)(sFigure2). Analyzed the connection between sleep diary-related metrics and the different species, we found Akkermansia muciniphila and Alistipes finegoldii exhibited a positive correlation regarding the time taken to fall asleep (in minutes). In contrast, Clostridium_sp_CAG_253 showed a negative correlation in this regard. Additionally, Alistipes finegoldii was positively correlated with total sleep duration (in minutes), but negatively correlated with the frequency of dreams and sleep efficiency. Furthermore, Alistipes finegoldii and Akkermansia muciniphila demonstrated a negative correlation, as did Alistipes finegoldii and Holdemania filiformis in terms of sleep quality(sFigure3).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the gut flora characteristics and metabolic pathways of children who followed a consistent sleep schedule. The results of the study indicated significant differences in species composition, bacterial diversity, enrichment, and metabolic pathways between children who went to bed before 21:30 and those who went to bed after 21:30. Particularly, we observed a higher presence of Akkermansia_muciniphila and Bacteroidetes, Verrucomicrobia, Firmicutes flora, Alistipes_finegoldi and Holdemania_filiformis flora, Firmicutes_bacterium_CAG_95in children who adhered to an early bedtime(Earlier than 21:30). Establishing a healthy circadian rhythm by going to bed and waking up early is crucial for both physiological and psychological development. The gut microbiota exerts an influence on sleep regulation by modulating immune and neural pathways, known as the gut-brain axis20.Sleep disorders have detrimental effects on cognitive development, and the presence of Akkermansia muciniphila, a specific gut microbe, plays a key role in maintaining microglial homeostasis, which indirectly affects cognition21. In a study conducted by Yao ZY et al., it was observed that sleep deprivation in pregnant rats resulted in a noticeable rise in the population of thick-walled bacterial phylum22. This indicates that lack of sleep can have a considerable influence on the composition of gut flora, particularly affecting the posterior wall phylum23. Another research also found a positive correlation between the abundance of thick-walled bacterial phylum and factors such as sleep efficiency, concentration of interleukin 6, and abstract thinking24. These findings suggest that disruptions in sleep patterns can have significant effects on both the gut microbiota and various cognitive functions. In our investigation, we specifically analyzed the metabolic pathways of children who followed the early to bed, early to rise routine. O-cresol is involved in the aerobic degradation of toluene and plays a role in brain neurotransmission and behavioural-emotional control via the metabolic pathway II, which involves the aerobic degradation of 4-methylcatechol25. Amino acid metabolic pathways undergo alterations in sleep-wake disorders, however, the precise mechanism of this change remains elusive26. Sleep and metabolism interact with each other and serine/threonine metabolism may regulate sleep via neurotransmitters27.

The recognition of children’s sleep problems as a significant public health issue during childhood growth and development has increased. Current interventions for addressing these sleep problems include nutrition, exercise, cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia, and acupressure28. A study found that adjunctive interventions to improve low ferritin levels and vitamin D3 deficiency were beneficial for sleep in children and adolescents29. However, there is a lack of research on pharmacological interventions for sleep. This study focuses on the metabolic mechanisms of the organism in children who adhere to an early-to-bed, early-to-wake schedule, specifically exploring the role of intestinal flora. The findings of this study are expected to offer new insights for future pharmacological interventions for children with sleep disorders.

The present study is limited in several ways. Firstly, it did not conduct further investigations into the connection between gut microbiota and small molecule metabolism in children who follow early sleep and wake schedules. This means that a more comprehensive understanding of the metabolic mechanisms related to the gut microbiota of these children could not be obtained. Moreover, the small sample size used in this study restricts its ability to accurately characterize the gut microbiota of all children who adhere to early bedtime and early rise routines. Additionally, the information on sleep habits in this study was obtained from parents who were not rigorously monitored for their own sleep patterns. Furthermore, the recruitment of participants did not include rural children, and although there was some variation in lifestyle habits between the two groups, the limited sample sizes prevented a more representative analysis. To address these limitations, future research should consider increasing the sample size, recruiting children from different regions (including both rural and urban areas), and performing additional metabolomic assessments to gain a deeper understanding of the metabolic mechanisms involved.

Conclusion

The study concluded that there were notable variations in species diversity, abundance, and metabolic pathways in the gut flora between individuals who maintain a consistent sleep schedule and those with irregular sleep patterns. It emphasized the importance of sufficient sleep in promoting healthy cognitive and physical development in children. The outcomes of this examination offer a fresh perspective for exploring pharmacological approaches to address sleep disturbances in pediatric patients.
