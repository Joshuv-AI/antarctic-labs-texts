Smoking cessation duration and risk of Alzheimer’s disease: a nationwide cohort study in Korea
Authors: Seong-Ho Moon, Min-Ho Kim, Jae-Hong Ryoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmoking is a modifiable risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease, yet the temporal pattern of risk reduction following smoking cessation remains unclear. We investigated whether Alzheimer’s disease risk declines according to the duration of sustained smoking cessation in a large nationwide cohort.MethodsUsing the Korean National Health Insurance Service database (2002–2023), we identified 1,403,636 adults with stable longitudinal smoking patterns based on repeated biennial health examinations. Smoking status was classified as never smokers, current smokers, or sustained quitters, with cessation duration categorised into 2-year intervals (< 2, 2–3, 4–5, 6–7, ≥ 8 years). Incident Alzheimer’s disease was defined as at least two separate NHIS insurance claims. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals, adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and metabolic factors.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 10.5 years, 58,519 incident Alzheimer’s disease cases were identified. Compared with current smokers, sustained quitters exhibited progressively lower hazards of Alzheimer’s disease with increasing duration of abstinence, demonstrating a dose–response pattern. Individuals who had quit for < 2 years showed a risk reduction relative to current smokers (adjusted HR 0.899; 95% CI 0.819–0.986), while those with ≥ 8 years of abstinence had substantially lower risk (adjusted HR 0.582; 95% CI 0.423–0.801). When referenced to never smokers, those with < 2 years of abstinence retained excess risk, whereas cessation for ≥ 2 years was associated with Alzheimer’s disease risk approaching that of never smokers.ConclusionsSustained smoking cessation was associated with a graded reduction in Alzheimer’s disease risk, with approximately 2 years of abstinence needed to approach never-smoker risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02132-2.

Introduction

Smoking is a well-established modifiable risk factor for cognitive decline and has been linked to neurodegenerative disease, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), although findings from epidemiological studies have not been entirely consistent [1–5]. Smoking promotes oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, cerebrovascular dysfunction, and blood–brain barrier disruption, all of which are implicated in the pathogenesis of AD [6–8]. Prospective cohort studies and systematic reviews indicate that current smokers face a 30% to 80% higher risk of developing AD compared with never smokers [2, 6]. Given that AD has a long preclinical phase, identifying modifiable exposures that can delay or mitigate its onset is a global public health priority [3].

Given these established harms, smoking cessation is widely recommended as a core strategy for dementia prevention across the life course [9, 10]. Several longitudinal studies have reported that quitters exhibit lower risks of dementia compared with current smokers, suggesting that cessation may partially mitigate smoking-related neurodegenerative risk [2, 11]. However, despite these observations, critical gaps remain in the existing literature. In particular, the temporal dynamics of AD risk reduction following smoking cessation have not been adequately characterised. Most studies classify smoking status simply as never, former, or current, thereby treating former smokers as a homogeneous group despite substantial heterogeneity in time since smoking cessation [2, 12]. This lack of granularity masks potential differences in risk trajectories between short-term and long-term quitters, leaving clinicians with insufficient data to counsel patients on how long they must remain abstinent to meaningfully reduce their risk.

Furthermore, a significant proportion of epidemiological research has focused on all-cause dementia rather than AD specifically [2, 11–15]. While this approach increases statistical power, it obscures aetiological distinctions between AD and other dementia subtypes, particularly vascular dementia, which differs in neuropathology, latency, and susceptibility to vascular risk factors [16]. This distinction is especially relevant when evaluating smoking, an exposure that exerts both vascular and neurodegenerative effects. Moreover, smoking-related excess mortality often introduces competing-risk bias, which potentially can lead to an underestimation of AD risk among current smokers in long-term cohort studies [15].

To date, only a few studies have examined the duration of smoking cessation in relation to AD, and these have often relied on small sample sizes or restricted populations, such as older men only [11]. There is a clear need for large-scale, population-based evidence to quantify the time course of risk attenuation. Therefore, using a nationwide, population-based cohort from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), we aimed to evaluate the association between smoking cessation duration and the risk of incident AD using granular 2-year interval categories, and determine the duration of sustained abstinence required for quitters to approach AD risk levels comparable to those of never smokers.

Methods

Data and study population

We conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) covering the period from 2002 through December 31, 2023. As a compulsory, single-payer system, the NHIS covers nearly all Korean citizens and facilitates general health assessments for adults every two years. The NHIS database has been open to researchers since 2002, offering detailed insights into health screening outcomes, diagnoses, and therapeutic histories [17].

Among 6,246,568 individuals with complete smoking status and pack-year information who underwent health checkups during 2009–2013, we applied sequential exclusion criteria as shown in Fig. 1. We excluded 85,594 individuals with a diagnosis of dementia (F00–F03) allowing for overlapping codes before the index date, 3,977,219 individuals who underwent fewer than four health checkups or had intervals > 36 months between examinations before the index date, and 207,619 individuals who transitioned from non-smoking to smoking or relapsed after quitting. We further excluded 118,335 individuals who initiated smoking at subsequent examinations and 454,165 individuals with inconsistent smoking status. The final analytic cohort comprised 1,403,636 participants: 1,103,294 never smokers, 64,460 sustained quitters, and 235,882 current smokers (Fig. 1). The index date was defined as the date of the last eligible health examination during 2009–2013.

The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Hospital (IRB No. KHUH 2023-04-023). As the investigation used a registry-based, anonymised dataset, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Exposure: smoking status and duration of smoking cessation

Time-updated smoking status was obtained from self-reported questionnaires administered during biennial NHIS health examinations (2002 through the index period). To minimise exposure misclassification, we included only individuals with at least four examinations and a consistent longitudinal smoking pattern across assessments. Participants were classified into three mutually exclusive categories: (1) never smokers, who consistently reported never smoking at all examinations; (2) sustained quitters, who reported current smoking at the initial examination but subsequently reported cessation and maintained abstinence at all later examinations without relapse; and (3) current smokers, who consistently reported active smoking at each examination. For sustained quitters, the quit date was estimated as the midpoint between the examination at which cessation was first reported and the preceding examination, and smoking cessation duration was calculated from this estimated quit date to the index date. Sustained quitters were categorised into two-year cessation intervals: <2, 2–3, 4–5, 6–7, and ≥ 8 years.

Outcome ascertainment: incident Alzheimer’s disease

Incident AD was ascertained using NHIS insurance claims records with the ICD-10 code F00 (dementia in Alzheimer’s disease) from the data from NHIS occurring after the index date [18]. To improve diagnostic specificity and reduce outcome misclassification, only individuals with at least two separate NHIS insurance claims carrying the F00 code were considered to have incident AD. Follow-up continued until the date of the second F00 diagnosis, death, or 31 December 2023, whichever occurred first.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were assessed using data derived from the health check-up conducted between 2009 and 2013, alongside claims records from the NHIS database. Where patients underwent multiple check-ups during this period, data from the final examination were used. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. Systolic and diastolic blood pressures were measured by trained medical personnel using an automated sphygmomanometer, following a rest period of at least 10 min in a seated position. Fasting blood glucose, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transferase (γ-GTP), triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and total cholesterol levels were analysed using blood samples obtained after an overnight fast. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation.

Lifestyle behaviours were determined via self-reported questionnaires. Pack-years were calculated as the product of the number of cigarette packs smoked daily and the total years of smoking; participants were subsequently classified into four categories: <10, 10 to < 20, 20 to < 30, and ≥ 30 pack-years. Alcohol consumption was assessed based on self-reported drinking frequency and categorised as 0–1 day per week or 2–7 days per week. Exercise was defined by the weekly frequency of moderate- or vigorous-intensity exercise and categorised into 0–2 days/week and 3–7 days/week. Comorbidities were ascertained using ICD-10 diagnosis codes recorded in the NHIS insurance claims data. Hypertension was identified using code I10, diabetes mellitus using codes E10–E14, and dyslipidaemia using code E78. A comorbidity was considered present if the relevant diagnosis code appeared on at least two separate claims prior to the index date.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised as means (SDs) or numbers (percentages) and compared across smoking groups using analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Incidence rates were calculated per 100,000 person-years.

Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident AD were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Sustained quitters were analysed in two-year cessation intervals, and models were fitted using current smokers and never smokers as separate reference groups. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, total cholesterol, alcohol consumption, exercise, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia). In addition, as a sensitivity analysis, we conducted competing-risk analysis with the death during follow-up to investigate the risk of AD according to smoking status and duration of smoking cessation, using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard regression [19].

Sex-stratified analyses were additionally performed. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using log-minus-log survival plots and was not graphically violated. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Figure was generated using Python (version 3.12; Python Software Foundation) with matplotlib.

Results

A total of 1,403,636 participants was included in the final analysis: 1,103,294 never smokers (78.6%), 64,460 sustained quitters (4.6%), and 235,882 current smokers (16.8%) (Table 1). Never smokers were older on average (51.10 ± 12.02 years) than sustained quitters (46.38 ± 11.29 years) and current smokers (44.01 ± 10.86 years) (P < 0.001). Men predominated amongst sustained quitters (98.30%) and current smokers (98.05%), while women accounted for most never smokers (77.14%) (P < 0.001). At baseline, anthropometric profiles and laboratory findings differed significantly across groups, with higher mean BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, and liver enzymes in sustained quitters and current smokers than in never smokers. Among sustained quitters, smoking cessation duration was most commonly < 2 years (38.43%), whereas 8.36% had maintained cessation for ≥ 8 years. During a mean follow-up period of 10.50 ± 1.81 years, 58,519 incident cases of AD were identified. The crude proportion with AD differed across groups (P < 0.001), with 4.87% in never smokers, 1.67% in sustained quitters, and 1.56% in current smokers (Table 1).

Incidence rates

Overall incidence rates (per 100,000 person-years) are presented in Table 2. Current smokers had an incidence rate of 149.15 per 100,000 person-years. Rates were generally lower with longer smoking cessation duration, declining from 197.45 among those who quit < 2 years to 67.62 among those who quit ≥ 8 years. Never smokers had a higher crude incidence rate (463.54 per 100,000 person-years). In sex-stratified analyses, the same pattern was observed in men. In women, estimates for longer smoking cessation categories were unstable because of very small numbers of participants and events, including no events in the ≥ 8 years category (Supplementary Table 2).

Hazard ratios using never smokers as the reference

Overall, with reference to never smokers, the adjusted HR for AD was elevated in sustained quitters, but remained lower than that in current smokers in the total population (Supplementary Table 6). When smoking cessation duration was further examined, current smokers showed a higher hazard of AD, and sustained quitters who had quit for < 2 years also showed a significantly elevated HR compared with never smokers (adjusted HR 1.136; 95% CI 1.039–1.243), indicating residual excess risk shortly after smoking cessation. In contrast, among individuals who had quit for ≥ 2 years, HRs declined progressively with increasing duration of abstinence, and the risk approached that of never smokers (Supplementary Table 7).

In sex-stratified analyses, similar patterns were observed in men, with HRs among current smokers and short-term quitters, followed by progressive attenuation with longer cessation duration. In women, estimates were more variable and confidence intervals were wide, particularly in longer cessation categories, reflecting the small number of events and limited statistical power.

Among participants aged ≥ 65 years, current smokers remained associated with a higher hazard of AD compared with never smokers (adjusted HR 1.144; 95% CI 1.027–1.273; Supplementary Table 8). Sustained quitters aged ≥ 65 years demonstrated a similar pattern of risk attenuation with longer smoking cessation duration; however, associations were attenuated after multivariable adjustment and confidence intervals widened, suggesting reduced precision in older individuals. Despite this attenuation, the overall trend toward lower AD risk with longer duration of smoking cessation remained consistent with that observed in the total population.

Discussion

Main findings

In this large-scale nationwide cohort of over 1.4 million Korean adults, we observed a clear, graded association between the duration of smoking cessation and the risk of incident AD. Relative to current smokers, AD risk was significantly reduced even among men who had quit smoking for < 2 years and declined progressively with longer durations of abstinence, exhibiting a dose–response relationship (Table 3).

Although never smokers showed higher crude incidence rates driven by their older age and female predominance (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2), complementary analyses referenced to never smokers demonstrated that residual excess risk persisted among individuals who had quit smoking for < 2 years, whereas individuals who had quit for ≥ 2 years exhibited AD risk comparable to that of never smokers, with sustained abstinence associated with further risk stabilisation and a marked risk reduction among long-term quitters (≥ 8 years) (Supplementary Table 7). Together, these findings delineate a time-dependent trajectory in which smoking cessation confers early risk reduction relative to continued smoking, but at least 2 years of sustained abstinence appear necessary for AD risk to approximate that of never smokers, with longer-term cessation yielding additional risk reduction.

To our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to characterise this temporal pattern of AD risk reduction using finely stratified two-year cessation intervals. These associations remained robust after extensive multivariable adjustment and were broadly consistent in men and in older adults, whereas female-specific estimates were imprecise because of the small number of events and are therefore presented in the Supplementary Material.

Comparison with previous studies

Although numerous studies have investigated the relationship between smoking and AD, most have categorised smoking status simply as never, former, or current, without stratifying by time since cessation [2, 12, 20, 21]. Consequently, few studies have comprehensively examined the specific association with duration of smoking cessation.

One of the few longitudinal studies to consider smoking cessation reported no statistically significant reduction in AD risk for either short-term (< 4 years) or long-term (≥ 4 years) quitters. Moreover, cessation duration was categorised coarsely (≥ 4 years vs. < 4 years), and the study population was limited to older men [11]. Consequently, this approach did not allow for the assessment of a detailed dose–response relationship across finer cessation intervals.

Several meta-analyses have reported an increased risk of AD among current smokers [2, 22, 23]. While these studies suggest reduced risk among former smokers, these pooled estimates obscure heterogeneity within the former smoker category, which in practice includes individuals who quit months earlier as well as those who quit decades earlier, a limitation explicitly noted in prior work [2]. Our results suggest that such aggregation obscures a clinically meaningful gradient in AD risk by grouping individuals with heterogeneous smoking histories under an imprecise “former smoker” label. In contrast, by applying strict longitudinal definitions of current smoking, sustained cessation, and never smoking, and by quantifying cessation duration in discrete intervals, we were able to identify a clear time-dependent risk gradient.

The 2020 Lancet Commission on dementia prevention identified smoking as a major modifiable risk factor across the life course and concluded that stopping smoking, even in later life, may reduce dementia risk, although the temporal dynamics following cessation were not specified [9]. More recently, a large Korean cohort study examining changes in smoking intensity reported that smoking cessation was associated with a reduced risk of AD, whereas smoking reduction without complete cessation conferred no benefit and was even associated with increased risk [24]; however, that study did not evaluate the duration of sustained abstinence.

In contrast, our study adopts a conceptually distinct approach by focusing exclusively on AD, rather than all-cause dementia, and by explicitly quantifying the association duration of smoking cessation and AD risk using granular two-year cessation intervals. Notably, individuals who had quit smoking for less than two years already exhibited lower hazards of AD compared with current smokers, whereas risk among short-term quitters (< 2 years) remained higher than that of never smokers. This early divergence in risk, observed before long-term abstinence was achieved, has not been previously reported and suggests that partial reversal of smoking-related neurovascular or inflammatory processes may begin shortly after cessation. Importantly, when never smokers were used as the reference group, short-term quitters (< 2 years) remained at higher risk, whereas cessation durations of two years or longer were associated with progressively lower hazards, approaching or falling below the risk observed in never smokers in both the total study population and participants aged ≥ 65 years (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). To our knowledge, this bidirectional comparison—simultaneously contextualising short-term cessation against both current and never smoking—has not been addressed in prior studies of smoking cessation and AD.

Distinguishing Alzheimer’s disease from all-cause dementia

A critical methodological issue in the existing literature is the frequent use of all-cause dementia as the primary outcome, despite substantial heterogeneity in aetiology across dementia subtypes. AD differs fundamentally from vascular dementia and other dementias in its neuropathological trajectory, temporal latency, and interaction with vascular risk factors. Several influential studies and meta-analyses have combined AD with vascular dementia or unspecified dementia [2, 14, 22]. While such approaches improve statistical power, they complicate interpretation when investigating exposures—such as smoking—that exert both neurodegenerative and vascular effects.

By restricting our outcome definition to clinically diagnosed AD, we avoided conflation with other dementias and were able to examine smoking cessation in relation to a disease characterised by long preclinical phases and slow neuropathological accumulation. This distinction is particularly important given evidence that smoking-related mortality can introduce competing-risk bias in cohort studies [15, 22], because some smokers may die before developing or receiving a diagnosis of AD. However, in our competing-risk analysis using the Fine–Gray model, the results were very similar to those of the primary Cox models, suggesting that this bias did not materially affect the main findings.

Potential mechanisms linking smoking cessation to reduced Alzheimer’s disease risk

Although mechanistic inference is beyond the scope of this epidemiological study, existing evidence provides biological plausibility for our findings. Smoking has been linked to increased amyloid-β accumulation, tau pathology, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and blood–brain barrier dysfunction, all of which are central to AD pathogenesis [6–9]. Smoking cessation may mitigate these processes through several pathways.

First, smoking cessation reduces systemic and cerebral oxidative stress, which has been proposed as a key upstream driver of Alzheimer’s pathology [8, 9]. Second, smoking cessation improves cerebrovascular function and reduces chronic inflammation, potentially slowing the accumulation of Alzheimer’s-related neuropathology [9]. Given the long preclinical phase of AD, it is biologically plausible that meaningful risk reduction requires sustained abstinence over many years, consistent with the gradual risk attenuation observed in our study.

Sex-specific considerations

Consistent with smoking patterns in Korea, the prevalence of smoking among women in our cohort was very low, resulting in limited statistical power for sex-specific analyses in long-term cessation categories. Nevertheless, the direction of association in women was broadly consistent with that observed in men, with never smokers consistently exhibiting lower hazards of AD than current smokers (adjusted HR 0.818; 95% CI 0.724–0.926) in Supplementary Table 3.

In prior Asian cohort studies examining smoking cessation and dementia risk, women were frequently excluded altogether because of extremely low smoking prevalence. For example, one longitudinal Korean study limited its analysis to men only due to the extremely low prevalence of smoking among women in Korea [11]. As a consequence, evidence on the relationship between smoking cessation duration and AD risk in women remains scarce. Given the paucity of prior evidence on smoking cessation duration and AD risk in women, our results provide a starting point for future investigations designed to elucidate potential sex-specific trajectories of risk reduction.

Public health relevance

These findings support smoking cessation as a key strategy for AD prevention. While risk reduction was evident even within the first 2 years after cessation, the magnitude of benefit increased progressively with longer abstinence, with the most pronounced risk reduction observed among individuals who had quit for 8 years or longer. These results support public health recommendations that emphasise the dual importance of early cessation and durable, long-term abstinence [10].

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale, population-based study to comprehensively evaluate the association between smoking cessation duration and the risk of AD, based on a nationwide cohort of more than 1.4 million individuals with repeated health examination data from 2002 to 2023. The large sample size and extended follow-up period enabled a rigorous longitudinal assessment with substantial statistical power and enhanced generalisability.

Importantly, our study retained women in the analytic cohort, allowing assessment of smoking cessation in both sexes and providing rare population-based evidence on AD risk trajectories among female quitters.

Although smoking status was assessed using self-reported questionnaires, exposure classification was strengthened through the application of stringent longitudinal definitions. Sustained quitters were defined exclusively as individuals who reported current smoking at baseline and subsequently maintained complete abstinence across all follow-up examinations, without relapse. By excluding participants with inconsistent smoking patterns—including new smokers, transient quitters, and relapsed smokers—we minimised exposure misclassification and ensured that the sustained quitter group reflected sustained smoking cessation rather than temporary or unstable behaviour.

In addition, the use of granular two-year cessation intervals, rather than broader categories such as five or ten years, allowed a detailed characterisation of both early and progressive changes in AD risk according to duration of abstinence. This approach enabled identification of temporal patterns that would likely have been obscured by conventional smoking classifications.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, smoking-related variables were obtained from self-reported questionnaires and were not biochemically verified, which may have introduced social desirability and recall bias, leading to potential misclassification of exposure. However, the use of repeated assessments and stringent definitions of sustained smoking cessation likely mitigated this limitation. Second, smoking status in the NHIS health examination questionnaire was assessed using a general smoking question and was not restricted specifically to cigarette smoking alone. Therefore, although conventional cigarette smoking accounts for the vast majority of tobacco use in Korea, a small number of pipe or cigar users may also have been included [25]. Third, the timing of smoking cessation was estimated as the midpoint between the health examination at which participants first reported cessation and the preceding examination, introducing some imprecision in cessation duration estimates. Fourth, because smoking is associated with increased mortality, competing-risk bias is an important methodological consideration in studies of incident AD. We therefore performed a Fine–Gray competing-risk analysis treating death during follow-up as a competing event, and the results were very similar to those of the primary Cox models, suggesting that competing mortality did not materially alter the main findings. Nevertheless, some residual bias cannot be completely excluded in this observational setting. Fifth, exploratory analyses during model development suggested that pack-years, considered as a potential adjustment variable, did not materially change the HRs or 95% CIs. Likewise, adjustment for variables beyond age and sex resulted in little further change in the estimates. However, because pack-year distributions differed across smoking cessation-duration categories among sustained quitters, we cannot exclude the possibility that part of the observed risk gradient reflects differences in prior cumulative smoking exposure as well as smoking cessation duration itself. Sixth, individuals who successfully quit smoking may differ from continuing smokers in unmeasured health behaviours, healthcare utilisation, or health-seeking behaviour. Although we adjusted for several lifestyle and metabolic factors, residual differences in these characteristics may have influenced the likelihood of AD diagnosis.

Nevertheless, this approach is standard in longitudinal studies based on periodic health examinations and is unlikely to have substantially biased our findings. Finally, as the study population consisted predominantly of Korean adults, the generalisability of our findings to other ethnic or racial groups requires further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this nationwide cohort study demonstrates that the risk of AD declines progressively with increasing duration of smoking cessation. When current smokers were used as the reference group, AD risk was already reduced among individuals with short-term smoking cessation (< 2 years), indicating early benefits of quitting, and declined further in a dose–response relationship with longer durations of abstinence, with particularly pronounced risk reduction observed among those who had quit for ≥ 8 years. However, analyses referenced to never smokers showed that short-term cessation (< 2 years) was insufficient to fully eliminate excess risk, whereas sustained abstinence for 2 years or longer was associated with risk levels comparable to those of never smokers. This study highlights the importance of both initiating smoking cessation early and maintaining durable abstinence for AD prevention.
