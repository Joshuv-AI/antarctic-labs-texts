A nomogram for predicting the disease-free survival of patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yao Pan, Xiao-Jie Wang, Jia-Ping Zhou, Jie-Ni Yu, Ri-Sheng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate prediction of disease-free survival after radical resection in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains challenging. Therefore, this study establishes and validates a predictive model for disease-free survival (DFS) after radical resection in patients diagnosed with PDAC.MethodsThis study gathered clinical and preoperative imaging characteristics of 258 patients with PDAC who underwent radical resection at our institution. The patients were randomly allocated into a training set (n = 218) and a validation set (n = 40) at an 8:2 ratio. Survival outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with the log-rank test. A nomogram was developed following univariate and multivariate Cox analyses. The performance of the nomogram was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curves analysis (DCA). An X-tile analysis was conducted to identify three risk groups.ResultsBody mass index, preoperative carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) > 1000 U/mL, N stage, superior mesenteric vein involvement, and superior mesenteric artery involvement were independent influencing factors of DFS (p < 0.05). Subsequently, a predictive nomogram was developed. The C-index of the model in the training set and validation set was 0.66 (95% confidence interval (95% CI): 0.64–0.68) and 0.60 (95% CI: 0.53–0.67), respectively, with calibration curves indicating good agreement. DCAs demonstrated the clinical utility of the nomogram model. Additionally, the nomogram classified patients into three distinct score groups (low, medium, and high).ConclusionsWe successfully identified independent factors influencing DFS and developed a user-friendly and efficient prediction nomogram.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02301-7.Keywords: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, Nomogram, Prognosis, Radiography, Disease-free survival

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) ranks as the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Surgical resection of the primary tumor and regional lymph nodes is the most effective treatment for patients with PDAC, and postoperative chemotherapy enhancing survival rates compared with surgery alone [2]. Considering the higher incidence of toxic effects of FOLFIRINOX, Gemcitabine-based chemotherapy remains the most widely used standard first-line regimen [2–4]. Variations in patient prognosis during chemotherapy can be attributed to factors such as tumor cell drug resistance [5]. Ongoing research focuses on understanding tumor prognosis, with investigators pinpointing metabolic reprogramming as a critical driver of tumor progression and the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Various markers, including inflammation-based hematological markers, immunonutritional prognostic ratios, serum nutritional biomarkers, and serum exosomal hsa-let-7f-5p, have been linked to malignancy prognosis and postoperative outcomes [6–10]. However, the clinical acquisition of these indicators poses significant challenges. Hence, this study primarily relies on routine laboratory tests and imaging techniques to evaluate PDAC prognosis. CA19-9, a marker produced by exocrine epithelial cells, serves as a valuable tool for monitoring treatment response [11, 12]. CA19-9 levels exceeding 1000 U/mL may indicate poorer overall survival [13], while a 50% reduction or normalization of CA19-9 levels correlates with improved survival [14, 15]. Several imaging parameters, such as tumor size [16], the change in T1 relaxivity (ΔR1) between non-enhanced and contrast-enhanced equilibrium phases [17, 18], and the fractional extracellular space [19] during the equilibrium phase of computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have been identified as potential indicators of PDAC prognosis. Furthermore, the field of radiomics in PDAC represents a crucial research area [20, 21], highlighting the significant role of imaging characteristics in both diagnosing and predicting PDAC prognosis. This study primarily incorporates conventional imaging features to ensure the broad applicability of our model.

A nomogram that can assign relative weights to multiple predictors and provide a quantitative estimate of the probability of survival information [22] has been successfully used to assist in clinical decision-making [23]. In this study, we aimed to develop and validate a straightforward nomogram model using commonly employed radiographic and clinical data to predict Disease-free survival (DFS) in patients with PDAC after radical surgery, with the aim of devising strategies prior to commencing postoperative follow-up.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital, which waived the requirement for informed consent. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients with pathologically confirmed PDAC; (2) patients who underwent radical surgery; (3) patients with preoperative clinical and imaging (CT or MRI) data; and (4) patients with complete follow-up data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) limited data and (2) poor imaging quality. Finally, 258 patients from 2015 to 2023 were included in the study, which were randomly split into training and validation sets at a ratio of 8:2 (Fig. 1).

Patient flow diagram

Follow-up

Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the duration from the completion of curative surgery during which the patient survived without evidence of cancer recurrence or new primary malignancy. In this study, DFS was determined based on specific criteria: (1) absence of tumor recurrence confirmed by serial follow-up imaging studies, primarily abdominal ultrasonography, contrast-enhanced CT, MRI, chest CT, bone scans, and PET-CT. The imaging surveillance protocol included evaluations every 3 months in the first postoperative year, every 3–6 months in the second and third postoperative years, and every 6 months thereafter beyond the third year. (2) Absence of a second primary malignancy: Patients were monitored for the development of new primary cancers unrelated to the index PDAC; the occurrence of such an event was considered the DFS endpoint.

Clinical and pathological data

The clinical data include factors such as age, gender, abdominal discomfort, jaundice, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, drinking, Body mass index (BMI), preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) > 5 µg/L, CA19-9, and neoadjuvant therapy. Pathological data collected encompass the pathological differentiation grade, vascular invasion, nerve invasion, and the number of positive lymph nodes. N stages were determined based on the number of positive lymph nodes, following the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) eighth edition TNM staging criteria. Some patients in the study received postoperative adjuvant therapy, which included the mFOLFIRINOX regimen (oxaliplatin, irinotecan, leucovorin, and 5-fluorouracil), gemcitabine plus capecitabine, gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, gemcitabine monotherapy, S-1 monotherapy, and radiotherapy. Due to variations in treatment protocols, a wide range of regimens used, and incomplete data for some patients, adjuvant therapy was not considered as a collected variable in the analysis.

Imaging examination and characteristics

All patients underwent abdominal CT or MRI imaging before surgery. Contrast-enhanced CT examinations were conducted using multidetector-row CT (SOMATOM Definition Flash; Siemens Healthcare). The scanning parameters were as follows: detector configuration 128 × 0.6 mm, tube voltage 120 kVp, tube current 200 mAs, slice thickness 5 mm, slice interval 5 mm, and a pitch of 0.6 mm. A total of 120 ml of the contrast agent was administered into the antecubital vein using a pump injector at a rate of 3–4 mL/s. The arterial and portal venous phases were obtained at 40–50 s and 80–90 s after the injection of the contrast medium, respectively. The pancreatic MRI included dual-echo in- and out-of-phase imaging, breath-hold single-shot fast spin-echo T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), navigator-triggered fat-suppressed fast spin-echo T2WI, diffusion-weighted imaging using two b-values (0 and 800 s/mm2), and T1-weighted imaging before and after contrast agent injection. For dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, three-dimensional gradient-recalled echo imaging was performed before contrast injection and during the pancreatic phase (30–35 s after contrast injection using the bolus-triggering technique), Portal Venous Phase (65–80 s), and delayed phases (3 and 5 min). Gadoterate meglumine (0.2 mL/kg; Dotarem, Guerbet, Aulnay-sous-Bois, France) was used as the contrast agent at an injection rate of 2.0 mL/s followed by a 20-mL saline flush using a power injector.

Imaging variables included tumor location, tumor size, extrahepatic bile duct dilation, intrahepatic bile duct dilation, pancreatic duct dilation, and the involved vessels (portal vein, superior mesenteric artery involvement (SMA), superior mesenteric vein (SMV), splenic vascular, and intraperitoneal trunk artery). The criteria for vascular invasion were based on the degree of vascular contact of the tumor, and a contact surface greater than 180° indicated positive vascular invasion. In addition, T stages were determined according to the tumor size and vessels involved based on the AJCC eighth edition of the TNM staging criteria. All the images were independently evaluated by two experienced abdominal radiologists who were blinded to clinical outcomes and each other’s assessments. If consensus could not be reached, a third senior radiologist (with > 15 years of experience in abdominal imaging) was consulted to make the final determination. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistics. The kappa values for key imaging features ranged from 0.882 to 0.965, indicating substantial agreement.

Statistical analysis

Variables were summarized using descriptive methods, and survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier estimates and Cox regression. Continuous variables are presented as medians with ranges, and categorical variables as numbers with percentages. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was employed to create survival curves, and the log-rank test was utilized to compare survival outcomes among different groups. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis (P < 0.1) underwent multivariate Cox regression analysis to identify the final features (P < 0.05), which were then utilized to develop the nomogram. Variables with P < 0.10 were selected for inclusion in multivariate Cox regression based on both statistical significance and clinical relevance to ensure the inclusion of potentially meaningful predictors. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0 software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). A nomogram for predicting DFS was created using R 4.1.0 software (Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Vienna, Austria; http://www.r-project.org/). The discrimination and calibration abilities of the nomogram were assessed using the C-index and calibration curves. The curves were generated to evaluate the clinical utility of the model. X-tile analysis (https://medicine.yale.edu/lab/rimm/research/software/) and Kaplan-Meier curves were employed to determine the optimal cutoff value, dividing the data into three groups (low, middle, and high scores). The performance of the nomogram was validated using both the training and validation sets.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 258 patients with PDAC were included in this study, of whom 163 (63.2%) experienced relapse during the follow-up period. Among these patients, 141 (64.7%) in the training set (n = 218) and 22 (55%) in the validation set experienced relapse.

Nomogram construction

A total of 29 variables encompass clinical, pathological, and imaging features. P < 0.1 was set to be statistically significant in the univariate analysis to avoid eliminating valuable factors. Multivariate cox regression analyses

The DFS prediction model, utilizing the nomogram, was developed through Cox regression analysis. The nomogram indicated a unique score for each predictor, which was summed to derive the total score. Subsequently, the prediction probability increased proportionally with the total score (Fig. 2).

Nomogram for DFS prediction of patients with PDAC

Performance of the nomogram

The C-index of the model in the training set and validation set was 0.66 (95% CI: 0.64–0.68) and 0.60 (95% CI: 0.53–0.67). The calibration curve of the nomogram model illustrated that the predicted probability aligned well with the actual probability in both the training and validation sets (Fig. 3). The nomogram exhibited a net gain in clinical decision-making in both cohorts (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that the nomogram model had moderately prediction accuracy and clinical value. Additionally, the nomogram score for each subject in the cohort was computed, and X-tile analysis identified cutoff values of 13.4 and 18.1 to stratify the cohort into three score groups (S2). Table 3 presents the patient survival statuses in the low-, middle-, and high-scoring groups in both the training and validation sets. Survival curves for the three groups in both cohorts were generated using the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis method, and the log-rank test revealed significant differences in survival curves among the groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In the current study, we developed and validated a nomogram-based DFS prognostic model for patients with PDAC who underwent radical resection. Despite advancements in treatments such as surgery and chemotherapy, the median survival time for patients with PDAC remains limited. Estimating physicians’ DFS could greatly aid in postoperative patient management. A nomogram, a simple graphical tool, integrates various clinical and radiographic predictors by assigning relative weights to each predictor, offering a quantitative survival probability estimate [22–24]. Our nomogram, which we developed, demonstrates moderately predictive performance for DFS. Furthermore, patients categorized as low, moderate, and high risk based on the nomogram showed significantly different DFS outcomes (p < 0.001 and p = 0.0075 for training and validation cohorts, respectively). This highlights the model’s effectiveness in predicting postoperative outcomes in patients with PDAC. It also enables the identification of high-risk individuals (with low scores) prone to recurrence and metastasis, thereby facilitating the implementation of adjuvant treatments.

BMI reflects the nutritional status of patients before chemotherapy. Previous studies have had different opinions on the impact of BMI on pancreatic cancer prognosis. Fleming et al. showed that the proportion of lymph node metastasis, postoperative recurrence rate, and mortality in patients with a BMI > 35 kg/m2 were significantly higher than those in the control group [25], which cannot be proven in our study because there were no patients with a BMI > 35 kg/m2 in our training and validation cohorts. Some scholars believe that body mass index (BMI) is not related to survival. In our study, BMI was one of the factors finally included in the nomogram. We observed that patients with low BMI were more likely to have recurrence or metastasis, and with an increase in BMI, the possibility of recurrence and metastasis gradually decreased, which was similar to previous reports [26].

We found that preoperative serum CA19-9 level > 1000 U/mL were associated with worse postsurgical outcomes. In our study, patients with preoperative CA19-9 > 1000 U/mL were more prone to relapse or metastasis. This is similar to the findings of Hartwig et al. [27], they found that patients with CA19-9 levels > 1000 U/mL before treatment had a 5-year survival rate of 0, indicating a strong negative correlation between preoperative CA19-9 levels and prognosis. Besides, the HR of CA19-9 level > 1000 U/mL was 2.180 (95% CI 1.52–3.13), the second highest of all factors, with means that it has important predictive significance. Increased preoperative CA19-9 levels predict a higher tumor burden and shorter survival in patients with PDAC, which has been identified in many studies [28]. In neoadjuvant therapy, a relative or absolute reduction in CA19-9 levels after treatment can predict survival rates [11, 14, 29].

TNM staging is an internationally recognized standard for predicting prognosis. Previous studies have confirmed its importance in pancreatic cancer. The TNM stages of the patients were recorded according to the 8th edition of the TNM Committee on Cancer. The 8th edition primarily redefined the T and N stages compared to the 7th edition. The T stage was no longer limited to the pancreas and was divided into T1-3 according to the tumor size (2 and 4 cm). In T4, the easily influenced description of “unresectable tumor” was removed, and the criterion of common hepatic artery involvement was added. According to the number of positive lymph nodes, the original N1 stage was redivided into N1 (1–3 positive lymph nodes) and N2 stage (4 or more lymph nodes). The M-staging standard remained unchanged. In this study, univariate analysis showed that the T and N stages were correlated with patient DFS, but the results of multi-factor analysis showed that the N stage of the patient was an independent risk factor, suggesting that more attention should be paid to patients with a higher N stage during follow-up.

Previous studies have explored the predictive impact of radiological features on tumor chemotherapy prognosis, including tumor enhancement indicators, diffusion-weighted imaging parameters, and perfusion parameters derived from CT or MRI scans [30–32]. F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission computed tomography also holds promise; however, this technique is costly and susceptible to pancreatitis or biliary tract inflammation [33]. Hence, we opted for CT and MRI imaging as the primary imaging modalities. Furthermore, we primarily gathered routine imaging characteristics to enhance the practicality of our nomogram for clinical application. Following univariate and multivariate statistical analyses, the assessment of SMA and SMV involvement was integrated into the model, indicating that patients with involvement of these vessels had the poorest post-surgery outcomes. Prior research has indicated that SMA and SMV involvement are linked to PDAC prognosis. Ahuja et al. suggested that vein wall tumor invasion might serve as a more dependable predictor of oncological outcomes compared to traditional parameters [34]. However, the wide confidence interval for SMA (95% CI: 1.90–15.92) in our study suggests statistical instability, likely due to limited event numbers in the SMA-positive subgroup. While the point estimate indicates a potential strong association (HR = 5.506), the lower bound approaching the null value (HR = 1) warrants cautious interpretation. We recommend that this finding be considered exploratory, requiring validation in larger cohorts.

We acknowledge the moderate discriminative performance of our model, with C-indices of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.64–0.78) in training and 0.60 (95% CI: 0.53–0.67) in validation. Qin et al. developed a nomogram for predicting post-progression-free survival in patients with recurrent pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma after radical surgery, achieving C-indices of 0.739 (training) and 0.609 (external validation) [21]. Similarly, Xu et al. reported higher discriminative ability (0.789 training, 0.744 validation) using an MRI-radiomics nomogram for the prognosis of PDAC [35]. Our performance is comparatively modest; however, these existing models often require extensive parameter inputs or specialized laboratory markers not routinely available. Our simplified approach maintains acceptable discrimination while enhancing clinical accessibility. Crucially, we clarify that this model is designed primarily for risk stratification rather than precise individualized prediction. The moderate C-index (0.60–0.66) is acceptable for categorizing patients into broad risk groups to guide adjuvant therapy intensity and clinical trial enrollment, but insufficient for providing specific survival time estimates. The modest training-validation decrease (Δ = 0.06) suggests acceptable generalizability, though biological heterogeneity in PDAC inherently constrains predictability. We advocate using this model as a complementary decision-support tool for risk-adapted strategies, with future work focusing on multi-center validation and biomarker integration to enhance predictive precision.

Our study has several limitations. First, it utilized retrospective observational data, leading to potential selection bias and inherent confounding limitations. Second, the limited sample size may of validation cohort affect the precision of the C-index estimate. Based on simulation studies, C-index estimates with n = 40 events may have wider confidence intervals compared to larger cohorts. We observed a C-index of 0.60 (95% CI: 0.53–0.67) in the validation cohort, which overlapped with the training C-index of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.64–0.68), suggesting consistent discriminative performance despite reduced precision. Calibration assessment is particularly sensitive to sample size limitations. With 40 patients, calibration curves may exhibit Increased random variation in risk strata, especially in high/low risk tails, and overfitting to the validation data if calibration slopes/intercepts are adjusted. To address this, we reported calibration slope with bootstrap confidence intervals (1000 resamples). The model still requires further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts. Third, imaging features relied on common CT and MRI characteristics, thereby restricting the number of imaging factors considered. Additionally, owing to the heterogeneity of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy regimens and treatment protocols, alongside incomplete data for some patients, this study could not explore the specific association between individual adjuvant chemotherapy regimens and postoperative DFS. This limitation highlights the need for further investigations involving larger sample sizes and prospective studies.

In conclusion, we verified the prognostic factors of DFS and established a combined nomogram for predicting DFS, which incorporated BMI, preoperative CA19-9 > 1000 U/mL, N stage, and involvement of SMV and SMA. This prognostic tool may assist oncologists in identifying high-risk patients prone to recurrence and metastasis.
