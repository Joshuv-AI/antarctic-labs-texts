Cost-effectiveness of Pilates compared with home-based exercises for people with chronic non-specific low back pain: a trial-based economic evaluation
Authors: Caroline Ribeiro Tottoli, Ângela Jornada Ben, Everton Nunes da Silva, Judith E Bosmans, Maurits van Tulder, Rodrigo Luiz Carregaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on the cost-effectiveness of exercise therapies for chronic low back pain is scarce in Brazil. Despite the recommendation of exercise therapies by the Brazilian Ministry of Health, some therapies, such as Pilates, are not yet covered by the Brazilian healthcare system. The aim was to investigate the cost-effectiveness of a Pilates exercise program compared with home-based exercises in individuals with chronic non-specific low back pain. Design: Economic evaluation alongside a randomised controlled trial from a healthcare and societal perspective. Setting: Rehabilitation service. Participants: We recruited individuals aged 18–50 years with low back pain for ≥ 12 consecutive weeks, who were randomly allocated to either Pilates (n = 72) or home-based (n = 73) exercises groups. Interventions: Mat Pilates exercises with accessories supervised by qualified physiotherapists versus home-based exercises, twice a week, for six weeks. Main measures: Effect outcomes included: pain intensity (Numeric Rating Scale), disability (Quebec Back Pain Disability Scale), and quality-adjusted life-years (EQ-5D-3L). Costs outcomes included total healthcare and societal costs. All outcomes were measured at baseline, post-intervention, and six-month follow-up.ResultsFrom the healthcare perspective, a 1-unit reduction in pain intensity, disability, and quality-adjusted life year gained were associated with an incremental cost of $4, $1, and $61 for Pilates compared to home-based exercises under the time horizon of six weeks of intervention with six months of follow-up, respectively. From the societal perspective, a 1-unit effect gained was associated with a cost-saving of $295, $65 and $4,293, respectively. Pilates was considered cost-effective on both perspectives, compared to home-based exercises, on the current willingness-to-pay threshold of $15,500 (R$40,000)/quality-adjusted life year gained. Sensitivity analyses were consistent with the main findings.ConclusionPilates was cost-effective compared to home-based exercises for improving pain, disability, and quality-adjusted life year of individuals with chronic non-specific low back pain from the healthcare perspective and cost-saving from the societal perspective considering the threshold set by the Brazilian public health system.Trial registrationNCT03113292 (registration date 13/04/2017).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10202-7.Keywords: Economic evaluation, Back pain, Pilates exercise, Home-based exercise, Physiotherapy

Background

Low back pain (LBP) is a highly prevalent musculoskeletal condition, with an estimated 267 million new cases occurring globally each year [1]. This condition is a leading cause of disability and work absenteeism worldwide, accounting for approximately 64 million years lived with disability across all age groups [2–6]. Its global burden continues to rise, driven by population growth and ageing [7]. A substantial proportion of these cases progress to chronic non-specific low back pain, which is defined as persistent pain lasting more than 12 weeks without a known cause [2, 8]. This condition poses a significant global health challenge, affecting most adults at some point in their lives. Approximately 250 million people are affected by chronic non-specific low back pain each year, with around half seeking medical care [9, 10]. Chronic non-specific low back pain is therefore associated with substantial social and economic costs, and remains a major global public health issue [10, 11].

The cost of healthcare for treating low back pain has increased significantly. From 1997 to 2004, these costs rose from US$45.9 billion to around US$102.6 billion, reflecting an annual growth rate of over 12% [12, 13]. Furthermore, spending on outpatient services for chronic back pain rose by 129%, from US$15.6 billion between 2000 and 2001 to US$35.7 billion between 2006 and 2007 [14]. In 2021, it was estimated that LBP generated an annual total cost of around US$2 billion in low- and middle-income countries, equating to approximately US$1,226.25 per patient [15, 16]. Similarly, a 2023 study reported that annual direct costs associated with LBP in high-income countries ranged from €2.3 billion to €2.6 billion, underscoring the substantial global economic burden of this condition [16]. In Brazil, recent evidence has shown that low back pain is associated with significant health and societal costs [11, 17], with productivity losses accounting for around 80% of these costs. Furthermore, between 2010 and 2019, the national health system incurred annual expenses exceeding US$600 million [18], primarily due to low back pain, resulting in over 59 million lost workdays over a five-year period [17].

Physiotherapy interventions are the first-line treatment choice for many musculoskeletal conditions and therefore play an important role in the management of low back pain [19, 20]. Exercise therapy, such as Pilates, is widely recommended by international clinical practice guidelines for the treatment of chronic non-specific low back pain [20, 21]. Previous studies have shown the benefits of Pilates in improving pain and disability in people with chronic non-specific low back pain [22–28]. Recent systematic reviews have also suggested that, among different types of exercise, Pilates is more effective in improving pain and disability [29] compared with minimal interventions [30].

To the best of our knowledge, there is scarce evidence on the cost-effectiveness of exercise therapies focused on the management of chronic non-specific low back pain in Brazil [31]. With regard to Pilates, only one clinical trial with economic evaluation has been published to date, which examined the incremental cost-effectiveness of different weekly frequencies of Pilates exercises in people with chronic non-specific low back pain compared with booklet advice [32]. Furthermore, there is no study, at the international level, comparing the added value of Pilates with the home-based exercises that are usually prescribed. Although major international clinical guidelines strongly recommend exercise therapy, evidence on the comparative cost-effectiveness of different exercise modalities is limited. It is also worth noting that despite the widespread use of exercise therapies and their recommendation by the Brazilian Ministry of Health for the treatment of chronic non-specific low back pain, some therapies, such as Pilates, are not yet covered by the Brazilian public health system. Patients have to pay for Pilates exercises themselves (i.e. out-of-pocket payment). Therefore, expanding knowledge about the clinical and economic value of Pilates could provide relevant evidence to inform healthcare decision-making and resource allocation, as well as supporting the inclusion of evidence-based exercise programmes in public health policies. The aim of this study was to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of Pilates versus home-based exercise for individuals with chronic non-specific low back pain, in terms of pain intensity, disability and quality-adjusted life years, from both a health and a societal perspective.

Method

Study design, setting and location

An economic evaluation was conducted alongside a randomised controlled trial comparing Pilates exercises and Home-based exercises in a public rehabilitation setting in the city of Brasília, Brazil. The trial protocol has been prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03113292) and the protocol and effectiveness study have been published elsewhere [33, 34]. This trial-based economic evaluation was reported according to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluating Reporting Standards statement [35].

Study population

Inclusion criteria were: (1) male or female gender, aged between 18 and 50 years; (2) low back pain for more than 12 consecutive weeks prior to enrolment; (3) no Pilates or physiotherapy interventions for low back pain for at least six months prior to enrolment. Participants were excluded if they had: (1) a history of spinal trauma or fracture; (2) a diagnosis of spinal osteoarthritis, disc herniation or spondylolisthesis; (3) self-reported referred pain (e.g., abdominoplasty, appendicitis, abdominal and pelvic surgery); (4) previous spinal surgery; (5) presence of radicular symptoms (e.g., sciatica and cauda equina syndrome) classified as levels 3 and 4 by the Quebec Task Force [36] as levels 3 and 4; and (6) pregnancy.

After signing the informed consent, participants were randomly assigned into one of two groups: (1) Pilates exercises or (2) Home-based exercises. Briefly, the randomisation was done using a table of random numbers, and allocation was concealed by using opaque, sealed envelopes containing cards with the names of the interventions. The outcome assessors were not blinded because the outcomes were self-reported by the participants. However, the statistical analyst was blinded (i.e. the data was encoded). Detailed information can be found elsewhere [33].

Interventions

The full description of the interventions can be found elsewhere [34]. The rationale for the interventions is supported by previous research [27, 29, 37, 38] demonstrating meaningful improvements in individuals with chronic low back pain within this timeframe: a six-week intervention followed by a six-month period.

A qualified physiotherapist supervised the Pilates group face-to-face in a clinical setting, and the programme included Pilates exercises with accessories. In the first two sessions, participants were given instructions on the method and went through a familiarisation process. There were two sessions per week, sessions lasted 50 min and there was a maximum of 4 participants per session. If necessary, exercises were adapted and modified individually, taking into account the three levels of difficulty used in Pilates: basic, intermediate and advanced.

The home exercise group was supervised by another physiotherapist. The intervention consisted of general exercises such as postural exercises, muscle stretching and strengthening, and spinal mobilisation. These exercises were prescribed to be done twice a week for the same amount of time as the Pilates group, using a booklet with detailed descriptions and safety precautions to follow during the sessions. Throughout the intervention, participants from both groups were contacted weekly by email and/or text message for monitoring and to check for any adverse effects. During the weekly messages, the physiotherapist inquired about the participants' adherence to the exercise programme, whether they were performing the exercises as instructed and scheduled (twice a week). Additionally, a printed exercise sheet was provided to all participants to record their performance, adherence, and any adverse events.

Study perspective and time horizon

This trial-based economic evaluation was conducted from both a healthcare and a societal perspective. In the healthcare perspective, the analysis considered only the costs of the interventions and the costs of health care services provided by the public health system (i.e. values reimbursed by the Ministry of Health, to health care facilities) [39]. From a societal perspective, we added the costs of transport and caregiver support (i.e. informal care costs) and the costs associated with absenteeism and presenteeism (i.e. lost productivity costs) [39]. The time horizon was less than one year and consisted of six weeks of intervention with an additional six months of follow-up. Therefore, discounting of costs and effects was not required.

The trial-based economic evaluation provided information on healthcare utilisation by the patients enrolled in the randomised trial, as well as transport, caregivers, and productivity losses. For healthcare utilisation (e.g., medical visits, exams, and consultations) we used the values reimbursed by the Ministry of Health as available in the reference table for prices and reimbursement of the Brazilian public health system (in Portuguese, Sistema de Gerenciamento da Tabela de Procedimentos, Medicamentos, Órteses, Próteses e Materiais Especiais do Sistema Único de Saúde). For medication prices, we used the average prices paid by public health facilities available on the website provided by the Ministry of Health (health prices system – in Portuguese, Banco de Preços em Saúde), which has national coverage. For caregivers and productivity loss, we used the income reported by each patient included in the sample. Further details can be found in the Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Outcomes

Three outcomes were considered: pain intensity, disability, and quality-adjusted life-years. Pain and disability are relevant clinical outcomes for this population as low back pain is the leading cause of years lost to disability worldwide [2, 40, 41]. Moreover, quality-adjusted life years are commonly used as a concise measure of health in economic evaluations and are also recommended in Brazilian regulations [42, 43]. By considering the effects of interventions on both the quantity and quality of life [44], quality-adjusted life years enables decision-makers to assess and compare the effectiveness and cost-utility between interventions across different health conditions [45].

The main outcome of our cost-effectiveness analysis was quality-adjusted life years because they provide a standardised measure of health benefit that is used in policy and reimbursement decisions. Pain and disability were considered secondary clinical outcomes because they are core outcomes in low back pain research [46], demonstrating consistency and robustness of findings across clinically relevant measures. Data were collected by questionnaire at baseline, 6 weeks post-intervention and at 6 months follow-up.

To assess quality-adjusted life years, we used the Brazilian validated version of the European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 3 Level Version questionnaire [47]. This questionnaire assesses health states across five dimensions, including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Participants could choose from three response levels, indicating whether they experienced no problems, some problems, or extreme problems in each dimension. These health states were then converted into utility scores on a scale ranging from negative values (i.e., health states worse than death) to 1 (i.e., perfect health) using the Brazilian scoring system [47, 48]. The quality-adjusted life years calculation used the utilities for each of the time points studied, i.e. baseline (time point 0), post-intervention (6 weeks) and follow-up (6 months/24 weeks) were used [34]. Quality-adjusted life years were calculated using the area under the curve method [44]. In this approach, the average utility values between baseline and post-intervention and between post-intervention and follow-up for each participant were multiplied by the corresponding time periods (6 weeks = 0.12 years; 6 months = 0.5 years). The resulting values were then summed to obtain the total quality-adjusted life years. This calculation was performed on the imputed datasets using R-software, as described in the statistical analysis.

Pain intensity was measured by the Numerical Rating Scale [49]. This scale consists of numbers ranging from 0 to 10, where zero is equivalent to no pain and ten to the worst possible pain. Participants were instructed to indicate the number that best represented their average pain intensity during the previous week. To assess disability, we adopted the Brazilian version of the Quebec Back Pain Disability Scale Questionnaire [50]. This questionnaire consists of twenty questions that explore the level of difficulty individuals experience when performing their routine activities. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores representing a worst disability.

Measurement and valuation of resources and costs

Cost outcomes included total health care costs (i.e. health care utilisation costs—consultations, emergency admissions, hospital admissions, home care, drugs—and intervention costs) and total societal costs (i.e. health care utilisation costs, intervention costs, costs of informal care and costs of lost productivity). Costs were measured in national currency (R$) and later adjusted for purchasing power parity in 2022 (Int$ 1 = R$ 2.58), which was the latest year available from the World Bank [51] at the time of data analysis. The detailed description about cost categories and how the calculations were carried out are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Resources used in both interventions included the actual number of physiotherapy sessions completed by the participant (taking into account adherence) and the materials used in each session (i.e. sports fitness ball, Pilates resistance circle, Pilates foam roller, Pilates ball, elastic band and exercise mat). The unit cost of a physiotherapy session was based on the costs reimbursed to public health services by the Ministry of Health, available at http://sigtap.datasus.gov.br/. Material costs were based on market prices if the items were not available in the health pricing system. Intervention costs were calculated by multiplying the number of sessions used by the unit cost of a physiotherapy session plus the cost of the materials used (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). A specific form was used to measure health care use and informal care and was administered to participants at baseline, post-intervention and follow-up. Participants reported the frequency and amount of health care resources used in the previous 6 weeks (post-intervention) and 24 weeks (6-month follow-up) due to chronic non-specific low back pain.

Healthcare resources included the number of consultations with a health professional (physiotherapy and medical consultations, emergency and hospital admissions, number of home care visits, medication used, i.e. type, daily dose and frequency), transport (moves for consultations or emergency or hospital visits or to collect medication) and support from a carer or companion (hours spent by a carer or companion during the consultation due to low back pain and moves by a carer or companion). Unit costs of health care use (including unit costs of drugs) were based on national databases (http://sigtap.datasus.gov.br/) and the Health Price System (http://bps.saude.gov.br/). Unit costs for transport were based on market prices for petrol and public transport tickets: petrol price per litre; public transport considering the reference price of the city from ––– (round trip by bus or metro). The cost of hours spent by a caregiver or companion on low back pain was calculated based on the reported average wage (value of hour worked multiplied by the number of hours spent by a caregiver or companion on low back pain). Health care utilisation and informal care costs were calculated by multiplying the resources consumed by their respective unit costs.

Absenteeism was measured as the number of hours missed from paid work in the past 6 weeks and 24 weeks due to chronic non-specific low back pain. Presenteeism was measured by taking into account the value of the participant's hours worked, the number of hours worked per day, and the percentage of lost productivity at paid work (self-reported by the participant) in the previous 6 weeks and 24 weeks due to chronic nonspecific low back pain. The hourly wage reported by the participants was used to estimate the costs of absenteeism and presenteeism (i.e. lost productivity costs). Further details can be found in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle using RStudio version 4.2.1. Missing cost and effectiveness data (pain, disability, and utility values) were imputed using multiple chained equation imputation. Further details of the methodology can be found in the effectiveness study [34]. Briefly, we investigated the missing data mechanism and identified it as missing completely at random using Little's test. While complete case analysis could provide valid estimates under missing completely at random, we chose to use multiple chain equation imputation because it not only provides valid estimates, but also considers the uncertainty surrounding the imputed values [52]. Variables associated with missingness and outcomes, as well as potential confounders, were included in the imputation model for all baseline variables, including costs and benefits. To account for the skewed distribution of costs, predictive mean matching was used within the multiple equation chain imputation procedure. The number of imputed records was increased until the proportion of missing information was less than 5%, resulting in five imputed records [53]. The complete imputed datasets were analysed separately, and the estimates were grouped using Rubin's rules [54].

Differences in costs and effects between treatment groups at 6-month follow-up were estimated using seemingly unrelated regression. Significance was set at 5%, with a 95% confidence interval (95%CI). Covariates included in the model to adjust cost and effect estimates were group, age, sex, utility at baseline, cost at baseline, and low back pain prognosis (using the STartBack Screening Tool) at baseline. Costs are generally not normally distributed, so the joint uncertainty around differences in costs and effects was estimated using bias-corrected accelerated bootstrapping with 5,000 replications. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios were calculated by dividing the difference in costs between groups by the difference in effects, considering two perspectives (health care and societal). Two sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results. The first was a cost-effectiveness analysis including all randomised participants from both a societal and a healthcare perspective. The second analysis included only cases with complete data for all outcomes (pain intensity, disability and quality-adjusted life years) from both a societal and a healthcare perspective.

Uncertainty around the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was shown by plotting bootstrapped cost-effectiveness pairs on cost-effectiveness planes [55]. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves were also plotted, showing the likelihood of Pilates (new intervention) being more cost-effective than home-based exercise for a range of willingness-to-pay thresholds. The analysis used a willingness-to-pay of R$ 40,000/quality-adjusted life year (Int$ 15,500/quality-adjusted life year), the threshold defined by the National Commission for the Incorporation of Technologies into the Unified Health System in Brazil [42]. The effect for pain and disability was multiplied by −1 in the cost-effectiveness analysis to allow correct data interpretation in the cost-effectiveness plane.

Patient and public involvement

No participant was involved in the planning and development of this study. We plan to share the study findings with all participants and make them accessible to the public via popular science articles and social media.

Results

Participants

A total of 145 patients were included in the randomised trial. Of those included, 72 were allocated to the Pilates group and 73 to the home exercise group. As depicted in Supplementary Table 3, there were no clinically relevant differences in baseline characteristics between the groups. No adverse events were reported.

Participants in the Pilates group and the home exercise group had a mean age of 35.7 (SD = 9) and 37.1 (SD = 9) years, respectively. The proportion of male participants was comparable between groups (23% vs. 21%). The mean body mass index was 26.6 (SD = 4) kg/m2 in the Pilates group and 26.7 (SD = 5) kg/m2 in the home exercise group. SBST total scores (4.9 [SD = 2] vs. 4.6 [SD = 2]). Regarding SBST classification, the distribution across risk categories was comparable between groups, with approximately one-third of participants classified as low, medium, or high risk. More detailed information is provided in the effectiveness study [34]. In brief, 23 (16%) and 20 (14%) participants in the Pilates and home exercise groups respectively were lost to follow-up, although there were no relevant differences between the intention-to-treat and complete case analyses. In addition, 50% of the people in the Pilates group successfully completed about 70% of the sessions, while 50% of the people in the home exercise group completed at least 50% of the sessions. Sensitivity analysis showed no relevant between-group differences in adherence [34].

Effect outcomes

Further details on the clinical effectiveness results are presented elsewhere [34]. In short, Pilates exercises were considered more effective, as they resulted in statistically significant lower pain intensity and disability and higher utility scores than home-based exercises at post-intervention (6 weeks). In addition, there was a statistically significant overall positive effect of Pilates exercises compared with home-based exercises on disability (mean difference −4.4, 95%CI −7.6 to −1.1) and utility scores (mean difference: 0.049, 95%CI 0.022; 0.076), but not on pain (mean difference −0.077 95%CI −0.506; 0.352) at 6 months follow-up [34].

The outcome data at the follow-up time point were used in the cost-effectiveness analysis, as shown in Table 1. For the cost-effectiveness analysis, quality-adjusted life years were statistically significantly higher in the Pilates exercises group compared with the home exercise group (Table 1), but there were no statistically significant differences for pain and disability.

Total health care costs for Pilates did not differ significantly from those for home-based exercise (Table 2). Informal care costs associated with the use of a caregiver were $31.16 in the Pilates group compared with $29.57 in the home-based exercise group. Presenteeism costs were the largest contributor to societal costs in both groups. Lost productivity costs were not statistically different between the groups, nor were total societal costs.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

The main cost-effectiveness results are presented in Table 2. It should be noted that the cost-effectiveness point estimates may differ slightly from those presented in Table 1 and in the effectiveness study [34], as probabilistic statistical approaches were used for the (cost-) effectiveness analysis [57].

From a health care perspective, a 1-point reduction in pain score was associated with an average incremental cost of $4 for Pilates (i.e., more expensive, and more effective) compared to home exercise (Fig. 1). For disability, a 1-point reduction in disability score was associated with an incremental cost of $1 for Pilates (i.e., more expensive, and more effective) compared with home-based exercise (Fig. 2). In addition, 1 quality-adjusted life year gained was associated with an incremental cost of $61 for Pilates compared with Home-based exercises (Table 2). The probabilities of Pilates being cost-effective were 0.15, 1 and 1 (15%, 100% and 100%) compared to home-based exercise at WTPs of $0, $10,000 and $20,000/quality-adjusted life year gained, respectively (Fig. 3).

From a societal perspective, a 1-point reduction in pain was, on average, associated with savings of $295 for Pilates (i.e., less expensive, and more effective) in relation to Home-based exercises. For disability, the incremental cost-effective ratio indicates that a 1-point reduction in disability was, on average, associated with savings of $65 for Pilates (i.e., less expensive, and more effective) in relation to Home-based exercises. For quality-adjusted life years, Pilates was also dominant over Home-based exercises with the incremental cost-effective ratio indicating that 1 quality-adjusted life year gained was associated with savings of $4,293 for Pilates (Table 2). The probability of Pilates being cost-effective was 0.73, 0.95, and 1 (73%, 95% and 100%) compared to Home-based exercises at willingness to pay of $0, $10,000, and $20,000/quality-adjusted life year gained, respectively (Fig. 3).

The robustness of the cost-effectiveness analysis was confirmed by the sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

This study investigated whether Pilates exercises are cost-effective compared to home exercises. Our results showed that both interventions had similar clinical effectiveness in terms of pain and disability. However, Pilates exercises significantly improved quality-adjusted life years compared to home-based exercises, and this difference was considered clinically relevant (i.e. mean difference greater than 0.03) [58]. Although the cost of the home-based exercise intervention was significantly lower than that of Pilates exercises, the total health care and societal costs were similar. Cost-effectiveness analyses for pain, disability and quality-adjusted life years showed that from a healthcare perspective, Pilates exercises were more expensive but more effective than home-based exercises, whereas from a societal perspective, Pilates exercises dominated home-based exercises because they were more effective and less expensive. Our results also indicate a high probability of Pilates being cost-effective compared to home-based exercise at different willingness to pay thresholds. The results were confirmed in sensitivity analyses.

The cost-effectiveness of Pilates has been evaluated in a previous study comparing different doses of Pilates with advice (booklet) to treat people with chronic non-specific low back pain from a societal perspective [32]. Pilates exercises performed three times a week was considered the preferred option with a probability of being cost-effective of 0.78 at a willingness to pay of £20,000/quality-adjusted life year gained, and 0.85 at £30,000/quality-adjusted life year gained. However, Pilates exercises was not considered to be cost-effective compared to advice (booklet) for pain intensity and disability [32]. In contrast, our results showed that, from a societal perspective, Pilates exercises performed twice a week were cost-effective in terms of pain, disability and quality-adjusted life years compared with home-based exercises. In addition, our analyses showed that from a societal perspective, Pilates led to savings of about $4,000/quality-adjusted life year gained. The probability of Pilates being cost-effective was 0.90 at a threshold of $7,000 and 0.95 at $10,000/quality-adjusted life year gained.

It is widely acknowledged that the economic burden of low back pain is substantially influenced by the resulting loss of productivity [16, 17]. Our findings corroborate this aspect, as absenteeism and presenteeism were the main influencers of the cost outcomes. It can be argued that disability associated with low back pain represents a significant contributor to increased productivity losses [2, 59]. Furthermore, the poor prognosis of chronic low back pain due to psychosocial factors may also exert an influence [60]. It is important to note that the prevalence of disabling low back pain is rising in these countries, including Brazil [40, 61], which might explain to some extent the productivity losses. This may be attributed to a number of factors, including the prevalence of informal employment, the lack of adequate monitoring of occupational health policies and the weakness of social support systems [62]. In this scenario, the value of exercise therapy in the management of low back pain is becoming increasingly acknowledged [63, 64], which is particularly relevant for the context of low and middle-income countries. Moreover, recent evidence suggested that progressive physical activity was considered cost-effective from a societal perspective, and provided benefits such as a reduction in low back pain recurrence and back pain-related disability [65]. Systematic reviews investigating the cost-effectiveness of conservative treatments for low back pain, including exercise therapies, reported conflicting results due to heterogeneity, mainly in the cost valuation [31, 66–69]. Nevertheless, clinical guidelines [70–72] widely recommend exercise therapy as a first-line treatment for individuals with low back pain, and two systematic reviews demonstrated that exercise therapy, among other treatments, was considered cost-effective compared to usual care in individuals with subacute and chronic non-specific low back pain [66, 68]. Therefore, our findings provide high-value information on the benefits of exercise interventions applicable to the management of low back pain in a middle-income country, which also align with those of prior studies conducted in other countries [64, 65, 73]. These findings may prove beneficial for decision makers and health professionals in Brazil who are seeking to increase the utilisation of interventions that are recommended by guidelines for the management of individuals with low back pain.

In Brazil, the clinical management of people with chronic low back pain is mainly provided in primary care and outpatient clinics. However, a previous study demonstrated that individuals frequently use emergency departments and there is a frequent overmedicalization with unnecessary procedures (e.g., diagnostic imaging) [74], which has been strongly criticised by international clinical guidelines on low back pain [75, 76]. Our results are consistent with these recommendations, indicating that Pilates is a cost-effective intervention that can be utilised as an adjunctive strategy in primary care and outpatient settings in Brazil. Furthermore, approximately 65% of the Brazilian population is covered by family health teams, which represent the primary strategy for community health care in Brazil [77]. In light of these findings, it can be concluded that Pilates represents a cost-effective option from a societal perspective, particularly in comparison to the usual home exercises prescribed by physiotherapists.

The strengths of this trial-based economic evaluation include the random allocation of participants, allocation concealment, and intention to treat analysis. A further strength of this study is that it provides the first evidence on the cost-effectiveness of Pilates exercises in comparison with home-based exercises for the treatment of patients with chronic non-specific low back pain. Furthermore, a cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted from a societal perspective, encompassing all costs pertinent to the decision-making process (intervention costs, healthcare utilisation, informal care, and lost productivity costs) [45].

As a limitation, the results of this study may not be generalisable to health systems in other countries, given the existence of differences in terms of common practices and payment systems. A further potential limitation is the attrition rate (approximately 30%) at the six-month reassessment (follow-up). However, multiple imputation was employed to address the issue of missing data, and a sensitivity analysis was conducted using only complete data, which yielded comparable results, which mitigates this concern to some extent. Multiple imputation is widely regarded as the most appropriate method for addressing missing data [78]. In addition, the risk of recall bias cannot be excluded, as the recall period was relatively long (6 months of follow-up). This could have affected the accuracy of the self-reported data. Future studies could minimise this limitation by using shorter recall intervals. We also acknowledge the possibility of ceiling effects at the top of the scale in the EQ-5D-3L, particularly in healthy populations [79]. This could have limited the instrument’s sensitivity to detect subtle improvements. However, since the groups were randomised, these ceiling effects were evenly distributed between the treatment groups, thereby not affecting the cost-utility results. Future research could employ alternative measures with reduced ceiling effects or complementary qualitative assessments to capture a broader range of health states, and should also consider including comorbidity profiles as potential confounding factors or covariates to enhance the generalizability of the results.

Based on these findings, future research should replicate this study in other countries and in diverse health system contexts (e.g., public vs. private) to evaluate generalizability. Although the six-month follow-up was sufficient to assess medium-term effects, longer intervention durations or extended follow-up periods may be required to better capture the long-term retention and sustainability of Pilates benefits in individuals with chronic low back pain. Moreover, comparisons with other exercise-based interventions and subgroup analyses according to baseline disability, risk stratification (e.g., STarT Back), would provide valuable insights into which patients benefit most from this approach.

Our results suggest that Pilates exercise is a cost-effective alternative to home-based exercise for the management of chronic non-specific low back pain from the perspectives of both the Brazilian public health system and society.
