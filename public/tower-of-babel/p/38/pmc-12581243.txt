Multi-omics approaches investigate the bitter flavor in the shoot of Bambusa oldhamii Munro
Authors: Yulian Jiao, Nan Hu, Haitao Xia, Xiaowen Li, Jing Li, Yu Liu, Jinwang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBamboo, widely distributed in Southeast Asian and East Asia, is a palatable food resource, with its nutritional and low-fat properties, and functions in economic and ecological balance. As an important food source, some bamboo shoots often synthesize bitter chemicals in a special developing period or in a particular position of the shoot, which depends on the edible bamboo species. In this research, we focus on a sympodium bamboo, Bambusa oldhamii Munro, located in Southeast China, which has special biological properties. When the shoots are harvested underground, the shoots taste tender and tasty enough, but when the shoots erupt out of the soil surface with sunlight for days, the shoot has a little bitterness in the tip portion. This research conducts multi-omics approaches to investigate the bitter flavor formation with multiple developing phases of the shoot in B. oldhamii Munro.ResultsThe PacBio and Hi-C technologies were used to sequence the hexaploid species, B. oldhamii Munro, for de novo genome assembly. The assembled genome size of B. oldhamii is 1,446 Mb, with a scaffold-level N50 equal to 38,936,033 bp. A total of 88,140 genes in the genome were acquired, and about 53.89% of the genome is occupied by repeat components. The BUSCO score is 96.59%, suggesting a relatively complete and reliable assembly. Three subgenomes (ABC) include 35 pseudo-chromosomes based on the synteny analysis with another hexaploid species, Dendrocalamus latiflorus. The technologies of transcriptome analysis on the genome level and widely targeted metabolome were used to classify the key transcription factors and key bitter metabolites that account for the bamboo shoot flavor transition of B. oldhamii from underground to above ground, especially in the multiple developing phases. We finally mined out several bitter metabolites belonging to amino acids, flavonoids, terpenoids, purines, and other families, and several related pathways of Circadian rhythm, plant hormone signal transduction, flavonoid biosynthesis, and flavone and flavonol biosynthesis. Additionally, from joint analysis between bitter metabolites and transcription factors, we found that the family of bHLH and HB- transcription factors might influence the shoot bitter flavor transition and formation via influencing the accumulation of bitter metabolites in B. oldhamii.ConclusionsIn this research, we used Multi-omics methods to investigate the bitter flavor formation and transition in the shoot of B. oldhamii and hypothesized a simple model about this biological process. In a suitable circumstance (warm temperature and sunlight), the environment signals transduction influences the expression of transcription factor (bHLH, HB-, and others) which activate or suppress the expression level of genes or enzymes in the bitter flavor metabolites related pathways (biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, amino acid metabolism, biosynthesis of flavonoid, flavone, flavonol and isoflavonoid, plant hormone signal transduction and others), then causing the contents of bitter flavor metabolites (flavonoids, amino acids, terpenoids, cyanogenic glycosides and others) increase or decrease. This phenomenon influences the bitter flavor formation or transition in the shoot of B. oldhamii.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07529-z.

Background

Bamboos are a diverse group of flowering plants belonging to the Bambusoideae subfamily of Poaceae. Bamboo species have a strong basis of genomic studies over the years. The traditional classification of bamboo is based on the extension manner of rhizomes, including running bamboo, clumping bamboo, and mixed bamboo. Some records of genomic work were found among three categories of bamboo. Running bamboo (Monopodial bamboo), such as Phyllostachys violascens (Lei bamboo) and Phyllostachys edulis (Moso bamboo). The final genome length of P. violascens ‘Prevernalis’ (2n = 4x = 48) is 2.156 Gb, and it identified a total of 53,558 protein-coding genes [1]. The genome of Phyllostachys heterocycle (2n = 48) was assembled into scaffolds with a size of 2.05 Gb and identified 31,987 genes [2]. The advanced version of P. edulis (Moso bamboo) was assembled at the chromosome level and acquired 51,074 protein-coding loci [3]. Clumping bamboo (Sympodial bamboo), such as Bambusa oldhamii (Green bamboo) and Dendrocalamus latiflorus (Ma bamboo). The hexaploid genome of D. latiflorus assembled 70 allelic chromosomes (2.737 Gb) and annotated 135,231 protein-coding genes with allele-aware chromosome-level subgenomes (AABBCC) [4]. This research focuses on the genome and shoot flavor of sympodial bamboo, B. oldhamii (Green bamboo), which serves as an edible bamboo shoot in the southeast of Zhejiang Province, China, and has critical consumption and economic values. Mixed bamboo, such as Chimonobambusa utilis and Chimonobambusa quadrangularis, but until now, no nuclear genome sequence has been reported in the genus Chimonobambusa.

Bamboo shoots are popular vegetables used in Asian cooking for their nutritional properties [5–7]. However, some bamboo species were reported to produce a bitter flavor when the bamboo shoot is grown at a special developing phase or in a particular portion of the bamboo shoot, which limits the spread of bamboo shoot consumption. A lot of excellent researchers have investigated the bitter flavor in the shoots of different bamboo species. It has been reported that L-phenylalanine, uridine, L-tryptophan, and adenine are large bitterness flavors in the shoot of Pleioblastus amarus [8]. The content of amino acid, alkaloid, and flavonoid contributed to the shoot taste difference between Dendrocalamus brandisii and P. edulis [9]. The shoot of P. edulis harvested in winter is called “winter shoot” while in spring called “spring shoot”, hydrolyzed tannin and lignified components contributed to the bitterness and roughness of spring shoots respectively [10]. The total phenols, flavonoids, and bitter amino acids (phenylalanine, arginine, valine, isoleucine, and others) contribute to the shoot bitter flavor of P. amarus [11]. Cyanogenic glycosides are derived from aromatic (phenylalanine and tyrosine) and aliphatic (isoleucine, leucine, and valine) amino acids and the nonproteinogenic (cyclopentenyl glycine) amino acid [12]. Taxiphyllin was regarded as the main cyanogenic glycoside in different bamboo species. Taxiphyllin, with the properties of being unstable and thermolabile, easily decomposes to cyanide, which decreases with harvesting and processing [13].

Bambusoideae, including many bamboo species, the shoot shape, color, flavor, edible part, or the properties in each bamboo species were different. Some bamboo shoots have their best taste when grown out of the ground, such as Chimonobambusa quadrangulari, and some bamboo shoots have their best taste when grown underground, such as B. oldhamii (Green bamboo). The shoots of B. oldhamii (Green bamboo) emerged in the summer and have a vital economic value in the market because of their crisp, tender, and delicious taste when the shoots just emerge from the soil surface, and have the maximum edible portion. The bamboo shoots of B. oldhamii have a special fresh smell caused by methyl salicylate, methoxy-phenyl oxime, and other volatile aromatic constituents [14]. However, later harvested shoots will have a bitter flavor in the tip shoot of B. oldhamii when the green bamboo shoots erupt out of the soil surface, and with sunlight for several days, many researchers have analyzed these biological questions. The bitter metabolites of amygdalin, apigenin, arbutin, salicin, hypoxanthine, L-arginine, and solanidine could account for the bitter flavor transition when the shoot grows out of the ground [15]. In Dendrocalamopsis oldhamii (synonym of B. oldhamii), four key bitter chemicals (3-caffeoylquinic acid, arbutin, phenylalanine, and tryptophan) have been identified when the shoot grows from underground to above ground [16]. The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway [15–17], the circadian rhythm pathway [16], and others [16] might play a key role in different developing phases of B. oldhamii’s shoot.

Even though the bitter flavor of bamboo shoots and the debittering process have been searched for and applied a lot from distant times until now in different areas and countries. But the bitter flavor formation and regulation mechanisms of edible bamboo shoot were still lacking, and the genome sequence of B. oldhamii hasn’t been reported previously, which could solve many molecular biological questions on the genome level. In this study, we utilized multi-omics approaches to investigate the genomic and genetic basis for bitterness flavor of the shoot in B. oldhamii (2n = 6x = 70 ± 2). We obtained a chromosomal-level assembly (assembled with 1.45 Gb distributed in 35 assumed chromosomes), combined with a de novo functional gene annotation to study the general genomic structure. Key transcription factors linked to the bitterness metabolites were identified through co-analyses between the whole metabolome and transcriptome with different bamboo shoot developing stages of B. oldhamii. This research enriched the bamboo genome databases and laid the foundation for bamboo genetic and breeding research.

Methods

Plant materials for multi-omics research

The plant materials of B. oldhamii Munro (green bamboo) used in this research were collected from the local bamboo forest of B. oldhamii, which was planted by local farmers in Wenzhou City (Zhejiang, China). Prof. Jinwang Wang (one of the authors) identified the formal plant of Bambusa oldhamii Munro. We acquired permission from the local planter to pick the tissue samples of B. oldhamii and paid them. The leaf samples of B. oldhamii were used for genome size estimation and genome sequencing, and the different parts of tissues in B. oldhamii were used for genome assembly and annotation. For transcriptome and metabolome research, a total of seven developing shoot phases of B. oldhamii from the same bamboo forest of B. oldhamii were picked out to search for the bitter chemicals and key genes contributing to the taste transition from delicious taste to a little bitter taste. Phase I, renamed BoS1 (replicates with BoS1_1, BoS1_2, BoS1_3), are shoot bud samples whose edible parts were too small to eat. Phase II, renamed BoS2 (replicates with BoS2_1, BoS2_2, BoS2_3), the shoot hasn’t appeared on the soil surface, which has a major edible part and a delicious taste. Phase III, renamed BoS3 (replicates with BoS3_1, BoS3_2, BoS3_3), the shoot has just emerged out of the soil surface and has a major edible part and a delicious taste. Phase IV, renamed BoS4 (replicates with BoS4_1, BoS4_2, BoS4_3), the shoot has stretched out the soil surface about 5 cm, which has a little bitter taste in the tip. Phase V, renamed BoS5 (replicates with BoS5_1, BoS5_2, BoS5_3), the shoot has stretched out the soil surface about 10 cm, which has a bitter taste at the tip, and the internode cavity is visible. Phase VI, renamed BoS6 (replicates with BoS6_1, BoS6_2, BoS6_3), the shoot has stretched out the soil surface about 15 cm, and the internode cavity has enlarged. Phase VII, renamed BoS7 (replicates with BoS7_1, BoS7_2, BoS7_3), the shoot has stretched out the soil surface about 20 cm, and the internode cavity is enlarged and stretched. A total of 21 samples on behalf of seven developing bamboo phases of B. oldhamii were picked out and frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately and stored in a −80℃ ultra-low temperature freezer used for transcriptome and metabolome research.

Genome size estimation

Samples were placed in 500 μl Nuclei Extraction buffer, chopped with a sharp blade, then filtered through a 50 μm filter after 60 s. Followed by the addition of 2000 μL of staining buffer with RNase for 30 min in the dark. Nuclei suspension was analyzed by CyFlow Space Flow Cytometer (Sysmex Partec, Muenster, Germany) and the corresponding FloMax software. In this study, tomato was used as an internal standard for flow cytometry to estimate the genome size of B. oldhamii, carried out on Partec CyFlow Space. The Illumina sequencing was carried on in Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI) to estimate the genome size with GenomeScope 2.0 [18].

Genome sequencing and assembly

Library and sequencing were processed on PacBio® Sequel II HiFi (High fidelity reads) platforms based on the Circular Consensus Sequencing (CCS) model. Whole genome DNA with high quality was extracted and interrupted with Megaruptor, and the length of 13–16 Kilobase fragments was filtered out. The PacBio adaptor with hairpin structure was linked to the double DNA strand molecules, which then formed the SMRT bell library. When the library was constructed, the SMRT sequencing technologies were used to sequence the green bamboo genome on PacBio® Sequel II platforms with the Circular Consensus Sequencing (CCS) model. After sequencing, the data filtering was processed and assembled into a contig with Hifiasm [19]. Hi-C (High-throughput chromosome conformation capture) technology was used in this research [20]. After the experiments of Cell Culture, Chromatin Cross-linking, Cell Lysis, Chromatin Digestion, Biotin marking of DNA ends and blunt end ligation, DNA Purification, and others, the final Hi-C library was sequenced. The raw data was handled with quality control and acquired clean data with SOAPnuke (-n 0.02 -l 20 -q 0.4 -i -G 2 –polyX 50 -Q 2 –seqType 0) for data filtering [21]. The genome of B. oldhamii was processed with HiFi sequencing on PacBio Sequel II platforms and acquired 85.05 Gb of effective data from 4 sequencing cells. The Hi-C genome standard library was constructed and sequenced on DNBseq platforms. A total of 243.55 Gb of clean data were acquired from 254.29 Gb of raw data after filtering with SOAPnuke [21]. The clean data is mapped to the draft genome and processed for comparative analysis. Based on the comparative analysis and processed with cluster, sort, and orient, the draft genome was anchored on the chromosome with Juicer (default) [22] and 3D-DNA (default) [23], and evaluated with BUSCO (default) [24] against the embryophyta_odb10 database.

Genome annotation

Repeated annotation was based on Homolog sequence alignment and De novo prediction methods. The Homolog sequence alignment methods were based on the similarity sequences of RepBase [25] databases via RepeatMasker (default) and Repeat ProteinMask (default) [26]; the De novo prediction methods first built a repeat sequence database via RepeatModeler (v1.0.4) (default) and LTRharvest (default) [27], and then predicted with RepeatMasker (default) [26] and Tandem Repeats Finder (default) [28]. Gene structure prediction was combined with Homolog prediction, De novo prediction, and RNA-seq-assisted prediction. In the Homolog prediction methods, the software Exonerate (default) [29] and GeMoMa (default) [30]. The De novo prediction methods combined the results of Augustus (default) [31], GlimmerHMM (default) [32], SNAP (default) [33], Genscan (default), and Fgenesh (default). All the results were combined into the non-redundant and complete gene set via MAKER (default) [34] and EVidenceModeler (default) [35]. The annotation was combined with the annotated results of KEGG [36], Gene Ontology [37], InterProscan [38], SwissProt [39], TrEMBL, InterPro, and DIAMOND (default) [40].

Genome basic analysis

The identification of gene family was used with orthoFinder (v2.3.11) (-M msa -A mafft -S diamond) software [41]. The Single-copy orthologs (at least existing in 11 genomes or subgenomes) found by OrthoFinder were calculated to infer species richness. The information of five species (A. thaliana, B. amplexicaulis, D. latiflorus, O. sativa, and A. trichopoda) was used to assist in homolog prediction via the software of GeMoMa [30], Augustus [31], and SNAP [33]. The program MAFFT was used to progress the multiple sequence alignment, and RAxML [42] was used to construct the phylogenetic tree. Based on the phylogenetic tree results, the program of mcmctree in PAML software was used to estimate the species divergence time with the single-copy orthologs [43]. The calibration time was referred to the website of http://www.timetree.org/. The divergence time of O. sativa and A. thaliana is 130–200 million years ago (MYA), P. trichocarpa and A. thaliana are 100–120 MYA, which was filtered out as the calibration time. The genome synteny analysis used MCScan (–cscore = 0.7) to find the synteny blocks between genomes, and MCScanX [44] (-s 5 -e 1e-05 -a) was used to find the synteny regions inside the species based on the result of BLASTP v2.2.26 (e-value = 1e-10). The analysis of whole genome duplications (WGD) uses the 4Dtv methods, and 4Dtv stands for four-fold synonymous (degenerative) third-codon transversion. The software of CAFÉ [45] was used to estimate the expansion and contraction of gene families based on the phylogenetic results.

RNA-seq library construction and sequencing

After extracting the total RNA, mRNA enrichment and rRNA deletion were processed, then interrupted with a special buffer, and reverse transcription with random N6 primer to form the first-stranded cDNA and synthesize the second strands. Bubble adapter ligation of the double-stranded DNA amplified with PCR using special primers. The PCR products are heated into single strands and cyclized with bridge primers, and then sequenced to produce the raw reads. After removing the low-quality, adapters, and poly N (> 1%) reads with SOAPnuke [21], we required the clean reads and mapped them to the reference genome of B. oldhamii with HISAT [46]. All the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome gene sequences with Bowtie2 [47] and the expression of genes and transcripts acquired with RSEM [48]. For the RNA-seq data, the software of HISAT (default) [46] and StringTie (default) [49] were used to acquire the transcripts.

Abstraction and identification of metabolites

After all the samples were picked and stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer, 100 mg of each sample was weighed and put in the 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. In each tube, mix 1 mL 70% methanol aqueous solution and two small steel balls, then grind into powder with a tissue grinder (50 Hz, 5 min), stationary at 4 °C refrigerator overnight. After overnight centrifugation for 10 min at 4 °C (13000 rpm/min), the top liquid layer of centrifugal separation was filtered through a 0.22 μm filterable membrane and then processed with LC–MS analysis. Metabolite separation and identification were used with a UPLC (Waters, USA) series with a high-sensitivity mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA). Waters UPLC HSS T3 was used as a chromatographic column. The mobile phases were contained in 0.1% methanoic acid aqueous solution (buffer A) and 0.1% methanoic acid in 100% acetonitrile (buffer B). The elution was processed as follows: 0 ~ 2 min in 5% buffer B; 2 ~ 22 min in 5%−95% buffer B; 22 ~ 27 min in 95% buffer B; and 27.1 ~ 30 min in 5% buffer B. The flow rate is 0.3 mL/min, and the column temperature is 40 °C. The parameters of high-sensitivity mass spectrometer QTRAP 6500 Plus (SCIEX, USA) are as follows: metabolite source temperature is 500 °C, and metabolite spray voltage (IS) is 5500 V (positive mode) or −5500 V (negative mode). Metabolite source gas I (GS1), gas II (GS2), and gas curtain gas (CUR) are separately set at 40, 40, and 25 psi. The metabolite detection and identification were done using the MRM (Multiple reaction monitoring) model with Skyline (MacCoss Lab, USA) software and the extensive targeted metabolic standards database of BGI-Wide Target-Library.

Statistical analysis

The mass-spectrometric data were viewed with the software Analyst (SCIEX, USA), and the metabolites were quantified with BGI-WideTarget-Library. The differential accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were based on the condition of Fold-Change ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 0.83, and p-value < 0.05. The software metaX [50] was used for processing basic data statistical analysis, metabolites categories annotation, and function annotation [51–53]. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the joint analysis were based on the condition of Fold-Change ≥ 2 and p-value < 0.05.

WGCNA analysis

Co-expression analysis was performed using the weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) package in R under the guidelines of the published tutorials [54] combined with OmicStudio tools [55]. Genes with FPKM < 5 in 21 samples were filtered. Hierarchical clustering of the samples was conducted based on Euclidean distance computed on gene expression data, and integrated with the bitter metabolites. Modules were identified based on the dynamic tree cutting algorithm with the parameters of min Module Size at 50 and merge Cut Height at 0.25. For each module, the differentially expressed genes and transcription factors were determined, and the correlations between the expression of those genes with the accumulation of those metabolites were then subsequently calculated. Genes with high connectivity in the respective modules were considered hub genes. The co-expression relationships in modules were analyzed and visualized by Cytoscape [56].

Results

The biological characteristics of Bambusa oldhamii Munro

Clumping bamboo has sympodial rhizomes that are characterized by their rhizomes growing outwards from buds on a “mother” rhizome, eventually turning upwards to become new culms (Fig. 1a). The culm height of B. oldhamii ranges from 6 to 12 m, the culm diameter ranges from 3 to 9 cm, depending on the growth environment, the internode length ranges from 20 to 35 cm, and the wall thickness ranges from 4 to 12 mm, depending on growth conditions (Fig. 1b). There are multiple branches in each node of the middle and top culms in B. oldhamii, about 5 leaves in each little branch, and the leaf length ranges from 10–20 cm, and the leaf width ranges from 1.0 to 1.5 cm (Fig. 1c). The flowering period happens between May to November and includes bracts (3–5) and florets (5–9) (Fig. 1d). The shooting period is from May to November, the young edible shoots are sold as fresh vegetables on the market or marked as processed food, and the sheaths of the shoots are deciduous, leathery, dark brown spinous-hairy (Fig. 1e-g). The shoot of B. oldhamii had their best taste and maximum edible portion when they had not erupted out of the soil surface (Fig. 1h-j). When the shoot of B. oldhamii erupted out of the soil surface, the above-ground part of the shoot sheaths would be green with the sunlight and days (Fig. 1k). To comprehend this bamboo species more deeply, the genetic genome information of B. oldhamii was explored. Compared with the internal standard tomato with a genome size of 800 Mb and a peak around 51.03 using flow cytometric fluorescent detection (Fig. S1a), the estimated genome size of B. oldhamii is 1.375 Gb with a peak around 87.77 (Fig. S1b). The Illumina sequencing method was used to estimate the genome size of B. oldhamii around 1.33 Gb based on the 27 K-mer length (Fig. S2). Based on the above genome size estimation results, we processed the whole nuclear genome sequence with PacBio HiFi (40X) and HiC (100X) sequencing projects. After the PacBio HiFi and Hi-C sequencing of B. oldhamii, about 97.23% of contig sequences were anchored on the pseudomolecule chromosomes, and a total of 35 pseudochromosomes were acquired based on synteny analysis with the relative species D. latiflorus. The final Pseudochromosome genome was evaluated with the Chromosome Hi-C signal heatmap, corresponding to the Hi-C assisted genome assembly thesis (Fig. S3), and the final genome BUSCO evaluation is 96.59%.

The final assembled genome size of B. oldhamii is 1.446 Gb, the N50 of all the 1851 scaffolds is 38,936,033 bp, and the GC contents are 45% (Table 1). After the gene structure annotation, a total of 88,140 genes were acquired. All 88,140 genes were annotated in Nr, Swissprot, KEGG, KOG, TrEMBL, Interpro, and GO databases with rates of 76.87%, 50.46%, 49.24%, 47.25%, 76.70%, 62.48%, and 55.74% respectively. Finally, about 78.11% (68,843) of genes acquired the functional annotations (Table 1) (Table S1). The noncoding RNA, including miRNA, tRNA, rRNA, and snRNA, was annotated at the rate of 0.003297%, 0.043679%, 0.356499%, and 0.008666% respectively, on the whole genome (Fig. 1m) (Table S2). In the genome annotation of B. oldhamii, a total of 779,839,625 bp repeat genome sequences were acquired and occupied 53.89% of the genome size (Table S3). The basic genome analysis of B. oldhamii Munro

Through the identification and analysis of genome homologous genes and families can obtain single-copy gene families and multi-copy gene families. We statistics the families and genes in the following species: A. thaliana, A. trichopoda, B. oldhamii (subgenomes A, B, and C), D. latiflorus (subgenomes A, B, and C), O. latifolia, O. sativa, P. edulis (subgenomes C and D), P. trichocarpa, and R. guianensis (Table S5). Model of bitter flavor formation in the shoot of B. oldhamii

From the above joint analysis between bitter metabolites and transcription factors, we found that the expression of bHLH and HB- transcription factors was relevant to the accumulation of bitter metabolites might play roles in the biosynthesis and metabolic pathways of bitter metabolites. In this research, combined with our previous research [15], the mined bitter chemicals were searched against the KEGG pathway database, the bitter metabolites related pathways in each class were simplified and merged with flavonoids, amino acids, terpenoids, purine, solanidine, and hydrogen cyanide pathways (Fig. S17). The flavonoid bitter metabolites (Naringenin, Genistein, Genistin, Daidzin, Apigenin, Kaempferol, and Hesperetin) and other researched metabolites (Salicylate, Salicin, Arbutin, Amygdalin [15], Sinapic acid) are merged in the flavonoid-related pathway originally synthesized from bitter amino acid L-Phenylalanine (Fig. S17a). The bitter amino acids (L-Isoleucine, L-Leucine, L-Tryptophan, and L-Tyrosine) and arginine [15] merged in the amino acid biosynthesis and metabolic pathways (Fig. S17b). The terpenoid metabolites (Limonin, Ginkgolide A, Oleuropein, Nerol, Stevioside, Menthone, and Gentiopicrin) were merged in the terpenoid pathway originating from terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (Fig. S17c). Purine bitter metabolites (Caffeine, Theobromine, and Adenosine) and hypoxanthine [15] were merged in the Purine metabolism pathway (Fig. S17d). Solanidine and Amygdalin were detected in our previous research [15] were merged in the abbreviated biosynthesis of various alkaloids pathway (Fig. S17e) and the abbreviated cyanoamino acid metabolism pathway (Fig. S17f). Amygdalin is a kind of cyanogenic glucoside, but the majority of academics regard taxiphyllin as the major cyanogenic glucoside that exists in the edible bamboo shoot tip [57] and is easily decomposed to hydrogen cyanide (HCN), which causes a bitter flavor. The bitter amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, isoleucine, leucine, and valine) were substances for the biosynthesis of cyanogenic glucosides(Fig. S17f) in plants [12]. We consider that there are several kinds of cyanogenic glucosides that exist in the bamboo shoot, causing the bitter flavor transition, not just one kind of cyanogenic glucoside.

From the above analysis, we hypothesize a simple model about this research (Fig. 5a). In an appropriate environment, when the shoot of B. oldhamii grown out of the soil surface and accepted sunlight signals for days, the expression of bHLH and HB- transcription factors were activated or repressed which influence the genes’ expression in the related pathways (such as, Circadian rhythm; Plant hormone signal transduction; Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis; Flavonoid biosynthesis; Flavone and flavonol biosynthesis; and others) to determine the content accumulation variation of bitter metabolites (such as, flavonoids; amino acids; terpenoids; Purines and derivatives; Cyanogenic glycosides; and others), and finally causing the bitter flavor formation or transition in the tip shoot of B. oldhamii (Fig. 5a). There were exist diversity bamboo species in the world, and the bamboo forests have diverse usages, such as edible shoots collection, bamboo timber collection, city or road ornamental, germplasm preservation, soil improvement, nature reserve, and others. Multiple bamboo species and forests were used to produce edible shoots, which could be sold as fresh shoots or used to make processed food (Picked shoots, Salted shoots, Boiled shoots, Soused shoots, Canned shoots, Dried shoots) (Fig. 5b). The majority of bamboo forests were used for ecosystem balance and bamboo timber, such as bamboo weaving, bamboo charcoal, disposable tableware, bamboo fiber clothes, bamboo timber architecture, bamboo flooring, and bamboo crafts (Fig. 5c). This research was focused on the bamboo species of B. oldhamii, which is especially distributed in the southeast of China, and the shoots that emerged in summer are mainly used as an edible shoot locally. The hypothetical model about the bitter flavor formation or transition in the tip shoot is based on the Multi-omics data in B. oldhamii, which needs to be verified with deep research. Bamboo has a variety of species, and the shoot shape, color, taste, and properties of various bamboo species were different, and the edible portion or part was different depending on the bamboo species. Discussion

There have been reported many bamboo genomes and a complete bamboo database [58, 59], such as BambooBase, includes 18 genomes, 476 transcriptomes, 16 epigenomes, and 135 currently accepted genera of Bambusoideae [58]. Besides the traditional classification based on the extension manner of rhizomes, some researchers proposed a remarkable polyploid system of bamboo species drivers from plant evolution [59]. Herbaceous clades are usually diploid (2n = 2x = 20—24) including Olyra latifolia (Ola, 2n = 2x = 22), and Raddia guianensis (Rgu, 2n = 2x = 22); temperate and neotropical woody clades are usually tetraploid (2n = 4x = 46—48) including P. edulis (Ped, 2n = 4x = 48) and Guadua angustifolia (Gan, 2n = 4x = 46) [60]; and paleotropical woody clades are usually hexaploid (2n = 6x = 70—72), such as Bonia amplexicaulis (Bam, 2n = 6x = 72) [60]. This classification is not a fixed principle such as Bambusa odashimae, belonging to paleotropical woody clades, possesses about 104 chromosomes in karyotype study and genome research [61]. Thus, B. odashimae is a nonaploid bamboo species (3n = 9x ≈ 104) [61]. The hexaploid genome of D. latiflorus is assembled 70 allelic chromosomes (AABBCC) with a total genome size of 2.737 Gb [4]. Genome research of B. oldhamii (2n = 6x = 70 ± 2) in this study, similar to D. latiflorus [4], was assembled into 1.45 Gb and obtained 35 pseudo-chromosomes distributed in three sub-genomes (ABC), sub-genome A includes 12 pseudo-chromosomes, sub-genome B includes 12 pseudo-chromosomes, and sub-genome C includes 11 pseudo-chromosomes.

Genome sizes and protein-coding genes varied in different bamboo species [58]. As reported, the herbaceous bamboos clade including Olyra latifolia (639 Mb) and Raddia guianensisI (612 Mb) annotated 31,189 and 27,496 genes respectively [59]; temperate woody bamboo clade including Ampelocalamus luodianensisI (1,659 Mb), Hsuehochloa calcarean (1,298 Mb) and Phyllostachys edulis (2,036 Mb) annotated 48,870, 41,907 and 58,664 genes respectively [59]; neotropical woody bamboo clade including Rhipidocladum racemiflorum (1,711 Mb), Otatea glauca (1,448 Mb) and Guadua angustifolia (1,545 Mb) annotated 44,113, 43,141 and 47,971 genes respectively [59]; and paleotropical woody bamboos including Melocanna baccifera (1,077 Mb), Bonia amplexicaulis (850 Mb) and Dendrocalamus sinicus (1,439 Mb) annotated 51,908, 47,213 and 56,847 genes respectively [59]. The draft genome size of herbaceous bamboo Raddia distichophylla is about 589 Mb and annotated 30,763 protein-coding genes [62]. A total of 141,732 protein-coding genes were annotated in the genome of B. odashimae (3.36 Gb) [61]. The genome research of Dendrocalamus brandisii (2n = 6x = 70) with a total genome size of 2,756 Mb, identified 126,817 genes [9]. A total of 53,558 and 135,231 protein-coding genes were annotated in P. violascens ‘Prevernalis’ (2.156 Gb) [1] and D. latiflorus (2.737 Gb) [4]. In B. oldhamii (1.446 Gb), a total of 88,140 genes were acquired from annotation. We believed the genome research of B. oldhamii would lay a foundation for bamboo genetic and biological research, especially the following research associated with the bamboo shoot flavor transition from underground (tasty) to aboveground (a little better taste in the tip portion).

The bitter metabolites were major distributed in the family of amino acids, terpenoids, flavonoids, and others in this study. The bitter flavor amino acids include tryptophan, tyrosine, arginine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, phenylalanine, and others. In Pleioblastus amarus, L-phenylalanine, arginine, valine, isoleucine, and L-tryptophan have been identified as bitter components to influence the shoot flavor [8, 11]. The content of bitter amino acids (phenylalanine, valine, Leucine, and isoleucine) and alkaloids was higher in the edible shoot of P. edulis compared to D. brandisii [9]. The amino acid arginine has been detected in our previous research using three shoots developing phases of B. oldhamii [15]. Phenylalanine and tryptophan were identified in D. oldhamii (synonym of B. oldhamii) when the shoot grew from underground to above ground [16]. The amino acids of L-Isoleucine, L-Leucine, L-Tryptophan, and L-Tyrosine were identified in this research with seven shoots developing phases of B. oldhamii. Cyanogenic Glycosides are widely found in plants [63]. The bitter flavor amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, isoleucine, leucine, and valine) serve as precursors to produce cyanogenic glucosides [12]. Phenylalanine as a precursor for Amygdalin [12], Phenylalanine and Amygdalin have been detected in the shoot of B. oldhamii [15, 16]. But most researchers reported that taxiphyllin [13] is a cyanogenic glucoside majority exists in the edible bamboo shoot tip [57] and is easily decomposed to hydrogen cyanide (HCN) in boiling water around 2 h (boiling time varies depending on bamboo species [64]) for detoxification and make them fit for consumption [5, 57]. The content of phenolic compounds in bamboo shoots also decreased during heat treatment (boiled and stir-fried) [5]. Besides being consumed as fresh bamboo shoots for cooking, shoots can be consumed or processed with different methods (boiled, canned, pickled, fermented, dried, or others) [7], accompanied by lower bitterness and decreased HCN contents [5, 57]. The bitter flavor of shoots in B. oldhamii when grown out of the ground might be caused by kinds of bitter compounds and multiple kinds of cyanogenic glucosides.

The flavor and taste of each bamboo were different depending on different bamboo species. The contents of flavonoids were higher in the shoot of D. brandisii compared to P. edulis [9]. The total phenols and flavonoids contribute to the bitterness of P. amarus shoot [11]. The shoot of B. oldhamii has a special edible part and has a unique flavor when grown from underground to aboveground. Phenolic acids, lipids, and flavonoids were the main differentially accumulated metabolites in different developing phases of B. oldhamii’s shoots [17]. The pathways of plant hormone signaling, MAPK signaling, and Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis contribute to the Shoot Development of Sympodial Bamboo [65]. The pathways about the bitter metabolites to influence the flavor of bamboo shoot has reported a lot, such as bitter amino acid (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, leucine, isoleucine and others) [11] and phenolic acid [66] biosynthesis pathways in P. amarus, phenylpropanoids [15, 17] and flavonoid [17] biosynthesis pathways in B. oldhamii, hydrolyzed tannin biosynthesis pathway in moso bamboo (P. edulis) [10], the pathway of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, circadian rhythm, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and amino acid metabolism responsible for bitter compounds in the shoot of Dendrocalamopsis oldhamii (synonym of B. oldhamii) [16].

Even though numerous metabolites and related synthesis or metabolic pathways have been discussed contributing to the bitter flavor of bamboo shoots, there is rarely research on how the regulation mechanism works. Sunlight is an important environmental signal to influence bamboo shoot flavor transition and promote the shoot development, and shading treatment could reduce the bitter taste in the bamboo shoots of P. amarus via decreasing the accumulation of phenolic acids [66]. In this study, we find out that the families of bHLH and HB- (Homeodomain family in plants) transcription factors might play key roles in regulating or influencing the bitter metabolites’ accumulation in the shoot of B. oldhamii. The bHLH transcription factors function in photomorphogenesis, cell proliferation, cell elongation, and other aspects in Arabidopsis thaliana [67]. Phytochrome-interacting members PIF3 and PIF4, both bHLH proteins, form complex configurations and bind to the G-box (CACGTG) of DNA sequence in A. thaliana [68]. In grapevine, the bHLH protein VvMYC1 can interact with MYB proteins to induce promoters of the flavonoid pathway genes [69]. In cucurbits, the bHLH genes regulating biosynthesis of the bitter taste triterpenoids cucurbitacin [70] via regulated by two bHLH transcription factors, Bl (Bitter leaf) and Bt (Bitter fruit), in leaves and fruits, respectively [71]. In Medicago truncatula, jasmonate-inducible bHLH transcription factors, TSAR1 and TSAR2, conduct the biosynthesis of triterpene saponin [72]. In Lotus japonicus, MeJA-responsive bHLH transcription factor LjbHLH7 acts as an activator in regulating the biosynthesis of cyanogenic glucoside [73]. In almond, the bHLH2 controls transcription of the P450 monooxygenases, which are involved in the amygdalin biosynthetic pathway [74]. How do the environmental signals (sunlight, temperature, and others) or gene regulators (bHLH, HB-, and others) control the synthesis or metabolic pathways associated with the bitter metabolites’ accumulation or decrease in bamboo shoots? The details still need further research and investigation.

Conclusions

In this research, the technologies of PacBio and Hi-C were used to sequence and assemble the genome of Bambusa oldhamii Munro (1,45 Gb) distributed on 35 pseudo-chromosomes, and acquired 88,140 annotated genes. We used multiple bamboo shoot developing phases for metabolites detection and acquired bitter metabolites major distributed in the family of amino acids, flavonoids, purines and derivatives, terpenoids, and others. And from the joint analysis between transcriptome and metabolome, we acquired some important transcription factors in the bHLH and HB- families. We suppose that under specific environmental signals, such as temperature and sunlight, the key transcription factors in the bHLH and HB- families might be regulated by the environmental signals. The pathway (such as Circadian rhythm, Plant hormone signal transduction, Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, Flavonoid biosynthesis, cyanoamino acid metabolism, and others) might be regulated by the key transcription factors in bHLH and HB- families to influence the downstream contents of bitter metabolites, causing the shoot flavor changes from underground and aboveground. However, the mechanism of how the key transcription factors in bHLH and HB- families receive environmental signals and how the transcription factors regulating the bitter metabolites-related pathways are still in need of investigation and further research.
