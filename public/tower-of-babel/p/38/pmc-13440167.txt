Exploring nurses’ perceptions of cross-border mobility based on the cultural homology background: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Liebin Huang, Fei Xu, Angela Y M Leung, June Zhang, Renli Deng, Binbin He, Xin Wang, Miaoxin Huang, Ming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMuch literature discusses the migration of linguistically, culturally, and ethnically diverse nurses. However, little is known about nurses’ perceptions of cross-border mobility within culturally homogenous contexts.ObjectivesThis study explored the perceptions of Mainland Chinese nurses regarding cross-border mobility to Hong Kong and Macao Special Administrative Regions, using the capability, opportunity, motivation, and behavior (COM-B) model as a conceptual framework.MethodsUsing a qualitative descriptive design, semi-structured interviews were conducted between March and June 2024 with 16 nurses in nine cities in Guangdong Province, Mainland China. The interviews followed a predetermined topic guide developed based on the COM-B model. Subsequently, interview transcripts were subjected to deductive content analysis.ResultsWithin the three categories of the COM-B model, 9 key themes emerged, including capability (limited professional knowledge and skills, work language barriers), motivation (increasing income, greater career development opportunities, higher social status), and opportunity (government and healthcare organizational support, concerns about differences in nursing practices, consideration of family responsibilities, concerns about adaptation to life).ConclusionsAffected by differences in healthcare systems, Mainland Chinese nurses face certain adaptation challenges in cross-border mobility, but most hold positive attitudes and are willing to engage in short-term cross-border employment. Distinct from transnational nursing migration, language barriers to intra-China cross-border mobility among nurses mainly lie in language proficiency rather than cultural adaptation. Furthermore, the family-centered work ethic prevalent in Chinese society, underpinned by filial piety, constitutes an important factor influencing nurses’ cross-border mobility decisions. Chinese government authorities, healthcare institutions, and nursing managers should integrate these factors into management practices to provide targeted support for nurses’ cross-border mobility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14756-z.Keywords: Nurses, Nurse migration, Cultural homology, COM-B model, Qualitative

Introduction

The global shortage of nurses has emerged as a significant public health concern, with a forecasted worldwide deficit exceeding 4.8 million nurses and midwives by 2030 [1]. Projections indicate that the forthcoming shortage in nursing personnel will be aggravated by aging populations, heightened nursing demands, an aging nursing workforce, and sustained high turnover rates among nurses [2, 3]. Moreover, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic is expected to aggravate this shortage [4]. Under the combined influence of insufficient protective measures, heavy workloads, immense psychological pressure, and concerns about contracting the virus themselves or transmitting it to their families, a large number of nursing staff have chosen to resign [5].

To combat this nursing deficit, developed nations like the United Kingdom, the United States, Finland, and Japan are increasingly relying on supporting their healthcare systems through the migration of healthcare practitioners [6, 7]. In certain regions, migrant nurses can comprise up to 20% of the nursing workforce [8]. Main source countries for nurse migrants include developing nations such as the Philippines, Vietnam, Indonesia, India, and China in Asia [9, 10], alongside African countries like South Africa and Nigeria [11]. Consequently, the migration of nurses across borders commonly involves a transition from relatively resource-scarce to resource-abundant areas or nations [12].

In recent decades, research has unveiled numerous factors that act as facilitators and barriers to nurses’ cross-border migration [12, 13]. It is evident that migrant nurses do not form a homogeneous group, as nurses from diverse nations hold different expectations [14, 15]. While improved salaries, working conditions, and educational opportunities serve as common motivations for all migrant nurses [16], additional motivations vary due to differing political, economic, and cultural influences among nations. For example, the study by HA Yürümezoğlu and H Çamveren [16] research revealed that Turkish nurses’ migration was propelled by factors such as the nation’s unstable political climate and violence against women. South African nurses highlighted political corruption, high crime rates, racial tensions, and a high prevalence of HIV as crucial motivations for migrating to other countries [11]. Moreover, factors like flawed health policies, social rights, religious influences, existing recruitment agencies, and relevant national regulations all contribute to facilitating nurse migration [7, 17, 18].

However, when nurses choose to migrate, adjusting and adapting to a new work environment often poses various challenges including language, culture, and ethnicity [3, 19]. Barriers related to communication, cultural differences, perceived discrimination, racial bias, differences in nursing practices, and declining skills emerge as significant barriers to retaining migrant nurses [10, 20]. These barriers vary based on the origin and destination countries of the migrant nurses [10]. Notably, nurses from English-speaking nations may encounter fewer language barriers compared to those from non-English-speaking countries, especially when migrating to Europe or the United States [12]. Apart from workplace-related factors, aspects like geographical distance between the home and destination countries, challenges in securing permanent residency, overall quality of life, and favorable climatic conditions can influence migrant nurses’ willingness to remain in the host country [12, 14, 21, 22].

Despite extensive research on nurse migration, including studies involving migrant nurses from China [14, 20], the majority of studies have focused on the migration of linguistically, culturally, and ethnically diverse nurses [3, 6, 19]. This category of nurses is often referred to as culturally and linguistically diverse nurses [23]. However, with the ongoing trend of globalization and the increasing frequency of cross-border cooperation and exchanges, the concept of cultural homogeneity has become more important.

Cultural homogeneity refers to the shared cultural roots among different groups or regions. While influenced by historical factors that have formed a unique style, they maintain commonalities in core elements such as language, religious beliefs, values, customs, and habits [24, 25]. Cooperation between culturally homogenous neighboring countries faces fewer barriers. Given the current political landscape and the growing emphasis on maximizing efficiency and cost-effectiveness, cross-border migration exchanges involving cultural homogeneity hold great potential for the future. This trend is particularly notable in European Union countries. In China, this form of cross-border mobility is also developing rapidly and occurs within the country, presenting a unique scenario in China.

China, including the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) and Macao Special Administrative Region (MSAR), operates with a high degree of autonomy under the “One Country, Two Systems” principle. Residents of these regions must follow customs procedures for cross-border movements of people and trade information. However, this process does not involve migration between two distinct countries of origin and destination. Therefore, this study focuses on cross-border mobility, specifically referring to activities where nurses from Mainland China cross the border to provide medical services or engage in professional exchanges in the HKSAR and MSAR.

Moreover, the Chinese government is actively promoting the integration of the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA), which comprises nine cities in Guangdong Province, China, and a total of eleven cities in Hong Kong and Macao. The primary aim is to foster synergistic development among Mainland China, Hong Kong, and Macao, with a particular emphasis on the health sector [26, 27]. As a result, healthcare cooperation and exchanges among these regions have become more frequent.

HKSAR and MSAR, as leading international metropolises, offer nurses attractive job opportunities, salaries, working conditions, educational prospects, and family benefits comparable to those in other developed countries. Additionally, the cultural homogeneity shared by the three regions, along with their similar linguistic, cultural, and ethnic backgrounds, as well as convenient geographic proximity, significantly facilitates the re-adaptation and integration of nurses into new work environments.

As a result, the scale of cross-border practice of Mainland Chinese nurses to the HKSAR and MSAR is expected to continue expanding in the future, despite variations in healthcare policies and systems across the three regions. Data from the preliminary survey conducted by our research team also confirms this judgment: among 3,671 Mainland nurses, 39.5% expressed their willingness to work in HKSAR and MSAR [28]. This topic has become an important focus of research and policy formulation, and also an important supplement to the international research system on nursing talent mobility. Understanding the perceptions of Mainland Chinese nurses regarding cross-border mobility to the HKSAR and MSAR is of great practical significance for Chinese policymakers and nursing managers. It not only helps to reasonably guide nurse mobility to alleviate the shortage of nursing manpower in the HKSAR and MSAR, but also can promote the two-way transfer of nursing experience and professional knowledge by virtue of the advantage of cultural proximity, including attracting the return of nursing talents practicing in the HKSAR and MSAR, to achieve the joint improvement of regional nursing professional level.

To gain deeper insights into the perceptions of nurses from Mainland China regarding their willingness to travel to the HKSAR and MSAR for cross-border mobility, this study employed the understanding behavior framework (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behavioral model). This model is founded on three essential elements: capability, opportunity, and motivation, which interact to drive behavior. Capability refers to an individual’s ability to engage in relevant physical, mental, and skilled activities; opportunity pertains to external conditions that facilitate or enhance behavioral performance; motivation encompasses the various cognitive processes that guide and inspire behavior, including automatic aspects (e.g., habits and emotional responses) and reflexive aspects (e.g., planning and evaluation) [29]. By considering these three elements together, facilitators and barriers that influence the behavioral framework can be comprehensively identified. The COM-B model has been widely used to study nurses’ behaviors, such as exploring the factors that influenced the nursing behaviors of Chinese registered nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic [30].

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically explore the facilitators and barriers influencing cross-border mobility of nurses from Mainland China to the HKSAR and MSAR, using the COM-B model as the theoretical framework and cultural homogeneity as the core context, to provide empirical evidence for the management of regional nursing personnel mobility.

Methods

Study aims

To explore the perceptions of nurses from Mainland China regarding cross-border mobility to the HKSAR and MSAR, in the background of cultural homology.

Design

This study formed part of a broader mixed-methods study examining nurses’ perceptions of job retention and cross-border mobility in the GBA of China. The study used a qualitative descriptive design [31] with semi-structured in-depth interviews. Qualitative descriptive methods were chosen for their efficacy in capturing a detailed, direct, and firsthand insight into a phenomenon, particularly in situations with limited available information [32]. The COM-B model guided both data collection and analysis. The reporting of this study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist.

Participants and setting

The study employed purposive sampling to recruit nurses from nine cities in the Guangdong Province of Mainland China (specifically Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Foshan, Dongguan, Zhongshan, Jiangmen, Huizhou, and Zhaoqing, encompassing all cities in the GBA except the HKSAR and MSAR) from March 2024 to June 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) being employed as a registered nurse in a healthcare facility for more than one year; (2) holding a position involving direct patient care; and (3) having indicated willingness to engage in cross-border nursing practice in the preliminary study of our research team [28]. The criterion of having worked as a registered nurse for more than one year was based on the high turnover rate of newly graduated nurses during the transitional phase of their first year of employment [33]. To ensure diverse perspectives reflecting various perceptions, the study considered participants’ age, gender, marital status, and nursing work experience, since these factors have been identified in previous studies as key personal determinants influencing nurse migration [12]. Participants from Mainland China were selected from public tertiary care facilities with more than 1,000 beds.

The recruitment process involved the research team reaching out to the head of the nursing department at local hospitals in each city; 1–2 hospitals were chosen per city, resulting in the identification of 11 hospitals and 21 potential participants. Subsequently, the researchers contacted the nurses recommended by local liaison organizations via phone, email, or WeChat. From this pool, 16 eligible nurses volunteered to take part in the interviews. After identifying eligible participants, the researchers coordinated the interview logistics, determining the time, place, and format, following the acquisition of written or verbal informed consent. The sample size was established based on the principle of data saturation, ensuring that no new information emerged from the interviews. Following the analysis of a minimum of ten interviews, if three consecutive interviews failed to yield fresh insights, the interview process was concluded [34]. Adhering to these criteria, a total of 16 nurses were ultimately interviewed.

Data collection

This study developed an interview guideline based on the literature review and expert consultation. The guideline was refined through the results of pre-interviews with three nurses to form final interview questions aligned with the three essential elements embodied in the COM-B model. The guideline included: (1) Please share your current work situation; (2) Have you ever heard of cross-border work? From what sources did you learn about it? (3) What are your views on cross-border work? Do you support or oppose cross-border work, and what reasons inform your opinion? (4) If given the opportunity to work across borders, what main factors do you think would motivate or hinder you from doing so? (5) In your opinion, what actions should the government or relevant healthcare organizations take to assist nurses in working across borders? (6) Additional questions: Is there anything else you would like to share?

Data collection took place between March and June 2024. The interviews were conducted by the first and corresponding authors. The first author is a male registered nurse from Mainland China currently pursuing a master’s degree in nursing, and the corresponding author is a female PhD and professor at the school of nursing for over twenty years. She also possesses a decade of clinical experience and extensive expertise in qualitative research, particularly focusing on nursing management and education. The interview process prioritized the convenience of the interviewees. Depending on the situation, interviews were conducted via video phone or face-to-face, ensuring a quiet and interference-free environment for video calls and agreeing on the time and location for face-to-face meetings in advance. An audio recorder was used to record the entire interview and take notes. During the interviews, the order and content of the interviews were adjusted according to the participants answers to keep them relevant to the topic, and the non-verbal expressions of the participants were noted at all times and followed up with questions promptly.

Interviews were conducted in either Mandarin or Cantonese, depending on the participants’ preference. The average interview duration was 35 min, ranging from 28 to 58 min, and no participant exited during the study. After each interview, the researcher completed a memo and maintained a reflective diary to enhance interview quality.

Data analysis

After each interview, the audio recordings were transcribed into Chinese within 24 h. In addition, follow-up WeChat and phone calls were made with some participants to clarify unclear audio or transcribed data. The transcriptions were done verbatim, with the tone of voice and body language of the interviewees marked in the interview memos. Interview data were analyzed using directed content analysis [35]. Initially, two members of the research team independently and repeatedly read the transcribed text to gain an overall understanding of the data. The data were then deconstructed to select and code significant statements. Next, relationships and potentially contradictory content in the data were identified to further refine the coding and develop candidate categories, subthemes, and themes. A deductive analysis using the three basic elements of the COM-B model was conducted to categorize the themes and codes into the most relevant domains (capability, motivation, or opportunity). These were then returned to several participants for validation. Regular meetings between researchers were held to review and refine potential themes and subthemes. The themes were meticulously defined and refined to capture the key points of each interview and the specific data they represented.

During inductive coding and deductive categorization, if disagreements arose between two research members, a third member was consulted until a consensus was reached. This approach minimized errors, increased credibility, and enhanced confirmability [36]. The data analysis was performed manually with the assistance of Microsoft Word and Excel throughout the process. During the analysis process, the bilingual corresponding author translated the Chinese data into English to ensure an accurate reflection of the original meaning. Issues were then discussed with the study team member, who used English as their daily working language until a mutual agreement was reached. This process enhanced the accuracy, consistency, and credibility of the analysis [37].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the University Research Committee on December 14, 2022 (Approval No: RP/AE-06/2022/E01), and the research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [38]. Before the interviews, participants were briefed on a strict confidentiality protocol, which included ensuring anonymity, and the purpose and significance of the study. Informed consent was obtained from each participant, and they were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without facing any consequences. Furthermore, all recordings were securely destroyed after transcription to maintain confidentiality.

Rigor

To ensure the rigor of this study, strict adherence was paid to the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability in qualitative research [39]. Before conducting the interviews, the research team underwent standardized training in qualitative research methods. Throughout the research process, qualitative research specialists provided guidance. Purposeful sampling was employed to ensure a broad coverage and diverse perspectives of participants. Interviews were promptly transcribed and meticulously reviewed verbatim after each session. This rigorous approach maintained objectivity and minimized subjective bias, thus ensuring the authenticity of the collected information. To enhance the credibility and confirmability of the data, two researchers independently coded the transcribed data. Additionally, two experts in the field of care management within the research team were invited to review and validate the codes. The initial survey results were also shared with several participants for validation, further reinforcing the reliability of the study findings. Furthermore, a comprehensive description of the study context, participant selection criteria, participant characteristics, and data collection and analysis procedures were provided. This detailed information aimed to facilitate the transferability of the study findings.

Results

A total of 16 nurses were interviewed. The majority of participants were female (N = 15), married (N = 11), had children (N = 11), held a bachelor’s degree (N = 14), and possessed a senior title (N = 6). In Mainland China, nurses held titles such as nurse (junior), charge nurse (middle), associate nurse practitioner, and chief nurse practitioner (senior). The mean age of the participants was 35.25 years, with ages ranging from 22 to 48 years. On average, participants had 13.5 years of service, with the range spanning from 1 to 30 years. Participant information

The study identified 9 sub-themes under three main categories, aligning with the COM-B model. These sub-themes described the capability, motivation, and opportunity perceptions of nurses from Mainland China regarding traveling to the HKSAR and MSAR for cross-border mobility (Fig. 1).

The three main categories and the 9 sub-themes based on the COM-B model

Capability

Limited professional knowledge and skills

Some participants (N = 4), despite having a strong willingness to work cross-border, generally worried about whether their professional knowledge, skill reserves and academic qualifications could meet the nursing post access requirements of medical institutions in the HKSAR and MSAR. Among them, some participants had an associate degree as their initial academic qualification, and the undergraduate degrees obtained through follow-up online education have had limited recognition, which further exacerbated their concerns about insufficient personal qualifications. Although remarkable achievements have been made in the development of nursing profession in Mainland China and the nursing system has been continuously improved, compared with the relatively high post access thresholds and systematic professional training models in the HKSAR and MSAR, the interviewed nurses still had doubts about their professional competence, worrying that their existing knowledge structure and professional capabilities could not quickly adapt to the needs of local clinical work.

My abilities are limited, and my academic qualifications are not very high. I know there are differences in nurse access conditions and training models between Mainland China and Hong Kong and Macao, and I am worried that my academic qualifications and professional skills cannot meet the requirements of medical institutions there. I am afraid that when I work there, everyone else will be highly skilled, while I may be considered less professional. I fear that I won’t be able to keep up with others. If such an opportunity arises, I must enhance my abilities before considering working in such a place due to my current limitations. (Nurse 4)

Work language barriers

Work language barriers were identified as one of the main challenges to cross-border practice by the majority of participating nurses (N = 14). Although Mandarin is widely used on the Chinese Mainland, the dominant working languages in healthcare settings in the HKSAR and MSAR are Cantonese and English, which create a substantial communication barrier for nurses without proficiency in these languages.

My English is not good. (Nurse 5)

However, not all nurses were discouraged by language barriers; a small number of young nurses (N = 2) demonstrated strong willingness and confidence in overcoming language-related difficulties.

I believe that once you are in that environment, most challenges can be quickly overcome. For instance, after arriving in Guangzhou, I gradually picked up Cantonese and had no trouble communicating daily. Similarly, if I were to go to Hong Kong, I would pick up the language quickly for verbal communication. (Nurse 15)

Motivation

Increasing income

Increased income emerged as a significant motivator for the majority of participants (N = 12) who expressed a willingness to work across the border. Non-official data indicate that the average monthly income of registered nurses in Mainland China is around 5,325 RMB (≈$870). In contrast, the recruitment website provided by the HKHA reveals that most vacant registered nurse positions offer salaries above HK$35,000 (≈$4,375), while registered nurses in Macao earn around 20,000 MOP (≈$2487).This income gap, with salaries in Hong Kong being four times and in Macao more than six times higher than those in Mainland China, highlights the highly competitive nature of these job opportunities.

If I can really let myself work there, the first thing that attracts me is that the salary in Hong Kong is very high, which is twice or three times higher than my current salary, which can greatly improve my life. (Nurse 1)

However, there were also participants (N = 2) hailing from relatively developed cities like Guangzhou and Shenzhen who expressed that financial incentives alone were insufficient to entice them to work elsewhere, as they deemed the local nurses’ income satisfactory.

This side (Shenzhen) gives me enough money to spend. (Nurse 7)

Greater career development opportunities

Career development opportunities emerged as the primary motivation for all participants (N = 16) when contemplating cross-border work during the interviews. The majority believed that the medical standards in HKSAR and MSAR were notably more advanced, particularly in Hong Kong, where the professional standards of nurses were perceived to be higher. Working in these regions offered the chance to engage with diverse working patterns and healthcare systems, facilitating the acquisition of extensive professional nursing knowledge and skills. These experiences were seen as instrumental in bolstering nurses’ professional competitiveness and paving the way for enhanced career development.

If there is an opportunity to work across borders, I believe it is valuable for individuals. We will learn, gain experience, and take it back. Subsequently, we can apply this knowledge in our clinical work. This can also carry out improvements for mainland hospitals. I view it as a process of mutual learning and mutual advancement. (Nurse 13)

I think Hong Kong is more advanced, and I would like to study and learn about the advanced technology and services. Well, this is one of the reasons I want to go out, and the second reason is that I want to broaden my horizons, for example, to see the medical services in different regions. And then, if I can go out, I would also think about my personal growth. (Nurse 1)

Higher social status

The study findings revealed that some participants (N = 3) considered the pursuit of higher social status for nurses as a motivation for working across borders. In Mainland China, where public perception tends to elevate doctors above nurses, nurses are often seen as lower in rank and viewed primarily as assistants to doctors. This perception results in a lack of respect for nurses by patients and their families. Although nurse’s social status has been increased due to their vital roles in fighting against COVID-19, lingering biases remain, and nurses still encounter challenges regarding their social status.

I often read on the Internet that nurses in Hong Kong, Australia, and Germany are particularly well paid, that is, the working system is particularly good, nurses are also very respected, and their social status is also high. This is quite attractive. (Nurse 11)

Opportunity

Government and healthcare organizational support

Management support from both the government and healthcare organizations was a focal point for the majority of participants (N = 14). This support was seen as crucial, with a specific emphasis on the role of the national government and relevant healthcare institutions. At the national government level, participants highlighted the significance of recent developments, particularly the Chinese government’s strategy for the synergistic growth of the GBA. This strategic approach has led to the establishment of official pathways for Mainland Chinese nurses seeking cross-border work opportunities in HKSAR and MSAR. Previously, the absence of official government channels meant that nurses interested in working across borders had to navigate through individual connections or intermediaries, many of which were unreliable. This lack of formal channels undoubtedly presented significant challenges for nurses aiming to pursue opportunities across the border.

Now the government is leading the exchange program. We used to have few opportunities. Unlike before, it is much easier to work there now. (Nurse 13)

Before there were fewer opportunities. For example, as we learned from intermediaries, the cost was also relatively high. So we didn’t think too much about going abroad or to other regions. (Nurse 1)

Regarding healthcare institutions, nurses expressed a desire for comprehensive onboarding training programs and arranging probation periods to help them better adapt to the new environment. They also hoped that nursing managers would foster a positive organizational culture to ensure fair treatment and equity for cross-border nurses, alleviating their concerns about relationships with colleagues and patient attitudes.

The nursing licenses on both sides are not mutually recognized. The education we receive is not the same. In Hong Kong, everything is in English. The medicine is also in English. If no special training, it is too difficult for us to adapt. This is not realistic. (Nurse 9)

Initially, I think, I may need to check whether I am suitable for Hong Kong and whether there is anything I need to do to integrate into Hong Kong society, including the social environment and medical environment. There is also the acceptance of Hong Kong residents towards the work of Mainland medical personnel. This is also part of the social environment. That is to say, whether I can integrate, depends on factors such as the acceptance of Hong Kong hospital colleagues and leaders. These factors have a great impact on this work. It would be much better if the government and hospitals are willing to help us. (Nurse 12)

Concerns about differences in nursing practices

Concerns about differences in nursing practices were repeatedly mentioned by the many participants (N = 10). This is attributed to the varied healthcare systems resulting from the distinct political, economic, and administrative frameworks in the three regions. The nature of nursing roles in HKSAR and MSAR ‘s vacant nursing positions, along with variations in nursing roles across different regions, are key factors influencing their considerations about engaging in cross-border work.

If this cross-border work is to be implemented, what I would like to know is what kind of talents are lacking and what kind of nurses are needed in Hong Kong. Also, if we mainland nurses go there, what kind of work will we be engaged in? That is to say, if the work there is just very simple and basic care like brushing teeth and washing faces, that is something I am not willing to do. (Nurse 6)

I’m not sure what the nurses do over there. Is it the same as ours here? (Nurse 8)

However, there were also some participants (N = 2) who maintained a positive outlook on this matter. They believed that, overall, the healthcare environments were relatively similar, and from a technical standpoint, the differences were not substantial, making the adjustment manageable.

No matter what, some general things should be the same, such as skills, everyone does that work every day. (Nurse 12)

Consideration of family responsibilities

Family responsibilities emerged as a significant factor reported by many participants (N = 13), especially those who were married and had children. In Chinese culture, the family holds a central position and is considered the basic unit of society. Filial piety, respect for elders, and the continuation of family lineage are highly valued. For married nurses with children, their work considerations go beyond their own needs; they also need to take into account their families, including their spouses’ work commitments, and the education and care of their children.

Actually, I am quite willing to take on the challenge. But it may also be due to family reasons. If it were possible that I am not married and have no family ties, then I might be willing. If it is like now, suppose I am in my mid-twenties and have been working for one or two years. I would strive for it if there is such an opportunity. But I have children and a family now, there will be more restraints. It is a very real problem right now. (Nurse 11)

For unmarried nurses, as they grow older, the responsibility of caring for elderly family members becomes more significant. Coupled with pressures from relatives and personal aspirations for marriage, the prospect of moving to HKSAR and MSAR alone to start fresh becomes more challenging.

Because after all, I am at this age. I may be thinking about my major event in life (marriage) in the future. (Nurse 7)

If I don’t go out, I will consider family factors. Because if it is far away from my home, it is not easy for me to go home daily. Moreover, as family members get older, I will also think about them. If they need me, I hope I can be by their side. If I am outside, it is not easy for them to come over, and it is not easy for me to go home daily. Well, this may be the most important obstacle for me to choose cross-border work. (Nurse 6)

Nevertheless, given HKSAR and MSAR ‘s convenient geographical location, some nurses remain optimistic and are willing to pursue short-term cross-border work. If circumstances permit, they are also open to long-term commitments.

I think if I make a choice, I may choose a short-term one. (Nurse 3)

If all aspects are very appealing to me, long-term (cross-border work) is not out of the question. (Nurse 15)

Concerns about adaptation to life

Majority of the participants (N = 13) raised concerns about adapting to life in HKSAR as a barrier to their cross-border work. Hong Kong is known to be one of the most expensive places to live with crowded and costly living conditions. A report indicates the average living space per person is only 172 square feet [40], which is much lower than that in Mainland China and MSAR. Some participants preferred living in Mainland China. However, commuting to and from HKSAR would significantly lower their quality of life. Therefore, even with potentially higher incomes in HKSAR, the overall quality of life may not improve when considering the high cost of living. In comparison, opting for the MSAR for cross-border opportunities might present a more favorable choice.

That is to say, in terms of income, will the standard of living in the local community be higher than that in the mainland? Although the income may seem to have increased, considering the local prices, particularly, the housing will living standard improve? (Nurse 13)

Maybe if working over there provides an income that is enough for me to rent a house there and raise a family, I would be willing. Because for cross-border work, the impact on the quality of life is too great. I think one has to spend at least two hours commuting a day, that is too much and affects one’s own life. (Nurse 14)

Additionally, concerns include the adaptation to the multicultural environment brought about by HKSAR’s internationalization and the rebuilding of social networks, which are also worries for nurses.

There are also many foreigners in Hong Kong. I’m afraid I won’t be able to adapt myself. (Nurse 3)

That place is completely unfamiliar. There is no one familiar around, feeling like no place to release emotion. (Nurse 15)

Discussion

This study represents the first attempt to explore perceptions of cross-border mobility among nurses from Mainland China to HKSAR and MSAR under the background of cultural homology. The findings indicate that most nurses hold positive attitudes toward cross-border practice, particularly favoring short-term exchange programs, while long-term cross-border practice still faces multiple practical challenges including family responsibilities and lifestyle adaptation. This study provides a new perspective for understanding intra-China nurse mobility and offers empirical references for the management of regional nursing workforce mobility, optimization of nursing education, and promotion of medical cooperation, which carries important practical significance for improving the quality of regional nursing services.

The study identified limited professional knowledge and skills, together with work language barriers, as core capability-related factors hindering nurses from engaging in cross-border practice. Due to systematic differences in nursing licensing standards, training systems, and clinical practice models between Mainland China and HKSAR, MSAR, some nurses expressed concerns over whether their knowledge and skills could meet the job requirements of medical institutions in these regions. Existing research has confirmed that professional knowledge, skills, and language proficiency represent essential prerequisites for cross-border nursing practice [41]. Nursing practice in developing regions is often regarded as less developed than that in developed regions [42]. Therefore, clear recognition of personal capability gaps compared with destination regions and identification of pathways for capability improvement have become central concerns for nurses pursuing cross-border practice [19].

In this study, several nurses reported difficulties in acquiring Cantonese and English for work, which is consistent with language barriers documented in previous studies [3, 43, 44]. Such barriers increase the complexity of interpersonal and written communication [45]. Unlike previous studies on transnational nursing migration involving culturally non-homogeneous groups, the language barriers in this study were limited to linguistic knowledge and did not involve language-culture adaptation. Language-culture adaptation refers to the cultural, behavioral, and psychological adjustments experienced by immigrants during interactions with a new language and related culture [46] and represents a major capability-related obstacle for transnational nurses from non-homogeneous cultural backgrounds. Such nurses also need to address implicit barriers, including racial communication gaps and cross-cultural ethical conflicts [46, 47]. In contrast, the shared linguistic roots, cultural traditions, and ethnic background between Mainland China and HKSAR, MSAR significantly reduce the pressure of language-culture adaptation for cross-border nurses. This constitutes a prominent feature of intra-China nursing mobility and provides natural convenience for communication and collaboration between nurses from Mainland China and local patients and colleagues, laying a favorable foundation for their cross-border practice.

Nevertheless, deficiencies in nurses’ practical language application, medical terminology proficiency, accent adaptation, and ability to follow conversational speed cannot be ignored [48]. Targeted measures should be developed to address these issues. Relevant training is recommended to focus on practical language use involving common clinical medical terms, medical order transcription, and medication-related communication to reduce the negative impact of language barriers on cross-border nursing work [49]. In addition, when formulating recruitment standards, orientation procedures, and pre-service training programs for cross-border nurses, medical institutions in HKSAR and MSAR should fully consider the actual needs of this group and clarify specific training plans related to licensing criteria, language, and professional competence, to alleviate capability-related concerns among cross-border nurses [46].

In terms of motivation, increasing income, greater career development opportunities, higher social status are the main facilitators driving the cross-border mobility of nurses. Economic factors are widely recognized as the core driving forces for migration from developing regions to more advanced areas [18, 50], and the desire for income growth has also been confirmed as the primary motivation for nurse migration [51]. Compared with nurses planning transnational migration (culturally non-homogeneous groups), nurses in this study under the culturally homogeneous background have more focused migration motivations: the migration motivations of transnational nurses often include the pursuit of foreign living environments and social welfare systems, and they have to bear hidden costs such as cultural alienation and long-term family separation [47, 52]; while the migration motivations of nurses in this study mainly focus on career development (such as learning advanced nursing models) and income improvement. Benefiting from the advantages of cultural homology and geographical proximity, they do not face the hidden cost of long-term family separation, which also makes their willingness for short-term cross-border mobility may higher than that of transnational nurses.

While income differences between Mainland China, MSAR, and HKSAR influence nurses’ willingness to pursue cross-border work. However, for participants from Shenzhen or Guangzhou, this is not the case, as their income is already substantial, and their motivation is more focused on career development. The significance of career development opportunities for immigrant nurses has been repeatedly mentioned in previous studies [15, 18]. Similarly, all our participants emphasized that HKSAR and MSAR’s different work models and healthcare system would facilitate their professional growth.

As in other developing countries or regions [16, 53], Social status is the challenge faced by the nursing profession. Society lacks a clear understanding of the nursing profession, with stereotypes labeling it as “unprofessional” or “not mature” [53, 54]. In rectifying these stereotypical impressions, social media plays an important role.

In terms of opportunity, support from governments and medical institutions represents a key factor attracting nurses to engage in cross-border practice, whereas differences in nursing practice, family responsibilities, and concerns about life adaptation significantly hinder their willingness to pursue cross-border work. Compared with nurses from culturally non-homogeneous groups, nurses in this study under the culturally homogeneous background show obvious advantages in cross-border mobility opportunities: opportunity barriers faced by transnational nurses mainly include visa policy restrictions, difficulties in mutual recognition of foreign medical licenses, and cultural integration barriers [55]. Benefiting from the coordinated development policy of the GBA, government-led cross-border nursing mobility pathways have been gradually improved, which has significantly lowered policy barriers to license mutual recognition and cross-border mobility. This difference confirms the feasibility and convenience of nurse cross-border mobility under the background of cultural homology. In this study, support from the government, institutions, and nursing managers is what nurses collectively desire. Before the HKHA established a dedicated cross-border nursing pathway, most nurses relied on migration agencies for cross-border work consulting, which resulted in high training costs [14]. Furthermore, integration of migrant or cross-border nurses requires time and resources to adapt to new work environments and nursing roles, fostering a sense of belonging [56]. This process heavily relies on support from local colleagues, nursing managers, and the entire organization [57]. An umbrella review identified three key areas for this integration: (1) Internal organization: Creating a supportive work environment through anti-racism and anti-discrimination measures, language and professional development support, peer collaboration, and fair recruitment practices; (2) Socio-cultural aspects: providing cultural training and enhancement programs to support learning and integration; and (3) Professional development: focusing on licensing, job placement, career and skill development, and workplace mentoring [41]. These measures can provide references for the integration of cross-border nurses. Accordingly, strategies for the management of cross-border nursing talents can be improved by strengthening support and guarantees in the short term and perfecting institutional collaboration in the long term.

Concerns about nursing practices in this study mainly arise from uncertainties regarding the need for professional skills and unfamiliarity with new nursing practices. This is due to differences in healthcare systems, where HKSAR and MSAR adopt Western practices that emphasize patient-centered care, autonomy, entitlements, and strong communication, with a focus on fundamental nursing care [20, 58]. In contrast, Mainland China’s healthcare system is more physician-centered, prioritizing patient compliance and involving simpler nurse-patient interactions, with much of bedside care handled by family members, aides, or students [14, 58].This disparity poses challenges for Mainland Chinese nurses adapting to these novel aspects. It is hoped that institutions in HKSAR and MSAR can address these differences by developing clear guidelines and providing necessary resources to certify the professional knowledge of cross-border nurses, ensuring suitable positions, and offering support from the organization, managers, and colleagues to help bridge the gap in nursing practices.

It is important to note that the high number of married participants with children in this study has led to conflicts between family responsibilities and work, resulting in a lower willingness for long-term cross-border work. This may be linked to China’s family-centric culture, which emphasizes values like filial piety, such as caring for elders and prioritizing children [59]. The findings are indeed consistent with the current expectations of the Chinese government regarding mutual exchanges among nurses from the three regions to some extent. The partial alleviation of nurse shortages in HKSAR and MSAR, coupled with returning nurses benefiting from knowledge transfer, highlights the positive outcomes of cross-border exchanges.

However, short cross-border exchanges can waste resources for healthcare institutions in HKSAR or MASR and have other negative effects. The learning outcomes for nurses involved in these exchanges may also be uncertain. Therefore, the duration of cross-border exchanges is a key factor to consider when implementing nurse cross-border work.

In addition, concerns about adapting to new living conditions, particularly housing issues, are also important factors for participants in this study. Hence, offering accommodation for cross-border nurses in HKSAR or MASR would be an effective incentive, especially during their initial period of adjustment to the unfamiliar environment.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Grounded in the unique context of cultural homology between Mainland China and Hong Kong and Macao regions, it provides a new empirical perspective for research on nurse cross-border mobility under culturally homogeneous backgrounds, which differs from previous Western-dominated studies that mostly focus on cross-cultural issues such as language differences, cultural adaptation, racial prejudice, and immigration discrimination.

Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations. Although guided by the COM-B model, the coverage of its dimensions was not theoretically comprehensive, given the nature of qualitative descriptive research that focuses on core themes. In the capability dimension, the investigation mainly centered on professional knowledge, skills, and language barriers, without further exploring deeper capability domains such as psychological adjustment and role adaptation. In the motivation dimension, the analysis concentrated on explicit, goal-oriented occupational and economic motivations, without distinguishing the internal differences between automatic motivation and reflective motivation. Future research may optimize the research design by expanding the scope of participants, for example, including nurses with actual cross-border working experience, and improving research methods, for example, using the theoretical domains framework to develop interview guides, to supplement the model elements not covered in this study and further enrich findings on nurse cross-border mobility under culturally homogeneous backgrounds.

Conclusions

The cross-border movement of nurses within China is expected to become increasingly prevalent. Many nurses are willing to engage in cross-border work, particularly for shorter durations. Notably, all participants in the study cited career development as their primary motivation, indicating a strong allure towards diverse nursing practices. Moreover, the desire for increased income also serves as a significant driver for cross-border mobility. Language barriers, concerns about differences in nursing practices, and concerns about adaptation to a new life partially impede the flow of nurses across borders, emphasizing the necessity for targeted management support from governmental and healthcare organization.

In contrast to other studies, the work language barriers identified in this research pertains primarily to language proficiency rather than language acculturation. Additionally, the unique aspect of Chinese society’s family-centric work ethos underpinned by the culture of filial piety adds a distinct dimension to the challenges faced by nurses. These insights underscore the importance for government bodies, healthcare institutions, and care managers to integrate these factors into their management strategies. Formulating policies that facilitate cross-border exchanges and emphasizing the transition and adaptation of cross-border nurses to new work environments can provide crucial support encompassing organizational integration, professional development, and family life balance.

Future research endeavors should concentrate on addressing the work-related challenges encountered by cross-border nurses, aiming to enhance their integration and effectiveness in diverse healthcare settings.
