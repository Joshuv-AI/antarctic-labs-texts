Mapping the HPV Detection Landscape in Botswana: A Narrative Review of Diagnostic Platforms and Their Impact on Cervical Cancer Control
Authors: Leabaneng Tawe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimCervical cancer is a major public health issue in Botswana, driven by high HIV prevalence and limited screening access. Molecular detection of high‐risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) is crucial for prevention and early diagnosis. The aim of this narrative review is to synthesize studies evaluating seven HPV detection platforms used in Botswana, focusing on diagnostic performance, feasibility, and scalability.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, PMC, Google Scholar, and regional repositories up to July 2025 to identify studies evaluating HPV detection platforms in Botswana. Eligible studies included those assessing molecular HPV assays or genotyping tools in Botswana populations, reporting diagnostic performance or feasibility data. Data extracted covered study population characteristics, sample types, detection platforms, hrHPV prevalence, and performance metrics. Findings were qualitatively synthesized to compare platforms by sample type and application, as heterogeneity precluded meta‐analysis.ResultsThe Cepheid Xpert HPV assay, with near‐point‐of‐care capability, self‐sampling suitability, and high sensitivity (96%) and specificity (90%), is well‐suited for national programs despite costs of $15–$25 per test. AmpFire, costing $10–$15 per test, requires minimal equipment, offers good field feasibility (92% sensitivity, 88% specificity), and is promising for community deployment. Laboratory‐based assays like Roche Linear Array and Abbott RealTime deliver broader genotyping and > 93% sensitivity but require centralized infrastructure. PathoChip microarray detects novel HPV types yet is cost‐prohibitive (> $150/test). Double‐nested PCR aids retrospective formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) analysis but lacks practicality. These platforms collectively enhance cervical cancer prevention strategies in Botswana.ConclusionsThis review identifies feasible HPV detection platforms for Botswana's cervical cancer control. Cepheid Xpert and AmpFire assays offer accurate, affordable, and field‐ready options for early diagnosis and wider screening access. Integrating these molecular tools into existing HIV and reproductive health services could enhance prevention, early detection, and progress toward WHO cervical cancer elimination goals.Keywords: Botswana, cervical cancer, detection platforms, human papillomavirus, point‐of‐care testing

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer‐related morbidity and mortality among women in Botswana and the broader sub‐Saharan region [1]. In 2020, cervical cancer accounted for approximately 31.9% of all female cancer diagnoses in Botswana, with women living with HIV (WLH) comprising nearly 69% of cases [2]. Persistent infection with high‐risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) is the primary etiological agent of cervical cancer, and HIV‐induced immunosuppression significantly increases the risk of hrHPV persistence and progression to high‐grade cervical neoplasia [3, 4]. In response to this burden, Botswana has implemented a multipronged approach to cervical cancer control, including HPV vaccination (introduced nationally in 2015), visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) with “see‐and‐treat” protocols, and integration of molecular HPV testing into screening algorithms [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO)'s 90–70–90 strategy highlights HPV DNA testing as the preferred primary screening tool in high‐burden, limited‐resource settings [6]. Recently, various HPV detection platforms have been evaluated in Botswana, spanning point‐of‐care (PoC) assays to high‐resolution genotyping tools. The Xpert HPV assay (Cepheid), a cartridge‐based PCR platform suitable for near‐PoC use, has been piloted among WLH [7, 8].

One study demonstrated 92% overall agreement between self‐collected and provider‐collected vaginal samples with a 30% hrHPV prevalence in this cohort [9]. Additionally, AmpFire multiplex isothermal amplification, targeting 15 hrHPV genotypes, was assessed in indigenous western Botswana communities, revealing an hrHPV prevalence of 32.7%, with types 39, 51, 52, and 56 most prevalent in women with dysplasia [10]. Advanced genotyping methods have also provided critical insights. The PathoChip pan‐pathogen microarray used on cervical cancer biopsies (2013–2017) detected high rates of uncommon hrHPV types, HPV 26 (96%) and HPV 34 (92%), particularly among WLH, underscoring gaps in current vaccine coverage [11]. In Botswana, both the Roche Linear Array (LA) and the Abbott RealTime HR‐HPV assay have been used for HPV genotyping and screening, each serving distinct purposes depending on study design and resource availability [12, 13]. The LA is a reverse line‐blot hybridization assay capable of detecting 37 HPV genotypes, including multiple concurrent infections, making it particularly useful for epidemiological and research studies that require detailed genotype resolution [13, 14]. For example, Ermel et al. [13] used the LA to characterize HPV genotypes in invasive cervical cancer tissues collected in Botswana, revealing a predominance of HPV‐16 and HPV‐18 alongside other hrHPV types such as HPV‐35 and HPV‐58. Similarly, Tawe et al. [14] summarized multiple Botswana‐based studies employing the Roche platform to explore HPV diversity among women with HIV and cervical disease. By contrast, the Abbott RealTime HR‐HPV assay implemented on the automated Abbott m2000 platform detects 14 high‐risk HPV types, reporting HPV‐16 and HPV‐18 individually while grouping the remaining 12 high‐risk types as “Other HR‐HPV” [11]. This assay's automation, closed system, and relatively short turnaround time make it more practical for large‐scale clinical screening rather than detailed genotyping. Validation studies indicate that the Abbott RealTime assay demonstrates high clinical sensitivity and specificity for detecting clinically relevant infections [15]. In Botswana, the platform has been adopted in hospital‐based screening studies and public health programs for detecting hrHPV among women with cervical lesions or HIV co‐infection [11].

Despite this growing technological diversity, critical gaps remain regarding the comparative diagnostic accuracy, operational feasibility, and scalability of these platforms within the country's health system, particularly in resource‐limited and rural settings. This review seeks to systematically synthesize published evidence on all HPV detection platforms evaluated in Botswana, with particular focus on their sensitivity, specificity, cost‐effectiveness, and field applicability. Additionally, their utility was assessed across the screening‐to‐diagnosis continuum, including their roles in public health surveillance and research settings. By consolidating these insights, this review aimed to provide a robust evidence base to guide policy formulation, inform clinical practice guidelines, and shape research priorities in the ongoing effort to prevent cervical cancer in Botswana and similar high‐burden, low‐resource settings.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy

To ensure comprehensive coverage of existing evidence, a structured narrative review was conducted to synthesize literature on HPV detection platforms evaluated in Botswana. While some elements of systematic searches were incorporated to enhance transparency, the focus was on gathering, assessing, and thematically organizing relevant research rather than conducting a formal meta‐analysis. L.T. conducted a comprehensive literature search across multiple bibliometric databases, including PubMed, PubMed Central (PMC), Europe PMC, Google Scholar, and regional repositories such as the African Journals Online (AJOL). The search covered all available publications up to July 2025. Search terms and Boolean operators were used in various combinations, including: “HPV detection Botswana,” “human papillomavirus Botswana,” “HPV testing,” “HPV genotyping,” “cervical cancer screening Botswana,” “HPV assay performance,” and “HPV diagnostics Africa.” The search strategy was refined iteratively by screening reference lists of included articles to identify additional relevant studies.

Only peer‐reviewed journal articles were considered. However, gray literature (e.g., unpublished theses, institutional reports, or policy briefs) was not included, primarily due to inconsistent methodological quality, limited accessibility, and lack of peer review, which could compromise comparability across studies. This exclusion was justified to maintain data reliability and methodological rigor in summarizing assay performance and implementation evidence. Although additional journal databases such as Scopus or Web of Science could provide broader coverage, many Botswana‐based or region‐specific studies are indexed in PubMed, Google Scholar, and AJOL, which are more inclusive of regional and open‐access sources. Therefore, the selected databases were deemed adequate and contextually relevant for identifying the complete spectrum of HPV detection studies conducted in Botswana.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were conducted in Botswana or included Botswana cohorts, evaluated molecular HPV detection assays, platforms, or genotyping tools, reported diagnostic performance measures such as sensitivity, specificity, or agreement metrics, as well as feasibility or epidemiological findings, and were published in peer‐reviewed journals or recognized conference proceedings. Exclusion criteria comprised studies that did not involve Botswana populations, reviews, opinion pieces, or editorials lacking original data, and non‐English language articles without accessible translations.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

From each eligible study, data extracted included study design, population characteristics (HIV status, age group), sample type (cervical swabs, self‐collected vaginal samples, formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded [FFPE] tissues), HPV detection platform used, hrHPV prevalence, and diagnostic performance metrics (sensitivity, specificity, kappa coefficients). Additional information on operational feasibility, assay turnaround time, and implementation challenges were also noted when available. A qualitative synthesis approach was employed to compare the characteristics and utility of different HPV detection platforms. Given heterogeneity in study designs and outcomes, a qualitative synthesis approach was employed to summarize and compare HPV detection platforms by sample type, use case, and diagnostic performance.

Data Quality Management

To ensure data accuracy and reliability, all data extraction was performed by L.T. and independently repeated three times, given the limited number of studies on HPV detection platforms in Botswana. L.T. also assessed the methodological quality of included studies using an adapted checklist, evaluating key aspects such as study design, sample handling, assay validation, and completeness of reporting. This rigorous process ensured that only studies meeting a minimum quality threshold contributed to the narrative synthesis.

Data Analysis

A qualitative comparative analysis was performed to summarize assay performance across studies. We organized findings according to key outcome arms, including hrHPV detection rates, diagnostic accuracy, and operational feasibility. Descriptive statistics (proportions) were used to highlight trends, and thematic synthesis was applied to interpret implementation challenges and contextual factors affecting assay utility. This approach allowed for a systematic yet flexible comparison of HPV detection platforms relevant to the Botswana context.

Results

Search Results

A total of 1559 records were identified through database searches and an additional 9 records from other sources. After removing 770 records (767 duplicates and 3 removed for other reasons), 789 records remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 578 records were excluded, leaving 211 reports sought for retrieval. Of these, 30 reports could not be retrieved, resulting in 181 reports assessed for full eligibility. During full‐text assessment, 174 reports were excluded, mainly due to being conference abstracts or studies not involving HPV detection (n = 161), studies focused on specific sites (n = 5), or case studies (n = 8). Ultimately, seven studies met the inclusion criteria and reported the HPV testing platforms used (Figure 1).

Overview of Identified HPV Detection Platforms in Botswana

Literature search identified seven main HPV detection platforms evaluated in Botswana, each varying in methodology, sample type, and application context. These platforms include: Cepheid Xpert HPV assay, Abbott RealTime HR‐HPV assay combined with in‐house multiplex qPCR, PathoChip pan‐pathogen microarray, Roche Linear Array, Anyplex II HPV28 assay, AmpFire multiplex HR‐HPV assay, and double‐nested PCR.

Diagnostic Platforms for HPV Detection in Botswana: Applications, Performance, and Public Health Implications

Multiple molecular platforms have been utilized in Botswana to detect and characterize hrHPV infections, reflecting diverse needs across screening, clinical diagnostics, and molecular epidemiology. The Cepheid Xpert HPV assay, a cartridge‐based PCR platform, enables near PoC testing with a rapid turnaround (~1 h). Among WLH, it demonstrated 92% agreement between self‐collected and clinician‐collected samples with an hrHPV prevalence of ~30%. While well‐suited for screening programs, implementation is limited by cartridge supply chains and competition for GeneXpert devices shared with tuberculosis and HIV testing.

The Abbott RealTime HR‐HPV assay, targeting 14 hrHPV types, has primarily been applied to FFPE cervical and anogenital cancer tissues. Complemented by in‐house multiplex qPCR for refined genotyping, these tools have mapped genotype distributions in WLH [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Similarly, double‐nested PCR (MY09/11 + GP5/6 primers) has enhanced HPV detection in degraded FFPE samples, ensuring accurate prevalence estimates [16]. The PathoChip pan‐pathogen microarray, though primarily research‐focused due to high cost and technical demands, revealed strikingly high rates of HPV‐26 (96%) and HPV‐34 (92%) in cervical cancer biopsies, genotypes not covered by current vaccines [17]. For population‐based surveillance, the Roche LA PCR assay and Anyplex II HPV28 assay have provided broad genotyping capabilities. These platforms reported hrHPV detection rates of 79%–92% across young women and invasive cervical cancer cases, underscoring their utility for molecular epidemiology [10, 13, 19]. In low‐resource and field‐based contexts, the AmpFire Multiplex HR‐HPV assay, utilizing isothermal amplification, has shown hrHPV prevalence of 32.7% in rural Botswana, with types ‐39, ‐51, ‐52, and 56 predominating over the globally common HPV‐16 and HPV‐18 [10]. Its portability and minimal infrastructure requirements make it attractive for decentralized screening.

Cost and Operational Comparison of HPV Detection Platforms

Building on the platform overview in Tables 1 and 2 compares the cost and operational characteristics of HPV detection platforms currently used or evaluated in Botswana. The Cepheid Xpert HPV assay, despite its higher cost per test ($15–$25), remains highly suitable for PoC use due to its rapid ~1 h turnaround and compatibility with self‐collected samples, making it particularly efficient when integrated with existing TB/HIV GeneXpert networks. To address competition for these devices across programs, several policy strategies could be considered, including scheduled time‐sharing between TB/HIV and HPV testing, establishing dedicated HPV testing modules within high‐volume GeneXpert sites, and leveraging mobile GeneXpert units for outreach in rural areas. In contrast, low‐cost methods like double‐nested PCR ($5–$10) offer affordability for retrospective analyses but have low throughput and long turnaround times (6–8 h), limiting their utility in routine screening. AmpFire Multiplex, at $10–$15 per test, emerges as a cost‐effective option for rural and low‐resource settings, combining minimal equipment needs with field‐friendly operation (Figure 2) and could be deployed in parallel with GeneXpert platforms to relieve device‐sharing pressures.

Discussion

This review underscores the dynamic and evolving landscape of HPV detection platforms in Botswana, reflecting the nation's commitment to reducing cervical cancer incidence amidst a high HIV burden and constrained healthcare resources. The variety of molecular and genotyping assays identified, ranging from rapid, PoC tools to centralized, high‐throughput laboratory platforms, illustrates a pragmatic approach to optimizing HPV detection at multiple levels of the healthcare system. The Cepheid Xpert HPV assay emerges as a cornerstone for frontline screening due to its rapid turnaround time (~1 h), ease of use, and compatibility with self‐collected samples. With reported sensitivity and specificity of approximately 96% and 90%, respectively, and an estimated cost of $15–$25 per test, the Xpert assay aligns with Botswana's goal of increasing screening coverage, particularly among WLH who face elevated risks for persistent hrHPV infections and accelerated progression to cervical cancer [4]. However, operational constraints, particularly limited device availability, cartridge supply chain disruptions, and the shared use of GeneXpert machines for tuberculosis and HIV diagnostics, pose significant barriers to scale‐up [6]. Addressing these bottlenecks through improved procurement strategies, resource allocation, and regional collaboration is critical for sustaining and expanding molecular HPV screening coverage.

Centralized laboratory‐based platforms, including Abbott RealTime and Roche LA assays, continue to play an indispensable role in research and surveillance. These assays offer broad HPV genotype coverage (up to 37 types for Roche LA), high analytical sensitivity (> 93%), and the ability to generate critical epidemiological data on genotype distribution in cervical cancer and pre‐cancerous lesions [20, 21]. Despite these strengths, their reliance on sophisticated infrastructure, specialized equipment, and skilled personnel limits their scalability for population‐level screening, especially in rural districts. Similarly, the PathoChip microarray demonstrates exceptional sensitivity (~98%) and capacity for detecting rare or non‐vaccine genotypes, such as HPV‐26 and HPV‐34, but its high cost (> $150 per test) and complexity confine its utility to research rather than clinical screening programs [17].

In mapping the diagnostic platform landscape for HPV detection in Botswana, two widely referenced systems, Roche LA and Abbott RealTime, merit deeper discussion to provide balanced coverage alongside other assays used in sub‐Saharan Africa. The Roche is a reverse line blot hybridization assay targeting the L1 region of the HPV genome and detecting over 37 genotypes in a single reaction. Within the VALGENT framework, the LA demonstrated a clinical sensitivity of 97.6% (95% CI 93.3–99.5) and specificity of 91.7% (95% CI 90.0–93.2) for CIN2+ lesions, showing non‐inferiority compared with the Digene Hybrid Capture 2 (HC2) reference assay [22, 23]. Despite its broad genotyping capacity, some analytic limitations have been noted, for example, compromised detection of HPV‐52 in the presence of co‐infecting types such as HPV‐33, ‐35, and ‐58, necessitating reflex testing to confirm ambiguous results [24]. Operationally, the assay requires manual hybridization and interpretation of band patterns, making it more labor‐intensive and suitable for reference laboratories conducting surveillance and research rather than for large‐scale screening programs [25, 26]. Nevertheless, its ability to provide comprehensive genotype information is valuable for tracking HPV type distribution and evaluating vaccine impact in Botswana's population.

By contrast, the Abbott RealTime High‐Risk HPV assay is a fully automated, real‐time PCR platform that detects 14 high‐risk HPV genotypes with partial genotyping for HPV‐16 and HPV‐18/45. The assay's integration of extraction, amplification, and detection reduces operator variability and offers faster turnaround times suited to centralized screening. In a population‐based study in Slovenia, the RealTime assay achieved a clinical sensitivity of 91.8% (95% CI 87.8–94.5) for CIN3 lesions compared with 89.1% (95% CI 84.8–92.3) for HC2, confirming high diagnostic agreement [27]. Another large‐scale validation demonstrated 100% sensitivity and 93.3% specificity for CIN2+ detection among women aged over 30 years [28] while further analyses confirmed its compliance with international consensus guidelines for primary cervical cancer screening [25, 29]. The assay's reliability and automation make it advantageous for routine HPV‐based screening programs in Botswana, where centralized laboratory infrastructure is developing. However, considerations around instrument cost, reagent supply chains, and maintenance support remain critical barriers to sustained implementation in low‐ and middle‐income settings [30, 31].

The AmpFire multiplex assay, utilizing isothermal amplification, offers a promising, cost‐effective alternative for community‐based settings, with rapid turnaround (~1 h), moderate sensitivity (~92%), and minimal equipment requirements [32]. Its affordability ($10–$15 per test) and field adaptability make it a candidate for decentralized screening initiatives, though further large‐scale validation and cost‐effectiveness analyses are warranted to establish its role in national programs. Additionally, double‐nested PCR methods, while low throughput and less suitable for primary screening, remain valuable for retrospective analysis of FFPE tissues, aiding diagnostic confirmation in settings with constrained histopathology capacity [16].

Collectively, Botswana's HPV diagnostic ecosystem demonstrates a tiered and adaptive framework leveraging rapid PoC assays to expand access while maintaining centralized genotyping tools for surveillance and research. Moving forward, integration of PoC HPV testing with existing HIV care infrastructure, coupled with robust referral systems to ensure timely treatment, will be critical for maximizing the public health impact of molecular screening. Equally important is the need for sustained investment in supply chains, workforce training, and local laboratory capacity. Continued research into emerging HPV genotypes, optimization of screening algorithms, and the long‐term effects of combining vaccination with molecular diagnostics will be essential to achieving Botswana's cervical cancer elimination goals [6].

In addition to supply chain challenges, several implementation barriers specific to rural settings may hinder the effective deployment of HPV diagnostic platforms in Botswana. Many rural facilities face critical staff shortages and limited technical capacity, which constrain the ability to operate and maintain molecular testing systems, particularly those requiring specialized training. Patient acceptability also poses challenges, as long travel distances, limited awareness of HPV testing, and concerns about privacy can reduce uptake and follow‐through. Furthermore, integration with existing health infrastructure is often difficult in rural clinics that have limited space, unreliable power supply, and competing demands from established HIV and TB programs. These interconnected factors may significantly affect the feasibility, sustainability, and scalability of HPV testing initiatives in rural areas and, therefore, must be considered in planning and policy decisions.

Equitable access to HPV testing remains a key challenge in Botswana, especially for marginalized and rural women who face greater barriers to screening. Limited availability of molecular testing in rural clinics, long travel distances, and higher indirect costs can reduce uptake. Concentrating advanced platforms in urban centers may further widen disparities. Addressing these gaps requires prioritizing PoC options, community outreach, and integration of HPV testing into existing HIV and primary care services. Equity‐focused, community‐tailored implementation plans will be essential to ensure broad population benefit.

Due to substantial heterogeneity across study designs, participant populations, diagnostic assays, and reported outcomes, a statistical meta‐analysis was not feasible. Accordingly, we adopted a narrative synthesis approach to accurately describe the available evidence and avoid inappropriate quantitative pooling that could misrepresent the underlying data. Notably, many of the studies reporting diagnostic performance metrics were relatively small and disproportionately enrolled WLH. Although some investigations included both HIV‐positive and HIV‐negative participants, the current evidence base remains narrow, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to the broader population of women in Botswana. Importantly, the available cost data largely reflect immediate or per‐test expenditures and do not capture broader operational and programmatic costs, including personnel training, instrument maintenance, supply‐chain reliability, and logistical support. The absence of such comprehensive economic assessments limits our understanding of the long‐term sustainability and scalability of HPV testing within routine health systems. In addition to supply‐chain constraints, rural clinics face multiple implementation barriers, including shortages of trained healthcare personnel, limited patient awareness and acceptability, and challenges integrating HPV testing into already strained primary healthcare systems. These factors may significantly influence the feasibility and sustainability of HPV‐based screening strategies in rural Botswana. The substantial variability in reported outcomes across studies limited the establishment of standardized outcome categories and precluded direct comparisons or ranking of diagnostic platforms. These differences, stemming from variations in study design, populations, and reporting practices, underscore the challenges of synthesizing evidence across heterogeneous studies and justify our qualitative, narrative synthesis approach.

This review has several limitations. First, the considerable variability in study methodologies and outcome reporting precluded meta‐analysis, limiting the ability to quantitatively compare findings across studies. As a result, this review relied on narrative synthesis, which, while appropriate given the evidence landscape, may offer less statistical precision than quantitative approaches. Second, the available diagnostic performance data are limited by small sample sizes and study populations that are not fully representative of the general population. While certain studies included both HIV‐positive and HIV‐negative women, many disproportionately focused on WLH, thereby restricting the applicability of findings to the wider demographic profile of women in Botswana. Also, the cost analyses reported in the included studies were restricted in scope, focusing primarily on per‐test costs without incorporating essential long‐term operational expenses such as maintenance, training, and supply‐chain logistics. This limitation constrains the ability to fully evaluate the sustainability and real‐world economic impact of HPV testing programs. This review did not comprehensively assess health system constraints in rural settings, including workforce shortages, community acceptability issues, and service‐integration challenges, which may further limit the implementation of HPV testing in under‐resourced areas.

Finally, this review highlights the inconsistency in outcome measures across studies, which restricted the ability to perform quantitative comparisons or formally rank diagnostic platforms. This variability highlights the need for future research to adopt harmonized reporting standards to facilitate more robust cross‐study evaluations. Many performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, and cost estimates, were derived from small or pilot studies, often in HIV‐positive populations, and may not generalize to broader screening populations. The cost discussion primarily reflects upfront or per‐test estimates and does not capture long‐term programmatic expenses, such as equipment maintenance, personnel training, logistics, and reagent supply chains. I also excluded gray literature to maintain methodological rigor, which may have omitted some relevant findings. Implementation barriers in rural settings, such as workforce shortages, patient acceptability, and integration into existing health infrastructure, were only minimally addressed. Finally, although the review tables provide descriptive overviews of HPV platforms, they do not incorporate comparative scoring or a standardized evaluation framework due to study heterogeneity.

Conclusion

Botswana's adoption of diverse HPV detection platforms reflects a strategic approach to balancing diagnostic accuracy, cost, and accessibility in a high HIV prevalence setting. While rapid PoC assays like Cepheid Xpert and AmpFire hold promise for expanding population‐level screening, especially in rural and primary‐level clinics where simplicity, minimal training, and quick turnaround are essential, centralized laboratory assays remain vital in urban tertiary hospitals for epidemiological surveillance, high‐throughput screening, and detailed genotype analysis. Prioritizing PoC platforms at peripheral facilities and reserving more complex, high‐capacity platforms for referral laboratories can optimize resource use within Botswana's tiered health‐care ecosystem.

To maximize the impact of these technologies, national efforts must address logistical barriers, integrate HPV testing within HIV care pathways, and ensure that positive cases are linked to timely and effective treatment. Strengthening local laboratory capacity, enhancing procurement systems, and fostering partnerships across public and private sectors will be pivotal for scaling up molecular HPV testing. Ultimately, a coordinated strategy that combines innovative diagnostics, vaccination, and robust care pathways could position Botswana as a regional leader in cervical cancer control.
