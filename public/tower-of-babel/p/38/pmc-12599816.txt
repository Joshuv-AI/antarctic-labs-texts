Letter to editor: The impact of fixed segment length on the surgical outcomes of single-segment lumbar burst fractures: shorter segments are more prone to the formation of local stable osteophytes: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Aanchal Sharma, Reema Rasotra
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

The study by Chen et al. [1], which examines the connection between fixation segment length and radiologic results for lumbar burst fractures, caught our attention. Although the clinical focus is praiseworthy, we identified certain methodological issues that require further investigation, particularly in light of the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) standards. The abstract's absence of structured parts, such as specific statistical results, participant numbers, and significant limitations, may make it difficult for readers to assess it, even though the title is appropriate and concise [2]. The aims of the study are also less clear because the introduction does not present a specific hypothesis or goal based on PICOT (population, intervention, comparison, outcome, type of study) components [3].

The comparison groups, which consist of 26 patients in the short-segment group and 38 patients in the long-segment group, are unbalanced in the methods section; however, no sample size or power estimate is given. This is particularly troubling because the observed marginal significance (p=0.048) raises the possibility of type I error. Additionally, the study relies only on univariate testing, without multivariable correction for relevant confounders such as fracture type or age, which are crucial factors in observational studies [4]. The authors did not address participant attrition or missing data, although they did prolong the follow-up to 24 months [5]. These components are necessary for longitudinal research to be valid.

Furthermore, the results’ repeatability was called into doubt since, despite the fact that the radiological grading was done by two observers, inter-rater agreement data like Cohen’s kappa were not provided [6]. Lastly, protocol registration and data sharing—two increasingly crucial components of sound research practice—are not mentioned [7].

We encourage the authors to consider these issues in future work and thank them for initiating an important discussion in spinal surgery.
