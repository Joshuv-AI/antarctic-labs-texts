Burnout among Pakistani nurses: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Madiha Mukhtar, Hamideh Ebrahimi, Mojahid Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBurnout among nurses is a psychological syndrome characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment, typically arising from chronic workplace stress. Given the nursing shortage, high workload, and structural constraints within Pakistan’s healthcare system, an accurate estimate of burnout prevalence in this workforce is essential. This study aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of burnout and explore sources of heterogeneity among Pakistani nurses.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251251405) and conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, APA PsycINFO, Google Scholar, and Pakistan-specific sources up to 21 October 2025. Quantitative observational studies reporting burnout prevalence using validated instruments (primarily the Maslach Burnout Inventory [MBI], Oldenburg Burnout Inventory [OLBI], Copenhagen Burnout Inventory [CBI], and Professional Quality of Life scale [ProQOL]) were eligible. Study quality was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence studies. Meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model (REML) in STATA 17, and heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic.ResultsTwenty-three studies were included in the systematic review, and 17 studies (n = 3164 nurses) met criteria for quantitative synthesis. The pooled prevalence of occupational burnout among Pakistani nurses was 68%. Heterogeneity was extremely high. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated the stability of the pooled estimate. Meta-regression showed no significant association between publication year and burnout prevalence (p=0.896). Subgroup analyses by measurement tool and geographic region revealed no significant between-group differences. Egger’s test indicated publication bias; however, the Trim-and-Fill method did not change the pooled estimate.ConclusionBurnout is highly prevalent among nurses in Pakistan. This high prevalence may reflect underlying structural challenges such as workforce shortages, heavy workloads, and limited organizational support. System-level reforms are urgently needed to improve working conditions and strengthen nurse retention policies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04422-4.

Introduction

Burnout is a psychological syndrome characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment, often resulting from chronic workplace stress [1]. Globally, nursing is recognized as a high-stress profession due to long working hours, high patient loads, emotional demands, and resource constraints [2]. According to an international meta-analysis, approximately 30–45% of nurses worldwide experience high levels of emotional exhaustion, while more than 25% show severe depersonalization [3]. Moreover, findings from another global systematic review indicate that nearly 34% of nurses report a significantly reduced sense of personal accomplishment, highlighting a concerning trend in the prevalence of burnout among the global nursing workforce [4]. In Pakistan, the nursing workforce is confronted with a severe shortage of personnel and extensive structural constraints [5, 6]. Nurses working in clinical settings frequently encounter inadequate staffing levels, insufficient organizational support, limited educational capacity, and a high turnover rate [7]. These factors not only undermine the quality of care but also intensify their psychological burden and workload. Recent reports indicate that many healthcare facilities in Pakistan operate with a nurse-to-patient ratio significantly below international standards in some cases as low as one nurse per forty patients [8]. Such conditions lead to prolonged working hours, excessive workload, and ultimately an increased risk of burnout among nurses. Additionally, many Pakistani nurses report a lack of supportive systems, limited opportunities for professional advancement, unfavorable working conditions, and insufficient organizational recognition, all of which further exacerbate their occupational and emotional stress [9].

Over the past two decades, healthcare systems worldwide have increasingly acknowledged burnout as a critical occupational health issue affecting nurse well-being, job satisfaction, clinical performance, and patient outcomes [10]. Despite the growing global attention to the consequences of burnout, existing research in Pakistan remains methodologically fragmented and conceptually inconsistent. Many studies have relied on heterogeneous assessment tools or focused on isolated subdimensions of burnout, making it difficult to compare findings and derive an accurate estimate of its prevalence. Furthermore, most investigations are restricted to individual hospitals or specific cities, with limited consideration of potential geographical variations across Pakistani provinces, which may be shaped by regional differences in culture, organizational structures, and socioeconomic conditions.

In addition, a considerable number of studies lack rigorous methodological evaluation, do not report statistical power, and fail to assess publication bias—factors that can significantly influence the accuracy of prevalence estimates and the identification of predictors of burnout among nurses. Hence, conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis using a standardized instrument such as the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), accompanied by critical appraisal of study quality and formal evaluation of publication bias, is essential to produce robust and comparable evidence.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of occupational burnout among nurses in Pakistan and to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including geographic region and measurement instruments. By providing a standardized and evidence-based synthesis, this study seeks to inform future research, guide targeted interventions, and support evidence-based policymaking for improving nurses’ working conditions and well-being in Pakistan.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251251405 The conduct and reporting of this review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guideline [11], employing a structured four-stage process encompassing study identification, screening, eligibility appraisal, and final inclusion. The complete search strategy, including controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH/Emtree), free-text keywords, and Boolean operators, is provided in Supplementary File 1.

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted across the following electronic databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, Scopus, APA PsycINFO, and Google Scholar. To enhance retrieval sensitivity and obtain evidence representing the national context, additional manual searches were performed in Pakistan-specific academic platforms, including the Pakistan Journal of Medical Sciences, Journal of the Pakistan Medical Association (JPMA), and PakMediNet. No start date restriction was applied, and studies from database inception to 21 October 2025 were included.

Based on the PEO framework, this review addressed the following core question: What is the prevalence of occupational burnout (O) among registered nurses in clinical settings in Pakistan (P), in relation to work-related exposure factors (E)?

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Several eligibility criteria were established for this review. First, the study population had to consist exclusively of registered nurses working in clinical settings, including hospitals, primary healthcare units, and specialized wards across Pakistan, as well as final-year nursing students engaged in full-time clinical training. Second, studies were required to report the prevalence of burnout or provide quantitatively extractable data regarding burnout symptoms. Additionally, only studies utilizing standardized and validated measurement instruments for assessing burnout were eligible. Regarding study design, only quantitative observational research (including cross-sectional, cohort, and case–control studies) published in English were eligible for inclusion. This language restriction was applied because English is the primary language of academic education and scientific publication in the medical and nursing fields in Pakistan, and no relevant peer-reviewed studies are published in local languages. Studies investigating work-related or environmental determinants of burnout such as workload, staffing adequacy, shift schedules, job stress, emotional demands, and workplace climate were also considered relevant. Studies reporting burnout outcomes exclusively as mean scores or continuous scale values were incorporated into the qualitative synthesis; however, they were excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis due to the inability to derive prevalence estimates from such formats. Accordingly, only studies that reported burnout as percentage prevalence, based on defined cut-off thresholds of validated assessment tools, were included in the meta-analytic calculations.

Studies that did not provide nurse-specific data or combined results for healthcare workers without separate extraction for nurses were excluded. Research that did not assess burnout as an independent outcome (e.g., reporting only general stress or composite psychological scales without burnout-specific scores) was removed. In addition, non-original articles including narrative reviews, commentaries, letters, conference abstracts, and case reports were excluded. Studies lacking sufficient information to calculate prevalence, those with inaccessible full texts, and unpublished or non–peer-reviewed sources were also excluded.

Study selection process

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote, and duplicate entries were automatically removed. Screening was performed in two sequential stages. In the first stage, two independent reviewers proficient in English and Urdu screened titles and abstracts to determine relevance. In the second stage, full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed for final inclusion. Any discrepancies were resolved through dialog and, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer for consensus. The overall selection process is depicted in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data from eligible studies. Extracted information included: first author’s name, year of publication, geographic region and study setting, sample size and demographic characteristics, burnout assessment tool used, scoring or cut-off criteria applied, and reported prevalence or mean burnout scores. Where available, severity levels (e.g., emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment) and methodological quality scores were also recorded. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and mutual agreement.

Burnout assessment tools

The included studies used different validated instruments to assess burnout among nurses in Pakistan. Although these tools varied in conceptual focus, scoring systems, and diagnostic thresholds, they are widely recognized in occupational health research.

Maslach burnout inventory–human services survey (MBI-HSS)

The MBI-HSS assesses burnout across three dimensions: Emotional Exhaustion (EE), Depersonalization (DP), and Personal Accomplishment (PA). Scores were categorized as low, moderate, or high according to established cut-off values. In this review, burnout prevalence was extracted based on the original study definitions. In most studies, burnout was defined as high EE and/or high DP, with or without low PA, in accordance with standard MBI-HSS interpretation guidelines [12] .

Copenhagen burnout inventory (CBI)

The CBI assesses burnout across three domains: personal, work-related, and client-related burnout. According to established guidelines, scores ≥ 50 indicate moderate-to-severe burnout. In this review, burnout prevalence was extracted based on the original study definitions, and participants with scores ≥ 50 in at least one domain were considered to have burnout [13].

Oldenburg burnout inventory (OLBI)

The OLBI assesses burnout using two dimensions: exhaustion and disengagement from work. Scores were interpreted according to validated cut-off values. In this review, participants classified as having moderate or high levels of burnout based on the original study definitions were considered to have burnout, and prevalence estimates were extracted as reported [14].

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of all included studies was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool for prevalence studies. The tool comprises nine criteria, each rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.” A score of 1 was assigned to “Yes” responses, and 0 for others. Total scores ranged from 0 to 9, and studies were categorized as: low quality (≤ 3), moderate quality [4–6], and high quality [7–9, 14].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were executed in STATA version 17. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Cochran Q test and I² index, interpreted as low (< 25%), moderate (25–50%), substantial (50–75%), or very high (> 75%). Given heterogeneity levels, a random-effects model was applied to compute pooled burnout prevalence with 95% confidence intervals, visualized in forest plots. Standardized cut-offs for each instrument were used to recalculate prevalence for consistency.

Subgroup analyses were performed by burnout measurement tool and geographic region to explore sources of heterogeneity. Meta-regression examined potential effects of publication year and sample size on reported burnout prevalence. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test; where significant bias existed, adjustments were made using the Trim and Fill method.

Results

Systematic review

A total of 23 studies were included in the systematic review, all of which reported the prevalence of occupational burnout among nurses in Pakistan. Sample sizes across studies ranged from 15 to 379 participants, and most investigations were conducted in the provinces of Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and the Islamabad Capital Territory. Regarding measurement tools, the most commonly used instruments were the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OLBI). Reported prevalence rates of burnout varied substantially across studies, ranging from 29.1% to 100%. Meta-analysis

A total of 17 studies(n = 3164 nurses) met the eligibility criteria for quantitative synthesis, as they reported burnout prevalence based on defined cut-off values of validated instruments. Studies that reported burnout only as mean scores or descriptive measures were included in the systematic review but excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis. Using a random-effects model (REML), the pooled prevalence of occupational burnout among nurses in Pakistan was estimated at 68% (95% CI: 56%–79%, p < 0.001), indicating a high level of burnout within this workforce. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 99.69%), supported by Tau² = 0.0607 and a significant Cochran’s Q statistic (Q [16] = 1718.36, p < 0.001). To evaluate the stability of the pooled estimate, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted. The overall prevalence showed minimal fluctuation, ranging from 66.2% to 71.7%, demonstrating that no single study unduly influenced the pooled estimate. The largest deviation occurred when Khurshid (2024) was excluded, raising the prevalence to 71.7%, while exclusion of Munir (2025) produced the lowest value (66.2%). These narrow variations confirm the robustness and reliability of the meta-analytic findings, with no influential studies detected. Meta-regression

A random-effects meta-regression model was applied to assess the association between publication year and the prevalence of occupational burnout. The analysis indicated that study year had no statistically significant effect on burnout prevalence among nurses in Pakistan (β = 0.0022, SE = 0.017, p = 0.896). The model yielded an R² value of 0, suggesting that temporal variations do not explain the differences in reported prevalence rates. Consequently, no clear upward or downward trend in burnout prevalence was observed over time.Importantly, the absence of a significant temporal trend does not indicate stability or improvement in working conditions over time, but may instead reflect the persistence of underlying structural and systemic challenges within Pakistan’s healthcare system.The remaining heterogeneity (I² = 99.65%) is therefore likely attributable to other structural and contextual factors rather than publication year. Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s and Begg’s statistical tests. Egger’s test indicated a statistically significant presence of publication bias (β = − 6.77, z = − 3.30, p = 0.001), whereas Begg’s test was borderline significant (z = − 2.02, p = 0.052).To evaluate the potential impact of publication bias on the pooled prevalence estimate, the Trim-and-Fill method was applied using a random-effects (REML) model. The analysis suggested that no additional studies were imputed, and the pooled prevalence remained numerically unchanged before and after correction (0.68; 95% CI: 0.56–0.79). However, given the extremely high level of heterogeneity across studies (I² = 99.69%), the reliability of the Trim-and-Fill procedure may be limited in this context. Therefore, despite the unchanged pooled estimate, the presence of publication bias cannot be definitively ruled out. A funnel plot was also generated to visually assess publication bias and demonstrated asymmetry, with smaller studies showing greater dispersion around the pooled estimate. In highly heterogeneous datasets such as the present analysis, funnel plot asymmetry should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect genuine between-study differences rather than selective publication alone. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 23 studies, including 17 in the quantitative synthesis, to assess burnout prevalence among nurses in Pakistan. The pooled estimate of 68% (95% CI: 56%–79%) indicates a substantial burden of burnout in this workforce. However, given the extremely high heterogeneity across studies, this estimate should be interpreted as an overall indicative measure rather than a definitive national value. Compared with international evidence, the prevalence observed in this review is higher than that reported in many high-income countries [3, 38]. This difference highlights the challenging working environment within Pakistan’s healthcare system. Nevertheless, as this study is based on prevalence data, causal relationships between working conditions and burnout cannot be inferred. Meta-regression analysis showed no significant temporal trend in burnout prevalence over time. This finding does not imply stability or improvement in working conditions but may reflect the persistence of structural challenges within the healthcare system, including staffing shortages, limited resources, and organizational constraints [5, 7].

Subgroup analyses by geographic region were limited by the uneven distribution of studies, with most conducted in Punjab and none from Baluchistan. This limits the ability to draw robust conclusions regarding regional variation. Differences in healthcare infrastructure and service delivery across provinces may contribute to variability in burnout experiences [8, 9]. Subgroup analyses based on measurement tools revealed no statistically significant differences in pooled prevalence estimates. However, substantial variation in conceptual frameworks, scoring methods, and cut-off thresholds across instruments represents a major methodological challenge [12–14]. Measurement inconsistency appears to be a core contributor to heterogeneity and complicates cross-study comparisons. Greater standardization of burnout assessment, particularly through consistent use of validated tools such as the MBI, is needed in future research [12].The extremely high heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis reflects considerable variability in burnout prevalence across hospital types, clinical specialties, and organizational contexts. Similar patterns have been reported in previous international meta-analyses of burnout prevalence [3, 4] These findings suggest that burnout among Pakistani nurses is not a uniform phenomenon but is shaped by local working conditions and institutional environments. Although publication bias was suggested by Egger’s test, funnel plot asymmetry in this highly heterogeneous dataset is likely influenced by between-study variability rather than selective publication alone. In addition, reliance on self-reported instruments across studies may have influenced prevalence estimates [1, 10].From a policy perspective, the findings highlight the need for system-level strategies aimed at improving working environments and supporting nurse well-being [5, 6, 9]. These interpretations are based on contextual evidence and should be clearly distinguished from the empirical results of this review.

Overall, this review demonstrates that burnout represents a substantial and persistent challenge among nurses in Pakistan. Addressing this issue requires coordinated efforts at organizational and policy levels, informed by high-quality and methodologically consistent research .

Limitations

This review has several important limitations. First, the included studies were predominantly cross-sectional, which prevents causal inferences regarding the relationships between potential risk factors and burnout. Second, the very high level of heterogeneity, although partially explored through subgroup and meta-regression analyses, limits the generalizability and precision of the pooled prevalence estimate.Third, most of the included studies were conducted in Punjab, with limited representation from other regions, and no eligible studies were identified from Baluchistan. This uneven geographic distribution restricts the national representativeness of the findings. Fourth, insufficient and inconsistently reported information regarding hospital characteristics, nurse specialties, and work settings in the original studies limited the ability to conduct more detailed subgroup or meta-regression analyses to further explore sources of heterogeneity.Fifth, substantial variation in measurement instruments, scoring methods, and reporting practices across studies may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity and reduced the comparability of prevalence estimates.Finally, most included studies relied on self-reported questionnaires, which may have introduced reporting and recall bias and potentially led to overestimation of burnout prevalence.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that burnout is highly prevalent among nurses in Pakistan, with a substantial proportion of the workforce affected. Although the pooled estimate suggests a high overall burden, this figure should be interpreted with caution in light of the considerable heterogeneity across studies. Nevertheless, the findings consistently demonstrate a serious and widespread challenge within the nursing profession, reflecting persistent structural constraints such as workforce shortages, heavy workloads, and limited organizational support. The absence of an improving trend over time highlights the urgent need for comprehensive and sustained system-level reforms. Coordinated efforts by government authorities, healthcare organizations, and professional bodies are required to improve working conditions, promote nurse well-being, and strengthen workforce retention, all of which are essential for maintaining the quality of patient care and the resilience of Pakistan’s health system.
