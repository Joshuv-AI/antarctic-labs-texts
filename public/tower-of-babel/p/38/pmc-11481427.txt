Screening of key genes related to M6A methylation in patients with heart failure
Authors: Zelan Wu, Wupeng Liu, Xiaoyun Si, Jinfeng Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to identify m6A methylation-related and immune cell-related key genes with diagnostic potential for heart failure (HF) by leveraging various bioinformatics techniques.MethodsThe GSE116250 and GSE141910 datasets were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Correlation analysis was conducted between differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in HF and control groups, alongside differential m6A regulatory factors, to identify m6A-related DEGs (m6A-DEGs). Subsequently, candidate genes were narrowed down by intersecting key module genes derived from weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) with m6A-DEGs. Key genes were then identified through the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) analysis. Correlation analyses between key genes and differentially expressed immune cells were performed, followed by the validation of key gene expression levels in public datasets. To ensure clinical applicability, five pairs of blood samples were collected for quantitative real-time fluorescence PCR (qRT-PCR) validation.ResultsA total of 93 m6A-DEGs were identified (|COR| > 0.6, P < 0.05), and five key genes (LACTB2, NAMPT, SCAMP5, HBA1, and PRKAR2A) were selected for further analysis. Correlation analysis revealed that differential immune cells were negatively associated with the expression of LACTB2, NAMPT, and PRKAR2A (P < 0.05), while positively correlated with SCAMP5 and HBA1 (P < 0.05). Subsequent expression validation confirmed significant differences in key gene expression between the HF and control groups, with consistent expression trends observed across both training and validation sets. The expression trends of LACTB2, PRKAR2A, and HBA1 in blood samples from the qRT-PCR assay aligned with the results derived from public databases.ConclusionThis study successfully identified five m6A methylation-related key genes with diagnostic significance, providing a theoretical foundation for further exploration of m6A methylation’s molecular mechanisms in HF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04228-9.Keywords: Heart failure, N6-methyladenosine methylation, Bioinformatics, Gene Screening, Immune cells, Rank-sum test

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) ranks among the most prevalent cardiovascular diseases in clinical practice [1, 2]. The growing aging population and improved survival rates post-myocardial infarction have driven a significant surge in HF cases, establishing it as a critical health concern [3–6]. By 2030, the total cost of HF is projected to increase by 127% from $30.7 billion in 2012 [3, 7]. Despite the availability of effective treatments, the one-year mortality rate for patients with HF remains alarmingly high, ranging from 10 to 35%, with even poorer outcomes in advanced cases [8–11]. With the continuous advancement of high-throughput technologies in HF research, new biomarkers such as mid-regional pro-atrial natriuretic peptide and mid-regional adrenomedullin have been discovered. Currently, widely used biomarkers such as NT-proBNP and BNP perform well in diagnosing acute heart failure, however, they have limitations in predicting the risk of heart failure deterioration, guiding therapeutic decisions, or assessing long-term prognosis [12, 13].

Emerging research highlights the role of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) in regulating various processes in mammalian cells, including cell differentiation, immune response, and metabolism [14–16]. It also affects mRNA stability, which is crucial for multiple biological processes [17, 18]. For instance, within the m6A family, FTO suppresses immune gene expression, thereby reducing self-renewal and immune responses in leukemia stem cells [19, 20], while METTL3 enhances innate immune signaling pathways and inflammatory gene expression [21]. m6A methylation has been implicated in several cardiovascular diseases [21–23], with deficiencies in FTO and METTL3 potentially contributing to the onset of HF [24–26].

Inspired by the work of Khatereh Dehghani and colleagues [27], this study emphasizes the importance of early identification of high-risk patients through hematologic testing. This approach facilitates early intervention with myocardium-targeted genetic markers, potentially preventing the progression to advanced stages of HF. Given that the left ventricle is the heart’s primary pumping chamber, its dysfunction is one of the key markers of HF progression and the main site affected in heart failure. Changes in gene expression in the left ventricle are likely to most accurately reflect the pathophysiological processes of the disease. Therefore, we chose to study gene expression in left ventricular samples to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of advanced HF [28, 29]. Accordingly, this study analyzed publicly available myocardium-related datasets (GSE116250 and GSE141910) to identify differentially expressed m6A methylation-related key genes in the late stages of HF, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for further exploration of the molecular mechanisms involving m6A methylation and immune cells in HF. Through the analysis of gene expression in left ventricular samples, we can more directly reveal the molecular changes occurring during the progression of HF from the heart’s core functional region, providing important clues for the development of early diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data source

The GSE116250 and GSE141910 datasets, obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), contain sequencing data derived from human left ventricular tissue.

The GSE116250 dataset comprises gene expression profiles from 50 patients with HF, including 37 with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), 13 with ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM), and 14 control samples. The GSE141910 dataset includes data from 200 patients with HF, encompassing 166 with DCM, 28 with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), and 166 control samples. The GSE120852 dataset, which was derived from high-throughput sequencing, included 5 control samples and 5 left ventricular HF samples,

A total of 23 m6A regulatory factors were extracted from the literature [30], with expression data for 22 of these genes available in the GSE116250 dataset. The main elements of the study was shown in the flow chart (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Selection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and Differential m6A regulatory factors

The potential role of m6A regulators in the development of heart failure was further analyzed by identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between HF and controls. For this purpose, DEGs between the HF and control groups were identified using the limma package (version 3.46.0), applying criteria of |log2FC| > 1 and P < 0.05 [31]. The ggplot2 (version 3.3.4) and pheatmap (version 1.0.12) packages were subsequently employed to generate volcano plots and heatmaps, respectively, for visualizing differential gene expression patterns [32]. The 22 m6A regulatory factors underwent differential analysis via a rank-sum test.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and functional analysis of DEGs associated with m6A regulatory factors

To further understand the biological significance of m6A methylation in the process of heart failure (HF). A spearman correlation analysis was then conducted between the identified DEGs and m6A regulatory factors. Imported a correlation pairs into Cytoscape for network mappingA correlation network of m6A-DEGs was constructed for visualization [33] The STRING database (https://string-db.org), applying filtering criteria of |COR| > 0.6 and P < 0.05 to pinpoint m6A-related DEGs (m6A-DEGs). A correlation network for m6A-DEGs was constructed and visualized [33]. The STRING database (https://string-db.org) was used to construct a PPI network for the identified m6A-DEGs. Functional analysis of m6A-DEGs, focusing on their involvement in biological processes and signaling pathways, was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 3.18.0) [34], utilizing Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. The enrichment analysis results were visualized through bar plots generated with the enrichplot package (version 1.10.2) [35].

Immune infiltration analysis

In order to elucidated the role and proportional changes of immune cells and stromal cells in HF, the MCP counter was employed to estimate the proportions of 11 immune cell types and stromal cells across all samples in the GSE116250 dataset. The analysis tool was R language (version 4.0.3). Differences in immune cell proportions between the HF and control groups were subsequently analyzed by the mcp_counter algorithm and visualized using box plots.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To gain insight into the immune microenvironment of HF by identifying the gene modules most associated with changes in immune cell ratios, WGCNA was conducted using the WGCNA package (version 1.70-3) to identify modules and genes most closely associated with differential immune cell proportions [36]. Genes with expression values exceeding 1 were selected for clustering, with outliers excluded to maintain analysis accuracy. The optimal soft threshold (β) was determined to best fit a scale-free network distribution. A hierarchical clustering tree was then constructed based on calculated adjacency and similarity metrics, with modules identified using the mixed dynamic tree-cutting algorithm and a minimum module size of 100. Differential immune cell proportions served as trait data for selecting modules correlated with these traits, with MEDissThres set to 0.2 to merge similar modules analyzed by the dynamic shear tree algorithm. A module-trait correlation heatmap was generated to identify modules most pertinent to differential immune cell proportions, and key module genes were extracted from these critical modules for further analysis.

Establishment of diagnostic model and validation of key gene expression levels

Our main goal was to identified m6A-regulated differentially expressed genes (m6A-DEGs) that have a key role in heart failure (HF) diagnosis. In this study, candidate genes were identified by intersecting m6A-DEGs with key module genes derived from WGCNA. Subsequently, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) analysis, conducted using the glmnet package, was employed to establish a diagnostic model. The genes forming the optimal diagnostic model were designated as the key genes in this study. To evaluate the model’s accuracy, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and Area Under the Curve (AUC) values were calculated. Samples from the GSE120852 datasets were log2 (x + 1) transformed used R package (V 3.4.1) to showed the distribution of gene expression levels in the HF and NC groups by plotting violin plots with the ggplot2 package (V 3.4.4). And ROC curves was performed in the dataset to evaluate their diagnostic performance by pROC package (V 1.18.4).

Construction of nomogram

In order to predicted the probability of HF occurrence by gene expression level, gender and age. The prognosis of clinicopathological features was further investigated by plotting the nomogram of the prediction model in the validation set GSE141910 and training set GSE116250. Nomogram plots were drawn using the regplot package (V 1.1), and calibration curves for the predictive models were plotted using the val.prob function of the rms package (V 6.7-1). ROC curves were plotted using the pROC package (V 1.18.4). If an AUC value was closer to 1, the model was more accurate.

Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of key genes

In order to unravel the signaling pathways closely associated with key m6A-related genes in HF and thus provided insight into the understanding of m6A regulatory mechanisms in HF. Using the KEGG gene set, single-gene GSEA enrichment analysis of key genes was performed with the clusterProfiler package (version 3.18.0) [34], aiming to investigate the regulatory pathways associated with m6A methylation.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

To validate the results obtained from public databases, five normal blood samples (control group) and five samples from individuals with HF (HF group) were collected from clinical sources. Inclusion criteria required participants to meet the diagnostic criteria for chronic heart failure outlined in the “2018 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Heart Failure,” and to be aged 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria included severe infection, significant liver or kidney dysfunction, malignancy, severe endocrine or autoimmune diseases, and mental disorders. A control group of healthy individuals undergoing outpatient physical examinations at our hospital during the same period was also selected. The study received approval from the hospital’s ethics committee. RNA extraction and qRT-PCR were performed following standard protocols. Total RNA was isolated from the 10 samples using TRIzol, then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the SureScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Servicebio, China). qPCR was subsequently conducted with 2x Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio, China). Each experiment was conducted in triplicate, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–△△Ct method [37].

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were performed using the R programming language. Unless otherwise specified, data comparisons between groups were conducted using a rank-sum test, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

DEGs and m6A regulatory factors

By comparing the gene expression profiles of the two groups of samples, we were able to identify genes and regulatory networks that may play key roles in HF. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the molecular basis of HF and may reveal new therapeutic targets. The differential analysis identified 293 DEGs between HF and control samples, with 204 genes upregulated and 89 genes downregulated in HF samples (Fig. 1a-b). Additionally, differential expression analysis of 22 m6A regulatory factors revealed significant differences in 12 factors, including WTAP, ALKBH5, and FMR1, between HF and control samples (Fig. 1c-d).

The PPI network and functional enrichment of m6A-DEGs

Correlation analysis between DEGs and differential m6A regulatory factors resulted in the identification of 93 m6A-DEGs (Fig. 2a). A PPI network was constructed with a confidence level of 0.4, filtering out spurious proteins. The resulting network comprised 41 proteins, with 41 nodes and 80 edges (Fig. 2b). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that m6A-DEGs were associated with processes such as extracellular matrix organization, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, and glycosaminoglycan binding (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 1). KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the enrichment of m6A-DEGs in pathways including Hedgehog signaling, platelet activation, and Rap1 signaling (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Table 2).

Immune infiltration analysis between the HF and Control groups

In delving into the characterization of the immune microenvironment in HF, we performed an immune infiltration analysis that focused on understanding the differences in the distribution of different immune cell types in HF samples versus control samples. Immune infiltration analysis revealed a higher proportion of macrophages/monocytes, neutrophils, and endothelial cells in the HF samples (Fig. 3a). Differential analysis further demonstrated a significantly increased proportion of T cells, CD8 + T cells, B cells, monocytes, macrophages/monocytes, neutrophils, and endothelial cells in the HF group compared to the control group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3b).

WGCNA screening for modules related to differential immune cells

Before constructing the gene co-expression network for the HF study, we performed cluster analysis on all samples to ensure data integrity and consistency. Sample clustering results indicated the absence of outlier samples. Subsequently, sample clustering and phenotype trait heatmaps were constructed (Fig. 4a-b). The optimal soft threshold was determined to be 8, where average connectivity approaches 0, and the unscaled R2 on the vertical axis nears the threshold of 0.85 (red line) (Fig. 4c). Using the dynamic tree-cutting algorithm, 16 modules were initially identified (Fig. 4d). By setting MEDissThres to 0.2 and merging similar modules, the number was refined to 15 modules (Fig. 4e). The module-trait correlation heatmap revealed that the MEmagenta module (T cells: Cor = 0.69, P = 2e-10; CD8 + T cells: Cor = 0.69, P = 4e − 10) had the highest positive correlation with differential immune cells, while the MEgreen module (T cells: Cor = -0.39, P = 0.002) showed the highest negative correlation (Fig. 4f). Consequently, these two modules were identified as key modules. A total of 1,526 key module genes were selected for further analysis, comprising 584 genes from the MEmagenta module and 942 genes from the MEgreen module (Fig. 4g-h).

Key gene selection and validation of expression levels

To further explored the mechanism of m6A modification in the immune microenvironment of HF, we performed screening and analysis of key genes. By intersecting m6A-DEGs with key module genes, 11 candidate genes (NEBL, LACTB2, HLA-DQA1, NAMPT, HBA2, SCAMP5, FGF7, HBA1, HBB, GCOM1, and PRKAR2A) were identified (Fig. 5a). A diagnostic model was constructed using LASSO analysis, with the optimal model achieved at λ = 0.0318. The five genes constituting this model—LACTB2, NAMPT, SCAMP5, HBA1, and PRKAR2A—were subsequently considered key genes for further analysis (Fig. 5b). The ROC curve analysis demonstrated high accuracy, with AUC values of 0.944 for the training set and 0.964 for the validation set (Fig. 5c). Validation of key gene expression levels revealed significant differences between the HF and control groups in both the training and validation sets (Fig. 5d-e). Specifically, SCAMP5 and HBA1 were significantly upregulated in the HF group, while LACTB2, NAMPT, and PRKAR2A were significantly downregulated. The expression trends of key genes were consistent across both datasets. Correlation analysis indicated a significant negative correlation between the expression of LACTB2, NAMPT, and PRKAR2A and immune cells (COR < -0.2, P < 0.05), whereas SCAMP5 and HBA1 showed a significant positive correlation with differential immune cells (COR > 0.2, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5f).

Functional enrichment of key genes

To delve deeper into the biological roles and underlying mechanisms of key genes within the heart failure (HF) context, we conducted a single-gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) focusing on five pivotal genes: HBA1, LACTB2, NAMPT, PRKAR2A, and SCAMP5. Single gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that the HBA1 high-expression group is significantly enriched in pathways related to the intestinal immune network for IgA production and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 8a). In the LACTB2 high-expression group, enrichment was observed in ribosome and pyruvate metabolism-related signaling pathways (Fig. 8b). Additionally, the NAMPT high-expression group showed enrichment in pathways such as protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, cell apoptosis, and TNF, HIF-1, and NF-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling (Fig. 8c). For the PRKAR2A high-expression group, enriched pathways included sulfur metabolism, insulin signaling, and pyruvate metabolism-related signaling pathways (Fig. 8d). Finally, the SCAMP5 low-expression group exhibited enrichment in insulin signaling, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, ribosome, and spliceosome-related signaling pathways (Fig. 8e).

qRT-PCR validation

qRT-PCR experiments revealed that LACTB2 and PRKAR2A expression levels in the blood samples of patients with HF were markedly lower than those in the control group, while HBA1 expression was significantly elevated in HF samples compared to normal controls (Fig. 9a-c), aligning with tissue data from public databases. Conversely, SCAMP5 expression was notably reduced in the HF group relative to the control group (Fig. 9d), potentially indicating differential expression between tissues and blood. Additionally, NAMPT did not exhibit significant differential expression between the HF and control groups (Fig. 9e).

Discussion

As HF research advances, growing evidence underscores the pivotal roles of immune factors and m6A methylation in various cardiovascular diseases, including HF. This study conducted a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis to identify key genes related to m6A methylation and immune cell involvement in the onset and progression of HF.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed that m6A-DEGs are significantly involved in biological processes pertinent to HF, such as extracellular matrix pathways [38], oxygen transport pathways [39], and collagen fiber organization (e.g., type I collagen metabolism) [40]. Additionally, many KEGG pathways enriched by m6A-DEGs are closely linked to HF, including platelet signaling pathways [41] and the modulation of cAMP signaling via Epac-Rap1 [42]. Notably, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle pathway emerged as a key enrichment in single-gene GSEA.

Five key genes associated with m6A methylation, with diagnostic potential, were identified. SCAMP5 and HBA1 expression levels were significantly elevated in diseased samples, while LACTB2, NAMPT, and PRKAR2A were significantly downregulated. Further disease-specific analysis highlighted significant expression differences for these five genes among patients with DCM, ICM, and HCM compared to controls. The diagnostic model demonstrated a markedly stronger predictive value for DCM and ICM than for HCM and PPCM, underscoring these genes’ diagnostic significance in DCM and ICM HF subtypes. Moreover, PCR validation experiments confirmed that early changes in LACTB2, PRKAR2A, and HBA1 expression in the blood of patients with HF were consistent with findings from public databases.

PRKAR2A, one of the four regulatory isoforms of protein kinase A (PKA), plays a critical role in enhancing Ca2+ currents and facilitating Ca2+ dissociation from myosin, thereby promoting both cardiac contraction and relaxation [43]. Nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), a rate-limiting enzyme in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) synthesis salvage pathway, is implicated in pressure overload-induced HF [44]. The study suggests that endogenous NAMPT sustains cardiac function and metabolism in HF, potentially explaining the observed (albeit not significant) increase in NAMPT levels in the blood of patients with early-stage HF. However, excessive NAMPT expression specific to the heart has been shown to be detrimental. The significant reduction in NAMPT expression observed in late-stage HF myocardial tissue may reflect the body’s self-regulatory responses during HF progression, warranting further investigation. SCAMP5 is linked to blood pressure [45], and both Stage 1 and Stage 2 hypertension are strongly associated with an increased incidence of HF in the general population [46]. SCAMP5 shows a significant decrease in expression in HF blood samples, which contrasts with tissue sample analysis results in online datasets, suggesting a need for further exploration of SCAMP5 and NAMPT roles in HF. HBA1, a gene encoding hemoglobin alpha globin, undergoes variations that enhance oxygen binding, contributing to hypoxia [47]. Hypoxia can both trigger HF and result from it, leading to clinical interventions like continuous low-flow oxygen therapy to improve cardiac function and myocardial oxygen supply [39]. The aberrant expression of HBA1 in HF group samples may disrupt oxygen intake, thereby influencing HF progression—a conclusion supported by blood sample validation results.

Single-gene functional enrichment analysis of key genes reveals that metabolic reprogramming of myocardial cells, characterized by reduced mitochondrial ketone body oxidation, is a hallmark of HF [48]. The ketone body metabolism pathway is enriched in both the LACTB2 and PRKAR2A high-expression groups. Notably, reduced expression of LACTB2 and PRKAR2A in blood or tissue samples from patients with HF suggests that the downregulation of these genes—and the consequent reduction in ketone body metabolism—plays a pivotal role in both the early and late stages of HF development. Autophagy, which recycles organelles such as ribosomes and the endoplasmic reticulum, is closely linked to HF, with dysregulation contributing to disease progression [49]. Our findings indicate a significant association between ribosome-related signaling pathways and the low expression of SCAMP5, as well as the high expression of LACTB2. Overexpressed LACTB2 may trigger PINK1-dependent mitochondrial autophagy [50], whereas SCAMP5 appears less involved in autophagy but instead may work with SNAREs to enhance cytokine exocytosis through Ca2+-mediated processes [51]. Reduced sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ uptake, a key feature of HF, may be related to the early-stage decrease in SCAMP5 expression observed in blood samples. Additionally, abnormalities in ER-synthesized proteins can impair Ca2+ uptake [52], leading to ER stress [53], and prolonged ER stress can induce apoptosis [54]. This study also found that protein processing in the ER and the insulin signaling pathway are enriched in the NAMPT high-expression and SCAMP5 low-expression groups. Insulin resistance, which alters systemic and neurohumoral environments, increases the risk of HF by affecting cardiac metabolism and signaling pathways [55]. The association of NAMPT high expression with apoptosis-related pathways suggests that the significantly decreased expression of NAMPT in late-stage HF myocardial tissue may be linked to alleviated ER stress and apoptosis pathways. Moreover, molecular pathways such as TNF-α (a pro-inflammatory cytokine) [56], HIF-1α (a transcription factor), and NF-κB (an inflammatory nuclear transcription factor) [57]—all of which regulate HF progression—are enriched in the NAMPT high-expression group. Inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway in mice has shown beneficial effects in HF [58], highlighting the potential regulatory role of NAMPT in HF. Additionally, HBA1 overexpression in HF is primarily associated with enrichment in the intestinal immune network affecting IgA production. Emerging evidence underscores the role of the gut microbiome in cardiovascular disease, with microbial imbalances observed in patients with HF [59]. Thus, it is speculated that these genes may promote disease progression through these biological processes.

Wesley et al. identified significant alterations in the monocyte-to-T cell ratio in patients with HF [60]. Neutrophils and Monocytes/Macrophages play pivotal roles in cardiac tissue repair [61]. Immune infiltration analysis revealed a significantly higher proportion of various immune cells in HF samples compared to controls, underscoring the beneficial impact of heightened immune infiltration in cardiac repair. Additionally, the expression of key genes—LACTB2, NAMPT, and PRKAR2A—exhibited a significant negative correlation with immune cells, whereas SCAMP5 and HBA1 showed a significant positive correlation with differential immune cells, consistent with their expression patterns in HF samples. Moreover, the strong correlation of NAMPT, SCAMP5, and HBA1 with Endothelial cells and T cells, alongside their differential expression in patients with PPCM, suggests that PPCM may involve the infiltration of Endothelial cells and T cells [61].

This study, however, has certain limitations. While mRNA expression of key genes was validated in five pairs of blood samples, the differential expression analysis between tissue and blood samples, as well as the functional analysis, requires validation with a larger cohort of clinical samples. Furthermore, although the initial integration of gene expression and ROC analysis assessed the predictive power of key genes for HF induced by various etiologies, further evaluation and diagnostic refinement necessitate the collection of additional clinical samples. Finally, both datasets used in the current study are derived from left ventricular tissues of advanced HF patients, which limits their potential application in early HF diagnosis. To overcome this limitation, future study designs should consider including samples from early-stage HF and expanding the sample size of the datasets to ensure data representativeness. This approach aims to identify genes with early diagnostic value to facilitate the early detection and intervention of HF.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has, for the first time, identified key genes associated with the progression of HF in relation to m6A methylation and immune cells. These findings lay the groundwork for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying HF and present potential targets for clinical diagnosis and treatment.
