Re-appraisal of dental clearance protocol before anti-resorptive agents for osteoporosis using the modified Delphi technique
Authors: Sarah Kho Xian Chua, Ruth Wei Choe, Catherine Hsu Ling Hong, Chee Weng Yong, Chien Joo Sim, Chong Meng Tay, Gabriel Keng Yan Lee, Jacinta Xiaotong Lu, Jing Li Lum, Joanne Ngo Uy, Mei Na Tan, Ren Jie Jacob Chew, Wataru Ode, Wendy Chia-Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients undergoing anti-resorptive agent (ARA) therapy are at risk of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ). Although existing guidelines encourage dental clearance prior to the initiation of ARA therapy, details on risk-appropriate treatment planning are insufficient.MethodsThis mixed-method study established a 13-person multidisciplinary expert panel to generate consensus statements through a modified Delphi process. Seven domains and 29 statements were identified through a comprehensive literature review and inputs from the expert panel. After two rounds of anonymous voting, 23 out of 29 statements achieved consensus.DiscussionDomain 1 recommended the tailoring of treatment plans to the patients’ medical history, dental history, social history, urgency of care, and life expectancy. Domain 2 recommended the removal of non-functional and misaligned teeth only if they are symptomatic. Domains 3 and 4 recommended that the management of caries and pulpal and periapical diseases in asymptomatic teeth does not have to precede the initiation of ARA therapy. Domain 5 recommended the removal of teeth with advanced periodontal disease but did not support the necessity of complete resolution of deep periodontal pockets prior to the initiation of ARA therapy. Domain 6 identified indications for the removal of dental implants prior to the initiation of ARA therapy and recommended the deferral of the management of most prosthesis-associated issues. Domain 7 which concerned the management of paediatric patients, failed to achieve consensus for any statements. This is congruent with the lack of evidence in the management of paediatric patients planned for ARA therapy.ConclusionThe consensus statements encourage clinicians to customise a risk-appropriate dental clearance protocol for each patient referred for dental clearance prior to ARA therapy for osteoporosis.

Introduction

Anti-resorptive agents (ARA) inhibit osteoclastic activity and modulate bone turnover and are prescribed for two main purposes: the prevention of osteoporotic fractures and the treatment of bone malignancies [1, 2]. A relatively uncommon complication associated with ARA therapy is the development of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ). MRONJ is characterised by the persistence of exposed bone in the maxilla or mandible that fails to heal within eight weeks, occurring in patients with a history of ARA treatment [1]. MRONJ may occur spontaneously or be precipitated by acute or chronic odontogenic infections or by dentoalveolar procedures such as extractions and mucosal irritation from dentures [1]. As such, the prevention of MRONJ through dental clearance prior to the initiation of ARA therapy is routinely advocated since the first clinical practice guidelines in 2006 [3].

Dental clearance typically entails a comprehensive oral examination and completion of the medically necessary dental treatment before commencing ARA treatment [4]. The aim of dental clearance is to eliminate or treat potential or existing dental pathology to minimise the need for future dental extractions that may elicit MRONJ [4].

Decision making is straightforward for teeth with a clearly unfavourable prognosis (e.g., retained roots, teeth with periodontal bone loss to the apex) or those with clearly favourable prognosis (e.g., teeth with only shallow caries that are not near the dental pulp). However, decision making surrounding the management of teeth with borderline prognoses is clinically challenging. This difficulty arises from several factors including the limitations of current risk assessment and dental prognostic tools [5], and the lack of longitudinal information that could help clinicians better understand the rate of disease progression. An aggressive treatment approach that advocates for the removal of all teeth with a borderline prognosis may effectively reduce the risk of MRONJ, however the resulting loss of multiple teeth can compromise patients’ oral function, overall health, and quality of life [6], underscoring the need to reassess these practices [1]. Furthermore, the risk of MRONJ in patients with osteoporosis is low at < 0.02–0.05% [1]. Even in the event of MRONJ occurrence, early surgical intervention combined with adjunctive therapies such as pentoxifylline, tocopherol, and teriparatide may be effective in halting its progression and reducing the morbidity [1, 7, 8].

With the low prevalence of MRONJ in patients with osteoporosis and better appreciation of the benefits of early surgical intervention, a standardised “complete” clearance approach similar to that conducted for patients on ARA therapy for oncological reasons is likely inappropriate [1]. Instead, dental clearance for osteoporotic patients should be customised based on individual risk profiles. Similar notions were recommended by the Scottish Dental Clinical Effectiveness Programme (SDCEP) [9]. The SDCEP suggested for the patient’s risk for MRONJ to be classified according to the type of anti-resorptive agent and other potential confounding medical conditions or medications [9]. It was also recommended for preventive advice to be personalised and to prioritise care that reduce mucosal trauma or may help prevent future oral surgeries [9]. However, specific and detailed guidance on the management on customising dental clearance is lacking in current guidelines. Therefore, this study seeks to develop a set of consensus statements for risk-appropriate dental clearance before ARA therapy for osteoporotic patients using a modified Delphi method.

Materials and methods

This is a mixed-methods study using a modified Delphi method approach to develop a set of clinical consensus statements on risk-appropriate dental clearance for patients with osteoporosis and are planned to receive ARA treatment (NUS-IRB-2024-841) [10]. The AGREE II framework was referenced in the preparation of this manuscript [11].

Generation of consensus statements

A scientific taskforce was established (CMT, CWY, JRJC, RWC, and SKXC) to conduct a literature review on the current recommendations regarding dental clearance prior to the initiation of ARA therapy for patients with osteoporosis. The following MeSH terms and keywords were used in the PubMed database search from inception till 3rd June 2024: Anti-resorptive, Bisphosphonate related osteonecrosis of the jaw, Bisphosphonate-associated osteonecrosis of the jaw [MeSH], BRONJ, Dental, Dental Care [MeSH], Dental clearance, Dental evaluation, Osteoporosis, Osteoporosis [MeSH], Medication related osteonecrosis of the jaw, and MRONJ. Publications which investigated, reported, or reviewed (i) the role of dental clearance on the prevention of MRONJ, (ii) dental disease risk assessment and prognostication for patients undergoing dental clearance, (iii) partial dental clearance, or (iv) compared between partial and complete dental clearance protocols were included. Studies were excluded if they were not written in English, presented as posters, conducted in-vitro, focussed on MRONJ prevention after initiation of ARA therapy, or evaluated treatment of established MRONJ. The screening and reviewing process were conducted independently by two members of the scientific taskforce (CWY, SKXC). Disagreements were resolved by seeking the opinion of a third member (JRJC). The title and abstract of 6487 publications were screened. Of these, 322 full-length articles were reviewed, and a final list of 25 publications were used as reference materials to evaluate current recommendations regarding dental clearance prior to initiating ARA for patients with osteoporosis. The included studies focused mostly on recommendations of dental clearance prior to ARA therapy, prevalence of MRONJ, risk factors of MRONJ and prevention of MRONJ after commencement of ARA therapy. Although there were limited studies which evaluated complete and partial dental clear protocols in oncologic patients, none were performed for ARA therapy. After three rounds of discussion, the task force identified seven domains and developed 29 statements addressing various clinical dilemmas in dental clearance for validation. These domains and statements were based on the global view provided by the literature review, the identified absence in recommendations, and the experts’ clinical experience in the management of patients undergoing dental clearance for osteoporosis.

Delphi process

The 29 statements were validated using the modified Delphi process which comprised of two rounds of anonymous electronic voting conducted via a web-based platform (https://www.qualtrics.com/), and an electronic forum (Fig. 1). An expert panel was formed and comprised of 13 senior members in the disciplines of Advanced General Dentistry, Endodontics, Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Oral Medicine, Periodontics, and Prosthodontics at the National University Centre for Oral Health, Singapore. The National University Centre for Oral Health, Singapore is one of Singapore’s two major national dental centres and provides tertiary dental care in a hospital setting, with the centre also having close ties with the National University of Singapore’s Faculty of Dentistry. All disciplines were represented by two clinicians including the Head of Division and another senior member in the discipline, except for Oral Medicine whereby there was only one full-time staff in this discipline. All expert panellists are routinely involved in either dental clearance or the management of oral complications related to ARA therapy. The panellists also declared no conflicts of interest.Fig. 1Flowchart of the Modified Delphi Process

Flowchart of the Modified Delphi Process

In the first round of voting, the expert panel members independently rated the 29 statements on a 9-point scale (1–3: Disagree, 4–6: Uncertain, 7–9: Agree) to indicate their level of agreement with each statement. Panel members also provided open-ended feedback or explanations for their responses. The qualitative and quantitative (i.e., median and mean scores attained for each statement) responses were collated and anonymously shared with all the expert panel members. The scientific taskforce amended the consensus statements based on the feedback received (Table 1). A pre-voting discussion on the amended statements was conducted with the expert panel via an anonymous online platform allowing members to raise and clarify any concerns. Discussion concluded once no further amendments were proposed. The amended statements were circulated to the expert panellists for a second round of voting. Consensus was deemed to be achieved for each statement when at least 11/13 (> 80%) votes selected the same response category (i.e., agree, uncertain or disagree). Consensus outcomes

A 100% participation rate by the expert panel was achieved in both rounds of voting. After the first round of voting, 18 out of the 29 statements achieved an “agree” consensus from the expert panel. Of these, four statements were revised in response to panel feedback to enhance clarity in the phrasing, despite having met the consensus threshold. The 11 statements that did not achieve consensus were amended based on the feedback. At the second round of voting, 23 out of 29 statements achieved an “agree” consensus. Five out of the 11 statements which did not achieve consensus in the first round of voting achieved an “agree” consensus (Table 1). No further disputes were raised after the second and final round of voting.

Domain 1: Risk-appropriate care

This domain covered eight statements (statements 1 to 8) which underscored the concept of customising risk-appropriate treatment plans for patients with osteoporosis who are planned for ARA therapy. All statements in this domain achieved the consensus of “agree” in the first round of voting. The dental clearance treatment plan should be modified according to the patient’s planned ARA therapy (route, agent), medical, social and dental histories, age and life expectancy, and amount of time available for dental treatment before initiation of ARA therapy.

Domain 2: impacted and misaligned teeth

This domain covered three statements (statements 9 to 11) and addressed the extraction or retention of misaligned and impacted teeth in the dental clearance treatment plan. Two out of three statements achieved the consensus of “agree” (Statements 10 and 11) in the first round of voting. Statement 9 did not achieve consensus. Although the level of agreement for this statement increased from 69.2% to 76.9% in the second round after the phrases “non-functional” and “not amenable to oral hygiene” were removed, the statement failed to meet the minimum threshold for consensus (80%).

Domain 3: caries

This domain covered eight statements (statements 12 to 19) on the management of carious teeth. Six out of eight statements achieved consensus after the two rounds of voting. Non-restorable teeth or teeth with guarded or poor prognosis were recommended for removal. In contrast, treatment is usually not required for asymptomatic non-carious cervical lesion.

In cases where pulp involvement is unlikely and the tooth is deemed restorable, restorative treatment does not have to precede the initiation of ARA therapy. Silver diamine fluoride may be used as an interim measure to arrest caries [12] to avoid delaying the initiation of ARA therapy. Statement 15, on the management of teeth with caries that were not in close proximity to the pulp, achieved consensus after an amendment clarifying that treatment should only be deferred if the tooth was asymptomatic.

Statements 17 and 19 did not achieve consensus. The expert panel members considered the disassembly of prostheses a low priority that would unnecessarily cause delays in the initiation of ARA therapy. Similarly, topical fluoride application was also considered low priority and of limited benefit unless patients were deemed to have a high caries risk. Despite amending both statements to incorporate the expert panel’s recommendations, neither statement achieved consensus in the second round.

Domain 4: pulpal and peri-apical diseases

The two statements in this domain (Statements 20 and 21) concerned the management of pulpal and peri-apical diseases, and both statements achieved consensus. Statement 20 originally recommended that endodontic therapy should ideally be initiated, and the tooth reviewed prior to the initiation of ARA therapy. The panel disagreed with the statement and recommended that endodontic therapy could be deferred for asymptomatic teeth. In the second round of voting, the statement achieved consensus after it was amended to state that endodontic therapy could be deferred to after initiation of ARA therapy.

Domain 5: periodontal disease

The three statements in this domain centred on the management of periodontal disease (Statements 22 to 24). Two out of three statements achieved consensus (Statements 22 and 24). Statement 22 required minor modifications to achieve consensus in the second round of voting. No consensus was reached on whether a single session of scaling and root debridement should precede the initiation of ARA therapy (Statement 23). Although the statement was refined to apply the criteria only for patients with severe periodontitis or significant calculus, 53.9% of the panel voted that they were “uncertain” on this recommendation and consensus was not achieved.

Domain 6: management of dental implants

This domain covered three statements (statements 25 to 27) on the management of dental implants. Two out of three statements failed to achieved consensus in the first round of voting (Statements 26 and 27). The original statement 26 was revised to clarify the definition of minor technical complications and to remove the need for prosthesis adjustment prior to the initiation of ARA therapy. The original statement 27 was amended to incorporate the panel’s feedback that dental implants with risk of peri-implant disease should be managed. All statements achieved consensus in the second round of voting.

Domain 7: management of paediatric patients

Both statements in this domain (Statements 28 and 29) failed to achieve consensus despite amendments. In the second round of voting, the majority of the expert panel remained “uncertain” with 92.3% and 53.9% selecting this option for Statements 28 and 29, respectively.

Final review of statements

No concerns or disagreements were raised by the expert panellists with regard to the final statements.

Discussion

Dental clearance is a collaborative effort between dental and medical practitioners to optimise treatment outcomes [13–15]. Ideally, the clearance process should strike a delicate balance between effectively minimizing the risk of future dental complications, avoiding unnecessary delays in the initiation of ARA therapy, and minimizing the impact on patients’ overall oral health. Dental practitioners may harbour concerns that inadequate dental clearance could expose patients to the risk of developing MRONJ [13–15]. This is a valid concern as MRONJ frequently requires surgical management and is often associated with additional complications and disease recurrence [1]. Conversely, medical practitioners may worry about the risk of osteoporotic fractures if ARA therapy is delayed while waiting for dental clearance to be completed, or that patients may be deterred from proceeding with ARA treatment after hearing about the dental treatment required as part of dental clearance. Therefore, this study aimed to provide recommendations on risk-appropriate treatment planning which would better balance the treatment objectives.

At the same time, oral health has been established as a cornerstone of healthy ageing, with the number and condition of teeth playing a crucial role in an individual’s nutritional status and quality of life [6, 16, 17]. In managing dental clearance, practitioners must also balance two crucial objectives: fulfilling clearance requirements to minimise risks associated with ARA therapy and safeguarding long-term oral health. This dual mandate necessitates careful consideration of immediate medical needs and future quality of life, underscoring the complexity of dental clearance in the context of ARA therapy and highlights the need for thoughtful, patient-centred decision-making.

The decision-making process regarding tooth preservation is often complex and multifaceted. Tanna et al. highlighted how the absence of clear guidelines impedes the dental management of osteoporotic patients [14]. Although guidelines from the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (AAOMS), European Calcified Tissue Society and Royal Australian College of General Practitioners have highlighted the differences in MRONJ risk when ARA is prescribed for osteoporosis versus malignancy [1, 18] and the importance of dental evaluation and treatment, they do not sufficiently guide the dental practitioners in their treatment planning. As existing guidelines do not provide sufficient information with regards to our research aims, adaptation from current recommendations was deemed unfeasible. Consequently, a modified Delphi method was determined to be the most appropriate approach for this study, allowing for the development of consensus-based recommendations to address the gaps in current practice guidelines.

Overall, the consensus indicated a preference for a less stringent approach to dental clearance for osteoporotic patients planned for ARA therapy. The panel could have been inclined towards a more conservative approach, where the decision making for these patients does not deviate too far from that for a regular patient (i.e., not planned for ARA therapy), with the exception that teeth with poor prognosis or risk of infection or which are likely to require dental extractions should not be left in situ.

This approach offers several advantages. Firstly, it reduces potential treatment delays, allowing patients to commence ARA therapy more promptly. Secondly, a simplified treatment plan involving fewer extractions or extensive restorative procedures may be more acceptable to patients, potentially improving treatment compliance and overall patient satisfaction.

Nevertheless, it is imperative to emphasise that this conservative approach does not negate the significance of comprehensive dental assessment and requisite interventions preceding the initiation ARA therapy. The primary objective remains the mitigation MRONJ risk, while concurrently striking a balance between the necessity for prompt medical intervention and patient well-being.

The statements from domains 2 to 7 aim to guide disease-specific treatment planning, however clinicians should exercise judgment based on the considerations outlined in domain 1. The results of domain 2 reflected the conservative approach by the experts, particularly in the recommendation to retain asymptomatic impacted teeth and non-functional teeth. This is due to the comparatively lower risk of MRONJ in patients on ARA therapy for osteoporosis (up to 0.05%), compared to the risk in patients for whom ARA therapy is indicated due to malignancy (up to 5%), or the risk of osteoradionecrosis with high dose head and neck radiotherapy (up to 17.6%) [19, 20].

Domains 3 and 4 identified parts of the dental treatment plan that do not need to precede the initiation of ARA therapy in patients with osteoporosis. These include the management of carious or necrotic teeth which have been deemed to be restorable. In addition, interim measures such as temporary fillings and silver diamine fluoride were recommended, to stabilise the teeth till definitive treatment can be rendered, if required. However, clinicians should be mindful that silver diamine fluoride results in irreversible black staining that may compromise aesthetics [12]. Domain 4 also addressed the management of endodontically treated teeth with persistent apical lesions. While the panel outlined a series of clinical and radiographic parameters to guide decision-making for such teeth, patients should still be informed of the low risk for future flare-up (reported to be 5.8% 20 years after primary treatment [21]), as well as the potential need for future treatment and the associated risks (which may include MRONJ) if such persistent apical lesions are left alone.

In contrast to the previous domains, domains 5 and 6 advocated for the removal of teeth and dental implants with poor prognosis and which are predisposed to infection, or which are likely to require future extractions. Notably, the extraction of teeth with periodontitis and explantation of dental implants with peri-implantitis is associated with an increased risk of MRONJ [2, 22].

Domain 7, which concerned paediatric dental patients, failed to achieve consensus. The lack of consensus may be attributed to the scarcity of clinical guidelines and literature specifically addressing paediatric dental clearance before ARA therapy, such that the effects of ARA therapy on paediatric patients and their susceptibility to MRONJ remain unclear [23]. Current recommendations are largely extrapolated from adult data, which may not be directly applicable to children due to differences in bone physiology, with paediatric bones harbouring greater osteogenic and remodelling potential than adult bone. The latest position paper from the AAOMS highlighted the limited data on MRONJ occurrence in the paediatric population, particularly for conditions such as osteogenesis imperfecta [24]. To date, there have been no cases of MRONJ reported with sufficient evidence among the paediatric population. Given these uncertainties, most panellists responded with “uncertain”, emphasising the need for further research and expert consensus in this area.

The modified Delphi method, while valuable, has inherent limitations [25]. One potential concern is the risk of bias in the selection of the initial statements for discussion by the scientific taskforce. Secondly, although the intention of sharing the interim results (after the first round) was to facilitate consensus by giving each expert a broader view of the panel’s collective opinions, this approach may inadvertently introduce peer pressure. Thirdly, although the experts were requested to base their feedback on scientific evidence, the limited availability of primary studies on dental clearance and clinical outcomes in patients on ARA limited constrained this approach. Consequently, it can be expected that some decisions were based on expert opinion. This limitation also influenced the decision not to conduct a third round of voting to achieve consensus on all 29 statements, as sufficient scientific evidence was lacking. Despite its limitations, the modified Delphi method remains a robust approach for synthesizing expert knowledge in areas with limited empirical evidence. Future research should address these gaps to strengthen the evidence base for dental management of patients on ARA therapy.

Nevertheless, consensus statements such as these represent only one component in the comprehensive care of dental patients planned for ARA therapy. Further investigation is needed to determine the necessity of universal dental clearance referrals for all osteoporotic patients planned for ARA therapy, and to develop a reliable triaging tool that can be utilised by medical professionals to sieve out patients who would truly benefit from dental clearance. There should also be a greater emphasis on post-clearance oral health maintenance, as well as patient and caregiver education [26]. This would empower both patients and primary care providers to prevent MRONJ. In addition, more primary studies providing insights into the effectiveness and safety of a partial dental clearance protocol for these patients should be conducted.

It is crucial to recognise that dental clearance is not a guarantee against future oral health issues but rather a snapshot of the patient’s oral health at a specific point in time. Oral health is a dynamic, ongoing process integral to a patient’s overall well-being, particularly in managing systemic conditions like osteoporosis. Healthy, functional teeth are essential to ensure patients’ masticatory ability and maintain a nutritious diet. We thus need to shift from defensive practices that prioritise concerns about future problems at the expense of current quality of life. A more holistic approach is necessary, balancing clearance requirements with long-term considerations. This approach recognises that maintaining good oral health is an ongoing process requiring continuous attention and care from both patients and healthcare providers.

The implementation process as important as the consensus statements. While it is anticipated that the lack of other detailed guidance will generate interest in the dental community, it will be useful to first determine the practices in dental clearance within each local community, identify areas of deficits and possibly modifying the wordings of the statements to fit each community. A pre-test and post-test will be meaningful in determine the recipients understand the intentions of the statements. The target community or recipients must also be informed that the statements are not hard and fast rules. Lastly, changes in patient’s experiences, treatment outcome and prevalence of MRONJ after the implementation of the consensus statements should also be evaluated.

Conclusion

This study gathered 13 experts to generate a series of consensus statements to facilitate dental treatment planning for osteoporotic patients planned for ARA therapy. Through a modified Delphi process, 23 out of 29 statements achieved consensus after two rounds. In general, the statements encourage clinicians to customise a risk-appropriate dental clearance protocol for each patient. Given the lower risk of serious complications after ARA for patients with osteoporosis, a strict and aggressive dental treatment plan appears unnecessary. While the statements are likely applicable for adult patients, further evidence is needed to inform recommendations for paediatric patients.
