Enable CAR T cell immunotherapy in glioblastoma by modifying its microenvironment via oncolytic adenovirus encoding bispecific T cell engager
Authors: Moon Jung Choi, Eui Young So, Bedia Akosman, Young Eun Lee, Alexander G Raufi, Anthony M Reginato, Clark C Chen, Sean E Lawler, Eric T Wong, Olin D Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent clinical trials show that CAR T cell therapies can initially blunt tumor growth in patients with glioblastoma (GBM). However, therapeutic efficacy remains limited by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and restricted immune cell trafficking across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). To counteract these challenges, we have utilized the oncolytic adenovirus (OV) Ad5-Δ24-RGD as a platform to overexpress a bispecific T cell engager (BiTE) targeting both CD3 on T cells and the GBM-specific tumor associated antigen IL-13Rα2. We first demonstrated that OV-BiTE can significantly increase the recruitment of T cells to GBM, both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, OV-BiTE treatment also markedly enhanced CAR T cell infiltration and tumor cytotoxicity in a GBM-BBB spheroid model, possibly through downregulation of endothelial junction protein expression. We then showed that intratumoral injection of OV-BiTE followed by infusion of combined EGFR and EGFRvIII CAR T cells was more effective than OV-BiTE supplemented with either CAR T therapy alone and led to significant tumor reduction in a GBM xenograft mouse model. In conclusion, our multimodal OV-BiTE plus CAR T cell immunotherapy is capable of overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and GBM resistance to treatment.Keywords: MT: Regular Issue, chimeric antigen receptor T, CAR T cell, immunotherapy, glioblastoma, tumor microenvironment, oncolytic adenovirus, immune cell engager, bispecific T cell engager, T cell recruitment, blood-brain barrier

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most lethal primary brain cancer, accounting for the majority of all brain tumor cases.1,2,3,4 The average survival duration for patients with GBM is 12–18 months after diagnosis, and the 5-year survival rate is less than 5%.5,6 Radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and surgical debulking remain the current standards of care for malignant glioma, but even tumor resection is difficult because of its location and the potential for neurological impairment.7 Its highly aggressive nature, molecular heterogeneity, the ability of resistant cancer stem cells to regrow post-therapy, the invasion of critical regions of the brain, and the inadequacy of achieving high therapeutic levels of chemotherapeutics in the brain because of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) are some of the key factors that constitute the vast amount of unmet need in patients with GBM.8 Immunotherapeutic agents like immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs),9,10 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells,11,12,13 and oncolytic viruses (OVs)14,15,16 have revolutionized cancer therapy in some tumor types. However, their success in GBM remains limited because the GBM tumor microenvironment is highly immunosuppressive, rendering T cells incapable of recognizing and eliminating malignant cells.17 Hence, there is a critical need to develop innovative and effective immunotherapies for GBM.

CAR T cell therapy uses autologous T cells engineered to target specific tumor antigens expressed on the surface of tumor cells for tumor eradication. CAR T cell therapies have produced sustained therapeutic effects in refractory hematological cancers, but their success in the treatment of solid tumors has been limited.18,19,20 The efficacy of CAR T cells is restricted in GBM mainly because of the high degree of tumor heterogeneity, the BBB, and a significantly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Several CAR T cell therapies targeting a range of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) such as EGFR, EGFRvIII, IL-13Rα2, HER2, B7-H3, and CD-147 are currently under clinical investigation in GBM.21 The loss of target antigen expression by glioma cells renders CAR T cell therapies ineffective, but targeting different tumor antigens can help overcome tumor resistance due to variable antigen expression.20 The three latest phase I clinical trials have demonstrated the feasibility, safety, and transient efficacy of intracerebroventricular delivered CAR T cells for the treatment of GBM in patients.22,23,24 However, despite their clinical significance, there are limitations to the utility of CAR T therapies.25 Collecting T cells from a patient is costly and time-consuming, especially for those with compromised immune systems. It is well established that T cells are responsible for graft-versus-host disease in patients. Additionally, CAR T cell therapy may cause severe side effects, such as cytokine release syndrome or neurotoxicity. Finally, CAR T cells struggle to penetrate the tumor microenvironment, which diminishes their therapeutic effectiveness. Hence, CAR T cell therapy needs further optimization in GBM, with improved accessibility to the brain, tumor cell targeting, survivability in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and the ability to proliferate and exert therapeutic effects with minimal toxicities.

OV therapy uses replication-competent viruses that can selectively replicate and kill cancer cells. Direct oncolysis releases a wide range of TAAs, danger-associated molecular patterns, and viral pathogen-associated molecular patterns, which trigger inflammatory immune responses in the tumor microenvironment.26 Most OVs that are in clinical trials in patients with GBM are being delivered locally to achieve an effective viral load in the tumors. A number of OVs are being tested both at the preclinical and clinical levels in GBM, among which some promising OV candidates are adenovirus (DNX-2401 [Ad5-Δ24-RGD]),14 poliovirus (PVS-RIPO),27 and retrovirus (Toca 511).28 Ad5-Δ24-RGD is a conditionally replicative oncolytic adenovirus specifically engineered to destroy cancer cells. A 24-base pair deletion in the retinoblastoma (Rb)-binding domain of the E1A viral gene confers this adenovirus with selectivity for cancer cells,29,30 and an integrin-binding RGD peptide has been incorporated into the H1 loop of the fiber to broaden tropism.31 Results from both preclinical32,33,34,35,36,37 and clinical studies14,38,39,40,41 indicate that Ad5-Δ24-RGD persists in human tumors for weeks, elicits tumor necrosis, triggers intratumoral immune cell infiltration, has no safety concerns, and can lead to long-term patient benefit. However, following OV treatment, an antiviral immune response mediated by natural killer (NK) cells and macrophages may limit viral replication and mitigate the oncolytic effects. GBM has an “immune-cold” or noninflammatory tumor microenvironment,42 which has been challenging for monotherapies with ICIs or CAR T cells. Since OVs can increase immune cell infiltration, which could be crucial in breaking immune tolerance and improving tumor responsiveness to immunotherapies, Ad5-Δ24-RGD may serve as a primer for enhancing the efficacy of ICIs or CAR T cells. To this end, there is an ongoing clinical trial evaluating the combination of Ad5-Δ24-RGD and anti-PD1 antibody for GBM (NCT02798406). Moreover, the combination of an oncolytic adenovirus expressing IL-7 with B7-H3-targeted CAR T cells showed higher efficacy than a single use of either of those treatments.43 In addition, the combination of OV with standard therapeutics like chemo- and radiotherapy can exert a synergistic effect in heterogeneous GBM.44 As a novel type of immunotherapy, a bispecific T cell engager (BiTE) combines two single-chain variable fragments (scFv) from different antibodies, one targeting CD3ε and the other targeting a TAA on the surface of tumor cells, which are connected by a short, flexible linker. Functionally, BiTE has unique therapeutic advantages as a specific tumor immunotherapy without the requirement of MHC-1 antigen presentation to activate T cells, form artificial immune synapses, and release perforin and granzyme to exert cytotoxic effects.45 When OV is used as a monotherapy, an antiviral immune response mediated by NK cells and macrophages may limit viral replication and mitigate the oncolytic effects. Thus, the synergy between OV and BiTE deserves special attention. We further posit that OV can be used as a genetic engineering platform to express BiTE, which combines two different immunotherapies as one therapeutic agent. In addition, the selective replication of OV in tumors can limit the expression of BiTE to tumors, reducing the possibility of non-tumor side effects of BiTE.

The rationale for the current study is 3-fold: (i) to turn a non-responding “immune-cold” GBM tumor into a responsive “immune-hot” tumor through OV treatment; (ii) to recruit host cytotoxic T cells through BiTE to kill IL13Rα2-expressing GBM tumor cells in an MHC-1 independent manner; (iii) to enable infused CAR T cells to target GBM heterogeneity by killing EGFR- and EGFRvIII-expressing tumor cells. Here, we show that intratumoral injection of OV-BiTE followed by infusion of combined EGFR and EGFRvIII CAR T cells can lead to significant tumor reduction in a GBM xenograft mouse model, and this multipronged approach is superior to OV or CAR T immunotherapy alone. In conclusion, our multimodal OV-BiTE plus CAR T cell immunotherapy is capable of overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and GBM resistance to treatment.

Results

Generation of CAR T cells targeting GBM and profiling of CAR T cell cytotoxic cytokines

Three 3rd-generation lentiviral CAR constructs (Figures 1A and S1) were generated, and CAR T cells targeting GBM tumor antigens IL-13Rα2, EGFR, and EGFRvIII were produced through lentiviral transduction. In addition to being frequently invoked previously by many investigators in their studies, these three GBM tumor antigens were also utilized in recent clinical trials22,23,24 and were detectable via western blots in commonly referenced GBM cell lines, e.g., U87 and U251, but not in normal human astrocytes (Figure 1B). Gene expression and surface protein expression of the three targeted tumor antigens were also analyzed by PCR (Figure S2A) and flow cytometry (Figure S2B), respectively. Fluorescence microscopy (Figure 1C) and flow cytometry analyses (Figure S2C) indicated that T cell transduction efficiency in this study was approximately 30%. For the ELISPOT assays, different groups of T cells were co-cultured with either U87 or U251 cells for 24 h, after which cytokine-positive spots for perforin, granzyme B, and TNF-α were imaged and manually counted (Figure S3). When co-cultured with U87 GBM tumor cells, the production of both perforin and granzyme B was significantly elevated in all 3 CAR T cell groups. Specifically, the mean spot counts indicating perforin secretion increased by 1.83-fold for IL-13Rα2, 1.50-fold for EGFR, and 1.92-fold for EGFRvIII compared to vector control CD8+ T cells (Figure 1D, left). The mean spot counts indicating granzyme B secretion increased by 1.85-fold for IL-13Rα2, 2.43-fold for EGFR, and 2.82-fold for EGFRvIII (Figure 1D, middle). Furthermore, proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α production showed a significant 1.74-fold increase for EGFR (Figure 1D, right). Similarly, significantly enhanced cytokine production was also obtained when CAR T cells were co-cultured with U251 cells. Perforin secretion increased by 1.85-fold for IL-13Rα2 relative to vector control CD8+ T cells (Figure 1E, left). The mean spot counts indicating granzyme B secretion increased by 2.04-fold for IL-13Rα2 and 1.87-fold for EGFRvIII (Figure 1E, middle). TNF-α production was also upregulated in all CAR T cell groups, with 2.23-fold for IL-13Rα2, 2.39-fold for EGFR, and 2.51-fold for EGFRvIII (Figure 1E, right). Additionally, upon co-culture with U87 cells, EGFR- and EGFRvIII-targeted CAR T cells exhibited a marked increase in IL-2 secretion compared with control T cells in a human cytokine array (Figure S4). Given that IL-2 is a key cytokine associated with T cell activation and effector function, these results indicate that antigen-specific engagement of CAR T cells enhances T cell activation in the tumor context. In summary, we have generated 3rd-generation CAR T cells that can recognize GBM-specific tumor antigens, and binding leads to enhanced cytotoxic cytokine production.

In vitro 2D and 3D GBM tumor cell killing by CAR T cells

Stable luciferase-expressing U87 and U251 cell lines were generated through lentiviral transduction and puromycin selection of the GBM cells at Brown University Legorreta Cancer Center Cell & Genome Engineering Shared Resource. For the 2D tumor cell killing assays, U87- and U251-luciferase expressing cells (U87-Luc and U251-Luc) were co-cultured either with medium alone, untreated CD8+ T cells, vector control CD8+ T cells, IL-13Rα2 CAR T cells, EGFR CAR T cells, or EGFRvIII CAR T cells at a 1:1 effector-to-target (E:T) ratio (Figure 2A). In separate experiments, the E:T co-culture conditions were experimentally determined and luciferase activities were quantified after 24 h of incubation (Figure S5). As shown in Figure 2B, GBM tumor cell viability was significantly reduced in all CAR T cell-treated groups compared to the control groups. In the U87-Luc cell line, while the control groups (untreated CD8+ T cells and vector-transduced T cells) maintained relatively high viability at 79.8% and 80.7%, respectively, the antigen-specific groups showed a marked decline. Specifically, treatment with IL-13Rα2, EGFR, and EGFRvIII CAR T cells resulted in a substantial reduction in cell viability to 58.3%, 53.0%, and 50.0%, respectively. Similar results were also obtained with the U251-Luc cells.

Furthermore, based on a 3D tumor cell killing assay that was previously established elsewhere.46,47 U87- and U251-spheroids were co-cultured either with medium control or various T cell groups as above, followed by propidium iodide (PI) staining for dead or dying cells. PI-positive cells were then visualized using a fluorescence microscope, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified. In all CAR T cell-treated groups, the MFI values were significantly higher than those of the control groups (Figures 2C and 2D). In addition, we also established a GBM BBB spheroid model (Figure S6) to assess the tumor-killing ability of the CAR T cells. As shown in Figure 2E, both EGFR and EGFRvIII CAR T cells demonstrated significantly higher GBM tumor killing than CD8+ T cell controls. In summary, our IL-13Rα2, EGFR, and EGFRvIII CAR T cells can more effectively kill GBM tumor cells than CD8+ cytotoxicity T cell controls in both 2D and 3D in vitro assays.

Generation of oncolytic adenovirus Ad5-Δ24-RGD-BiTE, production of BiTE, and oncolysis

We have adopted the AdenoQuick2.0 system (O.D.260 Inc, Boise, ID) to construct recombinant adenoviral vectors (Figure S7) and to produce Ad5-Δ24-RGD and Ad5-Δ24-RGD-BiTE adenoviruses. Figure 3A depicts the structure of BiTE and its intended mode of action. In this study, we proposed that BiTE secretion from adenovirus-infected GBM cells will build a concentration gradient that can recruit endogenous and therapeutic T cells into the tumor microenvironment. To demonstrate BiTE expression, adenoviral construct plasmid DNA was transfected into 293FT cells, and His-tagged BiTE protein was detected by western blot using an antibody against the His-tag (Figure 3B). To demonstrate oncolysis of GBM tumor cells by the adenoviruses, U87-Luc and U251-Luc cells and their respective spheroids were incubated with OV and OV-BiTE. Effective oncolysis of GBM tumor cells by the adenoviruses in 2D and 3D in vitro models was shown in Figures 3C and 3D. Thus, we have successfully generated oncolytic adenoviruses Ad5-Δ24-RGD and Ad5-Δ24-RGD-BiTE, where BiTE production by the latter construct was also established. Furthermore, OV- and OV-BiTE-induced lysis of GBM tumor cells and spheroids in vitro was demonstrated as well. Additionally, we observed increased infiltration of EGFR CAR T cells and EGFRvIII CAR T cells into GBM-BBB spheroids following OV-BiTE treatment (Figure 3E). Combined, these findings suggested that OV-BiTE not only induces tumor-targeted lysis of GBM tumor cells and spheroids but also enhances T cell penetration of the BBB-like spheroids. Consistently, CD8+ T cells, EGFR CAR T cells, and EGFRvIII CAR T cells induced a marked downregulation of endothelial junction proteins, including claudin-5, occludin, and ZO-1 (Figure S8), providing a mechanistic basis for the increased immune cell penetration across the BBB-like barrier.

Enhanced T cell recruitment in vitro by OV-BiTE

Here, we conducted a transwell assay to demonstrate enhanced recruitment of T cells in vitro by OV-BiTE compared to OV alone. As illustrated in Figure 3F, CFSE-labeled GBM cells (green) grown on the bottom of the lower chambers were infected by OV-BiTE or OV. Adenovirus containing cell culture medium was replaced with fresh medium after 4 h. DiD-labeled CD3+ T cells (red) were then added to the upper chamber of the transwell. After 24 h of incubation, the upper chambers were removed and the transwells were briefly centrifuged to pellet all the cells in the lower chamber. Both red and green fluorescence labeled cells on the bottom of the transwells were analyzed with a fluorescence microscope (Figure 3F). As a result, significantly more red fluorescent CD3+ T cells were detected in OV-BiTE infected group than in the OV infected group. In summary, we have demonstrated a significantly higher T cell recruitment in vitro by OV-BiTE treated GBM cells than OV alone treated or untreated GBM cells.

Enhanced T cell recruitment in vivo by OV-BiTE

Next, we utilized a xenograft mouse model to determine whether the enhanced in vitro T cell recruitment by OV-BiTE over OV alone could be replicated in vivo. As illustrated in Figure 4A, GBM U87-Luc cells were subcutaneously injected into the left flank of immunodeficient mice, and the tumors were allowed to grow for 20 days, followed by intratumoral injection of OV-BiTE or OV alone as described elsewhere.48 Three days after adenoviral treatment, human CD3+ T cells were injected via the mouse tail vein. Another 3 days later, tumor tissues were explanted for analysis. The apparent size and weight of the isolated tumor masses from the OV-BiTE or OV-treated groups were significantly lower than those of the untreated GBM controls, with the OV-BiTE group exhibiting even greater tumor reduction than the OV group (Figures 4B and 4C). The tumor tissues were then homogenized into single-cell suspensions, and infiltrated human CD3+ T cell populations were quantified by flow cytometry. Figures 4D and 4E show that OV-BiTE-treated tumors had significantly more human CD3+ T cells than those in the OV-alone group. These findings were further supported by immunohistochemical staining of human CD3+ cells, which were visualized as dark-brown spots in the tumor tissue sections. The numbers of human CD3+ cells in the OV-BiTE group were significantly higher than those in the OV or untreated GBM control groups (Figures 4F and 4G). In summary, we have demonstrated significantly higher T cell recruitment in vivo in OV-BiTE-treated GBM tumors than in OV alone treated or untreated GBM tumors.

Combination of OV-BiTE and CAR T cells effectively reduces GBM tumors in mice

Given the abovementioned in vitro GBM-killing efficacy of OV-BiTE and CAR T cells, as well as the enhanced T cell recruitment by OV-BiTE in vivo, we proceeded to investigate the potential synergy of combining these two immunotherapies in a mouse GBM xenograft model (Figure 5A). All mice were subcutaneously injected with GBM U87-Luc cells on the left flank. Twelve days later, adenovirus was intratumorally injected as described elsewhere.48 Another 3 days later, human CD8+ T cells or therapeutic CAR T cells were infused via tail vein injection. On the final day of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were isolated. Meanwhile, GBM tumor growth in different treatment groups was monitored at indicated intervals from day 0 to day 31 using a luminescence in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Figure 5B). There were no body weight losses in the mice (Figure 5C), and the luminescence intensity of the region of interest (ROI) was analyzed for each mouse throughout the entire duration of the therapy (Figure 5D). On days 24 and 31, all three OV-BiTE plus CAR T cell groups showed significant reductions in tumor luminescence intensity compared to the CD8+ T cell-treated group (Figures 5E and 5F), where the OV-BiTE with combined CAR T group exhibited even greater efficacy than OV-BiTE combined with either EGFR or EGFRvIII CAR T cells alone (Figures 5G and 5H). These results were then validated by assessing the size and weight of the explanted tumor masses (Figures 5I–5L). To further establish the GBM-killing effects of our multipronged immunotherapy, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on all tumor explants. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that cellularity, as indicated by hematoxylin’s purple-blue nuclear staining, in the untreated GBM control and CD8+ T cell-treated groups was visibly higher than that in the three groups receiving OV-BiTE plus CAR T cell treatment (Figure 6A). IHC staining of GBM-specific tumor-associated antigen IL13R⍺2 was significantly higher in the untreated GBM control and CD8+ T cell-treated groups than in the immunotherapy groups (Figure 6B, upper, and C). IHC staining of human CD3+ T cells was significantly higher in the immunotherapy groups than in the untreated GBM control and CD8+ T cell-treated groups (Figure 6B, lower, and D). In summary, we have demonstrated that sequential application of OV-BiTE and CAR T cells can effectively inhibit the growth of GBM tumor in our mouse model and that OV-BiTE followed by combined CAR T cells exhibits even greater efficacy than OV-BiTE combined with either EGFR or EGFRvIII CAR T cells alone.

Discussion

GBM is one of the most difficult challenges in oncology, with its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment posing significant barriers to effective therapy. Conventional approaches such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy have demonstrated limited success in extending survival, underscoring the need for innovative approaches. Immunotherapy, particularly CAR T cell therapy, has emerged as a promising modality. Recent clinical trials utilizing CAR T cell therapies in adult and pediatric high-grade gliomas have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of intracranial CAR T cell delivery, and provided evidence of transient responses, supporting further investigations of this transformative approach to treating a formidably aggressive cancer.22,23,24,49,50,51 Currently, there is no universal CAR T cell target for GBM identified yet. Hence, combinations of targets and engineered CAR T cells expressing immunostimulatory molecules to enhance their efficacy should continue to be developed. Our current study intends to fill such a gap by introducing an innovative multimodal approach to overcome the challenges associated with CAR T therapy in solid tumors. By intratumorally administering an engineered oncolytic adenovirus to overexpress a BiTE, we could demonstrate significantly enhanced recruitment of both endogenous and therapeutic T cells to the tumor. This approach aimed to remodel the tumor microenvironment by converting immunologically “cold” tumors, characterized by low immune activity, into “hot” tumors with enhanced immune activation, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy. However, potential side effects associated with inducing a “hot” tumor microenvironment in situ within the brain may occur, where excessive immune activation can lead to neuroinflammation, cerebral edema, or cytokine-mediated neurotoxicity. In a recent clinical trial, 1–2.5 × 10e7 CAR T cells were delivered intrathecally to patients with GBM.23 In another recent clinical trial, 2 × 10e8 CAR T cells were applied intratumorally or intraventricularly per infusion cycle, and the investigators found that locoregional CAR T cell administration was feasible and well tolerated in patients.22 In an earlier preclinical study utilizing an intracranial xenograft mouse model, 3 × 10e7 CAR T cells were injected via the tail vein and no adverse effects were reported.12 Nevertheless, our dosing and delivery strategy was conservatively designed to mitigate any potential risks. In comparison, we administered a substantially lower dose of 5 × 10e6 CAR T cells via a single intravenous injection. This reduced dosing was intentional because our OV-BiTE platform was designed to recruit both endogenous T cells and infused CAR T cells, as well as other activated immune cells within the tumor microenvironment (e.g., pro-inflammatory macrophages, NK cells, and dendritic cells), in order to achieve robust antitumor efficacy. We hoped that such a controlled and synergistic recruitment strategy would favor the conversion of the tumor into a “hot” immune niche while limiting off-target or brain-wide inflammatory responses. Consistent with this design rationale, our in vivo studies revealed no observable neurological deficits, weight loss, or other clinical signs of toxicity in treated mice, supporting the tolerability of this approach.

The BiTE was designed with two arms, one for IL-13R⍺2, which is specifically overexpressed on GBM cells and the other for CD3 on T cells. Through a BiTE-expressing oncolytic adenovirus, we expect increased cell lysis in tumor tissue and enhanced immune activity from T cells, thereby improving the therapeutic effect of CAR T cells. It is well established that both lytic and non-lytic cytokines are involved in the antitumor activities of CAR T cells.52 As part of the in vitro cytotoxicity evaluation, our CAR T cells were co-cultured with two different GBM cell lines at appropriate E:T ratios, and these engineered CAR T cells showed significantly higher cytokine production than that induced by primary CD8+ cells. Notably, the secretion of lytic cytokines, e.g., perforin and granzyme B, which are involved in direct induction of apoptosis, was particularly elevated. These results were further validated through antitumor toxicity evaluations using GBM cells and GBM spheroids. Differences in tumor antigen expression between the cell lines may account for the variability in significance among the CAR T groups. To evaluate whether CAR T cells could overcome the physical limitations of the tumor microenvironment, we also established a GBM BBB spheroid model constructed by layering GBM core spheroids with astrocytes, endothelial cells, and pericytes. CAR T cells induced significant tumor cell death even within the inner regions of the BBB spheroids, an effect that was corroborated by active downregulation of endothelial junction proteins and enhanced CAR T cell transmigration across the BBB-like barrier. This BBB-modulatory effect was further enhanced following OV-BiTE treatment, resulting in increased T cell infiltration into GBM-BBB spheroids and highlighting an additional mechanism by which OV-BiTE augments CAR T cell efficacy. While it is intriguing that our OV-BiTE platform may enhance the accessibility of CAR T cells to the tumor site by modulating endothelial junction function, a major barrier to successful immunotherapy in GBM, future studies including a more granular analysis of the underlying mechanisms—specifically the relative contributions of viral infection, BiTE-mediated T cell engagement, and local inflammatory cytokine production—would significantly enhance the impact of our current research.

In vivo tumor xenograft models were used to evaluate the functionality of BiTE. We utilized a subcutaneous GBM xenograft model, which enabled direct and reproducible intratumoral administration of the oncolytic adenovirus to rigorously assess local delivery, viral safety, and T cell recruitment dynamics. Because the primary objective of this study was to delineate the mechanistic synergy between the OV-BiTE payload and adoptive CAR T cell therapy, rather than to investigate central nervous system delivery barriers, the subcutaneous model provided a robust and quantifiable platform for evaluating localized immune activation and tumor clearance. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that an intracranial orthotopic model would more closely represent the GBM-specific microenvironment, including the BBB and neuroinflammatory risks. Thus, future studies using orthotopic models are warranted to further evaluate the clinical translatability and safety of our combination therapy. Mice treated with OV-BiTE showed significantly reduced tumor size and weight. Enhanced CD3+ T cell infiltration was also observed, likely due to BiTE secretion following adenovirus infection and lysis, facilitating T cell migration into the tumor. In the in vivo therapy experiments, both single CAR T and combi CAR T groups showed positive results, with the same number of CAR T cells but different anticancer therapeutic efficacy. The two single CAR T therapy groups showed no differences in tumor growth inhibition, but the combined CAR T cell group showed superiority compared to the two single CAR T cell groups. The observed results can be attributed to the highly heterogeneous nature of GBM,53,54,55 where a single tumor may express multiple tumor antigens. This suggests that utilizing two or more CAR T cells targeting different antigens is more effective than using either alone for GBM treatment. Unlike other multispecific or logic-gated CARs, which engineer multiple targets into a single T cell, our platform utilizes OV-delivered BiTEs to recruit a broader repertoire of both CAR T cells and endogenous T cells. This approach may provide more flexible coverage against the high heterogeneity of GBM compared to single-cell engineering strategies. Furthermore, H&E histology and IHC revealed a significant reduction in overall cellularity, decreased tumor-associated antigen IL-13R⍺2 expression, and increased CD3+ cell staining in tumor explants from the OV-BiTE plus combined CAR T cell group. In addition, we observed no side effects, such as body weight loss or hair loss, in animals during therapy.

In summary, our study has demonstrated robust antitumor efficacy of the multipronged OV-BiTE plus CAR T modality in preclinical GBM models. The potential clinical applications of this multimodal platform may address key challenges associated with CAR T cell immunotherapy in solid tumors, such as the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby extending CAR T cell utility beyond hematologic malignancies, where it has shown remarkable success. Moreover, the methodologies described in this study lay the groundwork for a novel combination treatment platform that synergizes the unique mechanisms of action of OVs, immune cell engagers, and cell-based immunotherapies. This approach may potentially transform the therapeutic landscape for solid tumors, providing a versatile framework for future translational studies and clinical applications. We anticipate that the principles and strategies presented here will contribute to the broader development of advanced immunotherapies for cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

GBM cell line U87 MG was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Cat# HTB-14, ATCC, Manassas, VA). GBM cell line U251 MG was purchased from Millipore Sigma (Cat# 9063001, St. Louis, MO). HEK293FT cells were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were purchased from Lonza (Walkersville, MD). All GBM cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 media (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (100×, Thermo Fisher Scientific). T cells were cultured in CTS OpTmizer T cell expansion SFM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, and L-glutamine (200 mM, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For lentivirus packaging HEK293FT cells were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All lentivirus production and transduction for generating luciferase-overexpressing cell lines were carried out by the Brown University Legorreta Cancer Center Cell & Genome Engineering Shared Resource.

T cell expansion and activation

CD8+ T cells were isolated from human PBMCs using the EasySep Human CD8+ T cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). After isolation, cells were counted using a TC20 automated cell counter (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). For cell expansion, Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 for T cell expansion and activation were added to the CD8+ T cells and cultured with IL-2 (PEPROTech, 100 IU/mL; Cranbury, NJ) in complete CTS OpTmizer T cell expansion SFM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To activate T cells before lentiviral transduction, the amount of Dynabeads was increased to twice the T cell number (1:1 for expansion and 2:1 for activation).

Generation of CAR T cells

Three lentiviral vectors encoding CAR constructs were custom-made based on pCDCAR1 by Creative Biolabs (Shirley, NY): IL13Rα2-scFv-CD28-4-1BB-CD3ζ-EGFP, EGFR-scFv-CD28-4-1BB-CD3ζ-EGFP, and EGFRvIII-scFv-CD28-4-1BB-CD3ζ-EGFP. High-titer lentiviruses were produced at the Brown University Legorreta Cancer Center Cell & Genome Engineering Shared Resource, followed by transduction of primary CD8+ T cells isolated from human PBMCs. For lentivirus production, 2 million HEK293FT cells were seeded in a 10 cm culture dish and incubated for 24 h in 10% DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). One day later, packaging plasmids (gag/pol, Tet, Rev, and VSVG) and a CAR vector were mixed with LipoD293 In Vitro DNA Transfection Reagent (SignaGen Laboratories, Frederick, MD), and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature (RT). The culture medium containing the transfection mixture was replaced with 10 mL fresh medium after 24 h of transfection. Culture medium was collected at 48 and 72 h and centrifuged to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and filtered using 0.45 μm syringe filters. To concentrate lentivirus, Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara Bio USA, Inc., San Jose, CA) was added to the supernatant at a 1:3 ratio, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h on ice, followed by centrifugation at 1,500 g for 1.5 h. The supernatant was then discarded, and the pellets containing lentivirus were resuspended in 100 μL HBSS solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lentivirus titer was determined using the Lenti-X p24 Rapid Titer Kit (Takara), and an MOI of 10 was used to transduce T cells. Polybrene at a final concentration of 8 μg/mL was added to the lentivirus suspension, and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at RT. After incubation, the mixture was added to the CD8+ T cell culture, and the plates were centrifuged at 700 g for 45 min at 32°C. One day after spinfection, the cells were centrifuged once at 1,200 rpm for 3 min, followed by a change of fresh medium containing 100 IU/mL of IL-2.

Co-culture for ELISA and ELISPOT cytokine detection assays

Cytotoxic cytokine release profiles of CAR T cells were evaluated in comparison with those of control CD8+ T cells using ELISA and ELISPOT assays. U87 and U251 GBM cells (2 × 10e5) in 2 mL culture medium were seeded into 6-well tissue culture plates. One day later, the cell culture supernatant was removed and replaced with 1 mL GBM cell culture medium and 1 mL T cell culture medium containing activated T cells (control CD8+ T cells or one of the three CAR T cells) in a 1:1 ratio. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37°C in a tissue culture incubator. ELISA or ELISPOT assays were then carried out following the manufacturers’ instructions.

Western blots

GBM cells and astrocytes were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with a protease inhibitor. Cell lysates were incubated on ice for 10 min, and protein samples were collected by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min. Total protein was quantified using a BCA assay. All samples were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane using a Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad). Transferred membranes were blocked with 3% skim milk in TBST buffer for 2 h at RT. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibody in fresh skim milk overnight at 4°C. A fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody was added to the washed membranes for detection. The proteins on the membranes were detected using Odyssey (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE). The following antibodies were used for tumor antigen detection: anti-beta actin (4970S), anti-IL13RA2 (85677S), and anti-EGF receptor (54359S) from CST (Danvers, MA); anti-EGFRvIII (XJ3728096) from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA); and anti-6X His tag (EPR20547) from Abcam (Waltham, MA).

Flow cytometry

CAR T cells were stained with DAPI, PE anti-human CD3 (OKT3), APC anti-human CD4 (RPA-T4), Alexa Fluor 700 anti-human CD8 (SK1), and anti-human IgG Fc (M1310G05), all of which purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA). For staining, the cells were washed three times with DPBS, resuspended in 500 μL PBS containing the target antibodies, and incubated at 4°C for 1 h. After the incubation, the cells were washed three times with DPBS and resuspended in 300 μL fresh DPBS containing DAPI. Cell acquisition and analysis were performed using an LSR II (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ). FlowJo software (Ashland, OR) was used for data analysis.

Human cytokine array assay

U87 GBM cells were seeded in 12-well plates and co-cultured with T cells or CAR T cells for 24 h. Culture supernatants (2 mL) were collected and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min, and 1 mL of clarified supernatant was used for cytokine analysis. Cytokine secretion was analyzed using a Human Cytokine Antibody Array (Abcam, ab133998) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Hanging-drop method for preparation of GBM-BBB spheroids

GBM cell line spheroids were generated using a hanging-drop culture approach. Briefly, GBM cells were dissociated into a single-cell suspension and adjusted to a density of 2.0–2.5 × 10e6 cells/mL in suspension spheroid medium consisting of Neurobasal medium supplemented with 5%–10% penicillin-streptomycin, 20 ng/mL EGF, 20 ng/mL FGF, and 0.1× GlutaMAX. To maintain humidity and prevent evaporation during spheroid formation, 8 mL PBS was added to the bottom reservoir of a culture dish. A total of 10–20 droplets (20 μL each) of the cell suspension were carefully dispensed onto the inner surface of the lid (resulting in approximately 4–5 × 10e4 cells per droplet). The lid was inverted and placed over the PBS-filled bottom dish, and droplets were incubated at 37°C for 24 h to allow self-aggregation and spheroid formation. After spheroids had formed, each spheroid was transferred to an individual well of a 96-well U-bottom plate containing 100 μL of suspension spheroid medium. Plates were placed on an orbital shaker at 600 rpm for 1 h to promote compact spheroid morphology and then maintained at 37°C until subsequent experiments.

Propidium iodide staining for detection of dead cells in spheroids

Spheroid killing was assessed by staining dead cells with propidium iodide (PI). After treatment or coculture with effector cells (e.g., CAR T cells) under the indicated conditions (including ET ratios and incubation times), PI was added directly to each well to a final concentration of 1–5 μg/mL (as optimized) and incubated at 37°C for 10–20 min, protected from light. PI fluorescence was imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (or confocal microscope) with appropriate excitation/emission settings (PI: Ex ∼535 nm/Em ∼617 nm). For quantification, images were analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji to measure PI-positive signal within each spheroid. Briefly, spheroid boundaries were defined in the bright-field channel, and PI-positive area or integrated fluorescence intensity was calculated and reported either as (i) PI + area normalized to total spheroid area or (ii) integrated PI intensity per spheroid, as specified. Background fluorescence was subtracted using an identical thresholding approach across groups. Data were collected from multiple spheroids per condition in at least 3 independent experiments.

BiTE-mediated T cell infiltration assay in GBM-BBB spheroids

To evaluate the effect of BiTE on T cell infiltration into GBM spheroids, GBM-BBB spheroids were generated using U87 and U251 GBM cell lines as described above. Fully formed spheroids were transferred to 96-well U-bottom plates and co-cultured with human CD8+ T cells, EGFR-CAR T cells, or EGFRvIII-CAR T cells in the presence or absence of BiTE-containing culture medium, as indicated. Effector cells were added directly to each well containing a single spheroid under identical conditions across experimental groups. After 24 h of co-culture at 37°C, spheroids were gently washed to remove non-adherent cells. Green fluorescent-labeled T cells were visualized using a fluorescence microscope to assess T cell penetration into the spheroid structure. Representative fluorescence images were acquired at identical exposure settings for all conditions, and T cell infiltration was evaluated based on the spatial distribution and intensity of green fluorescent signals within the spheroid mass.

Immunofluorescence staining of BBB junction markers

Human brain microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3) were co-cultured with EGFR-, EGFRvIII-, or combined CAR T cells for 4 h at 37°C. Cells were washed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. BBB junction proteins were stained with antibodies against Claudin-5 (Abcam, ab15106), Occludin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 71-1500), and ZO-1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, clone ZO1-1A12, 740002M) (1:100 dilution), followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000 dilution). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

BiTE construct and OV-BiTE adenovirus production

BiTE is a fusion protein comprising two scFv fragments of different antibodies on a single 55 kDa peptide; one scFv (mAb OKT3) binds to T cells via CD3ε, while the other targets the GBM-specific TAA IL13Rα2.12,56 These two scFv fragments were connected using a flexible 23-amino-acid glycine/serine (G/S) linker in the following orientation: mAb OKT3 (VH)-mAb OKT3 (VL)-mAb IL13Rα2 (VL)-mAb IL13Rα2 (VH). The coding sequence was then cloned into an adenoviral vector as described below. We have adopted the AdenoQuick2.0 cloning system (O.D.260 Inc, Boise, ID) to construct recombinant adenoviral vectors. As shown in Figure S7, we used a set of four shuttle plasmids that encode different regions of the virus genome: pAd1127 contains the serotype 5 adenovirus (Ad5) left ITR, packaging signal, the E1a-Δ24 region, and the pIX gene; pAd1128 encompasses the E2 region and most of the late genes; pAd1129 includes the E3 region with or without the BiTE coding sequence, and the RGD fiber gene; pAd1130 contains the E4 region and the right ITR. The shuttle plasmids were then digested with SfiI, ligated, and the ligation product was packaged into phage ƛ. The packaged phage ƛ was used to infect competent cells. To prepare cosmid DNA, 15 μg cosmid DNA was digested with PacI. Completion of digestion and DNA quality were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The DNA was precipitated with EtOH, centrifuged, and the DNA pellet was resuspended in 60 μL sterile TE buffer (pH 7.5). The adenoviral cosmid was then transfected into AdenoX 293FT packaging cells following the manufacturer’s instructions. For identification and titration of adenovirus, the Adeno-X GoStix and Adeno-X Rapid Titer Kit (Takara) were used. To test oncolysis of GBM cells by OV in vitro, an MOI of 100 was used.

In vivo mouse xenograft model

Human GBM U87-MG-luciferase cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic. Cells were harvested at 80%–90% confluency, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of serum-free DMEM and Matrigel matrix (Corning) at a concentration of 5 × 10e6 cells per 200 μL. Six-week-old male NSG mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ, Strain #5557, The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor ME) were anesthetized with isoflurane, and 200 μL of the cell suspension was injected subcutaneously into the left flank. Tumor growth was monitored twice a week using a digital caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width2)/2. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3 in volume, OV-BiTE and CAR T cell treatment commenced, as detailed below.

OV-BiTE and CAR T cell injection in mice

As illustrated in Figure 5A, on day 0, MOI 20 OV-BiTE (1 × 10e8 virus particles) in 100 μL PBS was intratumorally injected. Mice bearing subcutaneous GBM tumors were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the tumor area was sterilized with 70% ethanol. Using a 30-gauge needle, 100 μL of the OV-BiTE suspension was injected intratumorally at three separate sites to ensure even distribution within the tumor mass. Three days after OV-BiTE injection, 5 × 10e6 prepared CD8+ T cells or CAR T cells (E:T ratio = 1:1) were administered via tail vein injection into recipient mice under light isoflurane anesthesia. Mice were observed for 1 h to ensure recovery from anesthesia. Post-injection therapeutic responses were evaluated at predetermined time points, and tumor volume was measured twice a week. Efforts were made to minimize animal suffering, including the use of appropriate anesthetics and humane endpoints. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Rhode Island Hospital (protocol no.: #5041–23). The facility operates in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

IHC assays

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections (4 μm) were mounted on positively charged slides and air-dried overnight. Slides were baked at 50°C–60°C for 1 h, deparaffinized through graded xylene and ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval using EnVision FLEX Target Retrieval Solution, High pH (Agilent, K800021-2) in a pressure cooker (BioGenex, NW001-PC) at full microwave power for 10 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using Peroxidase-Blocking Reagent (Agilent, K800021-2) for 10 min at RT. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 30–60 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies diluted in DAKO Antibody Diluent (Agilent, S0809) overnight at 4°C. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-human CD3 (Abcam, ab17143; 1:200) and anti-IL13Rα2 (Abcam, ab250044; 1:100). After washing, sections were incubated with EnVision HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Agilent, K4061) for 45 min at RT. Visualization was achieved with DAB+ substrate-chromogen (Agilent, K800021-2) for up to 10 min. Slides were counterstained with Gill’s hematoxylin, blued with ammonia water, dehydrated, and mounted with coverslips.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired t tests. One-way ANOVA was employed for comparisons among multiple groups. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
