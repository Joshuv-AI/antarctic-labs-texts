Differentiating Xp11.2/TFE3 translocation renal cell carcinoma from clear cell carcinoma: performance of a radiomics model
Authors: Yi Liu, Kexin Wang, Yaofeng Zhang, Jiangtao Liu, Zuqiang Xi, Xiangpeng Wang, Yanfei Yu, Xiaoying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to assess the computed tomography (CT) features of transcription factor E3 gene fusion renal cell carcinoma (TFE3-RCC) and develop a radiomics-based model for preoperative discrimination between TFE3-RCCs and clear cell renal cell carcinomas (ccRCCs).MethodsA total of 34 pathologically confirmed TFE3-RCC and 98 ccRCC cases with preoperative renal CT were enrolled. Subjective image analysis was performed to determine the CT parameters that can distinguish TFE3-RCCs from ccRCCs. A random forest classifier was evaluated through 5-fold cross-validation to establish the radiomics model. Diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsPatients with TFE3-RCC were significantly younger (36.2 ± 14.4 vs. 56.8 ± 13.5 years, P < 0.001). Ten of the 34 TFE3-RCCs had calcification, whereas only four in the ccRCC group did (29.4% vs. 4.1%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the TOC enhancement ratio in the corticomedullary phase were significantly lower in the TFE3-RCC group compared with the ccRCC group (0.61 ± 0.11 vs. 0.90 ± 0.13, p < 0.001). The random forest classifier achieved stable performance with robust cross-validation metrics (AUC = 0.82 ± 0.17, accuracy = 85% ± 13%).ConclusionThe CT radiomics model exhibits favorable diagnostic performance for noninvasively differentiating TFE3-RCC from ccRCC, supporting its potential utility in preoperative personalized management of renal masses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16463-8.Keywords: Xp11.2 translocation/transcription factor E3, Renal cell carcinoma, Computed tomography, Radiomics

Introduction

Xp11.2 translocation/transcription factor E3 (TFE3) gene fusion renal cell carcinoma (TFE3-RCC) is recognized as a distinct pathological tumor. In 2004, it was formally designated as an independent subtype of renal malignancy by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. According to updated 2016 WHO classification of RCC, it is categorized within the MiT family of translocated renal cancers. This subtype shows a striking predilection for pediatric and young adult populations. It accounts for 46.7% of RCC in children, 15% in patients younger than 45 years, and only 1.0%–1.6% in adult RCC cohorts [2, 3]. Distinct from common RCCs, TFE3-RCC follows an aggressive clinical course with unfavorable outcomes, frequently presenting with locally advanced invasion or distant metastatic spread at initial diagnosis [4]. Accordingly, precise discrimination of TFE3-RCC from other RCC subtypes is clinically imperative to enable timely therapeutic intervention.

The diagnosis of TFE3-RCC is conventionally established by post-operative histology and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Owing to characteristic overexpression of the TFE3 fusion protein, IHC staining with TFE3-specific antibodies serves as a frontline diagnostic adjunct. Nonetheless, there were false-positive and equivocal findings of IHC results in some cases. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) has been validated as a more robust and definitive molecular assay for confirming TFE3 gene rearrangement in this tumor entity.

Over the past three years, several reports have been published about computed tomography (CT) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of TFE3-RCC [5, 6]. Based on those investigations, the primary imaging manifestations of this rare lesion on CT/MRI had been described. Nevertheless, these studies had relatively small sample sizes (ranging from 5 to 20 patients). Certain imaging features remain uncertain or inconsistent. This may lead to the misdiagnosis as other types RCC subtypes during imaging assessment, especially clear cell RCC (ccRCC), the most common histologic subtype in adult populations. Many similarities in CT imaging features between TFE3-RCC and ccRCC, resulting in it was a significant challenge to distinguish TFE3-RCC solely based on imaging features.

Radiomics analysis provides quantitative imaging metrics that are not achievable via radiologists’ visual assessment. A comprehensive review highlighted that radiomics models have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in RCC diagnosis, sometimes surpassing human clinicians in accuracy [7]. It has been widely utilized for distinguishing among different types of renal tumors, including the distinction between benign and malignant lesions as well as the classification of RCC subtypes on cross-sectional imaging [8, 9]. Recent researches have demonstrated the accurate differentiation of papillary RCC, chromophobe cell carcinoma, and angiomyolipoma from other RCC subtypes using radiomics-based models. However, there was no study about how to predict TFE3-RCC using radiomics-based model before therapy. In conjunction with subjective image features, the radiomics-based model may facilitate the quantitative differentiation of TFE3-RCC from ccRCC.

In this study, we conducted subjective assessment of CT image findings and clinical information in 34 TFE3-RCCs and 98 ccRCCs. Moreover, we established a combined radiomics-based model for the differentiation of TFE3-RCC from ccRCC. ccRCC was selected as the reference tumor due to its more heterogeneous radiologic appearance compared with other RCC subtypes (i.e., papillary RCC and chromophobe RCC)—a phenotypic feature that may overlap with TFE3-RCC.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

This retrospective study was approved by the local ethics committee (Ethical Number: 2024 − 881) and conforms to the Declaration of Helsinki with informed consent waived. A total of 41 consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed TFE3-RCC and 2118 patients with ccRCC who underwent enhanced CT examination between February 2015 and May 2024 were enrolled. The inclusion criteria for TFE3-RCC patients were as follows: (1) diagnosis of TFE3-RCC with positive FISH results through pathological examination; and (2) availability of complete CT images. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of any treatment, including chemotherapy or radiotherapy, before CT imaging; (2) presence of severe artifacts that could make segmentation of the tumor difficult on CT images; (3) incomplete clinical and imaging data; and (4) negative FISH results. For the ccRCC patients, 183 patients were excluded because of prior treatment before CT imaging (n = 122), severe artifacts (n = 41), and incomplete clinical and imaging data (n = 20). Finally, a total of 34 patients with TFE3-RCC (male: female = 18:16, mean age = 36.2 ± 14.4 years) and 1935 patients with ccRCC (male: female = 1306:629, mean age = 58.3 ± 14.8 years) were included.

For the control group, we selected 98 patients (male: female = 67:31, mean age = 56.8 ± 13.5 years) using propensity score matching (PSM) from among 1935 patients. The matching was performed at a ratio of 1:3. Fig. 1 shows the process of patient recruitment.

Image acquisition

CT examination protocols are shown in Table S1 in the supplementary material. The contrast agent (iopromide 370 mg/ml or iohexol 320 mg/ml) was injected at a dose of 2 ml/kg at a flow rate of 2.5 ml/s. CT scanning included the plain scan, corticomedullary (30 ~ 35 s after contrast injection), nephrographic (60 ~ 70 s after contrast injection) phase, and delayed phase (190 ~ 200 s after contrast administration). Scanning parameters were set as follows: tube voltage ranging from 80 to 120 kV, reconstructed slice thickness of 1–1.5 mm, and a reconstruction interval of 1 mm.

Image analysis

The evaluation of all patients’ image information was performed independently by two abdominal radiologists (with 5 and 15 years of experience). A third radiologist with 18 years of experience in genitourinary imaging was asked to make the final decision in case of disagreement. Lesion assessment included average diameter of the mass; tumor location; tumor heterogeneity; shape of the tumor; presence of calcification, fat, and necrosis; tumor margin; high attenuation on pre-contrast scan; enhancement pattern; presence of intra-tumoral or peritumoral vessels; renal vein invasion; lymph node metastasis; and distant metastasis.

ROI annotation

An AI-based segmentation model was employed to delineate the kidney mass region in the nephrographic phase [10]. The AI-generated segmentations were subsequently checked and validated by two readers (with 10 and 30 years of experience in urogenital radiology) working in consensus, who made manual adjustments to the boundaries when necessary. The finalized segmentation masks were designated as the ROIs for subsequent analysis (Fig. 2a-d).

Radiomics model development

The ROIs on CT images were preprocessed through resampling to ensure standardized spatial resolution and intensity normalization to achieve uniformity. The PyRadiomics package in Python (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/features.html) was used to extract the radiomic features, i.e., shape-based features, first-order statistical features, and texture features derived from gray-level co-occurrence matrices (GLCM) and gray-level run-length matrices (GLRLM). Feature values were normalized using the Z-score method.

Synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) was applied to generate synthetic samples for the minority class, reducing potential bias toward the majority class. Z-score normalization was implemented to standardize all features. Multicollinearity reduction was achieved by calculating pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) across all features, with those exhibiting PCC > 0.99 being removed. Feature selection optimization was achieved through recursive feature elimination with cross-validation, iteratively removing features with the lowest importance rankings from the random forest classifier until achieving the optimal subset. Hyperparameter optimization (number of trees, maximum depth) was implemented using bootstrap resampling (n = 1000 iterations) with out-of-bag error (OOB) estimation. Finally, a random forest classifier was used to establish the final model. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Radiomics model development workflow. Schematic diagram illustrates the multi-stage pipeline for radiomics model development. (1) ROI Annotation: Renal masses on CT images were initially segmented by a pre-trained deep learning model and subsequently verified/revised by two radiologists. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.1.3, https://www.r-project.org/). Continuous variables were tested for normality and expressed as the mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or as medians (interquartile range) if non-normally distributed. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as patient numbers and compared using the χ2 test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

In this study, a total of 132 patients (34 TFE3-RCCs and 98 ccRCCs) were included. Among the participants, there were 85 male patients and 47 female patients, with median ages of 56.0 years (interquartile range, 40.0–65.3 years). The patients with TFE3-RCC were significantly younger than those with clear cell RCC (36.2 ± 14.4 vs. 56.8 ± 13.5 years, P < 0.001). Ten of the 34 TFE3-RCCs had calcification, whereas only four in the ccRCC group did (29.4% vs. 4.1%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the TOC enhancement ratio in the corticomedullary phase were significantly lower in the TFE3-RCC group compared with the ccRCC group (0.61 ± 0.11 vs. 0.90 ± 0.13, p < 0.001). Utilizing 500 decision trees with 4 variables randomly selected at each node split, the random forest classifier achieved stable error rates of 18.9% (OOB), 19.4% (TFE3-RCC), and 18.4% (ccRCC), with convergence observed beyond 300 trees (Fig. 4a).

Variable importance analysis revealed firstorder_90Percentile as the dominant predictor, exhibiting maximal discriminative power across metrics: highest Gini decrease (12.53, Fig. 4b), greatest accuracy reduction (0.068, Fig. 4c), earliest node splitting (mean depth = 2.06, Fig. 4d), and frequent root node utilization (86 trees, Fig. 3e). Complementary contributions emerged from glcm_JointEnergy (Gini = 9.82; accuracy = 0.053; depth = 2.43) and firstorder_Uniformity (Gini = 6.94; accuracy = 0.049; depth = 2.47), reflecting synergistic interplay between intensity histogram primacy and textural refinement.

Hierarchical analysis (Figs. 4d–e) delineated a decision architecture where first-order features (depth < 2.5) initiated primary splits, while GLCM/GLRLM textures (depth > 2.5–3.4) optimized subclass discrimination.

Multimetric correlation matrices (Figs. 4g-h) validated analytical coherence, showing strong negative Gini-depth association (r=-0.816, P < 0.001), and near-perfect correlation in tree-node scaling (r = 0.954, P < 0.001). This integrated workflow confirms the diagnostic superiority of intensity-driven biomarkers while establishing textural heterogeneity as critical for subtype differentiation.

Performance of the radiomics model

The radiomics model demonstrated robust predictive performance across 5-fold cross-validation. As summarized in Table 3, the model achieved mean AUC values of 0.79 ± 0.14 (training) and 0.82 ± 0.17 (testing), with corresponding accuracy rates of 0.78 ± 0.15 and 0.85 ± 0.13 respectively. While sensitivity showed moderate consistency (0.75 ± 0.11 training vs. 0.83 ± 0.12 testing), specificity remained stable across folds (0.81 ± 0.18 training vs. 0.86 ± 0.14 testing). Notably, the fifth fold exhibited perfect test performance (AUC = 1.0), suggesting potential overfitting that warrants further investigation. The high degree of overlap in ROC curves across folds (Fig. 5) visually confirms the model’s consistent discriminative ability, with mean test AUCs ranging from 0.75 to 1.00.

Discussion

TFE3-RCC was first reported in a pediatric patient by Tomlinson et al. [11], and subsequent studies have verified its occurrence in adolescents and adults [12, 13]. The clinical prognosis of TFE3-RCC is highly heterogeneous, with indolent behavior in pediatric patients but aggressive progression in adult patients [14]. Surgical resection remains the first-line treatment for non-metastatic TFE3-RCC, whereas anti-angiogenic targeted therapy and immunotherapy are under clinical investigation for metastatic disease [15]. Therefore, accurate preoperative differentiation between TFE3-RCC and ccRCC is critical for treatment planning and precision medicine. This present study analyzed CT features of TFE3-RCC and developed a radiomics model for distinguishing TFE3-RCC from ccRCC.

Our study identified distinct clinical and CT characteristics of TFE3-RCC compared with ccRCC: patients with TFE3-RCC were significantly younger (36.2 vs. 56.8 years, P < 0.001), had a markedly higher rate of intratumoral calcification (29.4% vs. 4.1%, P < 0.001), and exhibited a significantly lower TOC enhancement ratio in the corticomedullary phase (0.61 ± 0.11 vs. 0.90 ± 0.13, P < 0.001). The radiomics model integrating first-order intensity and textural features showed a biologically interpretable hierarchical structure, in which intensity histogram metrics (especially the 90th percentile) dominated classification, while GLCM/GLRLM textural features optimized subtype discrimination. The random forest classifier achieved stable performance with an out-of-bag error rate of 18.9% and robust cross-validation results (mean test AUC = 0.82 ± 0.17, accuracy = 85% ± 13%). Despite the limited sample size, these findings suggested that radiomics signatures could characterize tumor microenvironmental heterogeneity and enabled CT-based differentiation of TFE3-RCC in clinical practice.

TFE3-RCC is a rare, distinct subtype of RCC characterized by Xp11.2 translocation involving the TFE3 gene, with a strong predilection for children and young adults [16]. It accounts for 20–40% of pediatric renal tumors but only 1–1.6% of adult renal tumors [6]. In contrast, ccRCC is the most common histologic subtype in adults, predominantly affecting males over 40 years old. Our study confirmed that TFE3-RCC patients were significantly younger than those with ccRCC (P < 0.001). A meta-analysis showed a higher female incidence of TFE3-RCC [17], which may be related to sex steroids. Previous studies reported that estrogen increased the risk of topoisomerase IIβ-mediated TFE3 gene breaks, thereby promoting TFE3-RCC [18, 19]. Although gender difference was not statistically significant between the two groups in our study, a female predominance was observed in the TFE3-RCC cohort.

Intratumoral calcification was detected in 29.4% of TFE3-RCC cases in this study, corresponding to histopathologically confirmed psammomatous calcification, a classic feature of TFE3-RCC [20]. Previous radiologic studies have described punctate or patchy calcifications in TFE3-RCC on contrast-enhanced CT [21, 22]. In contrast, calcification is rare in ccRCC, making intratumoral calcification a valuable imaging biomarker for differentiating these two subtypes.

The TFE3-RCC group showed a lower TOC enhancement ratio (0.61) and less frequent wash-out (38.2%) than the ccRCC group, consistent with the findings of Ma et al. [23]. On unenhanced CT, most TFE3-RCC lesions were hyperdense relative to renal parenchyma, which may be attributed to hemorrhage, proteinaceous fluid, high cellularity, or a combination of these factors [24]. Enhancement pattern and degree reflected tumor microvascular density. The ccRCC group showed comparable enhancement to the renal cortex in the corticomedullary phase (TOC ratio = 0.90) and frequent wash-out in the excretory phase (88.8%), representing the classic “fast-in and fast-out” pattern. In contrast, TFE3-RCC showed heterogeneous enhancement after contrast administration, likely due to uniform intratumoral microvasculature [25]. It also exhibied moderately prolonged enhancement on dynamic CT, consistent with its unique vascularity and angiogenic profile [26].

Diagnosis of TFE3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma is challenging due to its rarity, overlapping features with ccRCC, and considerable interobserver and geographic variability. A stepwise diagnostic algorithm is therefore necessary in clinical practice. Key indicators include young patient age, intratumoral calcification, and relatively low enhancement in the corticomedullary phase on computed tomography. Immunohistochemical markers such as TFE3, cathepsin K, HMB45, and Melan-A facilitate recognition, while FISH or NGS provides definitive molecular confirmation [27]. Standardized diagnostic criteria and consistent workflows are crucial to reduce variability and improve preoperative diagnostic accuracy for this rare tumor.

The radiomics model incorporating first-order intensity parameters and textural features demonstrated a biologically interpretable hierarchy. Notably, the selected radiomic features corroborated key findings from visual image interpretation. The model achieved favorable performance in distinguishing TFE3-RCC from ccRCC, relying mainly on the first-order 90th percentile and GLCM-derived textural features, which capture both intensity distribution and textural complexity. The lower 90th percentile value in the corticomedullary phase among TFE3-RCC patients reflected weaker enhancement relative to ccRCC. GLCM features quantified spatial pixel intensity relationships and reflected tumor heterogeneity [28]. For example, GLCM analysis has been used to differentiate thymoma from thymic carcinoma on CT and to develop a CT urography-based radiomics model for preoperative grading of ccRCC [29]. These results highlight the critical role of texture features in radiomics for improved evaluation of tumor subtype and differentiation.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop a radiomics model with high sensitivity and specificity for differentiating TFE3-RCC from ccRCC. The model showed robust performance in 5-fold cross-validation (mean training AUC = 0.79 ± 0.14, mean test AUC = 0.82 ± 0.17). Shi et al. [30] established a risk-scoring system for predicting adult Xp11.2 translocation RCC with a sensitivity of 81.0%. Our radiomics model achieved better diagnostic performance and enabled non-invasive preoperative prediction using routine imaging data.

Our findings further validate the clinical value of radiomics for noninvasive assessment of renal tumors. As summarized in a recent comprehensive review, radiomics and machine learning models yield excellent diagnostic accuracy (80–90%) in renal cell carcinoma, often comparable to that of subspecialty radiologists [7]. However, broader clinical implementation remains limited by insufficient external validation and a lack of multicenter prospective data. Our study extends current knowledge by focusing on rare renal tumor subtypes, supporting the need for standardized models to translate radiomics into routine clinical practice.

Furthermore, radiologists and clinicians should be aware of important postoperative imaging pitfalls that may simulate local tumor recurrence or residual disease. For example, retained oxidized cellulose (Surgicel®), a frequently used hemostatic material in partial nephrectomy, may form foreign-body granulomas that mimic recurrent renal cell carcinoma on CT and MRI [31, 32]. Given this clinical demand and diagnostic difficulty, future studies may attempt to establish the radiomic-based model for differentiating foreign-body granulomas from local tumor recurrence or residual disease. Careful correlation with surgical history and multimodality imaging is critical to avoid misdiagnosis, unnecessary biopsy, or unwarranted reoperation, thereby improving the accuracy of postoperative renal tumor follow-up.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-center design and small sample size of TFE3-RCC limit the generalizability of the result, which is unavoidable for studies of rare tumors. Further studies with multi-institutional external validation cohorts are warranted to improve the generalizability and clinical applicability of our model. Second, only one phase of contrast-enhanced CT was used for radiomic feature extraction. The incorporating multiphasic CT data may further improve diagnostic performance in the future study. Third, other RCC subtypes were not included as comparators, which restricts the clinical applicability of the model. Further studies should include all major RCC subtypes to enhance diagnostic specificity.

In conclusion, CT-based radiomics combined with key clinical and imaging features allows reliable preoperative differentiation between TFE3-RCC and ccRCC. These findings highlight the potential of radiomics for non-invasive preoperative classification of TFE3-RCC. In the future, the independent external validation cohorts will be recruited to validate the model’s clinical generalizability. After further validation in large-scale, multi-institutional prospective cohorts, this approach may improve the clinical utility of our model and facilitate personalized management of renal cell carcinoma.
