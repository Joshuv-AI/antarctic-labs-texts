Transformation of Thoracic SMARCA4‐Deficient Undifferentiated Tumour to Squamous Cell Carcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Heng You, Yuanyue Yi, Ran Wang, Jiayu Li, Zhi Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThoracic SMARCA4‐deficient undifferentiated tumour (SMARCA4‐dUT) is a rare and highly aggressive malignant tumour with poor response to conventional chemotherapy and a poor prognosis, for which there is currently no standard treatment. This article details a case of SMARCA4‐dUT in which next‐generation sequencing (NGS) revealed a frameshift mutation in SMARCA4 (N259Tfs44), along with high PD‐L1 expression (tumour proportion score, TPS 90%) and a high tumour mutational burden (TMB, 24.58 mutations/Mb). The patient achieved a progression‐free survival (PFS) of over 32 months following treatment with pembrolizumab combined with conventional chemotherapy. However, the tumour eventually recurred and transformed into SMARCA4‐deficient squamous cell carcinoma (SMARCA4‐dSCC). To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of histological transformation in SMARCA4‐dUT. Immunotherapy may represent a potential treatment option for SMARCA4‐dUT, particularly in patients with high TMB and PD‐L1 expression.

Introduction

Thoracic SMARCA4‐deficient undifferentiated tumour (SMARCA4‐dUT) is a clinically rare but highly aggressive adult malignant tumour, primarily found in the mediastinum, lungs and pleural regions [1]. In 2021, it was formally defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a distinct disease entity, separate from SMARCA4‐deficient non‐small cell lung cancer (SMARCA4‐dNSCLC). Its histopathological hallmark is the loss of SMARCA4 expression on immunohistochemistry [2].

Nowadays, the treatment for SMARCA4‐dUT continues to be uncertain. This disease, primarily treated with chemotherapy, has been classified as a subtype within sarcomatoid carcinomas in previous studies, but the overall prognosis is not good. Even for patients who undergo surgical resection, the recurrence rate remains high, often necessitating adjuvant systemic chemotherapy [3]. Recent studies show that some SMARCA4‐dUT patients exhibit high tumour mutational burden (TMB) and PD‐L1 expression, potentially profiting from immunotherapy [4, 5, 6, 7]. Although the general treatment prospect of this disease remains unfavourable, the use of immunotherapy in patients with high TMB and PD‐L1 levels may contribute to longer survival [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. This article reports for the first time a patient with SMARCA4‐dUT that transformed into SMARCA4‐deficient squamous cell carcinoma (SMARCA4‐dSCC).

Case Report

A 67‐year‐old male patient with a 40‐year smoking history was found to have a 4.2 cm × 3.8 cm mass in the left upper lobe of the lung (without symptoms such as cough, hemoptysis or chest pain) during a visit to a local hospital and was transferred to our hospital 20 days later (Figure 1). Repeat chest computed tomography (CT) revealed rapid enlargement of the mass to 6.3 cm × 4.1 cm, accompanied by significant mediastinal lymph node enlargement (Figure 2A).

The Ki‐67 proliferation index was as high as 85%–90% (Figure 3K) and PD‐L1 expression was significant (tumour proportion score, TPS = 90%) (Figure 3L). Reticulin staining revealed a positive reaction in the surrounding stroma of the tumour nests. According to the TNM classification, the patient was clinically staged as T3N2M0. Drawing on previous research reports and considering the patient's favourable baseline conditions, the patient was treated with liposomal paclitaxel in combination with nedaplatin and pembrolizumab. Treatment‐related severe myelosuppression necessitated chemotherapy dose reduction in Cycles 2–3 and discontinuation during Cycles 4–5. After completing four cycles of combined therapy followed by 11 cycles of pembrolizumab maintenance, sustained partial response (PR) was achieved with 83% tumour volume reduction (Figure 2B), maintaining progression‐free survival (PFS) beyond 32 months.

Tumour recurrence occurred 18 months post‐treatment cessation. Chest CT imaging demonstrated a 255% volumetric tumour increase (Figure 2C). A re‐biopsy of the tumour tissue was performed: squamous‐like nests within thrombi, with small round nuclei accompanied by spindle cell transformation, persistent eosinophilic cytoplasm, reduced nuclear‐cytoplasmic ratio, nuclear compression, active mitosis and absence of necrosis (Figure 4A). IHC analysis revealed: CK/P40 (+) (Figure 4B,C), focal weak positivity for CK7/CK5/6 (Figure 4D,E), SMARCA4 (Figure 4F), TTF‐1/NUT (−), a Ki‐67 proliferation index of 20% and PD‐L1 expression < 1%. Despite positive P40 staining, the diagnosis of SMARCA4‐dUT was retained, given the insufficient expression of CK5/6. NGS demonstrated that although the SMARCA4 frameshift mutation had reverted to the wild type, the missense mutations in CDKN2A (p.H83Y) and TP53 (p.Y163C) remained consistent with the initial findings.

Eleven months following tumour progression, the patient was readmitted to our hospital for a biopsy. In the lesion, the nuclei exhibited significant compression and deformation, accompanied by prominent mitotic figures and distinct areas of necrosis (Figure 4G). IHC results demonstrated that tumour cells were positive for CK5/6, P40 and P63 (Figure 4H–J), whereas SMARCA4 (Figure 4K), CK7 and TTF‐1 were negative. The Ki‐67 proliferation index revealed that approximately 40% of the cells were Ki‐67 positive (Figure 4 L). On the basis of the positive staining for CK5/6, P40 and P63, the patient's lung tumour was diagnosed as SMARCA4‐dSCC. Routine NGS of nine genes in our hospital detected no mutations associated with lung cancer. Regrettably, due to personal reasons, the patient chose not to pursue further treatment.

Discussion

Prior research has traditionally classified SMARCA4‐dUT and SMARCA4‐dNSCLC as distinct entities, with SMARCA4‐dUT demonstrating greater clinical aggression [12, 13]. Here, we document a clinical case demonstrating phenotypic evolution from SMARCA4‐dUT to squamous cell carcinoma (SMARCA4‐dSCC). These observations challenge the categorical separation of these SMARCA4‐deficient thoracic malignancies, proposing instead a potential disease continuum where histological variants may represent progressive manifestations of shared molecular pathogenesis.

Serial SMARCA4 (BRG1) immunostaining remained negative throughout clinical monitoring. Initial 2020 tumour profiling showed absent CK5/6 expression, a squamous lineage marker. By 2024, full squamous differentiation manifested through CK5/6/P40/P63 co‐expression, establishing phenotypic conversion to SMARCA4‐dSCC.

This SWI/SNF complex serves as a tumour suppressor; however, when SMARCA4 is absent, the PRC is not adequately inhibited, facilitating tumour progression. In our patient analysis showed SMARCA4 (N259Tfs*44) frameshift mutation; and IHC assays confirmed continued SMARCA4 deficiency.

This case documents the first confirmed SMARCA4‐dUT progression to SMARCA4‐dSCC in a patient achieving durable remission with ICIs. While eventual recurrence occurred, the mechanisms underlying this phenotypic shift and initial treatment sensitivity remain uncharacterised. Critically, this observation challenges the conventional view of SMARCA4‐dUT and SMARCA4‐dNSCLC as entirely distinct entities, suggesting potential pathogenic continuity. Additionally, it provides clinical evidence supporting ICIs as a viable therapeutic strategy for SMARCA4‐deficient thoracic malignancies.
