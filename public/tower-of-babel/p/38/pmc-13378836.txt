Effect of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy on resilience of junior helper school of public health, Thailand
Authors: Uraiwan Madardam, Onggan Ranteh, Natjanan Wainam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:University students, especially those involved in peer support roles such as Junior Helpers within public health programs often face a variety of academic, emotional, and interpersonal challenges. This study investigates the effectiveness of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) in enhancing resilience among Junior Helpers enrolled in the School of Public Health in Thailand.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This study adopted a quasiexperimental design using a pretest–posttest control group approach. A total of 40 Junior Helpers enrolled in the School of Public Health in Thailand, were selected through purposive sampling. The participants were between 18 and 20 years of age and had scores of 27–34 on the psychological endurance subscale of the Resilience Quotient (RQ). These individuals were then randomly assigned to two groups using simple random sampling each group consisted of 20 participants. The experimental group participated in MBCT program consisting of eight sessions. Following the intervention, resilience scores in both groups were reassessed using the RQ scale. Data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the Mann–Whitney U test.RESULTS:Most participants in both groups were female. Most participants were 19 years old. Postintervention analysis revealed a statistically significant improvement in resilience scores among participants in the experimental group across all three core dimensions of psychological resilience: Psychological endurance (0.035), hope and psychological support (0.035), and overcoming obstacles (P < 0.001) at the 0.05 level of statistical significance.CONCLUSION:The findings suggest the potential of MBCT as an effective intervention for enhancing resilience and emotional well-being among student peer supporters.

Introduction

University students frequently encounter a range of stressors during their transitional stage of personal and academic development. Common challenges include academic pressure, time management difficulties, and social adjustment issues. These stressors tend to be more pronounced among students in health science programs, particularly those studying in public health. A number of studies have reported a high prevalence of depression among this population. For example, prevalence rates as high as 85.1%[1] and 45%[2] have been reported among health science students. In Thailand, research has shown depression prevalence rates of 31.2%[3] and 31%[4] among public health students. In addition, depressive symptoms have been observed in 50% of 5-year students,[5] and 34.5% of female students experienced depression.

These findings indicate that adolescents and young adults, especially those in the early years of university, face an elevated risk of developing depressive symptoms. Despite the increasing prevalence of mental health concerns among university students, studies report that this population has limited access to appropriate mental health services. This underscores the pressing need for universities to implement comprehensive and accessible mental health support systems for students.[3456]

In the context of public health education, students frequently take on peer-support roles commonly referred to as Junior Helpers who contribute to promoting mental health and well-being, as well as helping their peers in navigating academic and personal challenges. These roles require not only technical knowledge, but also psychological resilience, emotional regulation, and empathy to effectively manage stress and interpersonal demands.[7]

Resilience is recognized as a key psychological skill for managing and coping with mental health challenges, particularly among adolescents and young adults. It is commonly defined as an individual’s ability to adapt effectively to adversity and recover from difficult or stressful experiences. It also encompasses psychological endurance and the ability to rebound from life’s challenges and stressful situations. Research has shown that resilience can mitigate the symptoms of stress, anxiety, depression and other mental health issues.[8] Moreover, previous studies have also shown a direct relationship between psychological resilience and environmental stress.[9] Therefore, strengthening resilience in public health students is crucial to preparing students to effectively manage personal and academic challenges, as well as to maintain overall mental well-being throughout their education.

Psychological flexibility and resilience can be cultivated through a variety of approaches. Mindfulness practice, in particular, has been widely recognized as an effective technique in this regard. It is defined as nonjudgmental awareness of the present moment that involves acceptance of one’s thoughts, feelings, and experiences as they are. Research has demonstrated that mindfulness enhances empathy and compassion, supports better decision-making, strengthens immune function, and promotes neural plasticity.[1011] Consequently, it has been widely adopted as an approach to promoting happiness and mental well-being.[12]

Currently, mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) have gained increasing popularity in mental health care.[1314] These interventions encompass a range of evidence-based therapies that incorporate mindfulness practices into therapeutic frameworks. Notable examples include Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Dialectical Behavioral Therapy (DBT), Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), and Mindfulness-Based Relationship Enhancement (MBRE).[15161718]

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) encourages individuals to become aware of their thoughts without judgment and to acknowledge positive or at least neutral thoughts as valid, rather than automatically accepting negative thoughts as true. Fundamentally, Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) trains individuals in mindfulness, which involves paying attention to the present moment with openness and curiosity. This practice is characterized by a nonjudgmental awareness of one’s thoughts, emotions, and bodily sensations. Cultivating mindfulness helps individuals develop a different relationship with their thoughts and feelings, reducing tendencies toward rumination and enhancing emotional regulation. The mindfulness component of MBCT draws upon techniques from Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), a well-established approach developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn.[1920] Shahraki et al.[21] reported that mindfulness-based therapy effectively enhances psychological resilience in women. Similarly, MacKenzie and Kocovski[19] demonstrated that mindfulness-based cognitive therapy significantly reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Therefore, this study aims to examine the effectiveness of a Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) program on resilience among Junior Helpers in the School of Public Health at a university in the upper southern region of Thailand. Furthermore, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of MBCT in enhancing resilience among peer supporters, who simultaneously face their own challenges while assisting others. In practice, the findings suggest that MBCT can be a low-cost, scalable program for universities to strengthen peer support systems, integrate resilience training into public health education, and inform mental health promotion policies in higher education.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The study used a quasiexperimental, pretest–posttest experimental control group design.

Study participants and sampling

The study population consisted of 456 students enrolled in the School of Public Health at a university in the upper southern region of Thailand.

The sample comprised Junior Helpers who were in their first or second year of study during the second semester of the 2024 academic year. Sample size determination was based on guidelines for quasiexperimental research, which recommend 20–30 participants, with no fewer than 10 individuals per comparison group.[2223] A total of 40 participants were selected through purposive sampling, and subsequently assigned to either the experimental group (n = 20) or the control group (n = 20) using simple random sampling.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Junior Helpers from the School of Public Health aged between 18 and 20 years; (2) Currently enrolled as a first or second-year student; and (3) Scored 27–34 on the psychological endurance subscale of the Resilience Quotient (RQ) Assessment. (4) Willingness to participate in the research.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Junior Helpers with mental health conditions or a psychiatric diagnosis; and (2) Those who withdrew or discontinued participation during the study [See Figure 1].

The Resilience Quotient (RQ) Assessment, developed by the Department of Mental Health, Thailand,[24] is a 20-item self-report instrument rated on a 4-point Likert scale. It consists of three dimensions: (1) Tolerance to Pressure (items 1–10), (2) Hope and Encouragement (items 11–15), and (3) Overcoming Obstacles (items 16–20). The questionnaire assesses thoughts, feelings, and behaviors over the past three months. The rating scale is as follows: 1 = Not true, 2 = Sometimes true, 3 = Quite true, and 4 = Very true. The reliability of the instrument, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, is 0.89. Interpretation of resilience levels is based on the total scores for each dimension compared with predefined criteria. The Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) program used in this study was developed by the researcher based on the conceptual framework proposed by Segal et al.[25] integrating Beck’s[26] Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Kabat-Zinn’s[27] Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR). The program aimed to enhance mindfulness and promote awareness of one’s emotional states, thoughts, and feelings, leading to increased self-awareness and inner peace in daily life. The intervention was implemented in group sessions over 8 weeks, with one session per week. Each session lasted approximately 60 minutes and included four components: mindfulness practice, review of homework assignments, introduction of new topics, and assignment of the following week’s homework. The program consisted of eight key modules: 1) Managing Mental Distractions 2) A Mindful Approach to Life 3) Cultivating Emotional Detachment 4) Developing Awareness of Arising Thoughts and Letting Them Go Mindfully 5) Reflect on Personal Relationships 6) Mindful Communication 7) Loving-kindness and Forgiveness 8) Life Must Go On. After each session, participants were assigned weekly homework assignments to practice the skills learned in each session and prepare for the following session. Prior to implementing the program, the Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) program for enhancing resilience among junior helpers along with its detailed activity plan and facilitator’s manual was submitted for expert review. The panel consisted of three specialists: a psychiatrist, a psychiatric nursing instructor, and a public health academic with expertise in behavioral science. The experts were asked to evaluate content validity, accuracy, coverage, appropriateness, clarity, and language usage. Based on their feedback and recommendations, the program was revised and refined for improvement before its actual implementation. Procedures

The study was conducted in three phases:

1) Preparation Phase: This involved preparing the research instruments, intervention materials, and venue. The sample group was also prepared in accordance with the participant selection procedures.

1) Preparation Phase: This involved preparing the research instruments, intervention materials, and venue. The sample group was also prepared in accordance with the participant selection procedures.

2) Implementation Phase: Participants in the experimental group engaged in a group-based Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) program, delivered once per week over a period of eight consecutive weeks. Each session lasted approximately 60 minutes. After each session, participants were assigned weekly homework to be completed before the next session. The control group continued with regular academic activities provided by the School of Public Health.

2) Implementation Phase: Participants in the experimental group engaged in a group-based Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) program, delivered once per week over a period of eight consecutive weeks. Each session lasted approximately 60 minutes. After each session, participants were assigned weekly homework to be completed before the next session. The control group continued with regular academic activities provided by the School of Public Health.

3) Evaluation Phase: After the intervention, both the experimental and control groups were reassessed using the Resilience Quotient (RQ) scale to measure changes in psychological resilience.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage, were used to analyze demographic data (gender, age, and year of study). Resilience Scores were analyzed as follows: The differences in mean resilience scores before and after participation in the MBCT program between the experimental and control groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U Test. The differences in mean resilience scores before and after the MBCT program within the experimental group were compared using the Wilcoxon Matched Pairs Signed Ranks Test. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted after obtaining necessary ethical approvals from the ethics committee of Walailak university [Grant No. WUEC-25-008-01]. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to data collection.

Results

A total of 40 participants were equally divided into an experimental group (20 participants) and a control group (20 participants). The majority of participants in both groups were female, accounting for 80% in the experimental group and 70% in the control group. Participants were aged between 18 and 20 years, with most being 19 years old—representing 60% of the experimental group and 85% of the control group. The participants were first- and second-year students, with the majority being in their first year of study. [see Table 3].

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) in enhancing resilience among Junior Helpers from the School of Public Health in Thailand. Resilience was assessed across three core dimensions: Psychological Endurance, Hope and Psychological Support, and Overcoming Obstacles. The findings indicated that MBCT had a positive impact on all three dimensions within the experimental group.

These findings support previous research showing that MBCT fosters emotional regulation, enhances cognitive flexibility, and strengthens inner resources for coping with adversity.[28] These outcomes are particularly significant for Junior Helpers who serve as peer counselors and informal mental health supporters in their academic environments. Enhancing psychological resilience not only promotes their own well-being but also enhances their ability to support fellow students effectively.[282930]

The findings from this study indicate that participants in the experimental group demonstrated statistically significant improvements in psychological endurance following participation in the MBCT program. This aligns with previous research suggesting that mindfulness-based interventions can enhance an individual’s ability to remain emotionally stable, persevere through stress, and maintain mental clarity under pressure core characteristics of psychological endurance. This finding aligns with the work of van der Gucht et al.[31] who reported that adolescents receiving mindfulness training exhibited greater emotional control and resilience when facing emotional challenges, which are integral aspects of psychological endurance.

The increase in hope observed among participants may result from the development of greater self-awareness and acceptance fostered through mindfulness practice and cognitive restructuring exercises. According to Snyder’s Hope Theory[32] hope is driven by two key components: agency (goal-directed energy) and pathways (strategic planning to achieve goals). MBCT supports the development of these components by encouraging nonjudgmental awareness of thoughts and reappraisal of unhelpful thinking patterns. This suggests that mindfulness-based interventions can be effective in fostering hope. Prior research shows that mindfulness practices can reduce mental health challenges such as depression, anxiety, and stress[33] while simultaneously enhancing positive psychological traits such as happiness. Moreover, mindfulness practices have been found to increase levels of hope and motivation. Empirical studies have demonstrated that mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), such as Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) and Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), are significantly associated with improvements in psychological well-being. These interventions are particularly effective in fostering hope, resilience, and intrinsic motivation.[34]

These results underscore the idea that mindfulness practice not only reduces stress but also enhances psychological strengths such as hope and internal motivation that are fundamental for coping with adversity and fostering personal growth. MBCT’s mechanism of decentering allows individuals to perceive thoughts as transient mental events rather than absolute truths, thereby reducing emotional reactivity and fostering psychological strengths such as hope and resilience.[35]

The current study also demonstrated that MBCT enhances the ability to overcome obstacles among Junior Helpers, consistent with findings from previous research[3637] Mindfulness skills help individuals recognize their thought patterns, emotions, and impulsive behaviors, allowing for more intentional and adaptive responses rather than automatic ones when faced with challenges. Meta-analytic evidence shows a moderate positive correlation between mindfulness and resilience among university students (r = 0.465, P < 0.001).[37] Moreover, mindfulness-based programs have been shown to effectively enhance students’ stress resilience and overall well-being in educational settings.[38] Experimental findings further confirm that MBCT helps reduce emotional reactivity to stress, facilitating faster recovery and more adaptive coping when confronted with challenges.[36]

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) fosters meta-cognitive awareness and decentering, enabling students to recognize negative thoughts as transient mental events rather than absolute truths. This cognitive shift helps reduce emotional reactivity, promotes adaptive coping, and enhances psychological resilience. As a result, students develop psychological flexibility needed to effectively manage academic stress, interpersonal difficulties, and life challenges. Continued mindfulness practice enhances awareness of bodily sensations, emotions, and thoughts. Practices such as mindful breathing, body scanning, and seated meditation encourage individuals to observe thoughts, emotions, and feelings as transient, allowing for a deeper sense of calm and relaxation. Over time, these practices extend into daily life, encouraging nonjudgmental acceptance of unpleasant experiences rather than reactive or impulsive responses. This increased awareness contributes to improved emotional regulation, mental calmness, and overall happiness.[39]

Among Thai students, where cultural narratives may limit emotional expression, group-based MBCT offers a supportive and safe space for students to engage in shared reflection and emotional validation. This aligns with studies among Asian university populations, which have found that culturally adapted mindfulness interventions are effective in promoting well-being.[40]

Taken together, the findings suggest that MBCT is both a practical and impactful intervention for strengthening psychological resilience among peer-support student leaders, such as Junior Helpers. By addressing both personal and interpersonal aspects of mental health, MBCT contributes to a more supportive and mentally resilient campus environment.

Limitations and recommendation

While the findings of this study are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study’s sample size was small and institution-specific, limiting its applicability to broader contexts. The reliance on self-reported data may introduce bias. The study also focused solely on short-term outcomes and did not capture the long-term sustainability of the intervention’s effects. Future research should use longitudinal study designs, include qualitative methods, and involve more diverse student populations. It is recommended that higher education institutions integrate MBCT into student support services, especially for those serving in peer-support or leadership roles. Universities should consider integrating MBCT into mental health promotion programs. Further research should also investigate the long-term impacts of MBCT and its adaptability across different student demographics and cultural contexts.

Conclusion

The findings revealed that participants in the experimental group experienced statistically significant improvements across all three core dimensions of psychological resilience: Psychological endurance, hope and psychological support, and overcoming obstacles. These results suggest that MBCT can effectively strengthen specific aspects of resilience among university students who serve in peer-support roles. In the context of the education system, these findings highlight the potential of integrating MBCT-based programs into student development and mental health promotion initiatives. Such integration could enhance students’ emotional well-being, improve peer-support networks, and foster a more resilient academic community, thereby contributing to both personal growth and academic success.
