Iron/phosphine-catalyzed reductive cross-coupling to construct quaternary carbon centers
Authors: Qiao Zhang, Xiang-Yu Liu, Yu-Yu Li, Ming-Yao Huang, Shou-Fei Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransition metal-catalyzed C–C reductive coupling reactions avoid the direct use of sensitive metal reagents, representing a practical approach for constructing the skeletons of organic compounds. However, such reactions face significant challenges in constructing quaternary carbon centers. Herein, we report an example of iron-catalyzed reductive cross-coupling reaction of tertiary alkyl halides with allyl halides by using phosphine ligands. A directing strategy is developed for diverse reductive couplings of 3o C(sp3) with 1o C(sp3) and even 2o C(sp3). The directing effect of the carbonyl of amides and esters facilitates the iron catalyst in overcoming the substantial steric hindrance and numerous competing side reactions, allowing the reaction to proceed smoothly. The current study develops a directing strategy for highly steric reductive coupling reactions, and offers new insights into the efficient construction of complex C(sp3)–C(sp3) bonds using iron catalysts modified with phosphine ligands.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Homogeneous catalysis

Introduction

The construction of quaternary carbon centers has been a prominent research focus in organic synthesis and has received extensive attention1–5. Despite this interest, the field remains challenging due to significant steric hindrance and various side reactions6. Transition metal-catalyzed C–C coupling reactions are a direct method for constructing the skeletons of organic compounds and have also been used to construct quaternary carbon centers6,7. Currently, transition metal-catalyzed coupling reactions containing tertiary C(sp3) have been extensively studied, particularly in coupling with C(sp2)6–11. However, studies on coupling tertiary C(sp3) with other C(sp3) centers are relatively rare and predominantly rely on metal reagents (such as Grignard reagents and zinc reagents) that are sensitive to air and moisture and exhibit unsatisfactory functional group tolerance (Fig. 1a)12–20. Transition metal-catalyzed reductive coupling reactions, which avoid the direct use of sensitive organometallic reagents, present a more efficient method for constructing quaternary carbon centers. Nevertheless, these reactions are more complex due to the requirement of adding reductants and controlling the reaction sequence between coupling partners, thus imposing more stringent demands on the catalysts. In contrast to the significant progress of organometallic cross-coupling reactions, the research progress of reductive cross-coupling reactions has been very slow21. Consequently, successful examples of C(sp3)−C(sp3) reductive coupling are scarce22–27. To our knowledge, nickel catalysts exhibit better activity in constructing quaternary carbon centers than other metal catalysts (Fig. 1b)22–25. Therefore, developing new catalytic systems and coupling strategies to overcome the limitations of reductive coupling in constructing quaternary carbon centers could provide new pathways for building complex functional molecules and holds significant research value.

Iron is abundant, inexpensive, and exhibits good biocompatibility, making the development of iron-catalyzed reactions aligned with the principles of green chemistry28,29. Iron catalysts have shown significant potential in C–C coupling reactions involving Grignard/zinc reagents30–32. Despite this, research on iron-catalyzed C–C reductive coupling is relatively limited and has mainly focused on C(sp3)–C(sp2)/(sp) reductive couplings33–40. Quite recently, an elegant C(sp3)–C(sp3) reductive coupling between 3o and 1o alkyl redox-active esters has been reported by Kawamata, Baran, Shenvi and co-workers by using Fe/porphyrin as a catalyst (Fig. 1c)26. In addition, MacMillan et al. realized a photo/iron-catalyzed cross-electrophile coupling between alkyl halides and successfully constructed the quaternary carbon center lately, also used porphyrin as a ligand27. Herein, we report an iron/phosphine-catalyzed reductive coupling of tertiary alkyl halides with allyl halides, successfully forming 3o C(sp3)–1/2o C(sp3) bonds to construct various all-carbon quaternary centers (Fig. 1d). Notably, the 3o C(sp3) with 2o C(sp3) reductive coupling is previously unaccessible. This work further demonstrates the significant potential of iron catalysts in facilitating highly challenging coupling reactions through right choice of the ligands.

Results

Reaction development

In our initial study, we selected the tertiary alkyl brominated amide substrate 1a and allyl bromide 2a as starting materials for investigation (Table 1). Using Fe(BF-Phos)Cl2 (C1, 5 mol%) developed by our group as the catalyst41–44, Zn powder as the reductant, and TMEDA (N,N,N’,N’-tetramethylethylenediamine) as the additive, the reaction in THF (tetrahydrofuran) solvent for 4 h yielded the coupling product 3a with an optimal NMR yield of 93% (85% isolated yield) (entry 1). Control experiments revealed that both the iron catalyst and Zn powder were essential for the reaction (entries 2–3), while the ligand and additive TMEDA somewhat facilitated the reaction (entries 4–5). According to the literatures, TMEDA preferentially coordinates to the iron center, the resulting iron complex might either suppress β-hydride elimination side reactions of alkyl halides or modulate the halogen abstraction rate by low-valent iron species, thereby enabling efficient C–C coupling45,46. Substituting Zn with Mn resulted in no conversion of the starting materials (entry 6), and using In as the reductant led to only a small amount of the desired product, with the major product being the reduced byproduct 3a’ (entry 7). Replacing TMEDA with LiCl resulted in a substantial decrease in yield (entry 8). Moreover, using other bisphosphine-ligated iron catalysts (C2–C5) significantly decreased the product yield (entries 9−12). Complexes of cobalt, nickel, and copper with bisphosphine ligand BF-Phos as catalysts resulted in yields below 20% for the coupling product (entries 13−15). These results indicate that the iron catalyst has a unique advantage in this reaction.

Substrate scope

Upon determining the optimal reaction conditions, we evaluated the substrate scope (Fig. 2). Firstly, using allyl bromide as the model substrate, we assessed tertiary bromoamide substrates containing gem-dimethyl groups. We found that the electronic properties of substituents on the benzene ring of the amide substrate had little effect on the reaction, resulting in good conversions to the coupling products (3b-3e). Additionally, bromoamide substrates with various heterocyclic substituents, including furan (3f), thiophene (3g), indole (3h), pyridine (3i), and morpholine (3j), also underwent smooth reactions. The reaction demonstrated excellent functional group tolerance, successfully yielding coupling products from substrates containing alkenyl (3l, 3m), alkynyl (3n), halogen (3o), sulfonyl (3p), cyano (3q), acetal (3s), esteryl (3u) groups with good yields. However, substrates containing a keto carbonyl group (1t) showed lower reaction efficiency and more complex outcomes, including dehalogenated byproducts and allyl bromide-carbonyl addition byproducts. Aryl/cycloalkyl and tertiary alkyl amide substrates also gave coupling products in good yields (3v-3x), but amide substrates with strong coordinating groups resulted in only 39% yield (3y). Notably, substrates with pharmacologically active fragments successfully participated in the reaction, yielding the corresponding products (3z-3ab) with good to high yields. When the gem-dimethyl groups in the amide substrate were replaced with cycloalkyl groups, the reaction proceeded smoothly, with the size of the cycloalkyl ring having little impact on the outcome (3ac-3ae). Additionally, replacing the gem-dimethyl groups with methyl and ethyl or aryl groups in the amide substrates resulted in moderate to good yields of the coupling products (3af-3ai). Finally, we evaluated saturated amide-functionalized substrates (3aj, 3ak) and other primary/secondary alkyl bromoamide substrates (3ao-3as), all of which reacted smoothly to give moderate yields. However, for the cyclic bromoamide substrate 1al, the reaction efficiency was lower, yielding only a small amount of the desired product. When fully-substituted amide substrates were used in the reaction, a large amount of debromination products were generated, leading to low yields of the related coupling reactions. We hypothesized that this was due to the excessively large steric hindrance of this type of substrate. In addition, when the substrate contains an alkyl ester as a directing group, the reaction can occur smoothly to obtain the product (3am, 3an), whereas the substrate containing a α-ketone cannot afford the coupling product under standard reaction conditions.

In order to further explore the generality of this reaction system, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of various alkyl and allyl halide substrates. Tertiary alkyl bromide substrate 1at afforded the coupling product 3at in 46% yield, accompanied by debromination and elimination byproducts. Substrate 1au containing a sulfonyl group exhibited moderate reactivity, yielding product 3au in 30% yield. Additionally, secondary bromide substrate 1av smoothly provided the coupling product 3av. In contrast, primary alkyl bromide 1aw displayed poor reactivity, producing only trace amounts of the coupling product. For allyl substrates, allyl iodide demonstrated good reactivity, whereas allyl chloride was less reactive. Substituted allyl substrates afforded coupling products 3ax-3aab in yields ranging from 17% to 83%. The secondary allylic bromide 1aac afforded 3o C(sp3)–1o C(sp3) coupling product 3aac in low yield. When using crotonyl bromide as substrate, the 3o C(sp3)–2o C(sp3) coupling product 3aae was predominantly obtained. Other secondary allyl allylic bromide 1aaf, 1aah also generated the 3o C(sp3)–2o C(sp3) coupling product, although only with modest yields. However, the aryl-substituted allyl halogenated substrates involved in the reaction are more complex and do not show better results.

Gram-scale and transformation experiments

To demonstrate the application potential of this reaction, we conducted a gram-scale reaction and explored the transformation potential of product 3a (Fig. 3). The gram-scale experiment of the model reaction (8 mmol) afforded 1.43 g of product 3a with 82% yield, maintaining a good yield efficiency (Fig. 3a). The carbonyl group in the product can be converted into secondary amine 4 through a simple reduction reaction in 76% yield (Fig. 3b). The olefinic double bond, an important structural unit in synthetic chemistry, also showed versatility in transformation reactions. Under mild conditions, it afforded the brominated lactam product 5 in 88% yield (Fig. 3c). Oxidative conditions produced the lactone compound 6 in 88% yield (Fig. 3d). Additionally, in the presence of a silver catalyst, a fluorinated lactam compound 7 was obtained in 81% yield (Fig. 3e)47. These successful product transformations fully demonstrate the practicality of this reductive coupling in synthesizing high-value compounds that are otherwise difficult to access.

Preliminary mechanistic studies

In order to gain a deeper understanding of the reaction mechanism, we performed a series of control experiments (Fig. 4). The substrate 8 containing a cyclopropane ring yielded both the coupling product 9 and the ring-opened product 10 (14% yield). Similarly, substrate 11 yielded both the coupling product 12 and a cyclized product 13 (13% yield with 1:1 d.r., Fig. 4a). These radical clock experiment results suggested the possible formation of radicals in the reaction48,49. To further elucidate the reaction mechanism, radical trapping experiments were performed using TEMPO and 1,1-diphenylethylene as trapping agents. The resulting adducts were successfully detected by NMR and HRMS (see SI for details). Additionally, when DMPO was employed as the radical trapping reagent, the captured radical species were analyzed by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy and HRMS (see SI for details). In addition, when using 2a as the starting material and Zn as the reductant, the reaction yielded 24% of the homo-coupling product 2aa in the presence of C1, compared to only 8% yield without C1 (Fig. 4b). This significant difference suggests that the iron catalyst plays a crucial role in promoting the homo-coupling of substrate 2a, most likely through reduction to a low-valent iron species. Finally, when the reaction was catalyzed by Fe0(dppe)(dvtms)50, a good conversion to the coupling product was achieved (Fig. 4c), indicating that the reaction might be initiated by LFe0. In brief, this series of mechanistic investigations reveals the participation of radical intermediates and the promoting effect of the LFe0 catalyst during the reaction progress.

Preliminary mechanistic studies.

a Radical clock experiments with 8 and 11. b Homo-coupling of 2a catalyzed by C1. c Control experiments with Fe0 as the catalyst. d Proposed mechanism.

Based on experimental results and literature reports, we proposed a possible mechanism (Fig. 4d). In the initial stage of the reaction, allyl bromide reacts with Zn to generate allyl zinc reagent, the LFeII is reduced by the zinc reagent to intermediate I, which coordinates with the carbonyl group in substrate 1 to form intermediate II. The intermediate III and alkyl radical intermediate IV are generated through single electron transfer (SET). Intermediate III is reduced by allyl zinc reagent or Zn back to I, which undergoes SET with substrate 2a to give intermediate V, this intermediate is then reduced by allyl zinc reagent or Zn to intermediate VI, VI undergoes another SET with IV to form intermediate VII, which ultimately undergoes reductive elimination to yield the product and regenerate I, completing the catalytic cycle (Path A). In addition to this, the intermediate IV can also directly react with VI in a radical substitution reaction to generate the product (Path B). Previous literatures suggest that FeI species may also be generated under the reaction conditions51,52, therefore, we cannot exclude the FeI species-initiated pathways. Considering the possible presence of a higher amount of zinc reagents in the system, the possibility of direct coupling between the substrate and the zinc reagents in the system cannot be completely ruled out. Additionally, given the higher reactivity of substrates containing a carbonyl group, we speculate that the carbonyl group may serve as a directing group in the reaction, enhancing the reactivity of the substrate53. Because of the complexity of iron catalysis, we currently cannot rule out the possibility of the other alternative mechanisms54,55.

Discussion

We have achieved an iron-catalyzed 3o C(sp3)–1/2o C(sp3) reductive coupling reaction by using phosphine ligands, which not only breaks the boundary of iron-catalyzed reductive coupling, but also becomes one of the few catalytic systems to construct multiple quaternary carbon centers via reductive coupling strategy. Besides the use of phosphine ligands, another key to this success lies in the combination of the iron catalyst and the carbonyl directing group, which effectively suppresses common side reactions such as elimination and isomerization that typically occur in C(sp3)–C(sp3) reductive coupling reaction.

Methods

General procedure for Fe-catalyzed C(sp3)–C(sp3) reductive cross-coupling

In an argon-filled glovebox, an 8.0-mL vial was charged with zinc dust (0.4 mmol, 2.0 equiv), tetrahydrofuran (THF, 2.0 mL), and allyl bromide derivatives 2 (0.3 mmol, 1.5 equiv). The mixture was stirred at 23–26 °C for 10 min. Then, N,N,N’,N’-tetramethylethylenediamine (TMEDA, 0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv) was added, and the resulting mixture was stirred for another 5 min. Subsequently, Fe(BF-Phos)Cl₂ (0.01 mmol, 5 mol%) was introduced. Finally, compound 1 (0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv) was added all at once. The vial was taken out of the glovebox and stirred at 23–26 °C for 4 h. After the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and determined to be complete, the solution was passed through a short pad of silica gel and washed with ethyl acetate (EtOAc). The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure and then purified by silica gel column chromatography to obtain the desired product 3.
