Acquired von Willebrand Disease Associated With Severe Functional Mitral Regurgitation Improved by TEER
Authors: Yuki Obayashi, Shin Watanabe, Eri Toda Kato, Hiroki Shiomi, Chinatsu Yamada, Masayuki Fuki, Masahide Kawatou, Hisashi Sakaguchi, Kenji Minatoya, Koh Ono
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 75-year-old man with a history of small intestinal angioectasia underwent transcatheter edge-to-edge repair for severe functional mitral regurgitation, resulting in recovery of large von Willebrand Factor multimers. This case highlights the potential of transcatheter edge-to-edge repair to manage both heart failure and acquired von Willebrand disease associated with functional mitral regurgitation.

History of Presentation

A 75-year-old man was admitted to our department due to exacerbation of acute decompensated heart failure (HF) after conservative treatment for concurrent cholecystitis. His HF required intensive treatment including dobutamine administration and transient mechanical ventilation. His respiratory condition gradually improved; however, even after successful extubation, the patient remained classified as NYHA functional class IV. Past Medical History

The patient had a complex medical history, including recurrent HF admissions due to left ventricular dysfunction from a vasospastic myocardial infarction 16 years earlier, a suspected left ventricular thrombus and recurrent paroxysmal atrial fibrillation after catheter ablation, and small intestinal bleeding with angioectasia that led to the discontinuation of oral anticoagulation 3 years earlier (Figure 1). Differential Diagnosis

The potential exacerbating factors for the patient’s chronic HF included inflammation associated with concurrent cholecystitis and anemia resulting from the progression of renal anemia and the recurrence of small intestinal bleeding.

Investigations

Exercise stress echocardiography performed 6 months earlier during a recovered HF state already demonstrated severe functional mitral regurgitation (FMR) at a peak exercise workload of 40 W with an ergometer (Figure 2A, Video 1). Transthoracic echocardiography conducted during the current admission showed an enlarged left ventricle (left ventricular end-diastolic diameter: 73 mm) with a reduced ejection fraction (24.5%) and moderate to severe ventricular FMR with regurgitant volume (RV) of 44.7 mL and effective regurgitant orifice area of 0.32 cm2 (Figure 2B, Video 2). Subsequent transesophageal echocardiography further revealed severe FMR (RV 64 mL and effective regurgitant orifice area 0.77 cm2) between A2-P2 scallops, which was anatomically appropriate for transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) (Figure 3A, Video 3).Figure 2Transthoracic Echocardiography Before the Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair(A) Severe functional mitral regurgitation in an apical 3-chamber view by exercise stress echocardiography at a peak exercise workload of 40 W with an ergometer. Management

Considering the high surgical risk, a less invasive treatment was warranted. Our heart team concluded that the potential for TEER to improve HF symptoms outweighed the risk of exacerbating small intestinal bleeding by periprocedural antithrombotic therapy and the possible complications of TEER (eg, thrombosis, cardiac tamponade, clip detachment).

The TEER procedure was performed under general anesthesia and transesophageal echocardiography guidance as usual. After transseptal puncture, a MitraClip XTW (Abbott Vascular) was inserted from the right femoral vein with a 24-F steerable guide catheter and clip delivery system. After grasping the A2-P2 scallops, FMR reduced from severe to mild (Figure 3B, Video 4). Apixaban was started after TEER, and the postoperative course did not have major events. On the 22nd day after the TEER, vWF multimer analysis revealed increased large multimers and recovery to normal multimer distribution (Figure 4B).

Outcome and Follow-Up

After the successful TEER, the patient’s symptoms slightly improved but persisted at NYHA functional class IV. His blood pressure remained too low to allow for the safe initiation of other guideline-recommended medications, and his advanced age along with the severity of his additional comorbidities precluded the heart transplantation option. The patient was ultimately discharged from our hospital 76 days after the TEER procedure with the continued support of intravenous dobutamine and the care of a home-visiting physician, precluding the acquisition of long-term data on vWF multimer analysis.

Discussion

AvWD is associated with various cardiovascular diseases and mechanical supports such as aortic stenosis,1 hypertrophic cardiomyopathy,2,3 ventricular assist device,4 and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.5 However, AvWD due to mitral regurgitation (MR) is poorly understood.6 Especially, the association between AvWD and FMR secondary to left ventricular dysfunction is rarely reported.

This case strongly suggested that AvWD could attribute to FMR through preexisting small intestine angioectasia, as indicated by the recovered vWF high-molecular-weight multimers (HMWM) after TEER. Although not as widely recognized as Heyde syndrome, the association between AvWD and MR has been already reported in 1981.7 The potential mechanism underlying the development of AvWD in the setting of MR is similar to that observed in other cardiovascular disease state, whereby high shear stress promotes the proteolytic decomposition of vWF.8 However, previous reports on AvWD have mostly been limited to primary MR. This observation may be partly explained by the characteristics of flow dynamics of different MR etiologies. The regurgitant jet of primary MR is more likely to be eccentric and may cause higher shear stress with the left atrial wall near the mitral annulus compared with the typically more centralized and symmetrical regurgitant flow patterns seen in FMR.9 In fact, patients with FMR have been shown to have a higher level or activity of vWF than those with primary MR.9, 10, 11 As a result, the development of severe anemia may have been more pronounced in patients with primary MR, potentially leading to an earlier recognition of the AvWD-MR association in that population. In addition, a previous study in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy demonstrated that latent obstruction can alter vWF multimers.3 Similarly, it is possible that functional MR in this patient, which worsens during exercise, has a cumulative impact on vWF multimers.

There are 3 possible reasons why this particular case of FMR presented with the development of AvWD, despite the aforementioned mechanisms underlying the association between AvWD and MR. First, in the context of aortic stenosis, greater disease severity has been shown to correlate with higher shear stress, resulting in a decrease in HMWM. Likewise, in patients with MR, the increased RV associated with more severe MR appears to be a key determinant of the decreased HMWM.6 Thus, even if the shear stress created by the MR itself was not exceptionally high, the greatly increased RV may have been sufficient to precipitate the development of AvWD in the current case with FMR. Second, this patient’s MR was relatively eccentric, with the high-velocity regurgitant jet striking the posterior atrial wall and reaching the ceiling of the left atrium. Although the main etiology of MR in this case was ventricular FMR, asymmetrical posterior-dominant leaflet tethering resulted in an eccentric jet pattern. The collision of MR jet with the left atrial wall and ceiling may have generated localized regions of particularly high shear stress, further promoting the degradation of HMWM and the subsequent emergence of AvWD. Third, this patient was relatively older at 75 years of age and had chronic kidney disease, which are known to be risk factors for angioectasia. These comorbidities may also contribute to the development of clinically significant bleeding due to AvWD.

Conclusions

AvWD should be considered as a potential complication in patients with severe MR and concomitant gastrointestinal bleeding, even in the setting of FMR. Additionally, a detailed assessment of the MR jet morphology in FMR, which generally has lower shear stress than primary MR, may be crucial for understanding the subsequent emergence of AvWD. TEER may be a promising treatment option to manage both HF symptoms and the associated bleeding diathesis with severe FMR complicated by AvWD.
