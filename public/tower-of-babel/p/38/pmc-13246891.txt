Diagnostic performance of vector flow imaging–derived wall shear stress and radiofrequency echo-tracking–derived arterial stiffness indices in severe carotid stenosis
Authors: Nezih Yaylı, Atiye Cenay Karabörk Kılıç, Burcu Küçük Biçer, Fatih Öncü, Taylan Altıparmak, Mahi Nur Cerit, Halit Nahit Şendur, Hale Zeynep Batur Çağlayan, Mehmet Koray Akkan, Hüseyin Koray Kılıç, Suna Özhan Oktar, Ahmet Baran Önal, Bijen Nazlıel, Erhan Turgut Ilgıt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of vector flow imaging (VFI)–derived hemodynamic parameters and radiofrequency echo-tracking–derived arterial stiffness indices (RF-ASI) for detecting severe carotid artery stenosis.MethodsThis prospective, single-center observational study included 90 patients with carotid artery disease examined between May and July 2024. Stenosis severity was categorized as <50%, 50%–69%, and ≥70% according to the Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound (SRU) Doppler criteria. In patients with stenosis ≥50%, severity was confirmed using digital subtraction angiography, computed tomography angiography, or magnetic resonance angiography. At the plaque level, VFI-derived wall shear stress (WSS) and turbulence metrics, as well as RF-ASI, were measured. Correlation analyses, group comparisons, and receiver operating characteristic analysis were performed to evaluate each parameter’s ability to detect ≥70% stenosis.ResultsAmong the 90 patients (mean age, 70.4±8.7 years; 55.6% male), peak-segment mean wall shear stress (WSSmean) demonstrated the strongest correlation with stenosis severity (rs=0.87, P<0.001). WSSmean also showed the highest diagnostic performance for detecting ≥70% stenosis (area under the curve, 0.96; 95% confidence interval, 0.92 to 0.99; cutoff, 2.25 Pa), compared with other VFI-derived metrics and RF-ASI. Segmental analysis revealed that WSS values were highest at the plaque peak, whereas turbulence-related metrics were more pronounced in distal segments. In SRU-discordant cases (7 of 90), WSSmean agreed with anatomic imaging–based classification (≥70% vs. <70%) in six of seven lesions.ConclusionVFI-derived WSSmean demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance for detecting severe (≥70%) carotid stenosis. In contrast, RF-ASI showed limited discriminatory ability and may primarily reflect global arterial stiffness rather than focal plaque severity.

Graphical abstract

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is a leading global health problem and the second most common cause of death and disability [1]. Atherosclerotic carotid artery disease (CAD) accounts for approximately 20% of ischemic strokes and is often clinically silent [2]. While severe stenosis may lead to ischemia due to hypoperfusion, ischemic stroke may also occur in the absence of significant stenosis as a result of plaque rupture and thromboembolism [3]. Although digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is the gold standard for the evaluation of CAD, its invasive nature limits its routine use for diagnostic purposes [4]. The use of contrast media in computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) further limits routine application, particularly in patients with renal failure [5,6]. Therefore, ultrasound (US) is widely used as a first-line modality because it is noninvasive and readily available [6]. However, the velocity-based stenosis grading of the Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound (SRU) criteria provides limited information regarding the hemodynamic complexity of CAD [7].

The role of hemodynamic forces in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis is increasingly being investigated [8]. Through mechanotransduction pathways, hemodynamic forces are converted into biochemical responses within endothelial cells [8,9]. Among these forces, wall shear stress (WSS) is a tangential force exerted by blood flow on the endothelial surface, and altered shear patterns have been implicated in plaque development and vulnerability [9]. Turbulent flow can generate irregular and oscillatory shear forces along the vessel wall, leading to localized mechanical stress that may trigger inflammatory signaling [10,11]. Furthermore, the mechanical properties of the vessel wall interact with intraluminal hemodynamic forces; accordingly, arterial stiffness can influence local flow dynamics by altering wave propagation and wall deformation [12].

Novel US-based techniques, such as vector flow imaging (VFI) and radiofrequency (RF) echo-tracking, have been introduced to evaluate these hemodynamic forces [13–15]. Previous VFI studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between WSS and stenosis severity and have established cutoff values for WSS in the detection of severe carotid stenosis [15,16]. Subsequent work further showed improved diagnostic performance with the incorporation of turbulence-related parameters [17].

Radiofrequency echo-tracking–derived arterial stiffness indices (RF-ASI) have been widely investigated as markers of vascular aging and have shown consistent associations with subclinical atherosclerosis [18,19]. However, despite this growing body of evidence, RF-ASI have not been incorporated into carotid stenosis grading frameworks. Moreover, VFI-derived hemodynamic metrics and RF-ASI have not yet been evaluated concurrently at the plaque level within the same patient cohort.

Therefore, this study was designed with three specific aims. The first aim was to compare the diagnostic performance of VFI-derived hemodynamic parameters and RF-ASI for detecting severe (≥70%) internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis. The second aim was to determine whether VFI-derived parameters provide complementary hemodynamic information beyond SRU-based stenosis classification. The third aim was to characterize the spatial distribution and interaction patterns of VFI-derived parameters across plaque segments through plaque-based segmental analysis.

Materials and Methods

Compliance with Ethical Standards

This prospective observational study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval No. AEŞH-EK1-2024-0021), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study Design and Patient Selection

Between May and July 2024, a total of 130 consecutive patients with suspected or known CAD who were referred to Department of Radiology, Gazi University Faculty of Medicine for carotid Doppler US were initially screened for eligibility (Fig. 1).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years and (2) presence of an atherosclerotic plaque at the carotid bifurcation (bulb/proximal ICA). Carotid stenosis severity was categorized as <50%, 50%–69%, or ≥70% according to the SRU criteria. In patients with ≥50% stenosis, the severity of stenosis was subsequently confirmed using CTA, DSA, or MRA.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-atherosclerotic ICA stenosis, such as dissection or vasculitis; (2) complete ICA occlusion; (3) absence of confirmatory imaging (CTA, DSA, or MRA) in patients classified as having ≥50% stenosis on Doppler US; (4) inadequate image quality for Doppler or VFI acquisition resulting from poor breathing cooperation, marked vessel tortuosity, or other technical limitations; and (5) prior carotid endarterectomy or carotid artery stenting.

In total, 90 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. Demographic and clinical data, including cardiovascular risk factors and history of stroke, were systematically recorded for all participants.

Conventional Carotid Doppler US

All US examinations were performed using a single US system (Mindray Biomedical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) equipped with an L9-3U (1.8–9.8 MHz) linear array transducer. All examinations were conducted by a single radiologist with 5 years of experience in vascular US to ensure procedural consistency. The common carotid artery (CCA), bifurcation, external carotid artery, and ICA were evaluated using B-mode imaging. Intima–media thickness was measured on longitudinal CCA images, and the arterial lumen was systematically evaluated for the presence of atherosclerotic plaques. Plaque length, thickness, and echogenicity were recorded for each lesion. Plaques were classified using an echogenicity-based scale ranging from predominantly hypoechoic plaques (type 1) to predominantly hyperechoic or calcified plaques (type 5). Initial stenosis grading was performed according to the SRU consensus criteria [7]. At the plaque level, following technical optimization that included angle correction and velocity scale adjustment, Doppler sampling was performed at the visually most stenotic segment identified on combined B-mode and color Doppler imaging. Peak systolic velocity and end-diastolic velocity values were then recorded. In patients with ≥50% stenosis on Doppler US, final stenosis grading was determined using North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET) criteria based on DSA (n=45), CTA (n=10), or MRA (n=5) findings.

VFI and RF Echo-Tracking–Derived Stiffness Indices: Acquisition and Quantitative Analysis

After the Doppler examination, the V-Flow mode was activated. The transducer was positioned in a longitudinal-oblique plane to visualize the stenotic segment, and acquisition parameters were standardized as follows: frequency, 5 MHz; depth, 2–4 cm; arrow life cycle, 25 ms; and arrow density, 10% [16]. A region of interest (ROI) was positioned over the stenotic segment for vector imaging. A high-frame-rate cine loop covering at least one complete cardiac cycle (approximately 2 seconds) was acquired for subsequent quantitative analysis.

After completion of the VFI assessment, the RF echo-tracking mode was activated on the US system. Measurements were obtained at the level of the stenotic plaque. To improve measurement consistency, the probe was aligned as parallel as possible to the vessel axis, and an appropriate ROI was selected. Patients were instructed to briefly hold their breath during image acquisition to minimize motion-related artifacts. Following automated segmentation of the anterior and posterior vessel walls by the system, vessel wall displacement and diameter changes were recorded using cine loops covering at least six cardiac cycles.

Quantitative measurements were performed using the peak systolic phase selected from the high-frame-rate cine loops. WSS analysis was conducted at three predefined plaque segments: proximal (upstream), peak (narrowest), and distal (downstream). ROIs were positioned on the plaque surface and aligned parallel to the local direction of flow. Two ROIs were placed per segment, and mean values of maximum wall shear stress (WSSmax) and mean wall shear stress (WSSmean) were calculated and expressed in pascals (Pa). WSS values were automatically derived by the system using velocity components parallel to the vessel wall and the radial distance from the vessel wall.

Turbulence measurements were obtained using the “Velocity ROI” function, with ROIs placed at the same three plaque segments. The turbulence index (TUR) and time-averaged turbulence (TAT) were automatically calculated based on changes in flow vector direction and magnitude and were reported as percentage values (%) [20]. Higher values reflect increasingly disturbed or complex flow patterns.

For RF echo-tracking analysis, six cardiac cycles in which arterial wall motion was optimally visualized were selected. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were entered into the system, after which the hardness coefficient (HC) and pulse wave velocity (PWV, m/s) were automatically calculated based on vessel dilatation, blood pressure data, vessel diameter (Dd), diameter change (ΔD), and pulse pressure (Δp).

All VFI and RF echo-tracking images were jointly reviewed by two radiologists, one with more than 20 years of experience in vascular imaging and the other with 5 years of experience. Image quality, ROI placement, and measured parameter values were reviewed collaboratively. Each measurement was performed three times, and the mean value was used for analysis. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphical visualizations were generated using Python (v3.10). Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation or median (interquartile range), whereas categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (n, %). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons of VFI and RF-ASI parameters among the three stenosis groups (<50%, 50%–69%, and ≥70%) were performed using analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test, depending on data distribution. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to assess associations between stenosis severity and imaging parameters, as well as interrelationships among VFI-derived metrics. To assess the diagnostic accuracy of VFI and RF-ASI parameters for detecting ≥70% stenosis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index. Differences in VFI parameters according to plaque location were evaluated using the paired-samples t-test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Descriptive Imaging Findings

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. A total of 90 patients were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 70.4±8.7 years, and 55.6% of the cohort was male. Stenosis severity was classified as <50% in 33.3% of patients, 50%–69% in 32.2%, and ≥70% in 34.5%.

The distribution of VFI-derived parameters across plaque segments, as well as plaque-level RF-ASI parameters, is summarized in Table 2.

Correlation between Parameters and Stenosis Severity

Peak-segment WSSmean showed the strongest correlation with stenosis severity (rs=0.87, P<0.001). In the distal segment, correlation coefficients were higher for turbulence metrics (TUR/TAT, rs=0.62–0.66) than for WSS metrics (rs=0.23–0.25). Plaque-level RF-ASI parameters demonstrated moderate correlations with stenosis severity (PWV, rs=0.46; HC, rs=0.45; both P<0.001) (Table 3).

Groupwise Comparison of Novel US Parameters

Fig. 2 demonstrates increasing peak-segment VFI parameter values with increasing stenosis severity, with detailed statistics provided in Supplementary Table 1. All peak-segment VFI metrics differed significantly across stenosis groups (all P<0.001), whereas proximal-segment differences were limited to WSSmax (P<0.001). In the distal segment, turbulence metrics differed significantly across stenosis groups (both P<0.001), whereas between-group differences in WSS metrics were more limited (WSSmax P=0.011, WSSmean P<0.05). RF-ASI parameters also differed across stenosis groups (P<0.001); however, their distributions overlapped substantially between the 50%–69% and ≥70% groups (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1).

Representative VFI and RF echo-tracking imaging examples from patients with <50% and ≥70% stenosis are shown in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively.

Diagnostic Performance of Parameters for Detecting ≥70% Stenosis

ROC analyses of peak-segment VFI metrics and plaque-level RF-ASI parameters for detecting ≥70% stenosis are presented in Fig. 5.

As detailed in Table 4, WSSmean demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance for detecting ≥70% stenosis (AUC, 0.96; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.92 to 0.99; cutoff, 2.25 Pa; sensitivity, 96.8%; specificity, 84.7%; P<0.001). TUR yielded an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI, 0.76 to 0.92), WSSmax an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI, 0.73 to 0.90), and TAT an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI, 0.72 to 0.90) (all P<0.001). PWV and HC showed lower diagnostic performance, with AUC values of 0.69 (95% CI, 0.58 to 0.80) and 0.68 (95% CI, 0.57 to 0.79), respectively (both P<0.001).

Discordant SRU–NASCET Subgroup

SRU-based grading and NASCET-based classification using CTA, DSA, or MRA were discordant in seven of 90 cases (7.8%). Using ROC-derived cutoffs for ≥70% stenosis, WSSmean correctly classified six of seven discordant cases, compared with three of seven for WSSmax, three of seven for TUR and TAT, and two of seven for PWV and HC. In these discordant cases, WSSmean exceeded the cutoff in three of four lesions classified as ≥70% by NASCET but <70% by SRU, and fell below the cutoff in all three lesions classified as ≥70% by SRU but <70% by NASCET. Individual plaque-level values are provided in Supplementary Table 2; findings are descriptive due to the small sample size.

Relationship between Hemodynamic Parameters

At the plaque peak segment, turbulence metrics showed moderate to strong positive correlations with WSS parameters (TUR vs. WSSmax, rs=0.58; TUR vs. WSSmean, rs=0.55; TAT vs. WSSmax, rs=0.61; TAT vs. WSSmean, rs=0.53; all P≤0.001). Additional correlations and corresponding scatter plots are provided in Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1.

Segmental Differences in Hemodynamic Parameters

Segmental comparisons are summarized in Table 5. Peak-segment WSSmax and WSSmean values were higher than both proximal values (both P<0.001) and distal values (WSSmax P<0.001, WSSmean P<0.05). TUR and TAT values were higher in the distal segment than in the proximal segment (both P<0.001).

Discussion

In this study, VFI-derived hemodynamic metrics, particularly peak-segment WSSmean, demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance (AUC, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.92 to 0.99) for identifying severe (≥70%) carotid stenosis when compared with other VFI-derived metrics and RF-ASI. Segmental analyses further indicated spatial heterogeneity in flow-related parameters, characterized by higher WSS values at the plaque peak and more pronounced turbulence in distal segments. In addition, WSSmean provided complementary information in the small subgroup of cases with discordant SRU-based grading and anatomic imaging–based stenosis classification.

Previous studies have investigated VFI-derived WSS metrics in relation to plaque vulnerability, cerebrovascular risk stratification, and stenosis severity assessment [21–25], whereas VFI-based turbulence analyses have more frequently remained qualitative or focused on segmental flow-pattern comparisons [26,27]. Qiu et al. [16] reported excellent diagnostic performance for detecting severe carotid artery stenosis in a VFI-based study (AUC, 0.96 when combined with conventional US). Similarly, Hong et al. [17] demonstrated good diagnostic performance for severe CAD using VFI-derived parameters (TUR, AUC≈0.80; WSSmean, AUC=0.84). In the present study, overall diagnostic performance for detecting severe CAD was comparable to that reported by Qiu et al. [16]. Moreover, the cutoff values defined for VFI metrics were close to those reported by Hong et al. [17] using a similar US platform (WSSmean, 2.647 Pa; TUR, 22.98%). Collectively, variability in reference standards, cohort characteristics, and device or software implementations likely contributes to differences in reported cutoff values and diagnostic performance across studies.

Segment-based distribution patterns of WSS and turbulence parameters across carotid plaques have been described in a limited number of prior studies [28,29]. These studies consistently report the highest WSS at the plaque peak and often describe higher WSS values proximally than distally [29]. In the present cohort, while peak-level findings were concordant with prior reports, WSSmax was higher distally than proximally. This divergence may be attributable to plaque morphology; accordingly, associations between morphological features and segment-based hemodynamic variables were examined (Supplementary Table 5). Plaque thickness and length showed positive correlations with peak- and distal-level hemodynamic parameters, whereas no significant correlations were observed at the proximal segment. Taken together, these morphology–hemodynamics relationships may help explain the relatively higher distal WSSmax observed in this cohort. Nevertheless, these associations should be interpreted cautiously given the correlational design and modest sample size and require confirmation in larger studies. Consistent with prior reports, turbulence metrics were higher distally than proximally [26,27], supporting a distal predominance of flow disturbance.

Previous investigations have also reported a negative correlation between WSS and turbulence parameters, most commonly at the carotid bifurcation level [30]. In contrast, the present analysis demonstrated a moderate to strong positive correlation between these parameters at the plaque peak (rs=0.53–0.61, P≤0.001), alongside a weak negative correlation between TAT and WSSmean at the proximal segment (rs=−0.21, P<0.05) (Supplementary Table 3), indicating segment-dependent relationships. This apparent discrepancy may reflect region-specific flow conditions within stenotic plaques, where localized acceleration and flow disturbance coexist [28]. Supporting this interpretation, a similar positive coupling between turbulence-related metrics and WSS has been reported using in vivo four-dimensional magnetic resonance imaging [31]. The observed peak-level WSS–turbulence coupling may therefore represent a high-stress hemodynamic environment, although its prognostic significance remains to be established through longitudinal studies.

Given its performance in discriminating ≥70% stenosis in this cohort, WSSmean may serve as an adjunct to SRU-based Doppler velocity criteria when classifying stenosis as ≥70% versus <70%. This complementary value was most evident in cases where SRU-based grading and anatomic imaging–based stenosis classification were discordant. Within this discordant subgroup (n=7), WSSmean aligned with the ≥70% versus <70% classification in six of seven lesions, whereas other VFI indices and RF-ASI demonstrated limited agreement. Specifically, WSSmean exceeded the ROC-derived cutoff in lesions classified as ≥70% by anatomic imaging but <70% by SRU and fell below the cutoff in the reverse discordant scenario (SRU ≥70% and anatomic imaging <70%). Although this analysis is descriptive and based on a small number of cases, it suggests that WSSmean may provide additional support when distinguishing borderline 50%–69% from ≥70% stenosis in discordant assessments.

RF-ASI have primarily been studied as markers of systemic vascular involvement at the CCA level, and their utility in classifying focal plaque-level stenosis has not been systematically examined [18,19]. In this cohort, plaque-level PWV and HC demonstrated lower discriminatory performance than VFI-derived hemodynamic metrics, suggesting that these indices may more accurately reflect a generalized arterial stiffness phenotype rather than focal luminal stenosis severity. PWV and HC showed weak but statistically significant associations with markers of systemic atherosclerotic burden, including peripheral artery disease, coronary artery disease, and hypertension (Supplementary Table 4). However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously in the absence of multivariable adjustment.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest and derived from a single center, underscoring the need for multicenter validation in more diverse populations. Second, VFI-derived metrics and RF-ASI are dependent on platform and software implementation; accordingly, the cutoff values reported here should be regarded as device- and protocol-specific and require external validation or recalibration across systems. Third, plaque-level RF-ASI measurements are susceptible to motion-related artifacts and local geometric factors, including respiratory or cardiac motion, vessel tortuosity, and plaque or wall heterogeneity. Additionally, formal inter- or intra-observer reproducibility analyses were not performed, which may have contributed to the lower performance of PWV and HC in plaque-level stenosis classification. Improved software support for standardized plaque-level ROI definition, including manual correction when necessary, may enhance reproducibility and should be explored in future studies. Finally, partial verification bias cannot be excluded, particularly in the low-grade stenosis group. In this cohort, arteries with SRU-based stenosis ≥50% underwent NASCET-based assessment using CTA, MRA, or DSA as the reference standard for ROC analyses of ≥70% stenosis, whereas arteries with SRU <50% did not undergo systematic anatomic imaging [32]. Although duplex US has good accuracy for detecting 0%–49% stenosis [5], the absence of a uniform anatomic reference in the <50% group may result in overestimation of specificity and AUC. Consequently, the diagnostic performance of WSSmean should be interpreted with caution, and future prospective studies applying a uniform anatomic imaging reference across all stenosis categories are warranted.

In summary, VFI-derived WSSmean emerged as the strongest ultrasonographic parameter for identifying severe (≥70%) carotid artery stenosis. Segmental VFI analysis revealed spatial heterogeneity in hemodynamic patterns, with higher WSS at the plaque peak and more pronounced turbulence in distal segments. In contrast, RF-ASI parameters, including PWV and HC, demonstrated limited added value for distinguishing severe stenosis relative to VFI-derived metrics. Importantly, WSSmean may provide complementary information in cases where SRU-based Doppler grading and NASCET-based stenosis classification are discordant.
