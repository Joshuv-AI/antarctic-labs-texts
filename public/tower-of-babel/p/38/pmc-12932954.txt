Recurrence plot reconstruction reveals chromosomal reorganization before territory formation
Authors: Yuki Kitanishi, Hiroki Sugishita, Yukiko Gotoh, Yoshito Hirata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromatin conformation capture methods such as Hi-C have improved understanding of nuclear architecture. However, reconstruction from single-cell Hi-C (scHi-C) data is challenging due to limited DNA contacts per cell. We have previously developed the recurrence plot-based reconstruction (RPR) method for reconstructing three-dimensional (3D) genomic structure from Hi-C data even from low-coverage DNA contact information. Here we used the RPR method to analyze scHi-C data derived from early-stage F1 hybrid embryos as a proof-of-concept for understanding of global chromosomal architecture. We found that paternal and maternal chromosomes become gradually intermingled from the 1-cell to the 64-cell stage, and that discrete chromosome territories are largely established between 8-cell and 64-cell stages. We also observed Rabl-like polarization of chromosomes from the 2- to 8-cell stage, which was mostly dissolved by the 64-cell stage. We also noted transient rod-like extension and parallel chromosome alignment at the 4-cell stage. These findings indicate dynamic chromosomal reorganization before territory formation. RPR and scHi-C together capture 3D chromosomal architecture of individual cells during early embryogenesis.

Introduction

Spatial organization of the mammalian genome plays a key role in transcription, RNA processing, and DNA replication [1–4]. The chromatin is hierarchically organized into local loops, into different scales of domains and compartments, and into chromosome territories (CTs) [5–8]. Hierarchical organization of chromatin is a foundation for gene regulation and cell function. Classically, spatial localization of genomic regions of interest in the nucleus has been elucidated with DNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) [9]. However, although recent endeavors have greatly increased the number of genomic loci to be analyzed [10, 11], FISH-based methods can analyze only targeted genomic loci of interest. By contrast, Hi-C, a derivative of chromosome conformation capture [12], can elucidate non-targeted and genome-wide pair-wise DNA contacts by sequencing after proximity-dependent ligation [8]. Application of Hi-C to bulk cell populations (bulk Hi-C) has revealed principles of chromatin organization, such as organization of chromatin into topology-associated domains and A/B compartments [8, 13–15]. Because bulk Hi-C per se cannot map regions in relation to the global nuclear geography [the three-dimensional (3D) genome atlas], bulk Hi-C is used with other imaging and epigenetic analyses (such as DNA-FISH and Lamin–DamID) to map regions in the 3D genome atlas and to understand how chromatin architectures are coordinated in the nucleus [16, 17]. Bulk Hi-C measures the average frequency of contacts between pairs of genomic regions across many cells, based on proximity ligation. However, it cannot reveal how multiple genomic regions are spatially organized together within individual nuclei [18]. Other methods can simultaneously identify contacts involving multiple genomic regions. These methods include genome architecture mapping (GAM; which is based on ultrathin cryo-sectioning, DNA sequencing, and the mathematical model SLICE) [19, 20] and split-pool recognition of interactions by tag extension (SPRITE; which identifies nearby genomic regions by barcoding during split-pool cycles) [21].

The 3D architecture of chromatin changes dramatically during cell differentiation and during cell state transitions. Even between cells of the same type and state, chromatin architecture is highly dynamic and heterogenous [22, 23]. It is thus crucial to investigate the 3D genome atlas at a single-cell level. Sequential FISH (seqFISH), in situ genome sequencing (IGS), single-cell SPRITE (scSPRITE), and single-cell Hi-C (scHi-C) have been developed to construct 3D genome atlases for single cells in a non-targeted and genome-wide manner. seqFISH is an advanced imaging technique that enables the identification and spatial mapping of thousands of RNA, DNA, and protein molecules within single cells by multiple cycles of probe or antibody hybridization, imaging, and removal [24]. IGS is a method to spatially map genome-wide paired-end sequence reads to their endogenous locations within intact cells [10, 25]. scSPRITE is an improved version of SPRITE, which perform split-and-pool barcoding in a single nucleus [26]. scHi-C (a modified version of the widely used Hi-C) has been applied to both haploid and diploid cells [27–39]. In diploid scHi-C, allele-specific single nucleotide variations (SNVs) are used to differentiate paternal and maternal alleles. However, reconstruction of the 3D nuclear architecture from scHi-C data has been challenging due to a limited number of DNA contacts recovered from a single cell as well as the limited frequency of SNVs within the genome. Tan et al. have developed a modified scHi-C method for diploid cells called Dip-C that employs a transposon-based whole-genome amplification procedure and a method for 3D genome reconstruction based on a beads-on-a-string algorithm and an imputation of the two chromosome haplotypes linked by each contact [38]. Other algorithms for reconstructing 3D chromosome structure based on scHi-C data have also been reported [39–43]. We reported the recurrence plot-based reconstruction (RPR) method that reconstructs the 3D chromosome structure from scHi-C data of either haploid [40] or diploid cells [42]. This method is based on a recurrence plot (a tool of non-linear time-series analysis for visualizing patterns within a time series) [40, 44, 45] and enables a unique 3D chromosome architecture to be reconstructed from even low-coverage DNA contact information. The RPR method has been mathematically proven [40, 46, 47]. While a beads-on-a-string algorithm is based on the local “shape” of chromosomes and does not necessarily reflect the global 3D coordinates of the nucleus, the RPR algorithm only assumes that consecutive genomic regions are spatial neighbors, and reflects the relative distance between genomic loci along the global 3D coordinates, which is beneficial for predicting neighboring chromosomal regions as well as global chromosomal architecture which involves multiple genomic loci [40, 42].

As a proof-of-concept, we analyzed 3D nuclear architectures of cells at early pre-implantation stages after fertilization with the use of the RPR method and a set of publicly available scHi-C data [48], given that chromatins at these stages undergo dramatic changes as development proceeds [49]. Our results confirm some of the observations obtained with FISH and IGS, such as gradual mixing of paternal and maternal alleles during progression from the zygote to the 64-cell stage. Our analyses also revealed that discrete (localized) CTs are established between 8-cell and 64-cell stages, and that unique chromosomal configurations, namely Rabl-like polarization and rod-like extension of chromosomes, transiently take place before CT establishment.

Materials and methods

scHi-C data processing

Processed scHi-C data of early-stage mouse embryos were obtained from Collombet et al. [48] (GSE129029). In addition, processed scHi-C data of postnatal neurons (postnatal day 0) were obtained from Tan et al. [50] (GSE146397). Details of quality control, Hi-C read mapping, and allele-specific assignment are provided in the original publications.

Visualization of RPR

We visualized the 3D chromosome structure regarding the top three components obtained from RPR analysis as 3D coordinates. Each segment was visualized in 1 Mb resolution and the overall structure was displayed as a beads-on-a-string structure by Pymol (version 2.4.0).

Exclusion of cells using the inaccuracy score

The standard deviation (SD) of the inaccuracy score was calculated to exclude cells with inaccurate structure as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S1h. We set the threshold of the inaccuracy score SD as 0.15 and the cells over this value were removed from further analyses. We excluded 35 early-stage mouse embryos based on the inaccuracy score threshold (12 from 1-cell, 3 from 2-cell, 3 from 4-cell, 3 from 8-cell, and 14 from 64-cell).

Calculation of the maternal–paternal intermixing score

Euclidean distances between each pair of all 1 Mb segments were calculated. The fifth percentile of shortest distances was a threshold for deciding whether any two bins were closely located (Supplementary Fig. S2). A “maternal–paternal intermixing score” for each 1 Mb segment was calculated as the proportion of neighboring segments with different alleles (out of all neighboring segments). Neighboring segments in the same chromosome were excluded from this calculation to remove the bias of genomically close segments. The average score for each cell was determined by averaging the overall maternal–paternal intermixing score of all 1 Mb segments.

Calculation of the mean chromosomal position of proximal pairs

In this analysis, we followed the method of Payne et al. [54] with some modifications. “Chromosomal position” for each 1 Mb segment was calculated as the relative position in the chromosomal axis, which took a continuous value from 0 to 1 (the p-arm terminus = 0, the q-arm terminus = 1). The mean chromosomal position (“proximal position score”) was obtained by averaging the relative chromosomal positions of proximal pairs, excluding intra-chromosomal pairs, in accordance with the method used for the allele mixing score. Finally, all chromosomes were divided into 100 segments and the average proximal position score for each segment was compared with their chromosomal position. While Payne et al. [25] defined proximal pairs as the 100 nearest spatial neighbors in the 3D space, we defined them as pairs with distances shorter than the fifth percentile threshold of all pairwise distances (see Supplementary Fig. S2).

Calculation of the chromosome territory score

The chromosome territory score (CT score) was calculated as the proportion of proximal segments on the same chromosome out of all proximal segments. The average score for each cell was determined by averaging the overall CT score of all 1 Mb segments.

Correlation between gene density and radial CT positioning

Protein coding gene density per 1 Mb for each chromosome was calculated based on a comprehensive gene annotation GTF file (https://www.gencodegenes.org/mouse/release_M20.html). Average radial CT positioning for each chromosome at each cell stage was determined by averaging radial CT positioning of all 1 Mb segments of each chromosome.

Cell cycle phasing

We obtained the phases for the cell cycle by following Collombet et al. [48]. Namely, first, for each cell, we identified the proportion of short-range contacts which are between 25 kb and 2 Mb as well as the proportion of long-range contacts which are between 2 and 12 Mb on the same alleles of the same chromosomes. Then, for the set of these cells, we fitted an ellipsoid with the method of Pu et al. [56]. We applied the atan2 function by using the center of the ellipsoid as the origin for atan2 and obtained the phase for each cell. Lastly, we adjusted the phases so that the line from the center of the ellipsoid to (0.15,0.35) becomes the phase 0.

Results

Application of the RPR algorithm to scHi-C data of early-stage mouse embryos

We applied the RPR method to analyze scHi-C data from diploid cells of early-stage mouse embryos [1-cell stage (n = 75); 2-cell stage embryos (n = 109); 4-cell stage (n = 121); 8-cell stage (n = 125); and 64-cell stage (n = 201)] published by Collombet et al. (2020) [51]. The data were obtained from highly polymorphic F1-hybrid embryos, generated by crossing female Mus musculus domesticus (C57Bl/6J) with male Mus musculus castaneus (CAST/EiJ) (Collombet et al., NCBI GSE129029). The paternal and maternal alleles were distinguished by SNVs between these genetic backgrounds: 22.6% (1-cell), 24.4% (2-cell), 21.2% (4-cell), 23.7% (8-cell), and 22.9% (64-cell) of genomic reads were uniquely assigned to the maternal (C57Bl/6J) genome, whereas 14.6% (1-cell), 18.0% (2-cell), 16.7% (4-cell), 17.9% (8-cell), and 17.7% (64-cell) of genomic reads were uniquely assigned to the paternal (CAST/EiJ) genome (Supplementary Fig. S1b). For performing RPR, we divided the genome into 1 Mb segments and used contact information of genomic segment pairs in which both segments harbored at least one SNV. A summary of the RPR method is as follows (Fig. 1a) [52] First, we constructed a weighted network from a contact map obtained from a single diploid cell Hi-C dataset. In this network, each node corresponds to a segment of an allele-distinguished chromosome. We connected two nodes if there is a contact between these corresponding chromosomal segments both of which have their alleles identified with SNVs. We also connected two nodes if they are on the same allele of the same chromosome within 10 Mb. Let i and j denote a node in the network, respectively. Then, we assigned the weight for the edge between nodes i and j by using the portion of common nodes against the nodes which are connected with either i, j, or both (this definition coincides with that of the Jaccard coefficient [56]). More precisely, the weight is the portion of common nodes subtracted from 1. Then, this weight can be regarded as the local distance between two corresponding segments i and j. The latter parts are similar to Lesne et al. [57], Hirata et al. [53], and Tenenbaum et al. [58]: we calculated the shortest distances between every pair of nodes in the network, followed by applying the classical multidimensional scaling to find a set of point arrangements preserving the shortest distances so that the top three components become our 3D reconstruction of chromosomes. This reconstruction represents relative spatial organization derived from Hi-C contact frequencies rather than absolute physical distances, and therefore does not include scale bars. The strengths of our method are that (i) we can reconstruct local structures more finely by reconstructing the local distances by the Jaccard coefficient appropriately compared with that of Lesne et al. [57], (ii) we can apply the method with sparser data because the proportion of common nodes is a robust statistic under such conditions, and (iii) we can use the information of pairs of chromosome segments in the Hi-C data, in which at least one of the segments does not harbor a SNV and thus were not used for 3D reconstruction, to evaluate the accuracy of reconstruction.

In this study, we defined the inaccuracy score for each reconstructed point by using the Euclidean distance between each reconstructed point and the spatial average of its corresponding spatial neighbors (the contact pairs found in the Hi-C data) (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Here, the spatial neighbors were defined as 1 Mb segments containing a contact pair. The more accurate the reconstruction is, the closer the spatial neighbors are expected to be to the reconstructed point in the 3D genome atlas. With the RPR method, the center of the nucleus tends to be more accurate compared with the periphery (Supplementary Fig. S1c). The overall results indicated that the majority of reads, even those with no overlapping SNVs, were assigned to their predicted spatial locations through co-localization with haplotype-resolved reads in the same location. However, some cells had relatively high average inaccuracy scores (with greater SD; Supplementary Fig. S1d, e); these cells also tended to show irregularly shaped nuclei (for example, see Supplementary Fig. S1h, left). We thus excluded the cells with a high inaccuracy score (SD > 0.15) from the following analyses (Supplementary Fig. S1f). We found no significant correlation between the inaccuracy score and the number of contact pairs detected by Hi-C (Supplementary Fig. S1g), suggesting that the high inaccuracy score is not solely due to the few reads of Hi-C contact pairs.

Spatial intermixing of paternal and maternal genomes during early-stage development

At the 1-cell stage, the parental genomes are separate, each in a haploid pronucleus. The parental genomes mix after this stage, but the time and extent of this mixing have been somewhat controversial [25, 59]. A DNA-FISH analysis that separately visualized parental centromeres in mouse interspecific hybrids [as well as an analysis of sperm-specific bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) labeling] has shown that parental genomes are topologically separate until the 4-cell stage and then gradually mix [59]. By contrast, an IGS analysis has shown that parental genomes mix from 1- to 4-cell stages [58]. We thus investigated the extent of mixing of the parental genomes based on the RPR of the 3D genomic architecture. We observed the parental genomes to gradually mix from the 1- to 64-cell stages (Fig. 1b). To quantitively measure genome mixing, we first calculated the distance of all pairs of 1 Mb genomic segments. We then defined proximity pairs (two closely located genomic segments) as the top (nearest) 5% of all pairs (Supplementary Fig. S2). To focus on inter-chromosomal arrangements, we only used the information about those genomic segments that had at least one proximity pair with a genomic segment outside of its own chromosome. We then calculated the fraction of proximity pairs with genomic segments containing different parental alleles (“the intermixing score”) in each cell. This fraction was close to zero at the 1-cell stage (as expected) but gradually increased as the zygote developed to the 64-cell stage (Fig. 1b). The mean intermixing score significantly increased between 1- and 2-cell stages, 2- and 4-cell stages, 4- and 8-cell stages, and 8- and 64-cell stages (Mann–Whitney U-test with Bonferroni correction, P < 10−27, P < 10−24, P < 10−3, and P < 10−4) (Fig. 1c). Intermixing scores were comparable between male and female embryos at all stages (Supplementary Fig. S4a, b). This indicated that parental genome mixing starts at the 2-cell stage irrespective of sex, consistent with the result from IGS analysis. Moreover, our results quantitatively demonstrate that parental genomes still significantly mix after the 4-cell stage, as shown by the DNA-FISH analysis [59]. Given that the intermixing score became close to 0.5 at the 64-cell stage (Fig. 1c), chromosomes appear to be positioned irrespective of their parental alleles by this stage. Notably, intermixing scores in 64-cell embryos were comparable with those observed in postnatal neurons (Supplementary Fig. S6a), suggesting that the parental genomes attain a mixing level similar to that of differentiated somatic cells by the late pre-implantation stage. Interestingly, however, the extent of chromosome mixing varies among cells at all stages (see examples of 64-cell stage mixing, Fig. 1d), suggesting cell-to-cell variability of chromosome organization, which has previously been proposed [59].

Formation and dissolution of Rabl-like configuration during early-stage development

Mouse chromosomes are acrocentric. In an early-stage mouse embryo, chromosomes are organized into a Rabl-like, polarized configuration: centromeres and their nearby telomeres are confined to one part of the nucleus while the remaining telomeres cluster in the other part [25, 60–62]. The extent of this polarization at each developmental stage has been controversial. A 3D FISH analysis of centromeric, pericentromeric, and telomeric DNA sequences showed that polarity levels were rather constant from 2- to 32-cell stages [62]. However, an IGS analysis showed that polarity increased from 1- to 4-cell stages [25]. This discrepancy probably arises from the different definitions of polarity used in these studies. The 3D FISH study defined polarity based on the relative spatial positions of telomeres and centromeres within the entire nucleus, whereas the IGS analysis defined it at the chromosomal level along the centromere–telomere axis reconstructed from 3D genome data. In our analysis, we adopted the latter definition and quantified polarity along the centromere–distal telomere axis of each chromosome. We thus measured chromosomal polarity along the centromere–distal telomere axis at each stage in our analysis. After labeling the p-arm and q-arm termini of each chromosome in the reconstructed 3D map, the polarization of chromosomes was visible from the 2- to 8-cell stages (Fig. 2a). To quantitatively analyze the extent of polarization, we calculated the mean position along the chromosomal axis (the p-arm terminus = 0, the q-arm terminus = 1) of proximity pairs for each genomic segment (but excluded proximity pairs comprising genomic segments within the same chromosome, as shown in Fig. 1c). We found a greater correlation (between the position of each genomic segment along the chromosomal axis and the mean chromosomal position of its proximity pairs) in 2-, 4-, and 8-cell stage embryos than in zygotes and 64-cell stage embryos (Fig. 2b). Comparable polarity patterns were observed between male and female embryos at all stages (Supplementary Fig. S4c). This indicates that chromosomes become polarized in a Rabl-like configuration from zygote to 2-cell stages (as reported by Payne et al. [25]) but this configuration becomes mostly dissolved from 8- to 64-cell stages in a sex-independent manner. This dissolution appears to start between the 4- and 8-cell stages, because the angle of the correlation curve becomes shallower at these stages (Fig. 2b). Notably, the low polarity observed at the 64-cell stage was highly similar to that in post-mitotic neurons (Supplementary Fig. S6), suggesting that by this stage the embryo has largely transitioned into a non-polar chromosomal organization comparable with that of differentiated somatic cells.

Our RPR analysis of scHi-C data thus indicated that polarity increases from 1- to 4-cell stages (consistent with the IGS results), begins to decrease between the 4- and 8-cell stages, and then undergoes a marked reduction between the 8- and 64-cell stages, eventually reaching a polarity level similar to that of neurons.

Establishment of discrete chromosome territories during early-stage development

Within the nucleus, each chromosome is confined to a discrete, patch-like region called a CT [5–7]. The spatial organization of CTs is not random and is critical for gene regulation and genome stability [62]. It remains unclear, however, when discrete (patch-like) CTs emerge during early-stage development. We thus evaluated the extent of intra-chromosomal interactions (compared with that of inter-chromosomal interactions) at each stage by using the 3D genomic architecture based on the RPR method. We again defined proximal pairs as the top (nearest) 5% of all pairs and calculated the fraction of proximity pairs in which the genomic segments were on the same chromosome in each cell (“the CT score”). The CT score slightly increased from 1- to 8-cell stages and greatly increased from 8- to 64-cell stages, irrespective of sex (Fig. 3a, b; Supplementary Fig. S4d, e). This suggests that discrete CTs are largely established between the 8- and 64-cell stages. The increase of the CT score between the 8- and 64-cell stages was found in all chromosomes in both sexes but to a lesser extent for shorter chromosomes, especially for chromosome 19 (Fig. 3c), probably because of their already packed (non-elongated) configuration at the 8-cell stage.

We also measured the distance between the termini of each chromosome to assess overall chromosome shape. The average distance between the termini greatly increased from the 2- to 4-cell stages and decreased from the 4- to 64-cell stages, irrespective of sex (Fig. 4a, b; Supplementary Fig. S4f, g). Greater values of this score appeared to represent an extended (rod-like) shape of chromosomes, found particularly at the 4-cell stage (Fig. 4a), and the reduction of this score precedes the onset of discrete (patch-like, non-elongated) CTs from the 8- to 64-cell stages.

(b) Box plots showing the distance between the termini of each chromosome at different developmental stages. (c) Schematic summary of chromosomal architecture during early-stage development. Rabl-like polarization of chromosomes starts at the 2-cell stage, which appears to precede rod-like extension and parallel alignment of chromosomes around the 4-cell stage. This rod-like extension is then followed by intermixing of maternal and paternal chromosomes and formation of CTs especially at the 8-cell and 64-cell stage, respectively. Although any causal relationship between these events should be experimentally addressed in future studies, this sequence of events may imply the role of Rabl-like polarization in straightening of chromosomes and effective mixing of chromosomes as suggested in yeast [64, 65], which may facilitate the establishment of CTs by reducing entanglement.

In pre-implantation female mouse embryos, Xist RNA begins to be transcribed from the paternal allele at the 2-cell stage and progressively coats the paternal X chromosome, initiating imprinted paternal X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) [63]. To examine the structural consequences of this process, we quantified changes in the CT score and the distance between the termini of the paternal and maternal X chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. S5a, b). Whereas zygotes and 2-cell stage embryos showed no detectable differences between the two X chromosomes, consistent with the absence of XCI at these stages, the paternal X chromosome exhibited a higher territory score and a reduced end-to-end distance from the 4-cell stage onward. These changes became particularly pronounced by the 64-cell stage, coinciding with the completion of imprinted paternal XCI, and are indicative of substantial compaction of the paternal X chromosome.

Discussion

In contrast to bulk Hi-C which can only measure simultaneous contacts between two genomic regions and thus cannot provide information about global genomic organization, the RPR method for scHi-C data can theoretically identify simultaneous contacts among multiple genomic regions and can measure the distance between any genomic regions of interest (even between regions that do not directly contact each other). In this study, we applied the RPR method to construct 3D genomic atlases of early-stage embryos at the single-cell level, with a modification of incorporating the inaccuracy score for data validation, analyzed dynamic changes in global chromosomal architecture at this stage, and obtained results that appear to be relevant to the setup process for CT formation including the timing of Rabl-like polarization, rod-like extension, and parental allele mixing in relation to CT formation.

Chromosomes are organized into localized CTs that are essential for the higher-order nuclear architecture. It remained unclear, however, when and how CTs are formed at the early stage of embryos. We estimated the extent of CT formation (the CT score) in this study as the proportion of intra-chromosomal interactions, among all chromosomal interactions, between 1 Mb genomic segments as estimated by the RPR method. Our results revealed that discrete CTs are mostly established between 8- and 64-cell stages during early embryonic development. Importantly, this timing appears to be preceded by the dissolution of Rabl-like polarized configurations and rod-like chromosomal extension.

The biological meaning of Rabl-like polarized configuration has remained enigmatic. It has been proposed to be just a result of the anaphase orientation of chromosomes without particular cellular roles. Of note, although the Rabl-like polarized configuration (which involves separate clustering of telomere and centromere termini) starts to form at the 2-cell stage (Fig. 2), the shape of chromosomes starts to elongate only at the 4-cell stage (Fig. 4). In addition, the distal ends of chromosomes appeared to cluster to a similar extent with the proximal ends (Fig. 2), which may not happen if the Rabl-like configuration is a mere remnant of the anaphase orientation. Also, both Rabl-like polarization and rod-like extension of chromosomes disappear around the time of CT formation at the 64-cell stage. This sequence of events implies a possible role for Rabl-like polarization in rod-like extension and parallel alignment of chromosomes, which may in turn avoid entanglement and thus promote effective mixing of chromosomes, as proposed in yeast as “horsetail movement” [64, 65]. The resultant mixing of chromosomes, especially those from different parental origins, may be necessary for establishing proper CTs and therefore should be completed before discrete CTs start to be formed. In addition to mixing of chromosomes, dissolution of entanglement per se may also facilitate efficient CT formation. This makes sense because, if unwanted entanglement of chromosomes (inherited from parental gametes) persists, it would be an obstacle for the establishment of chromosome territories (with little spatial overlap between individual chromosomes).

Previous work has demonstrated that condensin II, especially its subunit Ncaph2, drives lengthwise chromosomal compaction during interphase, whereas loss of condensin II results in Rabl-like polarization [66]. Based on this insight, we re-analyzed an independent single-cell RNA-seq dataset (NCBI GSE80810) [66, 67] and found that condensin II subunits, especially Ncaph2, show progressively increased expression from the 2-cell to the 64-cell stages, in contrast to condensin I subunits (Supplementary Fig. S8). These observations suggest a potential molecular mechanism that may underlie the transient rod-like chromosomal extension, which gradually diminishes from the 4-cell to the 64-cell stages (Fig. 4a, b).

Our results do not still exclude the possibility that the Rabl-like polarized configuration found in the 2-, 4-, and 8-cell stages is a simple remnant of the anaphase orientation of chromosomes. For example, if the chromosomal movement during the interphase (including S phase) is arrested, particularly at the 4-cell stage compared with the 2- and 8-cell stages, this may explain a greater occurrence of rod-like structure at the 4-cell stage. Interestingly, a recent report demonstrated specific DNA replication patterns (a somatic cell-like replication timing program yet with a slow fork speed) in 4-cell stage embryos [67, 68]. Given that the subnuclear localization is generally associated with replication timing in somatic cells, some chromosomal features (dynamics and location) during the interphase of the 4-cell stage may underlie the unique chromosomal configuration found in this study.

Interestingly, we found that a fraction of 64-cell stage cells still showed Rabl-like polarization of chromosomes, highlighting cell-to-cell variation of chromosome configuration even among cells at the same developmental stage (Fig. 4a). We estimated the cell cycle phase of individual cells by calculating the ratios of local to mitotic contacts, following the method of Collombet et al. [67]. Our analysis suggested, however, that the cell cycle phase was not obviously associated with the observed heterogeneity in allele separation, CT scores, and chromosome end-to-end distances (Supplementary Fig. S7). The heterogeneity in these points may instead reflect the angle of spindle orientation in relation to that in the preceding mitosis, or cell type-specific characteristics.

Radial positioning of CTs is not random but depends on chromosomal size, GC content, gene density, and transcriptional activity (see refererences in Bolzer et al. [6] and Fritz et al. [69]). These factors affect CT positioning in a nuclear shape-dependent and cell type-dependent manner. For example, CT position correlates with gene density in cell types with flat ellipsoidal nuclei (such as fibroblasts) but not in those with spherical nuclei (such as lymphocytes) [70]. Consistent with these observations, we found no significant correlation between gene density and radial CT positioning in early-stage embryonic cells, which have spherical nuclei due to weak cell–cell interactions (Supplementary Fig. S3).

In summary, by analyzing global chromosomal architecture, we observed a sequence of events (Rabl-like polarization and rod-like extension) that may be an important preparatory step for effective chromosomal mixing and subsequent CT formation. In future studies, it would be of great interest to overlay other chromatin-related information onto the 3D genome architecture revealed by the RPR analysis at a single-cell level. The RPR analysis in this study was performed at a 1 Mb resolution. Analyses at a higher resolution would be necessary for identification of contacts between gene promoters and enhancers and their relationships to epigenetic information such as DNA and histone modifications as well as to topologically associating domains (TADs) and compartments.
