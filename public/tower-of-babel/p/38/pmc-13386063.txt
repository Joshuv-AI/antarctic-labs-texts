Validation of a web-based clinical tool for predicting the risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia in extremely preterm infants: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Alaa Abuzaid, Shadi Noaman Garrada, Hebah Salem Abdullah Alsaihati, Zainab Alali, Fatimah Arif Alabbad, Jemila James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to critically evaluate the web-based National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) outcome estimator (2022) regarding its models on postnatal days 1, 3, 7, 14 and 28 in predicting grade 2 and grade 3 BPD as well as the combined outcome of death or grade 2 to grade 3 BPD at 36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA) or discharge if earlier.MethodsThis single-centred retrospective cohort study included neonates with birth weights of 501–1250 g and born between 23+0 and 28+6 weeks of gestational age. We used the C-statistic receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (AUC) to predict the predetermined outcomes of grade 2 and grade 3 BPD as well as the composite outcome of death or progression from grade 2 to grade 3 BPD at 36 weeks PMA or discharge, whichever came first.ResultsThe AUC values were 0.71 on postnatal day 7 and 0.76 on postnatal day 14, indicating that the prediction accuracy for the composite outcome of death or grade 2–3 BPD at 36 weeks PMA on days 7 and 14 has limited clinical utility.ConclusionThe NICHD web-based Neonatal BPD Outcome Estimator (2022) has moderate accuracy as evidenced by the AUCs of 0.76 on postnatal day 14 in predicting the outcome of grade 2–3 BPD and the composite outcome of death or grade 2–3 BPD at 36 weeks PMA or discharge if earlier.

Introduction

Modern perinatal and neonatal intensive care advances have improved the survival of extremely low gestational age neonates (ELGANs). However, the rates of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) have remained the same or even increased with increasing survival of ELGANs. Preterm infants with BPD are more likely to die during early childhood or survive with severe developmental disability. The presence of an outcome estimator calculator that could accurately predict the BPD outcome at 36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA) helps clinicians tailor the treatment approaches and the preventive strategies.

The first web-based prediction model for BPD outcome was developed by Laughon et al in 2011 and included a cohort of infants of 23–30 weeks’ gestation born in 17 centres of the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) Neonatal Research Network (NRN) trial from 2000 to 2004. Their prediction for BPD outcome improved with advancing postnatal age, increasing from a C statistic of 0.793 on day 1 to a maximum of 0.854 on day 28. On postnatal days 1 and 3, gestational age best improved the outcome prediction; on postnatal days 7, 14, 21 and 28, the type of respiratory support did so.1

Jensen et al, in 2019, revised the definition of BPD and conducted a multicentre study that included infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestation between 2011 and 2015 at 18 centres of the Eunice Kennedy Shriver NICHD NRN. The study concluded that the mode of respiratory support administered at 36 weeks’ PMA best predicted the BPD severity regardless of the supplemental oxygen use.2

Later, in 2022, Greenberg et al conducted a multicentre study that included a cohort of infants born between 2011 and 2017 and developed an updated web-based BPD outcome estimator. This estimator estimates the neonate’s risk of developing the new outcome-driven definition of BPD or death on postnatal days 1 (day of birth), 3, 7, 14, 21 and 28, respectively.3

As the survival of infants born at lower gestation has increased, it becomes essential to recognise BPD early so that preventive interventions can be targeted at high-risk infants, particularly for grade 2 and grade 3 BPD. The currently available predictive models are developed to estimate the risk and severity of BPD and provide prognostic information using a limited amount of readily available clinical information.

The primary objective of this study was to estimate the incidence of BPD in the study population and to validate the predictive accuracy of the web-based NICHD BPD Outcome Estimator (2022) across postnatal days 1, 3, 7, 14 and 28 for the composite outcome of death or grade 2–3 BPD at 36 weeks’ PMA. Additionally, the predictive performance of the estimator was evaluated separately for grade 2–3 BPD, with outcomes assessed at 36 weeks’ PMA or at discharge if earlier. The study further sought to determine which postnatal day model provides the best prediction for BPD outcomes. Finally, it aimed to identify strong predictors of BPD and assess the contribution of risk factors included in the updated NICHD BPD Outcome Estimator (2022).

Methods

Study design and setting

This observational study was conducted as a single-centre retrospective cohort study to assess the predictive accuracy of the NICHD web-based Neonatal BPD Outcome Estimator (2022) and the incidence and risk factors for BPD in neonates with birth weights of 501–1250 g and born between 23+0 and 28+6 weeks gestational age, enrolled in a level III neonatal intensive care unit, a major tertiary care hospital in the Eastern province of Saudi Arabia from February 2021 to January 2024. Neonates with life-threatening congenital anomalies who died within 24 hours of birth, neonates transferred to other higher centres before reaching 36 weeks PMA, and those with missing data were excluded.

Definitions

The study used the outcome-driven diagnostic criteria used by the NRN investigators to define the severity of BPD at 36 weeks PMA.2 This definition categorises disease severity according to the mode of respiratory support used at 36 weeks’ PMA, regardless of the use of supplemental oxygen. No BPD was defined as breathing in room air at 36 weeks’ PMA; grade 1 BPD as receipt of nasal cannula ≤2 L/min (or hood O2); grade 2 BPD as nasal cannula >2 L/min, nasal continuous positive airway pressure or nasal intermittent positive pressure ventilation; and grade 3 BPD as invasive mechanical ventilation. For infants discharged home before 36 weeks’ PMA, respiratory status at discharge was used to determine the severity of BPD.

Surgical necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) was defined as modified Bell’s stage III B. Sepsis was defined as a blood and/or cerebrospinal fluid culture growing a recognised bacterial or fungal pathogen if the infant was administered antibiotics for ≥5 days or until death. Patent ductus arteriosus was defined as haemodynamically significant (hs-PDA) that requires pharmacological treatment. Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is defined as any stage that requires interventions, either laser or intravitreal bevacizumab.

Statistical analysis

A non-probability sampling method was employed during the study period. Data from the enrolled neonates were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records and recorded in an MS Excel worksheet for analysis. The key details included maternal and neonatal characteristics, as shown in table 1. Gestational age was defined by the best obstetric estimate of completed weeks, based on either the last menstrual period or first-trimester ultrasound.

The data were entered into the NICHD web-based Neonatal BPD Outcome Estimator (2022), where details of gestation age, birth weight, gender, type of respiratory support, fraction of inspired oxygen concentration (FiO2) in per cent, use of antenatal steroids and surgical NEC on the pre-specified postnatal days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21 and 28. The probability for individual outcomes of death, severity of BPD (grade 3, grade 2, grade 1) and no BPD was provided as an output on the prespecified days by the estimator and was noted as a percentage. The estimated risk of grade 2—grade 3 BPD and the composite outcome of death or grade 2—grade 3 BPD were then compared with the neonate’s actual outcome at 36 weeks of PMA or discharge if earlier. Details of other neonatal morbidities included haemodynamically significant PDA, surfactant requirement, pulmonary haemorrhage, pulmonary interstitial emphysema (PIE), use of diuretics (thiazide and spironolactone), Pulmicort nebulisation, inhaled nitric oxide, inotropic support, Apgar score at 1 min and 5 min, intraventricular haemorrhage, periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), culture-proven sepsis, cholestasis, ROP and acute tubular necrosis.

All data entered in an MS Excel worksheet were analysed using IBM SPSS software V.20.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Categorical data were represented as frequency and percentage, while continuous data were depicted as the mean and SD for normally distributed data or as median and first and third quartiles for skewed data. χ2 or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the predicted risk and actual outcomes. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The accuracy of the NICHD web-based Neonatal BPD Outcome Estimator (2022) was assessed using a C-statistic receiver operator characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) in estimating the probability of the predetermined outcomes. Univariate analysis was performed to assess the risk factors, and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent significant risk factors associated with an adverse BPD outcome at 36 weeks PMA, and the OR with 95% CI were reported. The analysis performed was two-tailed with significance set at p value <0.05.

Patient and public involvement

No patient was involved in the study.

Discussion

The global incidence range of BPD was studied in a large systematic review by Siffel et al,4 which showed 10%–73% in Europe, 18%–89% in North America, 18%–82% in Asia and 30%–62% in Oceania, which defined BPD as requiring supplemental oxygen at 36 weeks PMA. The overall incidence of BPD in developed nations has been reported to range from 20% to 75%, which is considerably higher than reports from developing nations.4 This highlights the wide variation of BPD incidence between countries, with an increased incidence associated with improved survival rates. This is probably due to the higher survival of smaller neonates and advanced neonatal care in developed countries. In addition, this wide range of incidences reflected interstudy differences in gestational age, which was inversely related to BPD incidence, birth weight and survival rates across populations and institutions.

The overall incidence of BPD among extremely preterm neonates born between 23+0 and 28+6 weeks of gestation and weighing less than 1250 g was 27%. Of these, 10.8% developed grade 1 BPD, 9.6% developed grade 2 BPD and 6.6% developed grade 3 BPD. The combined incidence of moderate-to-severe BPD (grades 2 and 3) was 16.2%, with an associated mortality rate of 37.8%. In contrast, Geetha et al.,5 in a study conducted at Singapore’s largest tertiary perinatal referral centre, reported a considerably higher incidence of moderate-to-severe BPD (67%) despite a lower overall mortality rate (19%). These differences may reflect variations in neonatal populations, perinatal care practices, survival of extremely preterm infants and BPD classification criteria across study settings.

In this study cohort, the relatively high mortality rate observed could be attributed to the extreme vulnerability of the study population, which included very preterm neonates (23+0–28+6 weeks) with very low birth weights (mean 853 g). Lower gestational age and birth weight are well-established predictors of mortality. In addition, there was a high burden of neonatal comorbidities, including significant rates of: haemodynamically significant PDA (63.5%), culture-proven sepsis (48.5%), severe intraventricular haemorrhage (18.7%), pulmonary complications such as pulmonary haemorrhage (20.7%), pneumothorax (11.6%) and PIE (11.6%). These conditions are strongly associated with increased mortality in preterm infants. The need for intensive respiratory and cardiovascular support—evidenced by high rates of inotropic support (59.8%), re-intubation (40.2%) and inhaled nitric oxide use (32%)—further reflects the severity of illness in this cohort. Additionally, the high incidence of severe BPD (grade 3) and its overlap with mortality (combined outcome 44.4%) suggests that many infants had progressive pulmonary disease, which is a known contributor to late neonatal death.

The risk factors associated with BPD are extensive and vary based on population and healthcare delivery systems. Geetha et al5 identified sepsis, treatment-requiring PDA, air leaks, FiO₂ requirement >25% and mechanical ventilation on day 7 as significant risk factors for adverse outcomes. However, after adjustment for confounding variables, only the need for mechanical ventilation on day 7 remained independently predictive of the composite adverse outcome (OR 1.97; 95% CI 1.3 to 3.1).

Similarly, in this study the authors found a significantly increased risk of BPD in lower gestational age, the presence of PVL, pulmonary haemorrhage and the need for inhaled nitric oxide therapy. Additionally, other significant predictors identified were very low birth weight, low Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min (indicating resuscitation at birth), haemodynamically significant PDA, use of inotropic support, PIE, surgical NEC (stage 111), culture-proven sepsis, cholestasis and acute tubular necrosis. Conversely, antenatal steroids were found to offer protective benefits against the development of BPD.

In our study, PVL was significantly associated with BPD in both univariate (p=0.025) and multivariate (p=0.020) analyses, indicating an independent association. This finding is biologically plausible, as both PVL and BPD are complications of extreme prematurity and share common pathogenic mechanisms including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, infection and prolonged exposure to intensive care interventions. The association may reflect the severity of prematurity-related injury and illness rather than a direct causal relationship between PVL and BPD.

The high ORs observed for hs-PDA and PVL indicate that these variables were independently associated with increased odds of BPD after adjusting for other covariates in the multivariable model.

The lower OR for gestational age (OR<1) suggests that increasing gestational age is protective against BPD, with higher gestational age associated with reduced odds of the outcome.

In contrast, although PIE is biologically expected to increase the risk of BPD, the observed OR was 0.375. However, this association was not statistically significant (95% CI 0.09 to 1.41, p=0.148), and therefore should not be interpreted as a protective effect. The finding may reflect limited sample size, the wide CI indicating imprecision or collinearity with other respiratory variables included in the model.

Most existing clinical prediction models have an AUC ranging from 0.50 to 0.76 and are poor to moderate predictors of BPD. Despite the increase in BPD prediction models published over the last decade, most of the prediction models are not used in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, the calibration of the models is often not appropriately evaluated in most models. These prediction models should be externally validated with a subsequent impact analysis before being adopted in clinical practice.610

In the present study, the authors conducted an external validation on the performance of the existing web-based NICHD BPD risk estimator and found moderate accuracy in predicting the outcome of grade 2–3 BPD and in the composite outcome of death and/or grade 2–3 BPD on postnatal day 14 of life as evidenced by the AUCs of 0.76, with a decrease in AUC<0.76 before and thereafter. On the contrary, similar validation was done by Baker and Davis in an Australian study, which included 187 neonates born between 23 and 28 weeks with a birth weight of 500–1250 g, with a substantial proportion being of Asian descent, and found that the NICHD BPD risk estimator accurately distinguished the neonates who died before 36 weeks or had severe BPD from those who did not.11

The NICHD online BPD risk estimator is limited by the options to include the advanced non-invasive modes of respiratory support, ethnicity and in addition, they lack the options to include the strong predictors of BPD as shown in this study, like the presence of PVL, pulmonary haemorrhage, the need for inhaled nitric oxide therapy and haemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus. The limitation includes the study being carried out in a single centre, and the relatively mature cohort (mean gestational age of 26 weeks) may limit generalisability, implying the need for a larger multicentre study in the Middle East region to validate the results more robustly.

The authors concluded that the NICHD web-based Neonatal BPD Outcome Estimator (2022) has moderate accuracy as evidenced by the AUCs of 0.76, on postnatal day 14, in predicting the outcome of grade 2–3 BPD and the composite outcome of death or grade 2–3 BPD at 36 weeks PMA or discharge if earlier. This external validation is crucial, as respiratory care in very preterm infants continues to evolve as goal oxygen saturation targets, ventilator management strategies and medication use change over time. Also, the authors suggest that to improve the predictive accuracy, additional significant risk factors that influence the outcome of BPD should be incorporated in future prediction models. This helps clinicians identify neonates at increased risk of BPD to tailor their therapeutic approaches and implement evidence-based preventive strategies early in the neonatal period.
