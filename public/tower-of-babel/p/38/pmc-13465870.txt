Relationship between norepinephrine dose and outcome in septic shock: a retrospective study
Authors: Lorenzo Antonino Calabrò, Marco Pasetto, Stefano Zorzi, Martina Polato, Eva Vitali, Chiara Faso, Sofia Castellani, Hassane Njimi, Katia Donadello, Leonardo Gottin, Filippo Annoni, Fabio Silvio Taccone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-dose norepinephrine (NE) is frequently required in septic shock, but the association of NE dose with outcome remains incompletely characterised. We aimed to evaluate the relationship between NE dosing, mortality and ischemic complications, and to identify clinically relevant NE dose ranges.MethodsThis retrospective single-centre cohort study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) between 2016 and 2022 with septic shock on admission, complete data on NE dosing and ICU mortality; patients receiving mechanical circulatory support were excluded. NE dosing variables (initial-dose, highest dose within 24 h, and peak ICU-dose) were collected. Adjusted optimal binning was used to identify NE peak-dose ranges associated with ICU and in-hospital mortality. Multivariable logistic regression was adjusted for age, APACHE II, and SOFA score, and time-dependent Cox models assessed associations with secondary ischemic outcomes.ResultsA total of 506 patients were included. Median NE peak-dose was 0.5 [0.2–1.2] µg/kg*min. Four NE peak-dose ranges were identified: 0.05–0.6, 0.61–1.2, 1.2–3.0, and > 3.0 µg/kg*min. ICU and in-hospital mortality increased stepwise across these ranges, from 20.7 and 32.4% in the lowest group to 100% in patients receiving > 3.0 µg/kg*min. This association persisted after exclusion of patients with withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies. Adjusted odds ratios for ICU mortality were 1.88 [0.97–3.64] and 2.55 [1.38–5.57] in the 0.61–1.2 and 1.2–3.0 µg/kg*min groups, respectively. NE peak-dose showed fair discrimination for ICU mortality (AUROC of 0.75), with an optimal Youden threshold of 0.78 µg/kg*min. Most ischemic complications were infrequent and not dose dependent, except for myocardial ischemia and unexpected cardiac arrest, which were significantly associated with higher NE doses.ConclusionsIn septic shock, increasing NE peak-doses are associated with a significant stepwise increase in ICU and in-hospital mortality across four distinct dose ranges. Ischemic complications are uncommon and largely independent of NE dose.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44158-026-00425-4.

Background

Septic shock is a leading cause of intensive care unit (ICU)-admissions and a major contributor to mortality and morbidity worldwide. Despite advances in critical care, mortality rates remain high, with recent data from Europe and North America varying between 30 and 50%, depending on patient severity and regional differences [1]. Septic shock is characterised by profound vasodilation and impaired tissue perfusion, driven by a dysregulated host response to infection, necessitating aggressive hemodynamic support to restore cardiovascular stability and organ function [2].

Current management guidelines emphasise the importance of early fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy to achieve and maintain adequate mean arterial pressure and tissue perfusion. Norepinephrine (NE) is widely regarded as the first-line vasopressor in septic shock, due to its potent vasoconstrictive properties mediated through α-adrenergic receptors and its modest β-adrenergic activity, which supports cardiac output [3]. While norepinephrine is effective in reversing hypotension, its use at high doses has been associated with worse clinical outcomes, including arrhythmias, stress cardiomyopathy, peripheral ischemia and immunosuppression [4, 5]. However, it remains unclear whether high norepinephrine doses directly contribute to mortality due to these adverse events or merely reflect greater disease severity.

Defining what constitutes a “high dose” of NE remains a subject of debate. The literature defines high-dose variably, with thresholds ranging from 0.5 to 2 µg/kg*min [6, 7]. This lack of unanimity reflects differences in patient populations, disease severity, and institutional practices and is compounded by the arbitrary nature of some cut-off definitions. These inconsistencies make it challenging to generalise findings and establish universally accepted thresholds, complicating the interpretation of existing evidence and highlighting the need for a clearer understanding of how dosing thresholds impact outcomes. Furthermore, NE doses are reported variously in the literature, rarely specifying the conjugated NE salt, thereby making it hard to compare and interpret the available results [8].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between increasing NE requirements and hospital mortality in patients with septic shock, while accounting for potential confounding factors. We hypothesised that higher NE dose ranges would be associated with progressively greater risks of death. We also sought to identify clinically meaningful NE dose thresholds associated with a significant increase in adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort, single-centre study including adult (> 18 years of age) patients consecutively admitted to the general ICU at Hôpital Universitaire de Bruxelles (HUB) in Brussels, Belgium, for septic shock between January 2016 and December 2022. Inclusion criteria were the presence of septic shock, according to standard criteria at ICU-admission [9], complete available data on dose of NE, hospital length of stay, and mortality at ICU discharge. Patients undergoing mechanical circulatory support (MCS) were excluded, because MCS significantly influences systemic perfusion, which becomes partially device-dependent, and also modifies vascular physiology (i.e. cardiac output, pulsatility, ventricular loading conditions and microcirculation). Institutional practices for the management of septic shock are described in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The study protocol was approved by the local Ethical Committee (P2023-332), which waived the need for an informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection

Demographics, clinical, hemodynamic, and laboratory data were collected on admission, as well as severity scores, including the Acute Physiologic Assessment and Chronic Health Evaluation Scoring System II (APACHE II) [10] and the Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) [11] scores. Vasopressors were initiated at ICU-admission, or during surgery for patients admitted after surgery. For each patient, the initial-dose of NE, the highest dose of NE during the first 24-h from the onset of septic shock and the peak-dose during the entire observational period were collected. The peak-dose and the highest 24-h dose were recorded if administered for at least 1 h. Additionally, time from NE initiation to peak-dose (time to peak), from peak-dose to a 50% dose reduction (peak-to-halving time), and from peak-dose to complete discontinuation (peak-to-weaning time) were recorded. Prescription of inotropes, intravenous hydrocortisone and a second-line vasopressors were also recorded. All NE doses are reported as base formulation equivalents for standardisation purposes, although the NE used in our ICU corresponds to the tartrate formulation (Laboratoire Aguettant, Lyon, France).

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was to identify NE dose cut-offs that are statistically associated with an increased risk of mortality in patients with septic shock, adjusted for age, APACHE II and SOFA score at patient admission. Secondary outcomes include the relationship between the NE peak-dose and the development of different ischemic complications (i.e. cutaneous or soft tissue ischemia; intestinal ischemia confirmed by contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scan; acute myocardial ischemia, defined according to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines [12]; new-onset atrial fibrillation in patients without a prior history of chronic atrial fibrillation, identified by continuous ECG monitoring; unexpected cardiac arrest, defined as a cardiac arrest occurring outside the context of withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy). Data collectors performed manual revision of ICU and hospital electronic records. Anonymised data (including demographic, clinical, microbiological, biochemical, ICU and hospital outcomes) were entered into a password-protected database.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 28 for Windows (IBM Corporation, Somers, NY). Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to verify the normality of continuous variables distribution. Categorical variables are presented as number (n) and percentage (%). Continuous variables are expressed as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR). Groups were compared using t Student test, Mann–Whitney U test, paired t test, chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Optimal binning [13–15], a method used to select a binning that maximises predictive information for a target outcome, was employed to identify NE levels based on significant changes in ICU mortality percentages. This approach was applied to the full dataset. Odds ratios were adjusted for age, APACHE II and SOFA score at hospital admission. Logistic regression was used to estimate the risk of death in intensive care and in hospital in terms of the identified NE levels. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs, using variables being associated with mortality in the univariate analysis) along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were hence calculated. Area under the receiver operating characteristics (AUROC) curve was computed and the optimal value was determined using the Youden method. All tests were two-tailed, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We performed time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the association between NE peak-dose and secondary outcomes, including peripheral ischemia, peripheral ischemia persisting after shock resolution, intestinal ischemia, myocardial ischemia, and cardiac arrest. NE dose was included as a continuous time-dependent variable, dividing each patient’s follow-up into pre- and post-peak intervals. Death and discharge were treated as a censoring event. All models were adjusted for age and SOFA score at ICU-admission, and robust standard errors were used to account for repeated intervals.

Potential sources of bias, including selection and information bias, were considered and minimised by consecutive patient inclusion, standardised data extraction from electronic medical records, and multivariable adjustment for disease severity. Residual confounding and treatment decision bias (e.g. withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy, WLST) could not be completely excluded. Missing values were assessed for all variables before analysis. For descriptive and univariate analyses, available-case data were used. For multivariable logistic regression models, a complete-case approach was primarily applied, as the overall rate of missingness for key variables was below 10%.

Results

Study population

Between 2016 and 2022, 541 patients were admitted to ICU with septic shock, 35 were excluded due to incomplete records, 506 were included in the analysis (Fig. S1). The median age was 66 [55–75] years, and 331 (65.4%) were male. The most frequent comorbidities were arterial hypertension (n = 273, 54.0%), diabetes mellitus (n = 182, 36.0%) and oncologic disease (n = 184, 36.4%). On admission, median APACHE II and SOFA scores were 24 [19–30] and 10 [8–13], respectively, with initial lactate levels of 3.6 [2.1–6.4] mmol/L. The main source of sepsis was abdominal in 234 (46.2%) patients, followed by pulmonary in 130 (25.7%) patients and urological in 54 (10.7%) patients (Table 1). The median time to antibiotic start from sepsis onset was 1 [0–3] hours, 101 (20.0%) patients received antibiotic prior to ICU admission. Norepinephrine and haemodynamic support

The median NE initial-dose was 0.2 [0.1–0.5] µg/kg*min, the median NE highest dose at 24 h was 0.4 [0.2–0.9] µg/kg*min, the median NE peak-dose was 0.5 [0.2–1.2] µg/kg*min, reached after a median of 8 [2–18] hours since the introduction of NE. The median time to NE halving from peak was 7 [3–14] hours, while the median time to NE weaning from peak was 46 [27–80] hours. An additional vasopressor was used in 29 (5.7%) patients (n = 23 vasopressin, n = 6 terlipressin). Inotropes were used in 119 (23.5%) patients (n = 93 dobutamine, n = 29 epinephrine), while hydrocortisone was administered in 208 (41.1%) patients (Table 2). There was a significant association between year of inclusion and in-hospital mortality (p = 0.03) with a trend toward lower mortality over time (coefficient − 0.015; p = 0.02). The median length of stay in ICU was 6 [3–11] days, while the median length of stay in hospital was 21 [10–45] days. In ICU non-survivors, the NE initial dose and the NE peak-dose were significantly higher, while the NE time to peak and the time from NE peak to NE weaning were longer when compared to survivors, in unadjusted univariate analysis. NE highest dose at 24 h and NE peak-dose were associated with ICU and in-hospital mortality at univariate analysis (p < 0.001), as well as age, APACHE II and SOFA scores, history of atrial fibrillation, liver cirrhosis and diabetes. Adjusted optimal binning procedure allowed identification of four patient groups with statistically significant difference in risk of death according to NE dose (Figs. 1. and 2 and ESM). In the multivariable analysis, adjusted odds ratios for ICU mortality in the 0.61 and 1.2 µg/kg*min and the 1.2 and 3 µg/kg*min groups were 1.88 [0.97–3.64] and 2.55 [1.38–5.57], compared to the lower ranges group, used as reference. Similar results were obtained for in-hospital mortality (9.43 [4.06–21.90] and 6.21 [2.70–14.30], respectively). The optimal Youden cut-off value to predict ICU mortality was 0.78 [0.7–0.79] µg/kg*min, with a sensitivity of 60.1% and a specificity of 75.9%, with AUROC discrimination performance of 0.75 [0.70–0.79] (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Area under the receiver operating characteristics (AUROC) curve of norepinephrine peak-dose (µg/kg*min) to predict ICU mortality

Secondary outcomes

Peripheral ischemia occurred in 21 patients (4.2%) at a median of 3 days (IQR 2–6) after shock onset and was not associated with peak NE dose (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.91–1.24; p = 0.42), including cases persisting after shock resolution in 14 patients (2.8%) occurring at 4 days (IQR 3–7) (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.79–1.28; p = 0.92). Intestinal ischemia occurred in 12 patients (2.4%) at 5 days (IQR 3–6) and was not associated with peak NE dose (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.94–1.29; p = 0.22). Myocardial ischemia occurred in 5 patients (1.0%) early after shock onset (median 1 [1–3] days) and was associated with higher peak NE dose (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.02–1.54; p = 0.04). New-onset atrial fibrillation occurred in 79 patients (15.6%) after a median of 2 (1–4) days and was not associated with peak NE dose (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.75–1.22; p = 0.73). Unexpected cardiac arrest occurred in 14 patients (2.8%) after a median of 3 (2–8) days and was strongly associated with higher peak NE dose (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.15–1.51; p < 0.01—Table 4). Discussion

In this large single-centre cohort of patients with septic shock, NE exposure was strongly associated with ICU and in-hospital mortality, with a clear gradient of risk across four different NE peak-dose ranges, which were identified using an adjusted optimal binning approach. ICU and in-hospital mortality rose from 20.7 and 32.4% in the lowest dose range to 39.1% and 45.5% in the intermediate range, and further to 59.0% and 64.0% in the higher range, reaching 100% in patients requiring peak NE doses above 3.0 µg/kg*min. Similar NE dose cut-offs were identified in the subgroup analyses according to the source of sepsis and after excluding patients with WLST. In addition, NE peak dose demonstrated a fair discriminatory performance for ICU mortality, with an AUROC of 0.75 and an optimal Youden threshold of 0.78 µg/kg*min. Both time to peak NE dose and duration of vasopressor therapy were significantly longer in non-survivors. Secondary ischemic complications were infrequent, with myocardial ischemia and unexpected cardiac arrest being the only events significantly associated with higher NE doses.

Increasing NE requirements and mortality in septic shock is biologically plausible and has been previously reported; however, the definition of high-dose NE varies widely across the published studies, largely due to differences in patient cohort severity, NE formulations, and resuscitation practices across institutions [16, 17]. In several previous studies, an NE dose of 1 µg/kg*min was selected a priori to define high-dose exposure, and mortality was subsequently compared between patients above and below this threshold, with reported mortality rates of 80–90% in patients exceeding such threshold. While pragmatic, this approach relies on an arbitrary cut-off that was not derived from outcome-driven analyses within the studied cohorts [6, 18, 19]. Other studies have reported 28-day mortality rates of 60–65% in patients receiving high-dose norepinephrine support [7, 20]; however, in a large multicentre cohort, the threshold used to define high-dose NE was relatively low, at 0.3 µg/kg*min [21]. Sato et al. associated higher NE doses within the first 24 h with increased in-hospital mortality [22], whereas Auchet et al. evaluated outcomes using a predefined high-dose threshold (≥ 1 µg/kg*min for ≥ 1 h) [7]. However, neither study explored vasopressor dose kinetics, related complications, or the impact of NE formulation, despite evidence that formulation may significantly influence the interpretation of “high-dose” therapy [16]. Two studies also reported that the cumulative NE dose during septic shock was not independently associated with mortality [7, 23], suggesting that vasopressor kinetics may be more clinically relevant than the absolute dose administered.

While our findings are consistent with previous reports, the present study provides additional clinically relevant information by evaluating several dynamic NE-related variables, including initial dose, highest dose at 24 h, peak-dose, time to peak, time to dose halving, and time to vasopressor weaning in a contemporary cohort of septic shock patients. Adjustment for major confounders, including APACHE II and SOFA scores, lactate levels, comorbidities, and relevant clinical variables, allowed a more balanced assessment of the association between NE exposure and mortality risk. Although NE dose should not be interpreted as a direct cause of death, escalating vasopressor requirements may represent a simple and dynamic bedside marker of persistent circulatory failure not fully captured by admission severity scores. Norepinephrine remains the cornerstone vasopressor therapy in septic shock; however, the prognostic interpretation of increasing dose requirements remains insufficiently standardised. Our results therefore provide pragmatic and clinically relevant data that should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant further validation in larger multicentre studies.

Although norepinephrine (NE) is recommended as the first-line vasopressor in septic shock, the literature remains heterogeneous regarding both the optimal way to quantify NE exposure and the definition of “high-dose” or “refractory” shock. Current Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines suggest considering vasopressin when NE requirements reach approximately 0.25–0.5 µg/kg*min [2]; however, these recommendations are based on limited evidence and do not establish validated prognostic thresholds for mortality or ischemic complications. More recently, refractory shock has been proposed to include patients requiring NE doses > 0.5 µg/kg*min [24], although this definition largely relies on expert consensus rather than robust clinical outcome data. While the relationship between NE dose and mortality is likely continuous rather than categorical, dose thresholds may still help identify clinically meaningful differences in patient risk profiles. In this context, the NE dose ranges identified in our study were not arbitrarily selected, but derived from an outcome-driven analysis designed to maximise discrimination between groups with distinct mortality risks. The threshold identified around 0.78 µg/kg*min should therefore not be interpreted as a therapeutic target or definitive cut-off, but rather as an exploratory prognostic signal. Clinically, it may help identify a transition from conventional septic shock to a phenotype of refractory circulatory failure, in which additional interventions, such as vasopressin, corticosteroids, advanced hemodynamic assessment, reassessment of source control, or discussions regarding proportionality of care, may become increasingly relevant.

In our cohort, complications traditionally attributed to norepinephrine (NE) exposure, including peripheral and acute intestinal ischemia, were relatively infrequent and were not clearly associated with increasing NE doses. New-onset atrial fibrillation occurred in 15.6% of patients, but was neither dose-dependent nor significantly associated with mortality. In contrast, unexpected cardiac arrest and myocardial ischemia, observed in 2.8% and 1.0% of patients respectively, were significantly associated with higher peak NE doses. Overall, these findings are broadly consistent with those reported by Anantasit et al. [4], who described slightly lower rates of vasopressor-associated complications in a comparable septic shock population. Importantly, the analysis of complications should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively low number of events. In addition, the true incidence of NE-associated adverse events may be underestimated, particularly for transient or less severe complications such as reversible peripheral ischemia, which are frequently underreported in retrospective observational studies. Our findings also suggest that ischemic and cardiovascular complications alone are unlikely to fully explain the marked increase in mortality observed at higher NE doses. Moreover, differences in adjunctive vasopressor strategies were unlikely to significantly affect our findings, as randomised trials evaluating early initiation of second-line vasopressors, despite reducing NE requirements, failed to demonstrate a survival benefit [25, 26]. Indeed, complications occurring during septic shock are likely multifactorial and should not be interpreted as direct consequences of vasoconstriction alone. Microvascular dysfunction, endothelial injury, coagulopathy, persistent tissue hypoperfusion, mitochondrial dysfunction, and underlying cardiovascular vulnerability probably all contribute to the development of ischemic complications in these patients [27].

This study has several limitations. Its single-centre design may limit generalisability to institutions with different resuscitation protocols, although it also ensured greater consistency in treatment strategies and strengthened internal validity. Moreover, the identified NE dose thresholds could be influenced by local practices regarding fluid resuscitation, vasopressor titration, timing of adjunctive therapies, and overall compliance with sepsis management bundles. As such, these thresholds may not be directly reproducible across institutions with different hemodynamic management strategies. The retrospective nature of the study may have led to underreporting of mild or transient complications, while major events are likely to have been reliably captured. Mortality patterns during the COVID-19 period may have influenced outcomes, although the overall temporal trend toward decreasing mortality remained unchanged. An important limitation of this study is that mortality in refractory septic shock may reflect both biological severity and real-world clinical decision-making, particularly regarding WLST, which occurred across all NE dose categories and may have influenced threshold derivation. Consequently, the identified NE dose thresholds should be interpreted as data-driven categories integrating disease severity and clinical practice rather than strict pharmacological cutoffs. Time from shock onset to vasopressor initiation was not systematically recorded, representing a potential source of bias, although early vasopressor administration is routinely implemented in our institution. Also, although the multivariable model was intentionally based on global severity indices to preserve parsimony and reduce overfitting, residual confounding may persist, particularly regarding comorbidities and treatment-related variables that could both reflect shock severity and influence outcome. Another methodological limitation relates to the use of an optimal binning strategy to derive NE dose categories, which was performed within the same cohort used for outcome analysis and may therefore introduce optimism bias and limit generalisability. As no cross-validation or bootstrap resampling was performed, the identified thresholds should be considered exploratory and cohort-specific, requiring external validation before clinical implementation. Finally, most patients receiving NE doses > 3.0 µg/kg*min died following WLST, which may introduce treatment-decision bias, although sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary results. Moreover, the rate of withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy was fairly distributed across all four NE dose ranges. All NE doses were reported as base formulation equivalents, limiting direct comparison with studies using other formulations.

Conclusions

In septic shock, increasing NE peak-doses were associated with a stepwise increase in ICU and in-hospital mortality across four dose ranges. Ischemic complications were uncommon and largely independent of dose, except for myocardial ischemia and unexpected cardiac arrest. NE dose stratification may represent a potential tool for future risk assessment, pending external prospective validation.
