Strengthening trauma care in South Africa: Tier 1 integration of emergency first aid responder (EFAR) programmes with emergency medical services
Authors: N Abdullah, S De Vos, M Slingers, C Hendrikse, H Geduld
Published: 2026

Introduction

Trauma is a global public health crisis, claiming approximately 4.4 million lives annually, and accounting for 8% of global deaths and an estimated 10% of disability-adjusted life years [1]. It disproportionately affects young adults in their prime productive years, being the leading cause of mortality within this demographic [2,3]. This is particularly stark in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), home to 80% of the global population but experiencing 90% of all trauma-related fatalities [1,4,5]. In many regions, prehospital mortality rates can reach 80%, highlighting the imperative for timely and effective emergency care interventions [6].

South Africa's trauma burden represents one of the most pressing public health challenges facing the country [1,2]. Interpersonal violence and road traffic crashes are the leading causes, producing injury-related mortality rates that far exceed global averages [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Data from the Western Cape Government Department of Health and Wellness (WCGHW) demonstrates that trauma accounts for roughly one-third (34.3%) of all Emergency Medical Services (EMS) cases, predominantly affecting socio-economically active men aged 21-40 years [11]. Beyond its clinical toll, trauma has profound economic and social consequences, depriving families of breadwinners and caregivers, further deepening the cycles of poverty and community vulnerability.

EMS systems across Africa remain nascent, with an estimated 61% of the continent lacking formal prehospital emergency services [12]. South Africa is one of the few African nations with a structured EMS [12]. South Africa's EMS operate within a dual public-private framework [13]. Private EMS services, accessible to only 17% of the population with medical insurance, provide rapid response with advanced resources. The Western Cape state-funded EMS works across a vast geographic area of 129,462 km², maintaining a limited fleet density of 1.9 ambulances per 100,000 population [11]. The lack of a unified national emergency number, combined with prolonged response times in rural areas and informal settlements, further strains an already overstretched system [14,15]. Projections indicate that, without targeted interventions, the prehospital workforce will soon be insufficient to meet population needs, especially given the outmigration of trained emergency care providers [[16], [17], [18]]. These converging challenges promote the development of community-based first responder networks.

The Emergency First Aid Responder (EFAR) model represents a structured approach to training community members to provide immediate basic care during medical emergencies until prehospital providers arrive. It offers an approach to addressing care gaps in access to timely care, particularly in underserved communities where formal EMS response times are prolonged [15]. EFAR programmes are designed to facilitate early trauma interventions, including bleeding control, airway management, fracture stabilisation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and psychological support [15,19].

Beyond the provision of immediate care, EFARs can also serve as the eyes and ears of the EMS system, providing EMS dispatchers with crucial scene information supporting location determination, resource allocation and scene preparation; transforming emergency response from a purely reactive external service into a community-owned, proactive system. Drawing on a Descriptive Program Review of a decade of implementation and informed by insights from two illustrative sites, this commentary reflects how the systematic integration of EFAR programmes as a Tier 1 EMS component can strengthen trauma care delivery in South Africa and offers practical guidance for other LMICs.

The EFAR System Model: Foundation for Integration

The EFAR model, first implemented in South Africa in 2012, is a community-based, low-cost emergency care strategy designed specifically for communities in low and middle-income countries. Its innovation lies in giving communities ownership and empowerment over their own emergency care. Conceptual summary of core EFAR programme principles.

Community Ownership: EFAR programmes are developed with and by communities. Local leaders, organisations and residents play an active role in shaping the programme, including responders and determining how the system integrates with existing community structures – such as community health committees, religious organisations, neighbourhood watch groups, and local informal groups related by a common cause)- to meet local emergency care needs.

Cultural Appropriateness: The model is adapted to local contexts, languages, and health beliefs, ensuring that emergency care is delivered in ways that respect and reflect community values.

Sustainability: By drawing on community members rather than relying on external staff, EFAR programmes aim to minimise ongoing costs while building lasting local capacity over time.

Empowerment Through Education: Beyond training specific responders, EFAR programmes educate broader community members about emergency recognition and basic response, creating a culture of preparedness and resilience.

Integration, Not Replacement: EFARs augment rather than replace formal EMS, serving as the first tier in a coordinated response system that connects communities to professional emergency care. The standard EFAR training programme is delivered over two days (approximately 16 hours) in local languages, covering basic life support, haemorrhage control, fracture stabilisation, scene safety and communication with EMS dispatch. Competency is maintained through programme-refresher sessions incorporating scenario-based practice and case reviews.

While we focus on EFAR integration within formal EMS structures, the majority of sub-Saharan African countries do not yet have established Tier 2 EMS systems [12]. In these contexts, standalone Tier 1 programmes — operating independently without a formal ambulance service — represent a critical and viable first step toward building prehospital care capacity. Examples from Zambia [20], Tanzania [21], and Uganda [22] demonstrate that community responder programmes can deliver meaningful reductions in prehospital mortality and morbidity even in the complete absence of formal EMS, providing an essential foundation for future Tier 2 systems. The Tier 1 Integration Framework described in this paper is therefore not limited to settings with existing Tier 2 capacity; it is equally applicable as a blueprint for standalone programmes seeking to formalise operations over time.

The EFAR system has been implemented across twenty-three municipalities in South Africa [15], with particularly extensive implementation experience in the Western Cape. A ten-year evaluation demonstrates that community-based prehospital responder systems can significantly support emergency care in under-resourced areas while generating positive impacts on community development [15].

The Tier 1 Integration Framework

We have identified five components for the integration of EFAR programmes into Tier 1 EMS systems (Fig. 2):Fig. 2Conceptual summary of essential components of Tier1 Integration framework.Fig 2: dummy alt text

Conceptual summary of essential components of Tier1 Integration framework.

Component 1: Standardised Training and Certification - Standardised EFAR training curricula aligned with EMS protocols, including basic life support, trauma care, scene safety, and communication protocols. Certification should be renewable and linked to continuing education requirements.

Lessons from South Africa: Early EFAR programmes had varied training approaches, resulting in inconsistent skill levels and confusion about responder capabilities. Standardisation, implemented progressively over the past decade, has dramatically improved both the quality of care and the confidence of EMS personnel in working with EFAR responders.

Component 2: Communication and Dispatch Integration - EFARs should be integrated into existing emergency dispatch systems, enabling direct activation and real-time communication with EMS controllers. Communication solutions need to function reliably in resource-constrained environments while maintaining data security and privacy standards.

Lessons from South Africa: In many settings, EFAR activation remains informal, often relying on personal phone calls or instant messaging groups to coordinate response. While these communication approaches are flexible, they may be unreliable and fragmented, and do not ensure data security. Establishing a formal dispatch and communication mechanism, integrated with the professional EMS system, is needed to improve activation rates, coordination, and overall emergency response efficiency.

Component 3: Hierarchical Response Protocol - Establish clear protocols defining EFAR roles within the broader emergency response hierarchy. EFARs should be positioned as first-tier responders who provide immediate care and scene assessment, with defined handover procedures when professional EMS personnel arrive.

Lessons from South Africa: Early tensions between some prehospital providers and EFARs arose from unclear role definitions and occasional territorial attitudes. Formal protocols that explicitly value EFAR contributions while respecting EMS professional authority have greatly reduced these tensions. Regular joint training exercises have fostered mutual respect, a culture of collaboration and smoother handovers.

Component 4: Quality Assurance and Supervision - Robust quality assurance mechanisms, including regular skill assessments, case review processes, and clinical supervision by qualified EMS personnel. This ensures maintenance of care standards while identifying areas for system improvement.

Lessons from South Africa: Initial quality assurance focused on compliance metrics (training completion, equipment checks) rather than actual performance. Shifting to case-based learning and supportive supervision has proven more effective for maintaining skills and identifying system weaknesses. EFAR responders respond better to mentorship rather than authoritarian or compliance-driven supervision.

Component 5: Resource and Equipment Management - Sustainable systems for providing, maintaining, and replenishing EFAR equipment, including basic medical supplies, communication devices, and safety equipment. Clear protocols for inventory management and replacement.

Lessons from South Africa: Equipment shortages have been a persistent challenge. Successful programmes typically combine multiple funding sources, negotiate bulk purchasing agreements, and establish clear responsibilities for inventory management. Community ownership of equipment, rather than individual responder ownership, has proven more sustainable.

Community Implementation Stories: Two Contexts, One Framework

The selected sites represent contrasting rural and urban implementation contexts, illustrating how the Tier 1 framework may be adapted across different service environments. While the examples from these two sites are promising, the framework's applicability beyond South Africa remains to be fully established and may vary depending on local context. Both sites represent sustained, functioning implementations and are used here to illustrate implementation learning rather than to establish generalisable effectiveness.

Site 1: Merweville – Building a lifeline in the Karoo

Merweville, a remote town within the Beaufort West Municipality of the Central Karoo district, faces significant geographical barriers to emergency healthcare access [23]. The 2011 Census recorded 1,592 residents across 445 households, with the municipal area experiencing a 3.7% annual population growth rate between 2011 and 2022 [24]. In this remote context, ambulance response times can exceed 90 minutes, creating a substantial gap in access to timely emergency care.

Since the introduction of EFAR training in 2015, community responders have developed robust capabilities in trauma and medical emergency management, including haemorrhage control, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and systematic patient assessment. The programme's success earned recognition through the 2024 Golden Award as "Lopende Ambulans" (Walking Ambulance), acknowledging excellence in community-based emergency response [25].

Internal communication platforms suggest sustained community engagement and operational readiness. The programme has cultivated a durable culture of preparedness, mutual support, and strong EFAR-EMS communication with a pattern of EFAR self-deployment and appropriate escalation of EMS resources.

Operational data from 2019–2025, shared with the communities and EFAR workgroups, documents 112 emergency responses (WCGHW Provincial EFAR coordinator: Marcus Slingers, personal communication, February 12, 2026), with 69% (n=77) resulting in ambulance transport to hospital, indicating effective integration with formal EMS services. The most frequent emergency presentations were bleeding injuries (21%), respiratory distress (5%), obstetric complications (5%), and chest pain (4%). Primary interventions included recovery position placement (43%) and haemorrhage control with bandaging (13%). Response times averaged under 10 minutes to first care provision. Notably, EFAR assessments have also contributed to more efficient resource utilisation: where EFARs determine the nature of an emergency to be of lower clinical acuity following initial scene assessment, this clinical information can be relayed to EMS dispatch to enable more appropriate resource allocation — including the potential to redirect ambulances to higher-priority calls. This represents a compelling argument for EFAR integration in resource-constrained settings, where every ambulance deployment must be optimised.

Site 2: Kuilsriver – Improving trauma response in an urban town

Kuilsriver represents the urban counterpart: a high-density community in Cape Town experiencing substantial trauma burden where conventional EMS faces the competing pressures of high call volume, traffic congestion, and proximity to major road corridors. In contrast to Merweville, Kuilsriver offers reliable cellular connectivity, and the programme has leveraged digital communication platforms—principally WhatsApp-based group coordination—for situation assessment, EMS activation, and case documentation. Training emphasises rapid group activation, scene safety protocols, and structured reporting procedures. Peer mentoring and group cohesion contribute to responder retention and psychological resilience. Furthermore, these digital exchanges show how EFARs have become an important link between community emergencies and formal EMS [15].

Analysis of patient care records from May to December 2024 confirms active operational engagement across diverse emergency presentations, including burns, hypoglycaemic events, seizure disorders, motor vehicle collisions, and head trauma. EFARs routinely collaborate with EMS, the South African Police Service (SAPS), and receiving healthcare facilities, demonstrating comprehensive integration within the emergency care system

Commonly reported interventions included airway positioning, haemorrhage control, and psychological first aid. Case reviews reveal collaborative scene management with EMS personnel, exemplifying complete Tier 1-to-Tier 2 integration. One responder noted: "Within 10 minutes, six of our EFARs were on the scene, managing the patient before professional services arrived." Another articulated their role succinctly: "We keep them alive until Metro (EMS) arrives." Average activation time was anecdotally documented at under 6 minutes in many cases, drawn from the 58 documented responses captured in the May to December 2024 patient care records. Systematic case-level data capture is ongoing to enable more precise aggregate reporting in future evaluations. This rapid response is consistent with findings from other community Tier 1 programmes globally: TraumaLink in Bangladesh demonstrated first responders reaching the scene within 5 minutes in 90% of cases [26], while a dispatch-integrated community EMS programme in Tanzania (TRHM) achieved a median dispatch-to-scene interval of approximately 1 minute [21] — collectively demonstrating that community-based decentralised models can achieve response times that far surpass formal EMS .

The integration of community volunteers into formalised emergency response structures addresses both geographical and capacity barriers in pre-hospital care. Response times of 6–10 minutes to first care provision represent substantial improvements over conventional ambulance response in both settings. The high rate of subsequent ambulance transport (69% in Merweville) indicates appropriate clinical decision-making and effective coordination with professional EMS services. Furthermore, the rural implementation model demonstrates that EFAR sustainability depends less on digital infrastructure and more on deeply embedded community ownership, strong relationships with local EMS station leadership, and clear activation pathways that function under low-connectivity conditions.

Digital communication platforms facilitate operational coordination, peer support, and documentation in both rural and urban contexts. The sustained community engagement evident in internal communications suggests that the EFAR model successfully cultivates local ownership and resilience. Recognition through awards and continued operational activity over multiple years demonstrates programme sustainability beyond initial implementation phases.

Critical Success Factors: What Makes EFAR Integration Work

Analysis of implementation experience highlights three categories of enablers—organisational, social-cultural, and technical.

Organisational Enablers

EFAR programmes that succeed have backing from provincial health departments and local municipalities. This support manifests in funding, formal recognition, integration into official emergency response protocols, and advocacy for programme expansion. Successful programmes have diversified funding from multiple sources: government health budgets, municipal community development funds, private sector partnerships, and international development aid. Programmes that track responses, collect outcome data, and analyse patterns can demonstrate impact, identify areas for improvement, and advocate for resources.

Social and Cultural Enablers

Every successful EFAR programme has local champions - respected community members who advocate for the programme, recruit responders, maintain community support, and navigate local politics. Programmes that adapt to local languages, customs, and health beliefs integrate more smoothly than those that impose external models. EFAR teams that reflect community diversity in terms of language, ethnicity, gender, and age gain broader trust and access. Communities that publicly celebrate their EFARs through award ceremonies, media coverage, and formal recognition create positive feedback loops that attract new responders and maintain community support. This observation is reinforced by evidence from elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa: a three-year mixed-methods follow-up of lay first responders in Uganda found that community recognition, skills confidence, and supplemental income benefits were key motivators for sustained programme participation [22], while a three-country review across West Africa similarly identified community embeddedness as a critical determinant of lay responder retention and longitudinal programme impact [27].

Technical Enablers

Communication systems must match local infrastructure realities. In areas with reliable cellular service, smartphone apps work well; in areas with limited coverage, SMS-based systems or radio networks may be more appropriate. Regular skill refreshers, continuing education, and scenario-based practice sessions maintain competence and confidence. Technical success requires close working relationships between EFAR programmes and EMS services, with regular joint training, shared case reviews, and open communication channels.

Looking forward

The trajectory of community-based emergency care in Africa appears to be at an inflexion point. As health systems across the continent grapple with persistent workforce shortages, rapid urbanisation, and the compounding pressures of climate-related disasters and interpersonal violence, the question is no longer whether Tier 1 community responder programmes should exist, but how they can be effectively embedded, evaluated, and sustained at scale. Drawing on the experiences of the WCGHW EMS, several emerging directions may shape their next generation of community-based prehospital care.

1. From Pilot to Policy, legislating Community Emergency Care — The most consequential shift in the next decade will be the move from programme-level implementation to legislative recognition of community responders as a defined tier within national EMS frameworks. In South Africa, the forthcoming Emergency Medical Services Bill represents an opportunity to define Tier 1 responders in statute, including establishing competency standards, liability protections, and sustainable public financing mechanisms. Without this legislative anchor, even high-performing programmes remain vulnerable to funding discontinuity and system marginalisation. Comparable legislative pathways are emerging across East and West Africa, and there is growing momentum within the African Union’s health agenda for continent-wide minimum standards for community emergency care — a development that could transform fragmented national pilots into a recognised continental care tier [[28], [29], [30]].

2. Artificial Intelligence and Adaptive Dispatch — The integration of artificial intelligence into dispatch decision-making represents a horizon for resource-constrained settings. Machine learning models trained on community response data could predict high-acuity event clustering by time, geography, and sociodemographic risk profile, enabling pre-positioned rather than reactive EFAR deployment. Low-bandwidth, offline-capable applications — increasingly feasible as smartphone utilisation increases across sub-Saharan Africa — could automate clinical documentation, trigger quality assurance flags, and feed anonymised outcome data into national surveillance systems. These tools will not replace the human dimensions of community trust and responder motivation but could reduce the data invisibility that currently limits the evidence base for LMIC prehospital policy.

3. Sustaining community engagement and responder capacity — The long-term sustainability of community responder programmes remains closely linked to ongoing community participation. Rising urban unemployment, informal economy fragility, and burnout from repeated traumatic exposures are reshaping the social contract between communities and their EFAR members. Future programmes will need to move beyond recognition and refresher training toward differentiated incentive architectures — integrating EFAR service into South Africa’s Expanded Public Works Programme or National Qualifications Framework accreditation pathways. These mechanisms would not only improve retention but also reframe community emergency care as a form of dignified and remunerated civic service.

4. Building the Evidence for Global Influence — If community prehospital care is to achieve equitable representation in global health financing frameworks, the evidence base must grow beyond descriptive implementation reports. The prehospital setting urgently needs prospective, multi-site cohort studies with standardised outcome metrics, health economic analyses demonstrating cost per disability-adjusted life year averted, and implementation science research capable of isolating which programme elements are essential versus context-dependent. Promising precedent already exists: a registry-based evaluation in Cameroon demonstrated that bystander intervention was associated with reduced early mortality among injury victims — the first evidence of its kind from sub-Saharan Africa — highlighting that community responders can demonstrate survival benefit even in settings without formal prehospital systems [31]. The Global Prehospital Consortium’s recently articulated Tier 1 priorities provide a framework within which African EFAR programmes are already uniquely positioned to contribute to defining data [19].

Conclusion

The integration of Emergency First Aid Responder programmes with formal Emergency Medical Services represents a practical opportunity to strengthen early access to emergency care in communities where formal EMS response may be delayed by geography, demand or resource constraints. Drawing on a decade of South African EFAR experience, lessons from two implementation sites, this commentary highlights how a Tier 1 integration framework can support community-based response while remaining complementary to professional EMS. The model may contribute to earlier care, improved scene information, stronger community preparedness, and more coordinated escalation to ambulance services. However, sustained multisectoral collaboration, formal governance, quality assurance, and prospective evaluation are required to determine effectiveness, cost, sustainability, and transferability across different contexts.
