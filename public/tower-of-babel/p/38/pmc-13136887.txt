DUSP11 is an RNA triphosphatase that limits PspCas13b activity by destabilizing gRNA abundance in mammalian cells
Authors: Jacob Purcell, Ling Liu, Roland W Calvert, Brooke K Hayes, Cheng Huang, Chen Davidovich, Gavin J Knott, Joseph Rosenbluh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe CRISPR-Cas13 system enables programmable RNA targeting with potential applications in therapeutics and research. However, while PspCas13b mediates efficient RNA knockdown following transient transfection, stable lentiviral delivery results in minimal activity, limiting its utility. Here, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen to identify mammalian factors that restrict PspCas13b activity. We discovered that DUSP11, an RNA triphosphatase, suppresses PspCas13b function by dephosphorylating the 5′-triphosphate of Pol III-transcribed guide RNAs (gRNAs), triggering their degradation. DUSP11 knockout increased gRNA levels 2.5–4-fold and enhanced PspCas13b-mediated knockdown across multiple cell lines. This enhancement was sustained for at least 27 days and enabled targeting of endogenous transcripts previously refractory to PspCas13b. Our findings reveal an unexpected host restriction of bacterial CRISPR systems and demonstrate that gRNA levels are a limiting factor. We provide a simple strategy to improve PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells. These results have implications for developing PspCas13b-based therapeutics and suggest that systematic identification of host factors regulating CRISPR components could enhance genome editing technologies.

Introduction

Type VI CRISPR systems use the Cas13 family of proteins that function as RNA-guided RNA endonucleases, providing bacteria with adaptive immunity against phages [1, 2]. Among these, Cas13b from Prevotella sp. P5-125 (PspCas13b) has emerged as a promising tool for RNA manipulation due to its dual HEPN (higher eukaryotes and prokaryotes nucleotide-binding) domains that mediate both target-specific and collateral RNA cleavage upon activation [3, 4]. The PspCas13b-guide RNA (gRNA) ribonucleoprotein complex recognizes complementary target RNA sequences through Watson–Crick base pairing, triggering a conformational change that activates the HEPN nuclease domains [5, 6].

In bacterial systems, upon target recognition, activated Cas13 nucleases cut the target RNA and induce programmed cell dormancy or death through induction of widespread collateral RNA degradation, effectively limiting phage propagation within the population [2, 7, 8]. Metagenomic analysis found that Cas13b is the most abundant Cas13 variant across bacterial species that use the type VI CRISPR system [9], suggesting Cas13b as a robust and effective mechanism of RNA degradation. PspCas13b RNase activity has been successfully harnessed for ultrasensitive RNA detection platforms in cell-free systems, where both on-target and collateral cleavage generate amplified signals [10, 11]. The unique properties of PspCas13b, including its compact size, high specificity, and programmable targeting, have generated substantial interest for therapeutic and research applications in mammalian systems [12]. Proposed applications include targeted RNA knockdown as an alternative to RNAi [13], base-specific RNA editing when fused to ADAR domains [13, 14], live-cell RNA imaging through fluorescent protein fusions [15], and viral RNA targeting for antiviral therapeutics [16, 17].

However, the implementation of PspCas13b in mammalian cells faces two critical challenges that have limited its widespread utility. First, the collateral RNase activity that provides antiphage defence in bacteria raises concerns about cellular toxicity and off-target effects in therapeutic applications [18–21]. While recent evidence suggests that collateral activity is more restricted and context-dependent in mammalian cells than initially feared [13, 22], comprehensive characterization remains essential for clinical translation. Second, and more puzzling, is the dramatic difference in PspCas13b efficiency depending on the delivery method: while transient transfection achieves robust target knockdown (60%–90%), lentiviral delivery, which is preferred for stable expression and in vivo applications, results in minimal activity (10%–30%) [23]. This delivery-dependent activity has severely hampered the development of stable PspCas13b cell lines and has limited its use in basic research and therapeutics.

The mechanistic basis for reduced PspCas13b activity following lentiviral delivery has remained elusive. We hypothesized that post-transcriptional regulation of the PspCas13b machinery in mammalian cells may explain this phenomenon. Here, we employed an unbiased genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen to identify mammalian factors that restrict PspCas13b activity. We discovered that DUSP11, a cellular RNA triphosphatase, negatively regulates PspCas13b by dephosphorylating the 5′-triphosphate of Pol III-transcribed gRNAs, leading to their degradation. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized host restriction mechanism and provide a straightforward strategy to enhance PspCas13b-mediated RNA targeting in mammalian cells.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

A375, DLD1, MIA-PaCa-2, and U87 cell lines were obtained from ATCC. HeLa cells were a generous gift from Dr Michael Lazarou (Walter and Eliza Hall Institute). HEK293T cells were obtained from Thermo Fisher (Cat. Num. #R70007). Cell-line identity was verified by STR at the Australian Genomics Research Facility. All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. HeLa, DLD1, MIA-PaCa-2, and U87 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. A375 were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines were subject to regular polymerase chain reaction (PCR) mycoplasma testing to ensure an absence of contamination.

Plasmids and cloning

Cas9 was expressed in all cell lines using Addgene plasmid #7331 0 [38] a gift from Dr Jason Moffat. mCherry was expressed in all cell lines using Addgene plasmid #8541 2 [39] a gift from Dr Michael Bassik.

All primers and oligonucleotides used for plasmid generation are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The PspCas13b expression plasmid was generated by amplifying the PspCas13b ORF from Addgene plasmid #10386 2 [13], a gift from Dr Feng Zhang. PCR product was digested with Esp3I and ligated into AgeI- and BamHI-digested lentiCRISPRv2-puro (Addgene #98290 [40], a gifted from Dr Brett Stringer).

PspCas13b gRNAs were expressed from a modified version of pRDA221 (Addgene #169142, a gift from Dr John Doench and Dr David Root) with the puromycin-resistance gene removed. To generate this plasmid, the following oligonucleotides were annealed and ligated between the BamHI and NheI restriction sites: 5′- GATCTTTGAATTTTGACCTTCTCAAGTTGGCGGGAGA CGTGGAGTCCAACCCAGGGCCCG-3′ and 5′- CTAGCGGGCCCTGGGTTGGACTCCACGTCT CCCGCCAACTTGAGAAGGTCAAAATTCAAA-3′.

gRNAs were generated by PCR using a single-stranded oligonucleotide template and the following primers: forward 5′-GGAAAGGACGAAACACCGGT-3′ and reverse 5′- TGTCTCAAGATCTAGAATTCAAAAAAGTTGTAATAGC CCCTCAAAACTGGACCTTCC-3′. PCR products were Gibson ligated into the backbone between the AgeI and EcoRI restriction sites. Individual single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were cloned into the PspCas13b-lentiCRISPRv2 vector generated above by annealing oligonucleotides corresponding to the spacer sequence and T4 ligating the product between Esp3I restriction sites. Spacer oligos had the following structure: 5′-CACCG-[20nt-spacer]-3′ and 5′-AAAC-[20nt-spacer reverse complement]-C-3′. All plasmids were isolated using Qiagen mini-prep or maxi-prep kits.

Plasmid and synthetic gRNA transfection

All plasmid transfections were performed in a 12-well plate format using TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent (Mirus Bio #MIR2306) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 × 105 cells were seeded ~18 h prior to transfection. For each well, 1 μg of total DNA was transfected per well using an equal mass of each plasmid. Synthetic 5′-OH Cas13b gRNAs were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies. Cells stably expressing PspCas13b, mCherry, Cas9, and an sgRNA were transfected with 100 nmol of gRNA per well using TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent as above.

Lentivirus generation and transduction

Lentivirus was generated by co-transfecting lentiviral vectors along with pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) and psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) into HEK293FT cells. Approximately 16 h post-transfection, the culture media was changed to DMEM containing 30% FBS. Lentiviral supernatant was then harvested 48- and 72-h post-transfection, pooled, and stored at −80°C.

Cell lines were transduced by addition of lentiviral supernatant and polybrene (1 μg/ml) to culture media, followed by 2 h centrifugation at 1070 × g and overnight incubation. After 24 h incubation, cells transduced with constructs containing an antibiotic-resistance gene were selected by addition of culture media containing 10 μg/ml blasticidin or 2 μg/ml puromycin. Cells transduced with constructs containing mCherry or GFP were isolated by FACS using a BD Influx cell sorter (BD Biosciences).

Generation of pooled sgRNA library

The sgRNA library for the CRISPR screens was prepared as previously described [41, 42]. Briefly, the sgRNA sequences obtained from the Brunello library [24] were ordered as a custom oligonucleotide pool (TWIST Biosciences) with flanking PCR adaptors and Esp3I cut sites (Supplementary Table S1). The final sequence of oligonucleotides in the pool is: 5′-AGGCACTTGCTCGTACGACGCGTCTCACACCG-[20nt-spacer]-GTTTCGAGACGTTAAGGTGCCGGGCCCACAT-3′. The sgRNA sequences were amplified from this pool with the following primers: forward, 5′-AGGCACTTGCTCGTACGACG-3′ and reverse, 5′- ATGTGGGCCCGGCACCTTAA-3′. The PCR product was then cloned via Golden Gate assembly into Esp3I-digested PspCas13b-NES-3xHA-lentiCRISPRv2. Ligated libraries were electroporated into DH5α electrocompetent cells (Invitrogen), and the plasmid DNA was extracted using a Qiagen maxi-prep kit. A 1000× representation of bacterial colonies per sgRNA was ensured to maintain library diversity.

CRISPR screens

HeLa cells stably expressing Cas9, mCherry, GFP, and either an mCherry-targeting or non-targeting PspCas13b gRNA were established via lentiviral transduction as per above. These cells were transduced with library virus at a multiplicity of infection of 0.3 and representation of 1000 cells per sgRNA in two biological replicates. Twenty-four hours post-infection, cells were selected with 2 μg/ml puromycin. Ten days post-infection, cells with the bottom 10% of mCherry levels were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting using a BD Influx cell sorter (BD Biosciences). Genomic DNA was extracted from these cells using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen).

High-throughput sequencing libraries were generated via one-step PCR to amplify the integrated sgRNA sequence as previously described [41, 42]. PCR products were then purified using AMPure beads and samples sequenced using a HiSeq system (Illumina). PoolQ software (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/gpp/public/software/poolq) was used for deconvolution and alignment of sgRNA reads. The MAGeCK software package (https://sourceforge.net/projects/mageck/) was used to analyse the CRISPR screens using default settings.

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed in PBS and resuspended in PBS containing 5% FBS for flow cytometry analysis. All samples were analysed using either an LSR II (BD Biosciences) or FORTESSA X20 (BD Biosciences). All flow cytometry profiles were analysed using FlowJo V10 software (Tree Star Inc).

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed in PBS and resuspended in RIPA buffer (CST #9806) containing complete protease inhibitor (Roche). Protein lysates were quantified using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher #2322) and then diluted in 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad #161-0747). Protein samples were separated by electrophoresis using Bio-Rad 4%–20% precast gels and transferred to an activated PVDF membrane using the Trans-Blot®Turbo™ Transfer System. Membranes were blocked in tris-buffered saline solution with 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20 (TBST) containing 5% bovine serum albumin for at least 1 h. Thereafter, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies at the indicated dilutions: β-actin (Santa Cruz, SC47778, 1:10 000), DUSP11 (Proteintech, 10204-2-AP, 1:1000), and HA-tag (Cell Signalling Technologies, C29F4, 1:2000). Membranes were then washed three times with TBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: anti-mouse IgG (Cell Signalling Technologies, 7076S, 1:10 000) and anti-rabbit IgG (Cell Signalling Technologies, 7074S, 1:5000). Membranes were then washed three times with TBST and developed using Clarity ECL detection reagent (Bio-Rad). Chemiluminescence was detected using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system.

RNA isolation, integrity analysis, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Sigma–Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Total RNA (1 µg) was converted to complementary DNA using Maxima reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher) in a 20 µl reaction with random hexamers and dNTPs. The expression level of genes was determined via qRT-PCR using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher) with the following conditions: denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification at 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 20 s. Primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S3. GAPDH was used for normalization unless otherwise indicated. The ΔΔCt method was used to analyse the gene expression levels.

Cell viability assay

Cell lines were transfected as per above. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were collected and stained with trypan blue dye (1:1 ratio). Live and dead cells were enumerated using an Invitrogen Countess 3 automated cell counter.

Data analysis

Data analysis and visualization was performed in GraphPad Prism software version 10. Specific statistical tests and numbers of independent biological replicates are mentioned in respective figure legends.

In vitro transcription and modification of the RNA 5′-phosphorylation

In vitro transcription of triphosphorylated (5′-PPP) PspCas13b gRNAs was performed using T7 RNA polymerase (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with double-stranded DNA templates generated by PCR. Following synthesis, reaction mixtures were treated with DNase I (NEB) to remove template DNA, and transcribed gRNAs were purified using the Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB). 5′-P and 5′-OH gRNAs were generated by treating 5′-PPP gRNAs with RppH (NEB) and Calf Intestinal Phosphatase (NEB), respectively, by incubating at 37°C for 2 h, then isolated as described earlier. All gRNAs were quantified by spectrophotometry and stored at −80°C.

Mass spectrometry of in modified RNAs

For LC-MS/MS, the RNA sample was directly injected for LC-MS/MS system analysis. Chromatographic separation of the sample was performed on a Vanquish Flex UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), on a PLRP-S (300 Å 3 µM, 2.1 × 150 mm, Agilent Technologies) at 80°C utilizing a 8 min 5%–30% gradient of solvent A (20 mM triethylamine, 80 mM hexafluoroisopropanol) and solvent B (20 mM triethylamine, 80 mM hexafluoroisopropanol, 50% methanol) at a flow rate 300 μl/min. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Ascend Structural Biology Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in negative electrospray ionization mode with the default source settings. Full mass data were acquired at a resolution of 120 000 (FWHM) at m/z 200. Data were reviewed in FreeStyle 1.8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and deconvoluted utilizing Xtract (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with the following parameters: charge range: 2–50; analyzer type: OT; relative abundance threshold: 0%; isotope table: nucleotide; negative charge; minimum number detected charge: 3.

Results

PspCas13b exhibits correlated on-target and collateral RNA cleavage activities that are related to gRNA and Cas13 levels in mammalian cells. To systematically evaluate PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells, we developed a three-vector lentiviral reporter system that simultaneously monitors on-target and collateral RNA cleavage (Fig. 1A). The system comprises of: (i) codon-optimized PspCas13b, (ii) a dual-fluorescence cassette expressing GFP and either an mCherry-targeting or non-targeting control gRNA, and (iii) mCherry. In this design, mCherry fluorescence reports on-target activity, while GFP fluorescence indicates collateral cleavage, as GFP transcripts should only be degraded through PspCas13b collateral RNase activity upon activation.

Transient transfection of all three plasmids into HEK293T cells resulted in robust PspCas13b activation, with reduction in both mCherry (on-target) and GFP (collateral) fluorescence compared to non-targeting controls (Fig. 1A). Importantly, we observed a strong positive correlation between on-target and collateral activities in both HEK293T and HeLa cells (Supplementary Figs S1A and B), indicating that collateral cleavage directly reflects PspCas13b activation status. Despite detectable collateral activity of the exogenously expressed GFP reporter, we found no evidence of global RNA degradation, indicated by no effect on ribosomal RNA (Supplementary Fig. S1C), or expression of high-abundance housekeeping transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S1D), or cell viability (Supplementary Fig. S1E), suggesting that PspCas13b’s promiscuous RNase activity is constrained in mammalian cells.

We next examined whether the delivery method influences PspCas13b activity, as previous reports indicated reduced efficiency with lentiviral transduction [23]. Following lentiviral delivery of our reporter system into HEK293T (Fig. 1B) or HeLa (Fig. 1C) cells, we observed significantly diminished PspCas13b activity compared to transfection. On-target knockdown decreased from 60%–90% (transfection) to 20%–35% (transduction) in HeLa and HEK293T cells, while collateral activity showed proportional reductions. U87 cells displayed higher activity of PspCas13b, achieving ∼69% on-target knockdown and 52% collateral RNA cleavage with lentiviral transduction (Fig. 1D), consistent with previous observations of cell-type-specific differences [19]. By quantifying PspCas13b protein level (Figs 1E and F; Supplementary Fig. S1F) and gRNA levels (Fig. 1G), we found that both protein and gRNA levels were higher in transfected cells, suggesting that the levels of the PspCas13b apparatus is critical for its activity. These results confirm that lentiviral delivery substantially impairs PspCas13b function and suggest that mammalian factors may restrict its activity.

Genome-wide CRISPR screen identifies DUSP11as a negative regulator of PspCas13b activity. To identify mammalian genes that regulate PspCas13b activity, we designed a pooled CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen (Fig. 2A). Given that PspCas13b activity decreases over time in transduced cells [23], we developed a dual-function vector enabling simultaneous expression of PspCas13b and Cas9-targeting sgRNAs. We cloned a genome-wide sgRNA library (based on the Brunello sgRNA [24] library design) into this vector, generating 77 441 unique constructs targeting 19 114 genes with 4 sgRNAs per gene, plus 1 000 non-targeting controls (Supplementary Table S1).

HeLa cells stably expressing Cas9, mCherry reporter, and either mCherry-targeting (PspCas13b active) or non-targeting (PspCas13b inactive) PspCas13b gRNA were transduced with the pooled library at low MOI (0.3) to ensure single sgRNA integration. After 14 days, cells were sorted by FACS into mCherry-high and mCherry-low populations. Deep sequencing revealed good library representation (99.5% of sgRNAs detected with >100 reads) and high correlation between replicate screens (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Depletion of sgRNAs targeting core essential genes [38] confirmed efficient Cas9-mediated knockout (Supplementary Figs S2B and C).

We used the MAGeCK pipeline [25] to identify genes knockouts that enhanced PspCas13b activity (enriched in mCherry-low fraction) (Supplementary Table S2). As hits, we considered genes with log2[fold change] > 1 and FDR < 0.1. In cells with non-targeting gRNA, enriched genes primarily comprised known transcriptional regulators (e.g. TAF4, TAF10) and RNA stability factors (e.g. ZCCHC14, PABPC1), representing non-specific effects on mCherry expression (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table S2). In cells with the targeting gRNA, we found three additional hits: DUSP11, PPRC1, and CCNF (Fig. 2C and D). These genes showed specific enrichment only in the presence of active PspCas13b, suggesting direct regulation of active PspCas13b rather than general effects on gene expression.

U87 cells had higher PspCas13b activity (Fig. 1D and [19, 23]). To identify genes that regulate PspCas13b activity in U87 cells, we used a similar screening approach. As expected, core-essential genes were depleted in U87 cells with targeting (Supplementary Fig. S2D) or non-targeting (Supplementary Fig. S2E) sgRNAs, demonstrating effective Cas9 gene knockout. Using the MAGeCK pipeline, we did not find any genes that were significantly enriched in the mCherry low or high fractions (Supplementary Fig. S2F).

DUSP11 restricts PspCas13b activity in human cells. To validate our screen candidates, we generated Cas9 sgRNAs targeting each hit and assessed their effects on PspCas13b activity. Genes affecting general transcription or messenger RNA (mRNA) stability (TAF4, PABPC1, ZCCHC14) reduced both GFP and mCherry expression regardless of PspCas13b activation status, confirming their non-specific effects (Fig. 3A and B). While PPRC1 initially appeared PspCas13b-specific in our screen, validation showed that it also functions as a transcriptional regulator, consistent with its known role as a transcription co-activator[26].

CCNF and DUSP11 knockout specifically enhanced PspCas13b activity without affecting mCherry fluorescence in control cells with non-targeting PspCas13b gRNA (Fig. 3A and B; Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). We found that DUSP11 was the most potent PspCas13b regulator, with knockout increasing on-target activity by 2.1-fold (from 24% to 51% mCherry knockdown) (Supplementary Fig. S3A) and collateral activity by 1.8-fold (from 16% to 29% GFP knockdown) (Supplementary Fig. S3B). The specific enhancement of both on-target and collateral activities upon DUSP11 knockout strongly supports its role as a PspCas13b restriction factor.

DUSP11 knockout enhances PspCas13b-mediated RNA cleavage across multiple contexts. As the most potent PspCas13b inhibitor, we first confirmed efficient DUSP11 protein depletion by Western blot (Fig. 4A and B; Supplementary Fig. S4A). DUSP11 knockout consistently enhanced PspCas13b activity across multiple cell lines (Fig. 4C–E and Supplementary Fig. S4B–E), demonstrating broad applicability. In HeLa cells, DUSP11 knockout increased mCherry knockdown from 23% to 42% (Fig. 4C and D), an enhancement that persisted throughout a 27-day time course (Fig. 4F and G).

To determine whether DUSP11 restriction of PspCas13b activity extends to endogenous transcripts, we targeted B2M, which encodes β2-microglobulin, a component of MHC class I complexes [27]. While control cells showed negligible B2M mRNA knockdown with PspCas13b, DUSP11 knockout enabled significant reduction of B2M mRNA levels (25% knockdown, P < .01) (Fig. 4H). To further assess the effect of DUSP11 knockout on the PspCas13b activity, we used FACS to measure B2M protein levels in HeLa cells transduced with a B2M-targeting PspCas13b gRNA. HeLa cells expressing a Cas9 control or DUSP11 sgRNA were transduced with a PspCas13b B2M or control targeting gRNA, and B2M protein levels was assessed by FACS (Fig. 4I). We found that DUSP11 knockout cells had a 46.2% reduction in B2M protein compared with a 6.2% reduction in cells with no DUSP11 knockout. These results demonstrate that DUSP11 restriction is not limited to exogenous reporters and suggests that endogenous RNAs that are refractory to PspCas13b targeting can become accessible upon inhibition of DUSP11. Using the 1 699 CCLE cell lines [28], we found the DUSP11 expression was uniform across most cell lines and was not significantly different in U87 cells (Supplementary Fig. S4F), suggesting the mechanism for increased PspCas13b activity in U87 is different.

DUSP11 destabilizes PspCas13b gRNAs through 5′-triphosphate dephosphorylation. DUSP11 functions as an RNA triphosphatase that removes 5′-triphosphate groups from RNA substrates, converting them to 5′-monophosphate [29, 30]. Based on this enzymatic activity, we hypothesized that DUSP11 dephosphorylates Pol III-transcribed PspCas13b gRNAs, which naturally bear 5′-triphosphates, leading to their degradation by cellular exonucleases (Fig. 5A). To test this model, we measured gRNA levels, following transduction, by RT-qPCR in control and DUSP11 knockout cells. DUSP11 depletion increased gRNA abundance 2.7-fold for control gRNAs and 3.8-fold for mCherry-targeting gRNAs (Fig. 5B), directly demonstrating that DUSP11 regulates gRNA stability. gRNA transfection had much higher levels of gRNA expression, which were also further increased in DUSP11 knockout cells (Supplementary Fig. S5A). The enhanced gRNA levels in DUSP11 knockout cells correlate with increased PspCas13b activity following transduction (Fig. 4D and E; Supplementary Fig. S4B–E) or transfection (Supplementary Fig. S5B), supporting a model where gRNA availability limits PspCas13b function in mammalian cells.

To confirm that 5′-phosphorylation status determines gRNA stability, we transfected synthetic gRNAs bearing 5′-hydroxyl groups, which cannot be substrates for DUSP11. These synthetic gRNAs mediated robust PspCas13b activity that was unaffected by DUSP11 status (Fig. 5C and D), confirming that DUSP11 specifically targets 5′-triphosphorylated RNAs. To directly assess the role of DUSP11 in regulating gRNA 5′ phosphorylation status and stability, we in vitro transcribed gRNAs and enzymatically engineered defined 5′-phosphorylation states, including 5′-triphosphorylated, 5′-monophosphorylated, and 5′-hydroxyl (non-phosphorylated) species. RNA mass spectrometry confirmed the integrity and phosphorylation status of each modified gRNA (Supplementary Fig. S5C). These gRNAs were transfected into DUSP11 knockout HeLa cells, and intracellular gRNA abundance was quantified (Fig. 5E). 5′-triphosphorylated gRNAs exhibited the greatest increase in abundance, consistent with DUSP11-mediated dephosphorylation of the gRNA 5′ end as a key determinant of gRNA stability and, consequently, enhanced PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells.

Based on these results, we hypothesized that the enhanced PspCas13b activity in U87 cells may be due to increased gRNA stability. Following transduction with two different gRNAs, we measured a 2–2.5-fold increase in gRNA expression in U87 cells compared with HeLa or HEK293T cells, explaining why these cells show better PspCas13b activity (Fig. 5F). These results explain why transfection achieves superior PspCas13b activity compared to lentiviral delivery systems.

To further validate the effect of DUSP11 on gRNA stability, we used a DOX-inducible DUSP11 overexpression vector. DOX treatment resulted in upregulation of DUSP11 protein (Fig. 5G and H; Supplementary Fig. S5D). We found that DUSP11 overexpression resulted in destabilizing gRNA expression (Fig. 5I and K) and reduced PspCas13b activity (Fig. 5J and L) in U87 or HeLa cells. These results demonstrate that gRNA stability is a major determinate of PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells and that DUSP11 regulates gRNA stability in mammalian cells.

To assess DUSP11 activity on gRNA stability from other Cas enzymes, we used Cas9 and RfxCas13d. DUSP11 knockout had no effect on sgRNA levels of Cas9 (Supplementary Fig. S5E). Like PspCas13b gRNAs, we found that DUSP11 knockout stabilized RfxCas13d gRNA expression by 2–3-fold (Supplementary Fig. S5F). However, RfxCas13d activity was not enhanced (Supplementary Fig. S5G). By comparing gRNA levels between PspCas13b and RfxCas13d, we found that basal gRNA levels were higher for RfxCas13d (Supplementary Fig. S5H), suggesting that the increased activity reported for RfxCad13d [31] is due to its better gRNA stability.

Discussion

Cas13 proteins target RNA and, in principle, could enable a broad range of applications inaccessible to other genome-editing platforms. Proposed uses include the study of post-transcriptional RNA modifications such as circRNAs [32, 33] and alternative splicing [34, 35]. However, the utility of these approaches has been challenged by reports of poor Cas13 activity in large-scale screens [36]. Two major limitations constrain its use in high-throughput functional screens: first, collateral RNA cleavage causes non-specific transcriptome-wide degradation; second, while plasmid transfection achieves robust target knockdown, viral transduction, required for large-scale screening, produces only modest RNA depletion [23].

In this study, we identified DUSP11 as a key negative regulator of PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells through an unbiased genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into the long-standing paradox of why Cas13b, despite being the most abundant Cas13 variant in bacterial systems, exhibits limited activity when delivered via lentiviral vectors in mammalian cells. We show that gRNA levels are a critical factor in determining the efficiency of PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells. We found that transfection results in a ∼1000-fold increase in gRNA levels compared with transduction. We demonstrate that DUSP11, an endogenous RNA phosphatase that has evolved as a defence mechanism against viral RNA [37], mediates dephosphorylation of the PspCas13b gRNA 5′-triphosphate, leading to gRNA destabilization and subsequent reduction in PspCas13b activity. The 2.5–4-fold increase in gRNA levels observed upon DUSP11 knockout directly correlates with enhanced PspCas13b activity, supporting a model where gRNA availability is rate-limiting for efficient RNA targeting in mammalian cells. Further supporting these observations, we found that U87 cells, which have high PspCas13b activity [19, 23], express higher levels of gRNA. DUSP11 expression levels are comparable between U87 and cells with low PspCas13b activity, suggesting that a different mechanism is responsible for the higher gRNA levels in U87 cells. Another possibility that we could not completely rule out is that an alternative isoform of DUSP11 that is not able to dephosphorylate PspCas13b is expressed in U87 cells. Understanding why certain cell types, like U87, are naturally more permissive to gRNA expression could inform the development of further strategies to enhance its activity. Moreover, overexpression of DUSP11 further suppresses gRNA stability and PspCas13b activity. Together, these results demonstrate that gRNA levels limit PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells. It is important to note that our study shows the importance of gRNA stability in PspCas13b activity, but we cannot exclude other factors, such as protein stability, that may affect PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells.

Aligning with published data, our results demonstrate that PspCas13b has on-target and collateral RNA cleavage activities in mammalian cells, with these activities being highly correlated. Interestingly, while collateral activity was detectable, it did not result in global RNA degradation or affect cell viability under our experimental conditions, suggesting that the collateral RNase activity of PspCas13b may be more restricted in mammalian cells compared to bacterial systems. This observation is consistent with recent reports indicating context-dependent collateral activity [19] and supports the therapeutic application of PspCas13b, provided that appropriate safety measures are implemented.

We validated both DUSP11 and CCNF as negative regulators of PspCas13b activity. While we were able to determine the direct mechanism of regulation by DUSP11, the mechanism of inhibition by CCNF is less clear. CCNF (Cyclin F) is an F-box protein involved in cell cycle regulation and protein ubiquitination. Its role in modulating PspCas13b activity may involve indirect mechanisms, such as regulation of RNA stability factors or cell cycle-dependent changes in RNA metabolism. Future studies should explore whether CCNF affects PspCas13b activity through direct regulation of gRNA stability or through broader effects on cellular RNA processing pathways.

Our findings have immediate practical implications for improving PspCas13b-based applications. Several strategies emerge for enhancing PspCas13b activity in mammalian cells: (i) genetic or pharmacological inhibition of DUSP11, (ii) use of chemically modified gRNAs resistant to dephosphorylation (it should be noted that chemically modified gRNAs could only be introduced via transfection), (iii) engineering of gRNA expression cassettes that produce protected 5′ ends, or (iv) development of PspCas13b variants that function efficiently with lower gRNA concentrations. The sustained enhancement of PspCas13b activity over 27 days following DUSP11 knockout suggests that long-term RNA targeting applications are feasible with appropriate optimization. Our study has practical implications for the use of PspCas13b in functional genomics strategies. Since gRNA stability is difficult to attain with lentiviral transductions, it is not likely to be a preferred strategy for pooled genetic screens. Supporting this conclusion, recent reports showed no significant hits in large scale Cas13 pooled screens [36].

This work also raises important questions about the regulation of other CRISPR systems in mammalian cells. Given that many CRISPR components rely on small RNAs transcribed by Pol III and contain a 5′-triphosphate, it is possible that DUSP11 or related phosphatases may affect the activity of other Cas proteins. We show that DUSP11 knockout increases the levels of RfxCas13d gRNA, but this did not result in better activity, suggesting that in the case of RfxCas13d gRNA levels are not a limiting factor. Systematic investigation of phosphatase effects on different CRISPR systems could reveal common regulatory mechanisms and inform the design of more efficient genome and transcriptome editing tools.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, while we demonstrated enhanced PspCas13b activity against both exogenous (mCherry) and endogenous (B2M) targets, the degree of knockdown achieved (∼25%–40%) remains modest compared to other RNA-targeting technologies. Second, our screen was performed in HeLa cells, and the role of DUSP11 in other cell types, particularly primary cells and tissues, requires further investigation. Third, the long-term effects of combining DUSP11 knockout with PspCas13b expression on cellular physiology and potential off-target effects need comprehensive evaluation.

In conclusion, our work identifies DUSP11 as a mammalian phosphatase that has evolved to recognize and dephosphorylate foreign small RNAs, restricts PspCas13b activity, and provides both mechanistic understanding and practical solutions for improving RNA-targeting applications. These findings highlight the importance of considering host cell factors when adapting bacterial CRISPR systems for mammalian cell applications and suggest that systematic identification and modulation of such factors could significantly enhance the therapeutic potential of CRISPR-based technologies.
