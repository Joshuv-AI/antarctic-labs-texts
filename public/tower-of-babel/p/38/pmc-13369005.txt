Immediate Implant Placement With Guided Bone Regeneration in Compromised Anterior Maxillary Sites: Aesthetic Outcomes
Authors: Nissaf Daouahi, Yosra Gassara, Chokri Abdellatif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmediate implant placement combined with guided bone regeneration can successfully rehabilitate compromised anterior maxillary sites by preserving peri‐implant tissues, optimizing emergence profile development, and achieving stable aesthetic integration.

Introduction

The management of aging and defective restorations remains a significant clinical challenge, particularly when abutment teeth are periodontally compromised and present a poor prognosis. Progressive periodontal breakdown, endodontic failure, and periradicular pathology frequently result in non‐restorable teeth. Conventional approaches, such as maintaining tooth‐supported prostheses or delaying implant placement, may worsen both biological and aesthetic outcomes by increasing biomechanical stress on adjacent teeth and promoting uncontrolled post‐extraction alveolar ridge resorption [1].

Tooth extraction in the anterior maxilla is consistently followed by rapid alveolar ridge remodeling, predominantly affecting the buccal bone plate. Horizontal bone loss of 30%–50% during early healing has been widely reported, especially in sites with thin facial bone or pre‐existing defects. These dimensional changes may compromise implant positioning, peri‐implant soft‐tissue support, and ultimately the aesthetic outcome [2].

Immediate implant placement has been proposed as a biologically driven approach to limit treatment time and support peri‐implant tissue preservation. When combined with atraumatic extraction and prosthetically guided positioning, survival rates comparable to delayed protocols have been demonstrated [3, 4, 5]. However, growing evidence shows that immediate implant placement alone does not prevent buccal bone resorption—especially in compromised sockets—potentially compromising soft tissue stability and aesthetic outcomes [6]˒ [7].

In contemporary implant dentistry, success is defined not only by implant survival but also by prosthetic integration, particularly in anterior implants. Evidence shows that soft tissue thickness and buccal bone support play a critical role in achieving predictable and harmonious aesthetic outcomes [8, 9].

Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is essential in immediate implant placement, especially in sites with buccal defects. It helps preserve bone volume, support soft tissue stability, and improve aesthetic outcomes. Xenogeneic grafts, in particular, provide long‐term volume stability. Ultimately, achieving optimal results depends less on implant timing and more on precise implant positioning combined with effective tissue augmentation [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

The Objective of this paper is to present a prosthetically guided approach for managing a compromised anterior maxillary restoration. Treatment planning focused on achieving optimal implant position, emergence profile, and peri‐implant soft tissue architecture. What distinguishes this case from previously published reports is the simultaneous management of multiple compromised abutment teeth with pre‐existing buccal defects in a multi‐unit anterior rehabilitation, combined with a specifically designed removable provisional strategy to protect the regenerative environment. This scenario presents a particularly challenging defect morphology and prosthetic complexity that is underrepresented in the existing literature.

Case History/Examination

A 52‐year‐old female patient presented with an aesthetic chief complaint (Figure 1). She was dissatisfied with an existing metal–ceramic fixed bridge replacing the maxillary canine.

Initial situation showing an aesthetic smile.

Clinical examination revealed compromised abutment teeth with radicular caries: the lateral incisor (tooth 12) and first premolar (tooth 14). Inadequate marginal adaptation and pronounced cervical gray discoloration were detected. Periodontal evaluation demonstrated reduced supporting bone and unfavorable crown‐to‐root ratios (Figure 2). Radiographic examination confirmed the hopeless prognosis of teeth 12 and 14. It revealed localized bone defects (Figure 3).

Investigations and Treatment

A treatment plan consisting of three three‐unit rehabilitations. It begins with atraumatic extraction of the abutment teeth, followed by immediate placement of implants with simultaneous guided bone regeneration (GBR), as proposed and accepted by the patient.

Atraumatic extractions were performed using periotomes to preserve the surrounding hard and soft tissues. Their meticulous use facilitated gentle tooth removal while maintaining the integrity of the buccal plate.

Three OSSTEM implants were placed immediately in the extraction sockets at the site of the lateral incisor (3.5 × 10 mm), the canine (4 × 11.5 mm), and the first premolar (3.5 × 10 mm). Implants were positioned slightly palatally, prosthetically guided, to avoid pressure on the buccal bone (Figure 4). Primary stability was achieved by engaging native apical bone. Insertion torque values ranged from 35 to 45 Ncm, and implant stability quotient (ISQ) values measured at placement ranged from 68 to 74, confirming adequate primary stability in all three sites. Buccal defect dimensions at the time of surgery ranged from 3 to 5 mm in width with residual facial bone thickness estimated at 0.5–1 mm, necessitating simultaneous GBR in all sites. A mucoperiosteal flap was reflected with minimal vertical releasing incisions to allow tension‐free primary closure following grafting.

Positioning and placement of implants.

Residual peri‐implant gaps and buccal defects were grafted using bone substitute (Smartbone). A resorbable collagen membrane was applied over the grafted sites and stabilized to prevent soft‐tissue collapse. Primary closure was achieved with tension‐free sutures to ensure membrane stability and promote healing (Figure 5). This approach preserved peri‐implant hard and soft tissues, guided soft‐tissue maturation (Figure 6). Radiographic evaluation during the healing period demonstrated a suitable osseointegration (Figure 7).

Peri‐implant buccal gap management, alveolar crest reconstruction, and final suturing.

Healing process.

Radiographic examination.

Regarding provisionalization, an immediate fixed provisional restoration was not placed to avoid functional loading and pressure on the grafted sites. Instead, a removable provisional prosthesis was delivered. This prosthesis was customized by relieving (hollowing) its intaglio surface in the areas corresponding to the surgical sites, thereby eliminating contact with the grafted regions. This design ensured protection of the grafted bone and membrane, minimized mechanical stress during early healing, and allowed undisturbed soft‐ and hard‐tissue regeneration while maintaining acceptable esthetics during the provisional phase.

Following a healing period, careful management of the peri‐implant soft tissues was initiated to sculpt an optimal emergence profile. Healing abutments were selected according to the diameter of each implant and placed to support and maintain the established transmucosal contour (Figures 8, 9, 10). These healing abutments played a critical role in stabilizing the peri‐implant tissues, preserving the emergence profile, and facilitating the transition toward the definitive prosthetic phase while ensuring optimal aesthetic integration.

Results and Conclusion

At the definitive prosthetic stage, implant‐supported restorations were delivered, achieving optimal functional and aesthetic integration. Clinical evaluation demonstrated stable peri‐implant soft tissues with well‐contoured marginal gingiva and complete papillary fill, contributing to a natural and harmonious emergence profile. The prosthetic contours provided adequate support to the peri‐implant mucosa (Figures 11 and 12). Aesthetic outcomes were assessed using the Pink Aesthetic Score (PES) and White Aesthetic Score (WES). The PES score was 12/14 and the WES score was 9/10, reflecting favorable soft tissue contours, papilla fill, and prosthetic integration. Peri‐implant mucosal stability was maintained, with no signs of recession or inflammation at the time of evaluation. Marginal bone levels appeared stable on periapical radiographs, with no significant crestal bone loss detected. The mucosal phenotype in this case was classified as thick, which is considered favorable for long‐term peri‐implant tissue stability. Follow‐up is ongoing, with clinical and radiographic evaluation planned at 1 year post‐loading.

Immediate implant placement combined with guided bone regeneration represents a predictable approach for rehabilitating compromised anterior maxillary sites. Careful implant positioning, appropriate grafting, and controlled soft‐tissue management facilitate preservation of peri‐implant hard and soft tissues, support optimal emergence profile development, and achieve stable, aesthetic outcomes.

Discussion

This clinical report documents the successful rehabilitation of a compromised anterior maxillary site using immediate implant placement combined with guided bone regeneration (GBR). The decision to extract the existing fixed dental prosthesis and its abutment teeth was based on unfavorable crown‐to‐root ratios, compromised restorative margins, cervical discoloration, and radicular caries—factors that have been consistently identified as predictors of long‐term failure for tooth‐supported restorations in the aesthetic zone, particularly when associated with periodontal breakdown and peri‐radicular bone loss. In such cases, continuation or extension of conventional prostheses can further compromise adjacent abutments and accelerate biological deterioration [16].

Immediate implant placement offers several recognized advantages, including reduced treatment time, fewer surgical interventions, and preservation of prosthetic space. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have demonstrated that, with strict case selection, atraumatic extraction, and prosthetically guided positioning, survival rates of immediate implants are comparable to those of delayed protocols. However, immediate placement alone does not prevent physiologic buccal bone resorption, particularly in compromised sockets, and may jeopardize soft‐tissue stability and aesthetic integration [9].

Importantly, contemporary implant success is no longer defined solely by implant survival but by aesthetic integration, which can be quantified using validated indices such as the Pink Aesthetic Score (PES) and White Aesthetic Score (WES). These standardized indices assess peri‐implant soft tissue and prosthetic integration, including papilla fill, mucosal contour, and crown morphology, and can be reliably evaluated using digital imaging techniques, including intraoral scanning, thus enhancing precision of aesthetic outcome assessment [1]. PES/WES values have been shown to correlate strongly with clinical and patient‐centered aesthetic outcomes in the anterior region [2]. Additionally, increased peri‐implant soft‐tissue thickness and supportive buccal bone have been associated with more favorable PES outcomes, highlighting the importance of meticulous hard‐ and soft‐tissue management to achieve predictable aesthetic success [17, 18].

For these reasons, GBR has become an integral component of immediate implant protocols in compromised extraction sites. The primary biological objectives of GBR are stabilization of the peri‐implant blood clot and exclusion of soft tissue from the regenerative space, thereby supporting ridge contour and buccal bone volume. Clinical and radiographic evidence indicates that simultaneous GBR significantly improves horizontal ridge stability and supports long‐term peri‐implant bone maintenance compared with immediate implant placement alone, particularly in sites with thin buccal bone walls or pre‐existing defects. Xenogeneic bone substitutes used in combination with resorbable collagen membranes provide space‐maintaining scaffolding and volumetric stability, contributing to enhanced hard‐ and soft‐tissue outcomes and reduced risk of soft‐tissue recession [3, 4].

Implant positioning remains a critical determinant of both biological and aesthetic success. Prosthetically guided placement, particularly with a slight palatal trajectory to avoid compressing the buccal plate, has been associated with improved peri‐implant soft‐tissue profiles and enhanced aesthetic outcomes. In this case, such positioning, combined with GBR, facilitated favorable tissue maturation and supported harmonious emergence profiles [5, 6].

From an aesthetic perspective, replacing the tooth–supported metal–ceramic restoration with implant‐supported single crowns eliminated cervical discoloration and allowed precise control of emergence profile contours. Appropriate implant diameter selection, avoidance of excessive buccal pressure and the use of contoured healing abutments facilitated guided soft‐tissue maturation. Recent evidence supports that individual single‐unit restorations in the anterior maxilla yield superior aesthetic integration by enabling independent shaping of peri‐implant soft tissues, without the biological limitations of compromised natural abutments [7, 19].

Of particular relevance in this case was the vertical soft tissue thickness and mucosal phenotype. Current evidence strongly supports the relationship between peri‐implant soft tissue thickness and long‐term crestal bone stability and aesthetic outcomes. A thick mucosal phenotype, as observed in this patient, has been associated with reduced marginal bone loss, lower risk of recession, and more favorable PES scores. These biological parameters were considered during treatment planning and contributed to the predictable aesthetic outcomes achieved. Provisional contouring was carried out progressively through modification of the removable prosthesis intaglio surface, allowing guided soft tissue maturation and controlled emergence profile development prior to definitive restoration delivery.

Contemporary literature indicates that long‐term success in the aesthetic zone depends less on the timing of implant placement per se and more on meticulous surgical execution, appropriate hard‐tissue augmentation, and prosthetic planning. Within this context, PES/WES serve as valuable objective outcome measures that reflect both biological stability and patient‐centred aesthetic success. The present clinical report supports immediate implant placement combined with GBR as a biologically sound and prosthodontically driven strategy for achieving stable peri‐implant tissues and favorable aesthetic outcomes in the anterior maxilla [15, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. As a limitation, the current report presents outcomes at the time of definitive restoration delivery without extended longitudinal follow‐up. Long‐term stability—particularly regarding crestal bone levels, soft tissue contour maintenance, and PES/WES evolution—will be evaluated through planned 1‐ and 3‐year follow‐up assessments. The authors acknowledge that short‐term aesthetic results, while promising, require longer observation to confirm stability and validate the protocol's predictability. Additionally, the absence of quantitative CBCT measurements of buccal bone thickness at baseline and follow‐up represents a methodological limitation; future reports should incorporate three‐dimensional radiographic analysis to objectively document tissue changes.

Pink Aesthetic Score (PES) and White Aesthetic Score (WES) evaluation—initial situation versus definitive restoration.

At baseline, the PES was 3/14, and the WES was 3/10, reflecting severe soft tissue compromise, papilla loss, cervical discoloration, and inadequate prosthetic integration associated with the failing metal–ceramic bridge. Following immediate implant placement combined with guided bone regeneration (GBR), atraumatic extraction, prosthetically guided positioning, and progressive emergence profile development, the PES improved to 12/14 and the WES to 9/10 at the definitive prosthetic stage, corresponding to an excellent aesthetic outcome by both indices. The single parameter that did not reach the maximum score in the final evaluation was soft tissue texture (PES: 1/2) and translucency/characterization (WES: 1/2), both attributable to inherent material limitations of the monolithic zirconia restorations and the early evaluation time point. These results support the predictability of the combined surgical and prosthetic protocol employed in rehabilitating this compromised anterior maxillary site [37].
