Exogenous Ochronosis With Vitiligo: A Therapeutic Challenge
Authors: Zhen Zhen Li, Chong Zhang
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

A 28‐year‐old woman presented with developed brownish‐yellow spots on the face 5 years ago after sun exposure. The lesions increased slowly, accompanied by erythema and itching. She was diagnosed with “melasma” and “skin melanosis”, and underwent treatments including intense pulsed light (IPL), picosecond laser, and chemical peels, but with no significant relief. After using hydroquinone cream externally, the condition significantly worsened, leading her to seek further treatment at our hospital, where she was diagnosed with exogenous ochronosis (EO). At the age of 5, she was diagnosed with vitiligo, hips and trunk scattered depigmented patches. She underwent treatments such as oral Chinese medicine, topical medications, and UV light therapy with poor outcomes, but later recovered on her own. Four years ago, she was diagnosed with extremely severe depression and is currently taking olanzapine, duloxetine hydrochloride, and zopiclone. Both her grandmother and cousin have vitiligo. When she first came to our department for treatment, partial depigmentation of the left eyelashes was visible (Figure 1a,b). Large brown‐black patches are present on the mid‐face and cheek areas bilaterally, with unclear boundaries (Figure 1a,b). The patches are smooth without roughness and exhibit telangiectasia (Figure 1c,d). Dermoscopic photograph shows scattered blue‐grey dots and globules that have a caviar‐like appearance (Figure 1e,f).

(c, d) Think view examination showed angiectasia. (e, f) Dermoscopic photograph shows scattered blue‐grey dots and globules that have a caviar‐like appearance. (g–l) After four times’ chemical peels and one IPL treatment, the patient's pigmentation and angiectasia were reduced.

EO typically presents as asymptomatic hyperpigmented macules, often located in areas exposed to the photo or zygomatic regions in a symmetrical pattern. The lesions are gray‐brown or blue‐black macules usually with caviar‐like papules [1]. The affected population often includes patients with a history of long‐term chloasma treatment [1]. EO can be secondary to the topical application of hydroquinone, phenol, or oral administration of antimalarials [2]. A preliminary diagnosis can be made based on the dermoscopic photograph [3] and exacerbation of hyperpigmentation after the use of skin depigmenting agents, which is an anomalous response [4]. This patient meets the diagnostic criteria for EO and had vitiligo since childhood; a literature search has revealed that there are currently no case reports of vitiligo combined with EO.

Considering the patient is diagnosed with EO and vitiligo, treatment has become exceedingly challenging. Despite individual case report on the use of intradermal VC injections for EO treatment [5], we are concerned that performing whitening injections on skin lesions could exacerbate vitiligo. The patient had previously undergone picosecond laser treatment with unsatisfactory results, so chemical peel with salicylic acid every 2–3 weeks is considered. IPL therapy may be considered at an appropriate time. Pretreatment assessments with think view and dermoscopy are to be conducted for each session, with regular follow‐up visits. After four times’ chemical peels and one IPL treatment, the patient's pigmentation and angiectasia were reduced (Figure 1g–j). Dermoscopy also showed improvement (Figure 1k,l).

Patients with vitiligo have abnormal skin pigment metabolism. For those with vitiligo combined with melasma and other hyperpigmentation disease, it suggests that we should exercise caution in using hydroquinone to avoid exacerbating the patient's condition. And salicylic acid peels may be safe for those diagnosed with EO and vitiligo.
