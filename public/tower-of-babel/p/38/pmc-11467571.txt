Introduction

A hydatid cyst is a condition resulting from parasitic infection caused by the larval form of Echinococcus granulosus, and in rare cases, Echinococcus multilocularis. The liver is the most commonly affected organ, accounting for 65–75 % of cases, while the lungs are affected in approximately 10–25 % of instances [1]. Genitourinary (GU) hydatidosis is an uncommon condition, typically associated with concurrent liver or pulmonary hydatid infection. The kidney is the most commonly affected GU organ in cases of hydatid disease (HD). However, pelvic HD is a rarer occurrence and can manifest with diverse clinical symptoms, sometimes leading to confusion with other pelvic cysts on imaging studies. An even more infrequent presentation is hydatid disease arising from the seminal vesicles, a condition that has been scarcely reported in the medical literature. It's crucial for healthcare professionals to carefully evaluate and differentiate these conditions to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management [2].

In this case study, we describe a 39-year-old male who had a previous history of a liver hydatid cysts two decades ago. Recently, he presented with a pelvic hydatid cyst originating from the seminal vesicle. Surgical intervention was employed to manage this condition.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old man referred to us after a large pelvic cyst was incidentally discovered during a transabdominal sonography examination. He had history of two liver hydatid cyst surgeries one 24 years ago and the other 18 years ago. Follow up transrectal ultrasound study revealed a well-defined isoechoic solid-multycystic lesion measuring 82*64*54 mm and 149 cc in volume, at posterior superior aspect of prostate which more probably originated from right seminal vesicle. Following three months of receiving medical treatment with Albendazole, the patient became a candidate for surgical intervention. Through the lower midline incision, a multicystic lesion of the seminal vesicle was discovered. This lesion was fully adhered to both the prostate gland and the vas deferens. To prevent injuring seminal vesicles and vas deferens which adhered severely in a young male, we decided to evacuate content of the cyst (daughter cysts) under sterilized condition. After placing a considerable amount of sterile gas soaked in chlorhexidine around the lesion, a small portion of the cyst wall, through which only the suction tube could pass, was opened and the contents were suctioned out and diluted. Chlorhexidine was replaced and removed after 15 min. There was no rupture of the cyst or leakage of its contents into the pelvic cavity during surgery.

During the six-month follow-up, the patient remained problem-free. He maintained potency and complete urinary continence. Additionally, the lesion decreased in size significantly in the subsequent ultrasound evaluation (Fig. 2). The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria. Discussion

Echinococcus granulosus is the causative agent responsible for hydatid disease, a parasitic infection. While the liver and lungs are the most frequent sites of infection, HD affecting the genitourinary system is rare, accounting for approximately 2–4 % of all hydatid infections [4]. Reported involvement includes the kidney, adrenal glands, and pelvic organs, such as the urinary bladder, seminal vesicle, fallopian tubes, prostate, and testis. Genitourinary hydatid disease manifests with a range of symptoms, spanning from complete asymptomatic cases to pelvic or lumbar pain, lower urinary symptoms, hematuria, and symptoms arising from the compression effect on adjacent organs [5,6].

Pelvic hydatid disease is uncommon and usually arises as a secondary manifestation associated with hepatic hydatid infection, similar to the case we presented, where the patient had undergone two hepatic surgeries long ago. However, primary involvement of pelvic organs without concurrent hydatid disease in other organs is even rarer, accounting for approximately 0.2–2 % of cases. Infection can disseminate to pelvic organs through peritoneal, hematogenous, or lymphatic spread. An exceptionally rare variant is retrovesical HD, which affects the genital organs in women (including ovaries and uterus) and the seminal vesicles in men [7].

Pelvic hydatid disease may exhibit imaging features similar to those of duplication cysts, genitourinary cystic neoplasms, bladder or vas deferens diverticula, ureterocele, teratomas, and other benign or malignant seminal vesicle cysts. However, when characteristic imaging findings of hydatid disease are present—such as daughter cysts, a detached endocyst, and multiple septations within a thick-walled larger cyst—diagnosis becomes easier. These features can be observed using ultrasound, CT scan, or MRI [5,6,8].

Surgical excision remains the preferred treatment for seminal vesicle HD. During surgery, extreme caution must be exercised to prevent cyst rupture or irritation of adjacent organs. While complete excision is ideal, in patients where the cyst adheres to adjacent organs, as in our case, partial cystectomy or cyst drainage should be considered. Pre-operative and post-operative medications, when combined with surgery, contribute to improved treatment outcomes. However, it's essential to note that medical treatments alone achieve effectiveness in approximately 30–40 % of patients [1,5,7,9]. In our case, with similar condition, we prescribed pre-operative medical treatment with Albendazole (10 mg/kg/day) for about 3 months and performed partial cystectomy due to significant adhesion of the cyst to adjacent structures and risk of pelvic organ injury. No intra or post-operative complication was encountered and we achieved to good outcomes during the patient's follow-up ultrasound.

Conclusion

Seminal vesicle hydatid cyst is a rare condition, but it should be considered when evaluating patients with retrovesical cysts, particularly in endemic regions. Drainage of hydatid cysts in a sterile situation has demonstrated efficiency and should be contemplated, especially in cases involving adjacent organ invasion.
