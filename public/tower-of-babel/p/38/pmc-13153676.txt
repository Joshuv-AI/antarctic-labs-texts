Machine Learning Models Incorporating Nursing Care Needs to Predict 180-Day Prognosis in Patients With Heart Failure
Authors: Takuya Nishino, Katsuhito Kato, Shuhei Tara, Daisuke Hayashi, Tomohisa Seki, Toru Takiguchi, Yoshiaki Kubota, Takeshi Yamamoto, Mitsunori Maruyama, Eitaro Kodani, Nobuaki Kobayashi, Akihiro Shirakabe, Toshiaki Otsuka, Shoji Yokobori, Yukihiro Kondo, Kuniya Asai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe number of patients with heart failure (HF) is increasing with aging of the population, resulting in a shift in care from hospitals to community settings. Although predicting medium-term prognosis after discharge could improve community-based management and reduce readmissions, no established model has integrated structured multidimensional assessments into HF prognostic modeling.Methods and ResultsThis multicenter study developed and validated machine learning (ML) models (i.e., logistic regression, random forest, extreme gradient boosting, and light gradient boosting) to predict 180-day mortality or emergency hospitalization in 4,904 patients with HF. Patients were randomly divided into training and validation sets (8 : 2). Nursing care needs, derived from structured nursing assessments that capture patients’ physical status and care dependency, were included as a predictive feature. All models demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance based on the area under the precision-recall curve, favorable calibration assessed by the calibration slope and Brier score, and effective risk stratification. The Shapley additive explanations algorithm identified nursing care needs as an important prognostic factor, alongside established laboratory variables for HF prognosis.ConclusionsML models incorporating nursing care needs effectively predicted the 180-day prognosis of patients with HF. The prominent contribution of nursing care needs underscores the value of incorporating structured multidimensional care-related information into prognostic modeling and highlights the importance of team-based post-discharge HF management.

Central Figure

Heart failure (HF), a global health challenge affecting an estimated 55.5 million people worldwide,1 contributes to an increasing number of hospitalizations.2,3 This trend has raised concerns about strained healthcare resources and rising medical costs, prompting a shift in HF management from hospital-based to community-based care.4 In this context, effective collaboration among hospitals, clinics, and community healthcare professionals is essential to ensure individualized and continuous care.5 Routine outpatient management is typically provided by primary care physicians and community healthcare teams, whereas hospital specialists perform periodic follow-up assessments. Sharing prognostic information across the care continuum facilitates data-driven decision-making to identify high-risk patients, coordinate timely interventions, and design tailored management plans, which may ultimately reduce unplanned readmissions among patients with HF.6

Machine learning (ML) models integrating multiple clinical factors have been increasingly used to predict outcomes in patients with HF and are expected to outperform conventional statistical methods in predictive accuracy.7–10 However, most existing ML models primarily rely on disease-specific pathophysiological factors, including laboratory data, electrocardiographic features, echocardiographic findings, and prescribed medications.11,12 In real-world HF care, a wide range of information is routinely collected by physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and other healthcare professionals. These data include a patient’s physical status, care dependency, medication burden, and social background, all of which may influence prognosis.13–16 Incorporating such multidisciplinary and care-related information into predictive modeling is crucial for improving the accuracy and clinical utility of medium-term prognostic prediction for HF management. Although studies have often relied on established activities of daily living (ADL) measures, including the Barthel index and the Katz ADL index, to assess the physical status of patients with HF,17–19 these scales mainly capture physical assistance needs and do not fully reflect the broader aspects of care dependency routinely evaluated in clinical nursing practice. In contrast, the nursing care needs used in the present study provide integrated assessment encompassing assistance with physical activities, comprehension of medical instructions, and behavioral risks, thereby serving as a practical indicator of a patient’s care dependency in routine clinical settings. Notably, despite the clinical relevance of these structured and multidimensional nursing assessments, no existing HF prognostic model has incorporated indicators that directly capture patients’ care dependency, a crucial yet overlooked determinant of post-discharge outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to develop and validate ML models that integrate structured multidimensional nursing assessments, specifically nursing care needs, into the prediction of 180-day post-discharge prognosis in patients with HF. Unlike most previous studies that focused on short-term outcomes (e.g., 30-day readmission),10 our models targeted medium-term outcomes essential for post-discharge risk stratification and multidisciplinary care planning.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted using an inpatient database from 4 affiliated hospitals of Nippon Medical School, namely Nippon Medical School Hospital, Musashi-Kosugi Hospital, Tama Nagayama Hospital, and Chiba Hokusoh Hospital. The database was constructed using Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) data and laboratory test values from medical records. The data for this study were accessed on July 21, 2024. The authors had no access to any personally identifiable information during or after data collection.

This study was approved by the Central Ethics Review Committee of Nippon Medical School (Approval no. M-2024-178) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained using an opt-out method, in accordance with the retrospective observational design of this study. Reporting followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis guidelines.20

The study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000054854).

Patient Selection and Endpoints

Patients with HF in this study were aged ≥18 years and had undergone emergency hospitalization in the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine or the Cardiovascular Intensive Care Unit at 1 of 4 Nippon Medical School-affiliated hospitals between April 2018 and September 2023. To be eligible for inclusion, patients also required a B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) concentration ≥100 pg/mL or an N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentration ≥300 pg/mL during hospitalization, in accordance with international HF definitions.21 Patients were excluded if their hospital stay was <5 days, if they died in hospital or were transferred to another hospital, or if they had <180 days of follow-up for endpoint ascertainment. The study endpoint was a composite of all-cause mortality and emergency hospitalization within 180 days of discharge. This composite outcome was chosen to capture clinically meaningful post-discharge deterioration requiring urgent medical attention, because both events represent critical failures of outpatient stability in HF care and are commonly addressed through the same post-discharge management strategies.

Variables

The DPC database collected data on all hospitalized patients, including demographics (age, sex), physical metrics (height, weight, body mass index [BMI; calculated by dividing body weight, in kilograms, by height, in meters, squared]), and clinical details (prior emergency hospitalization, comorbidities and procedures during hospitalization, and medications at discharge).22 Prior emergency hospitalizations were defined as those that occurred within 180 days before the index admission. Medications at discharge recommended by guidelines for HF and coronary artery disease were defined as guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT)23–25 (Supplementary Table 1). Medications other than those included in GDMT were defined as non-GDMT (ni-GDMT) medications. We focused on the number of ni-GDMT medications representing comorbidity burden and polypharmacy.

The nursing care needs score is a structured, multidimensional measure that reflects a patient’s physical status, degree of care dependency, and behavioral risk, as routinely assessed by nursing staff during hospitalization.22 This score is calculated as the sum of scores assigned to the levels of assistance required for turning over, transferring, oral hygiene, eating, and changing clothes (0 points, no assistance; 1 point, partial assistance; 2 points, full assistance), comprehension of medical instructions (0 points, yes; 1 point, no), and the presence of risky behavior (0 points, no; 2 points, yes), as detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Blood test results at admission and discharge were obtained from medical records and were defined as the first sample within 3 days of admission and the last sample within 14 days before discharge, respectively. After applying the inclusion criteria, BNP values were converted to NT-proBNP using the following formula:26

NT-proBNP = 10(1.1 × log10[BNP] + 0.57)

Numerical data were scaled using the StandardScaler method.

Imputation of Missing Data

Missing data for BMI and blood test findings were imputed using a single imputation method implemented in the Python MissForest package.27 All variables were included in the analysis, because the missing rate for all variables was <20%, which is the threshold for exclusion.

Predictive Model Development

We randomly divided the dataset into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%) using stratified sampling to preserve the endpoint occurrence rates of the original population. We used the following ML algorithms as predictive models: conventional logistic regression (LR), tree-based random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), and light gradient boosting machine (LGBM).28–30

In this study, we used recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) for each model to select the optimal subset of features specific to each model. RFECV is a robust method that recursively eliminates less important features and builds a model with the remaining features to identify the optimal subset. Specifically, RFECV was implemented for each model using a 10-fold stratified cross-validation strategy to maximize the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). After selecting the most relevant features, hyperparameter tuning was performed for each model using a grid search with cross-validation to determine the optimal model parameters. This step involved evaluating various combinations of hyperparameters and ultimately selecting the combination that maximized the AUROC for each model (Supplementary Table 3). The optimization process (Supplementary Table 4) identified the hyperparameter set with the highest AUROC for each model. All models were calibrated using isotonic regression.31 Feature importance was assessed using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values,32 which quantify the contribution of each feature to model predictions.

Model Validation

The bootstrap method was applied to the test set to evaluate the performance of the predictive models. In all, 2,500 bootstrap resamples were generated to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each performance metric. The discriminatory ability of the model was assessed using AUROC and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC).33,34 For calibration analysis, the predicted probabilities were divided into 10 deciles, and the mean predicted and observed outcome probabilities were plotted for each bin. The following 2 indices were calculated to evaluate calibration: (1) the calibration slope, which indicates the agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes; and (2) the Brier score, which measures the accuracy of the probability predictions. A calibration slope closer to 1 and a Brier score closer to 0 indicate ideal model performance.

Risk Classification and Survival Analysis

For risk stratification, probabilities predicted by ML models were classified into 3 categories. Given that the probability of rehospitalization within 1 year for patients with HF is approximately 30%,35,36 the categories were defined as low (<0.15), middle (>0.15 and <0.30), and high (≥0.30) risk. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method; the log-rank test was used to compare survival distributions between the stratified groups.

Software and Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.9.0 (Python Software Foundation; www.python.org) and R software version 4.2.2 Patched (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were 2-tailed, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The complete source code, for data preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, model evaluation, and SHAP analysis, is publicly available at GitHub (https://github.com/Takuya-Nishino/heart-failure-ml/).

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

Among 9,519 patients initially identified, we excluded 905 who died during hospitalization, 1,402 who were transferred to other facilities, 466 with a hospital stay of <5 days, and 1,842 without events and with <180 days of follow-up. Therefore, 4,904 patients were included in the final analysis, with 3,923 (80%) patients assigned to the training dataset and 981 (20%) assigned to the test dataset (Figure 1). Evaluation of Prediction Models

After model training, the predictive performance of the ML models was evaluated using the test dataset. The AUROC values for LR, RF, XGB, and LGBM were 0.734 (95% CI 0.698–0.769), 0.753 (95% CI 0.718–0.786), 0.744 (95% CI 0.708–0.778), and 0.752 (95% CI 0.716–0.785), respectively (Figure 2A). To further assess discriminative performance in the context of imbalanced data, AUPRC was calculated. The AUPRC values for LR, RF, XGB, and LGBM were 0.505 (95% CI 0.445–0.568), 0.513 (95% CI 0.454–0.579), 0.524 (95% CI 0.463–0.589), and 0.515 (95% CI 0.454–0.582), respectively (Figure 2B). Model Interpretation

The contribution of each feature to the prediction of outcomes was visualized using the SHAP algorithm; features were ranked in descending order of importance, with red indicating greater influence and blue indicating lesser influence on the predicted outcome (see Figure 3). Established risk factors for HF, including age, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), NT-proBNP, hemoglobin, and albumin concentrations, and the number of previous hospitalizations, ranked among the top contributors across all 4 models. Notably, the nursing care needs score, a key feature emphasized in this study, was identified as a highly influential predictor of outcomes, alongside the number of ni-GDMT medications, which likely reflect the burden of comorbidities and polypharmacy (Figure 3). According to the SHAP analysis, the nursing care needs score contributed 7.72%, 4.50%, 4.03%, and 5.92% to the outcome prediction in LR, RF, XGB, and LGBM, respectively. In the SHAP interaction heatmap, neither the nursing care needs score nor the number of ni-GDMT medications exhibited a strong interaction with other features in the LGBM model, suggesting that these features contributed independently to the model’s predictions (Figure 4). Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study, we developed and validated ML models incorporating nursing care needs to predict 180-day all-cause mortality or emergency hospitalization after discharge in patients with HF. The novelty of this study lies in integrating nursing care needs, a structured, multidimensional indicator of functional vulnerability and care dependency, into HF prognostic modeling. Unlike conventional ADL-based evaluations, nursing care needs additionally capture patients’ comprehension of instructions and behavioral risks, thereby offering a more comprehensive representation of a patient’s post-discharge risk profile.

The discriminative performance of our models was consistent with previously reported AUROC values (0.65–0.78) for long-term outcome prediction in patients with HF.8 “Discrimination” refers to the ability of a predictive model to correctly distinguish between positive and negative cases. In this study, the tree-based ML models exhibited discriminative performance that was equal to or superior to that of the conventional LR model, although these differences were not statistically significant. This finding is consistent with the concept that tree-based approaches, which can capture complex non-linear relationships, are more suitable than LR models that assume linearity, particularly because some features, including eGFR and BMI, exhibit U-shaped associations with outcomes.37,38

The ML models in this study exhibited favorable calibration, indicating close agreement between the predicted probabilities and the observed outcomes. Calibration is a key measure of how well a model’s predicted risks align with actual event rates, and it is particularly important for risk stratification and clinical applicability of predictive models.39,40 However, many predictive models for HF have been evaluated primarily using discrimination metrics, with limited assessment of calibration performance.12,41 In the present study, the observed event rates in each risk stratum (low, middle, and high risk) fell within the corresponding predicted probability ranges, suggesting that appropriate risk stratification for medium-term prognosis was achieved at the time of hospital discharge. Poorly calibrated models can be problematic because they lead to over- or underestimation of risk,42 thereby reducing their clinical reliability. Isotonic regression, a well-established calibration algorithm, was applied in this study.31,43 This approach ensured adequate calibration, as demonstrated by the calibration plots, and effectively mitigated the risk of systematic over- or underestimation. Nevertheless, a recent study proposed advanced calibration algorithms based on neural networks, suggesting their potential to further improve predictive precision.44

In SHAP analysis, the nursing care needs score and number of ni-GDMT medications were identified as highly influential predictors across all models, together with established prognostic factors for HF.45,46 This finding suggests that daily physical activity and care dependency, captured by the nursing care needs score, and medication complexity, reflecting comorbidity burden and polypharmacy, play crucial roles in determining the prognosis of patients with HF. Nevertheless, in this study, only the nursing care needs score was available to represent patients’ physical activity and care dependency, because our retrospectively collected dataset did not include other indicators of the patients’ physical condition. In our study, incorporating the nursing care needs score allowed the model to capture aspects of care dependency and functional vulnerability that are not reflected in conventional physiological variables. This variable, derived from structured nursing records within the DPC system, likely captured elements of patient frailty, cognitive status, and behavioral risk that are not reflected by physiological or laboratory measures alone. These findings support the integration of multidisciplinary care information, particularly nurse-assessed indicators, into future prognostic frameworks for HF management, facilitating more holistic and clinically meaningful risk stratification. Notably, modifications to the set of prognostic variables during model development, including the addition or exclusion of nursing care needs, resulted in minimal changes in global discrimination metrics. This pattern suggests that individual predictors provide overlapping and complementary information and that further gains in predictive performance may require capturing temporal changes in these factors rather than relying solely on static measurements at discharge.

We constructed the dataset for this study using DPC and clinical laboratory data, which are automatically stored in an electronic format and can be readily exported. The use of DPC and electronic medical records has been standardized across Japanese acute-care hospitals, and several clinical studies have used similar databases.47–49 Therefore, our results are generalizable and can be easily validated using data from other institutions. However, our dataset included only inpatients from an acute-care hospital offering advanced and specialized treatments, and therefore did not fully capture HF care delivered in community settings through hospital–clinic collaboration. Incorporating clinical data from primary care physicians and patient information, including social background, physical activity, and living conditions, recorded by healthcare professionals other than physicians, could facilitate the development of a more precise model for predicting HF progression. Building such an integrated regional database connecting hospitals, clinics, and community care teams will be the next challenge. To enhance the clinical applicability of our ML-based prognostic models, future studies should evaluate whether risk-guided interventions based on these predictions can improve patient outcomes. One promising approach is to conduct a prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trial wherein ML-derived risk scores are shared with a multidisciplinary care team, which then tailors post-discharge management strategies according to individual risk profiles. Comparing outcomes between an intervention group receiving ML-guided care and a control group receiving standard care would provide direct evidence supporting the clinical utility of the model. In clinical practice, even risk stratification models with moderate discrimination but good calibration and interpretability can still support decision-making, particularly by identifying patients who may benefit from intensified post-discharge follow-up or multidisciplinary interventions.

This study has some limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis based on routinely collected DPC claims data and automatically extracted electronic medical record data, important information including echocardiographic and electrocardiographic findings, vital signs, and social factors was unavailable. These missing variables may have influenced the model’s predictive performance. Second, the models were static, relying solely on variables collected during hospitalization, and could not update risk estimates because patients’ clinical conditions changed over time. Although this design reflects routine clinical workflows, incorporating time-updated variables or longitudinal data in future analyses may improve the model’s prognostic accuracy. Third, because the nursing care needs score is derived from routinely collected clinical assessments, some degree of variability may exist due to differences between evaluators or institutional practices, which could influence the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the study cohort was limited to patients with complete follow-up in Japanese acute-care hospitals. Thus, although comparability across similar institutions is high, the generalizability of our findings to community-based settings, primary-care environments, or other healthcare systems remains uncertain. Because this study was based on DPC data from acute-care hospitals in Japan, the applicability of the proposed models to other countries or healthcare systems may depend on differences in data availability, healthcare delivery structures, and clinical practice patterns, and external validation in diverse settings will be necessary. Finally, HF diagnosis was based on either an NT-proBNP threshold of ≥300 pg/mL or a BNP threshold of ≥100 pg/mL. Because these thresholds are not directly equivalent, some degree of misclassification is possible, and the conversion formula used during model development may have introduced errors.50

Conclusions

For patients with HF, ML models incorporating nursing care needs as a multidimensional measure effectively predicted all-cause mortality and emergency hospitalization within 180 days after discharge. These models exhibited favorable discrimination and calibration, enabling reliable risk stratification for individual patients. These findings identify care dependency as a missing link in HF prognostic prediction and emphasize the need to incorporate structured nursing assessments into future risk-stratification strategies and multidisciplinary care planning.
