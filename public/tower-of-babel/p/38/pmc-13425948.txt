The severity of fatigue, daytime sleepiness, and health-related quality of life, and their impact among community pharmacists in Malaysia
Authors: Rajkannah Poongan, Pravinkumar Vishwanath Ingle, Priya Manirajan, Shazia Jamshed, Palanisamy Sivanandy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth-related quality of life (HRQoL) is essential for all individuals, particularly those involved in patient care. Healthcare providers, especially community pharmacists, have been at the forefront during emergencies. The COVID-19 pandemic substantially affected healthcare professionals’ HRQoL due to increased workloads, emotional strain, and physical and psychosocial challenges while delivering essential services. Although the pandemic has subsided, these challenges persist globally. Therefore, this study assessed fatigue severity, daytime sleepiness, and HRQoL among Malaysian community pharmacists to evaluate their physical, mental, and social well-being.MethodsA nationwide, prospective, cross-sectional study was conducted among 209 community pharmacists across Malaysia, using the RAND Short Form-36 questionnaire for HRQoL assessment, the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) to evaluate fatigue, and the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) to assess daytime sleepiness. A structured, validated questionnaire consisting of 64 questions was used, and data were collected through convenience and snowball sampling methods.ResultsOf the 209 pharmacists who participated, 46% were aged between 31 and 40 years, and 88% were female. Regarding HRQoL, 33.4% rated their health as “excellent” and 37.8% as “very good,” while 20.1% reported their health had worsened compared to the previous year. A significant portion of participants (36.8%) reported limitations in physical activity, and half of them reduced their work hours. Emotional health issues affected 52.6% of participants’ performance, and 35.9% experienced health-related interference with social activities. Additionally, 67.5% reported severe fatigue, and 67.9% showed excessive daytime sleepiness.ConclusionMalaysian community pharmacists are significantly impacted by fatigue, emotional exhaustion, daytime sleepiness, and physical strain. These challenges affect their overall well-being and productivity. The results underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions on staffing, workload distribution, working hours, and adequate breaks to improve working conditions and support systems for pharmacists, aiming to enhance both their personal health and the quality of healthcare services they provide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14828-0.

Introduction

Community pharmacists are critical healthcare professionals who are at the forefront of delivering a variety of services that enhance public health. Globally, community pharmacists are actively involved in providing various patient care services, including dispensing medication, administering medication therapy, counselling for chronic illnesses, immunizations, health assessments, and patient education [1–3]. In Malaysia, community pharmacists are actively engaged in public health initiatives such as managing chronic conditions like hypertension and diabetes, weight management, and smoking cessation programs [4–6]. They also play a key role in promoting the rational and appropriate use of drugs and mitigating errors in pharmaceutical administration.

Community pharmacists face a demanding work environment characterized by heavy duties, long working hours, and the need for meticulous attention to prevent drug errors. Their Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) may be negatively affected by the physical and mental stress of their occupation. Research has shown that pharmacists often experience high levels of work-related stress, leading to exhaustion, reduced job satisfaction, and adverse health outcomes [5–7].

The challenges faced by community pharmacists have been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Pharmacists have been at the forefront of the epidemic, providing essential services such as administering COVID-19 vaccinations, counselling patients, and dispensing medications. The increased workload burden and fear of contracting the virus have significantly impacted their mental health and overall quality of life (QoL). These issues persist post-pandemic, highlighting the need for comprehensive research to identify and address factors influencing the HRQoL of community pharmacists [2, 8].

HRQoL is a comprehensive assessment that evaluates an individual’s overall health condition. The demanding nature of healthcare professionals’ duties may have a significant impact on their personal health and general QoL. HRQoL assessments provide valuable insights into the influence of factors such as job satisfaction, stress levels, and the work environment on individuals’ overall health [3, 4]. Despite the critical role community pharmacists play in healthcare, there is a lack of comprehensive studies specifically investigating their HRQoL. Previous research has primarily focused on isolated aspects of their occupation, such as job satisfaction and fatigue, without providing a holistic view of their overall well-being. Hence, a study was conducted to determine the HRQoL among community pharmacists in Malaysia, assess the work-related complications and health issues experienced by community pharmacists, and identify the factors associated with the quality of life of community pharmacists in Malaysia.

Methods and materials

Study design, population, and sample recruitment

A cross-sectional study was conducted among practicing community pharmacists in Malaysia using a validated structured questionnaire from March 2024 to February 2025. Malaysian fully registered pharmacists (FRP) working in community pharmacies nationwide and willing to participate were included in the study. Provisionally registered pharmacists (PRP), pharmacist assistants, and individuals who did not meet the survey’s inclusion criteria or were unwilling to participate were excluded. There were approximately 7,000 community pharmacists in Malaysia. Based on sample size calculations, 383 participants were required; after accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the target sample size was 421. However, only 209 pharmacists completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 49.64%.

Study instruments

The study instrument had four sections to analyse the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents, their HRQoL, Fatigue Severity, and daytime sleepiness. A total of 64 questions, prepared in English and embedded in a Google Form, were used to gather the information from the participants.

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [9–11]

The RAND Short Form (SF)-36 HRQoL validated questionnaire was used to assess the HRQoL of community pharmacists. There are eight scales that the SF-36 evaluates: Physical functioning (PF), role physical (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role emotional (RE), and mental health (MH). SF-36 measures two different concepts: a mental dimension represented by the Mental Component Summary (MCS) and a physical dimension represented by the Physical Component Summary (PCS). The PCS and MCS assessments are scored differently based on the contribution of each scale. Each of the eight SF-36 characteristics was assigned a raw score ranging from 0 (worst possible health status) to 100 (highest possible health status) by summing the item values. The raw score was then recalculated using specific methods for each dimension, as suggested in the RAND. Common scoring methods were used to determine the PCS and MCS scores. The validity and reliability of the SF-36 questionnaire were evaluated. Split-half reliability was calculated by correlating the scores of the odd and even halves in each SF-36 dimension. An internal consistency was considered acceptable with a Cronbach’s α score of 0.7 or higher.

Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) [12–14]

The effects of fatigue on individuals were assessed using the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS). Respondents to the brief FSS questionnaire must score their degree of weariness. Nine items on the FSS questionnaire rank the intensity of symptoms related to exhaustion. The score for each statement goes from 1 to 7, depending on how well it describes the person’s situation over the previous week and how much they agree or disagree with the statement as it pertains to them. A high number (such as 7) denotes strong agreement with the statement, whereas a low value (such as 1) denotes significant dissent. A total score of 36 or more indicates that the person may require additional testing by a doctor, while a score of less than 36 indicates that the person may not be experiencing weariness. But one shouldn’t use the scale to determine their diagnosis. The FSS consistently shows excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values typically ranging from 0.88 to over 0.94.

Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [15–17]

The general level of daytime drowsiness was assessed using the ESS questionnaire. Participants rated their likelihood of nodding off or falling asleep in various everyday scenarios. Each item in the ESS questionnaire was rated from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating no chance of nodding off or falling asleep, and 3 indicating a high likelihood. An overall score of less than 10 suggests minimal daytime tiredness. A score of 10 or higher indicates the need for further evaluation by a doctor to determine the cause of excessive daytime drowsiness and potential sleep issues. The ESS shows high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values typically ranging from 0.74 to 0.88.

Data collection, analysis, and ethics approval

The research received approval from the IMU Joint Committee on Research and Ethics with approval number MPP-1-2024(07). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the study, and all gathered information was kept confidential. The study was initially planned for six months using convenience sampling, but the response was low; hence, it was extended to one year, and the response rate was improved due to the adaptation of the snowball sampling method. Eligible individuals received study information, consent forms, and questionnaires through their registered email IDs with the Pharmacy Board of Malaysia. Two reminders were sent to complete the questionnaire, one at the end of the first week and another at the end of the second week. A pilot study was conducted before the main study to assess the face and content validity of the questionnaire, as well as to evaluate the feasibility of the study. The completed questionnaires were assigned to random code numbers, screened for accuracy, and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28.0. The responses are presented with descriptive statistics using frequency, percentage, mean, and SD. A p-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

Of the 421 eligible community pharmacists who were approached, 209 responded by completing the study questionnaire, giving a response rate of 49.64%. Most participants were aged 31–40 years (n = 96; 46%) and predominantly female (n = 184; 88%), Malay (n = 73; 34.90%) or Chinese (n = 87; 41.70%), with bachelor’s degrees (n = 91; 43.50%), had ≤ 5 years of work experience (n = 149; 71.30%), practiced in urban areas (n = 131; 62.70%), particularly in chain pharmacies (n = 79; 37.80%), working more than 45 h per week (n = 122; 58.37%). Health-related quality of life among the participants

The assessment of health-related quality of life using the RAND 36-Item Health Survey 1.0 Questionnaire revealed that about 33.4% (n = 70) rated their health as “excellent,” 37.8% (n = 79) as “very good,” 12% (n = 25) as “good”, 11.50% (n = 24) as “Fair”, and 5.30% (n = 11) as “poor”. The mean general health score was 2.17 (SD: 1.17), reflecting positive health among the participants. However, when comparing health to one year ago, only 10.5% (n = 22) felt their health was “much better,” 44% (n = 92) felt “somewhat better”, 16.80% (n = 35) felt “same”, 20.10% (n = 42) felt “somewhat worse” and 8.60% (n = 18) felt “much worse now than one year ago”. The mean score was 2.72 (SD: 1.15), suggesting overall somewhat better health among the participants than a year ago.

The analysis of how much their daily routine activities are affected/limited by their health now revealed that activities requiring vigorous effort, such as running or lifting heavy objects, were significantly limited for 36.8% (n = 77) of participants. Even moderate activities, such as moving a table (n = 73; 34.90%), climbing stairs (n = 83; 39.70%), or bending (n = 79; 37.80%), also showed limitations. The details are presented in Table 2.

Effect of health on daily routine activities (n = 209)

The examination of how physical and emotional health affected their work performance over the past four weeks revealed that around 50.7% (n = 106) reported cutting down time spent on work or other activities, and a similar proportion mentioned having difficulty performing the work or other activities, while 48.3% (n = 101) accomplished less than desired due to physical health. Similarly, emotional issues like anxiety or depression led to reduced work productivity for 52.6% (n = 110). The details are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

The assessment of the extent to which their health problems interfered with their social activities with family, friends, neighbours, or groups, and work during the past 4 weeks revealed that around 35.9% (n = 75) reported significant disruption to social activities due to health problems, while 73.7% (n = 154) experienced moderate to very severe bodily pain. Pain also interfered with normal work in 94.3% of participants (n = 197). The details are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

The assessment of how they feel and how things have been with them during the past 4 weeks revealed that 36.8% (n = 77) reported feeling full of energy most of the time, while 36.4% (n = 76) often felt tired or worn out. Feelings of nervousness (n = 71; 33.9%) and downheartedness (n = 74; 35.9%) were common, indicating emotional distress. Despite this, many participants felt calm (n = 80; 38.3%) and happy (n = 76; 36.4%) at least some of the time. The details are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

During the past 4 weeks, how much of the time physical health or emotional problems interfered with their social activities has revealed that about 35.9% (n = 75) of participants indicated “most of the time” interfered with social activities, while 31.2% (n = 65) experienced this “all the time”, 10% (n = 21) experienced “some of the time”, 14.8% (n = 31) experienced “a little of the time”. Whereas 8.1% (n = 17) did not experience at all. The mean score was 2.33 (SD: 1.28).

The assessment of their self-perceived health revealed that 40.7% (n = 85) believe they are as healthy as their peers, yet 36.8% (n = 77) expect their health to deteriorate in the future. While 37.8% (n = 79) described their health as “excellent”. The details are presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Fatigue significantly impacted physical functioning, with 34.9% (n = 73) identifying it as one of their most disabling symptoms, 34.4% (n = 72) felt demotivated due to fatigue, and had interference with carrying out certain duties and responsibilities. Discussion

The demographic profile showed that most participants were over 30 years old, female, and Chinese, consistent with previous Malaysian studies reporting a mean age of 35.33 ± 12.94 [18], female predominance (n = 159; 61.2%) [19], and a higher proportion of Chinese pharmacists (n = 67; 69.8% and n = 190; 73.1%) [18, 19]. The increasing feminization of pharmacy, often attributed to its perceived flexibility, raises concerns about gender-specific challenges, as female pharmacists report higher burnout and work–life imbalance due to caregiving expectations [20]. Gender-sensitive workplace policies, including flexible hours and parental leave, have been recommended to address these issues [21]. The ethnic distribution reflected Malaysia’s multicultural society, aligning with evidence that workforce diversity enhances cultural competence and organizational performance [18–22].

Most participants held a bachelor’s degree, while a considerable proportion had a master’s degree, consistent with prior findings where 87.3% held a bachelor’s qualification (n = 227) [19]. This trend reflects the growing emphasis on advanced education to support expanded clinical roles in pharmacy practice [19–23]. The majority of respondents had five or fewer years of community pharmacy experience, aligning with earlier studies reporting less than five years of experience (n = 48; 50.5% and n = 190; 73.5%) [18, 19]. This predominance of early-career pharmacists raises workforce retention concerns, as limited numbers of experienced pharmacists may affect mentorship and continuity of care [24]. Most participants worked full-time in chain pharmacies, consistent with previous reports of chain employment (n = 75; 59%) and full-time work status (n = 72; 57%) [20], reflecting the dominance of corporate pharmacy models and reduced opportunities for independent ownership [1, 19, 25].

Most pharmacists practiced in urban settings, consistent with findings that 80.3% (n = 395) worked in urban areas [26]. Rural areas continue to face access barriers to pharmaceutical care. Most participants served 100–200 clients daily, similar to earlier reports where 53.5% served over 100 clients per day (n = 139) [19], indicating high workloads. Additionally, most worked more than 45 h per week, exceeding the limits set by Malaysia’s Employment (Amendment) Act 2022 [1, 18–22, 26], which may contribute to poorer health and well-being among community pharmacists [1, 22, 26]. Sociodemographic characteristics, particularly gender, age, working hours, client load, and workplace setting, play a significant role in job satisfaction as well as physical and mental well-being. Addressing these factors is essential to improving the overall health, HRQoL, and well-being of working individuals.

In the present study, one-third of respondents rated their health as excellent, while an equal proportion rated it as very good. Few participants reported feeling much better than a year ago, whereas another one-third perceived their health as somewhat worse. These findings are consistent with earlier studies highlighting the role of subjective health perceptions in predicting health outcomes (β = 0.03, t = 5.2, p < 0.001) [27]. In contrast, more than half of the pharmacists (n = 69; 50.7%) in a study reported having unhealthy general health [28]. A positive self-assessment often correlates with better physical and mental health, while individuals reporting declining health may experience higher levels of stress and reduced functionality [27].

In the current study, one-third of participants experienced significant limitations in vigorous activities such as running or lifting heavy objects, while half of the study participants reported slight limitations. This finding is similar to a study that showed low physical activity among 35.3% of pharmacists, with longer work hours significantly associated with lower physical activity (p = 0.009) [28]. These responses suggest that physical health challenges are common, particularly with aging or chronic conditions. Research indicates that promoting regular physical activity can mitigate these limitations and improve overall HRQoL [29]. However, barriers such as fatigue, pain, and lack of access to exercise resources must be addressed to encourage participation [29, 30].

An analysis of physical health problems interfering with work or other daily activities revealed that more than half of the participants reported reducing the time spent on activities. This finding is similar to a qualitative study stating that pharmacists who are physically and mentally healthy are engaged in more effective pharmaceutical care services [31]. The high prevalence of these limitations underscores the significant impact of chronic health issues on productivity and quality of life. Studies suggest that workplace accommodations, such as flexible scheduling and ergonomic interventions, can alleviate these barriers [32].

In the present study, emotional health problems adversely affected productivity, with more than half of participants reporting reduced time spent on work or activities and difficulty performing tasks as carefully as usual. This finding aligns with previous research showing that 51% (95% CI 38–65%) [33] and 73% (n = 219) [34] of pharmacists experienced burnout in their workplace. It underscores the connection between emotional health and daily functioning. Poor emotional well-being, often worsened by stress and burnout, can result in significant productivity declines. Interventions like counselling and stress management programs play a crucial role in addressing these issues [35].

The evaluation of social functioning, based on how physical or emotional health impacts individuals’ social activities, showed that about one-third of respondents reported moderate interference with social activities, while a few experienced extreme interference. Severe and moderate pain levels were identified as key factors in disrupting social activities. Previous studies have shown that individuals with poor physical and psychological Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) tend to have smaller social networks (mean ± SD; 7.1 ± 5.7) [30]. Additionally, multisite pain (33%) and neuropathic pain (32%) can limit social interactions [36]. These findings underscore the significant influence of pain and emotional health on social interactions. Chronic pain, in particular, is linked to social withdrawal and a reduced quality of life.

In the present study, more than one-third of participants reported feeling calm and peaceful most of the time, with an equal proportion indicating feeling happy. However, a similar proportion also reported feeling downhearted and blue. These findings are consistent with an earlier study in which half of the pharmacists felt downhearted and blue (mean ± SD; 1.47 ± 0.75) [37]. These results highlight the dual nature of emotional experiences in the population. Positive emotional states contribute to resilience and coping, while negative emotions are often linked to mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety [38].

Studies have consistently shown that physical and emotional health domains are critical predictors of overall HRQoL. Physical limitations, role impairments, and pain have emerged as significant challenges, while emotional well-being and social functioning have highlighted areas for intervention. The findings emphasize the need for holistic approaches to health care, integrating physical rehabilitation, mental health support, and community engagement [35, 36, 38].

Fatigue severity assessment revealed that more than one-third of the participants feel less motivated when they are fatigued. Studies have consistently shown that high fatigue levels are associated with reduced motivational states (n = 26; 20%), lack of time (n = 64; 50%), and lack of financial incentives (n = 32, 25%) [20]. Exercise-induced fatigue is a common concern, especially in populations with limited physical resilience. The high agreement rate underscores the need for tailored exercise programs and fatigue management strategies. Literature suggests that graded exercise therapy can improve energy levels without exacerbating fatigue [39].

In the present study, a significant proportion of the participants experience a general susceptibility to fatigue. Many believe their fatigue interferes with their physical functioning, limiting their ability to engage in physical activity, preventing them from sustaining physical functioning, and believe that fatigue is one of the top three most disabling symptoms. Research indicates that factors such as stress, sleep disturbances, and medical comorbidities contribute to heightened fatigue susceptibility. Sustained physical functioning requires energy and endurance, both of which are compromised in individuals with high levels of fatigue. This aligns with studies reporting decreased exercise tolerance and physical capacity in fatigued individuals [40, 41]. Research has highlighted the importance of workplace accommodations, including flexible schedules and reduced workloads, for managing fatigue-related impairments [42].

The overall FSS scores revealed that more than two-thirds of the current study population are suffering from clinically significant fatigue. The high prevalence of significant fatigue within the cohort aligns with global trends [20, 39–42]. A holistic approach is essential to fatigue management that addresses both personal and professional domains [39, 43, 44].

The assessment of daytime sleepiness revealed that the majority of participants experienced moderate to high chances of dozing while sitting and reading, as well as while watching television. This finding aligns with a previous study, which indicates that all healthcare employees (n = 130; 100%) [45] exhibited moderate to severe excessive daytime sleepiness. These findings suggest that poor sleep quality makes individuals more prone to drowsiness during quiet, sedentary activities. Watching television, especially for extended periods or late at night, often triggers sleepiness due to monotonous visual and auditory stimulation. The high percentage reporting moderate drowsiness aligns with findings in studies where screen time is linked to fatigue and reduced alertness [46, 47].

More than half of the participants feel like dozing off when sitting inactive in a public place, such as a theater or a meeting. This scenario reflects the effect of environmental inactivity on sleep propensity. More than three-fourths feel dozed off while sitting as a passenger in a car for an hour without a break or lying down to rest in the afternoon. Prolonged car rides can exacerbate sleepiness due to repetitive motion and limited activity, and afternoon rest is often linked to natural circadian rhythms. The high chance of dozing underscores the importance of addressing sleep health among individuals who frequently commute or travel long distances and have inadequate sleep practices [48, 49].

The overall ESS scores revealed that the majority of participants suffering from excessive daytime sleepiness require further medical evaluation. The proportion of individuals with elevated ESS scores aligns with global data highlighting the prevalence of sleep-related issues and the need for a comprehensive intervention programme to minimize daytime sleepiness among healthcare workers [50]. Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed that the age, gender, role in pharmacy services, pharmacy location, client load, and weekly working hours of the present study individuals were strongly correlated with their HRQoL, fatigue severity, and excessive daytime sleepiness. These findings highlight the need for strategic interventions to optimize workflow, workload, client volume, and weekly working hours to protect community pharmacists’ health and well-being.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. The study was initially planned for six months using convenience sampling, but the response was low; hence, it was extended to one year, and the response rate was improved due to the adaptation of both convenience and snowball sampling methods. Use of these two sampling techniques may not be suitable for the cross-sectional study design, and they may have potential bias in the findings of this study. The low response may be due to many studies being conducted online, and the participants are overwhelmed by the number of requests to participate in studies. As there is no uniform distribution of samples from every state of Malaysia, the findings of this study may not be generalized, and there is a chance of self-reported bias and involvement of cultural aspects of HRQoL in reporting.

Conclusion

The findings highlight a multifaceted struggle encompassing physical, mental, and emotional stressors among the community pharmacists in Malaysia. Prevalent issues such as fatigue, emotional exhaustion, daytime sleepiness, and limitations in social functioning directly affect community pharmacists’ overall well-being. The study recommends that policymakers, pharmacy associations, and other relevant stakeholders take necessary steps to optimize staffing levels, redesign workplaces with ergonomic solutions, and implement flexible duty schedules to promote a healthier work–life balance among community pharmacists. Mental health support systems must be prioritized to combat burnout and emotional exhaustion. Offering continuous education and advanced training programs can equip pharmacists with the skills to manage complex patient interactions and expand their roles in chronic disease management. The adoption of digital health solutions, such as electronic health records and AI-driven tools, can streamline administrative tasks, freeing up pharmacists to focus on patient care.
