The turbulent front of a current on a horizontal bed driven by a continuous buoyancy flux in deep water
Authors: Sana Ramezani, Vincent H Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe study the turbulent front of a current on a horizontal bed driven by a continuous buoyancy flux using a large-eddy simulation (LES) method that does not rely on the Boussinesq approximation, thereby enabling accurate simulation of flows with substantial density contrasts. The primary focus of the simulation is the frontal region, where entrained ambient fluid combines with the source fluid entering the neck of the current head to form a bulging, elevated head. The bulging current head, sustained by the flow through its neck, is significant for its role in sediment transport and its destructive force in the atmosphere and ocean. Despite numerous previous studies, the vortex model of current head proposed by Prandtl [1] remains poorly understood. Analysis of the LES results shows that the turbulence entrains ambient fluid primarily through the formation of a Kelvin-Helmholtz (K-H) vortex. The LES determine the maximum velocity and maximum flow through the neck of the current head from the formation of the frontal vortex to the attainment of the vortex’s pinnacle. The vortex grows and then collapses, reaching a critical Richardson number of Ri 0.30 0.42. More than eighty percent of the turbulent entrainment of ambient fluid into the current occurs within the current head. The pinnacle flow through the neck—a function of the source Froude number—is uniquely related to the frontal velocity and volume entrainment rate by an overall structure-similarity relationship for the coherent turbulence in the front. We evaluate the numerical accuracy and the LES model through a mesh-refinement study and a comparison with laboratory observations.Keywords: Gravity current head, Continuous source of buoyancy, Kelvin-Helmholtz vortex, Large-eddy simulation, Boussinesq and non-Boussinesq currents

Introduction

Gravity currents driven by buoyancy are observed in the atmosphere and oceans when a dense fluid intrudes into a lighter ambient fluid. The advancing front of the current is a bulging, elevated current head responsible for resuspension of dust and sediment from the erodible bed and the destruction of structures in the atmosphere and the ocean [2–4]. The coherent turbulent motion at the advancing front has been a subject of significant interest in environmental science and engineering since the works of Karman [5] in 1940, Prandtl [1] in 1952, and Berson [6] in 1958. Prandtl [1] recognized vortex formation in the elevated head. Berson [6] observed the maximum wind after the arrival of the sea breeze and cold fronts in the atmosphere and linked the phenomena with Prandtl’s vortex model.

The turbulence front depends on whether the buoyancy source is instantaneous or continuous. The instantaneous sources are associated with events such as submarine landslides [4, 7]. On the other hand, a continuous source of buoyancy can maintain a current across a wide range of environmental conditions. The discharges of mine tailings and hypersaline brine from desalination plants are examples of sustained currents of engineering significance [8, 9]. In natural systems, river plumes, saline underflows, oceanic overflows, and sediment-laden turbidity currents in submarine canyons can likewise develop from a continuous source [10–12].

Studies of gravity currents often assume slight density contrasts and interpret the result using the Boussinesq approximation [13–19]. Most laboratory experiments were conducted for lock-exchange current [20–25], which is a two-layer problem. The return flow velocity in the two-layer current is comparable in magnitude to the speed of the advancing front. The dynamics of the lock-exchange current are complex, as demonstrated by the two-layer model of Hogg et al. [26], and not easily related to a one-layer current submerged in a deep ambient environment under investigation in this paper.

We conduct large-eddy simulation (LES) to study the turbulent front of a gravity current sustained by a continuous buoyancy flux on a horizontal bed in a deep ambient fluid, using a method that does not rely on the Boussinesq approximation, thereby enabling accurate simulation of flows with substantial density contrasts. The primary focus of the simulation is the frontal region of the sustained current, where entrained ambient fluid combines with the source fluid to form the elevated head. The current at the front, sustained by the combined flow through the head’s neck, is a coherent vortex as envisaged by Prandtl [1]. Still, the vortex’s structure remains poorly understood because current measurements in a laboratory setting are limited to the frontal speed and head elevation [27, 28]. Numerical simulations can determine the velocity, concentration and vorticity and the most significant flow through the neck of the current head, and then correlate them with turbulent entrainment and mixing in the frontal region.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the LES model and the setup for the numerical simulation. Section 3 presents the velocity, vorticity and concentration profiles obtained from the LES. We identify and locate the neck of the current head by the peak of the spatial flow profile presented in this section. A parametric study demonstrates that the K-H vortex in the gravity current head dominates the turbulent entrainment process, while the trailing current makes a relatively insignificant contribution. Section 4 examines the effect of stable density stratification and determines a critical Richardson number for the K-H vortex at its pinnacle. We show the rapid attainment of the current head toward a terminal state and evaluate key parameters of this state in Section 5, including the frontal speed and entrainment coefficients. Section 6 explore the similarity structure of the current head and its relationship with the pinnacle discharge through the neck of the head. Section 7 summarizes the main findings and conclusions. Appendix A compares the LES with the laboratory observation. Appendix B examine the role of wall friction on the current. The accuracy of the numerical simulation is determined by a mesh refinement study in Appendix C.

Numerical simulation platform

The numerical simulations relied on an open-source computational fluid dynamic solver known as twoLiquidMixingFoam. The solver and several pre-/post- processing utilities are part of a C++ toolbox maintained in a library that is customized and distributed by the OpenFOAM Foundation [30, 31]. Successful implementations of the OpenFOAM solvers for studying coastal and river engineering processes include the works by Higuera et al. [32–34] and Nguyen [35]. The twoLiquidMixingFoam solver in particular has been used by Gruber et al. [36], Zhang et al. [37], and Krpan and Končar [38] for related fluid dynamic simulations. Xie and Chu [39] used the solver to determine the impact force of Boussinesq current on a circular cylinder. Their simulation of the impact force agrees with laboratory observation by Ermanyuk and Gavrilov [17].

Maximum-to-frontal velocity ratio

Ambient fluid is drawn into the head of the current by the K-H vortex through a process known as the turbulent entrainment. The entrained fluid, combined with the trailing current, enters the back of the current head through a “neck" that connects the head to the body of the trailing current. The confluence in the neck leads to a velocity maximum below the vortex, where the speed exceeds the frontal speed . In panel (a) of Figs. 3 and 4, the solid red arrow denotes the velocity-maximum vector below the K-H vortex, and the open red arrow denotes the frontal velocity vector.

For the Boussinesq current sustained by a buoyancy flux 0.000192 m/s with = 0.007 shown in Fig. 3, the maximum-to-frontal speed ratio 1.73, 1.74, 1.77, and 1.67 at time 4.42, 7.00, 9.57, and 11.80, respectively. For the non-Boussinesq current sustained by a buoyancy flux 0.3278 m/s shown in Fig. 4, the maximum-to-frontal speed ratio 1.62, 1.61, 1.62, and 1.58 at time 3.52, 7.05, 8.81, and 10.57, respectively.

These maximum-to-frontal speed ratios obtained from the LES are consistent with the field measurements by Berson [6] in the atmospheric sea breeze and cold fronts, where the maximum-to-frontal velocity ratios are in the ranges varying from 1.6 to 2.0. Velocity measurements in gravity currents are rare. Most laboratory studies were based on flow visualization and dye concentration measurements. Berson [6] recognized the significance of the intense winds observed after the arrival of the fronts. His 1958 field measurements are the only velocity data for the coherent turbulence behind the front.

Summary and conclusions

The large-eddy simulation (LES) of the gravity current, using a solver that does not rely on the Boussinesq approximation, has provided velocity, vorticity, and concentration profiles to study the current of very high density contrast on a horizontal bed sustained by a continuous source. The simulations have provided a comprehensive dataset for flow visualization and the evaluation of significant flow parameters, including the terminal velocity, the height and length of the gravity current head (GCH), the volume entrained into the GCH, and other parameters not measurable in laboratory studies.

The LES shows the dominance of the K-H vortex in the GCH and the rapid attainment of the front at terminal velocity. More than 80 percent of the turbulent entrainment of the ambient fluid occurs in the GCH, while the trailing current rapidly attains a quasi-steady state. The vortex grows and then stabilizes at a critical Richardson number estimated to have a value of when the vortex attains its pinnacle. The pinnacle discharge , entering through the neck of the GCH, emerges as a significant internal scale linking front velocity, entrainment efficiency, and head structure. The collapse of data identifies the neck discharge as a robust scaling parameter for coherent turbulence over a wide range of source density contrast and Froude number. Furthermore, the LES has shown a stratification process with much slower entrainment rates into the bottom layers. The entrainment through the interface is quite small, accounting for only 12% to 27% of the total. A lower entrainment rate into the denser layers would lead to progressively more stratified flow after the initial mixing by the K-H vortex within the GCH.

The fractional error in computing the pinnacle discharge is less than 5%, which is considered acceptable because the numerical simulations of the frontal speed and entrainment rate agree with the laboratory observations by Sher and Woods [49]. The maximum velocity through the neck of the gravity current head, relative to the frontal speed, 1.6 to 2.7, is in the same range as the sea breeze and cold fronts observed in the atmosphere by Berson [6]. We do not expect the large-eddy simulation to reproduce all scales of turbulent motion. Despite the limitations of the large-eddy simulation, it has provided new insight into coherent turbulent motion in the gravity current head, well beyond what is observable in the laboratory.

The LES method used in this paper is computationally efficient, enabling a parametric study of the frontal vortex across a range of source conditions and density contrasts. A future direct numerical simulation (DNS) study of the entire current head can provide insights into the finer scales of turbulent motion. However, DNS is computationally expensive. The recent work by Dai and Huang [54], for example, has delineated the lobe and cleft structure accurately, but only in part of the gravity current head at the leading edge.

Finally, it should be noted that the source fluid in the present simulation is uniform in density. However, the dynamics of gravity currents in natural environments often involve the suspension, resuspension, and deposition of sediment. For sedimentary processes in gravity currents, see for example Wells and Dorrell [50]. Extending the present LES to include sediment in the source fluid on an erodible bed would be a worthwhile future project.
