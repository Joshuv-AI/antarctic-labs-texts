Impact of letrozole co-treatment in an antagonist protocol for IVF/ICSI: a retrospective study
Authors: Jing Lin, Fenglu Wu, Bian Wang, Qianqian Zhu, Jiaying Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe present study aimed to investigate the impact of combined use of letrozole in an antagonist protocol during IVF on live birth outcomes and to assess the safety of letrozole in terms of maternal and neonatal complications.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included women undergoing IVF/ICSI and fresh embryo transfer (ET) treatment with and without letrozole co-treatment from 2007 to 2021 at Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital (Shanghai, China). The primary outcome was the live birth rate, while the incidences of maternal and neonatal complications were secondary outcomes. Logistic regression models were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the analyzed outcomes. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a propensity score-based patient-matching (PSM) model, an inverse probability weighting (IPW) model, logistic regression models with women undergoing their first IVF-ET cycle, and subgroup analysis.ResultsOf the 4780 women enrolled in the study, 3887 underwent an antagonist protocol for ovarian stimulation, while 893 received letrozole co-treatment. In this cohort, letrozole co-treatment demonstrated comparable live birth rates to the use of antagonist protocol alone (logistic regression: aOR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.71–1.08; PSM: aOR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.77–1.22; IPW: aOR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.71–1.10). Notably, individuals with a body mass index (BMI) exceeding 24 and those with high ovarian response experienced higher live birth rates under the letrozole co-treatment regimen (BMI ≥ 24: aOR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.14-3.00; high response: aOR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.02–2.50). Letrozole co-treatment was also associated with decreased risks of gestational diabetes (aOR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.15–0.69) and small for gestational age (SGA) fetuses (aOR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.22–0.75) in fresh ET cycles. These finding were robust in both PSM and IPW models.ConclusionsOur findings suggested that letrozole co-treatment in antagonist protocol for IVF/ICSI was associated with a comparable live birth rate following fresh ET. Further prospective randomized studies are needed to verify our results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12958-024-01297-5.

Introduction

Letrozole, a selective aromatase inhibitor, is widely used as a first-line agent for inducing ovulation [1]. By inhibiting the conversion of testosterone and androstenedione into estradiol and estrone, letrozole effectively reduces estradiol production and mitigates suppressive effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis. This leads to a surge in follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) release and promotes follicular development [2]. While letrozole’s use in ovulation induction is well-established, there is growing interest in exploring its potential benefits during in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) treatment.

The use of letrozole in IVF has been proposed as a strategy to enhance follicular response and improve oocyte quality. This can be particularly beneficial for patients who do not adequately respond to standard doses of gonadotropins [3]. In addition to its endocrine and paracrine effects in the follicular phase, recent studies have highlighted the impact of letrozole co-treatment extending into the luteal phase [4]. IVF often leads to the development of multiple follicles with supraphysiological levels of estradiol and premature rises in progesterone levels [5]. This can result in luteal phase insufficiency and embryo-endometrial asynchrony [6, 7], negatively affecting the likelihood of successful implantation. The combined use of letrozole has been proposed as an alternative approach to normalize serum estradiol, thereby alleviating some of the detrimental effects of IVF treatment and allowing for embryo transfer (ET) in a more natural hormonal and uterine environment [4].

While previous studies have primarily focused on the endocrine parameters, the number of retrieved oocytes, and pregnancy rates [8], limited and conflicting evidence exists regarding the live birth rate associated with letrozole co-treatment during ovarian stimulation for IVF. Moreover, despite the relatively short timeframe of 2–3 days for letrozole’s pharmacological effects to manifest and its half-life of approximately 45 h [9], the potential implications of its combined use in IVF on maternal and fetal outcomes remain largely unexplored.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the impact of letrozole co-treatment in a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist protocol during IVF on the live birth rate in fresh ET and to evaluate the safety of letrozole in terms of maternal and neonatal complications.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Center for Reproductive Medicine of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, China. Women who underwent IVF/ICSI and fresh ET treatment with combined use of letrozole in a GnRH antagonist protocol between 2007 and 2021 were retrospectively screened in the institutional database. Infertile women who received standard GnRH antagonist protocol during the same period were included in the control group.

The inclusion criteria for women with expected normal ovarian reserve were as follows: age 18–40 years, regular menstrual cycle between 21 and 35 days, antral follicle count (AFC) ranging from 7 to 15, or FSH less than 15 IU/l in the early follicular phase. Diagnosis criteria for high ovarian response were: age 18–40 years, AFC above 15, or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) according to the Rotterdam consensus (two out of three criteria: oligo- or anovulation, clinical or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism, presence of polycystic ovaries) and exclusion of other causes [10]. Women with poor ovarian response (POR) were classified according to the Bologna criteria, which included two or more of the following: advanced maternal age (≥ 40 years) or any other risk factor, abnormal ovarian reserve test (AFC < 5–7 or anti-Müllerian hormone < 0.5–1.1 ng/ml), previous POR (cycles cancelled or ≤ 3 oocytes with a conventional stimulation protocol) [11]. Women were excluded if they had a history of repeated IVF/ICSI attempts, recurrent spontaneous abortion, chromosomal abnormalities, or incomplete data.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital (clinical trial number: not applicable) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As data were deidentified and all analyses were retrospective, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

IVF-ET procedures

All women underwent ovarian stimulation using a GnRH antagonist protocol. Gonadotropin was initiated from cycle day 2–3 at 75–300 IU/day until the human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) trigger day. The dosage was determined based on several factors, including the patient’s age, body mass index (BMI), ovarian reserve, and ovarian response in previous IVF cycles. Adjustments were made according to the follicle growth and serum hormone levels throughout the treatment. A daily injection of GnRH antagonist 0.25 mg was administered when a dominant follicle reached a mean diameter of 13–14 mm or when blood luteinizing hormone (LH) levels showed a notable upward trend; the dose was continued until the hCG trigger day. In the letrozole co-treatment group, patients were administered oral letrozole at a daily dosage of either 2.5 mg or 5 mg for five consecutive days starting from cycle day 2–5.

Final oocyte maturation was triggered by triptorelin 0.1 mg and hCG 2000 IU when two leading follicles reached ≥ 18 mm in diameter, and oocyte retrieval was performed 34–36 h after hCG injection. Oocytes were fertilized with IVF, ICSI, or IVF + ICSI based on sperm quality. Embryo quality was assessed according to the Istanbul Consensus [12]. Fresh ET was performed based on clinical practice, and any surplus embryos were cryopreserved. Luteal support was provided after ET.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the live birth rate, defined as the delivery of at least one live-born infant irrespective of gestational duration after fresh ET. The secondary outcomes were maternal and neonatal complications, evaluated for all live births after ET cycles, excluding cases of neonatal mortality and congenital anomalies. Maternal complications included twin pregnancy, gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, placenta previa, placental abruption, preterm premature rupture of the membranes (PPROM), Cesarean delivery. Neonatal complications included gestational age (preterm birth [< 37 weeks], very preterm birth [< 32 weeks], extremely preterm birth [< 28 weeks]), birth weight (low birth weight [< 2500 g], very low birth weight [< 1500 g], macrosomia [> 4000 g], small for gestational age [SGA], large for gestational age [LGA]), neonatal death (defined by mortality prior to hospital discharge after live birth), birth defects, and a composite of neonatal morbidity (neonatal jaundice, necrotizing enterocolitis, pneumonia, respiratory distress syndrome, sepsis, hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy). Of note, the incidence of neonatal death and birth defect was evaluated for all live births.

Statistical analyses

Baseline data were compared between groups using Student’s t-tests for normally distributed variables and with Chi-square test for categorical variables. Logistic regression models were employed when estimating odds ratios for the relationship between letrozole co-treatment and analyzed outcomes. Potential confounders were adjusted based on univariate analyses, previous studies, and biological plausibility. For live birth outcomes, adjustments were made by maternal characteristics, IVF method, number and stage of embryo transferred. For maternal complications, gestational diabetes and twin births were added to the adjusted analysis. For neonatal outcomes, the adjustment was moreover made by maternal complications, twin births, gestational age, and birth weight. All statistical analyses were performed with R v 4.2.2. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant.

The robustness of main study findings was assessed through several sensitivity analysis strategies: (1) a propensity score-based patient-matching (PSM) model; (2) an inverse probability weighting (IPW) model; (3) logistic regression models focusing on women undergoing their first IVF-ET cycle; and (4) subgroup analysis.

In the PSM analysis, cases with and without letrozole co-treatment were matched based on estimated propensity scores derived from a binary logistic regression analysis. The matching ratio was set at 1:1, with a caliper of 0.05. In the IPW analysis, estimated propensity scores were utilized to establish a weighted cohort. The effectiveness of these two models in achieving covariate balance was evaluated through the mean standardized difference. Covariates were considered well-balanced when the standardized difference was below 5% (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Furthermore, logistic regression models were employed specifically for women undergoing their first IVF-ET cycle. Subgroup analyses were then performed to examine potential variations in the analyzed outcomes across baseline characteristics.

Results

A total of 4780 participants were enrolled in the study, with 3887 women in the GnRH antagonist group and 893 women in the GnRH antagonist plus letrozole group. Demographic characteristics of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. After PSM, the baseline characteristics were well balanced between the two groups (Table 2) and the ovarian stimulation outcomes are summarized in Table 3. Women co-treated with letrozole received a lower gonadotropin dose and had a shorter stimulation duration with a significant suppression of estradiol concentrations on hCG day compared with the control group (P < 0.05). The letrozole group had a lower number of retrieved oocytes (P < 0.05), whereas there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of useable embryos and high-quality embryos (P > 0.05).

Live birth outcomes

Live birth outcomes are summarized in Table 4. The live birth rate in letrozole co-treatment group was comparable to the GnRH antagonist alone group after adjusting for confounders (204/893 [22.8%] versus 920/3887 [23.7%]; adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.88; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.71–1.08). This result was consistent in sensitivity analyses performed with both PSM (aOR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.77–1.22) and IPW (aOR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.71–1.10) models. For women undergoing their first IVF-ET cycle, the letrozole co-treatment group also showed similar live birth rate (aOR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.75–1.19). Subgroup analyses stratified by ovarian response, BMI, and PCOS status indicated that letrozole co-treatment was significantly associated with a higher live birth rate only for women with high ovarian response (high response: aOR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.02–2.50; normal response: aOR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.55–1.03; POR: aOR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.15–2.20) and a large BMI (BMI ≥ 24: aOR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.14-3.00; BMI < 24: aOR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.59–1.01).

Maternal and neonatal complications

Maternal complications are summarized in Tables 5 and 6. Women in the letrozole co-treatment group exhibited a decreased risk of gestational diabetes compared to those in the antagonist group after adjusting for confounding factors (8/203 [3.94%] versus 95/896 [10.6%]; aOR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.15–0.69). The finding was robust in both PSM (aOR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.16–0.90) and IPW (aOR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.14–0.74) models.

Letrozole co-treatment was also related to a reduced risk of PPROM (aOR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.12–0.97) in multivariate logistic regression models. However, the PSM and IPW analyses did not yield statistically significant differences. No significant differences were observed for other analyzed maternal outcomes including twin pregnancy, hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, placenta previa, placental abruption, or Cesarean delivery.

Neonatal outcomes are summarized in Tables 5 and 6. Fetus born in the letrozole co-treatment group exhibited a decreased risk of SGA as compared to those in the GnRH antagonist group (multivariate logistic regression models: aOR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.22–0.75; PSM: aOR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.19–0.73; IPW: aOR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.22–0.89).

Among the other analyzed neonatal outcomes (Tables 5 and 6), no significant differences were observed in terms of gestational age (preterm birth, very preterm birth, extremely preterm birth), birth weight (low birth weight, very low birth weight, macrosomia, LGA), neonatal death, birth defects, and a composite of neonatal morbidity (neonatal jaundice, necrotizing enterocolitis, pneumonia, respiratory distress syndrome, sepsis, and hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy).

Discussion

In this large retrospective study, we discovered that live birth outcomes resulting from the co-treatment of letrozole were comparable to those achieved with the antagonist protocol alone. Moreover, overweight women (BMI ≥ 24) and high responders experienced higher rates of live birth under the letrozole co-treatment regimen.

Existing literature evaluating the live birth outcomes of letrozole co-treatment in IVF/ICSI is limited and inconsistent. A meta-analysis incorporating 656 women showed no significant effect of letrozole co-treatment on live birth outcomes [8]. While several studies of poor responders demonstrated a significant effect favoring letrozole [13, 14], a number of other studies did not observe a discernible distinction in live birth outcomes [15–18]. A study involving high responders (n = 130) showed no significant effect of letrozole on live birth outcome [19]. For normal responders, a retrospective study (n = 252) found that letrozole might have no effect on the cumulative live birth rate [20]. It is important to note that the included studies have suffered from small sample sizes and heterogeneity in terms of choice of protocol (antagonist/agonist), starting dose of gonadotropin, and trigger criteria. In our present study, we aimed to overcome these methodological shortcomings and adjust for important confounders. We demonstrated that letrozole co-treatment led to comparable live birth rates overall. Through subgroup analysis, we discovered that letrozole had beneficial effect on high responders and overweight women (BMI ≥ 24). Further studies are needed to verify the reproductive outcomes of letrozole co-treatment in women across baseline demographics.

Our study revealed that women co-treated with letrozole required lower exogenous gonadotropin consumption and experienced shorter stimulation time, which is consistent with previous studies [8]. While we did not observe significant differences in the number of useable embryos, we found the use of letrozole may be associated with a significant reduction in the number of oocytes retrieved, which aligns with two previous studies [17, 21]. However, some larger studies reported that letrozole co-treatment increased the number of oocytes retrieved [22, 23]. A recent meta-analysis of 26 studies involving 3091 women concluded that no significant difference was observed in the overall effect [8].

The short half-life of letrozole and its administration during the early follicular phase allow for a sufficient period of time for complete washout before embryo implantation [9]. Previous data demonstrated that letrozole co-treatment did not impact follicle recruitment or endocrine parameters in the subsequent cycle [24]. A recent population-based study conducted in Japan indicated that the administration of letrozole during IVF did not pose an elevated risk of major congenital anomalies or compromise the neonatal outcomes compared to natural cycles [25]. A prior meta-analysis of 39 studies incorporating 4697 babies concluded that the incidence of congenital malformations in fetuses exposed to letrozole was comparable to that of the general population [26]. Our study also demonstrated that letrozole did not increase the risks of birth defects compared to the standard IVF protocol. However, there is still a gap in the research regarding the maternal and fetal risks associated with letrozole co-treatment in IVF/ICSI. To our knowledge, our study is the first to comprehensively assess the risks of maternal and fetal outcomes in women taking letrozole in a standard IVF protocol and identify a safety profile concerning maternal and neonatal risks.

Letrozole effectively reduces intraovarian and serum estrogen levels by inhibiting the conversion of androgens to estrogens in ovarian granulosa cells [27]. This allows for the maintenance of follicular phase estradiol closer to physiological levels, thus mitigating the negative impact of cumulative estradiol concentrations on oocyte quality and on endometrial receptivity [28]. Our study supports the notion that letrozole can facilitate appropriate follicular development and normal implantation potential without exerting adverse anti-estrogenic effects.

Several factors have been proposed to explain the improved reproductive outcomes associated with letrozole in IVF/ICSI. Firstly, letrozole has been shown to either sensitize the follicles to exogenous FSH and/or stimulate the production of endogenous FSH, leading to enhanced follicular response and improved oocyte quality [29]. Secondly, letrozole induces the accumulation of androgens to increase follicle sensitivity and growth [30]. Thirdly, letrozole co-treatment in the follicular phase reduces luteal pituitary suppression and exerts a prolonged effect on serum progesterone, LH, and androgens during the luteal phase [4]. Fourthly, reduced estrogen levels decrease the ubiquitination of estrogen receptors, resulting in up-regulation of these receptors and increased sensitivity to subsequent estrogen rise. This process leads to faster endometrial proliferation and increased blood flow in the uterus and endometrium [31]. Lastly, letrozole use during IVF may enhance endometrial receptivity by increasing the expression of integrin, leukemia inhibitory factor, and L-selectin [32]. Studies have reported improved endometrial receptivity in intrauterine insemination cycles using letrozole compared to clomiphene citrate, as well as similar endometrial histology to that of a natural menstrual cycle in letrozole-treated women, supporting this theory [33, 34].

Furthermore, supraphysiological estradiol concentrations during ovarian stimulation are considered to exert deleterious effects on various physiological processes, including leptin regulation, coagulation system, and early placental development [28], contributing to adverse obstetric outcome. Gestational diabetes, a common complication characterized by spontaneous hyperglycemia during pregnancy, develops when the normal metabolic adaptations to pregnancy fail to occur. According to the International Diabetes Federation, gestational diabetes affects approximately 14% of pregnancies worldwide [35]. A recent study in China demonstrated that letrozole treatment significantly reduced serum triglyceride levels and the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) value in infertile women with PCOS [36]. The mechanisms underlying the reduced risk of gestational diabetes with letrozole co-treatment during IVF are currently unknown, but it may be directly or indirectly associated with repaired β-cell function and/or insulin sensitivity [37]. SGA occurs in 13% of singleton births following IVF [38]. SGA is often a result of fetal undernutrition caused by maternal undernutrition or placental insufficiency. Evidence suggests that prematurely elevated estradiol levels inhibit trophoblastic invasion of spiral arteries [39], whereas low levels of estradiol during implantation allows for extravillous trophoblast invasion and vascular remodeling of uterine spiral arteries [40]. The reduced risks after letrozole treatment may be attributed to the optimal uterine environment that potentially has downstream implications for better placentation and improved maternal outcomes. Although our statistical analysis indicated a potentially favorable effect of letrozole treatment, this finding requires further validation through larger, well-designed studies.

Strengths and limitations

This analysis was subject to certain limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design of the study might introduce selection bias regarding the participants recruited and the IVF protocol employed, which could affect the external validation and generalizability of our study. Secondly, the dosages of gonadotropins and letrozole used in this study were not strictly controlled. Thirdly, the rare event and limited sample size of live births might result in the capture of unreliable associations. Moreover, our study did not collect and adjust for several potential risk factors that could influence maternal and neonatal outcomes, including co-existing medical conditions, family history, nutritional status, gestational weight gain, and maternal stress. Consequently, the conclusions derived from these findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, this study had several strengths that deserved recognition, primarily its large cohort size. Other strengths include the consistent practice within a single center, the analysis of diverse outcome variables, the use of multiple association inference models, and the stratified sampling based on baseline characteristics.

Conclusions

This large single-center study expands the current knowledge of letrozole use in an antagonist protocol for IVF/ICSI, indicating that letrozole co-treatment yields similar live birth rate in fresh ET. These findings provide valuable insights into the potential benefits and safety profile of letrozole in IVF treatment, guiding clinicians in optimizing treatment strategies for patients undergoing assisted reproductive technology treatments. Further prospective randomized studies are needed to verify our results.
