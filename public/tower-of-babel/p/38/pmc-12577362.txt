Impact of sarcopenia on clinical outcomes of older adults undergoing total knee arthroplasty for osteoarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiao-yu Zhang, Xin Zhou, Jin-chang Han, Si Wu, Xiao-qing Zhang, Qing-Tong Cui, Peng-tao Huang, Yun-yan Cai, Chao Zhang, Ai-feng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia is increasingly recognized as a significant factor influencing postoperative outcomes in orthopedic procedures. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the impact of sarcopenia on clinical outcomes in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for knee osteoarthritis (KOA), specifically focusing on knee function, quality of life, and complications.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive search on PubMed, Embase, and CENTRAL platforms, to identify eligible studies comparing clinical outcomes of sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic patients undergoing primary TKA for KOA. Heat map was generated to intuitively illustrate the statistical differences in various clinical outcomes between sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia patients. Meta analyses were also performed to compare the outcomes between groups with and without sarcopenia using mean difference (MD) or risk ratio (RR) as the effect sizes.ResultsOur systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 studies involving 93,440 patients revealed that sarcopenia was associated with worse post-TKA outcomes. Sarcopenic patients experienced significantly higher blood transfusion requirements (RR = 3.66, 95% CI: 2.86–4.69, p < 0.001), and a higher risk of overall complications (RR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.53–2.11, p < 0.001). Knee function, as measured by the WOMAC and KOOS scores, was inferior in early period (≤ 6months) in sarcopenic patients, but no significant differences were observed at 12 months. There is a consensus leans towards no difference in knee pain between the two groups. Sarcopenic patients showed slower walking speeds and increased fall risk.ConclusionsPreliminary finding was obtained that sarcopenia was associated with increased transfusion needs, higher complication risks, and delayed functional recovery, in patients undergoing TKA for KOA. To optimize postoperative outcomes, it is crucial to address sarcopenia in the preoperative and postoperative management, with a call for more prospective, high-quality research to solidify these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09245-z.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Sarcopenia, Knee function, Clinical outcomes, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Sarcopenia is defined as a condition characterized by decrease in skeletal muscle mass and decline in muscle strength that occurs as one ages [1]. It is a progressive and systemic muscle disease that leads to accelerated muscle loss and a subsequent decrease in physical function [2, 3]. Sarcopenia is associated with an increased risk of falling and higher mortality rates, which consequently lead to a reduced quality of life (QoL) for patients [4]. It is estimated that currently, there are approximately 50 million individuals worldwide suffering from sarcopenia, with predictions projecting that number to soar to 500 million by 2050 [5]. Among the elderly population, another disease with a high incidence rate is knee osteoarthritis (KOA) [6–8]. For those with end-stage KOA, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is currently the most effective surgical intervention to alleviate pain and regain mobility. However, despite the overall favorable outcomes of TKA for patients with end-stage KOA [9, 10], a small subset of patients remain unsatisfied with their clinical outcomes and knee function, even in cases where the surgery is technically successful. Various factors may contribute to this, such as age-related osteoporosis, sarcopenia, and diabetes [11–13].

There may be an interplaying relationship between sarcopenia and the development of KOA [14, 15]. The onset of sarcopenia could potentially expedite the progression of KOA, whereas patients with osteoarthritis may experience a exacerbation of sarcopenia due to their diminished motor capacity. While the common coprevalence between KOA and sarcopenia is well-established, evidence regarding whether sarcopenia affects the postoperative clinical outcomes of patients undergoing TKA remains scarce. In a systematic review by Longo et al. [16], five studies were included to analyze the impact of concomitant sarcopenia on the postoperative clinical outcomes of patients undergoing either TKA or total hip arthroplasty (THA). Due to insufficient primary evidence, the authors could not draw definitive conclusions from this body of literature and suggested that more research in this direction is needed. Additionally, four of the studies included in this review focused on patients who had undergone THA for hip fracture, leaving the high-level evidence base for the effect of sarcopenia on patients undergoing TKA for KOA insufficient.

Consequently, this study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis based on the available literature to achieve the following objectives: (1) to investigate whether the presence of sarcopenia impacts the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing TKA for KOA, including knee function, QoL, and knee symptoms; (2) to observe whether patients with concomitant sarcopenia are at an increased risk of developing postoperative complications, longer hospital stays, and higher transfusion requirements. By consolidating recent findings, this study addresses a gap in the literature and provides a clearer understanding of potential risks and clinical implications, guiding clinical decision-making and perioperative management, for this this growing patient population.

Materials and methods

This study adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guideline [17], with the checklist detailed in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Data sources

The data collection process encompassed independent literature searches by two researchers across three distinct databases: PubMed, Embase, and CENTRAL. A strategic synthesis of medical subject headings and free terms was employed, utilizing keywords such as “sarcopenia,” “total knee arthroplasty,” “TKA,” and “knee osteoarthritis.” The publication language was limited to English. Furthermore, the reference lists of the selected studies were also scrutinized for any further pertinent research.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Records retrieved from the three databases and the reference lists were meticulously vetted against specific eligibility criteria. These included: (1) patients who had undergone primary TKA for the treatment of KOA; (2) studies that compared the clinical outcomes (including length of hospital stay [LOS], pain symptom, knee functional assessment, and patients’ QoL) of TKA between patients with and without sarcopenia; (3) the sarcopenia was diagnosed by international general diagnostic criteria (such as European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People [EWGSOP], Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia [AWGS], Foundation for the National Institutes of Health [FNIH], SARC-CalF, or ICD-9 diagnosis code) or assessed by skeletal muscle quality indexes (such as skeletal muscle mass index [SMI], appendicular lean mass [ALM]) and (4) research articles categorized as either randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, or case-control studies were deemed suitable for inclusion.

Studies fulfilling the following criteria were excluded: (1) investigations concerning patients who had received THA for hip fractures or osteoarthritis; (2) reports focusing on the outcomes of revision procedures after primary TKA; (3) any research lacking a comparative analysis between sarcopenic and nonsarcopenic patient groups; (4) articles that took the form of systematic reviews/meta-analyses, literature reviews, comments, and letters to the editor.

Study screening and data extraction

The selection of studies commenced with two authors independently reviewing all electronic records extracted from the databases, applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ascertain their suitability. Superfluous entries were initially purged using the de-duplication feature of EndNote version X9. Thereafter, the two reviewers meticulously examined the titles and abstracts of the remaining entries to discard any clearly irrelevant ones. For the subsequent phase, the full texts of the remaining studies were acquired and meticulously analyzed to ascertain their eligibility for inclusion.

To extract pertinent data, we focused on the following elements: (1) study characteristics: the lead author’s name, year of publication, country of origin, duration of the study, study design, trial registration details, and follow-up period; (2) patients characteristics: total number of patients, gender distribution, average age, body mass index (BMI), skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), laterality of the TKA procedure, Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) grade of osteoarthritis, hemoglobin levels, total serum protein, the diagnostic criteria used for identifying sarcopenia, and the prevalence of major comorbidities or the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI); (3) treatment details: operation duration, LOS, and post-operative rehabilitation regimen; (4) clinical outcomes: clinical measures like gait parameters, muscle mass indices, the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities (WOMAC) Osteoarthritis Index, the Knee Society Score (KSS), and risk of post-operative complications (including systemic complications such as cardiovascular, pulmonary, and gastrointestinal morbidities, and surgical complications such as deep venous thromboembolism [DVT], blood transfusion, skin infection, and implant-related issues). The baseline characteristics (study/patients characteristics, and treatment details) were narratively summarized, while the clinical outcomes were quantitatively compared between sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups.

The methodological quality of the cohort and case-control studies that were ultimately included in our synthesis was critically appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Any studies scoring below 6 stars on the NOS were subsequently omitted from our analysis to uphold the integrity of our findings.

Statistical analysis

When comparing the outcomes between groups with and without sarcopenia, different statistical methods were employed depending on the nature of the outcome variable. If the outcome variable was continuous, the data were pooled using mean differences (MDs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) as the effect size. Should the outcome variable be binary, meta-analysis was performed with risk ratios (RRs) and their 95%CIs serving as the measure of effect size.

I² statistic was selected to evaluate the heterogeneity among studies. An I² value of 50% or greater signified substantial heterogeneity, prompting us to adopt a random-effect model for our analysis. Conversely, if the heterogeneity was found to be non-significant, a fixed-effect model was selected. In cases where significant heterogeneity was present and at least five primary studies were included in the meta-analysis, a sensitivity analysis was conducted. This entailed systematically excluding each study one by one to gauge its influence on the overall results.

Additionally, funnel plot was generated to qualitatively assess the risk of publication bias. Should such bias be detected, it was adjusted using the non-parametric trim-and-fill method.

To intuitively illustrate the statistical differences in various clinical outcomes between sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia patients, a heatmap which utilizes different colors to denote the significance of the comparisons was created. By doing so, it enables the rapid identification of clinical outcomes that show significant disparities between the two groups.

All data analysis for this meta-analysis was carried out using R language version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We deemed all statistical tests as two-tailed, with a p-value of less than 0.05 being indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Study selecting process

A detailed flowchart outlining our stringent study selection process can be found in Fig. 1. We began by conducting a thorough search, which yielded a total of 78 studies. During the preliminary screening phase, we eliminated 18 duplicate studies that did not contribute unique insights. Following this, we scrutinized the titles and abstracts of the remaining 60 studies. This step was crucial in weeding out irrelevant studies that did not align with our research objectives. As a result, 36 studies were deemed unsuitable and were excluded from further consideration. Undeterred by the narrowing of our pool, we then advanced to a more in-depth examination, reading the full text of the remaining 24 studies. Upon closer inspection, we made the decision to exclude 14 more studies, because they did not meet our specified criteria (6 not related to TKA, 3 designed as review, and 5 without comparison between sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups). Our meticulous process culminated in the selection of 10 eligible studies [18–27], which were deemed most relevant and informative for our systematic synthesis.

Summary of the included studies

A comprehensive overview of the 10 studies encompassed in our analysis, involving a collective of 93,440 patients (65,623 females and 27,817 males), is presented in Table 1. Geographically, the studies were distributed across several countries: China [20–22, 25, 27], the United States [19], Korea [18, 23], Japan [26], and Greece [24]. The designs employed across the studies were diverse, including seven retrospective/prospective cohort studies [18, 20, 22–26] and three case-control studies [19, 21, 27]. Qualitative synthesis for impact of sarcopenia on clinical outcomes

Figure 2 depicts a heatmap highlighting the significance of all available comparisons on various clinical outcomes between sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups. Within the 10 primary studies included, a total of 14 distinct clinical outcome measures were compared, encompassing: LOS, timed up and go test (TUG), walking speed, time to independent walking, time to chair rise, WOMAC score, knee range of motion, KOOS score, KSS score, cost of care, risk of falls, risk of lower extremity fractures, reoperation/readmission rates, and blood transfusion requirement.

Abbreviations: S, significant; NS, non-significant; LOS, length of hospital stay; LMI, lean mass index; ALMI, appendicular lean mass index; TUG, time up and go test; TIW, time to independent walking; TCR, time to chair rise; WOMAC, the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities; KOOS, the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score; KSS, the Knee Society Score; ROM, range of motion

Many outcomes were evaluated at multiple follow-up intervals. Overall, based on the available evidence, there are conflicting studies regarding whether there is a difference in post-operative outcomes between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic groups. Moreover, there are rarely more than two studies evaluating the same variable according to the current available evidences. It seems that the consensus leans towards no difference in pain between the two groups, assessed by WOMAC-pain and KOOS-pain scores. According to the study of Shon et al. [18], while early functional outcomes (WOMAC/KOOS, ≤ 6 months) were inferior in sarcopenic patients, no significant differences were observed at 12 months. Due to the heterogeneity of clinical outcome metrics used in the literature and the variability in the timing of follow-ups, there were limited indicators suitable for quantitative synthesis through meta-analysis.

Quantitative meta-analyses for impact of sarcopenia on clinical outcomes

A meta-analysis comparing the LOS between the two groups is presented in Fig. 3. The finding indicates that the sarcopenic group had similar duration of hospitalization with the non-sarcopenic group (MD = 0.33, 95%CI: −0.18−0.84, p = 0.207). Figure 4 shows the forest plot and funnel plot for the comparison of blood transfusion requirement. It is evident that the sarcopenic group had a significantly higher demand for blood transfusions than the non-sarcopenic group (RR = 3.66, 95%CI: 2.86–4.69, p < 0.001***). The funnel plot does not suggest any significant publication bias. Figure 5 represents the forest plot for the comparison of the overall complication risk between the two groups. It is apparent that the sarcopenic group had a significantly higher incidence of overall complications than the non-sarcopenic group (RR = 1.80, 95%CI: 1.53–2.11, p < 0.001***). Figure 6 presents the forest plot for subgroup analysis comparing the risks of postoperative complications between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic groups. The analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in systemic complications (RR = 1.53, 95% CI: 0.77–3.05; p = 0.227), surgery-related complications (RR = 1.28, 95% CI: 0.58–2.82; p = 0.541), DVT (RR = 3.48, 95% CI: 0.13–90.51; p = 0.454). However, implant-related complications were significantly higher in the sarcopenic group (RR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.62–1.94; p < 0.001***), as reported in a single study [24].

Discussion

The impact of sarcopenia on the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing TKA is multifaceted and concerning, as highlighted by our meta-analysis. Across the 10 studies, the impact of sarcopenia was evident across a wide array of clinical outcomes, suggesting a systemic influence on the postsurgical functional recovery process in the early period, blood transfusion requirement, and complication risk.

Recent studies have shown that the presence of sarcopenia significantly affects survival rates and the outcomes of surgical treatment in patients with various diseases [28–33]. Knoedler et al. [28] conducted a meta-analysis analyzing the impact of sarcopenia on postoperative outcomes across different types of surgeries, including 294 original studies. Their findings indicated that sarcopenic patients faced worse postoperative outcomes, higher risks of mortality and complications, longer LOS, and lower discharge rates following surgery. In orthopedics, sarcopenic patients experienced worse outcomes following spinal surgery [34–37]. For patients with KOA who underwent TKA, some retrospective observational studies have initiated preliminary investigations into this issue [18, 19, 22–26]. Ardeljan et al. [19] discovered that patients with sarcopenia undergoing primary TKA had longer hospital stays, higher risks of 90-day medical complications, falls, lower extremity fractures, and reoperations, increased 2-year implant-related complications, and higher 90-day costs. However, there is still a lack of high-quality evidence to assess this relationship and provide evidence-based medicine support.

In the current study, the data revealed a trend where sarcopenic patients experience poorer recovery in terms of knee function, assessed through measures such as the WOMAC and KOOS scores [18, 21], indicating compromised joint flexibility and reduced mobility. Shon et al. [18] evaluated whether sarcopenia occurring concomitantly with KOA impacts patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following TKA. The authors found that up to six months post-primary TKA, the PROMs in the sarcopenic group were inferior to those in the non-sarcopenic group, with the exception of the pain score; however, by 12 months post-surgery, no significant differences were observed between the two groups. This suggests that sarcopenic patients may require longer periods for functional recovery compared to non-sarcopenic patients. However, additional long-term studies with standardized outcome metrics for comparison are needed. Furthermore, these patients exhibited diminished physical function, as evidenced by slower walking speeds [21] and prolonged times for tasks like the time up and go test [21] and independent walking [25] after the procedure. The only one study by Shon et al. [18] showed that the early postoperative KOOS-QoL score was lower in sarcopenic patients, but that this difference disappeared by 12 months after surgery.

From a safety perspective, sarcopenic patients faced a heightened risk of developing complications following TKA. The meta-analysis revealed a significant increase in the likelihood of postoperative blood transfusions being required, which can be a marker for increased bleeding due to soft tissue atrophy and potential difficulties in achieving proper hemostasis [38]. While it is possible that sarcopenic patients may experience increased bleeding or impaired hemostasis (although this remains unconfirmed and requires further evidence), it is more likely that the lower pre-operative hemoglobin level in sarcopenia patients is the primary cause for the increased transfusion requirement [39, 40]. This also could be attributed to the reduced muscle mass that is characteristic of sarcopenia, which might be associated with less physiological reserve to cope with the blood loss during surgery [41, 42]. The increased need for transfusions not only implies a higher financial burden but also a potential marker for other postoperative complications. Another critical aspect is the increased risk of falling [19] and sustaining lower extremity fractures [19] in sarcopenic patients following TKA. These complications can lead to prolonged rehabilitation, reduced mobility, and in some cases, the need for further surgical intervention. The meta-analysis emphasizing the importance of preemptive measures to prevent falls and fractures in this high-risk sarcopenia group.

Another key outcome evaluated was the LOS between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic patients. However, our meta-analysis showed no significant difference in LOS between two groups of individuals. This result contrasts with some previous assumptions that sarcopenia may prolong recovery time and hospitalization. It should be noted that the available data were limited and somewhat heterogeneous, which may affect the ability to detect subtle differences. Despite the absence of a statistically significant difference in LOS, the overall economic impact of sarcopenia in the perioperative period remains a relevant concern. As demonstrated in one of the included studies [19], sarcopenic patients were associated with higher overall costs. This may be attributed to factors such as increased transfusion rates, potential for more intensive postoperative care, and increased risk of complications, even if not directly reflected in LOS data.

The meta-analysis, although limited by heterogeneity in outcome reporting, did provide some quantitative insight into the problem. The pooled effect sizes consistently pointed towards worse outcomes in the sarcopenic group, lending weight to the notion that sarcopenia is an independent risk factor for adverse events following TKA. From a clinical perspective, identifying sarcopenia preoperatively may help guide risk stratification and inform appropriate optimization measures. Furthermore, careful patient selection and possibly tailored postoperative care protocols could improve outcomes and reduce the incidence of complications in this vulnerable population. Incorporating preoperative screening for sarcopenia into routine clinical practice could allow for early identification of at-risk patients. Tailored interventions such as preoperative rehabilitation programs focusing on improving muscle mass and strength, nutritional supplementation, or protein intake, could be considered as part of a multimodal approach to enhance recovery and reduce the risk of complications following TKA in sarcopenic patients [43–45].

Despite the valuable insights gained from this meta-analysis, it is important to acknowledge the limitations posed by the heterogeneity in the reported outcomes and the variability in the timing of follow-up. As a result, we failed to quantitatively pool the effect of sarcopenia on many clinical outcomes following TKA. Future research with more standardized outcome measurements and follow-up schedules is warranted to provide even more robust evidence on the impact of sarcopenia. Secondly, the diagnostic criteria of the sarcopenia were different across the included studies, causing another risk of bias. Finally, the data were collected retrospectively in ten of the studies, which may cause inevitable bias on the collected data. Therefore, more prospectively designed studies, such as RCTs, are required in the future.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have obtained some preliminary findings indicating that sarcopenia patients are associated with delayed functional recovery, increased transfusion needs, and higher rate of complications following TKA. Future research should focus on strategies to prevent and manage sarcopenia in the context of TKA to improve patient outcomes and contain healthcare costs. Additionally, further studies with more standardized outcome measurements and follow-up schedules are required to provide more convincing evidence about the impact of sarcopenia following TKA.
