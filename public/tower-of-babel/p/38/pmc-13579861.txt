The burden and risk factors of NCDs in Zimbabwe: evidence from the Zimbabwe population based HIV impact assessment 2020 survey
Authors: Munyaradzi Paul Mapingure, Tafadzwa Dzinamarira, Innocent Chingombe, Enos Moyo, Amon Mpofu, Owen Mugurungi, Helena Herrera, Roda Madziva, Godfrey N Musuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) has emerged as a critical public health challenge in Zimbabwe, exacerbated by a complex interplay of socioeconomic factors, demographic shifts, and urbanization. This study leverages data from the Zimbabwe Population-Based HIV Impact Assessment (ZIMPHIA) 2020 survey to provide further insights and understanding of the prevalence and risk factors associated with NCDs in the country.MethodsUtilizing a two-stage, stratified cluster sample design, we analyzed data from 19,535 participants. We conducted univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to evaluate the relationship between self-reported NCDs and demographic factors, including age, gender, urban versus rural residency, HIV status, and socioeconomic status.ResultsFindings revealed that approximately 14.3% of the population reported being diagnosed with at least one NCD, with hypertension and diabetes presenting prevalences of 10.8% and 2.2%, respectively. Age was a significant predictor of NCD burden, and we also noted disparities, particularly among women and individuals in higher wealth quintiles.ConclusionApproximately one in seven Zimbabwean adults had been diagnosed with at least one non-communicable disease before 2020, with hypertension and diabetes the most common conditions. Older age increases NCD risk, and women and people in higher wealth quintiles carry a higher burden. These patterns suggest the need to keep prioritizing NCD prevention, screening, and management.Keywords: Noncommunicable Diseases (NCDs), HIV, Equity, Risk Factors, ZIMPHIA 2020

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a significant global public health challenge, currently accounting for approximately 75% of all deaths worldwide [1]. Notably, 82% of premature deaths, before the age of 70, occur in low- and middle-income countries, where health systems often grapple with limited resources and a high burden of infectious diseases [2]. The primary NCDs encompass cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes, with their rise driven by modifiable risk factors, including tobacco use, physical inactivity, unhealthy dietary patterns, and environmental pollutants [3]. Projections indicate that the global burden of NCDs will continue to increase, making prevention strategies imperative [4].

In the context of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the region is particularly vulnerable, experiencing a disproportionate burden of both infectious and NCDs [5]. The ongoing epidemiological transition indicates that NCDs are anticipated to surpass infectious diseases as the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the coming years [6]. Research highlights a growing burden of NCDs and related risk factors across SSA, although prevalence estimates vary substantially depending on the population studied and methods used. Some studies conducted among older adults, urban populations, or high-risk groups have reported hypertension prevalence approaching 48%, diabetes prevalence around 16%, and obesity prevalence up to 43% [5]. This increasing burden of NCDs is exacerbated by urbanization, lifestyle changes, and economic instability, further straining limited healthcare resources and complicating health systems already under pressure from infectious diseases [7].

Zimbabwe presents a multifaceted health landscape marked by a high burden of communicable diseases alongside a growing prevalence of non-communicable diseases and recurrent outbreaks of re-emerging infectious diseases [8]. While significant progress has been made in the fight against HIV/AIDS, largely aided by international assistance, NCDs now account for 31% of total deaths. This growing crisis has prompted the establishment of the noncommunicable diseases and injuries (NCDI) Poverty Commission in 2019, aimed at assessing both the causes and impacts of NCDs and injuries on the Zimbabwean population [9].

The Zimbabwe Population-Based HIV Impact Assessment (ZIMPHIA) 2020 survey provides essential data and insights into the health profile of Zimbabwe, particularly concerning the prevalence of HIV and NCDs and associated risk factors. This survey serves as a critical tool for understanding the epidemiological landscape, revealing the vital interplay between NCDs and socioeconomic factors [10]. We set out to analyze the burden and risk factors of NCDs in Zimbabwe, using findings from the ZIMPHIA 2020 survey. As Zimbabwe seeks to address this multifaceted disease landscape, findings from comprehensive assessments will be pivotal in guiding policy and shaping effective healthcare strategies tailored to the population’s unique challenges.

Materials and methods

The methodology of the Zimbabwe Population-based HIV Impact Assessment has been described elsewhere [10]. Briefly, ZIMPHIA 2020 used a two-stage, stratified cluster sample design, in which census enumeration areas (EAs) were selected in the first stage and households in the second. The survey included individuals aged 15 years and above. The primary outcome was self-reported prior diagnosis of at least one non-communicable disease (NCD). Participants were asked: ‘Have you ever been told by a doctor or health worker that you have any of the following chronic health conditions?’ Conditions included hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease, cancer or tumour, chronic lung disease, depression or mental health conditions, and other chronic conditions. Depression and mental health conditions were included because they are increasingly recognised as important chronic conditions that frequently coexist with physical NCDs and may influence healthcare utilisation, treatment adherence, and overall disease burden. Participants responding ‘yes’ to one or more conditions were classified as having ‘any NCD’. No independent clinical verification or biomarker confirmation of these diagnoses was available in the dataset. We analyzed and presented the baseline demographic characteristics and distribution of the NCDs. We constructed an outcome variable indicating whether individuals had been informed by a healthcare practitioner that they had any of the above NCDs. The outcome was defined as responding ‘yes’ to one or more of the specified conditions. Depression and mental health were included together with the physical conditions. We then explored various factors associated with having any NCD based on that combined outcome. We also examined NCD multimorbidity, defined as the presence of two or more self-reported NCD conditions in the same individual. A separate combined comorbidity variable was created by incorporating HIV infection status together with NCD conditions to explore the overlap between HIV and other chronic conditions. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess factors associated with self-reported ‘any NCD’. A separate combined chronic condition count variable was constructed by adding HIV infection status to the number of self-reported NCD conditions. For example, participants living with HIV and reporting two NCDs were categorized as having three total chronic conditions. Covariates included age, sex, province, place of residence (urban/rural), HIV status, employment status, and wealth quintile. Wealth quintiles were derived using the standard ZIMPHIA household wealth index approach, which is based on household assets, housing characteristics, and access to utilities and services. Participants were categorized into five wealth quintiles (poorest, second, middle, fourth, and richest) according to the distribution of the composite household wealth score. Variables were selected based on epidemiological relevance and data availability within the ZIMPHIA dataset. Analyses were restricted to participants with complete data for variables included in the regression models. Missing responses were minimal and were handled using complete-case analysis. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of ZIMPHIA, including sampling weights, clustering, and stratification, to generate nationally representative estimates. These statistical analyses were conducted using STATA®, version 18. Geospatial analysis was conducted in QGIS using province-level aggregated prevalence estimates to visualize the distribution of self-reported NCDs among people living with HIV across Zimbabwe. Choropleth map categories were generated using quintile classification to allow comparison of provinces based on the distribution of prevalence values across the dataset. The statistical significance of the findings was assessed using p-values < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Ethical considerations

All participants provided written informed consent. The study was carried out in line with the declaration of Helsinki ethical considerations. The study was approved by the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe, Number MRCZ/A/2500.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

The data used included 19,535 participants from a nationally representative population-based survey. Most participants were female (n = 11,871; 60.8%), resided in rural areas (n = 14,202; 72.7%), were not infected with HIV (n = 16,577; 87.1%), and were not employed in the last 12 months (n = 11,268; 57.7%). A high proportion of participants were aged 15–24 years (n = 5,793; 29.7%) and belonged to the poorest wealth quintile (n = 4,944; 25.3%). Burden of self-reported prior diagnosis of any NCD

About 14% of the population had an NCD (n = 2801; 14.3%). Hypertension was the most prevalent NCD (n = 2245; 10.8%), followed by high blood sugar or diabetes (n = 422; 2.2%). Provincial burden of self-reported prior diagnosis of any NCD among the respondents

Self‑reported NCD prevalence among people living with HIV varied across provinces, with the highest burdens concentrated in the eastern and northeastern regions (notably Manicaland and Mashonaland East, ~ 15.0–17.7% per the map’s legend), moderate prevalence in adjacent provinces (roughly 13.5–15.0%), and lower prevalence in several southern and western provinces including Matabeleland North, Matabeleland South, Bulawayo and Harare (approximately 7.8–13.5%). The pattern shows spatial clustering of higher reported NCDs in the east/northeast and lower reported burdens in the west/south, though these estimates reflect self‑reported prior diagnosis and therefore may be influenced by regional differences in health‑care access and screening.

Factors associated with self-reported prior diagnosis of any NCD

Older age groups had higher adjusted odds of reporting a prior NCD diagnosis compared with participants aged 15–24 years. Participants aged 25–34, 35–44, and 45–54 years were more likely to have self-reported prior diagnosis of any NCD than those aged 15–24, adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 2.81 (95% CI: 2.32–3.41), AOR = 5.67 (95% CI: 4.71–6.83), and AOR = 12.50 (95% CI: 10.37–15.06), respectively. Additionally, Participants aged 55–64 years had higher adjusted odds of self-reported prior NCD diagnosis than those aged 15–24, with AORs of 18.03 (95% CI: 14.91–21.80) and 30.23 (95% CI: 25.10–36.43), respectively. Female participants had higher adjusted odds of self-reported prior NCD diagnosis compared with males, AOR = 2.48 (95% CI: 2.24–2.75). Participants infected with HIV were less likely to have self-reported prior diagnosis of any NCD than the uninfected, AOR = 0.59 (95% CI: 0.52–0.67). Higher wealth quintiles were associated with higher adjusted odds of self-reported prior NCD diagnosis. Participants in the second, middle, fourth, and richest wealth quintiles were more likely to have self-reported prior diagnosis of any NCD than those in the poorest quintile, AOR = 1.25 (95% CI: 1.09–1.44), AOR = 1.65 (95% CI: 1.43–1.91), AOR = 2.16 (95% CI: 1.79–2.60), AOR = 2.80 (95% CI: 2.27–3.46), respectively. After adjustment for other covariates, province, area of residence, and employment status were not significantly associated with self-reported prior NCD diagnosis. More details are in Table 3.

Discussion

We set out to determine the burden and risk factors of self-reported NCDs in Zimbabwe. The findings reveal that approximately 14.3 per cent of the population were affected by at least one NCD. The rich dataset from the ZIMPHIA survey provides a nuanced understanding of the risk factors associated with NCDs, shedding light on demographic and socioeconomic variables that contribute to this public health crisis.

We also noted multiple self-reported NCDs, which significantly complicate healthcare management and can lead to worse health outcomes. Individuals with these interconnected conditions often face increased health risks due to shared risk factors like poor lifestyle choices, inflammation, and medication interactions. For instance, NCDs can exacerbate each other; hypertension can worsen kidney disease, while diabetes increases cardiovascular risk. Additionally, mental health issues such as depression can hinder adherence to treatment, complicating the management of these physical conditions. This interplay requires a multidisciplinary approach to care that addresses both physical and mental health needs concurrently, ultimately improving the quality of life for affected individuals [11].

The survey results indicate that the burden of NCDs increases significantly with age, particularly among individuals aged 54 and above. This trend is consistent with global observations where NCDs disproportionately affect older populations, as ageing is associated with biological changes, lifestyle factors, and accumulated risk exposure [12]. The risk of developing NCDs grows significantly with advancing age; older individuals are often more likely to be affected by multiple NCDs simultaneously, complicating management and treatment strategies [13]. Although the prevalence of self-reported NCD diagnosis was lower among younger participants, early prevention strategies remain important because behavioural and metabolic risk factors established earlier in life may contribute to later NCD development [14].

Gender disparities play a critical role in the prevalence of NCDs in Zimbabwe, with women exhibiting higher rates than men. This finding aligns with research indicating that women face unique health risks, influenced by social determinants such as gender inequality, economic dependency, and limited access to healthcare services [13, 15]. Women often experience different manifestations of chronic illnesses and may face barriers in receiving timely diagnosis and treatment, further exacerbating health disparities [16].

Another notable factor is socioeconomic status. Individuals in higher wealth quintiles may genuinely experience higher NCD burden due to lifestyle-related factors such as increased consumption of processed foods, sedentary lifestyles, and reduced physical activity [17, 18]. In addition, wealthier individuals often have better access to healthcare services and screening, increasing the likelihood of diagnosis and self-reporting of NCDs compared with poorer populations. Therefore, the observed differences may reflect both true differences in disease burden and differential detection.

The finding that PLHIV had lower adjusted odds of self-reported prior NCD diagnosis than HIV-negative participants differs from findings from several previous studies [19]. One possible explanation is healthy survivor bias, whereby PLHIV who remain engaged in long-term care may represent healthier individuals, while those with severe comorbidity may be underrepresented due to mortality or loss to follow-up. Additionally, PLHIV often have more frequent healthcare contact and screening opportunities, which would be expected to increase diagnosis and reporting of NCDs. Therefore, the observed lower odds are unlikely to reflect a biological protective effect of HIV against NCDs and should be interpreted cautiously [20].

Although unadjusted provincial differences in self-reported NCD burden were observed descriptively and in spatial mapping, these differences were not statistically significant after adjustment for other covariates in multivariable analysis. The observed spatial patterns may nevertheless reflect differences in healthcare access, diagnostic coverage, or underlying cardiometabolic risk profiles across provinces. Programmatic responses should therefore combine intensified prevention and management where reported burdens are high with strengthened screening and diagnostic outreach in lower‑reporting provinces to ensure geographic inequities in detection do not conceal unmet clinical need.

Limitations

The outcome relied on prior diagnosis and participant recall therefore misclassification and detection bias are possible, particularly among populations with limited healthcare access. Self‑report typically underestimates true prevalence, especially for conditions like hypertension and diabetes that are frequently asymptomatic and commonly undiagnosed in low‑resource settings. Consequently, the absolute prevalences reported here (e.g., 10.8% hypertension) should be interpreted as minimum estimates of population burden.

Because diagnosis requires access to health services, prevalence measured through self‑report is likely higher among groups with better access (for example, wealthier or female respondents who use health services more). This detection bias may inflate observed prevalence among wealthier groups and among women, and may mask true burdens in poorer and less‑connected populations.

ZIMPHIA 2020 is cross‑sectional, so associations reported here are correlational and do not establish temporality or causality between risk factors and NCD diagnosis.

Residual confounding, for example, by unmeasured behavioural risk factors, healthcare utilisation, or comorbidity severity may influence the associations, including the unexpected lower adjusted odds of self‑reported NCDs among PLHIV.

The survey question assessed whether respondents had ever been told by a health worker that they had a condition, which more closely reflects cumulative lifetime diagnosis rather than point prevalence. For episodic conditions such as depression, this approach may overestimate current prevalence because individuals with prior episodes may continue to report a previous diagnosis even if symptoms are no longer active.

The ZIMPHIA 2020 survey was conducted in 2020. Population health, service delivery, and risk factor distributions may have changed since data collection; trends in NCD prevalence or access to diagnosis may therefore have shifted. This temporal limitation constrains inference about current burden.

Although ZIMPHIA used a complex survey design and sampling weights, and our analyses accounted for key covariates, interaction effects (for example between HIV status and age or sex) and sensitivity analyses using alternative outcome definitions (e.g., excluding depression or treating multimorbidity separately) would further test robustness. Future analyses should examine these specifications and, where possible, triangulate self‑report with biomarker data.

However, the ZIMPHIA 2020 data provide useful, nationally representative insight into self‑reported NCD diagnoses in Zimbabwe. While absolute prevalences are likely underestimated due to self‑report and differential access to diagnosis, the consistent patterns by age, sex, and wealth identify priority groups for screening and preventive action. Strengthening primary care screening and integrating NCD services with existing platforms, while improving diagnosis among underserved groups, should be key components of Zimbabwe’s public health response.

Recommendations

Implementing regular screening programs targeted at high-risk populations, particularly the elderly and urban residents PLHIV, can facilitate early detection and intervention. Early detection has been shown to significantly improve health outcomes and reduce healthcare costs associated with the advanced management of chronic diseases [21].

Developing robust health education initiatives to increase awareness of NCDs and their risk factors is vital. Community outreach programs should focus on lifestyle modification, including dietary changes and increased physical activity, particularly in urban settings where lifestyle risks are more pronounced. Evidence suggests that health educational interventions can lead to substantial improvements in health [22].

Health policies should be tailored to address the specific health needs of women, ensuring equitable access to preventive care and treatment for NCDs. Incorporating gender-sensitive approaches in health planning and programming is essential for reducing disparities in health outcomes and improving overall population health [15].

Engaging multiple sectors, such as education, agriculture, and urban planning, can foster environments conducive to healthier lifestyles. A multi-sectoral approach is essential for creating comprehensive strategies that tackle the upstream determinants of health in an ageing cohort of PLHIV.

Conclusion

The ZIMPHIA 2020 survey offers critical insights into the burden and risk factors of NCDs in Zimbabwe and their potentially growing impact. The interplay of age, gender, education, socioeconomic status, urbanization, and HIV status illustrates the need for tailored public health strategies that address the multi-dimensional nature of health in the population. As the prevalence of chronic diseases continues to rise, robust policy responses that integrate NCD management into healthcare frameworks are essential to mitigate their impact thereby reducing associated costs and improving its position in addressing other current and future healthcare challenges.
