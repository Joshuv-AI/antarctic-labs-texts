Temporal profile of cerebrospinal fluid galactin-3 and associated cytokine responses after severe traumatic brain injury in patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Melisa Cetin, Ping Yip, Wing Sze Leung, Zhou-Hao Liu
Published: 2024

Introduction

There is a limited understanding of the pathophysiology underlying severe traumatic brain injury (TBI), a major cause of mortality and disability worldwide.1 Galectin-3 is an alarmin that promotes neurodegeneration in the TBI mouse model and studies have suggested it to function as a master regulator of cytokines, inducing immunopathological responses in various diseases.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 However, the temporal expression of galectin-3 in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of severe TBI patients has not been determined. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the temporal CSF expression of galectin-3 and associated cytokines in the CSF of severe TBI patients at days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-injury.

Methods

CSF from severe TBI patients (n = 35) was collected via external ventricular drain once daily for up to 7 days post-TBI, as well as singular non-TBI samples (n = 35). CSF galectin-3 and associated cytokines IL-1b, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-a, CCL2, and CCL20 were studied using the multiplex bead array. The C-reactive protein (CRP) blood levels and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores were also assessed. The Modified Rankin scale (mRS) was used to organise patients into three groups based on their functional outcome at 6 months post-injury: mRS 6 (died), mRS 5 (severely disabled), and mRS 1–4 (mild-to-moderately disabled). The mRS score was 1-4 for non-TBI control.

Results

This study demonstrated that CSF galectin-3 and several related cytokines were significantly increased in severe TBI compared to non-TBI patients. A comparison between groups based on the global disability mRS score revealed that the mRS 5 group had significantly increased galectin-3 at days 1 and 3 and IL-6 at days 3 and 5, compared with the mRS 6 group, post-injury. Furthermore, mRS 5 group had significantly increased levels of galectin-3 at days 1 and 5, IL-6 at days 3 and 5, IL-10 at day 5, and CCL20 at day 1 post-injury compared to the mRS 1-4 group. Cytokines IL-1b, TNF-a, and CCL-2 showed no significant difference between mRS groups and time points. Receiver operating characteristic curve analyses revealed that the area under the curve (AUC) for comparison between mRS 6 and mRS 5 were galectin-3 at day 1 (AUC = 0.798), day 3 (AUC = 0.764), day 5 (AUC = 0.764), and IL-6 at day 1 (AUC = 0.733) and day 3 (AUC = 0.733). Comparison between mRS 5 and mRS 1-4 were IL-6 at day 3 (AUC = 0.733) and day 5 (AUC = 0.859), and CCL-20 at day 5 (AUC = 0.060). There were no differences in sex, age, GCS score, CRP and types of trauma-induced haematoma based on the different mRS groups.

Conclusion

The study suggests that increases in proinflammatory cytokines galectin-3, IL-6, CCL-2, and CCL-20 at acute stages of injury is detrimental. Additionally, an increase in anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 at the acute stage is also unfavourable as patients who are unresponsive to the injury are more likely to die. In summary, changes in CSF galectin-3 and associated cytokines correlate with functional outcomes suggesting they are potential biomarkers for diagnosis, prognosis and a therapeutic target for severe TBI.
