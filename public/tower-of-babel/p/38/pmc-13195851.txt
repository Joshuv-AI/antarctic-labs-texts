Network analysis of suicidal tendency and mental health symptoms in college students: a gender-differences comparison
Authors: Mingfa Wu, Hui Wang, Ming Li, Zhenyang Gao, Qingjing Lei, Yiwen Yan, Dorje Drolma, Jingyu Yang, Zhongli Lan, Lijuan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide is a major global public health concern, particularly among university students, who exhibit higher rates than their non-student peers. Although gender disparities in suicide rates are well documented, the underlying network structure of suicidal tendencies and comorbid psychological symptoms across genders remains insufficiently understood.MethodsPsychometric network analysis was conducted to examine the interrelations between suicidal tendencies and core psychological symptoms among 1,021 vocational college students (488 males, 533 females). Gaussian Graphical Models were estimated using the EBICglasso method to identify central and bridge nodes, and Network Comparison Tests were used to evaluate gender differences in network structure, global metrics, and edge weights.ResultsGender-specific networks revealed distinct patterns. Suicidal preparation emerged as a highly central symptom and stronger bridge node among females, whereas hopelessness was a key bridge factor among males. Global strength (p = 0.131) did not differ significantly between genders, whereas global expected influence (p = 0.007) showed significant gender differences. Several nodes and edges also demonstrated gender-specific patterns.ConclusionsDepression emerged as a pivotal core symptom, and suicidal preparation acted as a crucial bridge linking psychological symptoms to suicide risk among vocational college students. Significant gender differences were observed in symptom centrality, bridge functions, and specific symptom–symptom interactions. Females appeared more vulnerable through pathways involving suicidal preparation, whereas males were more affected by hopelessness. These findings underscore the need for gender-tailored suicide prevention strategies targeting these distinct network mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04428-2.

Background

Research background and gender differences

In recent years, mental health problems among university students have become an increasingly serious concern worldwide, with suicide recognized as one of the most pressing global public health challenges [1]. According to the World Health Organization’s 2019 global health estimates, approximately 700,000 individuals die by suicide each year, making it the fourth leading cause of death among people aged 15–29 years [2]. This alarming statistic highlights the vulnerability of young populations during the transition to adulthood, a period often characterized by academic pressure, identity exploration, and emotional instability [3].

Gender disparities in suicide are pronounced, with suicide ranking as the fourth leading cause of death among males and the third among females in this age group [4]. China, which reports the highest suicide mortality in the Western Pacific region, recorded 72,515 male and 43,809 female suicide deaths in 2019. Suicide is also the second leading cause of death among Chinese youth aged 15–29 years [2, 5]. Evidence further indicates that university students are at particularly high risk, exhibiting suicide rates 2–4 times higher than those of their peers, with female students demonstrating significantly higher rates of suicidal ideation and attempts, even though completed suicide remains more common among males [6, 7]. In contrast, on the global level, male suicide rates remain approximately 2–3 times higher than those of females [8]. These findings underscore the severity of adolescent suicidality, and the striking discrepancy further highlights the multifaceted nature of suicide risk across gender and context.

Beyond these epidemiological patterns, psychological models suggest that suicidality arises from the interaction of individual vulnerabilities and environmental stressors [9]. Key risk factors include mood disorders, such as depression, personality disturbances, and deficits in emotion regulation, which may increase susceptibility to suicidal thoughts and behaviors [10–12]. Depression, in particular, has been consistently associated with suicidal ideation, planning, and preparatory behaviors, highlighting its central role in adolescent suicidality [13, 14].

Gender may influence these pathways through differences in emotional, cognitive, and social processing. Specifically, females are more likely to internalize distress, experience depressive symptoms, and engage in rumination, which can amplify suicidal ideation and preparatory behaviors [12, 15]. These patterns are expected to increase connectivity among internalizing and planning-related symptoms within female networks, leading to greater overall symptom propagation. In contrast, males may express distress through externalizing behaviors, increasing impulsive risk for completed suicide [12, 16, 17]. These gender-specific emotion regulation and coping patterns may increase connectivity among internalizing and planning-related symptoms in female networks, providing a plausible mechanism for greater symptom propagation risk.

Taken together, adolescent suicidality emerges as a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors, with gender-specific pathways shaping risk profiles. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for developing targeted interventions and for hypothesizing network-based differences between male and female students in symptom propagation.

Advancements in psychopathological network analysis

The emergence of psychopathological network analysis has provided innovative perspectives on the interactive mechanisms among psychological symptoms [18]. Unlike traditional latent variable models that assume symptoms are passive indicators of an underlying disorder, this approach conceptualizes mental disorders as systems of mutually interacting symptoms [19–21]. By mapping these complex networks, researchers can identify core nodes that maintain symptom clusters and bridge nodes that connect distinct symptom communities, thereby revealing potential leverage points for clinical intervention [22]. Existing research highlights that adolescent suicidal behaviors are closely intertwined with comorbid symptoms such as depression and anxiety [23]. Moreover, family cohesion has been shown to mitigate suicidal ideation, and this protective effect varies across gender and ethnic groups [24].

Despite notable progress, research on suicidal tendencies and psychopathological networks among vocational university students remain scarce, particularly regarding systematic gender differences in network structures. Previous studies have demonstrated that males and females often exhibit distinct mental health symptom networks, suggesting that gender may shape the organization and interconnection of psychopathological symptoms [25–27]. Within network analysis, key quantitative metrics—such as strength (S), expected influence (EI), bridge strength (BS), and bridge expected influence (BEI)—are critical for identifying central symptoms and understanding how specific nodes bridge distinct symptom clusters, thereby influencing the overall dynamics of suicidality networks [28].

The present study

The present study aims to address the aforementioned gaps by constructing network models integrating psychological symptoms and suicidal tendencies among vocational college students. Specifically, gender-based Network Comparison Tests (NCT) were employed to examine structural and strength-related differences between male and female networks. To ensure conceptual clarity, “suicidal preparation” in this study is operationalized as the presence of planning or preparatory behaviors preceding a suicide attempt, as measured by items assessing suicide planning and preparatory intent on the employed instrument [29, 30]. By identifying gender-specific symptom centrality and bridging characteristics, this study seeks to clarify distinct comorbidity patterns across genders. The results are expected to provide an empirical foundation for gender-sensitive psychological crisis intervention and to inform more effective suicide prevention strategies within vocational education settings.

Hypothesis

H1

Depression will emerge as the most central symptom in both the global network and gender-specific networks, exhibiting the highest strength and expected influence.

H2

Suicidal preparation is hypothesized to serve as a stronger bridge node among females, whereas hopelessness is expected to act as a more critical bridge node among males, reflecting gender-based differences in emotional regulation and coping.

H3

The female network will exhibit greater overall symptom propagation risk, reflecting stronger connectivity among internalizing and preparatory symptoms, as indicated by significantly greater global expected influence compared to the male network.

Methods

Participants

An online mental health assessment was conducted in 2024 using the “Xinhai System” among students enrolled at Hainan Vocational University of Science and Technology. Initially, 10,313 vocational college students participated in the primary screening phase. Of these, 2,005 students met the inclusion criteria and proceeded to the secondary screening via the Adolescents’ Suicide Tendency Scale (ASTS). Based on the secondary screening results, 1,386 students were randomly selected for further psychological evaluation with the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90). To ensure data reliability and authenticity, responses with deception scores greater than 3 on the ASTS were excluded. After this quality control process, a total of 1,021 valid cases (488 males and 533 females) were retained for the final analysis.

Measures

Adolescents’ suicide tendency scale (ASTS)

The Adolescents’ Suicide Tendency Scale (ASTS) is a 20-item self-report measure designed to assess four dimensions of suicidal tendency: deceptiveness (DEC), hopelessness (HOP), suicidal identification (SID), and suicidal preparation (SP). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The total score, calculated as the sum of items 6–20, ranges from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating stronger suicidal tendencies. The ASTS employed in the present study has been validated and was derived from a previous study [31]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the total ASTS was 0.735, with subscale coefficients of HOP = 0.843, SID = 0.900, and SP = 0.831, indicating that the scale had acceptable internal consistency.

Symptom checklist-90 (SCL-90)

The Chinese version of the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) comprises 90 items grouped into 10 dimensions: somatization (SOM), obsessive-compulsive (O-C), interpersonal sensitivity (I-S), depression (DEP), anxiety (ANX), hostility (HOS), phobic anxiety (PHOB), paranoid ideation (PAR), psychoticism (PSY), and other items (OTH). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely), with total scores ranging from 90 to 450. Higher scores indicate poorer mental health. Screening criteria included a total score > 160 or any factor score ≥ 2, with scores of 2–2.9 indicating mild symptoms and scores ≥ 3 indicating moderate-to-severe symptoms. The Chinese version of the SCL-90 used the present study has been validated previously [32, 33], and it was excerpted from the “Handbook of Commonly Used Psychological Assessment Scales” published in China [34].

In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.987 for the total scale. Subscale reliabilities were as follows: SOM = 0.863, O-C = 0.599, I-S = 0.854, DEP = 0.931, ANX = 0.895, HOS = 0.907, PHOB = 0.931, PAR = 0.926, PSY = 0.857, and OTH = 0.862.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed using SPSS (Version 23.0). Network analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.3.3). Psychological networks linking SCL-90 core symptoms and suicidal tendencies were constructed and estimated using Gaussian Graphical Models with the EBICglasso method to identify central and bridge nodes. Network visualization was performed using the qgraph package [35], while the bootnet package [36] was employed for non-parametric bootstrap analysis (nBoots = 500) to estimate confidence intervals for edge weights and evaluate the stability of centrality metrics. Gender-specific subnetworks (male and female) were compared using the Network Comparison Test (NCT) package [37] to assess differences in network structure, global metrics, and specific node and edge properties.

In the networks, nodes represent individual symptoms or suicidal tendency factors, and edges represent partial correlations between nodes. Blue solid lines indicate positive associations, red dashed lines indicate negative associations, and edge widths reflect the magnitude of the partial correlations. To quantify the relative importance and interconnectivity of nodes, four centrality metrics were calculated:

Strength (S): Measures the overall connectivity of a node by summing the absolute values of all its direct connections. Spearman correlation analyses (see supplementary Figure S1) indicated that suicidality was significantly and positively correlated with all psychological symptoms. Notably, correlations among psychological symptoms were generally stronger than those observed between internal factors of suicidality. Furthermore, males exhibited slightly higher inter-symptom correlations overall, whereas females showed marginally stronger correlations specifically in SP.

Network analysis

Supplementary Figure S3 illustrates the network model comprising two communities: SCL-90 core symptoms and adolescent suicidality. The global network exhibited a density of 0.807 (63 actual edges out of 78 possible), with a mean absolute edge weight of 0.072, indicating a densely connected yet weakly weighted structure. Centrality analyses revealed that depression (DEP) demonstrated the highest strength (S = 1.455) and expected influence (EI = 1.455), highlighting its pivotal role in both overall connectivity and potential cascading effects within the network. Bridge centrality metrics further identified DEP as the most influential bridge node (BS = 0.273, BEI = 0.273) linking the SCL-90 and suicidality communities. Although suicidal preparation (SP) exhibited the strongest bridge strength (BS = 0.512), its bridge expected influence (BEI = 0.181) was comparatively lower than that of DEP. All networks in the present stud demonstrated robust stability, with CS coefficients as follows: global network (S, EI, BS, BEI = 0.750, 0.750, 0.750, 0.750), male network (0.750, 0.750, 0.516, 0.672), and female network (0.750, 0.750, 0.672, 0.672). These findings suggest that the centrality metrics were reliable even when up to 75% of cases were randomly excluded, confirming the robustness of the estimated network structures.

Bridge centrality analysis identified minimal gender differences in BS, with both groups sharing three primary bridge nodes: SP (female: 0.543 vs. male: 0.270), DEP (female: 0.196 vs. male: 0.197), and HOP (female: 0.248 vs. male: 0.158). BEI analysis revealed gender-specific patterns of bridge functionality. Females demonstrated stronger bridging capacity through SP (female: 0.202 vs. male: 0.159), whereas males exhibited stronger BEI values for DEP (female: 0.196 vs. male: 0.197) and HOP (female: 0.071 vs. male: 0.158).

Collectively, the female symptom network showed stronger overall connectivity and greater propagation potential, particularly at nodes representing depression and suicidal preparation. Females also exhibited greater influence at key bridge nodes, suggesting more complex interrelations among psychological symptoms. In contrast, males demonstrated relatively higher bridging effects in anxiety, phobic anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms, reflecting gender-specific mechanisms of symptom propagation. Overall, suicidal ideation emerged as the primary bridging factor in females, whereas depression served this role in males.

Discussion

Understanding the interplay between psychological symptoms and suicidal tendencies in adolescents is crucial for effective prevention and intervention strategies. Prior studies have highlighted depression, hopelessness, and suicidal preparation as key contributors to adolescent suicidality [23, 31, 33, 45], yet few investigations have examined these variables within an integrated network framework that captures both direct and indirect symptom interactions. By applying network analysis to a large sample of university students, the present study provides novel insights into the interconnections among SCL-90 core symptoms and suicidal tendencies, identifies central and bridge nodes, and explores gender-specific network patterns. These findings extend the literature by demonstrating not only the relative importance of individual symptoms but also their roles in propagating psychological distress, thereby informing targeted, gender-sensitive interventions.

Global psychopathological network characteristics

In the present study, we examined the network structure of SCL-90 core symptoms and adolescent suicidal tendencies in university students. The global network exhibited high interconnectedness, with depression emerging as the central hub, demonstrating the highest centrality and bridging connectivity, confirming its role as a key connector among psychological symptom clusters [44]. Suicidal preparation, although less directly connected, functioned as a critical bridge linking symptom communities and mediating the propagation of distress [45–47]. These patterns highlight the pivotal roles of depression and suicidal preparation in the transmission of psychological symptoms and suggest priority targets for early intervention and preventive strategies.

Hopelessness has long been recognized as one of the most critical mental states in the suicidal process, representing a cognitive-affective state of negative future expectancy that bridges depressive affect and suicidal motivation [48]. Critically, depression drives suicidal behavior by reducing perceived control over life, thereby intensifying hopelessness [49]. Within the current network, depression’s centrality reflects its pivotal role in fostering suicidal tendencies, partly through this strong linkage with hopelessness. Empirically, hopelessness amplifies suicidal ideation and planning, predicting the transition from passive thoughts to active suicidal preparation [50]. It should be clarified, however, that the “depression” represented in the current network reflects emotional distress rather than a clinical diagnosis. While major depression perhaps has not consistently been identified as a direct risk factor for suicidal behavior, unbearable psychological pain (psychache) remains the most critical trigger in the suicidal process [51].

Importantly, chronic suicidal ideation—not merely intermittent suicidal thoughts but sustained preoccupation with suicidal thinking—may function both as a risk and as a self-regulatory strategy, depending on the individual’s personality, self-regulatory capacity, and life context. Ronningstam et al. show that such ideations can become part of one’s identity and serve a self-regulatory role, rather than always signaling imminent attempt [52]. Within the present network, hopelessness and suicide identification (i.e., recognizing or labeling oneself as suicidal) may modulate symptom propagation: hopelessness amplifies the transition toward suicidal preparation due to its negative future orientation, while chronic suicide identification without immediate intention may act as a buffer, maintaining ideation in a non-progressive state [53–55]. These dynamics underscore their potential as key nodes for early risk detection and targeted intervention.

By delineating both central and bridging nodes, the current study offers a more comprehensive account of adolescent psychopathology, clarifying how emotional (depression, hopelessness) and cognitive (suicide identification, preparation) symptoms dynamically interact to sustain suicidal processes.

Gender differences in network characteristics analysis

The study further constructed gender-specific mental health network models to examine the hypothesis that males and females would differ in the structural organization and centrality patterns of psychopathological symptoms. Contrary to this expectation, the global network characteristics—including density, average edge weight, and path length—showed no significant gender-based differences. This finding was corroborated by the Network Comparison Test (NCT), which indicated no significant global structural or strength disparities [56–60]. These results suggest that, at the overall system level, the organization of psychopathological symptoms may operate through largely shared mechanisms across genders. Despite the absence of significant global differences, node-level analyses revealed meaningful gender-specific patterns that deepen the understanding of adolescent psychopathology. Depression remained the most central symptom across both genders, confirming its stable function as a transdiagnostic core in psychopathological networks [61]. However, suicidal preparation showed notably greater centrality in females, suggesting that they may experience stronger emotional diffusion and self-focused rumination, which heighten preparatory suicidal ideation. In contrast, among males, hopelessness emerged as the dominant bridge node, acting as a conduit that transforms affective distress into behavioral risk.

These gender-specific configurations are consistent with established mechanisms of suicide vulnerability. Males tend to display greater behavioral decisiveness (e.g., pain tolerance, fearlessness) and stress-reactive suicidality, contributing to more action-oriented suicidal expressions [62, 63]. Conversely, females are more susceptible to persistent affective distress and internalized symptoms, which may explain why suicidal preparation assumes a more central and propagation potential in their psychological networks [63, 64]. Thus, while the overall architecture of psychopathological symptoms is comparable between genders, the key bridging pathways differ, reflecting distinct mechanisms of emotional regulation and suicide risk transmission.

Bridge centrality analyses highlighted suicidal preparation and hopelessness (HOP) as key bridging symptoms in both gender-specific networks [65], confirming their critical role in linking symptom clusters and facilitating the propagation of psychological distress. Consistent with our study hypotheses, suicidal preparation emerged as the dominant bridge in females, whereas hopelessness was more prominent in males. Clinically, this pattern reflects distinct motivational pathways: in females, suicidal ideation and preparation may function as extreme strategies of affect regulation in response to overwhelming inner pain, consistent with motivational models of adolescent suicidality [66]; in males, hopelessness may trigger suicidal behavior through “escape fantasies” when no other solutions seem possible, with potential narcissistic implications [52, 67, 68].These findings align with known gender-specific mechanisms of suicidality: females tend to internalize affective distress and engage in rumination, heightening the role of suicidal preparation in their symptom networks, while males are more likely to convert emotional distress into behavioral intent, with hopelessness acting as a critical trigger [69, 70].

Furthermore, the networks revealed gender-specific propagation pathways. In females, connections between obsession and phobia, as well as between paranoia, suicidal preparation, and suicidal identification, indicate increased vulnerability to anxiety- and identity-related crises [71–74]. In males, symptom propagation is primarily mediated through hopelessness, reflecting differences in resilience and emotional regulation.

These patterns underscore the value of network analysis in identifying gender-specific bridge symptoms, which can serve as practical targets for early intervention. Clinically, interventions that prioritize suicidal preparation in females and hopelessness in males may enhance the effectiveness of gender-sensitive suicide prevention strategies and targeted psychological crisis interventions in university settings. More broadly, mapping central and bridge nodes provides actionable insight for early screening and tailored mental health interventions.

Contributions

This pioneering study in China employs psychometric network analysis to map the interconnections between suicidal ideation and core mental health symptoms (depression, anxiety) among college students, explicitly examining gender differences for the first time. First, it identifies core symptoms with high network centrality, notably depression, and highlights critical bridge symptoms, particularly suicide preparation, which facilitate connections between symptom clusters. Second, the analysis reveals significant gender-specific patterns: while depression exhibits the strongest overall centrality across both genders, suicide preparation demonstrates markedly greater bridge functionality in females, acting as a primary connector to suicidal ideation, whereas hopelessness serves as the dominant bridge in males. Third, these findings provide clear implications for clinical practice and preventive strategies: depression should be a primary target for intervention, while suicide preparation and hopelessness warrant focused early-warning measures. Importantly, the observed gender differences suggest the need for distinct, stratified approaches, including precision screening, early-warning protocols, and targeted psychoeducation, to support evidence-based tertiary prevention in university settings [75].

Limitations and future directions

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, sampling exclusively from a single vocational university in Hainan restricts the generalizability of the findings to other regions or academic institutions, particularly given China’s socioeconomic and educational diversity. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal regarding symptom interactions and limits the ability to capture dynamic changes in network structure over time. Third, although most scales demonstrated acceptable reliability, the relatively low Cronbach’s α for certain subscales (α < 0.7) may compromise the stability of node estimates in network modeling. Fourth, the EBICglasso method, while widely used, tends to produce sparse networks and may overlook weaker but potentially clinically relevant connections. Fifth, reliance on self-report measures introduces the possibility of response biases, and the absence of contextual variables limits ecological validity.

Future research should address these limitations by employing longitudinal, multi-center designs, integrating behavioral and physiological indicators, and validating findings across diverse student populations using psychometrically robust instruments. Such approaches would enhance both the internal and external validity of network analyses and provide more actionable insights for gender-sensitive intervention strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that depression functions as the core symptom linking psychological distress to suicidal tendencies among vocational college students, while suicidal preparation and hopelessness act as key bridge symptoms mediating risk propagation. Gender-specific patterns were observed: suicidal preparation plays a particularly important role in females, whereas hopelessness is more prominent in males. These findings highlight the need for gender-sensitive mental health strategies, emphasizing early identification and intervention targeting bridge symptoms, alongside continued focus on depression as a central therapeutic priority. Overall, network analysis provides a nuanced framework for understanding symptom interactions and informing tailored campus suicide prevention programs.
