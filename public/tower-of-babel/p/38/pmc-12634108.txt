Ambient temperature and deaths from homicide in Brazil from 2010-2019: a nationwide space-time–stratified case-crossover study
Authors: Rahini Mahendran, Rongbin Xu, Pei Yu, Micheline S Z S Coelho, Paulo H N Saldiva, Shanshan Li, Yuming Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious evidence suggests a positive association between temperature and homicide, but the association was less clear in Brazil where homicide is one of the leading causes of death. This study aimed to quantify the association between ambient daily temperature and homicides in Brazil with potential lag effects and to quantify the temperature-attributed fractions of homicides in Brazil. A space–time-stratified case-crossover design with distributed lag models was used to evaluate the temperature-homicide association from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2019, in Brazil. The odds ratios, attributable fractions, and their CIs were calculated. Overall, every 5°C increase in daily mean temperature was associated with a 10.6% (OR = 1.106, 95% CI: 1.085–1.127) increase in homicidal deaths at lag of 0-8 days. The temperature-homicide association is stronger for female individuals and those aged 60-69 years, homicides by fights, sharp objects or firearm, and in north region. During the study period, 1.8% (95% CI: 1.1–2.7) of homicides could be attributed to temperature above immediate region-specific median temperature corresponding to 10 921 additional deaths (95% CI: 6350-15 372). Our nationwide study suggests that the homicides in Brazil may increase with temperature and recommends targeted preventions for certain risk groups to high temperature, considering future climate change circumstances. This article is part of a Special Collection on Environmental Epidemiology.

Introduction

Homicide is a serious global public health threat. According to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC),1 globally 6 homicides per 100 000 population was reported in 2017, corresponding to a total of 464 000 homicidal deaths. The second highest homicide rate in South America was reported in Brazil (150 per 100 000 population),2 and homicide rates in Brazil have been consistently high for the past 3 decades beginning in 1990 compared with the global average over the same period.3

Climate change has been incorporated into sustainable development goals as it became one of the highest priority areas for human health that warranted immediate actions. The rising average temperature along with climate change around the world is posing significant impacts on human health and wellbeing, such as increased heat-related mortality/morbidity, loss of labor capacity, and productivity.4 Previous evidence also suggests that increase in temperature may contribute to more violent crimes5 by increasing people’s aggressive behavior,6 or outdoor activities such as recreational pursuits such as sports, social gatherings, and sightseeing typically undertaken during holidays or leisure time which may lead potential victims and offenders to stay outside.

A considerable number of studies investigated the association between temperature and homicide. But the findings are inconsistent where some studies found a significant and positive temperature-homicide association, while others found no association. Mainly these studies are either based on weekly, monthly, or annual crime data7-9 and did not consider acute and short-term effects of temperature on homicides. The studies on monthly or annual crime data cannot capture the variations within a month. Of several studies with daily crime data to evaluate the temperature-homicide association, most were based on a single city or state and did not consider the potential lagged effects of temperature.5,10 A few studies used cross-sectional design while not considering regional confounding factors such as population density, varied socioeconomic conditions, and political and cultural contexts.7,11 With this background, the generalizability of these findings is questionable.

To overcome some of these limitations, a few recent studies have used time-stratified case-crossover design on daily crime data of single or multiple cities, found a positive linear association between ambient temperature and homicides,12-14 and further evaluated the lag impacts of ambient temperature on homicide using distributed lag models. In this design, short-term effects of temperature could be well accounted, and individual characteristics (time-constant confounders) and time-dependent confounders like day of the week, seasonality, temporal trends could also be adjusted.15,16 Further integration of distributed lag models into this design could help evaluate the potential lag impacts of temperature on homicides as recent studies suggest that effect of temperature on homicides lasts for a couple of days, creating a temporal displacement.17 Thus, time-stratified case-crossover design is widely used in other public health areas.18,19 In Brazil, case-crossover design has been used to evaluate the association of heat exposure with hospitalization for diabetes and undernutrition and association between ambient temperature and hospitalization for renal disease.20-22 However, no previous study investigated the association between ambient temperature and homicide in Brazil using the same epidemiologic approach. Whether temperature-homicide association varies by individual characteristics (age, sex), geographical location, and season also warrants further investigation.

This study aimed to quantify the association between ambient daily temperature and homicides in Brazil with potential lag effects. It also aimed to evaluate whether the temperature-homicide association varied by age, sex, location, and season and to quantify the fractions of homicides in Brazil attributed to ambient temperature.

Methods

Data collection

Data on homicidal deaths were collected from the Brazil Mortality Information System (Sistema de Informação sobre Mortalidade).23 We collected data from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2019, for all 510 Brazilian immediate regions covering 5 macro-regions (North, Northeast, Central-West, Southeast, and South). The 5 macro-regions were geopolitically defined for statistical purposes and to distribute government funding.24 The immediate-region, an administrative unit in Brazil, comprises groupings of municipalities. With 5570 municipalities across Brazil, they are organized into 510 immediate-regions. While the municipality serves as the smallest administrative unit, the immediate-region represents the next larger administrative level. It’s important to note that immediate-regions exhibit considerable variation in both area and population averages. Figure 1 shows crime rates per 100 000 and mean temperature for 510 immediate-regions in Brazil during the study period. The data comprised information of municipality (a smaller geographical unit than immediate-region), date, primary cause, age, and sex of each death record. The primary cause of death was coded according to the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Homicide deaths were defined as deaths with primary causes coded as assaults (ICD-10 codes: X85–Y09). The 3-character subdivision of the ICD-10 code was used to group the type of the assault into 7 categories for subgroup analysis. For example, from X93 to X95 refer to homicides from firearms (see Table S1).

(A) Crimes per capita for 510 immediate-regions in Brazil from 2010–2019. (B) Daily mean temperature for 510 immediate-regions in Brazil from 2010–2019.

The population-weighted daily mean temperature within each immediate-region was calculated from hourly temperature records from ERA5 reanalysis dataset at 0.25° spatial resolution (approximately 28 km × 28 km). Current publicly available ERA5 reanalysis dataset was produced by European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, and it includes a detailed record of the global atmospheric, land surface, and ocean waves data from 1950 onwards.25 Based on ERA5 data, we also calculated daily mean dew point temperature from hourly records. The daily mean temperature and dew point temperature were then used to calculate daily mean relative humidity using the humidity R software package.26

Weather specific to the immediate-region was represented by the average weather data obtained from all grids within the official boundary of immediate-regions. The population data for each immediate-region in each year were collected from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (BIGS). Daily weather data were linked to homicidal death records according to immediate-region and date. This study was approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee. The Brazilian Ministry of Health did not require ethics approval or informed consent for secondary analysis of anonymized data from the Brazil Mortality Information System.

Statistical analyses

Assessing the temperature-homicide association

The association between ambient temperature and deaths from homicide was assessed by a space time–stratified case-crossover design with conditional logistic models.13,22 For each homicidal death record, the daily mean temperature of the case period was compared with those in the control periods in the same immediate-region. Case period in this study was defined as the day when the death (from homicide) was recorded. For each homicidal death, the same days of the week in the same calendar month and year and the same immediate-region were considered as control periods. Therefore, having 3 or 4 control periods for each case period in this self-matched design could control confounders unlikely to change in a short-period such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, and climate characteristics, and time-dependent confounders (seasonality and day of the week).15,16

The conditional logistic regression model was used to fit the relationship between ambient temperature and deaths from homicides. In this model, dependent variable is binary, indicating the observation status (1 = case, 0 = control), and daily mean temperature, as an independent variable, was added to the model using cross-basis function.18 The cross-basis function was used to model both exposure-response and lag-response relationships at the same time. In the model, holiday effect was controlled as a binary variable, indicating whether the date was a public holiday. Daily relative humidity was adjusted for as a cross-basis function.

S1 and S2) and lowest value of BIC for linear model (1135047) compared to nonlinear model (1135108) showed that the relationship between homicidal deaths and temperature is linear. Therefore, in the cross-basis function of temperature, a linear function for exposure-response dimension and a natural cubic spline with 3 df for the lag-response dimension (0-8 days) were used. Nonlinear models were used in the cross-basis function of relative humidity for humidity-response dimension (df = 3) and lag-response dimension (df = 3, 0-8 lag days).

Unit of analysis is the immediate-region day. A space time–stratified case-crossover design was used to derive the national odds ratio (OR) using whole Brazilian data.28 Using conditional logistic regression model, we calculated the OR and 95% CI to estimate the association of homicidal deaths for every 5°C increase in ambient temperature. For the stratified analysis, time-stratified case-crossover design was applied to individuals grouped into the relevant subgroup, and the OR and 95% CI were calculated for each subgroup. Subgroup analysis included age group (0-19, 20-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59, 60-69, >70 years), sex, macro-regions, assault types (firearm, sharp object, blunt object, hanging strangulation and suffocation, fights, other means, and unspecified means), day of the week, weekend versus weekday, and seasons (spring, summer, autumn, or winter). Immediate region–specific seasons (hot, cold, or moderate) were also included in the stratified analysis. Hot and cold seasons were defined by the hottest and coldest 4 adjacent months for each immediate-region, respectively. The rest of the 4 months were defined as moderate season. Age and sex were missing for about 2.45% and 0.16% of the dataset, respectively; therefore cases with missing age and sex were excluded in the age- and sex-stratified analysis. Fixed-effect meta-regression with maximum likelihood estimates from the mvmeta package in R software was used to compare the differences in effect estimates for subgroups from the stratified analyses.20,21

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the findings. This included changing the lag days and df in cross-basis function of temperature and humidity. First, maximum lag days were altered from 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 days and df of lag days changed as 3 or 4 in the cross-basis function of temperature. Second, the df of exposure-response and lag-response dimensions changed as 3 or 4 in the cross-basis function of humidity.

R software version 4.0.2 was used to perform all the data analyses. The dlnm29, survival,30 and mvmeta31 packages were used to develop the cross-basis function, conditional logistic regression, and meta-regression, respectively. A 2-sided P value less than .05 was chosen to indicate statistical significance.

Association between ambient temperature and deaths from homicides

The association between daily mean temperature and homicidal deaths at lag of 0-8 days was relatively linear from the initial analysis (Figure S1). There was a positive and significant association between temperature and homicidal deaths observed at a lag 0-3 of days, which gradually decreased and were followed by temporal displacement. It means some homicidal deaths that were supposed to occur at a lag of 4-8 days have occurred in advance at a lag of 0-3 days due to increase in temperature at lag 0 day (Figure 2).

Lag pattern of association between deaths from homicide and every 5°C temperature increase in Brazil.

As shown in Figure 3, overall, every increase of 5°C in daily mean temperature was associated with a 10.6% (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.085–1.127) increase in deaths from homicides at lag of 0-8 days. There was a 15%–3% rise in homicidal deaths in female individuals (OR = 1.153, 95% CI: 1.084–1.226) for each increase of 5°C in daily mean temperature, which is lower than that in male individuals (OR = 1.101, 95% CI: 1.079–1.123). The associations between daily mean temperature and deaths from homicides for different age groups were inconsistent. The strength of the association significantly increased from successive age groups 40 years older and was higher in those aged 60–69 years old, which is a 16.7% increase in homicidal deaths (OR = 1.167, 95% CI: 1.051– 1.297, P = .004) for every increase of 5°C in daily mean temperature. Children and adolescents aged 0-19 years (OR = 1.113, 95% CI: 1.053–1.176, P < .001) and young adults aged 20-29 years (OR = 1.100, 95% CI: 1.066–1.136, P < .001), and 30-39 years (OR = 1.114, 95% CI: 1.072–1.157, P < .001) also showed a stronger effect size compared to those aged 40-49 years. The risk of homicidal deaths associated with increasing temperature was seen across all regions, and the highest association was observed in north region (OR = 1.290, 95% CI: 1.140–1.470, P < .001), whereas the association was weaker in the south region (OR = 1.067, 95% CI: 1.035–1.101, P < .001). Across different types of assaults, homicide from fights showed the strongest association with increasing temperature (OR = 1.216, 95% CI: 1.050–1.409, P = .009) followed by sharp objects (OR = 1.142, 95% CI: 1.088–1.198, P < .001) and firearms (OR = 1.105, 95% CI: 1.080–1.131, P < .001). Homicides from blunt objects, hanging strangulation and suffocation, other, and unspecified means of assaults showed a nonsignificant association with temperature.

Association between temperature (every 5°C increase in daily mean temperature) and homicide over 0-8 lag days (for subgroups: age, sex, 5 macro-regions, assault type).

Figure 4 shows the cumulative association of temperature with homicidal deaths during lags 0-8 days, stratified by day of the week and seasons. The risk of homicides was associated with temperature rise in all days of the week ranging from an OR of 1.066 (95% CI: 1.019–1.116) on Saturdays to that of 1.161 (95% CI: 1.102–1.223) on Fridays. There was a 12.2% increased risk of homicidal deaths on a weekday (OR = 1.122, 95% CI: 1.096–1.149, P < .001) compared to (7.8%) on a weekend (OR = 1.078, 95% CI: 1.045–1.113, P < .001). The association between temperature and homicides was stronger in the immediate region–specific cold season (OR = 1.112, 95% CI: 1.079–1.146, P < .001) compared to the moderate or hot seasons. However, as for all of Brazil, homicides happening during summer showed the strongest association with temperature rise (OR = 1.113, 95% CI: 1.054–1.175, P < .001) followed by spring, winter, and autumn.

Sensitivity analyses

The findings of sensitivity analysis indicated that the effect estimates were slightly changed when the maximum lag days and df of lag days of daily mean temperature were changed in the first instance (Table S3). The OR ranged from 1.085 (95% CI: 1.063–1.107) for lag of 10 days and df of 3–1.129 (95% CI: 1.111–1.148) at the lag of 6 days and df of 4. The results were robust to changing df and df of lag days of relative humidity (Table S4). These effect estimates included OR of the primary model (OR = 1.106. 95% CI: 1.089–1.127) and 1.109 (95% CI: 1.089–1.131).

Discussion

This study evaluated the association between temperature and homicide using a space time–stratified case-crossover design with the distributed lag models based on nationwide Brazilian daily morality data from 2010 to 2019. We found that the risk of homicidal death was positively and significantly associated with temperature during lag of 0-8 days. The associations were stronger in participants aged 60-69 years, female individuals, and in cities in the north region; this was only significant for homicide from fights, sharp objects, and firearms. We also identified that the homicide-temperature associations were higher during summer and on weekdays. In Brazil, 1.8% of total homicidal deaths could be attributed to the temperature above immediate region–specific median temperature during the study period.

The positive temperature-homicide association found in our study is consistent with previous studies.5,7-11 Recent studies using the same case-crossover design also found that the short-term exposure to ambient temperature is associated with various types of violent crimes,12-14 but the effect estimates of these studies are not comparable with our findings due to the use of different statistical models. Two of the recent studies used the same case-crossover design with distributed lag model and found that every temperature rise of 5°C in US cities was associated with a 4.2% (95% CI: 1.1–7.3) increase in intentional homicides over a lag 0-7 days,13 and 4.5% (95% CI: 1.03–1.06) increase in sex offenses over a lag of 0-8 days.27 These effect estimates are markedly lower, but lag patterns are quite similar with those of the present study, suggesting association of temperature rise with various types of violent crimes is similar. However, in Brazil, homicide rates may further increase due to its positive association with temperature, lower socioeconomic status32, higher crime rates,33 and poor gun control policies.34

The biological and routine activity theories are the 2 main theories used to explain the association between ambient temperature and homicide.7 The biological theory or temperature aggression theory suggests that hot temperature escalates aggression by increasing discomfort and frustration of people,35 but its underlying biological mechanism is still unclear. Meanwhile, the routine activity theory points out that warm temperature is likely to change routine activity patterns of people which may lead to violence.7 Warm weather makes people engage in outdoor activities more frequently36 and thereby increases social interactions, which may create a suitable environment for violence to occur.37 Increased uncomfortableness and altered routine activities of people as suggested by both theories could provide the basis for the increased homicidal deaths associated with warm weather observed in the present study.

In addition, warmer ambient temperatures are consistently associated with sleep disturbances.38 Impaired sleep quality (either in duration or architecture) has shown to interfere with the secretion of neurotransmitters, a situation that may modify brain functional connection lead to components such as cognition, emotion, and memory.39 In fact, sleep disturbances have been associated with increases in aggressive behavior in prison inmates40 as well as in the general population.41

The temperature-homicide association in our study varied for different groups. Our study found a strong association of temperature rise with the risk of homicides in female individuals and individuals aged 60-69 years . However, homicides in Brazil predominantly reported among male individuals,3 our finding of stronger temperature-homicide association in female individuals and individuals aged 60-69 years was similar to those from Japan suggesting that both groups may be sensitive and vulnerable to temperature rise.14 Additionally, both female individuals and individuals aged 60-69 experience greater difficulties in thermoregulation, impacting their ability to dissipate heat, increasing their risk of heat-related issues like aggression and making them more vulnerable to becoming victims of crime.42,43 The stronger temperature-homicide association in individuals aged 60-69, with no association observed in those older, may be due to their active participation in communities and social networks, making them more vulnerable to environmental stressors like temperature rise. In contrast, those who older than 70 years may be less mobile and engaged, resulting in reduced exposure to violent contexts.44 Unlike employed young men, older adults and women who are homebound, those frequently spending time outdoors during warm weather are more likely to become victims of violent crimes under routine activity theory.45,46 In addition, with the growing population of those aged 60-69 years, we can expect to see a rise in overall crime rates among elders47 and changes in reporting crimes may have increased the reported female crime rates.

The effect of daily ambient temperature on homicides is a relatively new area of study, though similar lag patterns have been studied earlier.13,27 The positive association between current day temperature and homicides occurring within 1-2 lag days suggests that some temperature-related homicides take time to develop, as explained by both biological and routine activity theories. In biological theory, an increase in temperature on a given day can disrupt sleep and elevate aggression levels the following day. Additionally in routine activity theory, rising temperatures may encourage more outdoor activities on the same day, which can lead to increased interactions between potential victims and offenders, raising the risk of conflicts that can escalate into homicide in the subsequent days as a means of revenge.

The harvesting effect or temporal displacement of homicides observed within 4-8 lag days in our study suggests that certain homicides are likely to happen eventually, with rising temperatures acting as a trigger that accelerates those homicides’ timing. Examples include intimate partner or family-related homicides and interpersonal homicides such as a means of resolving a conflict or following a dispute between neighbors or friends, which account for about 19% and 12% of all homicides globally.48,49 Some of these types of homicides often arise from various predisposing factors like relationship breakups, controlling behaviors, infidelity, jealousy, and fear,50 while temperature rise may act as a trigger, prompting those homicides to happen earlier by intensifying negative emotions like anger and rage or increasing alcohol consumption.

We observed that the association is stronger during summer which can be explained by biological theory, as rise of temperature in summer is likely to create uncomfortableness. In addition, summer in Brazil mainly leads to vacations where people have time off, and their routine activities are altered by providing opportunities for more violence.51 However, seasonal differences do not seem to affect the temperature-homicide association when looking at the seasonal group effects and difference in P values. Another finding in this study was the higher risk of temperature associated with homicide during weekdays compared with weekends which contradicts the previous findings13,51 and warrants further studies.

We also found that the association varied in different regions, which may be either related to socioeconomic status or temperature variability of different regions.52 There may be various reasons for the higher association in the north region. Amazon in the north region shares the international borders of Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia, the 3 biggest cocaine producers in the world. Due to the Amazon’s immense area and difficult access, it does not have adequate policing and control on its borders, making them routes for illicit activities and an increase in crime.53 Higher crime rates in the north region could also be due to high humidity, because high air moisture level could cause greater discomfort with high temperature.54

Findings of our study could have significant social implication to the sustainable development goals emphasizing climate change and violence, especially in the context of global warming. A significant proportion of homicides were attributable to temperature in our study, suggesting temperature plays an important role in the occurrence of homicides in Brazil; this proportion may increase as the temperature continues to increase with climate change.55 Additionally, our study also provides some useful information for targeted prevention programs, particularly for female individuals, individuals aged 60-69 years, and residents in the north regions. Targeted prevention programs aimed at reducing homicide rates could involve a range of interventions. These may include awareness campaigns tailored to female individuals, individuals aged 60-69 years and their families, along with the establishment of safety and support networks in high-risk areas. Additionally, implementing 24-hour emergency helplines can provide crucial assistance during times of need. Furthermore, integrating temperature early warning systems, with future temperature forecasts, could be invaluable to law enforcement agencies for strategic patrolling, particularly during high-risk periods such as days with elevated temperatures and subsequent days with lagged effects.

Our study has several strengths. First, this is the first study using time-stratified case-crossover design to assess the relationship between temperature and homicides in Brazil, apart from the US study. This design allows us to adjust confounders which are time-constant and time-dependent such as regional variations and temporal trends. Second, to our best knowledge, ours is the only study that has accounted for lagged effects of temperature on homicides and estimated the proportion of homicides attributable to temperature in Brazil. Although population attributable risk does not fully capture the complexities of shared causality, the formula we used in our calculations incorporates an attributable fraction among the exposed that minimizes confounding effect.56 Our findings indicate that ambient temperature accounts for 1.8% of homicides in Brazil, leaving 98.2% unexplained. This highlights the need for further research to identify other risk factors.57 Capturing lagged effects with the distributed lag non-linear model method is probably beneficial because our method would have captured those who took a longer time to pass away from injuries, hence it is less likely to underestimate the effect size. Third, this study covered all of Brazil spanning for a 10-year period, so the findings could be robust and generalizable to the general population. Finally, our stratified analysis based on age, sex, region, and season could provide insights and useful information for targeted prevention programs on temperature-related homicides. However, this study also had a few limitations. One limitation is that findings of our study may not apply to other countries, as it was based on the Brazilian population. Second, there may be underestimation of temperature-homicide association either from missing data and unreported deaths or using immediate region–specific average daily temperature to represent individual’s heat exposure. The use of finer-scale temperature data might elevate the level of estimates for the temperature-homicide association. The association, however, might also be overestimated if records were misclassified as homicides. Finally, due to unavailability of socioeconomic data and data on aggression level, outdoor activities, day-night variations, and housing conditions, air-conditioning, our study did not evaluate the potential mediators and population density as an effect modifier of temperature-homicide association, and thus further studies are warranted.

Conclusion

Daily ambient temperature was positively associated with homicides in Brazil. The association appeared to be stronger for homicides of female individuals or individuals aged 60-69 years, from fights, sharp objects, or firearm, and for homicides in certain regions. The association could also be modified by season and by day of week, such as the differences between weekdays and weekends. Our study suggests implementing targeted preventions for female individuals and individuals aged 60-69 years who are at high-risk to temperature-induced homicides considering the context of future global warming and warrant further research to identify target groups for the intervention.
