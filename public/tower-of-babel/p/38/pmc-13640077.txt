Artificial intelligence for pediatric rare disease diagnosis: a multimethod study integrating published evidence and clinician interviews
Authors: Jungang Zhao, Jiawei Luo, Qiu Li, Yaolong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric rare diseases are highly heterogeneous and are frequently associated with missed or delayed diagnosis, creating substantial burden for patients, families, and clinicians. Although artificial intelligence (AI), including large language model–enabled approaches, has shown potential for diagnostic support, translation into real-world pediatric care remains limited. A key gap is the mismatch between metric-centric evidence reporting and clinician-defined implementation needs. To address this, we integrated published evidence with clinician perspectives to derive an implementation-oriented evidence-to-requirements framework for AI-assisted pediatric rare-disease diagnosis.MethodsWe used a convergent multimethod design with two complementary evidence sources. First, we conducted a PRISMA-ScR scoping review of four databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus) from inception to December 2025 and included 28 original studies on AI-assisted pediatric rare-disease diagnosis. Second, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 21 pediatric clinicians from 15 departments at a tertiary children’s hospital in Chongqing, China, and analyzed transcripts using inductive thematic analysis. We then integrated findings side-by-side to identify convergences, divergences, and translational gaps.ResultsThe scoping review showed rapid movement toward multimodal and LLM-enabled approaches across several diagnostic task types, including screening or cohort identification, phenotyping, differential diagnostic support, and variant or gene prioritization. Translation-oriented evidence remained uneven, with limited prospective evaluation and inconsistent reporting of fairness, safety, and deployment context. Interview analysis identified four recurrent themes: diagnosis as time-pressured puzzle-solving; AI as a cognitive extender rather than replacement; trust dependent on traceable evidence and transparent reasoning; and demand for structured, actionable outputs with low workflow burden. Integrated analysis revealed a persistent implementation gap between metric-centric publication practices and clinician-defined requirements for real-world adoption.ConclusionsThis scoping review and qualitative interview study does not establish clinical effectiveness of AI-assisted diagnosis. Instead, it identifies implementation requirements that may guide future development and evaluation, including representative multicenter data, prospective validation, evidence traceability, actionability, safety, fairness, and workflow fit. Main limitations include restriction to English-language studies, reliance on umbrella rare-disease terminology, possible missed studies among unscreened records after ASReview-assisted screening, and a single-institution clinician interview sample.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03718-4.

Introduction

Rare diseases, often referred to as “orphan diseases,” represent a heterogeneous group of conditions with low prevalence. Since the enactment of the Orphan Drug Act in the United States in 1983, global attention to these conditions has steadily increased [1]. Although patient numbers for any single disease are small, there are over 7,000 known rare diseases globally. Approximately 70% are genetic in origin, and over 60% manifest during childhood, making pediatric rare diseases a neglected but critical public health issue [2]. Definitions vary across regions. The World Health Organization (WHO) does not enforce a single global definition due to differences in population and healthcare systems; however, WHO reports often align with the European Union definition of a prevalence below 5 per 10,000 individuals (i.e., fewer than 1 in 2,000) [3].

For pediatric patients, reaching a definitive diagnosis for a suspected rare disease is often a prolonged and distressing “diagnostic odyssey” [4]. Rarity, phenotypic heterogeneity, and symptom overlap with common conditions contribute to delayed recognition, repeated referrals, and potentially avoidable investigations [5, 6]. These delays reflect not only disease rarity but also the difficulty of integrating fragmented phenotypic, laboratory, imaging, and genomic information across specialties; across many rare-disease contexts, diagnostic delays may extend for several years, with substantial psychosocial and financial impacts on families [7].

Because these barriers are closely related to information integration, pattern recognition, and timely access to specialized knowledge, artificial intelligence (AI) offers a plausible approach to supporting rare-disease diagnosis. As early as 2015, diagnostic error was identified as a critical patient-safety issue, and AI was proposed as a means to improve diagnostic accuracy [8]. Recent advances in machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), natural language processing (NLP), and large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capacity to handle high-dimensional medical data and detect subtle patterns that may be difficult for humans to recognize [9].

AI applications in this domain are yielding preliminary successes. Deep learning models can analyze facial dysmorphology, medical imaging, or Whole Exome/Genome Sequencing (WES/WGS) data to support recognition of genetic syndromes and prioritization of candidate diagnoses [10]. The emergence of LLMs has further broadened these horizons. While general-purpose models like GPT-4 and Gemini Pro show promise, their accuracy in specialized pediatric cases requires improvement [11]. Consequently, domain-specific models, such as MedFound (pre-trained on massive electronic medical records), have begun to match or exceed human expert performance[ 12]. Furthermore, incorporating structured phenotype ontologies and curated biomedical knowledge resources, such as the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO), can support standardized phenotype representation, genotype–phenotype matching, and more transparent reasoning in complex cases [13, 14].

This multimethod study integrates PRISMA-ScR–identified publications with multi-department clinician interviews conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. The literature component characterizes current model capabilities and reporting gaps, whereas the interview component identifies clinician requirements for interpretability, actionability, trust, and workflow feasibility. Although the single-centre interviews were not intended to estimate generalizable effectiveness, they provided context-rich implementation insights that complemented the published evidence and informed an evidence-to-requirements framework.

Methods

Study design and overall approach

This study employed a multimethod design, integrating two complementary components:

A scoping review of artificial intelligence (AI) applications for the diagnosis of pediatric rare diseases, andA qualitative interview study exploring clinicians’ experiences, perceptions, and requirements related to AI-assisted diagnosis.

A qualitative interview study exploring clinicians’ experiences, perceptions, and requirements related to AI-assisted diagnosis.

Findings from the scoping review and the qualitative interviews were analyzed in parallel and compared side by side. Areas of convergence, divergence, and evidence gaps between the published literature and clinicians’ real-world needs were then synthesized into an evidence-to-requirements framework, intended to inform the future design, evaluation, and implementation of AI-assisted diagnostic systems.

The protocol for the scoping review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251272721). The review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and reported following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines [15, 16].

Scoping review methods

Orientation of the literature synthesis. The scoping component functions as a structured evidence stream for convergent integration. Narrative synthesis therefore emphasizes characteristics and reporting practices that can be compared directly with clinicians’ stated needs (e.g., validation design, provenance and transparency of outputs, safety and fairness analyses, deployment context), while deferring extended standalone descriptive cataloguing to supplementary materials or separate mapping syntheses when appropriate.

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from database inception through December 2025. The search combined terms for AI (including ML, DL, NLP, LLMs, and related algorithms), rare diseases, pediatric populations, and diagnosis/assessment concepts. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary File S2. Implementation-related concepts, including explainability, workflow integration, point-of-care usability, and implementation readiness, were extracted and synthesized as predefined domains rather than used as restrictive search filters.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they involved children younger than 18 years or included a clearly distinguishable pediatric subgroup. Eligible studies evaluated artificial intelligence applications, including machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing, large language models, or related computational methods, when they addressed one or more pediatric rare-disease diagnostic tasks. These tasks included screening or cohort identification, phenotyping, differential diagnostic support, and variant/gene prioritization. In this review, screening or cohort identification refers to identifying patients at elevated likelihood of a rare disease or disease group; phenotyping refers to extracting or structuring clinical features; differential diagnostic support refers to generating or ranking candidate diagnoses; and variant/gene prioritization refers to ranking candidate genes or variants using genotype and phenotype information. We included studies conducted in any clinical or laboratory setting. Eligible publication types were English-language original studies, including peer-reviewed articles and preprints when sufficient methodological detail was available for data extraction.

We excluded reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, letters, studies focused solely on treatment or prognosis without a diagnostic component, and records for which the full text was unavailable.

Study selection

Identified records were downloaded, managed, and de-duplicated using EndNote. Title and abstract screening was conducted using ASReview (version 1.6.5, oracle mode), a machine-learning-assisted prioritization platform. After deduplication, 1,005 records were imported into ASReview. We used a naive Bayes classifier (nb; alpha = 3.822), TF-IDF feature extraction with unigram features and English stop-word removal, the maximum query strategy (max), and the double balance strategy. Keywords were not used as separate features, title and abstract text were not split into separate feature blocks, and one record was presented to the reviewer at each iteration.

The active-learning process was initialized with six prior labels selected before screening: five records known from preliminary searches to be potentially eligible and one clearly noneligible contrast record. After the initial prior labels, ASReview ranked the remaining records according to predicted relevance. Two reviewers independently screened the prioritized queue. Each reviewer decision was entered as a binary relevance label based on the predefined eligibility criteria, after which the model was updated and the ranking of the remaining records was recalculated iteratively. Records judged clearly ineligible during title/abstract screening or post-stop verification were excluded at this stage. Records initially judged potentially relevant or uncertain underwent an additional title/abstract-level verification before full-text retrieval; only records remaining potentially eligible after this verification were advanced to full-text assessment. This verification was treated as part of the title/abstract screening stage rather than as full-text eligibility assessment. Screening was stopped when 300 consecutively screened records were judged irrelevant, consistent with a low-yield stopping approach in priority screening.

To reduce the risk of missed eligible studies after stopping, we performed a post-stop verification procedure. First, we manually checked a random sample of unscreened records (n = 100); no additional eligible study was identified. Second, we confirmed retrieval of prespecified sentinel studies (n = 10) identified a priori from domain knowledge; all prespecified sentinel studies were successfully retrieved during the ASReview-prioritized screening process. Overall, 596 records were manually screened before stopping or checked during post-stop verification. Of these, 551 were excluded during title/abstract screening or verification, and 45 were advanced to full-text assessment; 409 records were not manually screened after the stopping rule.

Following title-abstract screening, the same two reviewers independently conducted full-text eligibility assessments of potentially relevant studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer.

Data extraction and synthesis

A standardized extraction form was developed and piloted. Two reviewers independently extracted study characteristics (year, country, design, setting, single vs. multicenter), disease scope, diagnostic task type, data modalities and sources, sample size and labeling, AI methods and comparators, validation strategy (internal/external/prospective/temporal), performance metrics, fairness and safety considerations, interpretability approaches, deployment details, and reported limitations. Extracted data were cross-verified between reviewers for completeness and accuracy. Disagreements or inconsistencies were resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer when consensus could not be reached. Due to heterogeneity in diagnostic tasks, datasets, and outcome measures, findings were synthesized using descriptive and narrative methods rather than meta-analysis. Extraction fields were mapped to integration targets (e.g., validation strategy, reported metrics, explainability modality, fairness/safety analyses, deployment description) so scoping results could be compared side-by-side with interview themes in Sect. "Integrated synthesis: convergence, divergence, and an evidence-to-requirements framework".

Translation-readiness characterization

Because this was a scoping review, we did not perform formal risk-of-bias appraisal. To support implementation-focused interpretation, we characterized each included study across prespecified translation-readiness domains: validation strategy (internal/external/prospective), reporting of safety/fairness, explainability approach, workflow/deployment context, and prospective impact assessment. Domain-level summaries were used for descriptive comparison and multimethod integration rather than for exclusion or pooled effect estimation.

Qualitative interview methods

Qualitative reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ, 32-item checklist). The completed checklist is provided in Supplementary File S7.

Sampling and participants

We used purposive sampling to recruit clinicians from pediatric subspecialties and with varied levels of clinical experience. The final sample comprised 21 clinicians from 15 clinical departments at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China): rheumatology and immunology, endocrinology, orthopedics, cardiology, traditional Chinese medicine, hematology-oncology, rehabilitation medicine, child health care (developmental pediatrics), dermatology, otolaryngology, nephrology, neurology, neurosurgery, respiratory medicine, and neonatology. Clinicians were approached through hospital-wide departmental coordination to ensure coverage of the target subspecialties; when initially selected participants were unavailable, additional colleague referrals (snowball sampling) were used to support recruitment in those departments. Recruitment and interviews were conducted between January 2026 and March 2026. We did not maintain a centralized log of clinicians who declined participation or could not be scheduled; therefore, non-participation rates and reasons could not be quantified.

This strategy was designed to capture diverse diagnostic contexts relevant to pediatric rare diseases. We did not treat department as a stratum for inferential subgroup analysis. Instead, where subspecialty context shaped theme expression, we report these differences narratively in Sect. "Department-level emphases within shared themes (narrative cross-specialty comparison)" rather than as department-wise effect estimates.

Participant characteristics are reported in the Results section and in Supplementary File S6 (Supplementary Table S6.1) using anonymized IDs (P01-P21).

Data collection

Interviews were conducted by trained research team members with prior experience in qualitative health research. Interviewers did not hold administrative or direct line-management authority over participants. To reduce potential hierarchy-related or social desirability bias, participants were informed that interviews focused on general diagnostic workflows and expectations for AI support rather than evaluation of named individuals, departments, or institutional performance.

A total of 21 semi-structured interviews were completed, one per participant, lasting approximately 40–60 min. Participants were asked about their experience with general AI tools and large language models; prior use of a study-specific AI diagnostic platform was not required. Interviews were conducted in a format convenient to participants (e.g., in-person or secure video/telephone) using a predefined interview guide with open-ended questions and probes to elicit concrete examples from recent clinical cases.

Interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Recordings were transcribed using the Tencent Meeting automatic transcription function and then manually checked and corrected by the research team against the original audio to improve accuracy. Interview topics included rare disease diagnostic workflows and bottlenecks, information-seeking behaviors, perceived benefits, risks and limitations of AI-assisted diagnosis, trust, accountability, ethical considerations, and desired features and workflow integration requirements. Field notes were recorded to capture contextual information and support interpretation during analysis.

Qualitative data analysis

We used an inductive, reflexive thematic approach. Analysis proceeded concurrently with data collection so that the interview guide could be refined iteratively. Recruitment continued until thematic sufficiency was reached, defined as no substantively new themes emerging in successive interviews.

Two analysts independently coded de-identified transcripts using an evolving codebook. Coding, codebook development, analytic memos, and code matrices were managed using Microsoft Word and Excel-based documents. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with third-reviewer adjudication when required. Coding decisions, codebook revisions, and analytic memos were documented to maintain an audit trail.

After transcription and manual correction, interview records were returned to participants for confirmation and signature to verify transcript accuracy. Preliminary codes and final themes were not returned to participants for separate validation. To enhance trustworthiness, we used analyst triangulation, iterative memoing, and cross-specialty comparison of theme expression. Illustrative quotations were selected to represent thematic breadth while protecting participant confidentiality. Quotations were translated from Mandarin into English by a bilingual research team member and checked by another bilingual researcher for accuracy and preservation of meaning. Quotations were edited for clarity without changing intent.

Ethics and confidentiality

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2026 Ethics Review [Clinical Research] 152). All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the interviews. All identifiable information was removed from the transcripts, and the data were stored on access-controlled devices. Findings are reported in aggregate to minimize the risk of re-identification.

Integration of scoping review and interview findings

Findings from the scoping review and qualitative interviews were integrated using a convergent, side-by-side design with a predefined integration matrix.

Step 1: Independent synthesis.

Each evidence stream was first analyzed independently (scoping: study/task/validation/reporting dimensions; interviews: themes/subthemes/requirements).

Step 2: Joint mapping.

Two investigators mapped scoping dimensions to interview-derived requirements across predefined domains (evidence provenance, reasoning transparency, actionability, workflow feasibility, safety/fairness, and prospective impact).

Step 3: Consensus refinement.

Disagreements in mapping were resolved through consensus meetings, with third-investigator arbitration when required. The final evidence-to-requirements framework represents domains with support from both evidence streams and explicit documentation of divergence.

The joint display summarizing evidence convergence/divergence and requirement derivation is provided in Supplementary File S4.

Results

Study selection

The database search identified 2,949 records; after removal of 1,944 duplicates, 1,005 records were imported into ASReview for title/abstract screening. Forty-five reports were advanced to full-text assessment via ASReview. The remaining 960 records were not advanced to full-text assessment: 551 were excluded during title/abstract screening or verification, and 409 were not manually screened after the stopping rule. After full-text assessment of the 45 reports, 27 were excluded because of wrong study type (n = 16), wrong population (n = 6), or wrong outcomes (n = 5), and 18 studies were included via ASReview. Ten additional studies were identified from other sources and included after assessment. Overall, 28 studies were included in the scoping review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and trends

The chronological distribution of the included studies reveals a clear trajectory of technological evolution. Early research (circa 2018) predominantly relied on NLP and rule-based models [17]. A shift occurred between 2019 and 2022 with the introduction of multi-modal deep learning and combined genotype–phenotype systems [18, 19]. After standardizing publication years to the cited version, 16 of 28 included studies were published from 2024 onward, including one 2026 cited record that had been identified before the search cut-off as a 2025 arXiv preprint and was subsequently cited as the peer-reviewed Nature version. This recent concentration indicates a marked increase in research activity associated with large language models, graph-based models, and multimodal task-specific systems. Geographically, research is predominantly led by the United States (n = 10) and China (n = 6), followed by significant contributions from Europe (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Netherlands). Emerging contributions were also noted from India, Colombia, and Mexico, indicating a global broadening of interest. There is a perceptible trend toward large-scale, multi-center collaborations, such as the Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN) in the US [20] and multi-center metabolic screening cohorts in China [21, 22]. Regarding the disease spectrum, studies were categorized into “Pan-rare disease/Undiagnosed cohorts” (n = 10) [11, 19, 20, 23–28], focusing on broad differential diagnosis and ranking, and “System-specific/Disease-group” studies (n = 18) [21, 22, 29–37]. The latter covered metabolic disorders (n = 5) [21, 22, 30, 34] primary immunodeficiencies (n = 2) [35, 38] genetic syndromes with facial phenotypes (n = 6) [18, 29, 37, 39–41] and other areas like neurodevelopmental disorders and rare pediatric tumors [26,31, 32, 36, 37].

Scoping review findings relevant to implementation requirements

The scoping review findings are summarized across three result domains: diagnostic tasks, data modalities, and model families; validation strategies and reported performance; and explainability, fairness and safety reporting, and workflow deployment characteristics.

Diagnostic tasks, data modalities, and model families

Because “diagnosis” encompassed heterogeneous clinical tasks, we interpreted included studies according to their primary task type and workflow position. Applications clustered around four clinical needs: EHR-based screening and cohort identification (n = 7) [17, 30, 31, 33, 34, 38, 42]; phenotype-driven differential diagnosis and reasoning (n = 8) [11, 20, 23–25, 27, 28, 35]. Genomic prioritization and multi-omics integration (n = 7) [17–19, 26, 32, 41, 43]; and image-based or metabolic screening modalities (n = 6) [21, 22, 29, 36, 39, 40]. EHRs were used in 12 studies (structured fields and/or clinical notes) [17, 23, 25, 28, 30, 31, 33, 34, 38, 42]; HPO structured phenotypes appeared in 11 studies, often bridging clinical observations and genomics [11, 17, 20, 23, 24, 26, 28, 29, 43]. Multimodal fusion was reported in 10 studies [11, 18–20, 23, 24, 26, 28, 34, 43]. Sample sizes varied dramatically, ranging from small datasets (< 100, e.g., Young et al. 2025 [11] with 61 cases) to massive cohorts (> 100,000, e.g., Li et al. 2024 v [21] with 355,197 neonates for metabolic screening). Technically, the corpus spans traditional ML (n = 7), DL (n = 11), LLMs/NLP (n = 6), and knowledge-driven integration (n = 8), with recent work increasingly using RAG, graphs, and reasoning-oriented LLM pipelines [24, 28]. Validation strategies, reported performance, and translation readiness

Performance metrics were interpreted in relation to task type, because screening, phenotyping, differential diagnosis, and variant prioritization rely on different validation targets. Validation was heterogeneous. Internal validation was the most prevalent strategy (13 studies), using techniques such as k-fold cross-validation or temporal splitting [21, 22, 24, 25, 27, 29, 34, 35, 37, 39–42]. External validation was reported in 12 studies using independent cohorts or cross-institutional datasets (e.g., Zhao et al. 2025 [28] across multiple public benchmarks; Kauffman et al.,2025 [31] across hospital systems). Prospective clinical evaluation remained rare—exemplified by Schmidt et al. [26] within a national framework, contrasted with recruitment barriers described by Bhasuran et al. []. Screening-oriented models often reported high sensitivity under study conditions [21, 33], whereas differential diagnostic support and variant/gene prioritization tasks showed wide Top-k performance depending on phenotype completeness and curation [23, 18], with robustness concerns under noisy EHR phenotyping and heterogeneous presentations [40, 41].

Explainability, fairness and safety reporting, and workflow deployment

Explainability was reported in 19 studies [18, 20, 21, 23–26, 28–31, 33–36, 39, 43] evolving from feature attribution to more structured reasoning outputs. Methods included traditional feature-importance analyses and SHAP values for ML models, saliency maps for imaging, and attention-based interpretation in deep learning systems. Notably, recent agentic systems introduced traceable reasoning chains with evidence-linked outputs to enhance clinical verifiability and trust.

Fairness and bias reporting remained uneven. Several studies reported subgroup performance concerns (e.g., lower facial-dysmorphology accuracy in specific racial groups), and others acknowledged training-data imbalance toward European ancestry without quantitative adjustment [11, 20, 26, 32].

Despite these technological advances, full integration into clinical workflows remained limited. Most tools were implemented as standalone web applications (e.g., DeepRare [28], PhenoBrain [23], YieldPred [26]) or code repositories rather than embedded directly into EHR-based clinical decision support workflows [17, 18, 21, 22, 33].

Clinician interviews: themes on AI use and requirements (qualitative study)

The interview sample included 21 pediatric clinicians from 15 clinical departments. Of these participants, 15 were female and 6 were male. Professional ranks included attending physician (n = 14), chief physician (n = 6), and resident physician (n = 1). Rare-disease diagnostic experience was reported as frequent (n = 6) or occasional (n = 15). Detailed participant characteristics are provided in Supplementary File S6 (Supplementary Table S6.1).

The interviews yielded four themes (with subthemes) on AI-assisted diagnosis for pediatric rare diseases, providing a complementary qualitative evidence source for integration with the scoping findings. When interviews included subsequent discussion of a specific AI platform or prototype, those platform-specific comments were not included in the thematic synthesis reported here; the present analysis focused on general clinical needs and implementation requirements for AI-assisted diagnosis rather than usability feedback on a particular tool. Additional illustrative quotations, including divergent or cautionary perspectives on AI reliability, input quality, accountability, and workflow burden, are provided in Supplementary File S6.

Theme 1. Rare disease diagnosis as “puzzle-solving under time pressure”.

Clinicians described pediatric rare disease diagnosis as reconstructing a multisystem “puzzle” under time constraints, complicated by incomplete histories, subtle or evolving signs, and frequent cross-disciplinary coordination. Information seeking often occurred after working hours; literature searching and evidence synthesis were repeatedly described as time-consuming and inefficient, particularly for atypical presentations. Illustrative quotation: “I often have to use spare moments between clinical duties to think through difficult cases. If the diagnosis remains unclear, I continue searching the literature after work.” (P09, rheumatology and immunology).

Theme 2. AI as a “second opinion / cognitive extender,” not a replacement.

Participants valued AI for quickly broadening differentials, bridging knowledge gaps beyond one’s subspecialty, and reducing missed possibilities—especially for clinicians with less experience in rare diseases. However, they emphasized clear limits: dynamic clinical trajectories, bedside nuance (e.g., physical examination and gestalt), and the dependence of AI outputs on the quality and completeness of inputs. Illustrative quotation: “AI can broaden diagnostic thinking, but final judgment still depends on bedside examination, dynamic clinical changes, and physician experience.” (P19, respiratory medicine).

Theme 3. Trust depends on evidence provenance, transparent reasoning, and clinical actionability.

Clinicians consistently stated that trust requires traceable evidence (guidelines, reviews, primary studies), explicit linkage between patient features and ranked hypotheses, and avoidance of fabricated citations or unverifiable claims. AI was commonly framed as “literature-assisted thinking” rather than an authority; clinicians retained responsibility for final decisions. Concerns about privacy and medicolegal risk were present but varied across contexts and specialties. Illustrative quotation: “Trust depends on whether evidence is traceable and verifiable; otherwise AI outputs may be misleading.” (P11, otolaryngology).

Theme 4. High-priority requirements: ranked differentials, structured rationale, specific testing pathways, and workflow fit.

Department-level emphases within shared themes (narrative cross-specialty comparison)

The four themes above were consistent across departments, but their salience and concrete illustrations differed with clinical setting—reflecting variation in case mix, documentation burden, and coordination patterns rather than statistically contrasted subgroups. Illustratively, clinicians in inpatient-heavy contexts (e.g., neonatology, respiratory medicine, neurosurgery) more often emphasized consolidating fragmented external records, preparing complex cases for MDT, and timing AI support after initial investigations. Outpatient- or ambulatory-oriented departments (e.g., child health care, dermatology) highlighted visit-length constraints, the need for concise ranked outputs, and low-friction capture of phenotypic detail (including imaging or lesion documentation where relevant). Procedure- or intervention-linked perspectives (e.g., orthopedics, otolaryngology, neurosurgery) recurrently linked AI utility to narrowing pre-procedural differentials and prioritizing non-invasive next steps. Medical subspecialties with long diagnostic odysseys (e.g., rheumatology and immunology, hematology-oncology, endocrinology, nephrology, neurology, cardiology) frequently stressed evidence traceability, genetic and multisystem workup logic, and trust boundaries. Rehabilitation medicine and traditional Chinese medicine participants foregrounded longitudinal follow-up, heterogeneity of labels, and the value of structured summaries that could bridge multidisciplinary encounters.

Key divergence: an “implementation gap”

A consistent implementation gap emerged when comparing the published evidence base with clinicians’ real-world expectations. The literature predominantly prioritizes algorithm-centric performance reported under curated, retrospective, or semi-controlled conditions (e.g., accuracy, AUC, Top-k recall), while prospective validation, assessment of workflow impact, and reporting of deployment constraints remain limited.

In contrast, clinicians evaluate AI diagnostic support primarily through a use-in-practice lens: they emphasize (1) how outputs are generated—including evidence provenance, verifiable citations, and transparent reasoning; (2) how outputs are communicated—structured, prioritized, and cognitively manageable rather than exhaustive; and (3) how tools fit into clinical workflows, particularly under constraints related to time, data-entry burden, privacy, and accountability.

Together, these findings indicate that performance metrics alone are insufficient to predict clinical adoption. Bridging this gap requires expanding evaluation beyond accuracy to include evidence traceability, reasoning transparency, usability and workflow burden, and safety boundaries.

Evidence-to-requirements framework

By mapping evidence gaps identified in the scoping review against clinicians’ prioritized needs, we derived an implementation-readiness framework to guide the design and evaluation of AI-assisted diagnostic systems for pediatric rare diseases (Table 2). The framework comprises five core domains.

First, evidence provenance and traceability require systems to support citation validity, evidence hierarchy or level-of-evidence signaling, guideline recency, and explicit alignment between patient features and cited evidence. Second, reasoning transparency and uncertainty communication require explicit linkage between patient features, ranked diagnostic hypotheses, supporting/refuting evidence, and calibrated confidence or uncertainty statements. Third, clinical actionability requires structured differential diagnoses with supporting and refuting features, together with stratified and specific diagnostic workup recommendations, particularly actionable genetic testing strategies rather than generic suggestions. Fourth, workflow feasibility requires reduced data-entry burden, interoperability, context-sensitive integration, and formal usability and time-cost evaluation. Fifth, safety and fairness require monitoring for hallucinations and unsafe recommendations, subgroup performance assessment, explicit safety boundaries, and clear accountability framing.

Collectively, this framework operationalizes how the current evidence base and clinician perspectives can be translated into measurable targets for benchmarking, iterative system development, and prospective evaluation. Discussion

This multimethod study integrates PRISMA-ScR–identified evidence with multi-specialty clinician interviews as complementary analytic inputs to foreground clinical implementation requirements alongside published evaluation practices. Interview findings clarify why algorithmic advances do not necessarily translate into clinical adoption or routine practice: clinicians judge diagnostic support by evidence grounding, transparent reasoning, actionability, workflow feasibility, and safety—not headline benchmarks alone. The proposed framework should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and implementation-oriented rather than as a validated clinical effectiveness framework. It is intended to guide future system design, benchmarking, reporting, and prospective evaluation, not to establish that current AI tools are ready for unsupervised clinical use.

Across included publications, a chronological shift is evident: early work emphasized conventional NLP or billing-code phenotyping, whereas 16 of 28 included studies were published from 2024 onward, reflecting increasing activity in LLM-, graph-, and multimodal reasoning approaches. Screening and cohort-identification models are positioned upstream to flag patients for further review, phenotyping models often improve the structure of clinical inputs, differential diagnostic tools provide clinician-facing candidate diagnoses, and variant/gene prioritization systems support later-stage genomic interpretation. The main application clusters are summarized in Sect. "Scoping review findings relevant to implementation requirements" and Fig. 3; we avoid repeating that descriptive synthesis here and instead emphasize translation implications. EHR phenotyping can shorten delays but remains sensitive to site-specific documentation; reliance on structured coding may limit utility among undiagnosed patients, underscoring the value of robust learning from unstructured notes and longitudinal signals across heterogeneous settings. Reported screening and early-detection studies illustrate how machine learning can improve pre-diagnostic identification or screening performance relative to conventional thresholds or rule-based approaches in curated cohorts [21, 22, 30, 31, 33, 38].

Genotype–phenotype integration appears to be the most mature domain. Systems that combine HPO-encoded phenotypes with genomic data outperform single-modality approaches and can achieve strong ranking precision for causal variants, illustrating the value of structured phenotype representations in bridging clinical observations and molecular data [18, 20, 26]. Nonetheless, performance is often constrained by uneven coverage in curated genotype–phenotype and variant-interpretation resources, particularly for newly described genes, atypical phenotypes, variants of uncertain significance, and underrepresented populations, as well as by non-representative training data [29, 32]. European ancestry bias is repeatedly noted across genomic and phenotypic datasets, raising concerns that deployment without recalibration could exacerbate disparities in underrepresented populations [20, 26].

Clinician interviews contextualize these advances within the realities of pediatric practice. Across subspecialties, rare-disease diagnosis was consistently described as “puzzle-solving under time pressure,” shaped by incomplete histories, subtle and evolving signs, and the need for cross-disciplinary synthesis. This explains the appeal of AI as a “second opinion” or cognitive extender: clinicians valued the ability to rapidly broaden differential diagnoses, summarize knowledge beyond one’s subspecialty, and reduce omissions. At the same time, participants articulated clear boundaries: bedside nuance, evolving clinical trajectories, and the dependence of outputs on input quality remain domains where human judgment is essential. Most notably, trust was not driven by headline model performance but by three interlocking conditions—traceable and authoritative evidence, transparent reasoning that links patient features to ranked hypotheses, and clinically actionable outputs that support next-step decisions. Concerns about fabricated citations and hallucinations were prominent, aligning with evidence that LLM availability does not necessarily improve diagnostic reasoning in controlled evaluations and reinforcing the need for verification, workflow design, and training [44].

Synthesizing the two evidence streams highlights a persistent implementation gap. The published literature predominantly prioritizes model performance metrics reported in curated or semi-controlled settings, while prospective validation and reporting of workflow impact remain limited; only isolated studies completed prospective evaluations and others faced logistical barriers to recruitment [26, 34]. In contrast, clinicians emphasize how outputs are produced (evidence grounding and reasoning), how outputs are communicated (structured, prioritized, cognitively manageable rather than exhaustive), and how tools fit real workflows under privacy and accountability constraints. This mismatch suggests that accuracy alone is a poor predictor of adoption and that evaluation must expand to adoption-relevant dimensions. In addition, implementation costs and resource requirements were rarely reported in the included studies, limiting assessment of feasibility and scalability in routine pediatric care. In this context, LLMs and agentic systems represent an important direction. General-purpose LLMs show variable accuracy in complex rare-disease cases [11], and real-world experience indicates that generated differentials may be helpful yet still miss final diagnoses, supporting the need for careful integration and human oversight [45]. Retrieval-augmented and agent-based architectures (e.g., DeepRare [28] and GraphRAG [24]) are promising because they ground outputs in external knowledge sources and provide traceable reasoning chains, directly addressing clinicians’ verifiability requirements. However, bedside utility will still depend on validation rigor, workflow fit, safety governance, and explicit communication of uncertainty.

The evidence-to-requirements framework derived from this integrated analysis (Table 2) translates these findings into actionable design and evaluation targets. Specifically, AI-assisted diagnostic systems for pediatric rare diseases should enforce evidence provenance (including citation validity, evidence hierarchy signaling, and guideline recency), provide transparent reasoning and calibrated uncertainty communication, deliver clinically actionable outputs (ranked differentials with supporting/refuting features and stratified, specific workup guidance—particularly genetic testing strategies), minimize workflow burden through feasible integration and usability evaluation, and incorporate explicit safety and fairness safeguards, including monitoring for hallucinations/unsafe recommendations and routine subgroup performance assessment [46, 47]. These requirements respond directly to the mismatch between what the literature most often measures and what clinicians most often need, and they provide measurable targets for benchmarking and prospective evaluation.

Overall, the reviewed literature supports substantial research potential for AI-assisted pediatric rare-disease diagnosis, particularly in phenotype structuring, differential diagnosis, genotype–phenotype integration, and evidence-grounded reasoning. However, current clinical readiness remains limited by insufficient prospective validation, inconsistent safety and fairness reporting, limited EHR/workflow integration, underreported implementation costs, and unresolved questions about accountability in real-world care.

Several limitations should be considered. The scoping review was restricted to English-language studies, and because the search relied on umbrella rare-disease terminology rather than exhaustive disease-specific terms, we may have missed AI tools developed for individual rare conditions when titles, abstracts, keywords, or indexing terms did not explicitly identify them as rare diseases. Although eligible preprints were included to improve timeliness, some included evidence had not undergone peer review at the time of extraction and should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. A more targeted implementation-focused search may identify additional translational studies, but could also miss diagnostic AI studies that do not explicitly use implementation or usability terminology. Rapid methodological advances mean that newly released systems or updates after the search cut-off may not have been captured. The qualitative sample (n = 21 from 15 departments at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University) may over-represent clinicians more engaged with AI, and perspectives of other stakeholders (e.g., genetic counselors, nurses, informaticians, and patients/caregivers) were not included. Because all interview participants practiced at a single tertiary children’s hospital, the qualitative findings primarily reflect local workflows, systems, and service organization and should be generalized cautiously. Shared institutional affiliation between interviewers and participants may also have introduced social desirability bias; we attempted to mitigate this by focusing interview questions on general diagnostic workflows and expectations for AI support rather than on named individuals or units. Finally, when interviews included subsequent discussion of a specific AI platform or prototype, those platform-specific comments were not included in the thematic synthesis reported here; therefore, the findings reflect general clinical requirements rather than usability evaluation of a particular tool.

Conclusions

In conclusion, AI-assisted approaches show research potential for supporting multiple pediatric rare-disease diagnostic tasks, including early detection, cohort identification, differential diagnosis generation, and genotype–phenotype interpretation. However, translation into routine care remains limited by data representativeness, uneven validation rigor, limited prospective evidence, workflow burden, and safety/fairness concerns. By integrating published evidence with clinician perspectives, this study proposes an implementation-oriented framework that can guide future benchmarking, system development, reporting, and prospective evaluation. These findings should be interpreted as implementation hypotheses and priorities rather than definitive practice standards.
