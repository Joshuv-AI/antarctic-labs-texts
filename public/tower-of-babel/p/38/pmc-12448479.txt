Three-Year Outcomes After Programmatic Transitioning to Dolutegravir in the Context of Severe Civil Unrest in Haiti
Authors: Bernard Liautaud, Ana Sanchez Chico, Youry Macius, Sosina Abuhay, Patrice Joseph, Harrison T Reeder, Theo Bolas, Adias Marcelin, Colette Guiteau Moïse, Alexandra Apollon, Pierre Cremieux, Jean W Pape, Serena P Koenig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTenofovir disoproxil fumarate/lamivudine/dolutegravir (TLD) is widely prescribed in low and middle-income countries. Data on long-term outcomes are limited.MethodsWe included all persons with HIV (PWH) ≥15 years of age who initiated or switched to TLD in Port-au-Prince, Haiti. We described treatment outcomes by pre-switch viral load and assessed predictors of virologic failure using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsA total of 10 354 PWH initiated or switched to TLD from November 2018 to March 2021, and were included in the analyses. Of these, 2217 (21.4%) were ART-naïve and 8137 (78.6%) switched from an non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNTRI)-based regimen. Median follow-up time on TLD was 2.8 years (IQR: 2.3, 3.1). HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL was achieved at the latest measurement in 92.7% of recipients of care (RoC) with pre-switch suppression, 88.5% without pre-switch viral load, 58.3% with pre-switch failure, and 81.8% of RoC ART-naïve at TLD initiation. Among treatment-experienced RoC, predictors of ≥1000 copies/mL at latest test included younger age (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.34, 0.57 for age ≥50 vs <30 years), shorter time on ART (aOR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.89, 0.93/year), lower education (aOR: 1.31; 95% CI: 1.13, 1.52), and higher pre-switch viral load: (aOR: 7.23; 95% CI: 6.06, 8.63 for ≥10 000 vs < 1000 copies/mL).ConclusionsVirologic outcomes on TLD are outstanding for PWH with pre-switch suppression. However, rates of virologic suppression are suboptimal among PWH who were ART-naïve at TLD initiation, and among those with a history of pre-switch failure, additional interventions are necessary, including access to long-acting treatment regimens.Keywords: ART, ART resistance, HIV care continuum, integrase inhibitors

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), over 22 million persons living with HIV (PWH) are taking dolutegravir (DTG)-based antiretroviral therapy, with the overwhelming majority taking the single-dose combination tablet of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF)/lamivudine (3TC), and DTG (TLD) [1, 2]. DTG-based regimens offer many advantages over previous antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimens—high potency, a high genetic barrier to resistance, favorable tolerability, and few drug interactions [3]. It is anticipated that DTG will serve as the primary backbone medication for ART regimens in the US President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) programs for the foreseeable future [2]. Therefore, it is essential to maximize viral suppression among PWH, particularly in countries with limited access to drug resistance testing.

As fumarate/lamivudine/dolutegravir (TLD) became the preferred ART regimen over the last 4 years, persons living with HIVP (WH) in many settings were switched from first-line non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)-based regimens to TLD, without regard to viral suppression, and without drug resistance testing for those with virologic failure [1]. Studies from programmatic settings in LMICs have reported high rates of viral suppression on TLD, but data are limited beyond the first year of treatment [4–7]. Long-term data on TLD outcomes are also limited from the Latin America and Caribbean region [4, 8].

Pre-switch viremia is associated with future virologic failure on TLD [5, 6, 9, 10]. Recent studies from several African countries have demonstrated that suboptimal adherence is the leading cause of virologic failure on TLD, but emerging reports also document DTG resistance, particularly in PWH with virologic failure prior to switching to DTG-based regimens [11–15]. However, access to resistance testing remains very limited in many LMICs. Furthermore, in settings of severe civil unrest, treatment outcomes may be further compromised by structural barriers to adherence.

In contrast, the overwhelming majority of PWH with pre-switch viral suppression maintain virologic suppression after switching to TLD [5–7, 16, 17]. Longer-term data on virologic outcomes could provide evidence to support the implementation of DTG-3TC in this low-risk cohort. Studies from higher-income settings have demonstrated that DTG-3TC is non-inferior to standard three-drug therapy in eligible recipients of care (RoC), and this strategy is now recommended in US and European guidelines, and is being implemented in some countries in the Latin American and Caribbean region [18–24].

We conducted a retrospective analysis of longitudinal virologic outcomes for RoC who initiated or switched to TLD from a first-line efavirenz (EFV)-based regimen at the Haitian Group for the Study of Kaposi's Sarcoma and Opportunistic Infections (GHESKIO). We stratified results based on ART history and virologic status at TLD initiation.

METHODS

Study Setting and Characteristics of Recipients in Care

GHESKIO is a Haitian non-governmental organization and the largest provider of HIV care in the Caribbean. HIV care in Haiti is largely funded by PEPFAR and the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis, and Malaria. All care at GHESKIO is provided free of charge. The estimated adult HIV prevalence in Haiti is 1.7% [25]. Haiti is ranked 163rd of 191 countries on the Human Development Index, and during the study period, Haiti was experiencing a period of severe political instability and gang-related violence [26]. Over 80% of the capital city of Port-au-Prince is controlled by armed gangs, and GHESKIO's central facilities are located adjacent to some of the country's largest and most impoverished slums, where gang violence is most intense [27].

GHESKIO has been providing ART since 2003, and until 2017, viral load monitoring was not routinely available. First-line therapy included an NNRTI-based regimen in combination with two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs); in 2010, the preferred regimen was EFV/TDF/3TC. In 2018, the TLD regimen became available, and most RoC taking other regimens switched to TLD, regardless of viral suppression.

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of all PWH ≥15 years of age at ART initiation who initiated or switched to TLD at GHESKIO from 5 November 2018 to 21 March 2021, had at least one HIV-1 RNA test after starting TLD, and were ART-naïve at TLD initiation or switched to TLD from a first-line NNRTI-based regimen. RoC were followed until 21 March 2022, the date of database closure.

The baseline status of RoC was classified by ART regimen and pre-switch HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL or ≥1000 copies/mL at TLD initiation; those with no HIV-1 RNA measurement within 180 days prior to switching were classified as having no available viral load at TLD switch. A threshold of 1000 copies/mL was used to define virologic failure, in accordance with World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, because dried blood spot (DBS) testing is used at some community HIV clinics [28]. In a sensitivity analysis, a threshold of undetectable was used to define virologic failure; this included either HIV-1 < 50 copies/mL or <839 copies/mL (the threshold for DBS testing).

Retention in care was defined according to status at database closure. RoC were considered active if they were no more than 90 days late for their most recent appointment at the time of database closure; they were considered late if they were 91 to 365 days late for their most recent appointment, and as lost to follow-up if they were >365 days late. RoC were classified as dead if they were known to have died and transferred if they were known to have transferred to a different ART provider.

The outcome for viral load at follow-up testing was also dichotomized as HIV-1 RNA ≥1000 versus <1000, the same threshold as at study baseline. The main outcome of interest was dichotomized viral load at the most recent test, among those with viral load measurements. Longitudinal dichotomized viral load measurements for those with multiple tests after TLD initiation were also examined as described below. It was not feasible to include confirmatory viral load results, because there was variability in the timing and completion rate of confirmatory viral load testing.

Statistical Analysis

Data on sex, age, education, marital status, ART regimen at time of TLD initiation, time on ART at TLD initiation, and all HIV-1 RNA results were extracted from GHESKIO's electronic medical record (EMR). Baseline characteristics were summarized in the overall cohort using frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables.

We categorized viral load test results based on the duration from TLD initiation to 3 to 6 months, and then at 12, 24, and 36 months, with a window period of +/− 6 months, and described the proportion of patients with viral suppression (HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL) among those with viral load measurements during each period. We also reported the latest viral load measurement for each RoC prior to database closure. For RoC with at least two viral load tests, we calculated the proportion with virologic failure on the first and latest HIV-1 RNA test. For each of these outcomes, we stratified the results by ART regimen and virologic status at TLD initiation.

Finally, we identified predictors of having HIV-1 RNA ≥1000 copies/mL at the latest measurement using a multivariable logistic regression model based on observed characteristics of the RoC at TLD initiation, namely age, sex, education, civil status, residence location, time on ART, and virologic status. Binary variables included sex (male; female), education (primary or none; at least some secondary), and marital status (married; single/formerly married); categorical variables included age (≤30, 31–50, and ≥50 years), magnitude of pre-switch viremia (no recent viral load; 1000 to 4999 copies/mL; 5000 to 9999 copies/mL; ≥10 000 copies/mL), and residence zone in the Port-au-Prince region; and continuous variables included time on ART. The regression model excluded RoC who were ART-naïve at TLD initiation. Because missingness rates of the covariates were very low (<4%), we excluded RoC with missing data from the regression analyses. Data were analyzed using Stata version 16 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Consent Statement for Recipients of Care

Due to the retrospective study design, it was not feasible to obtain informed consent. This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards of GHESKIO, Weill Cornell Medical College, and Brigham and Women's Hospital.

RESULTS

A total of 10 932 PWH ≥15 years of age initiated TLD or switched to TLD from an NNRTI-based first-line regimen at GHESKIO from 5 November 2018 to 21 March 2021. Of these, 10 354 (94.7%) had at least one viral load test after starting TLD and were included in the analyses (see Table 1). The median age at TLD initiation was 42.7 years (IQR: 34.6, 51.4), 6054 (58.5%) were female, and 5053 (48.8%) had no education or primary school only (see Table 1).

Baseline characteristics (n = 10 354)

A total of 2217 (21.4%) were ART-naïve and 8137 (78.6%) switched from a first-line NNRTI-based regimen. Among the RoC switching to TLD, 5195 (63.8%) had pre-switch HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL, 1449 (17.8%) had no pre-switch viral load measurement, and 1493 (18.3%) had pre-switch HIV-1 RNA ≥1000 copies/mL. The median time on ART prior to switching to TLD was 4.37 years (IQR: 2.37, 7.49) at the time of switch to TLD (Table 1).

Among the total cohort, the median observed follow-up time on TLD was 2.8 years (IQR: 2.3, 3.1). Viral suppression rates remained stable over time in RoC with pre-switch suppression or no pre-switch viral load, but declined over time in those with pre-switch failure and in those who were ART-naïve at TLD initiation. Among RoC with pre-switch suppression who received viral load testing during each time period, the proportion achieving viral suppression was 94%, 93%, 93%, and 89% at the 3- to 6-month, and 12-, 24-, and 36-month periods, respectively (Figure 1). Among RoC with no pre-switch viral load, these proportions were 91%, 89%, 89%, and 89%, respectively. Rates of virologic suppression were lower among patients with pre-switch failure, with 71%, 66%, 64%, and 60% achieving viral suppression at each of these time points, respectively. In patients who were ART-naïve at TLD initiation, 87%, 83%, 79%, and 73% achieved viral suppression at each time point. Rates of viral suppression were lower when a threshold of undetectable was used (Supplementary Figure 1); among the total number of 7813 viral loads which were classified as undetectable, 7568 (96.9%) were <50 copies/mL and 245 (3.1%) were <839 copies/mL (conducted with DBS testing).

Proportion of recipients of care with HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL over time on TLD, by virologic status at TLD initiation.

The proportion of RoC with virologic suppression (HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL) at the latest test before database closure varied by viral load status at TLD initiation (Supplementary Table 1). Among ART-naïve RoC, 81.8% were suppressed at the latest test. Among RoC switching from a first-line NNRTI-based regimen with pre-switch virologic suppression or no pre-switch viral load, 92.7% and 88.5%, respectively, achieved suppression at their latest viral load test. Among RoC with pre-switch failure, the proportion with suppression at the latest test was 74.7%, 56.7%, and 54.1% for those with HIV-1 RNA 1000–4999 copies/mL, 5000–9999 copies/mL, and ≥10 000 copies/mL, respectively.

The majority of RoC had at least two viral load tests after initiating TLD, including 4490/5195 (86.4%) with pre-switch suppression, 1298/1449 (89.6%) without a recent pre-switch viral load, 1044/1493 (69.9%) with pre-switch virologic failure, and 74.6% (1653/2217) of ART-naïve RoC. Of these, virologic suppression was achieved at the latest measurement in 92.9% of those with pre-switch suppression, 90.2% of those without pre-switch viral load, 68.5% of those with pre-switch virologic failure, and 80.8% of those who were ART-naïve at TLD initiation (Figure 2). Among RoC with pre-switch suppression, 85.7% achieved suppression on both their first and latest tests on TLD; 12.2% achieved suppression on one of the two tests, and 2.2% had failure on both tests. Among RoC without pre-switch viral load, 81.4% achieved suppression on both their first and latest tests on TLD; 14.9% achieved suppression on one of the two tests, and 3.7% had failure on both tests. Rates of virologic failure were higher among RoC who were failing treatment or ART-naïve at TLD initiation. Among RoC with pre-switch virologic failure, 46.8% achieved suppression on both their first and latest tests on TLD; 35.5% achieved suppression on one of the two tests, and 17.6% had failure on both tests (Figure 2). Among ART-naïve RoC with at least two viral load tests after TLD initiation, 71.6% achieved suppression on both their first and latest tests, 20.4% achieved suppression on one of the two tests, and 8.0% had failure on both tests.

First and latest viral load results after initiation or switching to TLD.

In multivariable analyses of RoC who switched to TLD, predictors of virologic failure at latest test included higher pre-switch viral load or no recent pre-switch viral load, compared with pre-switch HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL (≥10 000 copies/mL: adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 7.23 [95% CI: 6.06, 8.63]; 5000 to 9999 copies/mL: aOR 5.61 [95% CI: 3.80, 8.29]; 1000 to 4999 copies/mL: aOR 2.81 [95% CI: 2.03, 3.89]; no recent pre-switch viral load: aOR 1.59 [95% CI: 1.30, 1.94]) and no education or primary school only (OR: 1.31; 95% CI: 1.13, 1.52). Older age (31 to 50 vs ≤30 years: aOR 0.73 [95% CI: 0.58, .90]; ≥50 vs ≤30 years: aOR 0.44 [95% CI: 0.34, .57]), and longer time on ART at switch to TLD (aOR: 0.91/year [(95% CI: 0.89, .93]) were associated with a lower risk of virologic failure (see Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study assessed virologic outcomes up to 3 years after the switch to TLD in the context of severe civil unrest in Haiti. Though the socio-political situation has presented extraordinary challenges to the health system, the overwhelming majority of RoC who were suppressed at switch to TLD maintained virologic suppression. In contrast, only about one-half to three-quarters of RoC with pre-switch viremia achieved virologic suppression at their most recent viral load test on TLD, with the magnitude of pre-switch viremia associated with future virologic failure on TLD. Rates of virologic suppression were also suboptimal in RoC who were ART-naïve at TLD initiation.

Though data from other sites in the Latin America and Caribbean region are limited, studies from various African countries have also reported an association between pre-switch viremia and future virologic failure on TLD. A study from five African countries in the International Epidemiology Databases to Evaluate AIDS (IeDEA) research consortium found that RoC who switched to DTG with HIV-1 RNA ≥1000 copies/mL had worse HIV treatment outcomes at a median follow-up time of 11 months, compared with those who had pre-switch suppression [6]. Cohort studies from Malawi and Lesotho reported that, though viremia after switching to TLD was rare, it was associated with pre-switch viremia [5, 10]. Furthermore, as we found in our study, the risk of viremia increased with higher pre-switch viral loads. A cohort study from South Africa reported rates of viral suppression of about 60% in RoC who switched to DTG from a failing NNRTI-based regimen, with lower rates of suppression in those with pre-switch HIV-1 ≥ 10 000 copies/mL [9]. These findings emphasize the importance of assessing the magnitude of pre-switch viremia, in addition to binary thresholds for virologic failure. These results also indicate the importance of improving access to alternative regimens, including long-acting treatment, for RoC with persistent viremia on TLD.

RoC who were ART-naïve at TLD initiation achieved rates of virologic suppression that were superior to those with pre-switch failure, but inferior to those with pre-switch suppression. Similar findings were reported for the A5381/Hakim study, which is one of the few observational studies to also include data for these three subgroups [29]. Viral suppression rates were lower in our cohort (80% vs 90% at 24 months), though A5381 was a prospective cohort study, and we report retrospective data for all GHESKIO RoC. Furthermore, in settings of severe civil unrest, such as Haiti, there are additional burdens to providing and receiving care. It is possible that PWH receive less adherence counseling in the early period after ART initiation. Moreover, activities of daily living, including medical visits, are more challenging due to road blockages and gang-related violence [30].

We also found that younger age, lower education, and a shorter time on ART prior to switching treatment were associated with virologic failure on TLD; these characteristics have also been associated with poor outcomes in other studies [31, 32]. Younger age was associated with poor outcomes in a study of second-line DTG-based ART in South Africa, and with viremia and disengagement from care among RoC on DTG in the IeDEA consortium [6, 9]. Demographic and social determinants are also associated with adherence among ART-naïve RoC who initiate DTG-based regimens. In the ADVANCE trial, younger age and unemployment were predictors of lower ART adherence, and adherence was correlated with viral suppression [33].

The majority of RoC with persistent viremia on TLD have a susceptible virus, demonstrating that adherence interventions are essential [5, 17, 34, 35]. However, emerging data demonstrate that integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-associated drug resistance mutations are detected in a subgroup of RoC with virologic failure on DTG-based regimens [17, 36]. A recently published rapid scoping review concluded that among ART-experienced PWH receiving a second-line DTG-based regimen, genotypic resistance testing may be beneficial to inform treatment changes [11]. In Lesotho, rates of virologic failure on DTG were low, but DTG resistance mutations were detected in nearly 10% of RoC with pre-switch viremia [10]. Four laboratory-based studies from Africa reported rates of INSTI mutations ranging from 3% to 21% [12–15]. However, data on emergent DTG resistance in programmatic settings in the Latin America and Caribbean region, with a predominance of subtype B virus, are limited. To date, no systemic surveillance testing for DTG resistance has been published among adults in Haiti, but a case of DTG resistance was detected in an ART-naïve infant identified during a national drug resistance survey conducted through the country's early infant diagnosis program [37]. We believe that RoC with pre-switch viremia and ongoing virologic failure on TLD in our cohort likely have similar rates of DTG resistance to reports from Africa, as the standards of care are similar [10–14].

Until access to HIV drug resistance testing is expanded, concerns for INSTI resistance will complicate the implementation of long-acting treatment in LMICs, particularly among RoC with a history of virologic failure prior to switch to TLD. The most promising long-acting regimen that will become available in LMICs in the foreseeable future is the combination of lenacapavir and cabotegravir; it is essential to avoid inadvertent lenacapavir monotherapy in RoC with clinically significant INSTI mutations [38].

Our study also provides additional longer-term follow-up data supporting the assertion that RoC with pre-switch suppression continues to maintain viral suppression after switch to TLD. In the VISEND trial, 99% of participants who switched from NNRTI-based ART with HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL maintained suppression at 144 weeks [39]. The Dolutegravir in Real Life in Lesotho (DO-REAL) study reported 4-month viral suppression rates of 98% [40]. A cohort of RoC from Malawi reported that viral suppression was maintained at 12 months after switching to TLD [5]. In the African Cohort Study (AFRICOS), 94% of RoC remained suppressed at their first viral load on TLD [16]. Furthermore, long-term data from the ADVANCE trial demonstrated that RoC with virologic failure on DTG-based regimens were often able to re-suppress without changing regimen [34].

Clinical trials conducted in ART-naïve and treatment-experienced RoC at low risk of DTG or 3TC resistance have demonstrated that DTG-3TC is non-inferior to standard three-drug regimens [20–23]. Several countries in Latin America, including Argentina and Brazil, are routinely implementing DTG-3TC [24]. However, DTG-3TC is not available in PEPFAR programs. Switching from TLD to DTG-3TC would avert TDF-associated renal and bone toxicity [41, 42]. This would require testing for hepatitis B, but inexpensive, point-of-care tests for hepatitis B surface antigen are now available [43]. The benefits of a TDF-sparing regimen may be even greater in LMICs, with limited capacity to monitor for renal function, lack of access to alternative regimens, and minimal options for those who develop renal failure. However, further data are needed in pregnancy, advanced HIV disease, and programmatic test and treat circumstances [44]. Furthermore, though 3TC resistance does not appear to be associated with worse outcomes, further data are needed [45]. Greater insights into the mechanisms and clinical impact of weight gain after TDF cessation are also important.

In order to achieve the 95–95–95 targets of the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) in the Latin America and Caribbean region, it will be necessary to provide HIV services in settings of civil and political unrest [46]. Our findings demonstrate that GHESKIO's HIV program remains highly functional, in spite of extraordinary challenges [30]. Armed gangs control over 80% of Port-au-Prince, with widespread violence, including attacks on health facilities, resulting in the closure of 80% of clinics and hospitals. With nearly three years of follow-up, about 85% of PWH were in care, and 87% had HIV-1 RNA <1000 copies/mL at the most recent testing; these results suggest ongoing progress in the reduction of HIV transmission in the population. Data on outcomes from other settings of conflict in the region are limited, but in the Central African Republic and Yemen, multi-month medication dispensing and community-based ART sites have been found to be effective for the provision of HIV care [47, 48]. GHESKIO also implements these strategies to ensure ongoing access to ART.

Our study is limited by being conducted in a large urban clinic, which may limit generalizability to other settings. It is also probable that the magnitude of the barriers to treatment adherence during this period of severe civil unrest varied substantially based on age, socioeconomic status, time on ART, and other variables. In addition, we were not able to categorize tests with virologic failure as initial or confirmatory testing. Furthermore, this analysis does not include adherence measures.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, virologic outcomes on TLD are outstanding for PWH with pre-switch suppression, even in the context of severe civil unrest in Haiti. However, rates of persistent viremia are high among PWH who experienced pre-switch failure. Additional interventions are critically needed for PWH and persistent viremia on TLD, including access to resistance testing and alternative treatment options for those in whom DTG resistance is detected.
