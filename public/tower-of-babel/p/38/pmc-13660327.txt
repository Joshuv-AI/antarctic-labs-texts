The IGF2–ERK–Fos axis drives a Müller cell glial-mesenchymal transition-like response to disrupt retinal homeostasis in early myopia
Authors: Jiaojiao Feng, Xiaoqi Gong, Yibo Han, Yunxiao Xie, Anfeng Luo, Hongtao Li, Wei Zhang, Jing Li, Xiaoyun Dong, Shuhan Li, Han Yu, Jike Song, Hongsheng Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myopia is increasingly recognized as a complex pathology involving retinal remodeling, yet the cellular mechanisms linking early visual stress to this process remain unclear. This study investigated whether Müller glial (MG) cells undergo a glial-mesenchymal transition (GMT)-like response in early myopia and identified the upstream signaling axis driving this transformation. We integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses to construct a high-resolution atlas of retinal cell heterogeneity in a guinea pig model of form-deprivation myopia (FDM). Single-cell analysis revealed an expanded MG population (18.9% vs. 6.9%) and identified a disease-enriched Fos⁺ MG subcluster exhibiting a transcriptional signature of GMT and activated extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. IGF2 signaling was elevated in myopic MG cells and correlated with ERK/Fos activation. In vivo siIGF2 attenuated myopia progression, preserved retinal function/structure, suppressed MG activation/proliferation and GMT/ECM marker expression, and reduced inner limiting membrane abnormalities and retinal stiffening. Conversely, in vitro, rhIGF2 activated the ERK/Fos pathway, promoting proliferation, migration, GMT-like response, and collagen synthesis in MIO-M1 cells. These findings demonstrate that early myopia involves Fos⁺ MG-driven retinal remodeling via GMT-like response, coupling visual stress to ECM and mechanical changes. Introduction

Myopia, characterized by excessive axial elongation of the eyeball, has emerged as a major global public health concern, with a particularly high prevalence in East and Southeast Asia [1]. The progression from early refractive error to high-risk structural pathology is of significant clinical consequence. Axial elongation imposes chronic biomechanical and metabolic stress on the posterior pole, leading to progressive retinal and choroidal thinning and subsequent degenerative changes [2]. These pathological alterations underlie myopic fundus lesions—such as fundus tessellation, peripapillary atrophy, and lacquer cracks—and, together with vitreoretinal interface abnormalities, predispose individuals to epiretinal membrane formation, foveoschisis, and myopic traction maculopathy, ultimately increasing the risk of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment [3]. Despite this, the earliest tissue-level events that commit the eye to progressive pathology remain poorly defined. The International Myopia Institute emphasizes that the pathological consequences of myopia are now “one of the most common causes of irreversible blindness,” underscoring the urgency of identifying actionable upstream mechanisms rather than merely tracking refractive endpoints [4].

Beyond refractive error and axial elongation, early myopia can be reframed as a disturbance of retinal homeostasis and the normally stable coupling between retinal architecture and function, changes that may precede and potentially instruct downstream remodeling in the growing eye [5, 6]. A substantial body of evidence supports the concept that the retina is not a passive receiver of defocus but a primary site of signal processing in ocular growth control [7]. Recent animal and clinical studies confirm that optically guided eye growth is regulated, at least in part, by retinal mechanisms [8]. It is widely accepted that abnormal visual input—such as prolonged near work in humans or form deprivation in animals—serves as an initiating factor for myopia [9, 10]. Mechanistic syntheses repeatedly emphasize the central role of intra-retinal processing in myopia development [8]. Consistent with this view, experimental paradigms demonstrate that the key growth-driving signals are generated locally within the eye, classically summarized by the observation that the “signals underlying myopia reside within the eye” [11].

Longitudinal clinical data further suggest that retinal homeostasis imbalance emerges early in myopia [6, 12, 13]. In children, a myopic shift is associated with attenuated thickening or net thinning in parafoveal retinal layers, implying an altered developmental trajectory of retinal architecture [12]. Functionally, human electrophysiology shows an ON-pathway functional deficit in myopia [6], and multifocal ERG features measured in childhood are predictive of subsequent progression rates [13]. Studies involving pharmacological inhibition or cell-type-specific gene knockouts in animal models indicate that multiple retinal cell types collectively contribute to myopia development [14, 15]. As a hierarchically organized neural tissue, the retina comprises diverse neurons and glial cells that form an intricate network for visual information processing [16]. Together, these concepts motivate a critical, tissue-centric question: what specific cellular programs in the early myopic retina destabilize homeostasis and initiate maladaptive structural remodeling?

Müller glial (MG) cells, the predominant glial subtype in the retina, serve as key custodians of retinal homeostasis and physiological function [17]. Spanning the entire neural retina, they integrate metabolic support, ion-water balance, neurotransmitter recycling, and neurovascular communication to maintain structural and functional integrity [18]. In response to injury or stress, MG cells transition from a quiescent state to a reactive, proliferative phenotype—a process marked by upregulation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and vimentin, cytoplasmic enlargement, and enhanced motility [19]. This activation is often accompanied by induction of a matrix-remodeling program with increased production of extracellular matrix (ECM) components [20]. This biology extends beyond nonspecific “reactive gliosis”. Emerging evidence indicates that MG cells can undergo a defined phenotypic shift known as glial-mesenchymal transition (GMT), characterized by mesenchymal-like reprogramming, increased proliferation and migration, and elevated expression of markers such as vimentin, GFAP, and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) [21]. Notably, GMT has been identified as a distinct glial state transition in several retinal pathologies, including diabetic retinopathy [22], idiopathic epiretinal membrane [23], and proliferative vitreoretinopathy [24]. This provides a coherent framework for investigating MG cell transdifferentiation. In experimental myopia, reactive MG proliferation with elevated GFAP expression has been reported [25]. This finding supports early glial activation in myopic retinas. However, whether early myopia also involves a defined GMT process remains unexplored.

Here, we combine single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) to resolve retinal cell states in situ and test the hypothesis that a specific MG state transition underlies the breakdown of retinal homeostasis in early myopia. Recent advances highlight that integrating scRNA-seq with spatial mapping can connect subtle cell states to their local microenvironments and communication networks [26]. Applying this integrated approach to the myopic retina, with a focus on MG cells, we aimed to uncover the critical regulators and intercellular interactions governing early pathological remodeling. By linking a defined upstream signaling pathway to MG cell reprogramming and subsequent tissue-level changes, this study seeks to establish a mechanistically grounded, potentially targetable entry point for early intervention to halt or delay retinal remodeling in incipient myopia.

Results

Form deprivation induces early myopic shift and structural remodeling of the retina

Before modeling, there were no significant differences in refraction or axial length between the right eyes of the FDM and normal control (NC) groups (Fig. 1a, b). After 2 weeks of modeling, the FDM group exhibited a significant myopic shift in refraction (− 2.90 ± 0.57 D) compared with the NC group (0.51 ± 0.63 D; p < 0.001; Fig. 1a). The axial length was also significantly increased in the FDM group (8.30 ± 0.05 mm) relative to the NC group (8.07 ± 0.05 mm; p < 0.001; Fig. 1b). This axial elongation was primarily attributable to vitreous chamber lengthening (FDM group: 3.66 ± 0.07 mm; NC group: 3.55 ± 0.03 mm, p < 0.001), while anterior chamber depth and lens thickness remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Ultra-widefield retinal imaging revealed distinct tessellated fundus changes in the FDM group (arrows in Fig. 1c), consistent with early retinal degenerative alterations, whereas no such findings were observed in the NC group. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated global retinal thinning in the FDM group (Supplementary Fig. 1b, 1c). The fundus was segmented into nine Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS)-like sectors centered on the optic disc for structural analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1d). A representative OCT image is shown in Fig. 1d. The retina of the FDM group was significantly thinner than that of the NC group across all quadrants (Fig. 1e). Histological examination further confirmed neuroretinal thinning near the optic disc, with the affected regions delineated by black borders (Fig. 1f). Spatial section analysis centered on the optic disc (Fig. 1g) showed significantly reduced retinal thickness across the nasal–temporal range from − 3 to + 3 mm (Fig. 1h).

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals Müller glial activation and compositional shifts

To delineate the molecular features of myopia at the single-cell resolution, scRNA-seq was performed on retinal tissues from eight FDM and NC guinea pigs using the DNBSEQ™ sequencing platform (Fig. 2a). Retinal tissues were enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions, and a total of 25,589 cells passed quality control filters (nFeatures > 200; nCount > 300). Among them, 12,728 (49.7%) cells were derived from FDM retinas and 12,861 (50.3%) from NC retinas. After dimensional reduction and unsupervised clustering, 21 distinct cell clusters were identified based on highly variable gene expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Cell type annotation was performed using canonical marker genes referenced from published datasets and the CellMarker database (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/) (Supplementary Data 1).

Cells were visualized using the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithm, revealing 21 distinct clusters corresponding to 8 major retinal cell types (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table 1). Each cell type included cells from both FDM and NC retinas, confirming the absence of batch effects and the representativeness of the dataset. For each cell type, three representative marker genes were used for cellular annotation (Fig. 2d), and their consistent, cell–type–specific expression patterns confirmed accurate lineage identification (Fig. 2e).

To identify retinal cell types susceptible to form deprivation, we compared the distribution and relative proportions of cell populations between the FDM and NC groups. The FDM group showed a significantly higher proportion of MG cells (18.9% vs. 6.9%) and a significantly lower proportion of rod cells (39.2% vs. 48.8%), while the proportions of other retinal cell types remained largely unchanged. These differences were consistent across biological replicates in each group (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2). Although cell dissociation sensitivity may influence absolute proportions, the findings suggest that MG cells undergo activated and reactive proliferation in the myopic retina, highlighting their potential role in myopia development. To further address this concern, we added an analysis of the cell numbers of major retinal cell types recovered in the scRNA-seq dataset. This analysis showed that the number of MG cells recovered from FDM retinas was increased (2387 cells vs. 889 cells), whereas rod-related populations were reduced (5083 cells vs. 6563 cells), supporting the interpretation that both Müller glial activation/expansion and rod vulnerability contribute to the observed compositional changes. These data are now provided in Supplementary Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2.

The results of GS and PCNA immunofluorescence (IF) co-staining showed that, compared with the retina in the NC group, PCNA immunoreactivity was significantly increased in the inner retinal layers of the FDM group, and PCNA and GS signals exhibited clear colocalization (Fig. 2f). Quantitative analysis revealed that, compared with the NC group, the proportion of PCNA-positive MG cells in the inner nuclear layer was significantly higher in the FDM group, indicating that form deprivation induced robust cell cycle re-entry and proliferation activation of MG cells (p = 0.002; Fig. 2g). Vimentin IF staining revealed a significant increase in MG cell processes in FDM retinas (Supplementary Fig. 2d), with relative fluorescence intensity elevated by approximately 13% compared with the NC group (p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 2e), indicating MG cell activation and reactive proliferation. Conversely, rhodopsin expression in the outer segment of photoreceptor cells within the outer plexiform layer was significantly reduced in the FDM group (Fig. 2h), with fluorescence intensity decreased by approximately 14% (p = 0.031; Fig. 2i), suggesting impaired maintenance or renewal of rod photoreceptors. TUNEL staining further demonstrated a significant increase in apoptotic cells within the outer nuclear layer (ONL) of the FDM group (Fig. 2j), with a significantly higher proportion of TUNEL-positive cells compared with controls (p < 0.001; Fig. 2k). Transmission electron microscopy revealed pronounced ultrastructural disruptions in photoreceptor cells of the FDM group, characterized by disorganized and fragmented outer segment discs with evident vacuolation (black arrows; Fig. 2l). The upper right panel highlights numerous condensed photoreceptor nuclei with aggregated chromatin, in contrast to the NC group, which exhibited an orderly, tightly stacked disc morphology and minimal nuclear heterochromatin.

Heterogeneity analysis identifies a disease-enriched, proliferative Fos+ MG subpopulation

Fos + MG Müller glia represent a reactive, proliferating subpopulation

Given the significant changes observed in MG cells in myopic retinas (Fig. 2c) and the limited understanding of their role in myopia pathogenesis, subsequent analyses focused on dissecting MG cell subpopulations. To determine whether MG cells could be subdivided into subpopulations reflecting different activation states or biological pathways, re-clustering analysis identified five transcriptionally distinct subpopulations (MG1–MG5) (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The distribution of these subpopulations in the retinas of FDM and NC groups is shown in Fig. 3a. Each subpopulation contained MG cells derived from both groups, indicating consistent representation across samples (Fig. 3a). Violin plots further highlighted the expression of specific marker genes for each cell subpopulation (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Among them, the transcription factor Fos was highly expressed in MG1 cells. Enolase 1 (ENO1), associated with glycolytic metabolism, was predominantly expressed in MG2 cells. The recoverin (RCVRN) was highly expressed in MG3 cells. Ribosomal protein L6 (RPL6) was highly expressed in MG4. Complexin (CPLX3) was specifically expressed in MG5. Accordingly, the five MG cell subpopulations were designated as Fos+ MG, ENO1+ MG, RCVRN+ MG, RPL6+ MG, and CPLX3+ MG. Figure 3b shows the distribution of these subpopulations in both groups, with Fos+ MG representing the predominant subpopulation. The proportion of Fos+ MG cells was significantly higher in the FDM retinas (63.13%) than in the NC retinas (34.73%) (Fig. 3b). Meanwhile, the number of Fos + MG cells in the FDM group (1510 cells) was also significantly higher than that in the NC group (315 cells) (Supplementary Fig. 3c). The violin plots of the top eight marker genes (by fold change) for each MG subpopulation (Fig. 3c) further demonstrates distinct transcriptional profiles across subpopulations.

To further characterize the biological roles of each MG cell subpopulation, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and gene set enrichment analyses were performed (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Fig. 3d). Fos+ MG cells were enriched in transcriptional profiles related to cell proliferation, adhesion, and ECM organization, suggesting cytoskeletal and structural support functions. ENO1+ MG cells showed enrichment in pathways associated with glucose metabolism and hypoxia response, indicating a potential role in metabolic energy supply to neurons. RCVRN+ MG cells expressed genes linked to phototransduction and visual perception, implying that this subpopulation may function as a specialized phototransductive glial element involved in retinal light signaling. RPL6+ MG cells were enriched in ribosome biosynthesis pathways, consistent with a role in protein synthesis. CPLX3+ MG cells predominantly expressed genes related to synaptic vesicle cycling and hormone secretion, suggesting involvement in vesicle and neurosecretory processes.

Given that MG cells were in an activated and reactively proliferating state, pseudo-time analysis was conducted to explore potential differentiation trajectories among MG subpopulations. The results revealed that Fos+ MG cells exhibited tighter clustering, forming a distinct differentiation-initiating population, suggesting that they may represent the origin of other subpopulations (Fig. 3e, f). This finding aligns with the functional enrichment results, which identified Fos+ MG as a reactively proliferating population. To validate the scRNA-seq results, IF staining was performed on retinal sections from healthy controls and individuals with myopia. Fos+ MG cells were identified by co-expression of Fos and vimentin (Fig. 3g). IF staining confirmed a higher proportion of Fos+/vimentin+ cells in myopic retinas than in normal controls (Fig. 3g).

Spatial heterogeneity of MG cells

To further investigate the spatial heterogeneity of MG cells in myopic retinas, we employed Stereo-seq, a novel ST technique with 500 nm resolution (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Consecutive 10 μm-thick frozen retinal sections from the FDM group were collected; the first was used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and the adjacent section was used for Stereo-seq analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4b). After data filtering, the quality of the Stereo-seq dataset was systematically evaluated based on total reads, base Q30, and barcode mapping rates. The average base Q30 scores for coordinate identity barcodes (CID) and molecular identity barcodes (MID) were 93.74% and 92.87%, respectively, with a unique read-mapping rate of 77.2%. Unsupervised clustering using the Seurat package and visualization with uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) identified 20 clusters, whose spatial distributions corresponded well with retinal architecture (Supplementary Fig. 4c). After integrating stained images with the gene expression data through the STOmics visualization system, retinal cells were classified based on tissue structure using StereoMap software (Supplementary Fig. 4c). A subsequent re-supervised clustering analysis of retinal regions identified nine distinct clusters (0–8), visualized by UMAP (Supplementary Fig. 4d), with spatial distributions consistent with the structural organization of the retinal slice (Supplementary Fig. 4e).

Subsequently, the spatial transcriptomic data were integrated with the annotated scRNA-seq dataset using the SPOTlight algorithm to map in situ transcriptional profiles and estimate the proportion of each cell type at every spatial spot (Supplementary Fig. 4f). The spatial distribution patterns of major cell types and MG cell subpopulations closely mirrored the anatomical organization of the retina, with rods, cones, and RPE cells localized primarily in the outer retinal layers, and bipolar (BC), amacrine/horizontal (AC/HC), and retinal ganglion cells (RGC) concentrated in the inner layers (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Spatial mapping of the five MG subpopulations based on specific marker genes identified from the scRNA-seq data revealed distinct localization patterns. Fos+ MG and RPL6+ MG were widely distributed across the retina, ENO1+ MG were enriched in the inner retina, RCVRN+ MG were mainly found in the outer retina, and CPLX3+ MG cells were sparse and scattered within the middle retinal layer (Supplementary Fig. 5a). To validate the spatial mapping, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the expression levels of shared cell types in the scRNA-seq and ST datasets. The strong correlations observed confirmed the reliability of cell-type annotations and spatial integration (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Furthermore, spatial Pearson correlation of marker genes from the five MG subtypes, performed using the cor function in R and visualized as a heatmap, revealed strong correlations between their transcriptional profiles and spatial distribution (Supplementary Fig. 5c). To independently validate the identification of MG cell subpopulations, we performed multiplex IF staining on retinal sections from the FDM group (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Specifically, we examined the spatial colocalization of MG cell markers (GS/GLUL) with markers for five MG cell subpopulations (FOS, ENO1, RCVRN, RPL6, CPLX3) (Supplementary Fig. 6b–f). The results confirmed the spatial distribution patterns observed in the Stereo-seq dataset, supported the localization of these MG cell subpopulations, and validated the exploratory spatial transcriptomics findings.

The central communication role of MG cells in the myopic retina

MG cell activation and reactive proliferation are complex processes involving extensive interactions with other retinal cell types. To explore these interactions, we compared intercellular communication networks among retinal cells from the FDM and NC groups using CellChat 3.0. A global signaling crosstalk map was constructed and quantified to visualize the total number and strength of intercellular interactions (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b), where redder colors indicate higher interaction intensity. From a global perspective, MG cells occupied a central position within the retinal communication network. Quantitative analysis revealed that both the total number and strength of intercellular interactions were significantly increased in the FDM group compared with the NC group (Fig. 4a). Specific differential communication patterns between the two groups of retinas were observed. To quantitatively characterize the differences in cellular communication between the two groups, heatmaps illustrating the number and strength of cell–cell interactions among retinal cells were generated (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 7c). These analyses revealed that MG cells exhibited the greatest number and strongest interactions in both groups. These interactions were significantly enhanced after form deprivation, particularly with RPE, AC/HC, and RGC cells. In contrast, cone cells showed substantially fewer and weaker connections with other cell types, including MG, RGC, BC, and AC/HC cells.

Pathway analysis implicates the IGF2–ERK–Fos axis in driving MG activation and ECM remodeling

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying myopia development, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were performed on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the FDM and NC groups. Functional enrichment of biological processes (BP) revealed that upregulated DEGs were primarily associated with axon development, gliogenesis, and ECM organization, indicating enhanced neuroglial proliferation and ECM remodeling in the FDM retina. Cellular component (CC) analysis showed that the upregulated DEGs were predominantly localized to cell–substrate junctions, focal adhesions, and the actin cytoskeleton. Within the molecular function (MF) category, upregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in cadherin binding, actin binding, and structural components of the ribosome. Conversely, downregulated DEGs in the FDM retina were significantly associated with BPs such as aerobic respiration, sensory perception of light stimulus, and oxidative phosphorylation. CC enrichment analysis revealed that these downregulated genes were largely localized to the mitochondrial protein-containing complex, inner mitochondrial membrane, and photoreceptor outer segments. MF enrichment analysis indicated that they were mainly involved in oxidoreductase activity, proton transmembrane transporter activity, and NADH dehydrogenase activity (Fig. 4c).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated DEGs in the retinas of the FDM group, compared with the NC group, were mainly involved in signaling pathways such as MAPK, Rap1, Ras, and focal adhesion, which are associated with cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Additionally, pathways related to neurotransmitter transmission (including glutamatergic and GABAergic synapses) and cellular morphology regulation linked to the actin cytoskeleton were also enriched. Conversely, downregulated DEGs were predominantly enriched in metabolism-related signaling pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, phototransduction, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and amino acid biosynthesis (Fig. 4d).

Based on these findings, MG cells in the myopic retina were abnormally activated and proliferative, exhibiting enhanced interactions with other retinal cell types. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these changes, we utilized CellChatDB, which catalogs known ligand–receptor interactions. Violin plots from scRNA-seq analyses showed significantly elevated IGF2 signaling gene in retinal MG cells of the FDM group (Fig. 4e). Notably, IGF2 is a critical growth factor that promotes cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival, suggesting its potential role in driving MG cell activation during the early stages of myopia. Protein–protein interaction network analysis further revealed strong associations between the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway (yellow nodes) and the ECM–receptor interaction pathway (green nodes), with node size reflecting the magnitude of differential expression (Fig. 4f). Strong functional interactions were observed between the differentially expressed proteins of the two pathways, suggesting that the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway may contribute to myopia development by modulating ECM production. Collectively, these findings indicate that IGF2 in the myopic retina promotes MG cell activation and proliferation through MAPK/ERK signaling, thereby driving ECM remodeling and contributing to the pathological progression of myopia.

Silencing IGF2 in vivo restores retinal homeostasis by blocking the ERK–Fos-driven GMT of MG cells in the early-stage myopia

Silencing of IGF2 attenuates myopia progression and restores retinal homeostasis

To test this hypothesis, we established an FDM guinea pig model and evaluated the effect of intravitreal injection of IGF2 small interfering RNA (siIGF-2) on myopia progression. At baseline (0 weeks), there were no significant differences in refraction or axial length among the four groups: NC, FDM, IGF2-silenced (siIGF2), and scrambled siRNA control (siCtrl) (Fig. 5a, b). After 2 weeks, the FDM group exhibited a significant myopic shift in refraction (0.26 ± 0.56D in NC vs. −2.83 ± 0.35 D in the FDM group, p < 0.001) and an increase in axial length (8.08 ± 0.04 mm in NC vs. 8.29 ± 0.04 mm in FDM, p < 0.001), confirming successful model induction. In contrast, compared with the FDM and siCtrl groups, the siIGF2-treated group showed a significantly attenuated myopic shift (0.11 ± 0.57 D in siIGF2 vs. −2.76 ± 0.34 D in siCtrl, both p < 0.001) and a suppressed increase in axial length (8.07 ± 0.04 mm in siIGF2 vs. 8.28 ± 0.03 mm in siCtrl, both p < 0.001). Notably, the siCtrl group exhibited myopic changes similar to those in the FDM group, underscoring the specificity of IGF2 knockdown. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that IGF2 plays a crucial role in myopia progression, and its silencing effectively mitigates the development of FDM.

Flash electroretinography (fERG) was performed to assess the effects of IGF2 inhibition on retinal function in FDM guinea pigs. Evaluations included scotopic (Rod) ERG, maximal combined response (Max), oscillatory potentials (OP-total), and photopic (Cone) ERG, with representative waveforms shown in Fig. 5c. Quantitative analysis revealed that the amplitudes of the Rod-b, Max-b, OP-total, and Cone-b waves were significantly lower in the FDM group than in the NC group (all p < 0.001). In contrast, siIGF2 treatment significantly preserved these amplitudes, maintaining values close to those of the NC when compared with the FDM and siCtrl groups (all p < 0.001; Fig. 5d). Furthermore, the implicit times of the Rod-b, Max-b, and Cone-b waves were significantly prolonged in the FDM and siCtrl groups but were significantly improved following IGF2 silencing, approaching NC levels (all p < 0.001; Fig. 5e). Collectively, these findings suggest that IGF2 knockdown mitigates the retinal functional impairment associated with FDM, highlighting its protective role in maintaining retinal electrophysiological integrity.

Histological examination using H&E staining further supported these results. Compared with the NC group, the FDM group exhibited a significant reduction in overall retinal thickness, with pronounced thinning in the ONL (Fig. 5f). Notably, intravitreal siIGF2 injection partially restored retinal thickness and preserved the layered retinal architecture, whereas the siCtrl group displayed similar degenerative changes to the FDM model. Quantitative spatial analysis of retinal thickness corroborated these observations (Fig. 5g). These findings suggest that IGF2 contributes to retinal degeneration during myopia progression, and targeted inhibition of IGF2 alleviates both functional and structural retinal damage induced by FDM.

Silencing of IGF2 attenuates MG cell reactive proliferation by downregulating the ERK/Fos cascade

scRNA-seq analysis revealed a significant increase in the expression of IGF2, KRAS, ERK1, ERK2, and FOS in MG cells from the FDM group compared with the NC group, indicating enhanced activation of the MAPK/Fos pathway in myopic retinal MG cells. Consistently, the expression of vimentin, a marker of MG cell activation, was also elevated in the FDM group (Fig. 6a). Violin plot analysis quantitatively confirmed these gene expression changes, suggesting that form deprivation induced MG cell activation through the IGF2–ERK/Fos signaling axis (Fig. 6a). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis further validated the scRNA-seq findings. The mRNA levels of IGF2, KRAS, RAF1, MEK1, MEK2, ERK1, ERK2, and c-Fos were significantly upregulated in the FDM group compared with the NC group. In contrast, siIGF2 treatment significantly reduced the expression of these genes compared with the FDM and siCtrl groups, with all differences reaching statistical significance. The expression levels in the siCtrl group were comparable to those in the FDM group, confirming the specificity of IGF2 silencing in modulating the ERK/Fos signaling pathway (Fig. 6b). To further validate the regulatory mechanism, western blot analysis was performed to assess protein expression changes following siIGF2 treatment (Fig. 6c). Compared with the NC group, the protein levels of IGF2, RAS, phosphorylated RAF1/RAF1 (p-RAF1/RAF1), phosphorylated MEK1/2 / MEK1/2 (p-MEK1/2 / MEK1/2), phosphorylated ERK1/2 / ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2 / ERK1/2), and c-Fos were significantly upregulated in the FDM group, indicating activation of the ERK/Fos signaling cascade (Fig. 6c, d). Moreover, the expression of GFAP and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA)—markers of MG activation and proliferation—was also significantly increased in FDM retinas. Notably, IGF2 silencing significantly downregulated IGF2 and its downstream effectors (RAS, p-RAF1/RAF1, p-MEK1/2 / MEK1/2, p-ERK1/2 / ERK1/2, c-Fos, GFAP, and PCNA) relative to the FDM and siCtrl groups, restoring their levels close to those observed in NC retinas (Fig. 6c, d). Protein expression in the siCtrl group remained comparable to that in the FDM group. These results provide compelling evidence that IGF2 mediates activation of the ERK/Fos signaling pathway and drives the reactive proliferation of MG cells in FDM.

To further assess the effect of silencing IGF2 on MG activation and proliferation, immunohistochemical and IF analyses of GS/GLUL, GFAP, and PCNA were performed on retinal sections (Fig. 6e). In the NC retina, PCNA-positive proliferating MG cells were scarce, whereas they were significantly increased in the FDM group and strongly colocalized with GS-positive MG cells, suggesting that MG cells had re-entered the cell cycle. At the same time, GFAP expression was markedly upregulated in the FDM retina, reflecting significant reactive gliosis. Notably, siIGF2 treatment reduced PCNA and GFAP signaling, partially reversing the FDM-induced changes, whereas siCtrl did not exhibit any significant protective effect (Fig. 6e). Quantitative analysis further confirmed these observations. Compared with the NC group, the proportion of GS+/PCNA+ MG cells in the inner nuclear layer of FDM retinas was significantly elevated, while IGF2 silencing attenuated this increase (Fig. 6f). Similarly, both the GFAP area fraction (Fig. 6g) and the proportion of GFAP+/PCNA+ cells (Fig. 6h) were elevated in FDM, whereas siIGF2 significantly reduced these parameters, bringing them closer to control levels. No significant differences were observed between the siCtrl group and the FDM group. Overall, these results suggest that form deprivation promotes MG cell proliferation and reactive gliosis, and that the IGF2 signaling pathway is a key driver of this glial activation response.

Silencing of IGF2 inhibits MG cell glial-mesenchymal transition, ECM deposition, and biomechanical stiffening in the myopic retina

Given that activated MG cells are key mediators of retinal remodeling and undergo GMT during this process, we assessed GMT and ECM-related markers in the retina. scRNA-seq analysis of the NC and FDM groups revealed that proliferation- and ECM-related genes (PCNA, TIMP2, FLT1, FBN1, ADAMTS1, FN1, ITGA2, ITGA6, COL9A1, and COL11A1) were predominantly expressed in MG cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Violin plots showed significantly higher expression levels of these genes in the FDM group than in the NC group, indicating transcriptional activation of ECM remodeling pathways during myopia progression (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Digital Western blot analysis further demonstrated that FDM significantly upregulated ECM-related proteins, including α-SMA, COL1A1, MMP2, FN1, and ITGA5, whereas siIGF2 treatment significantly reduced their expression relative to the FDM and siCtrl groups (Fig. 7a). A quantitative heatmap of relative protein levels confirmed that siIGF2 significantly reversed the FDM-induced upregulation of GMT markers compared with the siCtrl group (Fig. 7b). Immunohistochemical staining further demonstrated elevated expression of α-SMA and MMP2 in MG cells of the FDM group, which was substantially attenuated following siIGF2 administration (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c). IF staining was then performed to assess the expression of α-SMA, fibronectin (FN1), and MMP2. In the FDM group, α-SMA and MMP2 were significantly upregulated along the retinal inner layers (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 9a), with significantly higher fluorescence intensities compared with the NC group (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Fig. 9d). Similarly, FN1 expression was significantly upregulated in the FDM retina relative to the NC group (Fig. 7c, e). In contrast, siIGF2 treatment significantly reduced the expression of α-SMA, MMP2, and FN1, whereas the siCtrl group maintained high expression levels similar to those observed in the FDM group (Fig. 7d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 9d).

Masson’s trichrome and Sirius Red staining further revealed extensive collagen deposition in FDM retinas, whereas siIGF2 treatment alleviated these changes (Fig. 7c). Quantitative analysis confirmed that siIGF2 significantly reduced the proportion of collagen deposition and Sirius Red–positive areas compared with the FDM and siCtrl groups (Fig. 7f, g). Additionally, hydroxyproline content, a biochemical indicator of collagen accumulation, was significantly elevated in the FDM group but returned to near-baseline levels following siIGF2 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9e). Collectively, these findings indicate that siIGF2 mitigates MG cell GMT -like response and retinal remodeling by reducing ECM accumulation.

To further assess the effect of IGF2 silencing on myopic retinal remodeling, retinal ultrastructure was examined using transmission electron microscopy, and biomechanical properties were evaluated with a nanoindenter. In the NC group, the inner limiting membrane (ILM) surface appeared smooth and tightly adhered to the nerve fiber layer (Fig. 7h). In contrast, FDM induced significant thickening of the ILM and disordered ECM deposition, leading to loss of its distinct layered architecture. These pathological alterations were substantially alleviated in the siIGF2 group, which exhibited a relatively intact and compact ultrastructure comparable to the NC retina, whereas the siCtrl group retained the FDM-like changes (Fig. 7h). Spatial stiffness mapping revealed significantly increased retinal stiffness in the FDM group, with a peak Young’s modulus of 8.71 kPa versus 3.02 kPa in NC retinas. siIGF2 treatment significantly reduced stiffness to a peak of 3.79 kPa, indicating pronounced mechanical softening. Heatmap analysis further showed localized hardening in the FDM and siCtrl groups, while siIGF2 treatment restored a uniform low-stiffness distribution similar to that of the NC retina (Fig. 7i). Quantitative analysis revealed that the Young’s modulus increased to 5.57 ± 1.20 kPa in the FDM group, compared with 2.63 ± 0.32 kPa in the NC group (p < 0.001), and was significantly reversed by IGF2 knockdown (2.88 ± 0.50 kPa, p < 0.001 vs. FDM). The siCtrl group remained elevated (4.81 ± 1.72 kPa) without improvement (Fig. 7j). Collectively, these ultrastructural and biomechanical findings indicate that IGF2 contributes to myopia-induced retinal remodeling and stiffening, whereas targeted IGF2 silencing effectively mitigates matrix deposition and restores retinal mechanical properties.

Recombinant human IGF2 (rhIGF2) in vitro activates the ERK/Fos pathway to induce MG cell proliferation, migration, GMT, and ECM deposition

The rhIGF2 enhances the proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression of MG cells

CCK-8 assay revealed that rhIGF2 treatment significantly increased the viability of the Müller cell line MIO-M1 in a dose-dependent manner, with the most pronounced effect observed at 100 and 200 ng/mL (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Transwell migration assays further demonstrated that rhIGF2 significantly enhanced cell migration, showing a positive correlation between rhIGF2 concentration and the number of migrated cells (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Quantitative analysis confirmed significant enhancement at 10 ng/mL and 200 ng/mL (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Consistently, colony formation assays showed that rhIGF2 treatment significantly increased the clonogenic capacity of MIO-M1 cells in a dose-dependent manner, with the greatest effect at 200 ng/mL (Supplementary Fig. 10d, e). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated a shift in cell cycle distribution characterized by a reduction in the G0/G1 phase population and an increase in the S and G2/M phases, indicating accelerated cell cycle progression (Supplementary Fig. 10f, g). Similarly, EdU incorporation assays confirmed the proliferative effect of rhIGF2, as evidenced by a dose-dependent increase in EdU⁺ nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 10h, i). Collectively, these findings indicate that IGF2 stimulation enhances the proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression of MG cells.

The rhIGF2 induces GMT and ECM deposition in MG cells by activating the ERK/Fos pathway

The qRT-PCR analysis revealed that rhIGF2 treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of IGF2 and key components of the ERK signaling pathway, including KRAS, RAF1, MEK1/2, ERK1/2, and the downstream effector c-Fos (Fig. 8a). Digital Western blot analysis (Fig. 8b) and the corresponding quantitative heatmap (Fig. 8c) confirmed that rhIGF2 increased the total and phosphorylated protein levels of RAS, RAF1, MEK1/2, ERK1/2, and c-Fos, along with elevated expression of GFAP and PCNA, suggesting pathway activation and enhanced proliferation of MIO-M1 cells in vitro. IF staining of GFAP (Fig. 8d) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 8e) further demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity, suggesting that rhIGF2 stimulates MG cell activation. Additionally, rhIGF2 upregulated the expression levels of GMT and ECM-related proteins, including α-SMA, COL1A1, MMP2, FN1, and ITGA5, as shown by digital Western blot and quantified in heatmaps (Fig. 8f, g). Consistently, rhIGF2 treatment resulted in a significant, dose-dependent increase in hydroxyproline levels in MIO-M1 cells, indicating enhanced collagen synthesis or deposition (Fig. 8h). IF staining of α-SMA (Fig. 8i) and corresponding quantification further confirmed a significant increase in fluorescence intensity, particularly at concentrations of 10 and 200 ng/mL (Fig. 8j). Collectively, these findings suggest that IGF2 promotes MG activation and induces GMT through activation of the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK/Fos signaling pathway.

Discussion

By integrating single-cell transcriptomics with spatially resolved sequencing, this study establishes a high-resolution cellular atlas of the retina during early myopia progression. This study observed a significant increase in the number and proportion of MG cells in the FDM retina, which should be interpreted as a relative compositional change rather than conclusive evidence of an absolute expansion. Further analysis of the absolute recovery of cell numbers and independent histological quantitative analysis further confirmed the activation and proliferation of MG cells in the FDM retina. We identified a disease-specific expansion of the Fos⁺ MG subpopulation—a reactive cell state exhibiting transcriptional signatures of GMT-like response and ECM remodeling. Spatial mapping positioned these activated MG cells within the inner retina, where they shift from homeostatic support to a profibrotic phenotype characterized by upregulated GFAP, α-SMA, collagen I, and fibronectin. Mechanistically, we delineated the IGF2-RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK-Fos signaling axis as the central driver of this MG reprogramming and subsequent retinal stiffening. Functional validation confirmed that in vivo IGF2 silencing attenuated myopic progression, preserved retinal structure and function, and suppressed GMT-like response and biomechanical remodeling. At the same time, rhIGF2 recapitulated the pathway activation and phenotypic shift in vitro. These findings collectively reposition early myopia not merely as a refractive disorder, but as an active retinal remodeling process orchestrated by MG cell state transition, and pinpoint the IGF2–ERK–Fos axis as a novel target for disrupting the link between visual input and structural pathology.

Our data support a paradigm in which early FDM represents a retina-initiated homeostatic failure rather than a passive consequence of axial elongation. The discovery of a disease-enriched Fos⁺ MG state, coupled with a coherent IGF2–ERK–Fos transcriptional program and a shift towards ECM remodeling/GMT-like response, provides a cellular mechanism linking visual stress to structural pathology. This aligns with the classical view of the retina as the primary sensor regulating ocular growth, and with clinical evidence showing altered retinal signal processing in myopia [27–29]. MG cells, by virtue of their pan-retinal distribution and integrative functions, emerge as the key cellular orchestrators that translate functional stress into maladaptive structural remodeling, explaining how early functional deficits can precipitate lasting tissue reconfiguration.

Our findings significantly extend the current understanding of MG cell reactivity in myopia [30]. Müller cells are widely recognized as central regulators of retinal tissue homeostasis, while reactive gliosis is understood as a variable process that can be protective early but harmful when it becomes persistent and remodeling-oriented [31, 32]. Recent studies have explored the role of retinal glial cells in myopia, recognizing their role in maintaining retinal structure and function under stress [25, 33]. Beyond the recognized reactive gliosis, we demonstrate that early myopia involves a GMT-like response process, altering ECM composition and biomechanical properties—a mechanism not previously explored in depth. Herrera-Astorga et al. identified MG cell proliferation and neurogenesis in the fish retina during short-term dark adaptation [34]. This further confirms that MG cells can drive adaptive retinal remodeling under visual deprivation. Moon et al. similarly detected elevated GFAP expression in the retina of a rabbit myopia model. Proteomics analysis of CD29+ MG cells isolated via magnetic bead sorting revealed metabolic reprogramming [35]. This further supports our hypothesis that MG cells undergo significant molecular alterations during myopia progression. Additionally, this study identified a subpopulation of MG cells exhibiting elevated expression of the glycolytic enzyme ENO1 [36]. However, while that research focused on metabolic changes induced by visual stress, we propose that signaling pathways such as the IGF2/ERK axis regulate glial remodeling programs, providing a mechanistic explanation for the structural changes observed in myopia. Overall, our findings substantiate and extend the current understanding of MG’s role in myopia, aligning with the insights from these studies while adding new dimensions related to the mechanistic pathways and mechanical environment.

This study reveals the key mechanism by which MG cells undergo a GMT-like phenotypic shift in the early stages of myopia, driving retinal tissue remodeling and biomechanical alterations. These findings challenge the common compartmentalized perception in the field that GMT-like changes are typically discussed only in dominant traction/fibrotic diseases [37]. A high-quality study on vitreoretinal interface diseases has confirmed that MG cells can initiate the GMT process, characterized by enhanced migratory capacity and upregulation of myofibroblast-like gene expression, thereby promoting the formation of fibrotic membranes (such as idiopathic epiretinal membranes). Mechanistic research revealed that pro-fibrotic signaling axes (e.g., the TGF-β/SNAIL pathway) drive the interstitial characteristics of MG cells [21]. We observed similar patterns during the early stages of myopia, suggesting that the myopic retina undergoes not merely simple glial proliferation but likely an active remodeling process. High-resolution OCT studies first reported a high incidence of epiretinal membrane formation and tractional changes in highly myopic eyes, noting that MG cells migrating to the ILM synthesize new collagen, which generates tangential traction forces and leads to retinal remodeling [38]. Subsequent histopathological studies documented the presence of MG cells and newly synthesized collagen in the ILM of myopic retinas [39], with the ILM exhibiting highly irregular elongated indentations, suggesting abnormal MG insertions into the ILM and increased ILM stiffness [40]. Notably, histophysical observations in this study further situate these tissue remodeling processes within biomechanical transformations. Contemporary retinal disease models increasingly emphasize that the neural retina exists in a mechanically active environment and that mechanotransduction can shape pathology by altering cell behavior and ECM organization [41], [42]. By linking the MG-center remodeling program to measurable changes in retinal ECM and mechanical parameters, this study establishes a clear bridge between spatial organization and mechanical phenotypes, which is still underdeveloped in mainstream myopia theory.

The ERK (MAPK) pathway emerges as a central effector in this remodeling cascade. Prior studies have established ERK1/2 as critical mediators of MG activation, proliferation, and ECM production in various retinal injuries [43–47]. In response to injury or stress stimuli, such as high intraocular pressure, mechanical strain, or oxidative stress, ERK1/2 in Müller cells becomes rapidly phosphorylated, leading to a significant upregulation of GFAP expression and the initiation of a canonical glial response [43]. Gliosis in Müller cells can be attenuated by chemical inhibition of MAPK (ERK 1/2) activation, particularly under stress conditions [44]. Activation of ERK1/2 stimulates downstream transcription factors, including Fos and CREB, through the MEK/ERK cascade, promoting cell proliferation and migration, and inducing the expression of ECM-related genes such as collagen type I and fibronectin, which are key molecular hallmarks of GMT [45]. Stimulated Müller cells not only upregulate glial markers like GFAP but also initiate migration and secrete ECM components. Similarly, Niu L et al. reported that tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) regulates mouse retinal Müller cell proliferation through the MAPK (ERK1/2) signaling pathway [46]. Although prior studies have largely focused on neuroprotection or gliosis, the combination of enhanced GFAP expression, cell proliferation, and ECM secretion constitutes the central characteristic of the GMT phenotype [47], which is clearly dependent on ERK–Fos-mediated transcriptional and cell cycle activation [45]. Recent studies have shown that blocking Neuregulin-1/EGF signaling can delay myopia progression in guinea pigs by inhibiting ERK [48]. This aligns with our observation that IGF2 knockdown alleviates myopia by suppressing the downstream ERK–Fos pathway, suggesting that multiple upstream signals—including EGF-like factors and IGF2—may collectively regulate axial length via ERK signaling. Furthermore, transcriptomic analyses indicate that ECM-related gene alterations occur in both form-deprivation and lens-induced myopia models [49]. Our cell‑type‑specific mechanistic explanation—that MG cells, via IGF2–ERK–Fos signaling, transdifferentiate into a fibroblast‑like phenotype and remodel the retinal microenvironment—provides a coherent causal chain from visual stimulus to tissue‑level change.

Finally, the proposed IGF2–ERK–Fos cascade provides a biologically plausible upstream driver because growth-factor signaling is a canonical route to immediate-early transcriptional responses and cell-state change [50]. The IGF system engages receptor networks that can bias downstream signaling toward mitogenic pathways, including MAPK/ERK, depending on receptor context and ligand–receptor specificity [51, 52]. Among candidate upstream cues, IGF2 is notable because it is a growth-regulatory signal with prior experimental links to myopia modulation in mammalian models: intravitreal antisense targeting of retinal IGF2 has been reported to inhibit FDM in guinea pigs, positioning IGF2 as a functionally testable node rather than a purely correlative marker [53]. Additionally, rhIGF-2 promoted the progression of myopic refractive error and axial length in FDM under visual deprivation conditions [54]. A transcriptomic and proteomic study of a lens-induced myopia mouse model identified Igf2bp1, a regulator of IGF2, as associated with myopia [55]. Human genetics further implicates insulin/IGF pathway components—including IGF2—in high-myopia association studies, supporting clinical relevance and motivating mechanism-driven intervention strategies [56]. Importantly, IGF2 is able to effectively activate ERK and immediate-early transcription factors, offering a coherent causal chain from an upstream ligand (IGF2) to a transcriptional effector module (ERK–Fos) and ultimately to an MG-cell state transition (GMT-like response). Building on this, our study directly validated the functional role of IGF2 in MG cell-mediated retinal remodeling. Collectively, integrated the in vivo and in vitro results confirm that the IGF2–ERK–Fos signaling axis serves not only as a molecular link connecting external visual stress to MG cell GMT, but also as a key pathological mechanism driving retinal remodeling. Our work directly validates IGF2 as an upstream driver of MG‑mediated retinal remodeling and stiffening. Supporting this concept, Fan et al. found that blockade of the Notch signaling pathway with RO4929097 effectively inhibited MG cell gliosis and ECM overexpression in a mouse model of retinal injury [57], suggesting that inhibiting retinal remodeling signals can maintain retinal homeostasis [58]. This pathway thus represents a promising target for intervention, particularly given the development of safe delivery strategies such as neutralizing antibodies or siRNA. Supporting this concept, inhibition of other remodeling-related pathways (e.g., Notch, TGF‑β) has shown protective effects in retinal fibrosis models, suggesting that targeting MG reprogramming may preserve retinal homeostasis.

This study has several limitations. First, the FDM guinea pig model differs from human myopia in ocular anatomy and genetics; validation in primate models or human tissues is needed [59]. Second, while we used rhIGF2 to activate the pathway in vitro, the causality of each GMT output signal must still be validated in human Müller cells using selective IGF2/IGF1R antagonists or viral knockout techniques. Third, our single-cell and spatial data are cross-sectional, limiting dynamic or causal inference. Future studies should employ longitudinal sampling, fate-tracking, and interventional genetics to resolve the trajectory of MG state transitions and solidify causal relationships within the identified network. In addition, the Stereo-seq dataset was generated from a single FDM guinea pig retina for exploratory spatial mapping. Although key spatial findings have been independently validated in biologically replicated samples using multiplex IF, further studies with replicated spatial transcriptomic datasets are needed for conclusive statistical inference.

In conclusion, through integrated multi-omics analysis and functional validation, we define a novel pathologic pathway in which visual stress elevates retinal IGF2, activating an ERK–Fos-driven GMT-like response in Müller cells. This leads to excessive ECM deposition, biomechanical stiffening, and disruption of retinal homeostasis. This work re-conceptualizes early myopic progression as an active MG-cell-mediated retinal remodeling process and reveals the IGF2–ERK–Fos signaling axis as a targetable node for preserving retinal structure and function, offering a new avenue for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Animal models and procedures

Two-week-old male tricolor guinea pigs (Cavia porcellus, 90–110 g) were obtained from the Jinan Jinfeng Laboratory Animal Company. The animals were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with an illumination level of approximately 200–300 lx, relative humidity of 40%–60%, and a controlled room temperature of 25 ± 2℃. They had free access to food (guinea pig maintenance feed and fresh vegetables) and water supplemented with vitamin C. Upon arrival, all animals underwent ocular and visual screening to exclude those with cataracts, congenital myopia, or anisometropia greater than two diopters (D). Form-deprivation myopia (FDM) was induced using a white latex balloon (60% light transmittance) mounted onto a custom 3D-printed hood, as previously described. The hood completely covered the right eye while leaving the left eye, nose, mouth, and ears exposed, ensuring no additional pressure was applied to the cornea or eyelids. During each 12-hour light cycle, the hoods were inspected twice to confirm proper positioning, cleanliness, and fit and were replaced as needed.

All animal procedures adhered to the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Eye Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine.

Ocular biometric measurements

Refraction and axial length

Refractive status was measured before and after modeling and intervention by handheld streak retinoscopy (YZ24; Six Six Vision Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) using trial lenses. On measurement days, 1% tropicamide-phenylephrine eye drops (Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) were dropped into the conjunctival sac three times at 5-min intervals in a dark room to achieve cycloplegia; retinoscopy commenced 45 min later. The working distance was 40 cm. Refraction was recorded as the mean of the horizontal and vertical meridians. Each eye was measured three times, and the mean value was used for analysis.

Ocular axis length was measured using a high-frequency A-scan ultrasonography (11 MHz, Cinescan, Quantel Medical, France). Ocular surface anesthesia was performed using 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Puurs, Belgium) before measurements. Performing ocular axis length measurements using the instrument’s preset parameters, the anterior segment length was measured at 1558 m/s, the lens at 1723 m/s, and the vitreous chamber at 1540 m/s. The ultrasonic probe was aimed at the center of the pupil, touching the corneal apex vertically, and the stable waveform was recorded. Each eye was measured 10 times, and the average value was taken for analysis. The hoods of the animals with induced FDM were removed before the measurements and replaced immediately afterward.

Ultra-widefield fundus imaging

Retinal fundus images were obtained at week 2 using an ultra-widefield scanning laser ophthalmoscope (Optos Daytona, UK) under full mydriasis. Evaluate early signs of retinal degenerative lesions through imaging, such as fundus tessellation, peripapillary atrophy, and lacquer cracks.

Swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT)

In vivo retinal thickness was measured using wide-angle SS-OCT (TowardPi BMizar, TowardPi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The pupil should be fully dilated in a darkened room as described above. For examination, the guinea pigs were placed on a custom-made board designed to hold them in place. The scan should center on the optic disc, using OCT mode with a scan area of 24 mm × 20 mm (Supplementary Fig. 1b and 1c). Retinal thickness was defined as the distance between the ILM and the RPE. The images were further analyzed using built-in software to obtain regional variations in thickness. The Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDSA) grid was used to divide the analysis area into the following regions (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Finally, the software automatically constructed a color topographic map of retinal thickness (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Record and compare the thickness of the central zone and the inner and outer ring regions among the groups.

Electroretinography (ERG)

Flash-electroretinogram (f-ERG) assays were performed on guinea pigs in the NC, FDM, siIGF-2, and siCtrl groups, respectively, using a Visual Electrophysiology Instrument (OPTO-ERG, Optoprobe Science LTD, UK). Experiments were performed to assess ERG responses under both dark-adapted and light-adapted conditions. The guinea pigs were dark-adapted overnight, and all manipulations were performed under conditions of weak red light (< 1 lx) to minimize any possible effect of white light on the recovery from induced myopia. Animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), and pupils were dilated using 1% tropicamide phenylephrine eye drops, followed by topical anesthesia of the ocular surface with 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops. The guinea pigs were fixed on the operating table, and the electrodes were placed. The positive electrode was the cornea, the negative electrode was the cheek, and the ground was connected to the right buttock subcutaneously. The dark-adapted (Rod-ERG), maximal mixed response (Max-ERG), oscillatory potentials (OPs), cone response (Cone-ERG), and bright-adapted flicker response (Flick-ERG) were recorded sequentially.

Tissue processing and single-cell sequencing

Retinal collection and single-cell suspension preparation

At the end of 2 weeks of formant deprivation, guinea pigs were euthanized with an overdose of 100 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital injected intraperitoneally. The right eyes of guinea pigs were rapidly exhumed from the sockets and placed in a petri dish containing pre-cooled (4 °C) 1X PBS (Meilunbio, China) solution. Under a dissecting microscope, the extraocular muscles, orbital fascia, and episcleral vessels were carefully excised with ophthalmic scissors. At the fluid meniscus level, a circumferential wound was made 1 mm posterior to the limbus along the corneoscleral junction to remove the anterior segment. The vitreous gel was then aspirated, and the sensory retina was gently peeled away using Dumont forceps (Heidelberg, Germany); the retinal pigment epithelium remained tightly adherent to Bruch’s membrane and could not be fully separated. Retinas from 12 guinea pigs per experimental group were collected in groups of 4 retinas, generating 3 independent biological replicates, and single-cell RNA sequencing was performed to ensure that a sufficient number of cells were obtained. The detached retinal layers were washed twice in tissue washing solution, and the retinas were temporarily stored in MACS® Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) at 4 °C in order to maintain cell viability.

Retinas were transferred to 5 ml polypropylene round-bottomed tubes, and cell dissociation was performed using the commonly used papain dissociation protocol. A mixture of 20 units/mL papain (Worthington Biochemicals) and 0.005% DNase I was added and digested at 37 °C for 30 min, during which period gentle shaking was performed at 5-minute intervals. Dissociation was stopped with ovomucoid proteins. Cells were filtered through a 100 μm strainer (BD Corporation, USA) to remove clumped cells and centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min. After two washes with PBS (Invitrogen, USA), the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS solution containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The cells were counted and assessed for cell survival by trypan blue staining, and the average survival rate of the retinas was greater than 90% in all cases.

Single-cell RNA library construction and sequencing

The scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using a DNBelab C4 high-throughput single-cell kit (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China), a negative-pressure-based droplet microfluidic system. Briefly, the single-cell suspensions were converted into barcoded scRNA-seq libraries through the steps of droplet encapsulation, emulsion breaking, mRNA capture bead collection, reverse transcription, cDNA amplification, and purification. Sequencing libraries were characterized using the QubitTM ssDNA Analysis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. All libraries were paired-end sequenced by the DNBSEQTM sequencing platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China). The following read lengths were used: read 1 was 30 bp, including 10 bp cell barcode 1, 10 bp cell barcode 2, and 10 bp unique molecular identifier (UMI); read 2 was 100 bp transcript sequence; and the sample index was 10 bp.

Data preprocessing and quality control

Gene expression matrices were obtained using DNBelab C-series scRNA analysis software (https://github.com/MGI-tech-bioinformatics/DNBelab_C_Series_scRNA-analysis-software). Briefly, all samples were subjected to sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, and single-cell 3’ end UMI counting using default parameters. The processed reads were then matched to the Cavpor 3.0 guinea pig reference genome sequence using STAR (v2.5.3) software. Use the “barcodeRanks” function of the DropletUtils tool to remove background beads and beads with UMI counts less than a threshold, and automatically identify valid cells based on the distribution of UMI counts per cell. The cell filtering parameters included (1) filtering out cells with gene counts less than 200 or greater than 90% of the maximum gene count, and (2) filtering out cells with more than 5% of mitochondrial reads. To eliminate batch effects, all sequencing libraries were integrated using the “FindIntegrationData” and “IntegrateData” functions in the Seurat R package. Since the proportion of rod cells in the first batch of data in the two groups was too large and there was contamination of rod cells, these two samples were excluded from subsequent analysis. Principal component analysis was performed using highly variable genes (HVG) and principal component (PC) significance was calculated using the “JackStraw” function. The first 20 PCs were used for downstream cluster identification and visualization. For cell clustering, the “FindClusters” function was used with the “resolution” parameter set to 0.8, and modular optimized clustering was performed on the k-shared nearest neighbor graph. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) were applied for visualization.

Identification of differentially expressed genes(DEGs)

Specific marker genes in each cell cluster were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function in the Seurat package. Marker genes identified by the “FindAllMarkers” function need to have > 1.5-fold higher average expression in that cluster than in other clusters and have detectable expression in > 15% of cells from that cluster. The SingleR was utilized to annotate cell types by using known markers of retinal cell types. To find the DEGs between the retinas of the FDM and NC groups, we used the “FindMarker” function in Seurat to compare the gene expression levels of each cell type in the two groups. Genes with p_val_adj < 0.05 and |log 2 fold change| ≥ 0.5 were considered as DEGs.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these DEGs was performed using the TopGO R package. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the hypergeometric test in R. Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were selected by adjusted p-value ≦ 0.05. Annotation and functional association networks were also performed using the STRING online database, and protein interaction networks were visualized using Cytoscape (v3.10.3). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) algorithm was used to evaluate the enrichment score of the pathway or gene set in interest. The human genes in the gene set were converted to guinea pig homologs using the function “get LDS” in the biomaRt R package after downloading the hallmark gene set as a predefined gene set from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB version 6.1). The GSEA analysis was then performed using the “clusterProfiler” and “fgsea” functions in the R package. Finally, Normalized enrichment scores (NES) of all significant pathways were shown as an illustration using heatmaps.

Pseudotemporal trajectory and cell–cell communication analysis

The Monocle 3 software package was used to calculate pseudotemporal trajectories of Müller cell subpopulations in an unsupervised manner. All data used for trajectory analysis were extracted from Seurat objects. DDRTree was used for downscaling, and the “plot_cell_trajectory” function was used to visualize cell trajectories.

The cell-cell communication was analyzed and visualized using the R package-CellChat (version 2). CellChat contained a high-quality database of signaling molecule interactions, and differential expression analysis and statistical tests were performed on the cells to infer and visualize specific communication signals between different cells. We also performed quantitative analysis of communication strength and communication probability. We used visualization functions provided by CellChat, such as “plotNet” and “plotHeatmap”, to show the major communication pathways and signaling molecules between different cell types. Based on the expression of known interacting ligand-receptor pairs, potential intercellular communication signals were identified using the cellchatDB database. The communication probability was calculated by the “computeCommunProb” function, and the intercellular communication network was subsequently analyzed by the “netAnalysis” function.

Spatial transcriptomics

Tissue sectioning and H&E staining

After 2 weeks of modeling, we randomly selected one guinea pig from the FDM group, and the eyeballs were taken as described previously. An appropriately sized cryoembedding cassette was prepared, and it was first labeled for tissue orientation. Pre-cooled Tissue-Tek OCT (Sakura, USA) was filled into this cryomold, handled carefully to avoid air bubbles. Using pre-cooled forceps, place the prepared eyeballs into the OCT, ensuring that the OCT completely covers the tissue, and place the cryoembedding cassette containing the OCT and eyeballs on dry ice until the OCT is completely solidified and turns white and opaque. Transfer the embedded tissue to a -80 °C refrigerator for long-term storage. Three to four 10 μm-thick serial cryosections were cut from the embedded tissues for H&E staining and Stereo-seq library preparation using a Leica CM 1950 Cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Germany). H&E staining images were obtained using a Motic microscope scanner (Motic, China) for histopathological evaluation and retinal localization.

Stereo-seq chip loading

Subsequently, the Stereo-seq chip was washed with Nuclease-Free Water (Ambion, USA) supplemented with 0.05 U/µL RNAase Inhibitor (NEB, M0314 L, USA), and after washing, the perimeter and surface of the chip were blown dry with an air cylinder. With the chip face up, quickly place the vector on the PCR adapter at a pre-equilibrated temperature and incubate for 5 min at 37 °C. The sections were fixed in pre-cooled methanol at -20 °C for 30 min. Fixed tissue sections were stained with Qubit ssDNA dye (Thermo Fisher, USA) before fluorescence imaging to check tissue integrity. RNA integrity number (RIN) was determined using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA). Only samples with RIN ≥ 7 were used for transcriptomics studies.

Tissue permeabilization, reverse transcription, tissue removal, and cDNA release

Tissue patches on the microarrays were permeabilized using 0.1% pepsin (Sigma, P7000) in 0.01 M HCl buffer (pH = 2), incubated at 37 °C for 24 min, and washed with 0.1x SSC buffer supplemented with 0.05 U/mLRNase inhibitor (NEB, M0314 L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for washing. RNA released from permeabilized tissues was captured by DNB and reverse transcribed for 3 h at 42 °C using SuperScript II (Invitrogen, USA).

After in situ reverse transcription, the tissue chips were washed twice with 0.1 × SSC buffer and digested with tissue removal buffer for 30 min at 42 ℃. The cDNA-containing chips were then subjected to cDNA-release enzyme treatment at 55 ℃ overnight, and the chips in the reaction wells were washed by adding Nuclease-Free Water. The cDNA released from the microarray was further amplified using cDNA Amplification Mix and cDNA Primer (MGI). The PCR reaction was performed as follows: the first incubation at 95 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 15 cycles of a warm bath at 98 ℃ for 20 s, at 58 ℃ for 20 s, and at 72 ℃ for 3 min, and finally incubated at 72 ℃ for 5 min. The concentration of the resulting cDNA products was quantified by the Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Stereo-seq library preparation and sequencing

A total of 20 ng of DNA product was fragmented for 10 min at 55 °C using the Interrupting Mix System, and then the reaction was terminated by adding Stop Buffer, pipetting and mixing, and letting the reaction stand for 5 min at room temperature. The fragmentation product was amplified as described below: 25 µl of fragmentation product, PCR Barcode Primer Mix, and PCR Amplification Mix in a total volume of 100 µl. The PCR product was then purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Finally, the libraries were used for DNA nanoball (DNB) generation and finally sequenced on the MGI DNBSEQ-T10 sequencer at the National Gene Bank of China (CNGB) using a single-ended 50 + 100 bp strategy.

Raw stereo-seq data processing

Fastq files were generated by the MGI DNBSEQ-T10 sequencer. The CID sequence on read segment 1 was first mapped to the design coordinates of the in situ capture chip obtained from the first round of sequencing, allowing for a 1-base mismatch to correct sequencing and PCR errors. Reads containing N bases or more than 2 bases of MID were filtered out with a quality score of less than 10. The CID and MID associated with each read were appended to each read header. Mapped reads with MAPQ 10 were counted and annotated to their corresponding genes using an in-house script (available at https://github.com/BGIResearch/SAW). UMIs with the same CID and the same motif were folded, allowing 1 mismatch to correct sequencing and PCR errors. Finally, this information was used to generate an expression profile matrix containing the CIDs.

Combined analysis of stereo-seq and ScRNA-seq data

To verify the identity of the cell types characterized in our retinal sections, we co-analyzed scRNA-seq data with Stereo-seq data. Briefly, cells from Stereo-seq and scRNA-seq were subjected to SCTransform, FindIntegrationDataors, and IngegrateData in Seurat using the inverse principal components and further co-embedded in a low-dimensional space via UMAP. The scRNA-seq was mapped to the spatial transcriptome data as a reference data matrix with Cell_Type as the reference cell type. The “createCARDObject” function was used to create a CARD object to store the matrix and cell information. The “CARD_deconvolution” function was used to deconvolve the cell annotations from the reference data set into the spatial transcriptome, and the “CARD_visualize.prop” function was used to visualize the mapping results of each cell type in the spatial transcriptome.

Based on the set of marker genes for each cell type and the set of genes for each pathway, the “AddModuleScore” function of Seurat v4 software was used to calculate the score of marker genes for each cell type and the score of each pathway. First, the average value of all the genes in the gene set in each cell was calculated, and the matrix was divided into several bins based on this average value; then control genes were randomly selected from the cut bins as background values; finally, an average value was calculated for all the target genes and an average value was calculated for all the background genes, and the difference between the two values was the score of the gene set.

Molecular biology assays

Intravitreal injection and siRNA intervention

Guinea pig gene-specific IGF2 small interfering RNA (siRNA) (siIGF2: sense 5′-GACGGUCCUGACCUCCAAATT-3′) and negative control siCtrl were synthesized by Shandong Gene & Bio Co., Ltd (Shandong, China). Before intravitreal injection (IVT), guinea pigs were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (RWD Life Sciences), and the periorbital and ocular surfaces were disinfected with povidone-iodine disinfectant. After local anesthesia of the ocular surface with 1 drop of 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Puurs, Belgium), the periorbital area was covered with a sterile spreading towel. IVT was performed on the right eye of the modeled eye using a Hamilton microinjector (Hamilton 700, 25 µL, USA) with a 33G bevel-tip needle. siIGF-2 and siCtrl injections were performed every 2 days, meaning a total of 7 injections were performed during the 2-week intervention period. The injections were performed 1 mm behind the corneal limbus in the superior temporal quadrant, and 5 µL of the solution was slowly injected into the vitreous chamber. After the injection was completed, the needle was held in place for 5 s and then withdrawn to prevent fluid reflux. Ofloxacin eye ointment (Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) was applied to the eyes after the injection was completed.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA from MIO-M1 cells and guinea pig retinal tissues was isolated using a modified SPARKeasy Tissue/Cell Rapid RNA Extraction Kit (SparkJade, China). Total RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed into cDNA by SPARKscript II All-in-one RT SuperMix for qPCR (With gDNA Eraser) Reverse Transcription Kit (SparkJade, China). The qRT-PCR was performed using 2× SYBR Green qPCR Mix (With ROX) (SparkJade, China) through a Roche LightCycler 480 real-time quantitative PCR instrument (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences for the tissue and cellular target genes IGF2, KRAS, RAF1, MEK1, MEK2, ERK1, ERK2, FOS, and the endogenous gene GAPDH were detailed in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4. Relative expression was calculated by 2^–ΔΔCt.

Protein extraction and digital western blot

Lysis of treated MIO-M1 cells and retinal tissues was performed using RIPA buffer containing PMSF protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor. Cells and tissues were thoroughly ground at 4 °C using a grinder, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 8 min. Each sample was then ultrasonicated in an ice bath for 10 min and centrifuged again to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime Bio, China).

Protein expression levels were quantified by a capillary-based Abby Simple Western System (ProteinSimple, CA, USA). Protein concentrations were uniformly leveled to 2 µg/mL. Sample preparation, antibody dilution, luminescent solution configuration, sample uptake, immunodetection, washing, signal detection, and data analysis were performed sequentially according to the manufacturer’s protocols, in brief. The dilution concentrations of primary antibodies used for tissue and cell experiments were shown in Supplementary Table 5. Quantitative detection and analysis of chemiluminescent signals using Compass for SW 6.1.0 software (ProteinSimple).

Histopathological analysis

Immunohistochemistry

After the two-week intervention, the guinea pigs were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of 3% pentobarbital sodium solution (100 mg/kg). The eyes were removed as described previously and immediately placed in FAS eye fixative (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 4℃ overnight. After dehydration, transparency, and paraffin embedding, sections were prepared on a Leica RM2016 slicer (Leica Biosystems, Germany). Retinal tissue sections were placed in EDTA antigen repair buffer in a microwave oven for antigen repair and washed three times with PBS. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked by adding 3% hydrogen peroxide solution. A drop of 3% BSA was added to the histochemistry circle to cover the tissue evenly, and it was closed at room temperature for 30 min. Incubation with the primary antibody was performed overnight at 4℃. Primary antibody dilution concentrations were, anti-GFAP (1:50, A0237, ABclonal), anti-ACTA2 (α-SMA) (1:2000, 14395-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-MMP2 (1:200, 10373-2-AP, Proteintech) respectively. After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibody corresponding to the primary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. DAB was applied to develop the color, and hematoxylin was used to re-stain the nuclei. After dehydration and sealing, imaging was visualized using a microscope (Nikon E100, Japan). And the area of positive staining was analyzed using Fiji software.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence staining of retinal tissue, sections were prepared as described previously. For immunofluorescence staining of cells, MIO-M1 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature. cell crawls were closed with goat serum for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, the slices and crawls were each incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. For tissue sections, the primary antibodies applied included: anti-Vimentin (1:2000, 60330-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-c-FOS (1:5000, GB11363, Servicebio), anti-rhodopsin (1:100, sc-57432, Santa Cruz), anti-GFAP (1:200, 16825-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-PCNA (1:50, 60097-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-ACTA2 (1:500, 14395-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-MMP2 (1:500, 10373-2-AP, Proteintech), and anti-Fibronectin (1:200, 15316-1-AP, Proteintech). For cell crawls, the primary antibodies applied included anti-GFAP (1:100; 16825-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-ACTA2 (1:200, 14395-1-AP, Proteintech). After washing, it was incubated with a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody. Then, the slices were incubated with DAPI at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. slices were sealed with an anti-fluorescence quenching sealer and visualized with a fluorescence microscope (Leica, DM3000, Switzerland) and a 3DHISTECH (Pannoramic MIDI, Budapest) pathology slice scanner. Images were processed and analyzed using computer-based image analysis software (Fiji).

TUNEL staining

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling assays were performed on retinal paraffin sections using an In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, paraffin-embedded sagittal sections for the optic nerve were dewaxed and rehydrated, then incubated with proteinase K at 37 °C for 20 min. After PBS washing, sections were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture (TdT enzyme and labeling solution) at 37 °C for 1 h in a humidified chamber. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for visualization and mounted with an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium. The apoptotic index was calculated as the percentage of TUNEL+ cells among total ONL nuclei.

Masson’s trichrome and sirius red staining

The sections were deparaffinized in xylene. For Masson staining, the nuclei were sequentially stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin stain for 5–10 min, Masson’s blueing solution for 5 min, Lichtenstein’s red acidic reagent for 5–10 min, differentiated with 1% phosphomolybdic acid for 2 min, and stained with aniline blue stain for 2 min. For Sirius red staining, sections were placed in Sirius red-staining solution for 8–15 min. Finally, the sections were sealed with neutral gum and observed under a light microscope. Collagen fibers were stained blue in Masson’s trichrome, while muscle fibers, fibrils, and erythrocytes were stained red. The collagen fibers were red in Sirius red, and the background was yellow under the light microscope.

Hydroxyproline content assay

The collagen content after acid hydrolysis was quantified using a hydroxyproline (HYP) content assay kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The 0.2 g of sample or 5 × 10^6 cells were placed in 2 ml or 1 ml of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid extract, respectively, and boiled at 110 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, the pH was leveled to 6–8, and the supernatant was centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 20 min at 25 °C. Reagents were added sequentially according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated as required. Finally, the absorbance value of the sample at 560 nm was determined using an enzyme labeler. The HYP content was calculated based on the sample weight or number of cells.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Prepare retinal tissue quickly away from light into 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ tissue blocks in electron microscopy fixative, then fix overnight at 4 °C in the fixative away from light. After rinsing with PBS, specimens were re-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide solution. Following dehydration through a graded ethanol series, specimens were sequentially permeated with dehydrating agents and epoxy resin (Epon-812) embedding medium at varying concentrations. The specimens were finally embedded in Epon-812. Ultrathin Sects. (60–90 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica UC7rt), mounted on copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were imaged using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL JEM-1400FLASH) at 80 kV. Each copper mesh was first observed at low magnification to examine all cells, with images captured from selected regions of interest. Subsequently, specific ultrastructural alterations were examined.

Piuma nanoindenter

Retinal stiffness was measured using a Piuma nanoindenter (Optics11Life, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). After enucleation, retinas were gently isolated and mounted photoreceptor side down on agarose gel in a PBS-filled chamber. The ILM faced upward for indentation. A spherical-tip cantilever (radius 45 μm, spring constant ~ 0.05 N/m) was used to perform nano-indentation at 9 points (3 × 3 grid) within the central retina. Indentation depth was kept at 5–10 μm to ensure elastic deformation. Load-displacement curves were generated using the Piuma Nanoindenter Software (v3.5.0). Optics11 DataViewer Software (v2.5.0) was used to calculate the modulus from the load-displacement curves. Young’s modulus was calculated using the built-in software based on the Hertzian contact model. Average values and heatmaps were generated per sample, and stiffness differences among groups were compared statistically.

Cell culture experiments

MIO-M1 cell culture and treatment

The human Moorfields/Institute of Ophthalmology-Müller 1 (MIO-M1) cell line (RRID: CVCL_0433), an immortalized retinal MG cell line previously established and characterized by Professor G.A. Limb, was used in this study [60]. Cells were maintained in complete medium containing 90% Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Cytiva Inc., USA), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco Life Technologies, Grand Island, USA), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Recombinant human insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2; HY-P7019; MedChemExpress, NY, USA) was reconstituted at 100 µg/mL in carrier protein solution (0.1% BSA, 5% HSA, 10% FBS, or 5% trehalose) for long-term storage at − 20 °C, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

CCK-8 assay

MIO-M1 cells (2000 cells/well) were inoculated into 96-well plates overnight for 24 h. After the cells adhered, they were treated with DMEM medium containing concentration gradients of rhIGF-2 (0, 1, 10, 50, 100, and 200 ng/mL) for 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Cell proliferation ability was detected using the enhanced CCK-8 kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Subsequently, 10 µL of the enhanced CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Transwell migration assay

The 1 × 10^5 MIO-M1 cells were inoculated in serum-free medium and cultured for 24 h under different conditions. 10%FBS was added to the lower chamber as the chemotactic medium. Cells were migrated from the upper chamber to the lower chamber through the holes of the Transwell insert. After 24 h, the unmigrated cells were gently wiped off with a cotton swab moistened with PBS. The cells in the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, and 0.1% crystal violet was added for 5 min. After cleaning to clean the background, stained cells were photographed and counted under an optical microscope.

Colony formation

MIO-M1 cells in the logarithmic growth period were digested with 0.25% trypsin to make a single-cell suspension. The cell suspension was diluted to 1000 cells/well and inoculated into 6-well plates, and 2 mL of cell suspension was added to each well. After shaking evenly, put it in an incubator at 37℃ and 5% CO2 for 2–3 weeks, and change the culture medium every 3 days. Stop the culture when the number of cells in a single clone exceeds 50. Discard the culture medium, add 1 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde to each hole for fixation, and add crystal violet dye solution for dyeing. Photographs were taken by an optical microscope, and the number of clones was counted using Fiji software.

Cell cycle by flow cytometry

The cells were washed with PBS, trypsinized, and centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min to collect the cells. Resuspend the cells by adding approximately 1 mL of pre-cooled PBS in an ice bath. Add 1 mL of 70% ethanol pre-cooled in an ice bath and fix overnight at 4 °C. Wash the cells once with PBS, add about 1 milliliter of PBS pre-cooled in an ice bath, and resuspend the cells. Add 500 µl of propidium iodide staining solution to each tube of the cell sample. Incubate at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min and store at 4 °C in the dark. Cell-cycle assays were performed using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA).

Edu staining

EdU staining was performed using the BeyoClick™ EDU-488 cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Inoculate MIO-M1 cells in 6-well plates. The EdU working solution (20µM) of 2X preheated at 37 °C was added to 6-well plates in an equal volume and incubated for 2 h. After EdU labeling, the cells were successively fixed, washed, and permeabilized. Cells were evenly covered by adding 0.5 ml Click reaction solution to each well and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After washing the solution, add 1 ml of 1X Hoechst 33,342 staining solution to each well, and incubate at room temperature in the dark for 10 min to stain the cell nuclei. Fluorescence detection was carried out using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, DM3000, Switzerland). Calculate the average ratio of EdU-positive cells.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Differences between the two groups were evaluated using an unpaired two-tailed t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni multiple-comparison correction. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Unless otherwise stated, bar graphs, western blot images, and immunofluorescence data represent results from at least three independent biological replicates.
