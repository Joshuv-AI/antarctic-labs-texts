Cost Analysis and Resource Utilization of Gynecologic Cancer-Related Thoracenteses and Paracenteses
Authors: Yingao Zhang, Olivia Foy, Grace Pipes, Margaret I. Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This economic evaluation evaluates the frequency and cost associated with unindicated laboratory tests on paracentesis and thoracentesis specimens from patients with gynecologic cancers to highlight potential areas for improvement and waste minimization.

Introduction

Paracentesis and thoracentesis are common procedures for patients with advanced cancers and associated ascites or pleural effusion. Fluid studies may be diagnostic, although in patients with an established cancer diagnosis, routine fluid laboratory tests may be extraneous.1 In the context of rising annual health care costs and widespread adoption of electronic medical record systems, unnecessary laboratory testing contributes to waste and has been targeted in multiple cost-optimization interventions and models.2,3 Fluid studies on malignant ascites or effusions have not yet been analyzed in this regard. Thus, we aim to evaluate the frequency and cost associated with unindicated laboratory tests on paracentesis and thoracentesis specimens from patients with gynecologic cancers and to highlight potential areas of improvement for waste minimization.

Methods

This economic evaluation reviewed all consecutive patients diagnosed with a gynecologic cancer who underwent at least one paracentesis or thoracentesis at a single tertiary-care institution from 2015 to 2025. Patients were excluded if they had a second cancer or medical comorbidities that may contribute to ascites or pleural effusions (eg, congestive heart failure, cirrhosis, kidney failure). Clinical scenarios leading up to each procedure were evaluated by a physician (Y.Z,. O.F., or G.P. with clinical guidance from M.G.) to determine if the orders on the extracted fluid were indicated; for example, for a patient with known ovarian cancer and recurrent malignant ascites undergoing a therapeutic paracentesis, fluid studies such as cytology, fluid cultures, fluid albumin, or cell count would be considered unindicated in the absence of other clinical symptoms (such as fever, unexplained leukocytosis, or new abnormalities in kidney or liver function tests). Laboratory costs were obtained from internal institutional financial records. Fisher exact tests and simple logistic regression were used for comparative statistics, performed using GraphPad Prism 11.0.1 (GraphPad Software). This study was approved by the Cedars-Sinai Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent due to its retrospective nature and use of deidentified data. This study followed the CHEERS reporting guideline.

Results

The final analysis cohort included 216 patients with gynecologic cancers. Median age at diagnosis was 62.9 (IQR, 53.5-71.2) years. Most patients had ovarian, tubal, or primary peritoneal cancer (73% [158]), followed by endometrial cancer (19% [40]) and either cervical or vulvovaginal cancer (8% [18]). Of these patients, 78% (169) underwent at least one paracentesis, and 45% (98) underwent at least one thoracentesis. A total of 665 paracenteses and 311 thoracenteses were analyzed. Laboratory test results were sent on the fluid from 40% (395 of 976) of these procedures; of these, 79% (314) had at least one unindicated test performed. The most common unindicated laboratory tests were fluid culture (77% [247 of 320]), cell count (73% [239 of 326]), fluid protein (73% [200 of 274]), and cytology (66% [177 of 267]). Discussion

In patients with solid cancers with malignant ascites or pleural effusions, most paracenteses and thoracenteses are performed therapeutically and may be repeated many times over the course of treatment. In these patients, routine fluid laboratory tests should not be reflexively ordered unless there is a relevant clinical scenario. For example, in patients with cirrhosis with chronic ascites, laboratory tests on therapeutic paracenteses samples have been shown to increase health care cost without providing any discernible benefit.4 The potential cost-savings highlighted in this study represent a small portion of all cancer-related paracenteses and thoracenteses; gynecologic cancers contribute approximately 55% of malignant ascites but less than 10% of malignant pleural effusions.5,6 More studies are needed to confirm if this pattern of unindicated laboratory testing exists across other cancer types and other health systems.

We also observed that nononcology clinicians were more likely to order unindicated laboratory tests, although this is also in the context of more complex clinical care delivered in the inpatient or emergency setting. Additionally, within many electronic medical records, the option to include fluid laboratory tests is often bundled with the procedure order for paracentesis or thoracentesis, which may contribute to overordering. However, these insights may not be generalizable given the wide diversity of medical systems and limited by the single-institution and retrospective nature of this study. Nevertheless, future interventions such as routine incorporation of oncology clinicians within multidisciplinary care, targeted clinician education, or optimizing order sets should be tested in various care settings.
