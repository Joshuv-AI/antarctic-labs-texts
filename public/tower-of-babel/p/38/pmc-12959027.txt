Introduction

Mental health outcomes among children in conflict with the law in correctional facilities in Africa remain a critical yet under-researched area. Mental health is a significant concern within both public health and children justice systems, as incarceration can exacerbate psychological distress through exposure to trauma, violence and social isolation.1 2 Importantly, while children detention is often associated with punitive measures, structured correctional environments may also serve as settings that promote rehabilitation, psychological growth and positive behavioural change.3 These structured environments are commonly referred to as child and youth care centres. For example, in South Africa, children in conflict with the law who have been detained may be placed in child and youth care centres with secure-care components, as authorised under the Children’s Act 38 of 2005, read together with the Child Justice Act 75 of 2008. These Acts provide that a children’s court may order placement in a child and youth care centre if the parent or caregiver is unable to control the child or if the child exhibits criminal behaviour, and only when alternative care options are deemed inappropriate. Child and youth care centres are specifically designed to accommodate children in conflict with the law, providing a structured and supervised environment in accordance with the objectives of the child justice system. Despite not being dedicated mental health facilities, these settings play a crucial role in supporting the psychological well-being of children in conflict with the law by providing structured care, supervision and interventions that can mitigate distress and promote adaptive coping strategies.4

Globally, children in conflict with the law experience high rates of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder and conduct disorders.5 6 Access to mental health interventions, educational programmes and psychosocial support within child and youth care centres can enhance emotional regulation, build resilience and reduce recidivism. According to the WHO, mental healthcare is a fundamental human right and must be integrated into all settings, including child and youth care centres.7 Therefore, access to mental health interventions, educational programmes and psychosocial support can enhance emotional regulation, build resilience and reduce rates of recidivism.8 9 In Africa, children justice systems often face challenges such as overcrowding, limited resources and inadequate mental health services.3 10 Despite these challenges, research on the psychological outcomes of children in conflict with the law remains limited. Existing studies predominantly focus on determining the prevalence of psychiatric disorders among the general population.2 10 11

A child in conflict with the law is generally understood as an individual who is old enough to be held legally responsible for a crime but still below the age at which the law considers a person an adult. Internationally, the minimum age of criminal responsibility ranges from 6 to 18 years, while the age of criminal majority is typically 18 years.12 13 In Africa, these thresholds vary by country, reflecting differences in legal traditions and sociocultural understandings of childhood. For instance, South Africa and Kenya set the age of criminal responsibility at 12 years old.14 15 The United Nations defines juveniles as individuals aged 15 to 24 years, whereas national laws across the continent generally define children as persons under 18 years old.12 For this review, a child in conflict with the law is defined as any person above the minimum age of criminal responsibility but below 18 years of age, consistent with the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child and the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child. Equally important, the review will focus exclusively on children in conflict with the law, including those who have been charged with, found guilty of, or sentenced for committing a crime. Children residing in correctional facilities solely due to the incarceration of a parent or caregiver will be excluded, thereby ensuring that the review specifically addresses children who are themselves subject to the criminal justice process.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is a paucity of literature providing precise estimates of common mental health outcomes among children in conflict with the law in child and youth care centres across Africa. While several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined the mental health of children in conflict with the law globally, these studies consistently report elevated rates of depression, anxiety, substance use disorders and post-traumatic stress disorder.16,18 However, the existing evidence base predominantly reflects data from high-income countries such as the USA, the UK and Australia, with minimal representation from African contexts. According to Atilola et al,10 no study from Africa was deemed eligible in a global review of detained youth, primarily because Africa-specific research databases were not included in the review. These findings underscore the ‘hidden nature’ of African literature in this field and further justify the need for a systematic review that specifically examines mental health outcomes among children in conflict with the law in African child and youth care centres.

As a result, the unique sociocultural, economic and institutional factors shaping the mental health of children in conflict with the law in Africa remain poorly understood. Furthermore, most global reviews have concentrated on psychiatric morbidity and risk factors, with limited exploration of potential protective mechanisms, rehabilitative opportunities or psychosocial interventions that may exist within African child and youth care centres. Addressing these gaps is particularly important, as the African context is often characterised by resource-constrained mental health systems, overcrowded facilities and diverse legal definitions of children in conflict with the law. A context-specific synthesis is therefore essential to generate evidence that can guide contextually relevant policies, interventions and reforms within the continent’s child justice systems.

Thus, this systematic review aims to address this gap by synthesising the available evidence on mental health outcomes among children in conflict with the law in African child and youth care centres. By examining the full spectrum of psychological effects, from trauma-related disorders to rehabilitative interventions and psychosocial supports, this review will provide a comprehensive overview of the mental health challenges faced by children in conflict with the law in Africa, as well as the strategies that may mitigate these challenges. The findings of this review will inform policymakers, mental health professionals and child justice stakeholders in the development of interventions that not only mitigate harm but also promote rehabilitation. Furthermore, the review will contribute to the attainment of broader continental and global development objectives. Significantly, this review aligns with key policy frameworks that emphasise the well-being of children in conflict with the law and reinforce institutional accountability. It supports the African Union’s Agenda 2063, particularly its aspiration for a peaceful, inclusive and resilient continent.19 20 Congruently, the review reflects the priorities of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3 and 16, which intend to promote mental health and well-being, and promote peace, justice and strong institutions.21 This review aims to advance the mental health and overall well-being of children in conflict with the law, safeguard human rights within correctional settings and strengthen institutional responses to mental health challenges.

Ecological framework and mental health of incarcerated juveniles in Africa

The children in conflict with the law in the justice system often experience complex psychosocial challenges and require a framework that addresses these needs through targeted interventions.22 To fully grasp the mental health challenges faced by children in conflict with the law in African child and youth care centres, it is essential to adopt a framework that accounts for multiple, interacting environmental influences. One such framework is Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory, which views human development as occurring within a complex system of nested environments, from immediate surroundings to broader societal contexts.23 These systems include the microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem and chronosystem, each contributing uniquely to psychological outcomes.

At the microsystem level, a child in conflict with the law has direct interactions with peers, family and institutional staff that significantly influence their mental health. Many children in conflict with the law enter correctional systems with the experience of adverse childhood events, such as abuse or abandonment.24 The nature of relationships within these facilities, whether characterised by neglect or care, can positively or negatively affect their emotional well-being.

The mesosystem consists of the connections between various microsystems. In this context, it encompasses the relationship between the child in conflict with the law, their family and the correctional institution, as well as the interaction between rehabilitation staff and external support services. Maintaining strong family connections and having coordinated rehabilitation programmes are associated with better psychological and social outcomes.25 26 Moving outward, the exosystem involves wider institutional forces that indirectly influence the children in conflict with the law. Many correctional settings across Africa suffer from poor infrastructure, a lack of trained personnel and insufficient mental health services.27,30 These systemic deficiencies can worsen existing mental health conditions or contribute to the development of new ones. The macrosystem reflects the cultural and societal backdrop, including prevailing beliefs about mental illness and child justice. In numerous African societies, there remains considerable stigma around mental health issues, and justice systems often prioritise punishment over rehabilitation.3 31 32 Such societal norms limit efforts to implement supportive, trauma-informed care within correctional institutions.

The chronosystem introduces the element of time, capturing how life transitions or long periods of detention during key developmental stages affect mental health trajectories. Incarceration during adolescence, when identity and psychological stability are still forming, can have long-lasting consequences, particularly in environments lacking therapeutic support.33 34 Altogether, the ecological systems model provides a valuable lens for understanding the range of factors influencing the mental health of children in conflict with the law in detention. It highlights the need for interventions that extend beyond the individual level to address the structural, cultural and policy-related factors that shape the development and rehabilitation of children in conflict with the law.

Methods

To ensure a well-organised and transparent approach to planning and documenting this systematic review protocol, we have adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P).35 To facilitate comprehensive and unbiased reporting in our primary systematic review study, we will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-2020).35 36 In alignment with these frameworks, the methodological section of this protocol includes the following key components: eligibility criteria for studies, search strategies, data extraction methods, assessment of study quality, data synthesis and the presentation and discussion of results.

Eligibility criteria

Search strategy, electronic database and information sources

Initially, exploratory searches were conducted in multiple databases to refine the search strategy. These preliminary searches helped identify relevant keywords, subject headings and Boolean operators, and to assess the scope and availability of literature on children mental health in African correctional facilities. Insights from these initial searches guided the development of the final, comprehensive search strategy to ensure that the review captured all relevant studies while minimising irrelevant results. Thereafter, as depicted in online supplemental file 1, a comprehensive search will be conducted across a range of electronic databases, including PubMed, Sabinet, Scopus, EBSCOhost, Web of Science and PsycINFO. The search strategy will be customised for each database by employing Boolean operators, truncation and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to ensure comprehensive and accurate identification of relevant studies on mental health outcomes among children in conflict with the law in Africa.

Studies published from January 2015 to December 2025 will be considered. The review period aligns with the adoption of the African Union’s Agenda 2063, a strategic framework launched in 2015 to promote inclusive and sustainable development across the African continent. This timeframe enables the assessment of research contributions toward key aspirations of the agenda, particularly Aspiration 1, which seeks to expand access to quality healthcare services, Aspiration 3, which promotes human rights, justice and the rule of law and Aspiration 6, which focuses on the welfare and protection of children. It captures a critical decade of efforts aimed at enhancing mental health outcomes for children in conflict with the law in correctional facilities within the African continent. Additionally, to ensure access to credible and peer-reviewed data, grey literature sources will be excluded. As an example, the PubMed search strategy is presented below. The comprehensive search strategy, developed with support from an institutional librarian (see online supplemental material), includes combinations such as (“prevalence” OR “incidence” OR “epidemiology” OR “frequency” OR “occurrence” OR “common”) AND (outcome* OR effect* OR result* OR consequence* OR implication*) AND (“associated factors” OR correlates OR “risk factors” OR “contributing factors” OR “protective factors” OR promotive factors) AND (child* OR juvenile OR offender OR “juvenile delinquent” OR “juvenile criminal” OR “juvenile justice” OR “child justice”) AND (“resilience” OR “post-traumatic growth” OR “forgiveness” OR “optimism”) AND (“anxiety” OR “anxious” OR “anxiety disorder” OR “generalized anxiety disorder” OR “separation anxiety” OR “depression” OR “depressive disorder” OR “depressive symptoms” OR “major depressive disorder” OR “post-traumatic stress” OR “post-traumatic stress disorder” OR “PTSD” OR “suicidal ideation” OR “suicidal thoughts” OR “suicide attempt” OR suicidal behav* OR “self-harm” OR “self-injury” OR “sleep disturbance” OR “sleep disorder” OR “insomnia” OR “eating disorder” OR “anorexia nervosa” OR “bulimia nervosa” OR “binge eating disorder”) AND (“Africa”). Importantly, the geographical focus of this review will be limited to the 55 African countries, specifically targeting studies that examine mental health issues among children in conflict with the law. The search strategy will incorporate both current and historical names of countries that underwent name changes between 2015 and 2025, in line with the classification provided by the African Union.37 For instance, the Kingdom of Swaziland, which officially became the Kingdom of eSwatini on 19 April 2018, will be represented in the search strategy using both ‘Swaziland’ and ‘eSwatini’ to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Study records

The management of study records will involve systematically importing all identified studies into the EndNote reference manager. Following import, the titles and abstracts will be independently screened by two authors (MTR and SMN) to assess their relevance. Studies that meet the initial screening criteria will proceed to full-text evaluation, which will be conducted by three authors (MDS, MJT and EN-BQ) according to predefined eligibility criteria. To limit reviewers’ bias, all three reviewers will independently review each potentially eligible paper. We will document the number of excluded full texts along with the reasons for their exclusion. In cases of disagreements during full-text assessments, an arbitrator (TS) will be consulted to resolve the discrepancies. To ensure comprehensive data collection, we will also contact the study authors via email when additional information is required. The entire process will be depicted using the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.38

Data collection and data items

On completing the data search process, all eligible full-text articles will be systematically documented in a literature matrix table. This table will serve as a structured tool for capturing and extracting key information from the selected studies. The extracted data will include details such as author(s) and publication year, country of origin, study objectives, research design and methodology, sample size and participant characteristics, primary findings and an assessment of study quality. This process will be conducted independently by three authors (KP, MJT and MR) for all relevant papers to limit reviewers’ bias.

Risk of bias in individual studies

All eligible and included studies will undergo a thorough quality assessment using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).39 This tool is specifically developed to critically evaluate the methodological quality of quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies within systematic mixed-studies reviews. This assessment will be independently conducted by three authors (TAN, MM and EK), who will complete the checklist individually. In cases where discrepancies arise, a fourth author (TS) will be consulted to facilitate consensus.

Data synthesis

The decision to aggregate prevalence estimates through meta-analysis will be based on the extent of heterogeneity among the selected studies, as measured by the I² statistic.40 Notably, if substantial heterogeneity is identified (I²>75%), a meta-analysis will be deemed inappropriate. In such cases, prevalence estimates will be reported descriptively, incorporating median values and IQRs. Conversely, if heterogeneity is low to moderate (I²≤70%), the estimates will be pooled using a random effects model, performed in Jamovi (V.2.6.44 for Windows).41

In instances where significant clinical or methodological variability is observed, subgroup analyses will be conducted. When quantitative synthesis is not feasible, a qualitative narrative summary will be presented to outline the common mental health outcomes among children in conflict with the law in correctional facilities across Africa. A narrative synthesis will be conducted following the guidelines by Popay et al,42 which involve developing a preliminary synthesis, identifying patterns and relationships within and across studies and critically appraising the strength and consistency of the evidence. Following this, the findings will be further analysed using Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory.15 This theoretical framework will guide the thematic classification of mental health correlates across multiple environmental systems ranging from individual-level influences on broader societal and policy-level determinants, offering a structured understanding of how these multi-level factors interact to shape the mental health of children in conflict with the law in African correctional settings.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses will be conducted if a sufficient number of studies with comparable outcomes are identified. These analyses will explore variations in the prevalence and determinants of mental health outcomes among children in conflict with the law in correctional facilities across different contexts. Temporal subgroup analyses may be performed by categorising studies according to the period of data collection, such as pre-COVID-19, during COVID-19 and post-COVID-19, to examine potential temporal influences on mental health outcomes. Additionally, geographical subgroup analyses will be conducted by grouping studies based on their African regions of origin: Southern Africa, Northern Africa, Eastern Africa, Western Africa and Central Africa. This regional classification will support the identification of context-specific trends and factors influencing children in conflict with the law and mental health within correctional settings. Overall, these subgroup analyses aim to deepen the understanding of variability in depression prevalence and determinants across diverse temporal and geographical contexts.

Sensitivity analysis

To identify the sources of heterogeneity within a dataset characterised by substantial variability, we will use the leave-one-out technique. This method involves systematically excluding one study from the dataset at a time, followed by an evaluation to determine whether the removal results in a reduction in the overall heterogeneity level. By conducting this iterative analysis for each study, we can effectively pinpoint the specific factors contributing to the observed variability. This approach not only facilitates a more nuanced understanding of common mental health challenges among children in conflict with the law but also supports a more precise and comprehensive analysis of the data.

Ethics and dissemination

This research project does not require ethical approval from any regulatory ethics committee, as it exclusively relies on secondary data obtained from existing literature published in peer-reviewed journals. To maintain academic integrity and appropriately acknowledge all contributors, the study will follow the BMJ Open Referencing Style, ensuring a systematic and accurate citation of original authors and their work. The findings of this study will be disseminated to the public through journal publication and presentations at relevant national and international conferences.

Discussion

This systematic review protocol is designed to comprehensively examine the mental health challenges faced by children in conflict with the law in correctional facilities across African countries, while also exploring potential interventions and positive psychological adaptations. The discussion aligns closely with the study’s three primary aims, identifying prevalent common mental health disorders, evaluating psychological adaptations and examining modifiable institutional and psychosocial factors. By synthesising existing evidence, this review will address the significant research gap in this understudied population, providing insights that can inform policy and practice in child justice systems across the region. The Ecological Systems Theory serves as the foundational framework for understanding these complex issues, offering a multi-level perspective that captures the interplay between individual experiences and broader systemic factors.23 Meanwhile, the methodology following PRISMA 2015 and PRISMA 2020 guidelines ensures review’s findings will be both rigorous and thorough.35

The first specific aim of identifying prevalent mental health disorders focuses on synthesising evidence about conditions such as depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorders and conduct disorders among children in conflict with the law in Africa. Existing literature suggests these populations face particularly high rates of mental health challenges due to a combination of pre-existing vulnerabilities and the stressors of detention.30 43 However, research in African contexts remains limited and fragmented. By systematically collating data from diverse settings across the region, this review will provide a clearer picture of both regional variations and common patterns in mental health outcomes. This synthesis is especially crucial given the unique cultural, social and economic factors that shape mental health in African correctional systems, which may differ significantly from patterns observed in Western contexts. The findings will help establish baseline prevalence rates that can guide resource allocation and intervention planning.

Beyond documenting negative outcomes, the review’s second specific aim focuses on understanding psychological adaptations and potential positive developments among children in conflict with the law. This includes examining phenomena like resilience, post-traumatic growth and skill acquisition that may occur during detention. While much existing research emphasises pathology, emerging evidence suggests that structured environments can sometimes foster personal development, particularly when rehabilitation programmes are available.44 45 In the African context, where formal mental health services may be limited, understanding these adaptive processes becomes particularly valuable. The review will explore how factors like educational programmes, vocational training and social support contribute to positive psychological outcomes, offering a more balanced perspective on the detention experience. This focus aligns with global shifts towards trauma-informed care and strength-based approaches in child justice systems.

The third specific aim examines modifiable institutional and psychosocial factors that influence mental health outcomes. Correctional facilities in African countries often face systemic challenges, including overcrowding, resource limitations and insufficient mental health services.30 At the same time, cultural attitudes towards mental illness and children in conflict with the law may create additional barriers to care. The review will analyse how these various factors interact to shape mental health trajectories, identifying key leverage points for intervention. Special attention will be given to promising practices that have emerged in resource-constrained settings, as these may offer practical models for improvement. By taking an ecological perspective, the review can identify interventions at multiple levels—from individual counselling approaches to policy reforms—that could significantly improve outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory provides the ideal framework for organising and interpreting these complex findings.23 At the microsystem level, the review examines how daily interactions with peers, staff and family members influence children in conflict with the law and mental health. The mesosystem analysis explores connections between different aspects of a juvenile’s environment, such as how family involvement interacts with institutional programming. Moving outward, the exosystem level considers how institutional policies and resource allocations create structural barriers or opportunities for mental health support. The macrosystem perspective captures broader cultural and societal factors, including stigma and prevailing attitudes toward child justice. Finally, the chronosystem adds a temporal dimension, considering how developmental timing and duration of detention affect long-term outcomes. This comprehensive framework ensures the review captures the full complexity of factors shaping mental health in correctional settings.

Methodologically, the review adheres to rigorous PRISMA-P 2015 standards to ensure transparency and reproducibility.35 The search strategy encompasses both international databases and regional sources to capture relevant studies published between 2015 and 2025. Boolean search terms tailored to the African context will help identify studies that might otherwise be overlooked. The MMAT will be used to assess study quality, with a structured process for resolving discrepancies between reviewers.39 For data synthesis, statistical heterogeneity will guide the choice between meta-analysis and narrative synthesis, ensuring appropriate handling of diverse findings. Planned subgroup analyses will explore variations by factors like country, facility type and gender, while sensitivity analyses will assess the robustness of key findings. These rigorous methods will produce findings that policymakers and practitioners can use with confidence.

The implications of this review extend across multiple domains. For policymakers, the synthesised evidence can inform decisions about mental health service provision, staff training requirements and facility improvements. Clinicians may gain insights into culturally appropriate assessment and intervention approaches. The findings could also advocate for alternative approaches to child justice that balance accountability with rehabilitation. Importantly, the review will highlight critical knowledge gaps that require further research, such as the need for longitudinal studies or evaluations of specific intervention models. By grounding these implications in ecological theory, the review can suggest coordinated interventions at multiple system levels, from individual therapy to community reintegration programmes.

While offering significant potential contributions, the review does face certain limitations. The available literature in some African regions may be sparse, potentially limiting generalisability. Heterogeneity in study designs and measurement approaches may limit comparisons across settings. Additionally, the focus on published studies may overlook valuable insights from unpublished literature. Despite these challenges, the systematic approach and theoretical framework will maximise what can be learnt from existing evidence. Future research directions might include comparative studies across regions, intervention trials and long-term follow-up studies to assess post-release outcomes.

In conclusion, this systematic review protocol outlines a comprehensive approach to understanding mental health among children in conflict with the law in African countries. By aligning its aims with ecological theory and rigorous methodology, it promises to produce findings that are both academically robust and practically useful. The resulting synthesis will fill critical knowledge gaps while providing evidence to support improved mental healthcare and rehabilitation services in child justice systems across the region. Ultimately, this work contributes to the broader goals of protecting children in conflict with the law and supporting their successful reintegration into society.
