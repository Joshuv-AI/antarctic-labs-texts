Consensus statement on the multidisciplinary treatment of nasopharyngeal carcinoma with immune checkpoint inhibitors in China
Authors: G. Wang, J. Qin, W. Nong, D. Ma, C. Wang, H. Yu, S. Tang, M. Chen, J. Gao, T. Gao, L. Li, K. Hu, P. Yang, Q. Qiu, S. Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study aims to establish a multidisciplinary consensus on the treatment of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) in China, with a particular focus on the guidelines for the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to ensure scientific and rational treatment protocols.MethodsThe consensus was formulated based on the ‘guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of NPC (2024 edition)’ issued by the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology. The Delphi method was used to collect and consolidate expert opinions. Through repeated solicitation and integration of expert opinions, an expert consensus on combined ICI therapy for NPC was ultimately reached. Additionally, a literature review approach was used to facilitate consensus building.ResultsThe consensus encompasses the current status of NPC diagnosis and treatment, synthesizing existing clinical evidence to deeply discuss major clinical issues. The primary focus of the consensus is on the clinical practice principles and methods of combinatory ICI therapy. It provides guidelines for patient selection, treatment protocols, and management of adverse events in clinical practice. The consensus emphasizes that multidisciplinary collaboration can further enhance the treatment outcomes and quality of life for NPC patients. Future research needs to continue exploring optimized treatment protocols and predictive biomarkers to achieve more precise personalized therapy.ConclusionThe consensus underscores the role of ICIs in NPC treatment and provides understanding and reference for clinical application. Given the differences in drug approval and availability across countries and the high incidence of NPC in southern China, this consensus particularly focuses on the treatment of Chinese patients.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant tumor originating from the epithelial lining of the nasopharynx, characterized by its uneven global distribution, with a significant concentration in Asia, particularly in southern China. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the incidence of NPC is relatively low among global malignancies, but markedly higher in certain endemic regions. As of 2022, the global annual crude incidence rate of NPC was 1.5 per 100 000, whereas in China, the rate was 3.6 per 100 000, with the rates in Guangxi and Guangdong provinces being several dozen times higher than the global average.1 The onset of NPC is predominantly observed in individuals aged 40-60 years. Males are 2.75 times more likely to be diagnosed with NPC and have a 3.25 times higher risk of mortality compared with females.2 The insidious nature of NPC symptoms often leads to delayed clinical diagnosis until advanced stages.3 The primary clinical manifestations include epistaxis, nasal obstruction, tinnitus, hearing loss, cervical lymphadenopathy, headache, and facial numbness.4,5 NPC is histopathologically classified into three main types: keratinizing squamous-cell carcinoma, non-keratinizing carcinoma, and basaloid squamous-cell carcinoma. In endemic regions, non-keratinizing carcinoma associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection predominates.4 Based on the primary tumor site, size, extent of invasion, cervical lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis, NPC is staged as I, II, III, and IV, with stage I being early and stages II-IV being advanced. Prognosis is influenced by various factors, including staging, treatment modalities, biomarkers, and immune status.6 Generally, early-stage NPC patients respond well to treatment, whereas late-stage patients require more extensive and intensive therapeutic approaches. NPC exhibits high recurrence and drug resistance rates, ranging from 30% to 60%, with common recurrence sites including the primary tumor location, cervical lymph nodes, bone, lung, and liver.7,8 In recent years, significant advancements have been made in the treatment of NPC, encompassing surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. However, the prognosis remains suboptimal. Late-stage NPC, in particular, is highly invasive with extensive local infiltration, substantially increasing treatment difficulty and complexity, and significantly reducing patient survival rates. In this context, the application of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has opened new avenues for NPC treatment and has become an important therapeutic option for patients with NPC.

The ICIs targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) have revolutionized cancer therapy in recent years. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved several anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), such as pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and cemiplimab, for the treatment of various malignancies.9 Toripalimab, a humanized immunoglobulin G4 mAb, specifically recognizes and blocks PD-1. This drug has been approved by the FDA for first-line treatment of adults with metastatic or recurrent locally advanced NPC (LA-NPC) in combination with cisplatin and gemcitabine, or as monotherapy for adults with recurrent, unresectable, or metastatic NPC who have experienced disease progression during or after platinum-containing chemotherapy.10 ICIs encompass PD-1 antibodies, PD-L1 antibodies, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) antibodies. Major anti-PD-1 mAbs include pembrolizumab, nivolumab, camrelizumab, toripalimab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, and penpulimab. Ipilimumab is a representative CTLA-4 antibody. The JUPITER-02 randomized clinical trial and the CAPTAIN-1st study demonstrated that toripalimab or camrelizumab combined with gemcitabine and cisplatin (GP) significantly improved median progression-free survival (mPFS) and median overall survival (mOS) in patients with recurrent or metastatic NPC (RM-NPC).11,12 A phase II trial (NCT03097939) investigating the efficacy and safety of dual ICIs in the treatment of NPC evaluated the combination of ipilimumab and nivolumab in RM-NPC. The best overall response (BOR) rate for this dual ICI regimen was found to be 38%, with an mPFS of 5.3 months and a mOS of 19.5 months. This regimen was well tolerated, with few treatment-related adverse events necessitating discontinuation. Although the BOR did not meet expectations, patients with low plasma EBV DNA titers tended to have better responses and PFS.13

The Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) has included recommendations for immunotherapy in NPC in its 2023 guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of head and neck tumors, NPC, and the clinical application of ICIs. Specifically, the combination of tislelizumab with gemcitabine and cisplatin has been upgraded to a level I recommendation for first-line treatment of NPC, while toripalimab and camrelizumab have been elevated to level I recommendations for second-line or salvage therapy. However, due to space limitations, the discussion on ICIs within these guidelines is relatively brief. Therefore, this consensus, based on the recommendations from current authoritative guidelines both domestically and internationally regarding NPC immunotherapy, and considering the current state of NPC diagnosis and treatment in China along with existing clinical evidence, aims to provide a thorough discussion on key clinical issues. By integrating clinical experience from experts in various specialties, this consensus strives to establish a relatively standardized understanding and aims to offer a reference for the standardized application of ICIs in the treatment of NPC.

Methodology

This consensus refers to the ‘guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (2024 edition)’ issued by the CSCO. The ‘Standards for Developing Trustworthy Clinical Practice Guidelines’, published by the National Academy of Medicine in March 2011, were utilized as a model for organizing and preparing this consensus statement.14 Additionally, the methodology previously applied to the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer consensus guidelines was referenced in drafting this manuscript.15 The Delphi method was adopted to collect and summarize expert opinions, with specific steps including problem identification, expert team formation, multiple rounds of discussion, and consensus achievement.16 Through repeated solicitation and integration of expert opinions, an expert consensus on NPC immunotherapy was ultimately formulated. The Delphi method used to develop this consensus involved the following steps: (i) the issues lacking clinical evidence in the draft consensus were identified; (ii) an expert panel was assembled; (iii) during the first round of discussions, contentious issues were identified through electronic surveys where experts anonymously provided their opinions, with only the questionnaire administrator having access to personal information; (iv) in the second round, the summarized issues were reviewed, and consensus was formed; (v) in the third round, the expert panel made final judgments on any remaining points of contention, providing justifications for their decisions; (vi) a final conclusion was reached, with an agreement rate of 80% or higher. Ultimately, 16 experts participated in the survey, with detailed information available in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105763.

This effort aims to establish a consensus statement on the clinical application of tumor immunotherapy for NPC patients, ensuring the scientific and rationality of treatment protocols. Given the variations in drug approval and availability across different countries, and the high incidence of NPC in Asia, particularly in southern China, the focus of this group was on the treatment of Chinese patients.

As this study does not involve animal and patient experiments, ethical approval and consent to participate are not necessary.

Results

Selection of patients for ICI therapy

Advantaged patient populations for ICIs

PD-L1 is expressed in 89%-95% of NPC cells, suggesting that ICIs may be more efficacious in patients with high PD-L1 expression.17 Furthermore, several large clinical trials have demonstrated the survival benefits of anti-PD-1 mAbs, such as toripalimab, camrelizumab, and tislelizumab, in patients with RM-NPC, prolonging both PFS and OS.12,18 For instance, camrelizumab or toripalimab combined with GP as first-line therapy for NPC has significantly improved mPFS and safety compared with chemotherapy alone. Monotherapy with anti-PD-1 mAbs such as pembrolizumab has also demonstrated comparable therapeutic effects.19

Anti-PD-1 mAbs, including nivolumab, pembrolizumab, camrelizumab, toripalimab, and penpulimab have demonstrated significant efficacy and safety in RM-NPC patients who have failed or are intolerant to at least one systemic therapy, including chemotherapy. Patients with PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) ≥1% or EBV-associated RM-NPC may still benefit from PD-1 mAb treatment even after failure of platinum-based chemotherapy.19,20

In patients with metastatic NPC who experienced disease progression after second-line or later chemotherapy, results from the phase II clinical trial (NCT04220307) of the anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 bispecific mAb cadonilimab demonstrated significant therapeutic potential.13,21 Cadonilimab achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 30% and a disease control rate of 70%. These findings support cadonilimab as a promising candidate for ICI therapy, offering a new treatment option for patients unresponsive to conventional chemotherapy or those with rapidly progressing disease, potentially leading to favorable clinical outcomes. In addition, for high-risk NPC patients, specifically those with stages T1-T4, N2-N3, and T4N1, immunotherapy is recommended. The CONTINUUM study demonstrated an efficacy advantage by incorporating sintilimab into the standard chemoradiotherapy regimen, with a 3-year event-free survival (EFS) rate of 86.1% in the sintilimab group compared with 76% in the standard treatment group.22

For RM-NPC patients who experience disease progression within 6 months after adjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, specific ICIs are recommended. Toripalimab, administered at a dose of 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks, is one of the effective treatment options.18 This aims to inhibit immune checkpoints by activating the immune system, providing an additional therapeutic strategy for patients who have not responded to standard treatments.

Consensus statement 1

For high-risk NPC patients classified as T1-T4, N2-N3, and T4N1, ICI therapy is shown to improve disease-free survival rates. ICI therapy has demonstrated a certain level of efficacy and safety in patients with RM-NPC who have a TPS of ≥1%. For patients whose conditions continue to deteriorate after standard treatment, immunotherapy may be considered as a subsequent therapeutic approach. Additionally, patients with N3 stage, those with post-radiotherapy EBV DNA levels remaining higher than normal, and those with significant residual tumors after radiotherapy may derive greater benefits from ICI therapy (degree of expert consensus: 93.75%).

ICI treatment regimen for NPC

ICI-based first-line combination therapy

First-line ICI combination therapy for NPC primarily focuses on combining anti-PD-1 mAbs with the GP regimen. For patients with recurrent or metastatic NPC who meet the eligibility criteria, it is recommended to combine anti-PD-1 mAbs, such as camrelizumab, toripalimab, or tislelizumab, with the standard GP regimen. The treatment cycle is typically 21 days, with anti-PD-1 mAbs (e.g. camrelizumab 200 mg, toripalimab 240 mg, or tislelizumab 200 mg) administered on the first day of each cycle, and cisplatin (∼80 mg/m2) and gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2) administered on days 1 and 8. This combination therapy generally lasts for four to six cycles or until disease progression or intolerable toxicity occurs. In the absence of contraindications to ICIs, it is recommended to combine camrelizumab or toripalimab with the GP regimen for treatment. The specific treatment protocol follows the aforementioned guidelines. However, if the patient presents with contraindications to ICIs, the use of ICIs is not advisable (degree of expert consensus: 100%).

Second-line and later-line treatment strategies with ICIs

The management of RM-NPC following first-line platinum-based chemotherapy remains a significant clinical challenge. The objectives of second-line treatment include prolonging survival, controlling disease-related symptoms, and maintaining quality of life (QoL). In patients with platinum-resistant disease, deteriorating PS, and cumulative toxicity from prior treatments, a careful balance between efficacy and safety must be achieved. After failure of first-line platinum-based regimens, if patients are not candidates for local salvage therapy, single-agent treatment with drugs not previously used in the first-line setting is typically chosen, such as gemcitabine, docetaxel, or capecitabine. For patients who can tolerate chemotherapy, combination therapy involving chemotherapy with targeted therapy and immunotherapy is recommended following the failure of first-line treatment. Conversely, for patients who are less able to withstand chemotherapy, combinations of targeted agents with immunotherapy may be considered. Commonly used targeted agents include bevacizumab, anlotinib, apatinib, endostatin, and epidermal growth factor receptor mAbs.

Currently, advanced NPC has entered the era of immunotherapy. Monotherapy or combination treatments with agents such as camrelizumab, pembrolizumab, toripalimab, and nivolumab have gained significant importance in second-line and subsequent therapies, with median OS reported as 17.10, 16.50, 17.40, and 17.10 months, respectively.18,20,31,32 Anti-PD-1 mAbs are administered as monotherapy, with dosages adjusted based on patient characteristics and PD-L1 expression levels. For instance, the dosing regimen for toripalimab is 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks, while pembrolizumab is administered at 10 mg/kg every 2 weeks or 200 mg every 3 weeks. Cadonilimab, a bispecific anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 antibody, has demonstrated a 30% ORR and a 70% disease control rate in phase II clinical trials, providing an effective salvage treatment option for previously treated RM-NPC patients.31

Notably, an open-label, randomized phase III clinical trial (KEYNOTE-122) evaluated the efficacy and safety of pembrolizumab monotherapy compared with chemotherapy in platinum-pretreated RM-NPC patients. At a median follow-up of 45.1 months, the median OS for the pembrolizumab group was 17.2 months, compared with 15.3 months for the chemotherapy group, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.90 (95% confidence interval 0.67-1.19, P > 0.05), indicating no statistically significant difference. In terms of safety, the incidence of grade (G) 3-5 treatment-related adverse events was 10.3% in the pembrolizumab group versus 43.8% in the chemotherapy group. Therefore, while pembrolizumab monotherapy did not significantly improve OS in platinum-pretreated RM-NPC, it exhibited superior safety characteristics, with treatment-related toxicity significantly lower than that of chemotherapy.19 This provides important evidence for the use of immunotherapy in subsequent lines of treatment for NPC, suggesting that monotherapy with immunotherapy may represent a reasonable choice that balances efficacy and safety. For patients with RM-NPC who experience failure of first-line treatment or disease progression, participation in prospective clinical trials should be prioritized if there are no contraindications to ICIs. In subsequent-line treatments, ICIs can be used alone or in combination with chemotherapy. Recommended regimens include toripalimab at 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks, pembrolizumab at 10 mg/kg every 2 weeks or 200 mg every 3 weeks, nivolumab at 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks, or a combination of nivolumab at 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks with ipilimumab at 1 mg/kg every 6 weeks. If there are contraindications to ICIs, alternative salvage therapies such as docetaxel or capecitabine are recommended (degree of expert consensus: 100%).

Application of ICIs in conversion therapy

The goal of conversion therapy is to render previously unresectable or technically challenging tumors into resectable states, thereby providing the opportunity for curative surgery and potentially improving patient survival outcomes and long-term prognosis. A randomized phase II study (NCT02605967) evaluated the PFS of NPC patients treated with spartalizumab combined with chemotherapy. Although the combination therapy did not achieve the primary endpoint, patients experienced longer mOS and duration of response compared with chemotherapy alone. Moreover, spartalizumab demonstrated a safety profile consistent with other anti-PD-1 antibodies.33 The RATIONALE-309 study evaluated the efficacy and safety of tislelizumab combined with the GP regimen as a first-line treatment for RM-NPC. The combination therapy showed superior PFS compared with second-line treatments.34 In a real-world study, tislelizumab as a neoadjuvant treatment for patients with head and neck squamous-cell carcinoma and NPC significantly improved the ORR. This suggests that the administration of ICIs may provide patients with better surgical opportunities and potentially enhance long-term survival rates.35

Currently, there is no universally accepted definition of unresectable NPC, and consensus has not been reached regarding conversion therapy protocols, the optimal duration of treatment, or the timing of surgical resection. There is an urgent need for further clinical studies to define appropriate conversion therapy strategies, assess the safety of various treatment approaches and drug combinations, and evaluate surgery-related risks, potential complications, the risk–benefit ratio, and subsequent treatment plans.

Consensus statement 4

In conversion therapy, ICIs such as spartalizumab and tislelizumab have demonstrated potential in the treatment of NPC. Further clinical research is needed to explore optimal conversion therapy strategies, the impact of various treatment approaches and drug combinations on patient safety, the risks associated with subsequent surgery, potential complications, and the balance between risks and benefits. Given the favorable outcomes of radiotherapy in newly diagnosed NPC patients, it is not recommended for these patients to participate in clinical trials involving surgical approaches (degree of expert consensus: 93.75%).

Application of ICIs in LA-NPC

LA-NPC represents a significant patient population in clinical practice, and optimizing its treatment is crucial for improving cure rates. The potential benefits of advancing immunotherapy to LA-NPC have become a focal point of current research. Within the standard treatment framework for LA-NPC, the timing of integrating ICIs primarily focuses on the induction phase and immune maintenance. The combination of induction chemotherapy with anti-PD-1 therapy has been shown to significantly enhance the ORR, with tumor shrinkage being more pronounced compared with induction chemotherapy alone (ORR 88.1% versus 70.0%),36 while also reducing the risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis.37

In a multicenter trial, patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) combined with a PD-1 inhibitor demonstrated a 3-year OS rate of 89.5% following a camrelizumab-based induction chemotherapy regimen, with manageable toxicity.37 During the CCRT phase, the integration of ICIs significantly enhanced the efficacy of radiotherapy while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. The 2-year PFS for sintilimab combined with concurrent chemoradiotherapy reached 86.0%, significantly higher than that of the standard treatment group.22 Radiotherapy has been shown to up-regulate PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, thereby enhancing the immune activation effects of ICIs.38 The synchronous application of radiotherapy and ICIs may inhibit micrometastases and improve long-term survival.39

Furthermore, the removal of chemotherapy from the radiotherapy phase while incorporating nivolumab has demonstrated the ability to maintain efficacy while reducing treatment toxicity. This highlights nivolumab’s potent antitumor activity, low toxicity, and good tolerability.40 Similar efficacy has been corroborated in other retrospective analyses, such as a study involving 217 LA-NPC patients that compared the combination of ICIs with anti-PD-1 followed by CCRT, revealing that synchronous integration of ICIs with radiotherapy significantly improved tumor regression rates, providing better control strategies for locally advanced tumors.36 Importantly, this synchronous integration avoids the risk of treatment interruption and can serve as a complementary first-line treatment, particularly when dose optimization is appropriately managed.39

In the adjuvant phase, the addition of ICIs aims to reduce the risk of distant recurrence and metastasis, thereby improving OS. Adjuvant ICIs are particularly beneficial for high-risk LA-NPC patients, extending the duration of disease-free survival. The DIPPER trial reported that adjuvant camrelizumab treatment following induction chemotherapy and cisplatin-based chemoradiotherapy achieved the primary endpoint of improved EFS, although it did not enhance OS. QoL associated with camrelizumab treatment did not significantly deteriorate.41 Similarly, for patients with residual disease after treatment, the adjuvant regimen with the PD-1 inhibitor toripalimab exhibited antitumor activity, achieving an ORR of 95.7% and meeting the primary endpoint, thus providing a new option for controlling recurrence after conventional chemotherapy failure.42 Despite these studies confirming the potential of integration during the adjuvant phase, careful attention must be paid to patient selection and dose control to maximize benefits.

Consensus statement 5

Immunotherapy has advanced to the locally advanced stage of treatment. Various intervention modalities involving ICIs have demonstrated significant survival benefits; however, the optimal strategy for immunotherapy in LA-NPC still requires further research data for clarification. The sequential selection of induction, concurrent, and adjuvant treatment regimens has not yet been standardized. At each stage of treatment, systematic assessments of tumor regression and QoL for patients are essential. Specifically, during the induction treatment phase, it is crucial to evaluate immune-related adverse events to determine whether to continue the use of ICIs in subsequent phases. The application of ICIs must not compromise the efficacy of radiotherapy; if exacerbation of hypothyroidism or poor prognostic scores occur during concurrent chemoradiotherapy, the use of ICIs should be temporarily withheld. Additionally, the mechanisms of resistance remain a critical issue that needs exploration. Future research should focus on phase-specific assessments of ICI drug sensitivity and aim to identify more sensitive predictive indicators and biomarkers (degree of expert consensus: 100%).

Perioperative ICI treatment regimen

In surgically resectable NPC, ICIs are typically not included in perioperative treatment regimens. However, a study investigating the use of anti-PD-1 ICIs as consolidation therapy after endoscopic nasopharyngectomy (ENPG) in patients with recurrent LA-NPC showed promising results. Compared with the surgery-only group, the group receiving immunotherapy consolidation demonstrated a significantly improved 2-year PFS (80.0% versus 40.0%; HR 0.258), though there was no significant difference in 2-year OS.43 In summary, post-operative anti-PD-1 ICI consolidation may enhance PFS without causing severe or intolerable side-effects, suggesting that this approach could improve overall treatment outcomes for patients. However, current NPC treatment guidelines have not yet explicitly recommended perioperative ICI therapy. Therefore, the use of ICIs and their integration into perioperative treatment strategies should be guided by clinical research data and tailored to the specific circumstances of individual patients.

Consensus statement 6

For resectable recurrent NPC cases, ICIs are not recommended as a routine perioperative treatment. However, the use of anti-PD-1 ICIs as consolidation therapy after ENPG can significantly improve PFS in patients with locally advanced recurrent NPC. The perioperative ICI treatment regimen remains unclear, and the latest clinical study data and specific patient conditions need to be considered for personalized treatment (degree of expert consensus: 87.50%).

Consensus statement 7

With the demonstrated success of ICIs in the management of advanced NPC, there is consensus to extend this therapeutic modality to patients diagnosed with LA-NPC to potentially augment treatment effectiveness and mitigate treatment-associated toxicities (degree of expert consensus: 87.50%).

Rechallenge with ICIs

Patients with primary unknown melanoma have shown prolonged PFS upon ICI rechallenge, suggesting that these individuals may be more suitable candidates for further ICI therapy.44 Additionally, in a case involving a patient with advanced clear-cell renal carcinoma who experienced severe irAEs during initial ICI treatment, switching ICIs and combining with targeted therapy resulted in partial tumor regression and irAE control.45 This indicates that ICI rechallenge is feasible and may offer additional clinical benefits even after severe irAEs during the initial treatment phase under careful monitoring and appropriate patient selection.

The decision to rechallenge ICIs should be based on the severity of irAEs, the resolution of symptoms, tumor response, and the feasibility of alternative treatment options.46,47 For dermatological toxicities such as rash and/or pruritus, or reactive cutaneous capillary endothelial proliferation, ICIs can be considered for rechallenge once symptoms have resolved to G1. In cases of gastrointestinal toxicities, G2-G3 colitis induced by PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may allow for treatment rechallenge after symptoms have improved to G1 or lower. However, for moderate to life-threatening gastrointestinal toxicities caused by CTLA-4 inhibitors, ICI rechallenge is generally not recommended.

Regarding liver toxicity, for G2 transaminase elevations without increased bilirubin, ICIs can be restarted once alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase levels return to baseline. For G3 liver toxicity caused by combination PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors, it is recommended to rechallenge only with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. In cases of G4 hepatitis, rechallenging ICIs should be avoided.

In the endocrine system, patients with hypothyroidism do not need to discontinue treatment, and those with hyperthyroidism may restart ICIs after symptoms and thyroid function improve. Patients with primary adrenal insufficiency may resume ICIs following hormone replacement therapy. For pulmonary toxicity, G1 pneumonitis may allow for ICI rechallenge after radiographic improvement, while G2 pneumonitis can be considered for rechallenge once symptoms resolve to G1 and corticosteroid use has been discontinued or reduced to prednisone ≤10 mg (or equivalent). For G3-G4 pneumonitis, ICI rechallenge should be avoided.

For ocular toxicities, G2 events may allow ICI rechallenge after symptoms improve to G1 and upon ophthalmological consultation. For G3-G4 uveitis or scleritis, rechallenge should be avoided. In the case of neurological toxicities, G2-G4 myasthenia gravis patients should not undergo ICI rechallenge. For G1-G2 peripheral neuropathy, rechallenge can be considered when symptoms resolve to G1 or sensory neuropathies are well controlled. Mild to moderate aseptic meningitis may allow for rechallenge after complete symptom resolution. Any grade of Guillain–Barré syndrome, transverse myelitis, or G2-G4 encephalitis should preclude rechallenge.

For cardiovascular toxicities, G1 myocarditis may allow ICI rechallenge once symptoms subside, but G2-G4 myocarditis should avoid rechallenge. Moderate to severe inflammatory arthritis can be rechallenged after symptom control, but severe arthritis that significantly impacts daily life or QoL should not be rechallenged.

Tumor hyperprogression during ICI treatment

Hyperprogressive disease (HPD) is a pattern of tumor response characterized by an accelerated rate of tumor growth following treatment with ICIs. Approximately 30% of patients receiving ICI therapy may experience HPD.48 There is currently no standardized definition for HPD, but commonly used criteria include tumor progression within <2 months of treatment, with new lesions in at least two different organs; an increase in measurable lesions by at least 10 mm, and a ≥40% increase in the sum of target lesions compared with baseline; and a tumor growth rate (TGR) more than doubling.49, 50, 51 Among patients with RM-NPC, HPD was observed in 39.5% and 21.1% of patients according to RECIST criteria and TGR, respectively.52

Liver metastasis is considered the only significant predictor of HPD, while dual blockade strategies targeting the transforming growth factor-β and PD-L1 pathways have been associated with a higher incidence of HPD.52 A study comparing the efficacy and safety of anti-PD-1 ICIs combined with chemotherapy versus anti-PD-1 ICIs alone found that the incidence of HPD was as high as 39.0% in the monotherapy group, whereas it was only 3.8% in the combination therapy group. Key characteristics of HPD patients included elevated serum lactate dehydrogenase levels in 87.5% of cases, multiple metastatic sites in 93.8%, and liver metastasis in 56.3%.53 These findings suggest that combining chemotherapy with ICIs may help reduce the risk of HPD in RM-NPC patients and improve treatment outcomes. Therefore, patients should be informed of the risk of hyperprogression before initiating ICI therapy, and strategies for prevention, monitoring, and management should be provided. If HPD is confirmed during ICI treatment, an early switch to alternative anticancer therapies should be considered.

Consensus statement 8

It is advisable to integrate imaging assessments with the clinical characteristics to evaluate HPD. An immediate transition to alternative anticancer therapies should be initiated upon confirmation of HPD (degree of expert consensus: 100%).

Selection of effective predictive markers for immunotherapy efficacy

The level of plasma EBV DNA has garnered significant attention as a potential biomarker for predicting NPC patient response to ICIs. Patients with lower baseline plasma EBV DNA levels tend to exhibit higher ORR and longer PFS following ICI therapy.54 Furthermore, a reduction in EBV DNA levels during treatment has been associated with improved therapeutic responses, suggesting that plasma EBV DNA levels may reflect the tumor cells’ capacity to evade immune surveillance.

PD-L1 has been extensively studied as a predictive biomarker in immunotherapy; however, its association with clinical outcomes in NPC remains controversial. A meta-analysis has not identified a statistically significant difference in ORR between PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative patients, despite a trend suggesting higher ORR in those with elevated PD-L1 expression.54 This indicates that PD-L1 expression may have some predictive value in NPC, but further research is required to determine the optimal PD-L1 expression threshold and clarify its relationship with ICI efficacy.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) is often considered an indicator of the potential for neoantigen production and immunogenicity of tumors, theoretically correlating with immunotherapy response.55, 56, 57 However, current studies have not found a significant association between TMB and ICI efficacy in NPC.54,58 This may be influenced by variations in TMB cut-off values and the unique tumor microenvironment of NPC. Therefore, further research is needed to evaluate the role of TMB as a biomarker for predicting ICI responses in NPC patients.

In addition to the aforementioned biomarkers, the expression of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) has also been proposed as potential predictive markers. A high stromal density of MHC-II-positive cells has been associated with improved disease control rate, PFS, and OS.59 This indicates that HLA and MHC-related biomarkers may play a role in predicting NPC patient responses to ICIs. However, the significance of MHC-I/MHC-II gene expression in NPC patients receiving immunotherapy remains unclear. In solid tumors, the loss of HLA-1 expression is typically associated with poor prognosis and an immunosuppressive microenvironment.60 Although the clinical implications of reduction of expression of intratumoral MHC-I in patients undergoing anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy have not been thoroughly explored, approximately one-third of primary NPC tumors exhibit abnormalities in MHC class I genes, with inactivating mutations and rearrangements in HLA-A and HLA-B being the most common. Loss of HLA-A and HLA-B class I protein expression is associated with better PFS, whereas co-expression of both is correlated with poorer PFS. While the expression of HLA-A and HLA-B is not linked to ORR in NPC, the loss of HLA class I molecules may be associated with favorable outcomes in anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy.32 The clinical relevance of these biomarkers needs to be validated in larger cohorts, and non-T-cell-dependent mechanisms, such as the role of natural killer cells in NPC, should also be explored.

Consensus statement 9

Identifying effective predictive biomarkers is crucial for improving treatment efficacy, optimizing therapeutic strategies, and achieving precision medicine. Nevertheless, the clinical utility of plasma EBV DNA levels, TMB, and the expression of HLA and MHC as predictive markers still requires validation through large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies. These studies should adopt standardized testing methodologies and thresholds to reduce heterogeneity between research findings (degree of expert consensus: 100%).

EBV DNA as a comprehensive biomarker in NPC immunotherapy

Plasma EBV DNA has been established as a key biomarker, particularly noteworthy for its potential as a predictive tool for ICI therapy. The laboratory detection methods for EBV DNA primarily rely on the quantitative analysis of circulating EBV DNA in plasma, utilizing quantitative PCR technology to measure EBV DNA levels. This method exhibits high sensitivity and specificity and has been widely applied in the prognosis assessment, recurrence monitoring, and treatment response decision making for NPC. Multicenter clinical trials emphasize the need to pre-validate the consistency of detection across participating laboratories to avoid discrepancies in result interpretation due to regional differences.61 The use of identical PCR master mixes and calibration standards has been shown to significantly reduce inter-laboratory variability. The BamHI-W primer/probe set is recommended due to its highest sensitivity for EBV DNA and has been validated in large prognostic studies. Additionally, standardized plasma separation protocols are crucial for the standardization of EBV DNA detection. Such protocols include the collection of whole blood in EDTA-anticoagulated tubes, followed by immediate centrifugation to separate plasma, which is then frozen at −80°C and protected from repeated freeze-thaw cycles. For extraction, the Blood Mini Kit should be uniformly used, with a fixed starting volume of 400 μl of plasma and an elution volume of 50 μl. Each analytical batch must incorporate positive controls (e.g. Namalwa cell line DNA), negative controls, and no-template controls. Uniform calibration standards should be used, with periodic calibration against WHO reference materials. The master mix should be consistently used. These measures ensure the comparability of detection results. Current challenges include the lack of universally adopted standards globally, as differences in units used by various institutions (e.g. copies/ml versus IU/ml) and variations in detection platforms still affect the standardization of results.62

Determining the optimal cut-off value should consider the treatment phase, population characteristics, and clinical objectives, primarily through survival analysis and receiver operating characteristic curve optimization; however, the best thresholds may vary by region and treatment stage. Before treatment, a cut-off of 2300 copies/ml significantly distinguished OS and distant metastasis rates in LA-NPC patients.63 Studies in non-endemic areas support a threshold of 262.7 copies/ml as an independent prognostic indicator, which is considerably lower than reported values in endemic regions (1500-4000 copies/ml), likely due to differences in EBV strains, detection methods, and sample types.64 In studies monitoring EBV DNA dynamically after treatment, patients with undetectable levels (0 copies/ml) after induction chemotherapy exhibited significantly better PFS, while those with residual EBV DNA (>0 copies/ml) had poorer prognoses, particularly in N3 stage patients.62,65 Persistent positive EBV DNA (>500 copies/ml) after radiotherapy indicates a high risk of recurrence, necessitating intensified adjuvant treatment.66 Notably, a slow clearance rate of EBV DNA during treatment or transient positivity after treatment suggests treatment resistance, warranting lower cut-off values (e.g. ≤50 copies/ml) to identify early recurrence risks.67,68 In ICI therapy, high baseline EBV DNA levels (e.g. ≥10 000 IU/ml) correlate with poorer outcomes from monotherapy with PD-1 inhibitors, characterized by shorter median survival, significantly increased risk of disease progression, and reduced rates of durable response, indicating the need for more precise cut-off values to select populations that may benefit from ICIs.69

As a prognostic biomarker, EBV DNA load is closely associated with survival outcomes. Post-treatment positivity for EBV DNA is a strong independent prognostic factor, with negative patients showing significantly higher 5-year PFS rates.65,70 For patients whose EBV DNA fails to clear after induction chemotherapy, a 40% reduction in 5-year OS has been reported, implying a need for treatment intensification.71,72 EBV DNA possesses both predictive and dynamic monitoring value in ICI therapy. Patients with low baseline EBV DNA (<1000 copies/ml) experience significantly prolonged PFS and a 30% increase in ORR following ICI treatment.54 Conversely, high EBV DNA loads may indicate an immunosuppressive microenvironment, negatively correlating with PD-L1 expression and leading to ICI resistance.73 Patients exhibiting a >50% decline in EBV DNA within 4 weeks of ICI treatment have a significantly shorter median OS compared with those with a decline of ≤50%, and they also demonstrate lower rates of sustained clinical benefit. Furthermore, persistent positivity or strong rebound of EBV DNA strongly suggests disease progression.69

Consensus statement 10

The detection of EBV DNA is currently not standardized globally. It is recommended to uniformly utilize the BamHI-W primer/probe set to ensure sensitivity, standardize plasma separation protocols, and use consistent reagent kits. Each batch should include positive, negative, and no-template controls, and calibration should be carried out using WHO reference materials to minimize inter-laboratory variability. However, discrepancies in units and platforms used globally still need to be addressed. Cut-off values should be determined in conjunction with treatment phases and population characteristics. In endemic regions, a prognostic threshold of 1500-4000 copies/ml is suggested before treatment. Achieving zero EBV DNA after treatment is a critical endpoint, while residual EBV DNA or levels exceeding 500 copies/ml after radiotherapy indicate a high risk of recurrence. In ICI therapy, a baseline EBV DNA level of ≥10 000 IU/ml is associated with poor efficacy, necessitating more precise stratification. Post-treatment positivity is a strong adverse prognostic factor, while patients with low baseline levels (<1000 copies/ml) experience longer PFS and higher ORR. Persistent positivity or rebound of EBV DNA suggests disease progression. It is recommended to integrate biomarkers such as PD-L1 to comprehensively predict ICI efficacy and survival outcomes based on baseline levels, clearance rates, and endpoint status (degree of expert consensus: 100%).

Multidisciplinary management of ICI therapy

The multidisciplinary approach is crucial for the effective management of irAEs induced by ICIs, which can affect various organ systems including the lungs, intestines, liver, and eyes. Firstly, ICI-associated pneumonitis necessitates a comprehensive evaluation by clinicians, incorporating clinical symptoms, laboratory markers, endoscopic, and histological examinations to confirm diagnosis and devise appropriate management strategies.74 Additionally, for ICI-induced colitis, management guidelines have been proposed through a modified Delphi consensus method, encompassing hospitalization criteria, ICI management, and initial endoscopic evaluation, emphasizing the importance of an early multidisciplinary approach.75

The management of metabolic adverse events is also a critical aspect of ICI therapy. With the advancement of immunotherapy and targeted therapies, novel toxicity profiles have been revealed, necessitating the provision of comprehensive pharmacological and clinical evidence to manage these metabolic adverse events associated with new cancer treatments in a personalized manner.76 Establishing a multidisciplinary team focused on irAEs can significantly improve the management of severe hepatic toxicity induced by ICIs, leading to better patient outcomes through early detection and appropriate treatment. Additionally, the implementation of a multidisciplinary service for severe immunotherapy complications has been linked to enhanced patient outcomes and reduced health care utilization by safely integrating blood and tissue collection into routine care. Multidisciplinary tumor boards play a crucial role in providing timely expertise from various specialties, ensuring a cohesive approach to managing irAEs. Therefore, promoting interdisciplinary collaboration to enhance the management of ICI-related adverse events and improve patient care quality is essential in current oncology treatment.

Quality of Life and Patient Engagement

Patient QoL and engagement are central to the management of ICI therapy. Educating patients to ensure they fully understand the potential benefits and risks of treatment enables informed consent and promotes active participation in self-monitoring. Early identification and intervention for adverse events require patients to proactively report symptoms so that the medical team can respond promptly. Long-term monitoring and follow-up after treatment are crucial for maintaining QoL, as regular check-ups help detect and address potential issues early. For instance, comprehensive nutritional assessments and interventions should be implemented to prevent malnutrition, maintain body weight, and improve overall QoL in NPC patients who may experience long-term side-effects. This could include the use of oral nutritional supplements, enteral or parenteral nutrition support. Additionally, specific strategies such as immunonutrition formulas, L-arginine, and omega-3 fatty acids should be used to reduce inflammation in cases of mucositis and swallowing difficulties. Regular swallowing function exercises and evaluations, along with the timely discontinuation of unnecessary tube feeding, are also recommended.

Research has focused on the impact of ICIs on health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Notably, as patients age, the decline in HRQoL associated with ICI therapy appears to be more pronounced, with older patients showing greater reductions across multiple QoL dimensions.77 A systematic review further compared the actual effects of ICIs on HRQoL with those of other cancer therapies. While various tools have been employed to measure HRQoL, direct comparisons are challenging due to differences in reporting time points and data formats. Nevertheless, in most cases, the ICI treatment groups have demonstrated similar or even improved HRQoL compared with other therapies.78 Additionally, early tumor shrinkage has been identified as a biomarker predicting survival and patient-reported outcomes in ICI-treated patients, with early tumor shrinkage associated with significant improvements in OS and PFS after treatment.79 The diversity in trial designs and outcome measures suggests that current studies may not completely capture the impact of ICI-specific symptoms on HRQoL. This emphasizes the necessity for tools that are specifically designed to evaluate changes in QoL with ICI treatment.

Prospects

As the understanding of the biological characteristics of NPC deepens and ICIs demonstrate significant efficacy in treating NPC, immunotherapy is gradually transforming the standard treatment paradigm for this malignancy. Future research should focus on exploring the optimal combinations of ICIs with other therapeutic modalities through prospective clinical studies to improve response rates and treatment durability. Additionally, optimizing treatment sequencing and dosing will be crucial for enhancing efficacy while minimizing toxicity. Moreover, the development of reliable biomarkers is essential for the precise selection of NPC patients who are most likely to benefit from ICI therapy.

The unique adverse effects associated with ICI treatment necessitate enhanced monitoring, prevention, and management strategies, especially for vulnerable populations such as the elderly, patients with autoimmune diseases, and those with infectious conditions. Prospective studies should further refine and personalize toxicity management approaches, improve diagnostic and management standards, and promote multidisciplinary collaboration to ensure treatment safety. Given the high incidence of NPC in Asia and regional differences in its presentation, local studies should be strengthened to provide data supporting the development of region-specific clinical practice guidelines. For less developed areas, it will be important to explore cost-effective treatment strategies and drive health care policy reforms to broaden patient access to these therapies.

Advances in basic research are offering new directions for NPC immunotherapy, including the development of novel ICIs, NPC-specific vaccines, and the exploration of immune-cell therapies. The use of digital health technologies and telemedicine platforms to enhance patient management and follow-up is an important direction for improving patient QoL. While ICIs have achieved breakthrough progress in NPC treatment and may become a new standard of care, their long-term efficacy and safety still require further investigation.

Beyond the refinement of current therapeutic regimens, future immune-oncology trials in NPC should also address the limitations of ICI research to overcome extant challenges. Firstly, biomarker-driven precision design should be implemented. Dynamic biomarkers (e.g. serial EBV DNA, circulating tumor DNA, and specific immune-cell subset) ought to be integrated for patient stratification and real-time treatment adaptation. These biomarkers should be prespecified as secondary endpoints to facilitate iterative clinical decision making. Secondly, adaptive platform trials should be considered to evaluate multiple therapeutic strategies within a unified framework. For example, a high-risk LA-NPC platform could simultaneously assess induction regimens (ICI plus varying chemotherapy backbones), concurrent approaches (ICI with radiotherapy or with de-intensified chemoradiotherapy), and adjuvant strategies (ICI maintenance versus observation). Similarly, a second-line or later-line recurrent/metastatic platform could screen novel ICI combinations and resistance-overcoming strategies. Adaptive algorithms should permit mid-course modifications, such as enriching accrual to efficacious arms to enhance trial efficiency. Thirdly, trials dedicated to optimizing treatment sequencing and combination should be prioritized. Randomized phase II or compact phase III studies should be designed to directly compare alternative sequences or combinations. Exploration of next-generation ICI regimens—including bispecific antibodies (e.g. PD-1/CTLA-4 or PD-1/LAG-3), ICI plus T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and tyrosine-based inhibitory motif domains blockade, and ICI plus therapeutic cancer vaccines—is warranted. Fourthly, specific subpopulations and long-term outcomes require focused attention. Elderly patients, EBV-negative NPC, and individuals experiencing hyperprogression following ICIs are frequently underrepresented; dedicated trials evaluating ICI monotherapy or attenuated combination regimens in older or frail patients should be initiated. Protocols should mandate extended follow-up to capture OS beyond 5 years, delayed immune-related adverse events, and HRQoL. Finally, integration of real-world data with interventional trials is essential to contextualize efficacy and safety across broader populations. Hybrid prospective observational studies or the incorporation of historical registry cohorts as external controls should be used, and real-world investigations should inform the design of subsequent randomized studies. Collectively, these initiatives are anticipated to advance precision immuno-oncology while balancing efficacy, safety, and access across the heterogeneous NPC landscape.

Finally, it is noteworthy that this consensus is primarily based on the epidemiological characteristics, clinical practice data, and drug accessibility context in China, particularly in high-incidence areas. In non-endemic regions, the association with EBV is relatively lower, while the proportion of keratinizing squamous-cell carcinoma is comparatively higher. This discrepancy may lead to variations in sensitivity to ICIs, as EBV-positive tumors typically exhibit a higher TMB and enhanced immunogenicity.80 Furthermore, the value of EBV DNA as a core biomarker may diminish in non-endemic regions where the EBV positivity rate is lower. Additional research is required to validate its utility in these populations. Regarding drug accessibility, significant differences exist in drug approval status and availability across different regions. For instance, toripalimab has been approved for first-line and third-line treatment of NPC in endemic areas and has received orphan drug designation from the FDA, yet its accessibility remains limited in non-endemic regions.81 Currently, camrelizumab and tislelizumab are utilized in Asia.82 Moreover, variations in patient age, comorbidity profiles, and socioeconomic factors may also influence treatment adherence and follow-up. Despite these differences, the principles of multidisciplinary collaborative management, attention to irAEs, and biomarker-based patient selection emphasized in this consensus possess universal clinical value and should be applicable in a global context. Future efforts should focus on establishing localized diagnostic and therapeutic standards in non-endemic regions while exploring molecular subtype-guided precision therapy through cross-regional collaborations to improve outcomes for patients worldwide.

Consensus statement 11

To optimize the clinical application of ICIs, several key issues need to be addressed. The selection of appropriate patient populations for ICI therapy should be enhanced, and reliable biomarkers must be identified and validated to accurately predict treatment responses and adverse events. The optimization of ICI treatment regimens, including combination with other treatments, timing of therapy, and dose adjustments, requires further investigation. In particular, combination strategies involving ICIs with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery need to be supported by large-scale, multicenter clinical trials. Additionally, the necessity and optimal protocol for ICI rechallenge need to be clarified. During treatment, attention should be paid to patients’ nutritional status to improve overall QoL. Finally, the development of oral ICI medications to enhance patient compliance, reduce treatment costs, and benefit more NPC patients is also a future research direction (degree of expert consensus: 100%).
