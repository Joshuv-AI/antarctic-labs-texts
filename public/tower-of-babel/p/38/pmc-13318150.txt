Symptom severity assessment in patients with atrial fibrillation after catheter ablation using a smartphone application
Authors: Emma Sandgren, Konstanze Betz, Monika Gawalko, Astrid Hermans, Zarina Habibi, Dominique Verhaert, Suzanne Philippens, Bianca Vorstermans, Mandy Kessels, David Bovenkerk, Ben J M Hermans, Jeroen M Hendriks, Dennis den Uijl, Sevasti-Maria Chaldoupi, Justin Luermans, Theo Lankveld, Ulrich Schotten, Kevin Vernooy, Dominik Linz

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsSymptom severity in atrial fibrillation (AF) influences healthcare use, but conventional assessments lack temporal precision. We evaluated the feasibility of smartphone-based symptom severity assessment after AF catheter ablation and explored predictors and variability of symptom severity.Methods and resultsIn this analysis of the ISOLATION study, 140 patients used a photoplethysmography-supported smartphone application to record heart rhythm, symptoms, and symptom severity three times daily and when symptomatic for 7 days at 3, 6, and 12 months after ablation. Symptom severity was classified using the modified European Heart Rhythm Association scale. A total of 8575 recordings were analysed, of which 11% showed AF. Median adherence was 67%. Symptom severity was higher during AF, in women, and with higher heart rate. Nearly half of AF recordings were asymptomatic.ConclusionSmartphone-based symptom severity assessment after AF ablation is feasible and reveals substantial individual variability beyond rhythm status alone.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Photoplethysmography, Mobile health, Symptoms, Symptom severity, Catheter ablation

Introduction

In patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) increased symptom severity is associated with increased healthcare utilization.1 Therefore, symptom severity monitoring is crucial, but traditional symptom severity assessments rely on spot-assessments or retrospective recalls which lack the temporal precision to assess if symptoms correspond to AF.2

Advances in mobile health devices enable simultaneous assessment of symptom severity and rhythm status.3

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility of using a photoplethysmography (PPG)- based smartphone application to assess symptom severity in patients following AF catheter ablation. We also sought to identify clinical and demographic predictors of increased symptom severity and explore both between- and within-patient variability in symptom severity.

Methods

Study design

This analysis was part of the Institutional Review Board approved ISOLATION study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04342312; METC azM/UM, NL70787.068.19) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

As part of the follow-up, participants used a PPG-supported smartphone application to simultaneously record symptom, symptom severity and heart rhythm three times daily and additionally if symptomatic for seven days before the 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-ups. (Figure 1A).

Statistics

Categorical variables were presented as counts (n%) and continuous variables as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Associations were assessed using chi-square test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

A cumulative link mixed model was used to assess factors associated with symptom severity. The association between adherence and AF detection was assessed at the 7-day monitoring-period level using mixed-effects logistic regression with a random intercept for patient ID, adjusted for months since AF ablation. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Photoplethysmography recordings

In total, 8575 PPG recordings from 140 patients were analysed. These comprised AF (11%, n = 912), ventricular extrasystoles (31%, n = 2654), and sinus rhythm (58%, n = 5009).

Among symptomatic recordings (n = 1723), 27% were recorded during AF and 38% during sinus rhythm, with most rated as mild (54%) to moderate (40%) in severity.

Among all 140 patients, the median adherence was 67% (IQR 56–76%). The median number of PPG recordings per patient was 52 (IQR 36–61), and most patients completed three monitoring periods (median 3, IQR 2–3). (Figure 1B–D). Higher adherence was associated with AF detection: odds ratio 1.19 per 10 percentage-point increase, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.06–1.33, P = 0.003.

Factors associated with and predicting symptom severity

The symptom severity varied significantly between heart rhythms [χ2(16, n = 8575) = 823.7, P < 0.001], but not between symptom qualities. Importantly, almost half of all AF recordings were still asymptomatic (49%).

Between and within-patient variability in perceived symptom severity

In the population-level (fixed) effects analysis, with AF as the reference, ventricular extrasystoles and sinus rhythm were associated with lower symptom severity [odds ratio (OR) 0.25, 95% (CI) 0.15–0.58; OR 0.13, 95% CI 0.06–0.32], while female sex and higher heart rate were associated with higher symptom severity (OR 2.69, 95% CI 1.06–6.80; OR 1.05 95%CI 1.04–1.06). No significant effects were found for multimorbidity or age group. (Figure 1E).

The random-effect analysis showed substantial between- and within- patient variability, with 59% of the variability in symptom severity explained by differences in symptom reporting between patients, while 29% reflected within patient fluctuations on the measurement-level and 12% occurring within patients across monitoring periods. (Figure 1F).

Discussion

We present a unique smartphone app-based approach for assessing symptom severity in temporal relation to PPG-supported rhythm monitoring. Symptom severity assessment was feasible following AF catheter ablation. Atrial fibrillation recurrence, female sex and increased heart rate were associated with higher symptom severity. Although rhythm status showed the strongest population-level association, most variability in symptom severity reflected between-patient differences. Higher adherence to monitoring was also associated with greater AF detection.

Although validated AF-monitoring applications exist,4 few incorporate systematic symptom assessment tools. Combining self-reported symptoms with repeated rhythm measurements provides a fine-grained view of symptom-arrhythmia relationship, reduces recall bias, captures symptom fluctuations, and supports personalized decision-making. Nearly half of AF recordings were asymptomatic, underscoring limitations of symptom-based rhythm assessment after ablation. Adherence was 67%, lower than in the TeleCheck-AF project,3 likely due to longer follow-up and declining engagement over time.

Female sex and increased heart rate were associated with higher symptom severity, consistent with previous research,5,6 and physiologically plausible as women typically have higher resting heart rates,7 and increased ventricular rates reduce cardiac filling time and impair haemodynamic efficiency,8 which may intensify symptoms.

Symptom severity varied both between patients (59%) and within patients over time (41%),9 underscoring the need to address both medical and individual patient needs in AF care.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Only patients undergoing AF ablation were included, limiting generalizability to the broader AF population. In addition, participation required use of a mobile application, which may have introduced selection biases related to age, gender, and socioeconomic status. Furthermore, scheduled and symptom-triggered PPG recordings could not be distinguished because recording indication was not registered; thus, their respective numbers could not be determined.

Clinical implications

Atrial fibrillation burden is the preferred outcome measure after AF catheter ablation.10 However, symptom evaluation, particularly symptom severity, remains a key driver of clinical decision-making and therapeutic interventions. Conventional symptom assessment tools often capture both cardiac and non-cardiac symptoms. In contrast, mobile health technologies allow symptom assessment directly linked to PPG recordings. This temporal association may clarify whether symptoms reflect underlying AF. Clinically, our findings emphasize that symptoms should not be assumed to indicate AF recurrence, and that the marked between-patient variability supports individualized follow-up, with more intensive symptom–rhythm monitoring in selected patients.

Conclusion

Smartphone-based assessment of symptom severity is feasible after AF catheter ablation. Atrial fibrillation recurrence, female sex and increased heart rate were associated with higher symptom severity. Although rhythm status showed the strongest population-level association, most symptom severity variability was between patients underscoring the importance of patient-specific factors.
