Expert consensus-based review and refinement of dental radiographic quality management and radiation safety criteria in oral and maxillofacial radiography in Korea: A Delphi study
Authors: Hyun Jin Cho, Sam-Sun Lee, Ju-Hee Kang, Jo-Eun Kim, Kyung-Hoe Huh, Min-Suk Heo, Soon-Chul Choi, Han-Gyeol Yeom, Gyu-Dong Jo, Chena Lee, Hang-Moon Choi, Seo-Young An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to establish expert consensus on dental radiographic quality management and radiation safety evaluation items published by the Korean Academy of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology that lacked formal consensus-based validation, while improving terminological precision and ensuring consistency across evaluation criteria.Materials and MethodsCandidate items were systematically extracted from existing evaluation checklists and guidance documents without formally established expert consensus, covering intraoral and extraoral imaging, cone-beam computed tomography, and radiation safety management. A three-round Delphi survey was conducted with eleven specialists in oral and maxillofacial radiology. Items were rated on a nine-point scale, and consensus was defined using the following criteria: a mean score of 8.0 or higher, a coefficient of variation of 0.20 or lower, and an agreement rate of 80% or higher. Qualitative feedback was iteratively analyzed to refine item wording and terminology.ResultsThe initial 81 items were refined and expanded to 93 items in the second round and 95 items in the third. The proportion of items achieving consensus increased from 75.3% in the first round to 83.9% in the second and 94.7% in the third. The increasing agreement rates indicated progressive convergence of expert opinion across rounds.ConclusionThis study established expert consensus-based criteria for dental radiographic quality assessment and radiation safety management, providing a validated and structured foundation for improving assessment consistency, guiding future guideline development, and supporting standardization in dental radiographic practice.

Introduction

Dental radiography is essential for accurate diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up evaluation in the oral and maxillofacial region. With the transition to digital imaging and advances in high-resolution detector technology, the clinical use of panoramic, periapical, bitewing, lateral cephalometric radiography, and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has continued to increase. This expansion enhances diagnostic precision but also necessitates more rigorous radiation safety management and quality assurance, underscoring the need for clear and systematic standards. CBCT, in particular, enables three-dimensional anatomical reconstruction for precise diagnosis and surgical guidance. However, its effective dose can vary by nearly 20-fold (19–368 µSv) depending on equipment and exposure parameters.1 Dental radiographic imaging remains susceptible to patient positioning errors and image artifacts, which may compromise image quality and necessitate retakes.2345678 The diagnostic value of dental radiography can be maximized only when the international principles of radiation protection (justification, optimization, and dose management) are appropriately applied in clinical practice.91011

In Korea, the Korean Academy of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology (KAOMFR) has developed evaluation checklists and guidance documents for dental radiographic quality assessment and radiation safety management.12131415161718 These resources have supported clinical image interpretation and reduced unnecessary retakes. However, some items lack documented expert consensus, and inconsistencies in item structure, terminology, and descriptive clarity persist across documents. These gaps are particularly consequential because consistent and clearly defined evaluation criteria are essential for identifying image quality deficiencies and determining the need for corrective action or retakes. Positioning errors have been reported in up to 89% of panoramic radiographs,27 and motion or metallic artifacts are among the most common causes of image degradation.68 Nevertheless, tolerance limits and retake criteria remain inconsistently defined across existing evaluation materials. For CBCT, the need to adopt the As Low As Diagnostically Acceptable (ALADA) principle19 has been increasingly emphasized. However, its integration into clinical evaluation criteria remains limited. These limitations reduce the reliability of assessment tools, contribute to institutional variability in quality assurance, and may lead to unnecessary radiation exposure through repeated imaging or suboptimal exposure parameter selection.2021

Diagnostic reference levels (DRLs) are widely recognized as a practical tool for optimizing patient protection and image quality. Following the recommendations of ICRP Publication 135, Korea established national diagnostic reference levels (DRLs) for dental radiography, providing a reference for dose monitoring and supporting the optimization of patient radiation doses in clinical practice.2223 Nevertheless, the effectiveness of radiographic quality assurance depends on the use of clear, consistent, and clinically applicable evaluation criteria. Therefore, this study aimed to establish expert consensus on dental radiographic quality management and radiation safety criteria that lacked formal consensus-based validation, while improving terminological precision and ensuring consistency across these criteria.2425

Materials and Methods

Study materials

Survey items were developed based on dental radiographic evaluation checklists and radiation safety–related guidance documents currently used in Korea within the field of oral and maxillofacial radiology, particularly those published by the KAOMFR.121314,151617 The source documents were broadly categorized according to their clinical purpose into simplified clinical checklists, specialist-oriented technical guidelines, and institutional radiation safety and quality assurance standards.

Simplified clinical image quality checklists designed for routine use included evaluation forms for panoramic, periapical, bitewing, lateral cephalometric, and CBCT imaging. Specialist-oriented technical documents comprised expert-level panoramic evaluation forms and CBCT quality control guidelines. Institutional standards encompassed radiation safety management frameworks, quality assurance programs, and operational protocols relevant to dental radiographic practice.

Study design and procedure

A three-round Delphi survey was conducted between March 2024 and March 2025. An overview of the three-round Delphi process is presented in Figure 1. The initial Delphi questionnaire consisted of 81 items derived from the KAOMFR evaluation checklists and related guidance documents. Items were organized according to imaging modality, including panoramic, periapical, bitewing, lateral cephalometric, and CBCT imaging. For analytical consistency across rounds, items were subsequently classified into seven functional domains using standardized abbreviations: general image quality (I), panoramic radiography (P), periapical radiography (PA), bitewing radiography (BW), lateral cephalometric radiography (L), CBCT imaging (C), and extended functional domains (E) encompassing justification, optimization, occupational dose management, and CBCT quality control.

During the first Delphi round, items were presented without differentiation between expert-oriented and simplified versions to encourage broad conceptual evaluation and to minimize potential bias related to item provenance. Items extracted from the KAOMFR CBCT quality control guidelines were restricted to quantitative image quality criteria and reworded to ensure terminological consistency and clarity across imaging modalities. Prior to distribution, all items were screened to identify those lacking documented expert consensus, demonstrating inconsistencies in terminology or structural formulation, or containing ambiguous evaluative criteria. A Delphi approach integrating quantitative scoring with qualitative expert feedback was adopted to support systematic item refinement.

Each item was rated using a 9-point Likert scale, as recommended by the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method and widely adopted in Delphi-based consensus studies.2426 Optional free-text comments were collected to support qualitative refinement of the items. Consensus was evaluated using three predefined indicators: mean score (average, AVE), coefficient of variation (CV), and agreement rate (AR), defined as the proportion of experts rating each item as 7 or higher.252627 An item was considered to have achieved consensus only when all predefined thresholds were met (AVE ≥ 8.0, CV ≤ 0.2, and AR ≥ 80%), based on commonly used criteria in Delphi studies.252728 Quantitative analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA), and qualitative comments were analyzed through content analysis to identify recurring themes related to wording ambiguity, terminological inconsistency, unclear evaluative criteria, and limitations in clinical applicability. These findings informed decisions regarding item revision, deletion, or reclassification in subsequent rounds.

Questionnaires were distributed via email, and all responses were submitted independently without communication among panelists. An overview of the timeline, objectives, item numbers, and key activities for each Delphi round is presented in Table 1. In the first round, the original source of each item was concealed, which prompted expert feedback regarding potential duplication and ambiguity. From the second round onward, each item was assigned a unique identifier and labeled according to its source document. Because both expert-oriented and simplified evaluation forms are available only for panoramic radiography, the designation “expert version” was explicitly applied to relevant panoramic items to distinguish them from simplified versions intended for routine clinical use. For CBCT quality control items, the corresponding guideline document was provided as reference material to ensure accurate interpretation of the clinical context from which each item was derived. Following the first round, items were iteratively revised based on expert comments. Across the second and third rounds, items were added, deleted, or modified to enhance clinical relevance, clarity of expression, and practical applicability. Changes in item composition and functional domains across the three Delphi rounds are summarized in Table 2.

In the second round, additional items addressing justification, optimization, occupational dose management, portable intraoral radiography safety, and institutional radiation safety education and program management were introduced. These areas had not been comprehensively addressed in prior domestic Delphi validation studies18 and were not explicitly addressed as consensus-based items in the earlier evaluation materials. The inclusion of these items was based primarily on expert recommendations. Relevant international radiation protection documents, including European radiation protection guidance in dental radiology, the International Basic Safety Standards, IAEA safety reports on dental radiology, and NCRP Report No. 177, were consulted to ensure conceptual alignment.9102930 The newly added items were formulated to reflect expert consensus while considering the Korean clinical context, rather than directly adopting specific provisions from those documents. In the third round, three additional items were incorporated following strong recommendations from the expert panel: daily verification of image data storage, adjustment of the field of view according to patient anatomy, and periodic inspection of radiation-generating devices. These additions were intended to address gaps in CBCT operational safety and routine equipment maintenance within contemporary dental radiographic practice. Across the Delphi rounds, qualitative feedback from the expert panel informed both item-level refinement and the expansion of functional domains.

Expert panel

The expert panel comprised 11 specialists in oral and maxillofacial radiology from university dental hospitals across Korea. Participants were purposively selected to achieve balanced geographic representation and to reflect diverse clinical, academic, and administrative perspectives. Eligibility criteria included extensive experience in clinical practice and education, sustained involvement in radiographic quality control or radiation safety management, and documented contributions to guideline development or professional training. Detailed information on regional distribution, professional background, current positions, and areas of expertise is provided in Table 3. All panel members completed every Delphi round, resulting in a 100% response rate. Their continued participation throughout the study helped ensure consistency in the consensus process across all Delphi rounds.

Results

Response rate and overall consensus

All three Delphi rounds were completed by the same 11 experts, resulting in a 100% response rate throughout the study (n = 11). The proportion of items meeting the predefined consensus criteria increased across rounds: 75.3% (61/81) in Round 1, 83.9% (78/93) in Round 2, and 94.7% (90/95) in Round 3 (Tables 2 and 4). In the final round, the majority of items demonstrated a coefficient of variation within the 0.0–0.1 range, indicating a high level of agreement. Items that did not meet consensus criteria were either excluded or revised for reassessment in subsequent rounds. In addition, some items that met the consensus criteria were further refined based on expert feedback to improve clarity and terminological consistency.

Domain-specific categorization

All items were classified into seven domains: general image quality (I), panoramic radiography (P), periapical radiography (PA), bitewing radiography (BW), lateral cephalometric radiography (L), CBCT imaging (C), and extended functional domains (E). The extended functional domain was introduced in Round 2 and further expanded in Round 3 to include items related to justification, optimization, occupational dose management, and radiation safety operations. By the final round, this domain accounted for the largest proportion of newly incorporated items compared with the other categories.

Changes in item composition

The questionnaire underwent iterative refinement across the three Delphi rounds. Between Rounds 1 and 2, 14 items were added, 44 were revised (36 sentence-level modifications and 8 phrase-level additions), and 2 were deleted. Between Rounds 2 and 3, 3 additional items were added and 1 was deleted. Most revisions made in Round 3 involved wording refinement or structural clarification rather than substantive content changes. These patterns reflected progressive refinement of item clarity and clinical applicability through active expert engagement.

Qualitative refinement of items

Qualitative feedback contributed to improvements in clarity, internal consistency, and clinical applicability. In Round 1, comments primarily addressed ambiguous wording, inconsistent terminology, and insufficiently defined evaluation criteria. Items describing general concepts such as “image sharpness” and related qualitative descriptors were revised to include more explicit reference points, including anatomical landmarks and boundary delineation. Terminological inconsistencies were corrected, overlapping concepts were consolidated, and items with limited diagnostic relevance or feasibility in routine clinical practice were removed.

In Round 2, revisions focused on improving the specificity, practical applicability, and scope of the items. New items were introduced to address justification and optimization in clinical decision-making, the provision of clinical information when requesting radiographic examinations, and occupational dose management, including worker protection, education, and personal dose monitoring. In addition, practical operational and institutional safety measures, including portable intraoral radiography practices, shielding requirements, equipment inspection, and quality assurance program intervals, were incorporated. Terminology related to image artifacts and evaluation criteria was further standardized to improve consistency across items. These refinements also included clarification of anatomical evaluation criteria and more explicit boundary definitions for ambiguous descriptors.

By Round 3, most items had achieved consensus, and revisions to existing items were limited to minor wording adjustments and clarification of operational criteria. In addition, three additional items were introduced following expert recommendations, including CBCT-related operational practices as well as a general requirement for periodic inspection of radiation-generating devices. Further refinements included clarification of acceptable clinical exceptions and provision of illustrative examples for anatomical assessment criteria. Representative examples of item refinement throughout the Delphi process are summarized in Table 5.

A total of 90 items achieved consensus in the third Delphi round and constituted the final validated item set (Table 6). For panoramic radiography, items were retained according to their original source classification, as separate expert-level and simplified evaluation tools were available and maintained as distinct checklists.

Discussion

This study addressed the lack of verified expert consensus regarding items in existing dental radiographic evaluation checklists and radiation safety–related guidance documents issued by the KAOMFR. While existing evaluation materials have been widely used in clinical practice, this study provides the first consensus-based validation and refinement of these items through a structured Delphi method conducted over three iterative rounds, consistent with commonly adopted Delphi designs.242528 The iterative process facilitated refinement of item wording and the establishment of expert consensus, thereby contributing to the alignment of clinical practice with standardized evaluation criteria. This process ensured that the criteria for dental radiographic image quality and radiation safety management are not only conceptually sound but also practically applicable across diverse clinical environments.

Across the three Delphi rounds, the iterative refinement process led to the expansion and modification of items through additions, deletions, and revisions, reflecting active expert engagement. All panelists participated in every round, resulting in a 100% response rate and ensuring continuity throughout the consensus process.2427 The consistently high level of agreement and convergence of expert judgments across successive rounds indicated that the selected items were both clinically relevant and conceptually aligned among specialists.2728

Qualitative feedback played a critical role in improving item clarity and clinical utility.31 Content-analysis procedures enabled systematic categorization of narrative comments, identification of ambiguity, and targeted revision of problematic items.32 For example, vague or subjective expressions were reformulated into more objective descriptions by incorporating anatomical reference points and clearer evaluative criteria. Terminological inconsistencies were resolved through standardization, thereby enhancing consistency in image interpretation and reducing potential variability in assessment. In addition, certain criteria were explicitly limited to normal anatomical conditions to prevent misinterpretation in the presence of pathological variation.

Several items were removed because experts identified limited clinical relevance or feasibility in contemporary imaging practice. For instance, “image bending,” which mainly reflects film-based intraoral image distortion, was considered less applicable in contemporary digital radiographic systems. Similarly, certain CBCT-related assessment items did not achieve consensus due to the lack of clearly defined or quantifiable evaluation standards. These findings underscore the importance of clinical feasibility and interpretability in the development of practical evaluation tools.

Beginning in Round 2, additional items addressing justification, optimization, and occupational dose management were incorporated, reflecting domains that had not been sufficiently represented in prior KAOMFR evaluation materials. These additions were informed by established international radiation protection principles,930 while being adapted to the Korean clinical context. Furthermore, practical operational and institutional safety considerations, including portable intraoral radiography practices, shielding requirements, equipment inspection, and quality assurance program intervals, were incorporated to enhance real-world applicability. These changes indicate a shift from purely image-based evaluation toward a more comprehensive framework integrating both image quality and radiation safety management.

In Round 3, three additional items were incorporated based on expert recommendations, including CBCT-related operational practices as well as a general requirement for periodic inspection of radiation-generating devices. The inclusion of these items reflects the recognized importance of operational safety and quality control in contemporary dental radiographic practice. The rapid convergence of expert judgments on these newly introduced items further supports their clinical relevance and necessity for inclusion in standardized evaluation criteria.

Although all 95 items underwent iterative refinement throughout the Delphi process, 90 items ultimately achieved consensus in the final round. The remaining items did not meet the predefined consensus criteria, primarily due to variability in clinical feasibility or interpretive differences among experts. Such variability is expected in Delphi studies and may reflect variations in clinical environments, equipment availability, or patient case characteristics.28 These findings suggest the need for future development of more flexible or stratified evaluation criteria, as well as continued efforts to improve standardization and training in radiographic interpretation.

This study has several limitations. The consensus-based items established here have not yet been validated in diverse clinical settings, and the present findings do not constitute finalized guidelines. Future research should focus on clinical implementation, integration with diagnostic reference levels, and the development of practical scoring or weighting systems.
