Eighteen‐Month Real‐World Experience Using Mavacamten for Treatment of Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy in a Racially Diverse Population
Authors: Diego Ramonfaur, Alessio Gasperetti, Victoria E Blake, Bryana Rivers, Ali A Kassamali, Edward K Kasper, Lili A Barouch, Katherine C Wu, Jose A Madrazo, Richard T Carrick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy have increased symptomatic burden. Mavacamten was recently approved for treatment of obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy based on 2 randomized controlled trials. However, its use under real‐world conditions and in diverse populations is under‐studied.Methods and ResultsThis was a prospective observational cohort study of patients seen at the Johns Hopkins HCM center and prescribed mavacamten for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy between July 7, 2022 and January 6, 2024. Patients were followed longitudinally, with serial echocardiography and clinical evaluation as mandated by the risk evaluation and mitigation strategy program. Sixty‐six patients received mavacamten (mean age 59 years, 47% male, 29% non‐White [Black, Hispanic/Latino, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander], 47% obese). Before treatment, all patients had New York Heart Association class II (51.5%) or III (48.5%) heart failure symptoms. Initial maximum peak left ventricular outflow tract gradient was 107±46 mm Hg. Median treatment duration was 9 months. For patients on mavacamten after ≥6 months (n=43), symptoms improved by ≥1 New York Heart Association class in 72% of patients, and peak left ventricular outflow tract gradient decreased by 80±46 mm Hg, eliminating hemodynamically significant left ventricular outflow tract obstruction in 79.1% of patients. Mavacamten was temporarily discontinued in 3 patients due to left ventricular ejection fraction decrease <50%. There were no medication‐related adverse events. Effectiveness and safety were similar between White and non‐White patients, but symptomatic relief was attenuated in patients with body‐mass index ≥35 kg/m2.ConclusionsMavacamten was effective and safe when used under real‐world conditions in a racially diverse population of symptomatic patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Patients with comorbid obesity were less likely to experience symptomatic improvement while on mavacamten.Keywords: echocardiography, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, LVOT obstruction, mavacamten, obesitySubject Categories: Hypertrophy, Cardiomyopathy, Quality and Outcomes, Echocardiography, Genetics

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited heart disease with a prevalence of between 1 in 500 (0.2%) and 1 in 200 0.5%) within the general population. While disease manifestations may be heterogeneous, HCM is often characterized by hyperdynamic left ventricular (LV) systolic function and varying degrees of ventricular hypertrophy. 1 , 2 In approximately two thirds of patients, obstruction of systolic blood flow through the LV outflow tract (LVOT) 3 can lead to symptoms of exertional dyspnea or angina, as well as an increased risk of syncope. Clinical practice guidelines recommend a pharmacologic approach relying on either β‐blockers or nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers as initial treatment for symptomatic LVOT obstruction. 4 , 5 When significant obstruction persists despite these first‐line agents, invasive approaches such as surgical myectomy or alcohol septal ablation may help reduce obstruction‐related symptom burden through direct anatomic reduction of the interventricular septum. 4 , 5

More recently, cardiac myosin modulators that directly reduce sarcomeric hypercontractility have emerged as novel pharmacologic treatments of symptomatic patients with obstructive HCM. The first agent in this class, mavacamten, was recently approved for use in both the United States and Europe based on promising results from 2 randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trials: EXPLORER‐HCM (Clinical Study to Evaluate Mavacamten in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy) 6 and VALOR‐HCM (A Study to Evaluate Mavacamten in Adults with Symptomatic Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Who Are Eligible for Septal Reduction Therapy). 7 However, outcomes in EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM were assessed in cohorts of mostly White, nonobese patients with HCM, and little data currently exist regarding the effectiveness of mavacamten or feasibility of its use outside of the rigorous conditions of a randomized‐control trial (RCT).

Towards this purpose, we present our real‐world experience using mavacamten for treatment of obstructive HCM in a diverse cohort during the 18‐month period following establishment of a cardiac myosin modulator subspecialty clinic at our HCM center. In this study, we focus on patient characteristics and the longitudinal impact of mavacamten on both reduction of symptom burden and alteration of imaging‐based hemodynamic measurements and assess treatment effectiveness stratified by race and obesity.

Methods

This study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by local ethics and/or institutional review boards under a waiver of consent. Patients were enrolled in the study through a waiver of consent for review of clinical records. To maintain patient confidentiality, data and study materials have not been made publicly available, but a limited data set may be shared individually upon reasonable request to the corresponding author. Reporting of this observational study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist. 8

Study Population

Patients included in this prospective cohort study were part of the Johns Hopkins HCM Research Registry, an ongoing, observational study of patients evaluated and managed for HCM at the Johns Hopkins HCM Center. Patients were included in the current study if they (1) had a definite diagnosis of HCM, defined as the presence of maximal LV wall thickness ≥1.5 cm that was not otherwise explained by an alternative pathogenesis capable of producing a similar degree of hypertrophy, or maximal LV wall thickness ≥1.3 cm in the presence of a positive family history of HCM, (2) were prescribed mavacamten for treatment of obstructive HCM by the Johns Hopkins Mavacamten Clinic between July 7, 2022 and January 6, 2024 (the first 18 months of its operation), and (3) were ≥18 years of age. Patients were considered eligible for mavacamten prescription as per guideline recommendations 9 based on the presence of persistent HCM‐related heart failure symptoms and either (1) maximal LVOT gradient ≥50 mm Hg on most recent echocardiography despite baseline therapy or (2) intolerance of current HCM‐specific therapy and prior demonstration of LVOT gradient ≥50 mm Hg on echocardiography. There were no additional exclusion criteria for this study. All patients prescribed mavacamten at Johns Hopkins were enrolled; patient characteristics and outcomes were acquired from review of medical records.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was improvement in patient‐reported burden of heart failure symptoms as defined by New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, with class I representing no symptoms, class II representing exertional symptoms with mild functional limitation, class III representing exertional symptoms with significant functional limitation, and class IV representing symptoms at rest and severe functional limitation. Secondary outcomes included changes in LVOT gradient, either at rest or provoked via Valsalva or exercise stress echocardiography, and changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Safety outcomes included drop in LVEF <50%, cardiac and all‐cause hospitalization, and death.

Treatment Protocol

Patients with HCM referred to the Johns Hopkins Mavacamten Clinic were screened at an initial baseline evaluation by a clinical HCM cardiologist for mavacamten treatment candidacy. Those patients demonstrating (1) NYHA class II/III heart failure symptoms suspected to be secondary to obstructive HCM, (2) maximal LVOT gradient of ≥50 mm Hg despite maximally tolerated pharmacologic treatment with β‐blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, or disopyramide, and (3) LVEF of ≥55% were deemed eligible for treatment. Patients subsequently receive an initial mavacamten prescription and were enrolled in the Camzyos (Bristol‐Myer Squibb) Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy program, with dispensation of mavacamten pending insurance approval.

Once approved, patients were initiated on mavacamten at a dose of 5 mg/day with close monitoring via regular echocardiography and clinical evaluation as per manufacturer recommendations. Combined therapy with both β‐blocker and calcium channel blocker was transitioned to monotherapy with either β‐blocker or calcium channel blocker and treatment with disopyramide was discontinued before mavacamten initiation. Ongoing management was divided into initiation and maintenance phases (Figure S1). During the initiation phase, all patients underwent repeat clinical evaluation and echocardiography at 4, 8, and 12 weeks of treatment, and mavacamten dose was decreased to 2.5 mg/daily for LVOT gradients <20 mm Hg. At 12 weeks, patients entered into the maintenance phase during which they underwent repeat assessments at least every 3 months. If LVOT gradients remained ≥30 mm Hg on repeat evaluation, mavacamten dose was increased in a step‐wise pattern (2.5, 5, 10 and 15 mg/day) after which they underwent repeat assessments at 4 weeks following the dose adjustment. If at any point during treatment LVEF dropped below 50%, treatment was interrupted for 4 weeks with re‐initiation of mavacamten at the next lower dose pending LVEF recovery to ≥50%. Mavacamten was discontinued permanently in any patient demonstrating LVEF <50% on 2 separate occasions while receiving 2.5 mg/day. In patients with baseline LVOT obstruction only present after exercise‐based provocation, repeat stress echocardiography was performed to confirm resolution of exertional gradients if heart failure symptoms were refractory to Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy algorithm‐based mavacamten dosing.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as proportions for categorical variables and as mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables, as appropriate. Between‐group differences in baseline characteristics were made using Fisher exact test for categorical variables, Student t test for continuous, normally distributed variables, and Mann–Whitney U test for continuous, non‐normally distributed variables. Changes in primary and secondary outcomes in the interval between baseline screening evaluation and most recent evaluation were assessed for all patients receiving at least 6 months of mavacamten therapy using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired, nonparametric continuous data and McNemar's test for differences in dichotomous data. Subgroup analyses based on self‐reported patient race and body‐mass index (BMI) were also performed. In both subgroups, odds of nonobstructive LVOT gradient and NYHA class I symptoms in response to at least 6 months of mavacamten treatment were assessed using multivariable logistic regression with adjustment for patient age and sex. Missingness is reported in Table S1; a complete‐case analytic approach was used for all analyses. All analyses were performed in Python version 3.9.13 using the open‐source Pandas (v2.0.2), SciPy (v1.11.0), and Statsmodels (v0.14.0) statistical code libraries.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among 84 patients who were prescribed mavacamten during the 18 months of operation of the Johns Hopkins Mavacamten Clinic, 66 patients were ultimately dispensed the drug (Figure 1) and comprised our study population. Baseline characteristics are presented in the Table. For patients receiving mavacamten, mean age was 58.8±15 years, 31 (47%) were male, and 47 (71%) were White people. All patients were symptomatic, with either NYHA class II (n=34, 52%) or class III (n=32, 49%) symptoms despite routine baseline therapy with β‐blockers (n=59, 89%) and nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (n=19, 28%). All patients had prior assessment with standard echocardiography (66/66, 100%) or treadmill stress echocardiography (40/66, 61%). The majority of patients demonstrated obstructive LVOT gradients (≥50 mm Hg; n=61, 92%) either at rest (56±43 mm Hg; 45 [IQR: 28–81] mm Hg) or after provocation (107±44 mm Hg; 104 [IQR: 78–139] mm Hg) on the most recent assessment before mavacamten initiation, including 18 (27%) for whom obstruction was only present on exercise provocation (Figure S2). Five patients with history of LVOT obstruction (9%) had nonobstructive gradients on most recent echocardiography before mavacamten initiation, but unacceptable side effects associated with other pharmacologic treatments of obstruction (Data S1). All patients had initial LVEF ≥50%, with mean LVEF of 67±6%. Prior septal reduction therapy, including either alcohol septal ablation (n=4) or surgical myectomy (n=5), had been performed in 9 patients (14%). Table S2 shows baseline characteristics of those patients who were prescribed but did not receive mavacamten dispensation (n=18). Compared with those who did receive mavacamten, these patients were more likely to have been referred due to intolerance to current therapy (n=5 [27.8%] versus n=5 [7.6%], P value=0.03), but were otherwise similar. Reasons for patients not receiving mavacamten after its prescription included patient decision to defer treatment after detailed discussions about the mavacamten prescription process and potential side effects or loss of follow‐up (n=6), insurance coverage denial (n=5), and prohibitive out‐of‐pocket costs despite insurance coverage approval (n=2). Insurance coverage approval was pending at the end of the study window in 2 patients, and drug dispensation after coverage approval was pending in 3 patients.

Study Outcomes

Patients received mavacamten for a median duration of 8.9 [IQR: 4.6–14.8] months. Following start of treatment with mavacamten, patients underwent a median of 6 [IQR: 3–8] repeat clinical evaluations at a rate of 7.3 [IQR: 6.5–8.4] evaluations per year, and a median of 5.5 [IQR: 3.0–7.8] repeat echocardiograms at a rate of 7.2 [IQR: 6.3–8.4] echocardiograms per year.

For those patients still on mavacamten after at least 6 months (n=43), per‐patient changes in symptom burden and imaging‐based hemodynamic measurements are presented in Figure 3. For patients receiving mavacamten after at least 6 months of treatment (n=43), the most recent dose of mavacamten was 2.5 mg/day in 21% (n=9), 5 mg/day in 42% (n=18), 10 mg/day in 35% (n=15), and 15 mg/day in 2% (n=1) (Figure S4, Table S3).

Of the 18 patients who required exercise echocardiographic stress testing to elicit obstructive gradients, 10 patients received at least 6 months of mavacamten treatment (Figure S2). These patients tended to be on a lower dosage of mavacamten at the end of the study period, with 44% requiring 2.5 mg/day, and 33% requiring 5 mg/day. These patients experienced symptomatic relief at rates similar to the overall HCM population receiving mavacamten, with 70% having improvement by at least 1 NYHA class, and 10% having improvement by 2 NYHA classes.

Safety Outcomes

Three patients temporarily discontinued mavacamten due to decreases in LVEF <50%. All 3 patients demonstrated recovery of LVEF to ≥50% at 4 weeks and were subsequently resumed on mavacamten without incident. One patient transiently discontinued mavacamten due to symptoms of dizziness. One patient was hospitalized for noncardiac asthma exacerbation. One patient was hospitalized for elective coronary artery bypass grafting with concurrent surgical septal myectomy. There were no unplanned cardiac hospitalizations or patient deaths. One patient experienced new‐onset atrial fibrillation. Of the 10 patients with diagnosis of atrial fibrillation before initiation of mavacamten, 2 had recurrent, self‐terminating episodes of short duration, and 1 remained in persistent atrial fibrillation. Further details regarding safety outcomes are included in Data S1.

Effect of Patient Race on Treatment Outcomes

Our cohort included 19 (29%) non‐White patients (Black: n=10, 15%, Hispanic/Latino: n=4, 6%, Asian: n=3, 5%, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander: n=2, 3%). Compared with White patients receiving mavacamten (n=47, 71%), non‐White patients tended to be younger (51 versus 62 years old, P value=0.03) and were more likely to be female (79% versus 43%, P value=0.007), but otherwise had similar HCM disease phenotype (Table). Effectiveness of mavacamten was similar between White (n=28) and non‐White (n=15) patients receiving mavacamten treatment for at least 6 months (Figure S5). The majority of both White and non‐White patients had improvements in heart failure symptoms by at least 1 class (71% and 73%, respectively; P value=0.99) and reductions in maximum LVOT gradient (decreases of −75±44 and −83±48 mm Hg, respectively; P value=0.89). Compared with White patients for whom treatment with 2.5 mg/day was common (9 of 28, 31%), no non‐White patients were taking 2.5 mg/day after 6 months of treatment (0 of 15, 0%) (P value=0.020). There were no racial differences in hemodynamic and symptomatic response to at least 6 months of mavacamten after multivariable logistic regression (Figure 5).

Effect of Obesity on Treatment Outcomes

Our cohort had a high prevalence of obesity, with 31 (47%) patients qualifying as obese based on BMI ≥30 kg/m2. This included 14 (21%) patients with class I obesity (BMI 30–35 kg/m2), 12 (23%) patients with class II obesity (BMI 35–40 kg/m2), and 5 (8%) patients with class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2). For those receiving at least 6 months of mavacamten treatment, patients with class II or III obesity (n=12) were significantly less likely than nonobese patients (n=22) to have improvement in heart failure symptoms by at least 1 NYHA class (42% versus 86%, P value=0.015) despite no statistically significant difference in alleviation of LVOT gradients <50 mm Hg (67% versus 82%, P value=0.41) (Figure S6). Reductions in maximum LVOT gradient (decreases of −88±48 and −57±37 mm Hg, respectively; P value=0.061) were not statistically different, though there was a trend towards less improvement in patients with class II or III obesity. There was no association between obesity and hemodynamic response, but patients with class II or III obesity were less likely to report NYHA class I symptoms after at least 6 months of treatment (odds ratio: 0.14 [95% CI, 0.02–0.95]; P value=0.045. Figure 5).

Discussion

In this study we present early assessment of mavacamten use under real‐world conditions in a diverse population of patients with HCM during the first 18 months of a dedicated mavacamten clinic. Similar to prior RCT data, we found that patients with HCM receiving mavacamten for treatment of symptomatic LVOT obstruction had improvements in both burden of heart failure symptoms and severity of obstruction, and experienced minimal side effects. While effectiveness was similar for White and non‐White patients, patients with class II or III obesity had an attenuated symptomatic response to mavacamten treatment. While 3 patients required transient discontinuation of mavacamten due to reduction in LVEF <50%, mavacamten was generally safe and there were no major adverse events attributable to its use.

Real‐World Mavacamten Patient Profiles, Effectiveness, and Safety

While RCTs are the criterion standard by which the efficacy of novel medications should be assessed, they are frequently performed within highly selected patient populations and under rigorous supervision by trialists. For example, while the EXPLORER‐HCM 6 and VALOR‐HCM 7 trials clearly demonstrated the impact of mavacamten for reduction of HCM‐related symptoms and LVOT obstruction, patients included in these studies were racially homogeneous, had relatively low rates of obesity, and included few patients with prior septal reduction therapy. Assessing external validity of the results of these trials under more practical, real‐world conditions is thus an essential step in understanding the clinical use of this novel therapy. 10

In many ways, the population of real‐world patients with HCM receiving mavacamten in our study was similar to those represented by the EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM trials. All 3 cohorts included patients with average age of ≈60 years, comprised around ≈50% male and female patients, had high rates of background therapy with β‐blockers, and had comparable magnitudes of pretreatment LVOT gradients. Likewise, in comparison to the EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM trials, in our real‐world experience, we found a similar impact of mavacamten on patient‐reported burden of heart failure symptoms, with ≈60% to 70% of patients improving by at least 1 NYHA class. Significant reductions in LVOT gradient were also confirmed in our present study, with the average reduction in LVOT gradient of ≈70 mm Hg actually exceeding those seen in the 2 RCTs (in which LVOT gradient decreased by ≈45 mm Hg). We also found mavacamten to be safe and well tolerated, with only 1 patient requiring temporary discontinuation of drug due to a plausible side effect of dizziness, and 1 patient having a likely unrelated hospitalization for asthma exacerbation. Three patients did have transient decreases in LVEF requiring treatment interruption; however, all 3 patients were asymptomatic at the time, and all 3 had LVEF recovery on their 4‐week interval assessment, allowing for resumption of mavacamten at a reduced dose. The incidence of treatment interruption was therefore similar to findings from the 2 RCTs at ≈5%.

The cohort of patients assessed in our current study was racially diverse, with ≈30% non‐White patients, of whom ≈half were Black (n=10). This is in contrast to the EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM trials, in which only 7% and 14% of patients receiving mavacamten were non‐White, respectively, and Black patients were particularly under‐represented (n=1 and n=3, respectively). In a subanalysis of patients receiving at least 6 months of treatment (Figure S4), we found that the effectiveness was similar for both White patients and non‐White patients. Both groups demonstrated significant decreases in LVOT pressure gradients and around three quarters of patients from both groups had an improvement in symptom burden of at least 1 NYHA class. Our findings suggest that the effectiveness of mavacamten is maintained in more racially diverse cohorts. In agreement with this, the EXPLORER‐CN 11 was recently published showing similar impact of mavacamten on improvement in LVOT obstruction and heart failure symptoms in patients of Chinese descent. Interestingly, no non‐White patients were receiving the lowest mavacamten dosage (2.5 mg/day) after 6 months, compared with >30% of White patients. This is congruent with the EXPLORER‐CN trial where, despite a reduced starting dosage of 2.5 mg daily, 90% of patients were receiving 5 or 10 mg mavacamten daily at the completion of the study. These observations may be partly related to racial differences in cytochrome P450 oxidase (CYP450)‐mediated metabolism. 12 Future study investigating the impact of race on the pharmacokinetics of mavacamten may be warranted.

Obesity, particularly class II and III obesity, had high prevalence within our study cohort, with nearly half of patients qualifying as obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m2), and a quarter of patients having class II or III obesity (BMI ≥35 kg/m2). In a subanalysis of patients on mavacamten treatment for at least 6 months, we found that patients with BMI ≥35 kg/m2 were much less likely to have symptomatic improvement (≈42%) than nonobese patients (≈86%). This is congruent with BMI focused subanalyses from both the EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM trials where patients with BMI ≥30 kg/m2 were substantially less likely than those with BMI <30 kg/m2 to achieve the respective study primary end points. Interestingly, we found that this attenuated effect on symptom relief in morbidly obese patients occurred despite similar alleviation of obstructive gradients. This suggests that, rather than being secondary to inadequate mavacamten dosing, persistent symptoms in obese patients may be related to competing causes of exertional dyspnea and chest pain in this population (eg, diastolic dysfunction and/or heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, coronary artery disease, and noncardiac causes including obesity itself). Thus, relief of LVOT obstruction by mavacamten removes only 1 barrier to improved quality of life. Patients with obstructive HCM with competing causes of heart failure symptoms or significant limitations to their functional status secondary to other comorbidities may expect a reduced impact of mavacamten treatment on their overall burden of symptoms. Further investigation is also required to explore differences in mavacamten response among the subgroup of patients for whom LVOT obstruction requires provocation. 13

Thus, despite a number of important differences in the composition of patients receiving mavacamten, our study supports the external validity of both EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM when applied to real‐world, symptomatic patients with HCM.

Barriers to Treatment With Mavacamten

In our experience, a proactive, multidisciplinary team comprising physicians, pharmacists, nurses, and other patient‐support providers was essential in enabling patients to navigate the complex process of insurance prior authorization, secondary insurance appeals, patient financial assistance programs, and scheduling. However, despite these efforts, 15% of the 84 patients prescribed mavacamten during the initial 18 months of our dedicated mavacamten clinic were ultimately not dispensed this medication. There were 2 major barriers to its clinical use under real‐world conditions. First, the financial cost of mavacamten is significant, and has been estimated at up to $90 000 per year. 14 , 15 Without adequate insurance coverage, this expense is prohibitive for most patients. In our study, mavacamten was cost‐prohibitive for 7 patients, including 2 for whom this cost was prohibitive despite insurance coverage approval. Second, in order to receive mavacamten, patients must commit to frequent monitoring via both repeated clinical visits and repeated echocardiography for the duration of their treatment. In our study, nearly all patients required at least 1 dose adjustment during the first 6 months of therapy that prompted additional clinical and echocardiographic evaluations, and the average number of repeat clinical evaluations and echocardiograms was ≈7 per year. This heavy burden of monitoring was undoubtedly a factor for the 6 patients in our cohort who ultimately decided not to pursue treatment with mavacamten despite receiving insurance approval. Future efforts to reduce the frequency of treatment monitoring required to safely prescribe mavacamten may help alleviate some of this burden.

Future Directions

While the role of mavacamten within the broader context of alternative treatment modalities of symptomatic LVOT obstruction (eg, disopyramide and septal reduction therapy) continues to evolve, both the 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines 9 and recently released 2024 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines 16 have recommended mavacamten as second‐line pharmacologic therapy to be considered either before, or as an alternative to, septal reduction therapy. Nonetheless, real‐world data regarding the effectiveness and safety of this medication, particularly in populations poorly represented by prior RCTs, is essential. While this study represents the largest analysis of real‐world patients receiving mavacamten published to date, much larger cohorts of diverse patients are needed to adequately power further study of the effects of this drug in specific subpopulations. Fortunately, the DISCOVER‐HCM registry (NCT05489705) 17 is actively enrolling patients and is expected to include up to 1500 patients from throughout the United States. Additionally, interim results from long‐term extension of patients from the EXPLORER‐HCM trial were recently reported as part of the MAVA‐LTE study. 18 , 19 This analysis, which included control patients who crossed over to treatment with mavacamten, suggests sustained improvements in LVOT obstruction as well as heart failure symptoms through 48‐week follow‐up. Future studies are needed to explore the relative effectiveness, safety, and feasibility of long‐term mavacamten treatment compared with alternative treatment modalities for symptomatic LVOT obstruction.

Limitations

Patient and imaging data were clinically acquired. Serial echocardiography was performed as part of normal clinical workflow, and while these studies followed a prescribed HCM protocol with detailed assessment of LVOT gradients with and without provocation, the findings were not centrally interpreted; there may therefore be inter‐reader variability and higher longitudinal variance in LVEF and LVOT gradients than might otherwise be expected. NYHA functional class is also a subjective and imprecise measure of a patient's burden of heart failure symptoms. Given the lack of blinding in this observational, clinical study, we cannot exclude the possibility that some of the observed improvements in patient quality of life may be confounded by treatment bias in favor of mavacamten. Nonetheless, the proportion of patients reporting improvement by at least 1 NYHA class was similar to that observed in both the EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM trials. These subjective changes were also paralleled by decreases in more objective assessments such as LVOT gradients, suggesting biologic plausibility for these symptomatic improvements. Finally, this was a single‐center study at a dedicated HCM center and few patients were on mavacamten dosages >10 mg/day at the end of the study period. Our results may therefore not be representative of mavacamten use in other settings.

Conclusions

Real‐world patients with HCM receiving mavacamten for treatment of symptomatic LVOT obstruction demonstrated improvements in both symptom burden and severity of LVOT obstruction, while experiencing minimal side effects and no major adverse events during the study period. Effectiveness was similar for White and non‐White patients, but alleviation of heart failure symptoms was attenuated in patients with class II or III obesity. High cost and frequent, required monitoring may limit access to this medication for some patients with HCM who otherwise qualify for its use. Further study of the long‐term effectiveness and safety of mavacamten in even larger cohorts of real‐world patients is needed, particularly among those who are under‐represented.
