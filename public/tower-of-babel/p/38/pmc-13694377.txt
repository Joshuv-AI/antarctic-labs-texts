Cost-Effectiveness and Budget Impact of Lung Cancer Screening: A Systematic Review
Authors: Nuo Chen, Siyuan Wang, Jing Bai, Fanny Franchini, Kaung Mon Winn, Jingyi Hu, Jian Wang, Gang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionClinical trials have demonstrated the benefits of early detection of lung cancer (LC); however, the implementation of national LC screening programs remains under consideration. Economic evaluations provide evidence-based insights to support informed policy decisions.ObjectivesThis systematic review aims to comprehensively synthesize the health economic evidence on LC screening and examine the methodological challenges.MethodsWe searched six databases from inception to July 31, 2025. Articles were included if evaluating LC screening using a full economic evaluation and/or budget impact analysis (BIA). The Criteria for Health Economic Quality Evaluation (CHEQUE) tool was used for quality assessment of full economic evaluations. The International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) guidelines were modified as a quality checklist for BIA studies. Narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize the included studies.ResultsNinety-five articles were identified, representing 93 full economic evaluations and nine BIAs. Seventy-nine full economic evaluations supported the cost-effectiveness of LC screening, while five indicated it was not cost-effective. Seventy studies evaluated low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening compared with no screening. Twelve studies considered emerging technologies either as standalone screening modalities or as adjuncts to LDCT screening, including artificial intelligence, genomics, and biomarkers. All BIAs assessed an LDCT-based screening program. Eight studies found that it would increase the budget, while one reported the opposite. All included studies demonstrated substantial methodological variation, including differences in the definition of eligible populations, modeling approaches, assumed uptake rates, cost components, sources of utility values, and equity consideration.ConclusionOur review suggests that LDCT screening is generally cost-effective in high-risk populations, although implementing LC screening programs may entail a substantial budgetary impact. Emerging technologies, novel therapies, and combined screening have the potential to improve screening efficiency. Future economic evaluations are needed to assess these advances, while addressing existing methodological challenges to improve alignment between evidence. This review provides up-to-date economic evidence on LC screening and offers policymakers a clearer understanding of its financial implications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40273-026-01639-8.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Lung cancer screening is generally cost-effective in high-risk populations but may have substantial budget impact.Emerging technologies, novel therapies, and combined screening strategies may have the potential to improve screening efficiency.Substantial variations in methodology were identified, particularly in eligible populations, modeling approaches, uptake rates, costs, health utility inputs, and equity considerations.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) is the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the primary cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Compelling clinical evidence has supported that early LC detection through low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) significantly reduces mortality in high-risk populations [2, 3], however, national or territory-wide programs have only been implemented in a few regions, such as the UK, the Czech Republic, Poland, Croatia, Australia, China, Taiwan (China), South Korea, Israel, and the US [4]. Despite the demonstrated benefits, uptake of LC screening remains low compared with other cancer screening programs. Key implementation challenges include diagnostic uncertainty, program complexity, participant adherence, and equity considerations [5, 6]. The gap between clinical evidence and implementation highlights the critical need for robust economic evaluation of LC screening programs. Cost-effectiveness and budget impact analyses provide policymakers with critical evidence on whether screening represents good value for money and is financially sustainable, particularly given competing healthcare priorities and resource constraints.

LC screening is recommended for high-risk populations; however, current LDCT eligibility criteria rely largely on simplified risk factors, primarily smoking history, and do not adequately capture risk among nonsmokers. Notably, LC in never-smokers has become the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [7]. Hence, personalized risk-stratified approaches have been advocated for LC screening; however, these approaches introduce greater complexity, which may negatively affect the trade-offs between harms and benefits [8]. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), genomics, and biomarkers, have shown promise in improving the efficiency of LC screening [9]. AI may enhance multiple aspects across LC screening, including radiation dose reduction, nodule detection and characterization, and LDCT scan interpretation, particularly in regions with limited radiologist availability. Genomics and biomarkers may optimize eligibility selection, guide management of uncertain nodules, and personalize screening schedules. Several trials support the effectiveness of these technologies in addressing the unmet needs of LDCT-based LC screening, such as the BioMILD trial [10] and the 4-IN-THE-LUNG-RUN trial [11]. While these emerging technologies may represent promising alternatives or complements to LDCT, further economic evaluation is warranted to establish their cost-effectiveness.

Previous reviews have synthesized the economic evaluations of LC screening; however, their scope was restricted to specific interventions or outcomes [12–20]. Most reviews primarily examined the cost-effectiveness of LDCT screening in high-risk populations. Jahn et al. [19] assessed methods used in budget impact analyses (BIAs) of cancer screening but included only one study on LC screening. Fan et al. [18] expanded LDCT screening to include all screening modalities for LC screening, but the review concentrated on cost-effectiveness outcomes, without synthesizing budget impact evidence or explicitly addressing the methodological challenges in economic evaluations. While some overlap exists with the current review, none has comprehensively synthesized both full economic evaluations and BIAs. Given the growing interest in implementing national LC screening programs globally, a comprehensive assessment of economic evaluation evidence is essential to inform policy and resource-allocation decisions.

This systematic review aims to: (1) identify, critically appraise, and synthesize the economic evaluation evidence on LC screening; and (2) examine the methodological challenges in the economic evaluation of modeling LC screening. The findings are intended to support evidence-based decisions regarding the design and implementation of LC screening programs.

Methods

This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251069660) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21] (Supplemental Material 1).

Search Strategy

Six databases were searched for English-language articles: Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, EconLit and Scopus. The search strategies combined MeSH and free-text terms related to lung cancer, screening, and economic evaluation. Full search strategies are provided in Supplemental Material 1. The primary search was conducted on March 31, 2025, and updated on July 31, 2025.

Study Selection

Studies were included if evaluating LC screening. The evaluation methods of interest included full economic evaluations (i.e., cost-minimization analysis (CMA), cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), cost-utility analysis (CUA), cost-benefit analysis (CBA), and cost-consequence analysis (CCA)) and budget impact analysis (BIA). Exclusion criteria included studies involving populations with existing cancers, as well as reviews, abstracts, editorials, commentaries, letters, and publications not written in English.

Two reviewers (NC and JB) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by the full-text review of potentially eligible studies. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus, with any remaining disagreements decided by a third reviewer (SW). Covidence was used to manage the screening process.

Data Extraction

The data extraction form included: (1) general characteristics (author, published year, and country/region); (2) study characteristics (perspective, analysis type, intervention, comparator, eligible population, time horizon, screening frequency, uptake and adherence, test performance, cost components, risk prediction model, modeling used, software, lung cancer stages modeled, overdiagnosis, radiation exposure, model validation, equity considerations, and limitations); and (3) economic evaluation results (incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and/or incremental budget impact, sensitivity analysis, willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold) for each study. Additional characteristics, including costs, technologies evaluated, and their functions, were extracted from studies assessing emerging screening technologies.

A narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize the included studies. Emerging screening technologies were defined as any biomedical or digital innovations applied to LC screening modalities [22]. Equity considerations were defined as the explicit evaluation of the equity impacts of LC screening within economic evaluations using analytical approaches, including equity weighting, distributional cost-effectiveness analysis (DCEA), extended cost-effectiveness analysis (ECEA), multicriteria decision analysis, and mathematical programming [23]. Cost-effectiveness conclusions were based on ICERs and jurisdiction-specific WTP thresholds. For studies that did not explicitly report a WTP threshold, or multiple scenarios were evaluated, conclusions were based on the interpretations reported in the original studies.

Quality Assessment

For full economic evaluations, quality was assessed using the Criteria for Health Economic Quality Evaluation (CHEQUE) tool [24]. Compared with existing quality assessment tools, CHEQUE offers a more comprehensive approach that explicitly distinguishes between methodological and reporting quality. Study-level quality assessments based on the CHEQUE checklist are presented in Supplemental Material 1. CHEQUE provides two scoring methods: Method 1 assigns full credit to the attributes rated as “Not Applicable”, whereas Method 2 excludes such attributes from the final calculation. Method 2 was used in this study, as it is more appropriate when a substantial number of items are likely to be rated as “Not Applicable”. We visualized the assessment following the method developed by Dijk et al. [25] using R version 4.5.1, which was similar to the Risk of Bias visualizations generated by the robvis package. For BIA studies, we adopted the checklist developed by Jahn et al. [19], which was based on the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) guidelines [26, 27]. Two reviewers (NC and SW) independently assessed study quality.

Results

A total of 14,106 records were initially retrieved, of which 7,821 remained after the removal of duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 230 articles underwent full-text review. Finally, 95 eligible articles met the inclusion criteria, comprising 93 full economic evaluations [12, 28–118] and nine BIAs [35, 45, 56, 76, 108, 114, 119–121] (Figure 1). Reasons for full-text article exclusion are listed in Supplementary Material 1, and detailed characteristics and extracted data from the included studies are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Among the 93 full economic evaluation studies, two studies were reported in multiple publications: one by Snowsill et al. [12] and Griffin et al. [122], and the other by Field et al. [48] and Field et al. [123]. The most detailed publication from each study was retained for data extraction [12, 48], with duplicate reports excluded [122, 123]. One of the remaining 92 articles contained two independent models (a full model and a preliminary model) [58], which were treated as separate studies, resulting in a total of 93 full economic evaluation studies.

Study Region and Design

Thirty-three (35.5%) were conducted in Europe [12, 28–58], 28 (30.1%) in North America [59–86], 22 (23.7%) in Asia [87–108], six (6.5%) in Oceania [109–114], two (2.2%) in South America [115, 116], and two studies (2.2%) were conducted across multiple countries [117, 118]. The included studies comprised 53 CUAs, 18 CEAs [43, 46, 47, 52, 56, 58, 63, 68, 74–76, 78, 79, 81, 83, 87, 88, 101], and one CBA [42]. Twenty-one studies included both CEAs and CUAs [28, 32, 33, 35, 39, 41, 45, 48, 50, 58, 65, 66, 72, 82, 91, 106, 109, 111, 112, 116, 118].

Screening Modalities

LDCT was the most frequently evaluated screening modality, with 76 studies assessing LDCT screening as a standalone intervention. A small number of studies evaluated alternative imaging modalities, including chest X-ray (n = 3) [53, 72, 108] and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (n = 1) [63]. Twelve studies evaluated emerging screening technologies, including AI-based methods (n = 5) [30, 58, 84, 88], biomarker-based approaches (n = 5) [29, 54, 61, 96, 115], and polygenic risk score (PRS)-based strategies (n = 2) [91, 104]. These emerging technologies were evaluated either as standalone screening modalities [115] or as adjuncts to LDCT screening [29, 30, 54, 58, 61, 84, 88, 91, 96, 104]. No screening was the most common comparator, adopted by 70 studies (75.3%), followed by LDCT screening.

Eligible Population

Eligible populations varied across studies. Most studies (n = 76) included smokers as the eligible population, and 15 studies evaluated other high-risk populations [48, 51, 52, 55, 58, 64, 71, 81–83, 90, 92, 104, 107]. A small number of studies targeted the general population (n = 3) [36, 87, 88] or nonsmokers (n = 1) [118]. Fourteen studies (15.1%) applied risk prediction models to select target populations [12, 30, 35, 44, 48, 60, 66, 67, 89, 91, 104, 107, 114, 117]. Across these studies, nine different models were applied, including seven established models (PLCOm2009 [67], PLCOm2012 [35, 44, 60, 114, 117], PRS [91, 104], LLPv2 [12, 48], China NCC-LCm2021 [107], LCDRAT [60], and LungFlag [30]) and two models developed by individual research groups [66, 89].

Time Horizon

Nearly half of the studies (n = 45; 48.4%) used a lifetime horizon. Eleven studies (11.8%) did not explicitly report a time horizon [38, 52, 55, 56, 58, 61, 64, 79, 99, 101, 107]; however, a lifetime horizon could be inferred for seven of these studies based on the incorporation of life expectancy into the modeling [38, 55, 56, 61, 79, 101, 107].

Screening Frequency

Different screening frequency were used. Twenty-six studies evaluated once-time screening strategies [12, 29, 38, 40, 44, 48, 52, 55, 58, 64, 76, 77, 79, 83, 84, 88–90, 92, 95, 96, 102, 104, 115, 116]. Among the 74 studies that evaluated the repeated screening strategies with fixed intervals, annual screening was most frequently used (n = 55). Four studies assessed repeated strategies with variable intervals [39, 40, 86, 109].

Uptake and Adherence

Fifty-seven studies explicitly reported screening uptake rates, which ranged from 17% to 100%, with 31 assumed full uptake [29, 35, 38, 40, 43, 46, 51, 52, 55, 56, 59, 62, 66, 68, 70, 73–75, 81, 82, 86, 87, 91, 92, 95, 96, 98, 101, 105, 107, 112]. Particularly, one study evaluated the impact of uptake by assuming a 100% LC screening uptake for the intervention and 12.5% for the comparator [59]. Two studies differentiated uptake by ethnicity [113] or LC risk [36]. Among the 75 studies evaluated repeated screening, 45 studies clearly reported adherence rates ranging from 54% to 100%, with 36 assuming perfect adherence [12, 35, 37, 40, 43, 46, 47, 50, 51, 56, 60–62, 65, 66, 68, 73, 74, 77, 80–82, 85–87, 89, 91, 92, 95, 96, 98, 101, 103, 105, 107, 118].

Perspective and Cost Components

A healthcare perspective was used in most studies (n = 78; 83.9%), with cost components generally aligned with the stated perspective. Almost all studies (n = 92) included direct medical costs. Among the 33 studies that incorporated direct non-medical costs, most included screening recruitment costs (n = 31), covering expenses related to eligible population identification, invitations, reminders, consultations, and advertisements. Screening equipment costs were in only seven studies [29, 30, 42, 91, 95, 96, 110]. Among studies incorporating indirect costs, productivity losses were the most frequently included component (n = 12) [33, 59, 72, 77, 80, 88, 91, 95, 97, 104, 106, 116], followed by transportation expenditures (n = 9) [33, 72, 80, 88, 91, 95, 97, 104, 106].

Model Approaches

The most common used modeling approaches were microsimulation models (n = 23; 24.7%) [35, 40, 43, 46, 47, 54, 56, 57, 60–62, 65, 68–71, 77, 85–87, 93, 104, 114] and Markov models (n = 23; 24.7%) [37, 39, 45, 51, 63, 80, 89–92, 94–98, 105, 107, 108, 110, 113, 116–118], followed by combined decision tree and Markov models (n = 17; 18.3%) [28, 30–34, 36, 49, 50, 59, 67, 84, 99, 103, 106, 112, 115], and decision trees alone (n = 8; 8.6%) [29, 41, 58, 64, 79, 82, 83, 102]. Other approaches included life table analysis (n = 3) [73–75], discrete event simulation (n = 1) [12], combined decision tree and microsimulation model (n = 1) [58], and other modeling approaches such as regression or stage-shift models (n = 5) [38, 66, 78, 101, 109]. Eleven studies did not clearly report their modeling approach [42, 44, 48, 52, 53, 55, 72, 76, 81, 100, 111], while one study was conducted without any modeling [88].

Most model structures were based on the natural history of LC, incorporating screening and follow-up interventions pathways. Nine established natural history models were identified across the included studies: MISCAN-Lung [35, 46, 54, 60, 62, 65, 68, 86, 114], LCPM [60, 62, 65, 77], LCOS [60–62, 65, 85], The University of Michigan Model [60, 62, 65], and BCCRI-LungCan (US) [86]; SiMRiSC (Netherlands) [43, 56, 87]; Oncosim (also known as CMRR, Canada) [69–71, 86]; NCC-Lung (China) [104]; and ENaBL (UK) [12].

Lung Cancer Stage Modeled

Most studies (n = 84; 90.3%) incorporated LC stage at diagnosis in their models. Of the 49 studies (52.7%) that did not specify histological subtypes, stages I-IV were most frequently modeled disease states (n = 16) [28, 32–34, 44, 48, 50, 57, 69, 89, 90, 93, 94, 104, 106, 109]. Among the 35 studies (37.6%) that reported the histological subtypes, 24 modeled both non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small-cell lung cancer [31, 35, 37, 38, 46, 47, 52–54, 58, 60–63, 65, 70, 72, 77, 80, 85, 86, 100, 111, 114], while 11 focused solely on NSCLC [30, 45, 73, 78, 79, 99, 102, 112, 115, 116, 118].

Cost-Effectiveness Results

Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were the most commonly reported outcome (n = 52), followed by life-years (LYs) (n = 15) [42, 43, 46, 47, 52, 56, 63, 68, 74, 75, 79, 81, 83, 87, 101]. Twenty studies reported both QALYs and LYs [28, 32, 33, 35, 39, 41, 45, 48, 50, 65, 66, 72, 82, 91, 106, 109, 111, 112, 116, 118]. Other outcome measures included deaths averted [47, 76], correct identification of actionable nodules [58], cancer correctly detected and treated [58], 5-year survivors [78], health-adjusted life-years for life lived with disabilities [110], quality-adjusted life expectancy (QALE) [100], loss-of-QALE [100], and detection of LC cases [88]. Cost-effectiveness results were synthesized according to screening modality and eligible population (Supplementary Material 1).

Results Stratified by Modality

For LDCT, the majority of the 81 studies (n = 71) concluded that it was cost-effective in the relevant jurisdiction(s). Six reported mixed findings, with cost-effectiveness varying by the target population (stratified by sex [87], ethnicity [113], or LC risk [36, 71]), country [118], or WTP threshold [12]. Only four studies concluded that LDCT screening was not cost-effective, all published in or before 2018 [77, 80, 111, 112]. It should be noted that, subsequent studies from the corresponding countries (Australia and the US) have consistently supported the cost-effectiveness of LDCT [59, 60, 62, 65, 85, 86, 109, 114].

As for other imaging-based modalities, three studies evaluated chest X-ray screening. One found chest X-ray screening to be cost-effective compared with no screening [53], and another found it was less cost-effective than LDCT screening [108]. The remaining study found it was more costly than no screening without providing additional health benefits [72]. One study reported that Lung MRI was more cost-effective than LDCT screening [63].

Cost-effectiveness results of studies focusing on emerging technologies are presented in Table 3. AI-related technologies to support LDCT screening were evaluated in five studies [30, 58, 84, 88]. One study evaluated a machine learning-based risk prediction model (LungFlag) for screening eligibility and found it to be cost-effective [30]. Three studies evaluated AI-assisted diagnostic tools, and all reported them to be cost-effective [58, 88]. Two studies found AI-assisted tools represented a dominant strategy compared with unaided radiologist reading [58], while another reported that AI as a sole reader for diagnosis remained cost-effective until the cost of AI exceeded USD 1,240 [84]. Overall, AI-related strategies were generally found to be cost-effective or even cost-saving, largely through improvements in diagnostic accuracy and reductions in workload.

Polygenic risk scores were used in two studies to stratify risk and identify the high-risk population [91, 104]. One study reported that combining PRS with LDCT was cost-effective compared with LDCT alone or no screening [104]. The other found it was not cost-effective [91]. Although both studies focused on the Chinese population and used the same source of PRS performance data [125], their conclusions differed, mainly due to the differences in the target populations. The former study applied PRS to identify high-risk individuals from the general population, while the latter employed PRS to further stratify risk among heavy smokers.

Biomarkers were evaluated in five studies, including autoantibodies [29, 115], proteins [54], microRNAs [96], and hypothesis-based biomarkers [61]. One study used biomarkers as a standalone screening strategy, due to the lack of LDCT evidence in its specific context and found it was not cost-effective [115]. The remaining four studies evaluated biomarkers as adjuncts to LDCT screening [29, 54, 61, 96]. Among these, one used biomarker to select eligible populations before LDCT [29], two used biomarkers to further assess patients with lung nodules detected by LDCT [61, 96], and one evaluated both applications [54]. Three studies concluded that biomarker-based approaches were cost-effective and improved the cost-effectiveness of LDCT screening [29, 61, 96], whereas one found the strategy to be weakly dominated by LDCT alone [54]. This difference was mainly driven by biomarker costs, with the latter study applying a substantially higher biomarker cost than the others.

Results Stratified by Eligibility

As for the eligible populations, screening among heavy smokers was the most frequently evaluated strategy and was generally found to be cost effective. Screening was also found to be cost-effective for other high-risk groups, including individuals with a family history of LC [90], asbestos exposure [51], or suspected coronary artery disease [64]. Two studies found that using risk prediction models to select high-risk individuals was cost-effective compared with no screening [44, 48], while one reported that the cost-effectiveness depended on the WTP threshold [12]. Compared with conventional risk factor-based eligibility criteria, six studies reported improved cost-effectiveness using risk model-based strategies [30, 35, 60, 89, 107, 117], whereas five studies found either no clear distinction [66, 114], or no improvement [67, 91, 104]. The cost-effectiveness of risk model-based screening strategies was highly sensitive to risk threshold selected for determining screening eligibility.

Evidence for screening in the general population was mixed. One study reported that screening was cost-effective in rural China [88], whereas another reported it was not cost-effective in Hungry [36]. A third study found screening to be cost-effective in males but not in females in China [87]. Among nonsmokers, one study found that screening was cost-saving in Japan but not cost-effective in the US [118]. Across these studies, underlying LC incidence was a key determinant of cost-effectiveness.

Sensitivity Analysis Results

Sensitivity analyses identified several key drivers of the cost-effectiveness of LC screening. The most frequently influential parameters were screening cost [28, 33, 35, 39, 47, 52, 68, 87, 100, 101, 104, 107], health utility values [30, 36, 49, 50, 60, 62, 73, 85, 106, 107, 111], discount rates [32, 34, 46, 85, 92, 94, 105], test specificity or sensitivity [84, 89, 91, 96, 112, 113], LC incidence [43, 45, 90, 95, 110, 118], treatment cost [54, 75, 116], survival [12, 82, 104], mortality [67, 72, 109], LC prevalence [41, 115], LC stage distribution [31, 97], overdiagnosis [99, 102], uptake or adherence [70, 114], time for reading CT [58], screening frequency [51], risk prediction model threshold [117], LC growth [56], probability of LC diagnosed [53], recall rate [103], rate of opportunistic detection for early-stage LC in no screening [29], malignancy rate of missed pulmonary nodules [64], and effectiveness of positive LDCT screening results [108].

Combined Screening and Novel Therapies

Two studies from the same research team evaluated LC screening in combination with screening for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in the Netherlands [38, 57]. The initial study suggested that combined LDCT screening for LC, COPD, and CVD was likely more cost-effective than LC alone under the optimistic assumption of perfect test performance [38]. However, the subsequent study using a more comprehensive and realistic modeling framework found that only combined screening for LC and CVD remained cost-effective, whereas the addition of COPD screening did not [57]. This difference could be attributed to the limited benefits of early detection of COPD.

Two studies assessed the impact of integrating novel therapies (including targeted therapies and immunotherapies) into treatment pathways on both costs and survival outcomes associated with LC screening [56, 114]. One study found that LC screening became more cost-effective when novel therapies were available for advanced-stage LC compared with conventional treatment options [114]. Another study reported that LC screening remained cost-effective even when immunotherapy was extended to all patients rather than restricted to those with advanced-stage LC [56]. By shifting diagnosis toward earlier disease stages, LC screening was expected to reduce the use of more costly treatment for advanced disease, thereby improving overall cost-effectiveness.

Equity Considerations

According to the definition of equity considerations, only one US study explicitly incorporated equity into the economic evaluation using a DCEA approach to evaluate the impact of increasing screening uptake [59]. Three additional studies examined equity impacts through subgroup analyses but did not apply equity-specific methods [110, 113, 117].

Collectively, these four studies assessed strategies to increase screening uptake [59], implement LC screening [110, 113], or use the PLCOm2012 risk-based model to determine screening eligibility [117]. All reported reductions in health disparities across sex, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic groups. Cost-effectiveness was favorable in three studies [59, 110, 117], whereas the remaining study reported that cost-effectiveness varied by ethnicity, with LC screening being cost-effective for Māori but not for non-Māori, due to the substantially higher LC incidence among Māori [113].

Quality Assessment

The quality assessment results for the full economic evaluations are presented in Supplemental Material 1. Of the 93 studies, 55 (59.1%) achieved scores of at least 80% for methodological quality, and 60 (64.5%) achieved scores of at least 80% for reporting quality. Among studies scoring 90% or above, 17 studies (18.3%) achieved excellent reporting quality, whereas no study attained a methodological quality score above 90%.

Most studies scored highly across the “Decision Problem and Scope”, “Intervention and Comparator(s)”, “Perspective”, and “Population” domains. The remaining domains contained methodological or reporting attributes that were not or rarely adequately addressed. Specifically, the method-specific attributes included model validation in the “Modeling” domain, the use of the best available evidence for parameter estimation, data quality assessment in the “Data Inputs and Evidence Synthesis” domain, incorporation of health preferences reflecting relevant jurisdictions in the “Utilities” domain, probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) in the “Analysis” domain, and equity considerations in the “Equity Considerations” domain.

Similarly, the reporting-specific attributes that were less frequently addressed included reporting of validation processes in the “Modeling” domain, comprehensive reporting of identified consequences in the “Consequences” domain, separate reporting of costs and resource quantities in the “Costs and Resource Use” domain, ethical implications in the “Equity Considerations” domain, and discussion of potential bias in the “Transparency and Reporting” domain.

Budget Impact Analysis

Study Characteristics

Of the nine BIA studies, two studies (22.2%) conducted standalone BIAs [119, 120], one (11.1%) analyzed health and budget impact [121], and the remaining six (66.7%) were undertaken alongside CEAs [35, 45, 56, 76, 108, 114]. These studies were conducted across nine countries, with one study each from Germany, the Netherlands, Spain, Switzerland, Argentina, India, Australia, Canada, and the US. Eight studies evaluated the introduction of LDCT-based LC screening programs compared with no screening. The remaining study evaluated the introduction of LDCT as a replacement for the existing screening approach, comparing it with both chest X-ray screening and no screening [119].

All BIA studies targeted high-risk populations. Seven studies focused on heavy smokers, one study used risk prediction models to select the eligible population [35], and one study evaluated both approaches [114]. Time horizons ranged from 1 to 15 years, including 1 year [76], 5 -years [56, 108, 119], 10 -years [114, 121], and 15 -years [35, 45, 120]. One study analyzed a single screening [76], and the remaining analyzed repeated screening, most commonly annual [45, 108, 114, 119–121], or biennial screening [35, 56, 114, 120].

Seven studies reported initial LC screening participation rates, ranging from 30% to 100% [35, 56, 76, 114, 119–121]. Two studies assumed full participation [35, 56], while two assumed that participation rates increased progressively across screening rounds [120, 121]. Among the eight studies that evaluated repeated screening, six reported participation rates for re-screening adherence [35, 45, 56, 114, 120, 121].

All studies adopted a healthcare perspective and included the cost of screening, diagnosis, and treatment. Three studies additionally incorporated program management costs [35, 114, 120]. Treatment costs were most commonly modeled by LC stage (n = 7) [45, 56, 76, 108, 114, 119, 120], while one study adopted a phases-of-care approach [35], and another did not clearly report its costing framework [121]. Five studies took into account the costs of novel therapies into treatment pathways [35, 56, 114, 119, 120].

Among the nine BIAs, four (44.4%) used microsimulation models [35, 56, 114, 121], three (33.3%) employed a simple cost calculator [76, 119, 120], and two (22.2%) used the Markov model [45, 108]. Four studies incorporated histological subtypes when modeling LC stage-specific outcomes [35, 45, 114, 119]. Additional details are presented in Supplemental Material 1.

BIA Results

Most studies reported budget impact in monetary terms, while one expressing results as a proportion of the total healthcare budget [108]. Eight studies (88.9%) reported that introducing LDCT screening would increase the health system’s budget [35, 45, 76, 108, 114, 119–121]. In contrast, one study from the Netherlands reported that screening could yield substantial cost savings by reducing the costs associated with treating advanced-stage LC [56].

Three studies conducted one-way sensitivity analyses [76, 119, 120]. The most influential drivers of incremental budget impact were first-round participation [120], diagnostic costs [119], and smoking prevalence [76].

One study from Argentina applied a budget impact threshold as a decision rule and found that the introduction of LDCT screening exceeded the threshold for a high budget impact [119]. Consequently, cost-containment actions were recommended, such as negotiating lower CT scan prices.

Quality Assessment

Quality assessment outcomes of BIA studies are shown in Supplemental Material 1. Overall, three studies met at least 10 of the 12 checklist items [76, 119, 120]. Common quality issues included not reporting the estimated target population (n = 4) [35, 45, 108, 114], only three studies conducting uncertainty analysis [76, 119, 120], and only two studies including uncertainty analysis for different uptake rates [76, 120].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized the current health economic evidence on LC screening, identifying 93 full economic evaluations and nine budget impact analyses. Evidence from both types of analyses is important when considering the implementation of LC screening programs, as they address complementary policy questions. Full economic evaluations assess the long-term costs and health outcomes of screening, providing evidence on whether screening represents good value for money. In contrast, BIAs evaluate the short- to medium-term financial implications of screening program implementation, informing decisions about affordability and budgetary feasibility.

Global Evidence from Health Economic Evaluations

Existing studies have predominantly focused on cost-effectiveness, while evidence on budget impact remains limited. The included studies were published between 2001 and 2025, with over 60% appearing since 2020. The rapid growth of literature in recent years likely reflects the emergence of stronger clinical evidence, particularly following the publication of the NELSON trial results in 2020, which accelerated evaluations of national LC screening programs [126]. The geographical distribution of evidence was uneven. All identified studies were conducted in high- or middle- income countries, with about 80% originating from high-income countries. This pattern is consistent with the global distribution of the economic burden of LC, which is the greatest in these countries [127].

Current evidence generally supports the cost-effectiveness of LDCT-based LC screening. However, from a budget impact perspective, findings consistently indicate that introducing an LC screening program is likely to increase healthcare expenditures in the short to medium term. Although budget impact estimates are not directly comparable across studies due to the heterogeneities on healthcare systems, screening program designs, and costing approaches, their overall conclusions are broadly consistent. Future research should focus on improving screening efficiency and optimizing program design to maximize health gains whilst minimizing budgetary pressure.

Technology advancements and treatment innovations represent important opportunities to improve the efficiency of LC screening, although the current evidence base remains limited. Screening modalities have evolved from conventional imaging techniques such as chest X-ray, LDCT, and MRI to advanced approaches that incorporate emerging technologies, including AI, genomics, and biomarkers. These technologies have the potential to improve risk stratification, enhance diagnostic accuracy, and reduce patient burden [22]. Early economic evaluations indicate that AI-assisted screening tools could even reduce costs while enhancing diagnostic accuracy [58]. Advances in treatment, particularly immunotherapies and targeted therapies, are also beginning to be incorporated into economic evaluations of LC screening. These novel therapies have improved survival outcomes, thereby influencing both the cost-effectiveness and budget impact of LC screening programs. Furthermore, by shifting diagnosis toward earlier disease stages, LC screening may reduce the use of costly treatments for advanced disease, partially offsetting the additional costs associated with novel therapies [114].

Combined screening for multiple diseases is another promising strategy. Incorporating CVD screening into LDCT-based LC screening can improve cost-effectiveness. Beyond the scope of this review, multi-cancer early detection (MCED) genomic blood tests have also shown potential to enhance screening efficiency. MCED testing was excluded from our review because they are designed as adjunctive screening approaches for multiple cancers rather than LC-specific screening approaches. Nevertheless, evidence suggests that they may offer important economic benefits. For example, Hackshaw et al. [128] reported a significant reduction in diagnostic costs compared with separate screening for lung, breast, colorectal, and cervical cancer.

Emerging Technologies in Lung Cancer Screening

This review identified 12 studies evaluating three types of emerging technologies: AI-assisted screening, PRS, and biomarkers. Although the evidence base remains limited, current findings generally support the cost-effectiveness of AI-assisted approaches, whereas the cost-effectiveness of PRS- and biomarkers-based strategies remains uncertain. This uncertainty is mainly driven by model parameters regarding diagnostic performance and technology cost. The value-for-money of these emerging technologies may improve alongside the technological evolution where performance improves, and costs decline. For example, the recently developed biomarker test, LungCanSeek, has shown high accuracy in LC detection, with a reagent cost of USD 15 per test [129], which is substantially lower than the biomarker costs assumed in the studies included in this review.

The potential benefits of emerging technologies may not yet have been fully captured in the existing economic evaluations. All included studies focused primarily on their clinical utility, particularly accuracy in risk prediction or diagnosis, while broader benefits (such as the potential to increase screening uptake) have been neglected. LDCT screening faces limitations, including high false-positive rates, unavoidable radiation exposure, and limited accessibility, all of which contribute to the low participation rate [130]. Emerging technologies have been developed to address these barriers and improve screening efficiency. However, predicting uptake for these novel screening technologies remains challenging. One potential solution is to incorporate health preference evidence from discrete choice experiments among the targeting population into economic evaluations [131].

A critical challenge is the limited performance evidence of emerging technologies. Performance data were primarily derived from case-control or retrospective studies, which may not be representative of the target population of LC screening [132, 133]. Additionally, the performance of emerging technologies varied significantly across different ancestries and regions. The diagnostic performance of AI-assisted tools depends on the training data and the radiologists’ experience in their implementation [134], while the predictive power of PRS is highly ancestry-specific [135]. It should be noted that over half of the included studies relied on performance data derived from populations outside the country in which the economic evaluation was conducted, which may not accurately reflect real-world performance in the target population and limit the generalizability and policy relevance of the findings.

Inadequate assessment of costs associated with emerging technologies is another important limitation. Most studies omitted the implementation-related costs of emerging technologies, such as data acquisition, infrastructure, maintenance, and workforce training. These costs are fundamental to real-world adoption and could be a key determinant of their cost-effectiveness [136]. El Arab and Al Moosa [132] systematically assessed AI interventions in healthcare, highlighting four key cost components that significantly affect cost-effectiveness: technology acquisition, implementation and integration costs, ongoing maintenance and support, and indirect costs. Future economic evaluations should carefully incorporate implementation costs to provide more realistic and reliable estimates of value for money on emerging technologies.

It is worth noting that, among the emerging technologies identified in this review, only AI-assisted tools have already been implemented in routine clinical practice. In contrast, biomarkers and PRS have shown promising clinical applications but have not yet been widely adopted in routine practice. Hence, further large-scale real-world validation studies are needed to evaluate their performance and support broader implementation. An encouraging development is that several trials have demonstrated the potential of biomarkers for predicting LC incidence and mortality [137], such as the Early Detection of Cancer of the Lung Scotland (ECLS) trial and the BioMILD trial. The ECLS trial found that, after 5 years, both LC-specific mortality and all-cause mortality were significantly lower in the group using the autoantibody test (EarlyCDT-Lung) to select individuals for LDCT screening, compared with those receiving no screening [138]. The BioMILD trial found that a plasma 24-microRNA signature classifier (MSC) can be reliably integrated into LDCT screening to improve LC risk and mortality prediction accuracy [10, 139]. There is no direct evidence for trial-based evaluation, highlighting a gap between clinical utility and economic evidence.

Future economic evaluations should assess emerging technologies using the most up-to-date clinical evidence, particularly given the rapid pace of innovation in this field. Beyond the emerging technologies identified in this review, breath testing represents another promising approach for LC detection. Devices, such as E-nose, provide a non-invasive and convenient method for LC detection by analyzing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in exhaled breath [140]. A meta-analysis of VOCs found a pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates of 85% and 86% for LC detection, respectively [141]. As clinical evidence continues to emerge, breath testing has the potential to be integrated into current LC screening pathways and should be considered in future economic evaluations.

Methodological Challenges

Full Economic Evaluation

Eligible Population

Current screening eligibility is predominantly based on the age and smoking history criteria. Jongebloed et al. [142] examined 11 national or regional LC screening programs and found that 73% employed the age-smoking criteria, while only 10.7% to 27.8% of current smokers met the eligibility requirements. This highlights the limited coverage of age-smoking criteria, which may miss a substantial proportion of individuals who may develop LC. Risk-based approaches may offer a more efficient alternative. The PLCOm2012 risk prediction model has been shown to be more efficient than the 2013 USPSTF age-smoking criteria for selecting individuals, according to the interim results of the International Lung Screening Trial (ILST) [143]. In addition, some countries have begun adopting broader eligibility criteria. For example, China incorporates occupational exposure, family history, and COPD, while Poland additionally considers personal cancer history and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Future evaluations should continue to assess and optimize screening eligibility to enhance the overall value of LC screening programs.

Modeling Approaches

Substantial variation exists in the modeling approaches used to evaluate LC screening. Given that the benefits of LC screening accrue over a long period, natural history-based modeling approaches are well suited to address this issue [144]. Markov models are typically cohort-based and have a clear structure, but they are constrained by the cohort-based structure and limited ability to incorporate individual heterogeneity. In contrast, individual-level simulation models can better capture the complexity of screening pathways, disease progression, and individual heterogeneity; however, they require more extensive data inputs and rigorous model validation. The model selection in practice should therefore be guided by the availability and quality of local data, the complexity of clinical pathways, and the need to capture both short- and long-term outcomes.

The nine established natural history models identified in this review varied considerably in their structure and representation of disease processes, including LC progression, subtypes, staging, and mortality [144]. While these models provide a more comprehensive representation of disease processes and exhibit a higher level of accuracy, their development was more tailored to country-specific healthcare settings. Consequently, studies adapting such models across healthcare systems should use local data and conduct further validation to reduce potential uncertainty, as direct transfer may introduce bias in cost-effectiveness estimates. Furthermore, while these models often distinguish detailed histological subtypes, economic evaluations typically simplify these subtype-specific differences when estimating health utilities and treatment costs, which could overlook the important variations in benefit and cost estimations.

Uptake and Adherence

There is a lack of explicit and consistent definitions of participation parameters across the identified economic evaluations. Uptake refers to participation in the initial screening among eligible individuals, while adherence describes compliance with recommended subsequent or follow-up screening rounds among the eligible population [145]. However, these terms are not always clearly distinguished and sometimes used interchangeably [146]. Furthermore, assuming full uptake or adherence is highly likely to overestimate cost-effectiveness and does not reflect real-world data. Uptake of currently implemented LC screening programs remains low (e.g., 18% in the US [147], and 33% in China [148]), and adherence tends to decline after the initial screening round [149]. Given the non-trivial effect of participation parameters on economic evaluation conclusions, future evaluations should carefully choose and justify the more realistic estimates of uptake and adherence. In cases where local LC screening-specific information is not available, a pragmatic approach is to draw on evidence from clinical trials, other cancer screening programs in similar contexts, or LC screening programs in comparable jurisdictions, with appropriate and transparent adjustments to account for population heterogeneity and contextual factors.

Cost Components

Treatment costs used in existing economic evaluations may no longer reflect contemporary clinical practice, particularly given the increasing use of novel therapies. Although some studies included treatment costs for novel therapies, they did not capture the effects on prolonged survival, potential side effects, and the resulting changes in treatment pathways. As the treatment landscape continues to evolve, future evaluations should incorporate both the costs and long-term clinical consequences of novel therapies, which may require close collaboration with clinical experts.

In addition, costs related to program implementation and management, such as risk assessment costs, invitation fees, promotion costs, and equipment investment, were often omitted. A unique feature that distinguishes LC screening from other broader population-level cancer screening programs is to restrict potential participants to specific high-risk populations [150]. Hence, risk assessment should be considered an integral healthcare cost and incorporated into economic evaluations [9]. The long-term costs associated with screening-related complications were also poorly quantified within the included studies, such as radiation-induced adverse effects and overdiagnosis-related harms, which would underestimate the true costs of LC screening and overestimate its cost-effectiveness. A comprehensive costing framework is therefore needed to ensure that the truce cost of screening is captured in economic evaluations.

Health State Utility Sources

The derivation and use of health state utility data posed additional challenges. Nearly 80% of CUA studies partly or entirely rely on LC-specific values derived from systematic reviews, or from studies conducted in other countries. Three systematic reviews were mostly referenced, published in 2000 [151], 2010 [152], and 2020 [153], respectively. A common limitation with these reviews is the lack of sufficient granularity in stage-specific utility. In addition, health utilities from other countries may not be directly transferable, as significant differences in health state preferences exist across countries or regions [153]. This highlights the need to collect country-specific utility values that better reflect local populations, clinical practice, and health preferences.

Equity Considerations

Health equity is a key consideration in decision-making of LC implementation, yet its incorporation into economic evaluations has received limited attention. For example, Australia’s National Lung Cancer Screening Program places a strong emphasis on equity, aiming to ensure equitable access for all Australians, particularly priority and disadvantaged populations [154]. However, existing evidence captures only a narrow range of equity dimensions, with important factors such as geographic location, migrant backgrounds, and insurance access often overlooked [155, 156]. This issue likely reflects methodological and data-related barriers to their routine implementation [157]. As equity-focused analytical methods, such as DCEA, are becoming more well-known to the academic community, it is expected that equity considerations will become an essential component in future economic evaluations.

Quality Assessment

The overall quality of the included full economic evaluations was moderately good, although some CHEQUE items remained unaddressed. This may partly reflect the fact that CHEUQE, introduced in 2023, is a relatively new assessment tool that extends the scope of existing economic evaluation checklists [158]. In particular, CHEQUE incorporates methodological and reporting considerations related to equity and ethics, which have received less attention in previous literature. Consequently, some lower scores may reflect evolving methodological progress and expectations rather than fundamental deficiencies in study quality. Future economic evaluations could benefit from using the CHEQUE framework to strengthen both methodological rigor and reporting transparency.

Budget Impact Analysis

Similar to full economic evaluations, BIA studies are subject to several methodological challenges including inadequate accounting of cost components and overestimation of uptake rates. Additionally, other key elements such as the time horizon and modeling approaches can substantially influence the estimates of the financial consequences of implementing LC screening programs.

Time Horizon

The ISPOR guidelines [26, 27] recommend that a time horizon of 1-5 years is commonly adopted. However, the impact of introducing a new LC screening program may not be fully observable within such a short time horizon. Potential downstream benefits and corresponding cost savings may not be realized immediately. While the chosen time horizon should remain relevant to the needs of budget holders, extending the analytical horizon beyond the minimum recommended period may provide a more comprehensive and realistic assessment of budget impact [159].

Model Approaches

The ISPOR guidelines [26, 27] recommend that BIAs employ a simple cost calculator whenever possible, to ensure results are easily understood by budget holders. However, most included studies used more sophisticated models, including cohort-level or individual-level simulation models. One potential reason is that most BIAs were conducted alongside CEAs and therefore shared the same model framework.

Decision Rule: Budget Impact Threshold

The budget impact threshold serves as a measure of the affordability of new interventions from a budget holder’s perspective [160]. When the projected budget impact exceeds a predefined threshold, cost-containment or financial risk-mitigation measures may be considered to manage the associated expenditure. It supports decision-makers in determining appropriate budgetary responses. However, unlike cost-effectiveness thresholds, which are guided by well-established evidence-informed decision rules, budget impact thresholds remain less developed and are applied inconsistently across jurisdictions [161], which may limit the comparability and interpretation of the findings across studies.

Quality Assessment

The quality of the included BIA studies was generally suboptimal, assessed against the quality checklist. In this review, more than half (66.7%) presented BIA together with CEA. In these studies, BIAs were treated as a secondary component rather than a standalone evaluation, with typically only a summary of basic BIA results reported without sufficient detail to fully describe the model. Although BIAs and CEAs are closely related, they address different decision problems and require different analytical settings. Therefore, the methods and results of BIAs should be reported transparently to allow proper assessment and interpretation.

Limitations

This systematic review has a few limitations. First, only English-language publications were included. Second, we set our own context-specific criteria when applying the CHEQUE tool as recommended by its developers. This may lead to inconsistency in judgments across articles and reviewers. Third, the literature search was conducted in six selected databases. However, studies appearing exclusively in other databases or in grey literature from public authorities might have been missed. Fourth, data extraction was performed by a single reviewer; however, a calibration meeting was held before extraction, and a third reviewer independently verified 10% of the included studies to ensure accuracy and consistency. Finally, substantial heterogeneity in study settings and health systems constrained our ability to perform a meta-analysis to quantitatively synthesize economic evidence.

Conclusions

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the health economic evidence on LC screening to inform policy and implementation decisions. Existing evidence indicates that LDCT screening is generally cost-effective for high-risk populations, although implementing it at the national level would increase healthcare expenditures. Emerging technologies, such as AI-assisted tools, biomarkers, and PRS, have the potential to complement LDCT screening by improving risk stratification, guiding individual selection, evaluating lung nodules assessment, and increasing diagnostic accuracy. Novel therapies and combined screening strategies may further enhance screening efficiency. Future research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness and economic impact of these emerging technologies and advanced treatments. Finally, the substantial methodological variations identified in this review highlight opportunities to improve study design, quality, and reporting in future health economic evaluations.
