Cerebral small vessel disease and cognition in older adults across the neurodegenerative spectrum: insights from the COMPASS‐ND study
Authors: Dylan X. Guan, Zahinoor Ismail, Graham A. McLeod, Shahab Marzoughi, Eric E. Smith, Aravind Ganesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONImaging‐based cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) summary scores quantify CSVD burden. This study characterized CSVD scores and assessed their cognitive associations in older adults with various neurodegenerative diseases and cognitive profiles.METHODSBaseline data from 958 Comprehensive Assessment of Neurodegeneration and Dementia (COMPASS‐ND) study participants were analyzed (19.4% cognitively normal, 14.9% subjective cognitive decline, 40.3% mild cognitive impairment, 25.4% dementia). MRI markers of cerebral vascular injury (lacunes, microbleeds, white matter hyperintensities [WMHs], and enlarged perivascular spaces) were visually rated, and a cumulative CSVD score was generated. Cognition was measured using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Clinical Dementia Rating‐Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), and a composite neuropsychological battery test z‐score.RESULTSHigher CSVD scores were associated with greater Hachinski ischemic scores, poorer MoCA performance, worse CDR‐SB, and lower composite z‐scores. Associations were strongest for cognitive domains of executive function, attention, and learning.DISCUSSIONCSVD burden may further contribute to poorer cognition across neurodegenerative conditions and cognitive profiles.

BACKGROUND

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) has been established as the most common cause of vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) and a major contributor to mixed dementia. 1 , 2 CSVD comprises various pathologies that cause dysfunction of cerebral small vessels, including arterioles, capillaries, and venules. Current in vivo assessment of CSVD burden relies primarily on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Cardinal markers of downstream tissue responses to small vessel damage include white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) of presumed vascular origin, lacunes of presumed vascular origin, enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVSs), and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs). 3 These markers not only help identify CSVD in older persons but also provide insights into potential underlying mechanisms and pathologies based on the type and topological distribution of visible markers. For instance, uncontrolled hypertension and diabetes can cause hardening and damage of cerebral arteries, termed hypertensive arteriopathy or arteriosclerosis. This condition is characterized on MRI by deep CMBs, 4 lacunar infarcts, 5 and EPVSs in the basal ganglia. 6 In contrast, the gradual deposition of amyloid beta (Aβ) into the walls of small cortical and leptomeningeal arteries and arterioles, known as cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), is characterized by lobar CMBs 4 and by EPVSs in the centrum semiovale. 6

Individual imaging markers for CSVD have been linked to cognitive decline and dementia. 7 However, there is growing interest in summary measures that capture the cumulative burden of CSVD. 3 Unlike single CSVD imaging markers, such summary scores can account for several CSVD‐related lesions simultaneously, which is particularly important given that these lesions tend to co‐occur and share underlying etiological mechanisms. 3 Furthermore, CSVD is a heterogeneous disease that affects the whole brain, which may not be fully captured by individual lesion metrics. 8 Indeed, there is evidence suggesting that CSVD summary scores may predict cognitive and functional outcomes better than individual markers. 9 However, the majority of studies that have examined the relationship between MRI‐based summary CSVD scores and cognitive decline were conducted in post‐stroke populations, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 which represent only a subset of older adults at risk for dementia and who may experience cognitive decline. Because vascular brain pathologies often co‐exist and may interact with other dementia etiologies, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), 13 a detailed characterization of summary CSVD scores and their associations with cognition in a broader sample of older adults at risk of, or living with, dementia is warranted.

The aim of this study was to characterize CSVD burden using MRI‐based summary scores and to assess the associations between CSVD burden – measured using individual markers and combined burden scores – and cognition in a sample of older adults with various neurodegenerative diseases and levels of cognitive impairment, ranging from cognitively normal to dementia.

METHODS

Study design

We accessed data from the Comprehensive Assessment of Neurodegeneration and Dementia (COMPASS‐ND) study using the Longitudinal Online Research and Imaging System platform. 14 COMPASS‐ND is the signature clinical study of the Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging. The observational study collects clinical, neuropsychological, MRI, and biofluid samples from participants with various types of dementia or cognitive complaints, as well as cognitively normal controls. Participants are enrolled from 32 centers across Canada. Local ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee or Institutional Review Board at each participating center. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A detailed description of the COMPASS‐ND protocol and inclusion/exclusion criteria has been published elsewhere. 15

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We surveyed the literature using PubMed. Existing studies show that CSVD markers are associated with cognitive decline. However, research on summary CSVD scores and cognition has primarily focused on post‐stroke cohorts, with limited examination in broader neurodegenerative or mixed‐etiology populations. Interpretation: Our findings demonstrate that greater CSVD burden, quantified based on the number of MRI‐based markers present, is associated with poorer global cognition and domain‐specific deficits, particularly in executive function, attention, and learning. These results extend prior work by showing that cumulative CSVD burden is associated with poorer cognition across of the spectra of cognitive impairment and neurodegenerative diagnoses in a large, multicenter, deeply characterized cohort. Future directions: Future research should investigate CSVD score associations with cognition over time and how CSVD may interact with neurodegenerative disease pathologies, such as AD, in contributing to cognitive decline.

Participants

The most recent data release included 1167 participants who were either cognitively normal or had subjective cognitive decline (SCD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or dementia due to various etiologies. As we aimed to conduct a complete‐case analysis, participants were excluded for (1) missing MRI components due to absent/inadequate T2/fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) scans (n = 146), (2) missing data on Hachinski ischemic scores (n = 46), 16 and (3) no neuropsychological test battery data (n = 17). The final analysis sample comprised 958 participants. Figure 1 presents a flow diagram summarizing the participant inclusion/exclusion criteria. Imaging markers of CSVD

Brain MRI scans were acquired according to the Canadian Dementia Imaging Protocol, consisting of three‐dimensional (3D) T1‐weighted, proton‐density/T2‐weighted, T2*, FLAIR, diffusion‐weighted, or blood‐oxygen‐level‐dependent resting state sequences. 17 , 18 The protocol complies with the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging (STRIVE). 3 , 19 Procedures were harmonized on three major vendor platforms for qualification, quality control, and quality assurance.

Each scan was visually reviewed for STRIVE‐based evidence of vascular brain injury (infarcts, CMBs, WMHs, and cortical superficial siderosis [cSS]) by qualified neurologists or radiologists, described in greater detail elsewhere. 20 All visual ratings were performed blinded to participant demographics, medical history, and cognitive data. Infarcts were classified by size, number, location, and vascular territory, enabling further classification as lacunar or non‐lacunar. CMBs were classified using the Brain Observer Microbleeds (BOMBS) scale. 21 They were further categorized as lobar (i.e., present in the cortex or white matter of the frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, or insular regions) or deep (i.e., present in the basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, external capsule, brainstem, or cerebellum). WMHs were classified using the Fazekas WMH rating scale. 22

As data on EPVSs were not recorded as part of the original COMPASS‐ND vascular imaging protocol, these imaging markers were separately rated by a team of trained readers (DXG, GAM, SM, and AG) based on visual inspection of the structural MRI scans (T2w whenever available, or else T1w/FLAIR). EPVSs were rated according to an established visual rating scale based on the number of EPVSs observed in the midbrain, basal ganglia, and centrum semiovale. 23 Specifically, the midbrain was rated 0 (absent) or 1 (present), while the basal ganglia and centrum semiovale were rated 0 to 4: 0 = no EPVSs; 1 = mild (1 to 10 EPVSs); 2 = moderate (11 to 20 EPVSs); 3 = frequent (21 to 40 EPVSs); and 4 = severe (>40 EPVSs). A total EPVS score was computed as the sum of ratings across the midbrain, basal ganglia, and centrum semiovale. These PVS ratings were performed by trained raters who cleared an assessment of inter‐rater reliability with respect to an experienced neurologist (AG), achieving Gwet's agreement coefficient >0.6 and percentage agreement >70%. 24

We generated a validated CSVD score using a subset of the aforementioned imaging markers to measure cumulative CSVD burden, 10 as shown in Table S1. Specifically, the CSVD score (range 0 to 4) was computed by assigning one point each to the presence of (1) ≥1 deep CMB, (2) ≥1 lacune, (3) ≥10 EPVSs in the basal ganglia, and (4) periventricular Fazekas grade 3 or deep Fazekas grade 2–3. Because several alternative CSVD scoring methods have been proposed, we also conducted secondary analyses using two additional CSVD scores. 11 , 12 The first was a slightly more granular CSVD score (range 0 to 6) that weighted lesion burden differently: (1) one point for one to four CMBs or two points for the presence of at least five CMBs; (2) one point for ≥1 lacune; (3) one point for ≥20 EPVSs in the basal ganglia; and (4) one point for total Fazekas grade 3–4 or two points for total Fazekas grade 5. We refer to this six point CSVD score as the modified CSVD (mCSVD) score. The second score, which had previously been proposed as a non‐diagnostic staging measure of CAA burden, was scored based on (1) lobar CMBs (one point for two to four lobar CMBs or two points for at least five lobar CMBs); (2) EPVS (one point for ≥20 EPVSs in the centrum semiovale); (3) WMHs (one point for periventricular Fazekas grade 3 or deep Fazekas grade 2–3); and (4) cSS (one point for focal cSS and two points for disseminated cSS). It should be noted that the conventional CSVD score was previously thought to capture features of hypertensive arteriopathy; this is particularly true of the version ranging from 0 to 4, which only counts CMBs in deep locations and EPVS burden in the basal ganglia, locations associated with hypertension and not CAA. 25 By comparing whether observed associations were differed across the conventional CSVD, mCSVD, or CAA‐CSVD scores, we determined whether any observed associations depended on the operational definition of CSVD burden.

Cognitive and functional outcome measures

Outcome measures included the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Clinical Dementia Rating‐Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), a global composite neuropsychological battery test score, and clinical diagnosis (cognitively normal, SCD, MCI, and dementia). The global composite neuropsychological test battery score was derived from the full COMPASS‐ND neuropsychology battery, whose design, procedure, and validation have been described in detail elsewhere. 26 The battery, consisting of 28 tests, examined the cognitive domains of premorbid intelligence quotient (IQ), learning, memory, processing speed, attention and working memory, executive function, visuospatial function, and language.

To generate the global and domain composite neuropsychological battery test scores, we first extracted raw test scores across cognitive domains, excluding IQ: (1) learning (Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test [RAVLT] A1–A5 total immediate recall, Brief Visuospatial Memory Test–Revised [BVMT‐R] total recall, Face–Name Association immediate); (2) memory (RAVLT A7 delayed recall, BVMT‐R delayed recall, Face‐Name Association delayed recall, Digit Symbol Test free recall, Envelope Test retrospective recall score); (3) processing speed (Digit Symbol Test total, Simple/Choice/Basic Reaction Time task means, Delis–Kaplan Executive Function System [D‐KEFS] Color–Word Interference [CWIT] Color Naming time, Trail Making Test Part A [TMT‐A] completion time); (4) attention/working memory (WAIS‐III Digit Span total score, Envelope Test prospective recall score); (5) executive function (D‐KEFS Letter Fluency, Category Fluency, TMT‐B completion time, CWIT inhibition and switching times, Sentence Inhibition automatic and inhibition scores); (6) visuospatial function (Birmingham Object Recognition Battery total score, Judgment of Line Orientation score, BVMT immediate and delayed scores); and (7) language (D‐KEFS Category Fluency, National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center regular/irregular word reading total score, Semantic Word‐Picture total score, Semantic Associates total score, Northwestern Anagram total score, Sentence Repetition total score, Noun/Verb Naming total scores, Sentence Reading total score). To reduce the influence of extreme outliers on score standardization and subsequent analysis, raw scores were winsorized at the first and 99th percentiles. We then computed z‐scores for each raw test score relative to the sample distribution, with time‐based measures (i.e., reaction times, Color Naming times, CWIT times, and TMT‐A/B completion times) reverse‐coded so that higher z‐scores consistently reflected better performance. Finally, domain z‐scores were calculated as the mean of non‐missing standardized task scores, while the global composite neuropsychological test score was calculated as the mean of all available individual test z‐scores per participant. The number of non‐missing scores per test is available in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Demographic, CSVD markers, and outcome measures were summarized for the entire cohort and stratified by CSVD score using means, standard deviations (SDs), ranges, and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using ANOVA for continuous variables and chi‐squared (χ 2) tests for categorical variables. We also compared demographics between complete‐case participants and those excluded for missing data (i.e., incomplete case), given that missing data for imaging and outcome measures may not have been missing completely at random. In light of prior research demonstrating sex differences in CSVD severity in stroke populations 27 and in accordance with section 3.4 of the Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging (CCNA) Publications and Data Access Committee policy, we also performed group comparisons of CSVD severity by sex.

To determine the construct validity of the CSVD scores, which had not been operationalized previously in COMPASS‐ND, we modeled the association between each CSVD score (exposure) and the Hachinski ischemic score (outcome) using negative binomial regression. This model was selected because the outcome variable distribution was count‐like (right‐skewed whole numbers) with overdispersion (i.e., variance > mean). Because the negative binomial regression model uses a logarithmic link function, we exponentiated output coefficients to generate count ratios (CRs), which can be interpreted along the original scale of measurement: CRs indicate the multiplicative change in Hachinski ischemic score for every unit rise in CSVD score.

Next, for primary analysis, we modeled the association between CSVD score and four cognitive outcomes: MoCA total score (linear regression), CDR‐SB (median quantile regression), global neuropsychological battery composite score (linear regression), and cognitive diagnosis (ordinal logistic regression). Ordinal logistic regression was used when modeling cognitive diagnosis as an outcome given that the variable consisted of four ordered diagnostic categories (cognitively normal, SCD, MCI, dementia). Results of ordinal logistic regression are reported in terms of cumulative odds ratios (cORs). The Brant test was used to verify the proportional odds assumption for all ordinal logistic regression models. Median quantile regression was used in place of linear regression when standard assumptions were violated, as determined by visual inspection of residual diagnostic plots (Figure S1), to evaluate the association between CSVD burden and cognitive outcomes at a central level of the outcome distribution. Covariates included age, sex, and years of education. We also explored including sex×CSVD score interaction terms into models to identify potential sex‐dependent effects.

We conducted three separate secondary analyses to better understand our findings. First, we conducted parallel analyses using the mCSVD and CAA‐CSVD scores to explore the effect of CSVD score operationalization on any observed associations. Second, given that CSVD has been more strongly associated with specific cognitive domains, we explored associations between CSVD scores and each neuropsychological test battery domain score. Finally, we explored the associations between each previously described individual CSVD lesion and the four cognitive outcome measures. Specifically, we investigated the presence and number of CMBs (any, lobar, and deep), presence and number of lacunes, Fazekas WMH scores (periventricular, deep, and total), and EPVS score (midbrain, basal ganglia, centrum semiovale, and total).

Given that we performed a complete‐case analysis, we further assessed the robustness of our approach to missing neuropsychological data by conducting a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation by chained equations (20 imputations with predictive mean matching). Item‐level cognitive test scores were imputed using demographic, cognitive (MoCA and CDR‐SB), and individual CSVD variables, after which composite z‐scores were recalculated within each imputed dataset. Fully adjusted linear regression models of CSVD scores as predictors of global neuropsychological battery composite scores were conducted in each imputed dataset and results were pooled using Rubin's rules. 28 These pooled results were qualitatively compared to the models using the non‐imputed global neuropsychological battery composite scores.

For the primary analysis, which utilized the conventional four‐point CSVD score, a total of four comparisons were made given four cognitive outcome variables: MoCA, CDR‐SB, global neuropsychological test z‐scores, and cognitive diagnosis. These four comparisons were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure based on false discovery rate. 29 Corrected p values, hereby referred to as q values, are reported alongside uncorrected p values. A statistical significance threshold of q < 0.05 was used for the primary analysis. Due to the exploratory nature of the secondary analyses, the p values for these analyses were not corrected for multiple comparisons, and we used p < 0.05 as the significance threshold. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

Participants (n = 958, 47.2% female) were aged 72.0 ± 7.5 years and had completed 15.2 ± 2.8 years of education (Table 1). The most common cognitive diagnosis was MCI (40.3%), followed by dementia (25.4%), cognitively normal (19.4%), and SCD (14.9%). AD was the suspected etiology for nearly half (48.6%) of all participants diagnosed with dementia. The remaining dementia cases were attributed mostly to vascular pathology (16.9%), frontotemporal lobar degeneration (12.8%), and Lewy body disease (10.7%). Complete and incomplete cases did not differ significantly in terms of age, sex, and years of education, but complete cases were more likely to be diagnosed with SCD and MCI than incomplete cases (Table S3). The median time between neuropsychological testing and the MRI visit across participants was 16 (interquartile range: 7 to 33) days.

CSVD characterization

Across the entire COMPASS‐ND baseline cohort with available data (n = 958), lacunes were present in 142 participants (14.8%) and CMBs in 190 (19.8%), and average total EPVS scores were 5.2 ± 1.4. The majority (n = 689, 71.9%) of participants exhibited some degree of CSVD burden on MRI (i.e., score ≥ 1), with 309 (41.5%) assigned a score of 1, 217 (22.7%) a score of 2, 62 (6.5%) a score of 3, and 12 (1.2%) a score of 4. We observed sex differences in the presence of lacunes (male: 17.2%, female: 12.2%, p = 0.04) and CMBs (male: 23.3%, female: 15.9%, p = 0.005) but not in other individual CSVD markers or CSVD score (p = 0.13).

As expected, Hachinski ischemic scores were higher among participants with greater CSVD burden; every unit rise in CSVD score was associated with 1.20 times (95% confidence interval [CI]: [1.12, 1.30], p < 0.001) greater Hachinski ischemic score. This association was independent of sex (CSVD score × sex p = 0.26). Similarly, every one‐point rise in mCSVD and CAA‐CSVD scores were associated with 1.14 (95% CI: [1.08, 1.21], p < 0.001) and 1.09 (95% CI: [1.02, 1.16], p = 0.001) times greater Hachinski ischemic score, respectively.

Unadjusted analyses demonstrated that greater CSVD scores were associated with poorer cognition/function, irrespective of the outcome variable and CSVD scoring method (Table 2). These observed associations remained in the same direction and statistically significant after adjusting for covariates of age, sex, and years of education and accounting for multiple comparisons.

CSVD score associations with neuropsychological test battery domains

Greater CSVD scores were associated with poorer composite neuropsychological battery test scores across all domains (Figure S4). Associations were most pronounced for the domains of attention/working memory, executive function, and learning. Specifically, every unit rise in CSVD score was associated with a 0.08 lower z‐score in attention/working memory (95% CI: [−0.14, −0.03], p = 0.004) and executive function (95% CI: [−0.13, −0.02], p = 0.005), and a 0.09 lower learning z‐score (95% CI: [−0.14, −0.03], p = 0.004). The most modest effect was observed in the language domain (b = −0.04, 95% CI: [−0.08, 0], p = 0.04). None of these associations depended on sex (all CSVD score × sex p > 0.05).

The presence of any CMBs was associated with a higher Hachinski ischemic score (CR = 1.24, 95% CI [1.06, 1.46], p = 0.009), but not with MoCA, CDR‐SB, or the composite neuropsychological test battery z‐score (Table 3). DISCUSSION

This study aimed to characterize MRI‐based measures of CSVD burden and examine their associations with cognition in older adults with varying dementia etiologies and levels of cognitive and functional impairment. Some degree of MRI‐based CSVD burden was present in over two‐thirds of participants. The CSVD score was associated with greater Hachinski ischemic score and poorer scores on a cognitive screening tool, cognitive‐functional clinical endpoint measure, neuropsychological test battery z‐score, and more impaired neurocognitive diagnosis. These associations generally did not depend on sex and remained mostly consistent even after using two others CSVD scores, one of which was designed as a staging measure for CAA severity. As expected, most individual CSVD imaging markers were associated with greater Hachinski ischemic score when measured either in terms of presence/absence or number/severity. With regard to the primary outcome, WMH burden, as measured by Fazekas grade, showed the strongest and most consistent associations with lower cognitive performance among all the individual CSVD imaging markers, with a stronger effect size for periventricular rather than deep white matter burden. Other individual markers that showed associations with poorer cognition, albeit less consistently, included the number of CMBs (deep or focal), presence of lacunes, total and basal ganglia EPVS grades.

The observed associations between greater CSVD scores across the three operational definitions and greater Hachinski ischemic scores are largely consistent with the literature demonstrating that CSVD is the most common cause of VCI. 30 Among the three CSVD scores, CAA‐CSVD exhibited an effect size that was modestly lower than for the conventional and mCSVD scores. This difference, while modest, may be accounted for by the primary utility of the Hachinski scale to detect clinical features of multi‐infarct dementia that are more closely tied to hypertensive arteriopathy than CAA. 16 Results from the secondary analyses, which revealed that the presence of lacunes, deep CMBs, and basal ganglia EPVS were among the strongest predictors of Hachinski ischemic scores, further support this interpretation. Altogether, the findings related to Hachinski ischemic scores demonstrate that CSVD scores may serve as valid measures to detect CSVD contributions to cognitive impairment in a sample of older adults with various dementia etiologies. Different pathologically informed CSVD scores may help to determine the extent to which cognitive impairment may be attributable to certain CSVD pathology, such as hypertensive arteriopathy or CAA, further evidence is required and should not replace established diagnostic criteria. 31

Our findings related to cognitive outcomes are largely consistent with prior studies in stroke populations. For example, among older adults who survived intracerebral hemorrhage, higher CSVD scores were associated with a faster rate of both cognitive decline and incident dementia. 12 Our study further demonstrates that the association between a CSVD summary score and cognition extends to older adults with various neurodegenerative disease etiologies and/or degrees of cognitive and functional impairment; hence, CSVD summary scores may have utility in predicting cognitive and functional outcomes beyond stroke populations. This finding is important given increasing recognition of the interaction between vascular pathologies and other dementia etiologies. 32 For example, the presence of mixed pathologies, including vascular brain injury, lowers the threshold of AD pathological load required for overt symptoms to manifest. 33 Further, CSVD may even contribute to the pathogenesis of AD. Vascular risk factors, such as hypertension, as well as WMHs, have been shown to be associated with tau accumulation in the medial temporal lobe, 34 , 35 , 36 and reduced cerebral blood flow is associated with Aβ, tau, and synaptic biomarkers even as early as in the asymptomatic stages of the AD continuum. 37 CSVD summary scores may serve as a useful in vivo marker to further elucidate the effects of CSVD and its potential synergistic interactions with neurodegenerative diseases.

Deficits in processing speed, executive function, and attention are well documented as the most impaired cognitive domains in persons with CSVD, although evidence indicates that all major domains can be affected. 38 In line with these findings, we observed a dose‐dependent relationship between higher CSVD burden and poorer performance across all assessed cognitive domains. As expected, the strongest associations were with executive function and attention/working memory. Notably, we also observed a pronounced association with the learning/encoding domain, consistent with studies that distinguish this process from long‐term memory or retention. 39 By contrast, the association with processing speed was comparatively modest. This may reflect the smaller number of tests that measure processing speed in the COMPASS‐ND neuropsychological test battery, 26 potentially reducing sensitivity to differences in processing speed.

Early forms of the CSVD summary score adopted a simple additive approach that assigned one point to the presence of four main CSVD markers. 10 Since then, newer approaches have taken into account the severity and location of lesions, as well as other imaging markers. These revised CSVD scores better predict various outcomes in stroke patients and differentiate between types of CSVD. 11 , 12 , 40 In our study, we additionally included a six‐point and CAA‐CSVD score but did not find major differences in their association with our cognitive outcomes. However, there is continued interest in adding to or refining these imaging measures of cumulative CSVD burden. We found that the number of CMBs was more strongly associated with neuropsychological test performance than a binary presence variable. Several recent studies attempted to generate a global CSVD or brain frailty score based on continuous, or at least more granular, variables, such as by averaging z‐scores across several small vessel disease imaging markers (measured continuously) 41 or by constructing a latent CSVD burden variable using structural equation modeling and confirmatory factor analysis. 42 , 43 , 44 Although these studies support the idea that continuous CSVD scores have enhanced ability for predicting cognitive impairment, it should be noted that they require greater computational resources than simple additive approaches that can be derived relatively quickly from visual inspection. Hence, while continuous CSVD scores are promising for enhancing our understanding of CSVD in research settings, additive CSVD scores remain more practical for clinical use. Our findings were consistent across two different additive CSVD scores.

Our study had some important limitations. The cross‐sectional design of this study precludes any inferences about the temporal order of CSVD imaging markers and their association with cognition. Future studies should investigate whether MRI‐based CSVD summary scores are associated with poorer cognitive outcomes over time, such as incident dementia. These studies may also benefit from including non‐cognitive outcomes, such as neuropsychiatric symptoms and mild behavioral impairment, which have also been increasingly linked to vascular pathology in older adults. 45 , 46 Furthermore, as previously discussed, there is some evidence that the CSVD scores employed in this study have room for refinement, either through the addition of new and more granular measures. In addition, adopting a continuous scoring approach may further improve CSVD score measures. We did not perform quantitative/volumetric measurements of the examined markers like WMHs; however, our primary interest was to evaluate markers in a manner that would be feasible in typical clinical settings without additional software. EPVSs were also rated using two different sequences: T2‐FLAIR and T1w. While the vast majority of EPVS ratings were done on T2‐FLAIR, this heterogeneity may have introduced noise into the EPVS ratings and CSVD score. While we explored possible sex differences in the associations between CSVD scores and cognitive outcomes, we did not investigate gender differences. Finally, this study could not conclusively determine whether the cognitive impairment observed in participants with greater CSVD burden was solely attributable to CSVD, especially given that many participants likely had other underlying etiologies, or whether the various CSVD scores were fully specific for an underlying etiology. The CAA‐CSVD score employed in this study, for example, should not be considered diagnostic for CAA. Future studies should consider more in‐depth exploration of pathologically informed CSVD scores.

In conclusion, CSVD summary scores were associated with poorer cognition in a sample of older adults with different neurodegenerative diseases and/or degrees of cognitive impairment. WMH grade was the individual component of the CSVD summary scores that was consistently associated with poorer cognition. Future studies should investigate longitudinal associations between MRI‐based CSVD summary scores and cognitive and functional trajectories in various older adult populations to better understand CSVD.
