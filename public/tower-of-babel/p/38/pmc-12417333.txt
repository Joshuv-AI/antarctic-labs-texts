A Case of Creeping Disease Caused by Gnathostoma in a Pregnant Woman Treated With Ivermectin
Authors: Sonoka Uchida, Hidetoshi Maruyama, Yoshihito Yokoyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe encountered a case of gnathostomiasis in a pregnant woman; she was successfully treated using ivermectin, and the baby was delivered without complications. Administration of ivermectin during pregnancy may be considered after evaluation of the number of weeks of pregnancy and the risk to mother and child.

Introduction

Gnathostomiasis is a zoonotic disease caused by the parasite Gnathostoma. Gnathostoma requires two intermediate hosts and one final host to complete its life cycle [1]. Early records show cases of human gnathostomiasis in Southeast Asian countries such as Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, Indonesia, and Thailand, where high numbers of cases have been reported [2]. Other regions in Asia where cases of human gnathostomiasis have also been reported are China, India, Japan, and Korea [1].

Gnathostoma can infect humans via three routes: oral, transplacental, and cutaneous [1]. The main route of human infection is through the consumption of raw intermediate hosts such as freshwater fish, frogs, snakes, and livestock that contain third‐stage larvae [3]. Endemicity of the disease is attributed to the habit of eating raw fish in endemic foci such as in Thailand and other Southeast Asian countries, China, and Japan [1]. Although transplacental infection has not been reported in humans, it has been reported in cats with severe Gnathostoma infection [4]. Furthermore, based on a case in which a 3‐day‐old infant was found to be infected with the gnathostome parasite, a report proposing perinatal transmission of this disease has been published [5]. Moreover, constant barehanded processing of infected meat from the second intermediate host could also be a route of infection in humans [2, 6].

The most common clinical feature is cutaneous migration; however, in severe cases, it may invade the internal organs, including the eyes, lungs, gastrointestinal tract, spinal cord, and central nervous system, among others [1, 7]. These cases can result in blindness, troubled breathing, paralysis, and even death [8, 9, 10]. Therefore, early treatment of this condition is necessary.

Although surgical and drug treatments have been reported for gnathostomiasis, drug therapies have been widely used in recent years [2, 11]. Among the various antiparasitic drugs, only ivermectin and albendazole are effective against gnathostomiasis [12]. Ivermectin has been shown to be effective as either a stat dose of 0.2 mg/kg or as doses of 0.2 mg/kg on two consecutive days. According to previous studies, the cure rates for a single dose of ivermectin or two consecutive days of treatment were 76%–95.2% and 100%, respectively [13, 14, 15]. Albendazole 400 mg once or twice daily for 21 days has also been used for the treatment of gnathostomiasis and had cure rates of 93.9% and 94.1%, respectively [16]. Ivermectin appears to be more effective than albendazole, even with short‐term administration.

However, the use of antiparasitic drugs in pregnant women has not been proven safe. Limited data are available on the use of both ivermectin and albendazole in pregnant women. They are used only when the benefits outweigh the disadvantages. The first trimester of pregnancy is the organ formation period, and there is a risk of fetal malformation, so maternal drug use during this period should be approached with caution. Conversely, during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy, there is a possibility of fetal toxicity via the placenta.

In this report, we describe a case of gnathostomiasis in a pregnant woman who was treated successfully using ivermectin during the third trimester of pregnancy without complications to the baby. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of gnathostomiasis during pregnancy.

Case History/Examination

The patient was a 32‐year‐old gravid 3 para 2 woman who lived in Aomori Prefecture, Japan. She had become pregnant spontaneously and underwent a prenatal check‐up at our department. Her medical history was unremarkable and she was not taking any medications. Her only allergy was hay fever. She did not drink alcohol or smoke during her pregnancy, and she had no history of traveling abroad during this period either. The pregnancy was uncomplicated, and abdominal ultrasound showed that fetal growth was appropriate for gestational age, with no fetal abnormalities detected. During pregnancy, she had eaten raw freshwater fish on multiple occasions in accordance with the local seasonal customs in her area. At 37 weeks of gestation, an itchy, bulging rash was observed only on the left upper abdomen, and the patient was administered a topical steroid. However, her symptoms did not improve, and she was referred to the Department of Dermatology at 38 weeks and 0 days of gestation. On dermatological examination, a fist‐sized erythema was observed on the left upper abdomen with subcutaneous spiral induration (Figure 1a). She told us that a family member who had shared daily meals had been diagnosed with creeping disease due to Gnathostoma. During the same period, an outbreak of gnathostomiasis had occurred in the area where the patient lived, with over 300 confirmed cases. Peripheral blood examination revealed no elevation in leukocyte count or abnormalities in white blood cell differentiation, including eosinophilia. A serum antibody test for gnathostomiasis was not performed.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Based on her history and symptoms, the patient was diagnosed with creeping disease caused by Gnathostoma. Differential diagnoses included prurigo of pregnancy, pruritic urticarial papules and plaques of pregnancy, and allergic dermatitis; all of these conditions are characterized by multiple, widespread rashes, and they do not present with spiral indurations; therefore, they did not match the patient's symptoms. Although the patient's symptoms closely resembled those of cutaneous larva migrans, there were no confirmed cases in the patient's area of residence, and the patient had no history of travel to endemic areas.

The patient was treated symptomatically with topical steroids and oral antihistamines without further antiparasitic medication because she was pregnant. However, further creeping expansion was observed over the course of a week (Figure 1b). The patient was deeply concerned about transplacental transmission of the Gnathostoma and desired early therapeutic intervention with antiparasitic drugs. We explained to the patient that ivermectin could be a beneficial option according to the package insert, although reports on the safety of its oral administration during pregnancy are limited. Although teratogenicity has been reported at high doses in previous animal studies, the risk seemed low because the patient was already 39 weeks pregnant. The patient agreed with the above explanation and was referred to the dermatology department where she received a single dose of ivermectin 12 mg (0.18 mg/kg) on the same day.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The skin symptoms resolved within a few days. The baby was delivered spontaneously at 40 weeks 0 days of gestation. The baby weighed 3248 g and had an Apgar score of nine points at both 1 and 5 min. Furthermore, we informed the patient that the risk of transfer of ivermectin to the breast milk was considered low and unlikely to have any adverse effects on the infant. The patient decided to breastfeed, and the baby continued through the postpartum period without any complications. No tests were performed to confirm healing, but no recurrence of the creeping disease was observed.

Discussion

Since the first report in Thailand in 1889, approximately 5000 cases of gnathostomiasis have been reported worldwide [17]. In the area where this case occurred, fry fish caught during winter are traditionally eaten raw. The background of this case was a contemporaneous gnathostomiasis pandemic that was attributed to the consumption of raw freshwater fish caught in freshwater lakes, as diagnosed through the removal of Gnathostoma from the skin lesions of a few other patients. Although approximately one case of gnathostomiasis caused by consumption of raw freshwater fish occurred every 2–3 years in the area where the patient lived, an outbreak of this magnitude had never previously occurred, and the cause of the outbreak is unknown [18]. People living in areas where this disease is prevalent should take measures to prevent infection. Effective preventive measures for this disease include avoiding raw fish and undercooked meat, and wearing gloves and washing hands when handling infected meat from second intermediate hosts [11].

According to our PubMed search, this is the first report of gnathostomiasis during pregnancy. Three main reasons exist for the lack of reports of gnathostomiasis infection in pregnant women. First, this disease occurs mainly in developing countries; therefore, there may be cases of pregnant women being infected that have not been reported. Second, pregnant women may tend to avoid eating raw fish and meat. Third, the diagnosis of gnathostomiasis is difficult, and it may have been misdiagnosed as other diseases.

The clinical diagnosis of gnathostomiasis is relatively easy; however, a definitive diagnosis is difficult. Although worm removal is necessary for a definitive diagnosis, the worms are fast‐moving and may stray into the subcutaneous tissue; the probability of excising them is low. The main symptoms are skin swelling with pain and pruritus due to skin crawling; however, in rare cases, deep infection of the central nervous system and eyeballs may occur, resulting in a poor prognosis [1, 19]. Eosinophilia in the peripheral blood and positive serum antibodies against Gnathostoma are also considered auxiliary diagnoses [20]. In this case, the cause of the absence of eosinophilia in the peripheral blood is unknown, but it may be due to the fact that only skin symptoms were present and the infection was not severe. In one previous report, eosinophilia was only observed in 7/16 cases of gnathostomiasis, bolstering the theory that it should be used only as an adjunct to diagnosis [21]. In the current case, the subcutaneous spiral mass, dietary history, and outbreak in the area led to a clinical diagnosis. A serum antibody test was not performed because it would have taken too long to obtain the results. However, we determined that a diagnosis could be made based on the patient's medical history and symptoms. Fortunately, the patient only had cutaneous symptoms and did not develop a deep infection.

In the past, surgical excision of the worms was the most common treatment, but in recent years, the efficacy of drug therapy using antiparasitic drugs such as ivermectin and albendazole has been reported and is becoming more widespread [7]. However, safety reports regarding the use of antiparasitic drugs during pregnancy are limited. Albendazole reportedly causes teratogenicity and infant weight loss in rats and rabbits, and its package insert prohibits its use in pregnant women in Japan. However, a few studies report that no adverse effects were observed in the offspring when albendazole was administered to pregnant women [22, 23].

On the other hand, Ivermectin is an FDA class C drug and one review has reported that the use of ivermectin after the first trimester of pregnancy is probably acceptable [24]. The recommended use during pregnancy is listed as “Human Data Suggest Low Risk” in the book “Briggs Drugs in Pregnancy and Lactation” [25]. Teratogenic effects of ivermectin have only been observed in rats and rabbits when the dose is high enough to be toxic to the mother, and the package insert states that it can be administered when the benefits of use outweigh the risks. Ivermectin has previously been administered to pregnant women. In two previous studies with 110 and 200 participants, in which ivermectin was administered during the first trimester of pregnancy in an area endemic for onchocerciasis, no increased risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, or congenital anomalies was observed [26, 27]. In these studies, in 97 (88%) and 170 (85%) cases, respectively, the medication was administered during the first trimester of pregnancy. Although evidence regarding the use of ivermectin during pregnancy is limited, the risk to the child is considered low when ivermectin is used after the first trimester of pregnancy, in compliance with the prescribed dosage.

There have been no previous reports of gnathostomiasis during pregnancy; however, there have been reports of other parasitic infections such as strongyloidiasis. Strongyloidiasis is treated similarly to gnathostomiasis, using antiparasitic drugs such as ivermectin or a benzothiazole. According to a review of strongyloidiasis during pregnancy that was reported in 2021, the treatment approach varies between severe and non‐severe cases [28]. In severe cases, it is stated that the risks outweigh the benefits; therefore, ivermectin should be used. In non‐severe cases, evaluation of the risks and benefits of ivermectin use is difficult [28].

In this case, because the patient was in the third trimester of pregnancy, we had the option of delaying the administration of the antiparasitic drugs or inducing labor and administering them postpartum. However, the patient strongly desired early treatment. After careful discussion of the matter with the patient based on current evidence, the decision was made to proceed with administration.

Although ivermectin is excreted in breast milk, its use during lactation has not yet been reported. However, the relative intake of ivermectin from the mother to the fetus through breastfeeding is estimated to be 0.98%, which is not considered enough to adversely affect the infant [29]. In this case, ivermectin was administered only as a single dose. At the time of delivery, 1 week had already passed since the dose had been administered, and the mother's blood level itself was assumed to be low; therefore, breastfeeding was considered possible.

This case represents an extremely rare instance of gnathostomiasis occurring during pregnancy. In cases of gnathostomiasis during pregnancy, it is important to provide adequate, up‐to‐date information and determine the treatment plan based on the gestational age, severity of symptoms, fetal condition, and patient preferences. From the perspective of teratogenicity, the use of antiparasitic drugs during the first trimester of pregnancy should be avoided. Furthermore, to clarify the safety of antiparasitic drug use during pregnancy, further case accumulation is necessary.

Conclusion

Although gnathostomiasis often causes skin lesions, in some cases it can cause deep infections of the central nervous system or eyes, resulting in a poor prognosis. The administration of antiparasitic drugs during pregnancy must be carefully considered based on the gestational age, severity of symptoms, fetal condition, and patient preferences. Further case accumulation is necessary to clarify the safety of antiparasitic drug use during pregnancy.
