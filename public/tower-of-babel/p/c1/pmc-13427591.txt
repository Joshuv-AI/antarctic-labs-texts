Characteristics of pediatric drowning out-of-hospital cardiac arrests in the United States
Authors: Christopher E. Gaw, Chelsea B. Kadish, Christopher B. Gage, Sara Helwig, Jacob C. Kamholz, Shea L. van den Bergh, Kayla M. Riel, Ashish R. Panchal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsDrowning is a leading cause of pediatric death in the United States, yet data characterizing these arrests is limited. Our study aimed to describe demographic and emergency medical services (EMS) response characteristics for drowning-related pediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (pOHCAs).MethodsUsing the 2023 National Emergency Medical Services Information System dataset, we conducted a cross-sectional study of EMS-attended 9-1-1 responses involving children 1 day–17 years old who experienced a drowning-related pOHCA. We used descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression (adjusted odds ratio; 95% confidence intervals) to identify factors associated with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).ResultsOur study included 753 drowning-related pOHCAs. Most arrests were toddlers aged 1–4 years (57.8%), males (64.5%), at private residences (72.5%), and in urban settings (83.7%). EMS clinicians were commonly dispatched during the summer (46.7%) and between 12:00 and 17:59 (47.4%). Arrests were often unwitnessed (73.7%) and received bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) (81.3%). Fifty-four percent of adolescent arrests were unwitnessed compared to 80.5% among toddlers (P < 0.001). Most arrests (81.3%) received bystander CPR. Shockable rhythms were uncommon (4.6%). EMS-witnessed ROSC was obtained in 42.0% of arrests. In multivariable regression, adolescent age (3.25; 1.25–8.79), witnessed arrests (1.59; 1.03–2.45), and bystander CPR (2.83; 1.67–4.96) were associated with higher odds of ROSC.ConclusionsMost drowning-related pOHCAs involved young children, with age-based differences in scene characteristics and pre-EMS arrival factors. Adolescent arrests were more frequently witnessed and more likely to obtain ROSC. Opportunities exist to improve drowning prevention among children and enhance the chain of survival.

Introduction

Drowning is the third leading cause of unintentional injury deaths worldwide.1 In the United States (US), drowning is the leading cause of death for children 1–4 years of age,2, 3, 4 and one of the leading causes of death for children and adolescents of all ages.5 Though drowning death rates among individuals 0–17 years of age declined by 38% from 1999 to 2019,2 segments of the US population remain at increased risk,6 with rates increasing since 2019.5 Drowning-related morbidity and mortality can be reduced through strengthening the Drowning Chain of Survival, which emphasizes early recognition, rescue, and resuscitation in drowning cases.7, 8

Drowning-related pediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (pOHCAs) are unique due to children’s higher susceptibility to hypoxemia9, 10 and likelihood to present with non-shockable rhythms.11, 12, 13, 14 Prior regional and single-center studies have described how early rescue and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) are associated with improved survival and neurological outcomes.12, 13, 15, 16 In a recent pediatric prehospital study, infants, males, and nonurban locations were identified as predictors of critical injury from drowning, though this study did not exclusively examine prehospital cardiac arrests.17 Although emergency medical services (EMS) clinicians are often the first medical providers at the scene of a child drowning, data on the prehospital response to drowning-related pOHCA remains limited.12, 16, 17

Understanding the epidemiology and EMS response characteristics to drowning-related pOHCAs can generate evidence to guide further development of resuscitation protocols and primary prevention efforts for this patient population. Therefore, to address gaps in the current epidemiologic literature, our primary study aim was to characterize the demographic, scene, and EMS response characteristics of drowning-related pOHCA using a national EMS database. As a secondary aim, we conducted exploratory modeling to evaluate the association of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) with demographic, scene, and EMS response characteristics among this population.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective, observational study using the 2023 National Emergency Medical Services Information System (NEMSIS) dataset. The study was determined exempt under federal regulations for secondary analysis of existing data by the American Institutes for Research Institutional Review Board (Project EX00687). Our data reporting adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.18

Study design and sample

The publicly available NEMSIS dataset serves as the centralized national repository for EMS patient care records across the US and its territories. NEMSIS utilizes a standardized national data dictionary with defined variable elements and coding structures, which are adopted by participating EMS agencies. Deidentified data from local EMS agencies are compiled by state EMS offices and submitted to the NEMSIS Technical Assistance Center, where data are cleaned and stored.19 NEMSIS includes over 150 variables, encompassing patient demographics, EMS characteristics, treatments and procedures performed, EMS temporal-spatial event information, and patient disposition.19, 20 The dataset includes EMS activations from greater than 90% of EMS agencies that handle 9-1-1 calls nationwide.21 In the 2023 NEMSIS dataset, 14,369 EMS agencies from 54 states and US territories contributed data on 54,190,579 EMS activations.22

Using the 2023 NEMSIS dataset, we identified all 9-1-1 responses (NEMSIS variable: eResponse.05 – “2205001”) involving pediatric patients 1 day to 17-years-old. Among these cases, we subsequently identified those documented to have experienced pOHCA before or after EMS arrival (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.01 – “3001003”, “3001005”) due to drowning or submersion (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.02 – “3002003”). These responses comprised our study cohort and represented EMS-attended cases with documented cardiac arrest (Fig. 1), and are not limited to cases in which EMS initiated resuscitation.Fig. 1Cohort selection of EMS-attended drowning-related pediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrests.

Measures and study outcomes

For our primary aim, we analyzed age (NEMSIS variable: age in years – categorized into infants [1 day–<1 year], toddlers [1–4 years], school-aged children [5–12 years], and adolescents [13–17 years]), sex (NEMSIS variable: ePatient.13 – male, female), US Region (NEMSIS variable: USCensusRegion – Midwest, Northeast, South, West), and level of EMS care (NEMSIS variable: eResponse.15 – BLS, ALS, Specialty Critical Care). Urbanicity categories (NEMSIS variable: Urbanicity – Urban, Suburban, Rural, and Wilderness) were computed by NEMSIS using the US Department of Agriculture Office of Management and Budget Urban Influence Codes.23 Incident location (NEMSIS variable: eScene.09 – Private Residence, Recreational, Commercial, Street/Road, and Other [including Healthcare, Institutional Residence, Other, Public Area, School)]) was analyzed using NEMSIS list definitions.24

To describe EMS care processes, we examined binary (yes, no) indicators of witnessed arrest (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.04), bystander CPR (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.05), and ROSC (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.12). We further characterized categorical variables describing automated external defibrillator (AED) use (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.07), first monitored rhythm (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.11), and arrest outcome at the end of EMS event (NEMSIS variable: eArrest.18). Date and time of dispatch (NEMSIS variable: eTimes.03) were categorized by season, day of week, and time-of-day (overnight [22:00–05:59], morning [06:00–11:59], afternoon [12:00–17:59], and evening [18:00–21:59]).

For our secondary aim, we evaluated the association between select patient demographics, scene characteristics, and EMS response characteristics, and the outcome of ROSC, which was categorized as a binary (yes, no) indicator of any ROSC documented during the EMS-attended event.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to evaluate patient demographics, pOHCA characteristics, EMS care process characteristics, and seasonality and time-of-day patterns in EMS dispatches. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, with percentages calculated using complete case analysis unless otherwise specified. We used the Pearson’s χ2 test to compare categorical variables across age groups. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05.

To evaluate the association of EMS-attended ROSC and patient demographics, scene characteristics, and EMS care processes, we developed a multivariable logistic regression model with covariates selected a priori. Given covariate data distributions, the selected variables were collapsed from the original descriptive categories to ensure adequate cell sizes. Covariates included age (categorized as per above), sex, urbanicity (urban versus non-urban [including suburban, rural, and wilderness]), location (private residence, recreational, other), advanced airway placement (binary: yes, no), witnessed arrest (binary: yes, no), bystander CPR (binary: yes, no), and documented epinephrine administration (binary: yes, no). Analyses were restricted to complete cases for all variables in the model. Overall model fit was assessed using likelihood ratio testing. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Outcomes are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We conducted our analyses using R 4.5.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Most drowning-related pOHCAs occurred in urban settings (83.7%), the Southern US region (53.5%), and involved advanced life support units (93.1%). Nearly two-thirds of arrests occurred in males (64.5%). Drowning-related pOHCAs most commonly involved toddlers (57.8%), followed by school-aged children (22.0%) and adolescents (12.2%). Most drowning-related pOHCA did not have documented AED use prior to EMS arrival (74.8%). The minority of first monitored cardiac rhythms were shockable (4.6%). Within age groups, adolescents had the highest proportion of drowning-related pOHCAs with a shockable rhythm (11.4%, 8/70). ROSC was documented in 42.0% of our study cohort and demonstrated borderline significance by age (p = 0.05). Within age groups, infants (29.3%, 17/58) had the lowest proportion of ROSC whereas adolescents (52.6%, 40/76) were observed to have the highest proportion.

Characteristics and factors associated with ROSC

The proportion of witnessed arrests was 37.8% (108/286) in the ROSC group compared to 16.1% (63/392) in the group without ROSC (Appendix A). Of arrests with ROSC, 88.9% (224/252) of arrests received bystander CPR, compared with 75.4% (255/338) of arrests without ROSC. Among arrests with ROSC, 45.0% (131/291) had epinephrine administered, compared with 82.1% (330/402) of arrests without ROSC.

In our multivariable logistic regression model (Table 3), the odds of ROSC were higher among adolescents compared to infants (aOR, 3.25 [95% CI, 1.25–8.79]) and witnessed arrests compared to those unwitnessed (aOR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.03–2.45]). Compared to arrests without bystander CPR, those receiving bystander CPR had 2.83 times higher odds of ROSC (95% CI, 1.67–4.96). Discussion

In this national evaluation of drowning-related pOHCAs utilizing 2023 NEMSIS data, we identified that these events commonly involved males and toddlers, consistent with prior studies leveraging downstream epidemiologic datasets.2, 6, 25, 26 Also consistent with prior studies,4, 16, 27, 28, 29 EMS clinicians frequently responded to drowning-related pOHCAs at private residences and recreational settings. Drowning-related pOHCA dispatches demonstrated a temporal distribution, more often occurring on afternoons, weekends, and during the summer. Furthermore, arrests were frequently unwitnessed, particularly among infants and toddlers. Approximately four in five drowning-related pOHCAs received bystander CPR and few identified arrest rhythms were shockable. ROSC was documented in two out of five arrests, with higher proportions seen among older age groups. In multivariable regression modeling, older age, bystander CPR, and witnessed arrests were associated with higher odds of ROSC. Collectively, these findings highlight age-based differences in setting, timing, and pre-EMS factors, with implications for both prevention strategies and early resuscitation.

In our study, EMS clinicians commonly responded to drowning-related pOHCAs at private residences and recreational settings, with recreational settings accounting for an increasing proportion of these responses among older children. These location differences paralleled age-based variations we observed in unwitnessed arrest status. Infants (76%) and toddlers (81%) demonstrated the highest proportions of unwitnessed drowning-related arrests, consistent with prior studies describing unwitnessed drowning incidents in this age group.4, 28, 29 In contrast, only approximately half of our adolescent arrests were unwitnessed (54%), which may reflect adolescent engagement in public or group water-based recreational activities, where other individuals may be present.4, 16, 27, 28, 29

Overall, these age-based differences highlight the importance of supporting prevention strategies tailored to both age- and development-related drowning risks.4, 28 Infants and toddlers are developmentally curious, only beginning to develop motor skills for swimming, and often unaware of potential water hazards,4 underscoring the importance of environmental and structural safeguards. These include eliminating or emptying small water hazards (e.g., buckets of water, filled bathtubs) and installing pool fencing and gates to prevent drowning. To supplement structural barriers, caregivers should focus on maintaining close proximity, sustained attention, and continuous supervision of infants and young children who are in or around standing bodies of water.30

In contrast, prevention strategies for school-aged children and adolescents can focus on water safety proficiency and safe behaviors,4, 31 including swimming in designated areas, using life jackets or flotation devices, and avoidance of alcohol or other impairing substances while engaging in water-related activities. Though our study did not directly assess these exposures, these recommendations are supported by professional policy guidelines,4 and reflect ongoing areas of focus in drowning prevention research.

Our data demonstrated both seasonal and temporal patterns in EMS dispatches to drowning-related pOHCAs, with most events occurring during the summer months, on weekends, and in the afternoon and early evening hours. These findings are consistent with prior pediatric drowning studies and likely reflect children’s increased exposure to bodies of water and water-related recreational activities during these times.4, 28, 29, 32 Prevention strategies should incorporate these temporal factors in both messaging and resource allocation, especially in public recreation settings.4 Of note, infants were the age group with the highest proportion of EMS dispatches during the overnight hours, reinforcing the importance of passive structural safeguards to protect this population.

In our cohort, approximately four in five (81%) drowning-related pOHCAs received CPR before EMS arrival. As mortality from drowning is often driven by respiratory failure after submersion, which can progress to hypoxemia-associated cardiac arrest,9, 10 early ventilation and CPR are critical to patient survival.10, 13, 15, 33, 34 In the existing literature, bystander CPR is associated with improved outcomes in drowning-related arrests,12, 13, 16 though bystanders may not consistently initiate CPR35 or provide CPR with rescue breathing.36, 37 Although our study was not optimized to evaluate bystander CPR on outcomes, we noted that infants (29%) and adolescents (34%) demonstrated the highest proportions not receiving bystander CPR within age groups. This may highlight an opportunity to further strengthen bystander education and preparedness to resuscitate children of all ages following drowning.

Approximately two in five (42%) arrests in our study were documented to achieve ROSC during the EMS encounter. In the pOHCA literature, ROSC has been described in approximately 20–36% of cases,38, 39, 40 with some studies demonstrating slightly higher ROSC rates among drowning-related arrests.41, 42 In a recent prehospital drowning study by Popp et al., the authors identified ROSC in 37% of their study cohort, similar to our study findings.43 Shockable arrests were uncommon in our cohort (5%), likely reflecting both the relatively low occurrence of cardiac arrhythmias among pediatric patients11, 12, 14 and hypoxia-based mechanism of most drowning-related arrests. Adolescents demonstrated a slightly higher proportion of shockable arrests (11%), consistent with prior studies.44, 45 Overall, these findings reinforce current resuscitation guidelines, which prioritize early airway management and CPR as priority interventions.46, 47 AED use is supported by these guidelines as adjunctive care, if available, and can potentially guide CPR delivery,48, 49 even in cases with non-shockable rhythms.

In exploratory multivariable regression modeling, we identified older age, witnessed arrests, and bystander CPR with higher odds of ROSC in EMS-attended drowning-related pOHCA. Overall, our findings are consistent with prior studies evaluating outcomes in drowning-related arrests12, 13, 16 and reinforce the evidence supporting the importance of prompt drowning recognition and resuscitation.7, 8 In our cohort, some of the associations we observed may reflect differences in drowning mechanisms or timing of drowning recognition. For example, the lower odds of ROSC observed with epinephrine administration may reflect greater illness or injury severity, such as prolonged submersion or delayed resuscitation before EMS arrival. Some of these variables are incompletely captured within NEMSIS, and further work is needed to comprehensively delineate the relative effects of these prehospital factors on patient outcomes. The use of standardized reporting frameworks like the Utstein template in such work are critical for rigorous outcome evaluation and future comparability across studies.50

Limitations

Not all EMS agencies participate in NEMSIS. Therefore, our findings may not reflect all pediatric drowning events, such as those not resulting in EMS activation or those managed entirely within the healthcare system. However, the NEMSIS database is a comprehensive registry and represents >90% of activations in the US. Cardiac arrest can be defined with alternative variable definitions within NEMSIS,51, 52 and we utilized standard NEMSIS definitions for cardiac arrest and cardiac arrest etiology to reduce data variability that may be observed with use of other definitions. Cardiac arrest etiology is determined by EMS clinician assessment based on available scene information and may be subject to misclassification. Incomplete documentation by EMS clinicians could affect our findings, although missing data in our study was low. Although NEMSIS employs standardized data definitions which participating EMS agencies follow, variability in documentation and interpretation of variables across EMS agencies may affect data consistency. Race and ethnicity were not included in our analysis due to limitations in data completeness and reliability within NEMSIS, representing an important area for future investigation given known disparities in drowning.6, 27

As NEMSIS does not reliably capture submersion duration or the timing of bystander actions, these clinically important variables could not be incorporated into our exploratory regression model. Additionally, NEMSIS is an administrative database not linked to healthcare facility data; we were unable to analyze long-term patient outcomes, including survival to hospital discharge and neurologic sequelae. Similarly, some pre-event scene details and risk factors may be better captured in other data sources.53, 54 Despite these limitations, our study findings identify unique epidemiological characteristics of drowning-related pOHCAs managed by EMS clinicians early in the chain of care.

Conclusion

Drowning-related pOHCAs managed by EMS clinicians often involve young children, with age-based variations observed in both scene characteristics and factors occurring prior to EMS arrival. Prevention strategies should therefore incorporate temporal, age-, and development-specific considerations. Both the public and EMS clinicians should be cognizant of current care guidelines for presumed child drowning-related cardiac arrests, which emphasize airway and breathing management concurrently with chest compressions. Further investigation into the relationship between observed prehospital factors and downstream consequences, including ROSC, survival, and neurologic outcomes, remains a critical area of future research. Given the benefits of prevention and early intervention, opportunities exist to strengthen the Drowning Chain of Survival for children across all age groups.
