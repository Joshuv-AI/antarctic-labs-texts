Trends and Racial and Ethnic Disparities in Maternal Cardiovascular Health in California
Authors: Elleni M. Hailu, Suzan L. Carmichael, Jonathan M. Snowden, Audrey Lyndon, Elliott Main, Mahasin S. Mujahid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo address the maternal health crisis in the United States, it is important to closely examine the epidemiologic trends of and racial and ethnic disparities in maternal cardiovascular health.MethodsWe used statewide birth hospitalization and vital statistics records from California (2007–2019; N=6 117 886) to examine the prevalence, trends, and racial and ethnic disparities of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy overall (ie, chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension/preeclampsia, or eclampsia), and chronic hypertension and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia separately. We also examined a construct indicating ideal cardiovascular health before and during pregnancy using the subset of data with information available on clinical cardiovascular health indicators (ie, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, body mass index [kg/m2], and gestational weight gain adjusted for gestational age; N=5 636 185). To quantify racial and ethnic disparities, we used modified Poisson regression models (with robust standard errors), controlling for sociodemographic characteristics, pregnancy‐related factors, and year fixed effects.ResultsThe age‐standardized prevalence of adverse outcomes worsened over time for all racial and ethnic groups. Compared with non‐Hispanic White (White) individuals, non‐Hispanic Black mothers had higher risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (risk ratio [RR], 1.60 [95% CI, 1.59–1.62]), including chronic hypertension (RR, 2.34 [95% CI, 2.29–2.38]) and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia (RR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.49–1.52]). American Indian/Alaska Native mothers were less likely to have ideal cardiovascular health both before (RR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.65–0.67]) and during pregnancy (RR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.78–0.81]) compared with White mothers. These differences persisted throughout the study period.ConclusionsOur findings reveal stark racial and ethnic disparities in maternal cardiovascular health, highlighting the urgent need to investigate and address their structural determinants.

The cardiovascular health (CVH) of a person giving birth is a crucial indicator of their pregnancy‐related outcomes, their long‐term well‐being, and the health of their child. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Cardiovascular conditions are the leading causes of maternal deaths in the United States, accounting for 33.6% of all pregnancy‐related deaths between 2007 and 2016. 7 Approximately 8% of all maternal deaths during the same period were also attributed to pregnancy‐related hypertension alone, a key cardiovascular disease risk factor. 7 , 8 In addition to the increased risk of maternal morbidity and mortality, individuals whose pregnancies are complicated by hypertension are at a much higher risk of adverse cardiovascular events (eg, myocardial infarction and stroke) later in their lives than those with normotensive pregnancies. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Maternal cardiometabolic conditions, including hypertensive disorders, also have serious implications for the survival and development of infants. 3 , 4 , 6 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 In recognition of its lasting consequences, the American Heart Association and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists have highlighted CVH among reproductive‐age and pregnant women as an important condition that needs close monitoring and attention. 17 As such, evaluating prepregnancy and gestational CVH, for example, on the basis of a combination of risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes, occurring before and during pregnancy, can provide valuable insights into preventive strategies that improve both life‐course and intergenerational outcomes.

Addressing maternal CVH is particularly important within the context of the maternal health crisis in the United States, which is characterized by worsening rates of and persistent racial and ethnic disparities in adverse pregnancy outcomes. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Black individuals' risk of maternal death is 3 to 4 times higher, and their risk of life‐threatening pregnancy‐related complications is 2 to 3 times that of White individuals. 23 , 24 , 25 Even though the rate of these adverse maternal health outcomes has been increasing for all racial and ethnic groups, estimates show that increases are disproportionately higher among Black and American Indian/Alaska Native populations. 19 , 26 , 27 Inequities in maternal morbidity and mortality are also mirrored in the risk of their common causes, including in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) and other cardiometabolic conditions. Early evidence indicates that compared with other racial and ethnic groups, Black women are more likely to enter pregnancy with nonoptimal CVH and to develop HDP. 28 , 29 , 30 Consequently, they are at a higher risk of acquiring their associated complications. Cardiovascular conditions and HDP also account for a significantly higher proportion of maternal deaths among Black than White women. 7 However, there is limited work that comprehensively assesses maternal CVH indicators, quantifies their racial and ethnic inequities, and surveils their change over time. This is urgently needed to identify points of intervention and population subgroups that may be more vulnerable to the adverse implications of cardiovascular conditions.

To fill these gaps, using statewide data from hospital discharge and vital statistics records in California between 2007 and 2019, this study examined the prevalence, trends, and racial and ethnic disparities of pregnancy‐related cardiometabolic health indicators, including in well‐established constructs (ie, HDP and its subtypes) and more novel CVH metrics (ie, ideal prepregnancy and gestational CVH adapted from the American Heart Association's Life's Simple 7 index). We compare patterns across these outcomes to systematically evaluate cardiometabolic risk in mothers.

Methods

Data Access

Investigators trained in human subject research may direct requests to obtain data used in this study to the California Department of Health Care Access and Information.

Study Population

Data were from all births (live births and stillbirths) delivered at 20 weeks of gestation or later in California between 2007 and 2019 and were obtained from the California Department of Health Care Access and Information. The data included birth hospitalization discharge files linked with vital statistics records (birth and fetal death certificates). Data linkage was successful in ≈96% of the births. 31 In the case of twin/multiple births, we kept the first recorded birth to avoid duplicates. We then removed observations with missing information on the study outcomes and covariates (maternal age, race and ethnicity, education, insurance payer, parity, multiple births), which resulted in an analytic sample of 6 117 886 observations for our examination of HDP and its subtypes. For our analyses evaluating ideal CVH before and during gestation (based on a metric adapted from the American Heart Association), we restricted this sample to include births with complete information on the ideal CVH metric components, which included 5 636 185 observations (Figure S1). This study was approved by the State of California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the institutional review board of Stanford University. The study was deemed exempt from needing to obtain participant informed consent. The study followed the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline for cross‐sectional studies.

Study Outcomes

We evaluated CVH indicators before and during pregnancy using data from both vital statistics records and International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes from birth hospitalization discharge files (Tables S1 and S2, Figure S2). We examined HDP, defined based on the diagnosis of gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia, or chronic hypertension with or without superimposed preeclampsia. We also separately assessed chronic hypertension and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia. Ideal CVH before and during pregnancy was evaluated using a modified metric from the American Heart Association's Life's Simple 7 index, which was designed for CVH promotion and disease prevention, and has been widely applied to monitor cardiovascular disease risk in clinical and research settings. 32 Informed by prior literature, this metric was adapted to accommodate data availability from vital statistics and birth hospital discharge records and included 4 indicators of optimal CVH. 3 , 4 , 30 Individuals were categorized as having ideal prepregnancy CVH if, before pregnancy, they did not have hypertension (ie, no chronic hypertension), did not have diabetes, did not smoke, and had a body mass index of <25 kg/m2. Individuals were categorized as having ideal gestational CVH if they did not have gestational hypertension/preeclampsia, did not have gestational diabetes, did not smoke during pregnancy, and were not in the high weight gain for gestational age category according to the Institute of Medicine's 2009 weight gain guidelines for pregnant women. 33 We used previously validated charts to determine weight gain for gestational age categories, where we first obtained a Z score of absolute pregnancy weight gain based on prepregnancy body mass index categories (underweight to obesity class 3) and gestational age at delivery, and subsequently predicted weight gain at 40 weeks of gestation using these weight‐gain Z scores according to the aforementioned charts. 34 , 35

Race and Ethnicity

Self‐reported maternal race and ethnicity was determined from vital records and included non‐Hispanic White (hereafter White), non‐Hispanic Black (hereafter Black), Hispanic/Latinx, Asian/Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native, and “Other” categories. The Other race and ethnicity category comprised individuals who self‐identified as Other. We assess race and ethnicity as a sociopolitical category that indicates marginalization due to racism. 36 , 37

Covariates

Based on prior literature, we adjusted our regression models for the following strong risk factors of adverse maternal health outcomes: maternal age (<20, 20–34, >34 years), education (high school or less, some college, college graduate, missing/unknown), insurance/hospital payer information (private, public, uninsured/other/unknown), parity (primiparous, multiparous), and multiple births (singleton, multiples) as reported in vital statistics records.

Statistical Analysis

In descriptive analyses, we first determined the distribution of population characteristics in the overall sample and by race and ethnicity. We calculated and visualized the annual age‐standardized prevalence of all outcomes per 1000 births within each racial and ethnic group, using rolling averages to smooth over yearly fluctuations in the occurrences of the outcomes. We then determined the average annual percent change (AAPC) in age‐standardized prevalence of each outcome (per 1000 births) for each racial and ethnic group using the National Cancer Institute's Joinpoint Trend Analysis Software. 38 , 39 AAPC is a weighted average of annual percent changes obtained from underlying segmented log‐linear regression model coefficients over a fixed time interval. For our analyses examining racial and ethnic disparities, we used modified Poisson regression models with robust standard errors to estimate risk ratios (RRs) and 95% CIs of our outcomes comparing racially marginalized individuals (Black, Hispanic/Latinx, Asian/Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Other) to White individuals. We use White individuals as the reference to capture racialized systems of oppression that impact the health outcomes of minoritized racial groups. 40 All regression models were adjusted for maternal age, education, insurance, parity, and multiple births, and included fixed effects for the year of birth hospitalization. In sensitivity analysis, we evaluated the age‐standardized prevalence of individual ideal CVH components both before and during pregnancy. We also examined changes in racial and ethnic disparities by estimating and visualizing RRs of each outcome within each time period specified for calculating rolling average prevalence. Analyses were conducted using Stata/IC, version 15.1 (StataCorp LLC).

In the overall sample, throughout the study period, 8.9% of individuals had HDP, 2.1% had chronic hypertension, and 7.6% developed gestational hypertension/preeclampsia (Table 1). The prevalence of each of these outcomes was the highest among Black individuals (HDP, 14.3%; chronic hypertension, 4.7%; gestational hypertension/preeclampsia, 11.5%) compared with all other racial and ethnic groups. American Indian/Alaska Native individuals had the next highest prevalence of HDP (12.2%), chronic hypertension (3.4%), and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia (10.2%). Asian/Pacific Islander individuals had the lowest prevalence of HDP (7.2%) and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia (6.0%), while Hispanic/Latinx individuals had the lowest prevalence of chronic hypertension (1.8%).

Less than half (48.7%) of the overall sample had ideal prepregnancy CVH, and 43% of all individuals had ideal gestational CVH (Table 2). Both prepregnancy and gestational ideal CVH were lowest among American Indian/Alaska Native individuals; only 32.8% and 30.8% of individuals in this group had ideal CVH before and during pregnancy, respectively (Table 2). Following American Indian/Alaska Native individuals, Black individuals had the next lowest prevalence of ideal prepregnancy (39.1%) and gestational (36.2%) CVH. Asian/Pacific Islander individuals had the highest prevalence of ideal prepregnancy (70.7%) and gestational (49.4%) CVH compared with all other racial and ethnic groups.

Changes in Prevalence Over Time

For each racial and ethnic group examined, the age‐standardized prevalence of HDP, chronic hypertension, and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia increased across study years, with Black individuals consistently experiencing the highest prevalence of these outcomes compared with all other racial and ethnic groups (Figures 1, 2 through 3). Age‐standardized AAPCs for HDP, chronic hypertension, and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia were statistically significant for all racial and ethnic groups (Table 3). The highest increases in age‐standardized prevalence of these outcomes were seen among those who self‐identified as Other race and ethnicity (AAPC for HDP, 7.52% [95% CI, 5.43%–9.64%]; AAPC for chronic hypertension, 9.88% [95% CI, 2.15%–18.17%]; and AAPC for gestational hypertension/preeclampsia, 7.38% [95% CI, 3.45%–11.41%]).

Throughout the study period, the age‐standardized prevalence of ideal prepregnancy CVH declined for all racial and ethnic groups while the prevalence of ideal gestational CVH increased slightly, with American Indian/Alaska Native individuals consistently experiencing the lowest prevalence of ideal CVH across all study years, compared with other racial and ethnic groups (Figures 4 and 5). Age‐standardized AAPCs indicated that while all racial and ethnic groups experienced statistically significant declines in ideal prepregnancy CVH, those in the Other race and ethnicity category were seen to have the largest decline (AAPC, −2.48% [95% CI, −4.20% to −0.75%]), which was similar in magnitude as the decline experienced by Hispanic/Latinx individuals (AAPC, −2.42% [95% CI, −2.48% to −2.35%]). AAPCs for ideal gestational CVH were not statistically significant for most racial and ethnic groups, except for White individuals, who experienced small increases (AAPC, 0.51% [95% CI, 0.36%–0.63%]) and Hispanic/Latinx individuals, who experienced a small decrease (AAPC, −0.39% [95% CI, −0.54% to −0.26%]). Our sensitivity analyses examining components of ideal CVH revealed slight decreases in smoking both before and during pregnancy and similarly small declines in high gestational weight gain throughout study years for all racial and ethnic groups (Figures S3 and S4).

American Indian/Alaska Native individuals had the next elevated risk of these outcomes compared with White individuals (HDP: RR, 1.41 [95% CI, 1.37–1.46]; chronic hypertension: RR, 1.69 [95% CI, 1.59–1.81]; gestational hypertension/preeclampsia: RR, 1.39 [95% CI, 1.34–1.44]). In comparison with White mothers, Asian/Pacific Islander individuals had lower risk of HDP (RR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.78–0.80]), chronic hypertension (RR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.91–0.94]), and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia (RR, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.77–0.79]). While Hispanic/Latinx individuals also had lower risk of chronic hypertension compared with White individuals (RR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.91–0.93]), those self‐identifying as Other had higher risk (RR,1.22 [95% CI, 1.05–1.43]).

American Indian/Alaska Native individuals were less likely to have ideal CVH both before (RR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.65–0.67]) and during pregnancy (RR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.78–0.81]) than White individuals. Following the risk for American Indian/Alaska Native mothers, Black mothers were also less likely to have ideal prepregnancy (RR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.77–0.77]) and gestational (RR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.94–0.95]) CVH. Compared with White individuals, Hispanic/Latinx individuals and individuals who self‐identified as Other race and ethnicity were also less likely to have ideal CVH before pregnancy (Hispanic/Latinx: RR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.81–0.81]; Other: RR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92–0.97]) and were more likely to have ideal CVH during pregnancy (Hispanic/Latinx: RR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.14–1.14]; Other: RR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.07–1.14]). On the other hand, Asian/Pacific Islander individuals were more likely to have ideal CVH both before (RR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.24–1.24]) and during pregnancy (RR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.24–1.25]) than White individuals.

Changes in Racial and Ethnic Disparities Over Time

Although not always consistent, estimates of all adverse outcomes comparing Black and American Indian/Alaska Native individuals with White individuals appeared to be elevated throughout the years examined, with the exception of chronic hypertension, in which RRs seemed to slightly decrease during the study period, albeit remaining significantly >1 (Figures S5 through S9). Estimates comparing Hispanic/Latinx and Asian/Pacific Islander mothers with White mothers for HDP and its subtypes generally remained below and around 1.

Hispanic/Latinx individuals experienced worsened ideal prepregnancy CVH compared with White individuals, mirroring the worsening trend observed when comparing Black with White mothers. While both Hispanic/Latinx and Asian/Pacific Islander individuals were more likely to have ideal gestational CVH compared with White individuals, differences appeared to diminish throughout study years. Period‐specific RRs for those who self‐identified as Other race and ethnicity were highly imprecise due to few observations.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence, trends, and racial and ethnic disparities of multiple indicators of maternal CVH, including HDP and its subtypes (chronic hypertension and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia) and ideal prepregnancy and gestational CVH. We found that the prevalence of almost all adverse outcomes worsened over time for all racial and ethnic groups, except for ideal gestational CVH, in which slight improvements were observed. Black individuals had the highest risk of all pregnancy‐related hypertension outcomes compared with White individuals, while American Indian/Alaska Native individuals had the highest risk of nonoptimal prepregnancy and gestational CVH. Our findings underscore the need to prioritize maternal CVH as a key point of intervention to effectively address racial and ethnic inequities in pregnancy‐related complications and to improve the health of mothers overall.

Our results documenting worsening maternal CVH for all racial and ethnic groups, apart from ideal CVH during pregnancy, are consistent with recent studies. 30 , 41 , 42 , 43 For example, using national vital statistics data, Bruno et al documented that, on average, the prevalence of HDP between 1989 and 2020 increased by 3.6% annually. 41 Relatedly, Wang et al reported that over 8 years, optimal prepregnancy CVH prevalence fell by 10.4%. 30 Other studies have also found comparable results for similar outcomes. 42 , 43 However, existing work has primarily examined the prevalence of maternal cardiometabolic indicators separately and mostly during short time periods. Moreover, investigations of racial and ethnic differences across these outcomes and how they may change over time remain scarce. A novel strength of our study is leveraging statewide hospitalization and vital statistics data across multiple years to comprehensively assess several CVH indicators both before and during pregnancy and to document stark racial and ethnic inequities in each of the outcomes examined.

In our study, the prevalence of HDP and its subtypes were the highest among Black mothers. These patterns remained consistent across study years, as Black individuals had persistently worse outcomes in these measures than other racial and ethnic groups. RRs comparing Black with White individuals for overall HDP, chronic hypertension, and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia were also higher than RRs for other racial and ethnic groups. The starkest Black–White differences in risk were observed for chronic hypertension, where Black individuals had >2 times the risk of White individuals, while Hispanic/Latinx and Asian/Pacific Islander individuals were found less likely to have chronic hypertension compared with White individuals. This is consistent with prior studies that have documented increased likelihood of HDP among Black women. 29 , 44 , 45 , 46 Given the long‐lasting implications of pregnancy‐related hypertensive disorders, which have been found to increase the risk of chronic hypertension, 47 diabetes, 48 stroke, 12 , 49 heart failure, 11 and other cardiovascular outcomes 50 long after childbirth, our results also provide additional context around the increased risk of cardiovascular diseases among nonpregnant Black women. Estimates indicate that Black women have higher risk of cardiovascular disease morbidity and mortality than any other racial and ethnic group, 51 , 52 and may be especially at risk during their childbearing years. 53 Taken together, our findings highlight the need to pay special attention to the health of pregnant and reproductive‐age Black women.

We found that American Indian/Alaska Native women had the next highest risk for HDP and its subtypes and the lowest prevalence of ideal prepregnancy and gestational CVH than any other racial and ethnic group. Compared with White individuals, American Indian/Alaska Native individuals also had the lowest likelihood of ideal prepregnancy and gestational CVH. Although limited in number, existing work documenting a greater burden of poor cardiometabolic conditions among American Indian/Alaska Native versus White women corroborates our results. 54 In our study, Black mothers had the next lowest likelihood of ideal prepregnancy and gestational CVH. Throughout the study period, American Indian/Alaska Native and Black individuals also had consistently lower prevalence of ideal CVH before and during pregnancy, while those who self‐identified as Other and Hispanic/Latinx experienced slightly higher declines in ideal prepregnancy CVH prevalence. The ideal CVH metric as a health‐promoting construct has been widely used in evaluating heart‐healthy factors and in preventing clinical manifestations of cardiovascular disease among nonpregnant individuals. 32 However, it has rarely been applied to monitor the well‐being of pregnant individuals and potential racial and ethnic inequities in this population. The few existing studies that have adapted this construct have found substantial geographic and population differences. 30 , 43 , 55 Our results add to this growing evidence and point to the need for interventions that promote CVH among racially marginalized individuals to prevent more adverse outcomes that may follow.

In this study, while the prevalence of ideal prepregnancy CVH declined across the time period examined, we saw slight improvements in ideal gestational CVH for most racial and ethnic groups. This finding contrasts with a prior study incorporating survey and clinical examination‐based components of the Life's Simple 7 index, which found that between 1999 and 2014, as few as 4.6% to 8.5% of pregnant National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey participants had optimal CVH. 43 It is likely that our ideal CVH metric, constructed from relevant data available within hospitalization and vital statistics records, does not capture the full extent of cardiometabolic risk profiles, particularly on the basis of physical activity, diet, and cholesterol levels. This may mask further racial and ethnic inequities that may exist. The slight increases observed could also be due to increased availability of prenatal CVH counseling in more recent years due to quality improvement initiatives unique to California, such as the Cardiovascular Disease in Pregnancy and Postpartum Task Force directed by the California Maternal Quality Care Collaborative. 56 This may be particularly true among those with poor prepregnancy CVH, who were found to have an increased likelihood of receiving postpartum CVH counseling in a prior study. 57 This is also consistent with studies documenting improvements in CVH‐relevant behaviors during pregnancy. 58 , 59 In sensitivity analyses, we found small improvements in gestational weight gain, along with a reduction in smoking during pregnancy, which may also explain the slight increases in ideal gestational CVH. Despite these results, the stark racial and ethnic disparities we found in this outcome remain concerning.

The persistent racial and ethnic disparities we observed across a range of outcomes are likely driven by structural racism, which determines the quality of the neighborhood environments in which individuals grow and live and their lifetime access to socioeconomic opportunities and psychosocial resources, essential in maintaining optimal CVH both before and during pregnancy. 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 Structural racism limits the availability of health‐promoting amenities, goods, and services that are necessary for preventing cardiovascular disease. 70 , 71 It also heightens chronic stress, which increases physiological wear and tear and subsequent risk of cardiometabolic dysfunction. 72 , 73 , 74 Thus, it is important that future studies specifically explicate structural forces behind the racial and ethnic disparities that we observed. Given the especially dire outcomes documented, it is particularly imperative that studies investigate how structural racism impacts maternal CVH among Black and American/Alaska Native individuals, who have endured centuries of extreme policies and practices. It is also important to note that while quantifying racial and ethnic inequities is a crucial step toward improving health outcomes, not addressing race as a sociopolitical category may risk reifying racism by unintentionally attributing differences to individual characteristics. 75 , 76

This study has several limitations worth noting. First, we obtained our data from hospital discharge and vital statistics records, which lack complete information on crucial elements of the Life's Simple 7 ideal CVH construct (eg, diet and physical activity). This is additionally important given that prepregnancy body mass index and gestational weight gain had the most contribution in determining ideal prepregnancy and gestational CVH compared with other components of the construct. It is also possible that reporting errors and changes in the definition of outcomes may have led to misclassification of CVH status. 77 Individuals who may have had shorter gestation due to adverse pregnancy outcomes (eg, delivered preterm) may also not have had sufficient time under observation for the development of the conditions examined. This could potentially underestimate the total burden of and racial and ethnic inequities in nonoptimal outcomes. The adoption of different clinical strategies, such as use of aspirin for the prevention of severe pregnancy‐related hypertension, may also have influenced our findings. 78 However, our results across the various outcomes examined are comparable with each other and with results of prior studies.

Additionally, while the Life's Simple 7 ideal CVH metric was originally constructed for use in nonpregnant populations, based on prior literature, we use pregnancy‐specific indicators to evaluate ideal gestational CVH (eg, absence of gestational hypertension/preeclampsia). 4 , 30 , 55 This limits our metric's representativeness in fully reflecting CVH in pregnancy, and guidelines that specifically focus on CVH promotion among pregnant individuals are needed. For example, while the pathophysiology of preeclampsia remains understudied, it is possible that it may be related to various environmental, clinical, and biological factors unique to a given pregnancy. 79 There is also a lack of consensus on whether preeclampsia is an independent driver or an early indicator of future cardiovascular dysfunction. 80 As such, additional research is needed to disentangle its contribution to CVH during pregnancy and beyond. Relatedly, research that closely investigates how CVH trajectories before and during pregnancy may influence racial and ethnic disparities in the risk of other adverse pregnancy outcomes (eg, preterm birth) is also warranted. Furthermore, while our study documents alarming racial and ethnic inequities across several cardiometabolic risk indicators, we do not examine the structural determinants of these differences. It is also likely that there are important within‐group variations in CVH across racial and ethnic groups by factors such as socioeconomic status and nativity. 44 , 81 It is crucial that future studies assess these heterogeneities and use intersectional approaches in uncovering drivers of disparities. 82

In summary, by leveraging comprehensive statewide data, our study adds to the mounting evidence on the need for prevention efforts promoting CVH both before and during pregnancy. The increasing trends we observed for almost all adverse maternal CVH indicators in this study are a cause for concern, as they may indicate potential future increases in the prevalence of more serious cardiovascular diseases among mothers, and provide further insights into the maternal health crisis across the nation. The persistent racial and ethnic gaps we found also highlight the urgent imperative to address CVH among racially marginalized individuals as a key preventive measure for eliminating stark racial and ethnic disparities in pregnancy‐related complications and subsequent long‐term health outcomes. They underscore the need to prioritize preconception interventions and address structural determinants of health to effectively improve maternal cardiovascular well‐being and ultimately ameliorate racial and ethnic inequities. 83
