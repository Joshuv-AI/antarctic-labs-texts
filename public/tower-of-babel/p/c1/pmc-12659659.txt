Bridging the Nervous-Endocrine System and Immune Response, in Human Chagas Disease Pathology
Authors: Florencia Belén González, Brenda Dinatale, Wilson Savino, Oscar Adelmo Bottasso, Ana Rosa Pérez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic Chagas disease can affect multiple organs, most notably the heart and gastrointestinal tract, and in some cases, the nervous system. However, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of this parasitic infection remain incompletely understood.SummaryEvidence from studies in both mice with acute Trypanosoma cruzi (T. cruzi) infection and in patients with Chagas disease has revealed a range of immune-neuroendocrine alterations and metabolic disruptions. In this review, we highlight key findings in human Chagas disease related to these abnormalities and discuss their potential contributions to disease pathogenesis.Key MessagesIn the context of chronic Chagas disease, the neuroendocrine-immune axis operates as a dynamic interface, integrating systemic immune-endocrine processes with localized responses in the central nervous system (CNS), with each component influencing disease advancement and organ-specific pathology through distinct yet interconnected mechanisms.

Introduction

Chagas disease, caused by the parasite Trypanosoma cruzi (T. cruzi), is one of the parasitic diseases with huge impact on public health in Latin America. In recent decades, globalization and the continuous migration of individuals between endemic and non-endemic areas have contributed to the spread of this disease to other regions worldwide. The most recent estimates indicate that approximately 7 million people are currently infected, with an average of 28,000 new cases and more than 10,000 deaths reported annually. Additionally, nearly 90 million individuals are at risk of contracting the infection [1].

This trypanosomiasis begins with an initial acute phase, which generally lasts 2 months (Fig. 1). The symptomatology during this phase can range from being asymptomatic to, in a smaller proportion of cases, presenting with overt symptoms [2]. During this phase, parasites can be detected in the circulation. The immune response at this stage relies on the coordinated action of specific antibodies capable of lysing the circulating trypomastigote form, IFN-γ-activated macrophages that destroy intracellular replicative amastigotes, and, later, cytotoxic T lymphocytes that eliminate infected cells. The infection triggers a persistent inflammatory response that can progressively damage multiple organs – particularly the heart and gastrointestinal tract – and, in some cases, the nervous system.

Chagas disease and nervous system involvement.Acute phase courses with patent parasitemia, and T. cruzi control is mediated by macrophages, antibodies, and T cells. During this phase, patients may remain asymptomatic or present with a wide range of clinical and neurological manifestations. During the chronic phase, 20–30% of patients developed chronic Chagas myocarditis (CCC). Cognitive deficits have been reported in patients with chronic Chagas disease, regardless of whether they have developed CCC or not. Clinical and neuro-immune manifestations in acute, chronic, and reactivated T. cruzi Infection during HIV co-infection. Main clinical manifestations and pathological processes during reactivation in patients co-infected with HIV may include fever, myocarditis, and widespread inflammation, while nervous system involvement often manifests as meningoencephalitis, neurocognitive deficits, and other CNS disorders. The immunosuppression caused by HIV co-infection facilitates parasite persistence, with parasite detection in cerebrospinal fluid and amastigote nests in the brain and dissemination, increasing the risk of nervous system invasion and severe neurological outcomes, often accompanied by myocarditis.

After the disappearance of the parasite from the bloodstream, a large proportion of individuals may remain asymptomatic for the rest of their lives, whereas approximately 20–40% of infected individuals progress to a symptomatic chronic phase, with chronic Chagas Cardiomyopathy (CCC) being the most common clinical manifestation [2] (Fig. 1). The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of CCC are not yet fully understood. Two main pathogenic processes have been proposed: 1# myocardial damage resulting from localized inflammatory responses due to the persistence of parasitic niches within cardiac fibers; 2# tissue injury driven by a dysregulated immune response. Both mechanisms are believed to contribute to the progressive cardiac dysfunction observed in CCC [3]. In this context, earlier studies from our group conducted in patients with CCC demonstrated elevated levels of pro-inflammatory mediators alongside a dysregulated activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, as ascertained by reduced levels of cortisol and dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)-sulfate (DHEA-S). These findings suggest that the persistence of a pro-inflammatory milieu, in combination with an impaired endocrine anti-inflammatory response, may contribute to the myocardial damage seen in CCC patients [4, 5].

The neuroendocrine and immune systems are tightly interconnected through cytokines, hormones, neuropeptides, neurotransmitters, and various endocrine and metabolic factors, which operate via systemic, paracrine, and even autocrine pathways to maintain host homeostasis, ensure protection against infectious agents, and limit tissue damage. Under acute conditions, this integrated response is typically resolved once physiological balance is restored. However, in the context of chronic infectious stress, dysregulated interaction may occur, often with detrimental host consequences. Given the interdependent nature of these systems, immune responses to infection can induce significant endocrine and metabolic alterations. Thus, the integrated signaling network among the immune, endocrine, and nervous systems optimizes the detection of both intrinsic and extrinsic injury-associated signals, allowing the orchestration of a proper adaptive systemic response [6].

A well-characterized example of this ancient interplay is the physiological stress response, which is primarily driven by neural stimuli and peripherally induced pro-inflammatory cytokines. These signals activate the HPA axis, resulting in the release of cortisol (corticosterone in rodents) by the adrenal glands. Cortisol enhances glucose availability to meet the metabolic demands of stress and plays a critical role in modulating the immune response by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and promoting anti-inflammatory pathways [6, 7].

However, during chronic infections such as Chagas disease, persistent immune activation can disrupt the mechanisms bridging the neuro-immune and immuno-endocrine pathways. In such contexts, these alterations likely impair the host’s ability to properly regulate inflammation, thereby contributing to ongoing tissue damage and disease progression.

Neuroinflammation and Central Nervous System Involvement in Chagas Disease

The central nervous system (CNS) is also known to be affected during both acute and chronic phases of Chagas disease in humans. Numerous studies in murine models have demonstrated the presence of T. cruzi in various brain regions involving multiple cell types, such as microglia, astrocytes, and neurons [8–10].

Approximately 5–10% of acutely infected individuals may develop meningoencephalitis, often associated with headaches, seizures, lethargy, or mood changes. In addition, patients may present encephalitis characterized by elevated levels of albumin, leukocytes, and trypomastigotes in the cerebrospinal fluid [11]. Neurological symptoms during the acute phase include impaired concentration, muscle abnormalities such as myoclonus, bradykinesia, dyspraxia, general weakness, and speech difficulties. This condition is particularly severe in children under 2 years of age and is frequently fatal when accompanied by myocarditis and heart failure. Seizures and meningoencephalitis have also been observed in a minority of congenital cases [12].

In the chronic phase of Chagas disease, patients may present a range of neurological manifestations, including dementia, anxiety, confusion, memory loss, and various sensory and motor deficits [13, 14]. Cognitive dysfunction in chronically infected patients has been documented through poorer performance on neuropsychological tests such as the Mini-Mental State Examination, the Wechsler Memory Scale, and the WAIS global intelligence test, when compared to healthy controls [15, 16]. Cognitive deficits are consistent with white matter pathology, manifesting as impairments in nonverbal reasoning, information processing, problem-solving, and learning abilities [17]. Neuroimaging studies, including computed tomography scans, have also revealed brain atrophy suggestive of neuronal and glial cell loss [13], which likely contributes to cognitive decline. Similar findings have been observed in chronically infected mice, supporting the translational relevance of these animal models [13]. Notably, cognitive impairment in Chagas disease appears to be independent of the presence or severity of cardiomyopathy, suggesting that mechanisms beyond heart failure – such as chronic inflammation – may contribute directly to the neurological deficits observed [16]. Furthermore, pro-inflammatory pathways, particularly those involving enhanced systemic levels of TNF-α, appear to play a role in neurobehavioral changes. In chronically T. cruzi-infected mice, depressive-like symptoms can be reversed by anti-TNF treatments, indicating the involvement of TNF-mediated neuroinflammation. Other inflammatory mediators induced at brain level but also systemically, such as IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-1β, and nitric oxide (NO) may also contribute to a state of generalized inflammation, potentially disrupting neurotransmitter systems and further exacerbating cognitive and mood disturbances [13, 17–21]. Finally, although detailed data on cognitive impairment stratified by age in Chagas disease are limited, aging itself is a well-established risk factor for cognitive decline. Mechanisms such as oxidative stress, inflammaging, microvascular alterations, and dysregulation of the HPA axis are known to contribute to age-related cognitive deficits [22]. In patients with Chagas disease, these processes may be amplified, accelerating brain aging and increasing susceptibility to cognitive dysfunction in older T. cruzi-infected patients.

Notably, the CNS is a primary site for T. cruzi reactivation in immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with HIV (Fig. 1). Chagas disease reactivation (CDR) is typically associated with severe brain damage, increased IL-17 expression in the CNS, and low peripheral CD4+ T-cell counts [14, 23]. Meningoencephalitis in patients with CDR often coincides with high amounts of trypomastigotes in the cerebrospinal fluid [24]. Cerebral abscesses (“chagomas”) are often described as including macrophages, neutrophils, microglia, astrocytes, and perivascular lymphocytes.

Astrocytes can act both as cellular reservoirs for the parasite and as modulators of the inflammatory microenvironment [25], and may play a key role in the CDR. T. cruzi may persist silently within astrocytes during the chronic phase, even in the presence of locally secreted IFN-γ. Strikingly, rather than controlling the infection, IFN-γ appears to enhance astrocyte infection by the parasite [9]. Furthermore, infected astrocytes also produce TNF-α and NO, which may contribute to tissue damage in the CNS [13]. In the context of HIV co-infection, T. cruzi can coexist with the virus within astrocytes. Although a reduction in HIV replication has been observed, this occurs alongside decreased apoptosis in co-infected astrocytes. These phenomena appear to be mediated by IL-6 and other parasite-derived soluble factors, as observed in vitro. Such circumstances may favor not only the intracellular persistence of both pathogens but also their eventual reactivation under immunosuppressive conditions [26]. These findings suggest that astrocytes are not merely passive targets of infection, but active participants in regulating T. cruzi persistence and the CDR.

In a recent systematic review with a total of 152 articles included (62% case reports or case series), the reported prevalence of co-infection varied widely depending on region and screening context, ranging from 0.1% to 1% in unselected populations, and reaching 26–48% among hospitalized patients with HIV screened for T. cruzi infection. Reactivation occurred predominantly in individuals with CD4 T-cell counts below 200 cells/mm3 (83%). The CDR at the CNS level is commonly evidenced by the development of meningoencephalitis and/or cerebral lesions, which represented 68% of reported cases. Overall mortality among reactivation cases was 59%, increasing to 77% in those with CNS involvement [27]. Early identification of T. cruzi infection in people with HIV is essential because of the systemic and nervous complications associated with CDR, including parasite reactivation. The immune reconstitution syndrome arising following effective antiretroviral therapy also imposes a timely administration of anti-trypanosomal therapy [28].

HPA Axis and GC Bioavailability in Human Chronic Chagas Disease

The activation of the stress system in response to neural or blood-borne signals – such as pro-inflammatory cytokines released during an infectious challenge – triggers the HPA axis, leading to the secretion of adrenal steroids, including glucocorticoids (GCs) and DHEA. During infections, GCs exert anti-inflammatory effects and promote an immune response that is often suboptimal for eliminating intracellular pathogens. In contrast, DHEA counteracts the Th2-skewing effects of GCs while also exerting strong anti-inflammatory actions. Thus, the cortisol/DHEA or cortisol/DHEA-S ratio serves as a valuable indicator of the hormonal modulation of cell-mediated immunity during infections.

As previously reported, studies in patients with chronic Chagas disease have shown elevated systemic levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly in those with clinical manifestations. This persistent inflammatory milieu could disrupt the regulation of the HPA axis, potentially contributing to disease progression. Notably, individuals chronically infected with T. cruzi who develop cardiac involvement – the most common clinical form – often exhibit dysregulation of the HPA axis, reflected by an increased cortisol/DHEA-S ratio [4, 5]. This imbalance is primarily attributed to a marked reduction in DHEA-S levels, despite relatively preserved cortisol concentrations. However, although DHEA – and therefore DHEA-S – typically declines with age, in Chagas disease, this reduction appears to be independent of chronological aging, but linked to elevated circulating cytokines, a pattern also noted in other acute and chronic conditions [29, 30]. Interestingly, asymptomatic patients with chronic Chagas disease tend to exhibit lower systemic levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and a more balanced cortisol/DHEA-S ratio [4]. While the precise mechanisms driving these differences remain unclear, variations in the central and peripheral levels of immune-endocrine mediators are likely involved. Regardless of the cause, an altered cortisol/DHEA or cortisol/DHEA-S ratio may impair inflammatory control, contributing to tissue damage. Notably, parasitic infections such as malaria and schistosomiasis – which generally have less severe clinical courses – are associated with increased DHEA levels, suggesting a protective role for this hormone [31, 32]. In this respect, in patients with CCC and reduced ejection fraction, higher levels of DHEA-S were independently associated with more favorable echocardiographic parameters and lower NT-proBNP levels. In this study, during a median follow-up of 40 months, elevated DHEA-S levels were linked to a significantly reduced risk of all-cause mortality, heart transplantation, and left ventricular assist device implantation [33]. Accordingly, experimental studies have demonstrated that DHEA treatment enhances the immune response during T. cruzi infection, although a direct correlation between DHEA levels and cytokine-induced HPA activation has not yet been established [34, 35].

In the chronic phase of the disease, the unfavorable endocrine profile – characterized by an increased cortisol/DHEA-S ratio – combined with the extensive antigenic diversity of T. cruzi, may promote polyclonal immune activation and increase the likelihood of autoreactive events [36]. Although the autoimmune hypothesis in Chagas disease is still debatable, the impaired adrenal counter-regulation could create permissive conditions for autoreactivity. Further investigation into the immuno-endocrine imbalance and its role in T. cruzi-associated autoreactivity is warranted.

Turning to GCs, once they are released by HPA activation and enter the cells, their cytoplasmic bioavailability is regulated by the balance of their active (cortisol) and inactive (cortisone) forms. In humans, the enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (11β-HSD1) is mainly responsible for the transformation of cortisone into cortisol, meaning that the expression and activation of 11β-HSD1 influence GC activity [37]. In addition, the GC receptor (GR) presents isoforms with different activities. GR-α promotes the classical genomic actions of GC, while GR-β acts as a dominant negative inhibitor of GR-α function, thereby also modulating cellular sensitivity to GC and potentially contributing to hormone resistance under chronic inflammatory conditions [38]. In this regard, we recently provided further evidence of a possible association between CCC development and altered regulation of GC-mediated circuits, which fail to control systemic inflammation (Fig. 2). To gain a comprehensive view of the GC-related regulatory system, this study assessed the expression of GR isoforms (GR-α/β), 11β-HSD1, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) [39]. Results showed that GR-α expression in PBMCs did not differ between CCC and healthy controls, but 11β-HSD1 expression was elevated in CCC patients [39].

These cytokines act as signals that activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. b Activation of the HPA axis is reflected in the production and release of ACTH, which stimulates the secretion of GC (cortisol in humans) and DHEA-S by the adrenal gland. Tristetraprolin (TTP) is an RNA-binding protein that binds to AU-rich elements in the 3′ untranslated regions of target cytokine mRNAs – such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ – promoting their destabilization and degradation. By doing so, TTP acts as a crucial anti-inflammatory regulator. Its expression is regulated by stress-related signals. Particularly, TTP is upregulated by GR-α activation, exerting a key role in inflammation control [40]. Indeed, TTP-deficient mice exhibit an excessive accumulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine mRNAs and their encoded proteins, leading to severe systemic inflammation [41]. In addition, the absence or low expression of TTP in cardiac tissue may predispose to mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and functional failure [42].

Regarding GR activation and TTP expression in PBMCs from CCC patients, the downstream regulation of GR appears to be impaired. TTP expression is decreased, while the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in PBMCs tends to increase, leading to an augmented pro-inflammatory cytokine/TTP ratio (interleukin [IL]-6/TTP and interferon-γ/TTP), which may be involved in the failure to compensate for systemic inflammation in these patients [39]. Interestingly, the same study showed that GR expression was increased in cardiomyocytes from individuals with CCC, possibly as a compensatory mechanism to enhance responsiveness to GC. This upregulation of GR immunoreactivity was associated with cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. Notably, the increase in GR expression may result from the pronounced inflammatory infiltration and fibrosis characteristic of CCC, as this pattern was not observed in ischemic cardiomyopathy samples, which lack immune cell infiltrates. In this context, a positive correlation was observed between the intensity of the inflammatory infiltrate and GR expression in cardiomyocytes [39]. These findings suggest that in CCC patients, elevated GR expression is a widespread feature among myocardial cells, possibly reflecting an adaptive response to chronic inflammation, although in parallel may also contribute to hypertrophic remodeling.

Alterations in Other Anterior Pituitary Hormones during Human Chagas Disease

Several hormones secreted by the anterior pituitary, such as adrenocorticotropic hormone, growth hormone (GH), prolactin (PRL), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), as well as the gonadotropins – luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone – are known to influence the immune system activity. Conversely, cytokines can modulate the secretion of such hormones [43]. GH and PRL, often classified as stress-related hormones, are typically upregulated during physiological stress, similar to GC, a response partly attributed to the stimulatory effects of pro-inflammatory cytokines on the pituitary. Notably, PRL and GH exert immunostimulatory actions that may offset the immunosuppressive effects of elevated GC [44].

Experimental models and clinical studies have demonstrated disruptions in PRL and GH regulation during infection with T. cruzi, potentially linked to the pathogen’s ability to invade pituitary tissue [45, 46]. Given the demonstration that in acute myocardial infarction, the supraphysiological levels of PRL are detrimental in terms of cardiac metabolism and microvascular integrity [47]; the elevated PRL of CCC patients may therefore contribute to the pathophysiology of their cardiac disease [4].

GH plays a dual role, influencing both metabolic regulation and immune function. In individuals chronically infected with T. cruzi, systemic GH levels are markedly elevated in those with CCC, accompanied by an increased GH/IGF-1 ratio, suggesting a disruption in the somatotropic axis [4]. Despite these heightened basal levels, CCC patients exhibit a blunted GH response to glucose or insulin challenge, indicating a possible deficiency in GH reserve [48].

GH may affect cardiac tissue directly or via IGF-1 through specific myocardial receptors. Therefore, elevated GH in CCC may contribute to myocardial injury by enhancing immune-mediated inflammatory responses, which are key drivers of disease progression. It is worth reminding that in Laron Syndrome patients, a genetic condition resulting from GH receptor mutations, with elevated GH levels and low IGF-I, are resistant to several diseases, including a remarkably lower incidence of Chagas disease among residents in Chagas-endemic regions of southern Ecuador (Dr. Jaime Guevara-Aguirre, personal communication to JJK, [49]). Notably, a hormonal profile mimicking that of Laron Syndrome – high GH and low IGF-I – significantly reduced infection rates in vitro in human and mouse cell lines, suggesting this hormonal milieu may contribute to the partial resistance to Chagas disease observed in LS patients [49].

Regarding the pituitary-thyroid axis, studies in endemic regions of Argentina (including Santa Fe, Santiago del Estero, and Chaco provinces) suggest that this axis remains largely unaffected in chronic Chagas disease. Both asymptomatic individuals and those with CCM show TSH and T4 concentrations comparable to healthy controls, although patients with severe forms of CCM exhibit mildly reduced T3 levels [4].

Endocrine and Metabolic Alterations in Human Chagas Disease

The contribution of metabolic disturbances to the progression and severity of Chagas disease in humans remains insufficiently explored. A study conducted in Brazil reported a higher prevalence of diabetes among women diagnosed with CCC compared to the general female population [50]. At the same time, another study showed no association between metabolic disturbances and Chagas disease [51]. In addition, infected individuals residing in endemic rural areas frequently present with low body weight, as indicated by a body mass index below 20 [52]. In contrast, T. cruzi-infected individuals who have migrated to urban environments often develop overweight or obesity [5, 53, 54]. These changes in body composition appear to be associated with altered dietary patterns post-migration, reduced physical activity typical of urban living, and the socio-economic and educational backgrounds of the affected individuals.

Our studies among urban-dwelling patients with chronic Chagas disease revealed elevated circulating levels of insulin and triglycerides, along with an increased HOMA-IR index and reduced HDL concentrations – features consistent with a prediabetic metabolic state, which may in turn enhance cardiovascular risk [4, 5]. Overweight and obesity are recognized as contributors to chronic low-grade inflammation, a factor that can adversely affect immune function [55]. Moreover, infected human adipose tissue-derived stem cells, obtained through liposuction procedures, showed elevated secretion of IL-6, CXCL-10/IP-10, CCL2/MCP-1, CCL5/RANTES, and leptin [56]. Among patients with CCC, we have also found elevated systemic levels of IL-6 and TNF-α as well as alterations in TNF-α receptor expression in PBMCs [4, 5], suggesting that the inflammatory profile associated with excessive body weight may act as an additional risk factor promoting early cardiac damage in Chagas disease. It is already known that adipocytes are target cells of T. cruzi infection, implying that parasite infection of adipose cells could also promote tissue and systemic inflammation [57]. Yet, data on adipokines in this context remain inconsistent. One study identified reduced leptin levels in Chagas disease patients with congestive heart failure [58], whereas another reported no significant differences between CCC patients and healthy controls. However, we observed modestly increased leptin levels, particularly among those patients with CCC [5]. Discrepancies between these findings may be attributable to differences in patient classification across studies. Supporting our results, elevated leptin concentrations have been reported in non-chagasic individuals with chronic heart failure, potentially linked to leptin’s hypertrophic effects on cardiomyocytes [59, 60]. Given that cardiac hypertrophy is a hallmark of CCC, elevated leptin may then favor disease progression. Moreover, increased leptin may reflect heightened systemic inflammation, especially in CCC patients, because of its cytokine-like properties.

Unlike an earlier study in patients with advanced heart failure showing increased adiponectin levels potentially linked to autonomic dysfunction, we found no significant changes in adiponectin concentrations among chagasic individuals [5, 58]. Although current data on metabolic dysregulation in human Chagas disease remain limited, our findings highlight the need to consider overweight and obesity – and their associated immunometabolic effects– as key factors in the clinical assessment of patients, particularly in view of the growing prevalence of these conditions.

Sexual Hormones and Chagas Disease

Although studies examining the influence of sex hormones on the establishment and progression of human Chagas disease are limited, available evidence suggests that men are more likely than women to develop CCC [61, 62]. The underlying mechanisms behind this sex-related disparity remain to be fully elucidated. Most of the current knowledge on differential susceptibility to T. cruzi infection comes from experimental models, where males consistently exhibit higher parasitemia levels and shorter survival times compared to females [63–65]. These observations may reflect a protective role of estrogens in modulating the immune response, or conversely, an increased vulnerability in males, possibly associated with immunosuppressive effects of testosterone.

Autonomic Dysfunction and Neuro-Immune Crosstalk in Chagas Disease

Autonomic dysfunctions are commonly reported in patients with chronic Chagas disease exhibiting cardiac or gastrointestinal involvement, thus in keeping with the neurological involvement initially described by Carlos Chagas [66]. In the gastrointestinal tract, injuries to the parasympathetic nervous system are essential for the development of dilations and organ dysfunction. Subsequent studies confirmed significant ganglionic damage and a substantial reduction in subepicardial intramural neurons. Experimental models of T. cruzi infection further supported these findings by demonstrating that cardiac neuronal infection was accompanied by inflammation around ganglia and degeneration of nerve fibers and Schwann cells [67]. The mechanism of neuronal loss in Chagas disease is not completely clear; one possibility is the result of direct parasitism of neurons and supporting cells, acute periganglionic inflammation, and anti-neuronal autoimmune reactions [12].

Both human and experimental Chagas disease are characterized by cellular and humoral autoreactivity toward nerve structures, constituting another possible mechanism of inflammation at the neuroendocrine level [68]. In this sense, recent studies characterized the monoclonal antibody A2R1 constructed using B cells from patients with chronic Chagas heart disease. A2R1 specifically binds to parasite tubulin and cross-reacts with mammalian tubulin found exclusively in the brain, staining regions in and around neuronal cell bodies in both the peripheral and CNSs. Such a human antibody targeting T. cruzi tubulin that cross-reacts with a human neural antigen, supports the role of molecular mimicry in the pathogenesis of Chagas disease [69].

Anti-β1 and anti-M2 antibodies are functional autoantibodies targeting components of the intracardiac autonomic nervous system [70]. In a study conducted among 64 patients with CCC, 42.2% were reactive for anti-β1 and 45.3% for anti-M2 antibodies. Most relevant, both anti-β1 and anti-M2 autoantibodies appear to interfere with the heart autonomic regulation in CCC subjects. Anti-β1 antibodies are more consistently associated with increased sympathetic activity, higher cardiac workload, and reduced heart rate variability – features that are typically linked to greater cardiovascular risk and disease progression. In contrast, anti-M2 antibodies may reflect impaired parasympathetic control, although the available data remain limited. Collectively, these findings suggest that anti-β1 and anti-M2 autoantibodies contribute to autonomic imbalance and progressive cardiac dysfunction in CCC. Notably, the stronger association of anti-β1 with increased rate-pressure product and reduced heart rate variability parameters, points to a potentially pathogenic role in the impairment of myocardial function [71].

Also, substantial evidence now supports the view that both sympathetic and parasympathetic (vagal) branches of the autonomic nervous system (ANS) are capable of modulating immune responses, particularly by shifting the balance from a Th1-dominated profile toward a Th2-biased response [72–75]. Neurotransmitters released by autonomic nerve terminals interact with specific receptors expressed on immunocompetent cells, triggering signaling pathways that regulate immune activity [76]. Thus, autonomic dysfunction in Chagas disease seems to potentially influence other metabolic and inflammatory pathways. In addition, the crosstalk between the ANS and the immune system is particularly relevant, as neuronal destruction caused by T. cruzi infection has been documented in both experimental models and human tissues [67, 77, 78]. Parasympathetic denervation and sympathetic hyperactivity observed in CCC may not only exacerbate cardiac dysfunction but also contribute to the persistent pro-inflammatory environment that characterizes disease progression. Experimental evidence further suggests that modulation of autonomic signaling can influence parasite control, cytokine profiles, and tissue damage [63], highlighting the ANS as a relevant regulator of immune and disease outcome in Chagas disease.

Conclusion

In summary, growing evidence highlights the complex interplay between the nervous, endocrine, and immune systems in the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of Chagas disease. The neuroendocrine-immune axis functions as a dynamic bridge linking systemic immune-endocrine interactions with local CNS responses, each contributing through distinct but interconnected mechanisms to disease progression and organ-specific pathology (shown in Fig. 3). Recognizing the dual nature of this crosstalk – encompassing both peripheral and CNS-mediated effects – is essential for a comprehensive understanding of Chagas disease pathogenesis which may also help identify biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Future research should aim at further characterizing the mediators of this crosstalk, integrating clinical, immunological, and neuroendocrine data to support more precise and personalized approaches to disease monitoring and treatment.
