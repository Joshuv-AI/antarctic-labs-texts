A multimodal multitask deep learning model for predicting stroke lesion and functional outcomes using 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata
Authors: Kimberly Amador, Anthony J Winder, Jens Fiehler, Philip A Barber, Matthias Wilms, Nils D Forkert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute ischemic stroke is a major global health challenge, leading to long-term disability or death without timely intervention. Among neuroimaging modalities, spatio-temporal (4D) computed tomography perfusion (CTP) is widely used to assess cerebral blood flow and guide acute treatment decisions. Beyond this role, recent studies have demonstrated its potential to predict lesion outcomes (irreversible tissue damage at follow-up) and functional outcomes (long-term functional independence). Although inherently related, these outcomes are typically modeled and predicted separately, ignoring shared patterns that could enhance predictive accuracy. Multitask learning provides a promising solution by leveraging shared representations across both related tasks. However, only a few stroke studies have adopted this approach so far, and those that did primarily relied on imaging data alone. Thus, we developed CTPredict, the first multimodal, multitask deep learning model that simultaneously predicts follow-up stroke lesions and functional outcomes (specifically, 90-day modified Rankin Scale) from 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata. CTPredict integrates modality-specific encoders for feature extraction, a multimodal fusion module with cross-attention mechanisms to focus on relevant features from both data sources, and task-specific branches for outcome prediction, all within a computationally efficient framework for multitask learning. Evaluated on a challenging multi-center dataset of 111 AIS patients, CTPredict achieved a 0.23 Dice score and 0.77 accuracy for the lesion and functional outcome prediction tasks, respectively, outperforming single-task variants (0.21 Dice score, 0.73 accuracy). These results demonstrate the benefits of multitask learning and highlight CTPredict’s potential to enable more streamlined, data-driven, and personalized stroke outcome predictions in clinical practice.Keywords: Stroke, Outcome prediction, Multimodal, Multitask learning, CTPSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, with ischemic stroke (caused by an obstruction of blood flow to the brain) accounting for the majority of cases1. Effective management of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) requires rapid and accurate assessment of brain injury and clinical effects, given the time-sensitive nature of this disease and interventions. This process relies on combining neuroimaging, which helps to identify the clot location and tissue viability, with clinical evaluations to assess symptom severity, onset timing, and treatment eligibility2,3. Together, these complementary assessments enable precise diagnosis and timely intervention, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Within this context, computed tomography perfusion (CTP) has become a preferred clinical neuroimaging modality for evaluating AIS in many centers due to its widespread availability, speed, and ability to visualize cerebral blood flow dynamics4. Specifically, CTP involves acquiring sequential 3D CT images over approximately 60 seconds, at 1–2 second intervals, following the injection of an iodinated contrast agent. These time-resolved images capture the passage of the contrast agent through the brain, creating a spatio-temporal (4D) representation of cerebral blood flow. The raw CTP data, however, is inherently complex and high-dimensional, making its direct analysis and interpretation challenging. Thus, specialized post-processing algorithms are used clinically to extract (semi-)quantitative perfusion metrics for each voxel, such as cerebral blood flow (CBF), cerebral blood volume (CBV), mean transit time (MTT), and time-to-maximum of the residue function (Tmax)5. The resulting perfusion parameter maps are then thresholded to predict tissue viability, differentiating the irreversibly damaged infarct core (rCBF ) from the potentially salvageable penumbra tissue (Tmax )6. These thresholds, which were established and validated in clinical trials7,8, are commonly used for determining a patient’s eligibility for reperfusion therapies, such as intravenous thrombolysis or endovascular thrombectomy. Beyond acute treatment selection, the extent and localization of the core and penumbra also carry prognostic value. For example, patients with large cores are more likely to experience poor recovery, whereas those with smaller cores and larger penumbras may achieve better outcomes if reperfusion is successful8,9. However, these associations remain coarse and provide only limited insight into lesion progression and long-term functional recovery.

Clinical outcome measures provide a distinct perspective by directly evaluating neurological recovery and functional independence. These tools provide independent yet complementary insights to imaging findings, supporting decision-making across the continuum of care, from initial treatment to rehabilitation. Among the most widely used are the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), which evaluates global disability and independence, and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), which quantifies neurological deficits10. The mRS, in particular, is commonly used as a primary outcome measure in clinical trials for investigating the efficacy of new treatment approaches and within stroke outcome prediction models, due to its ease of use and broad applicability. This 7-point scale ranges from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death), with intermediate scores indicating varying levels of disability11,12. Administered through a structured interview, it aims to quantify the impact of physical, cognitive, and communication impairments on functional independence, with even a one-point change considered clinically significant13. The mRS has been shown to correlate only moderately with infarct volumes in follow-up imaging (coefficients of 0.4–0.5)14–16, demonstrating that lesion size alone is not a reliable predictor of clinical and functional outcomes. Other factors, such as lesion location and patient-specific information, are also known to influence recovery17. Related work on stroke outcome prediction

Deep learning approaches for stroke outcome prediction have primarily focused on two interrelated tasks: lesion outcome prediction and functional outcome prediction19,20. This progress has followed several distinct directions, outlined below.

Lesion outcome prediction

Also referred to as final infarct segmentation, this task aims to estimate the extent of brain tissue damage at follow-up (e.g., 2–7 days post-stroke) based on acute imaging modalities. In recent years, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely adopted for this task, utilizing inputs such as diffusion-weighted MR imaging (DWI)21, non-contrast CT (NCCT)22, CT angiography (CTA)23, and perfusion maps derived from either perfusion-weighted MR imaging (PWI)24–26 or CTP27. These CNN-based models have been shown to significantly outperform traditional machine learning and widely adopted clinical thresholding methods, demonstrating their ability to more effectively capture spatial and temporal patterns in imaging data. However, the reliance on perfusion maps in many of these models presents notable limitations, including susceptibility to noise introduced by deconvolution algorithms28 and a potentially limited ability to fully capture all relevant hemodynamic patterns, which may reduce predictive accuracy and reliability. To overcome this, recent research has shifted toward directly analyzing raw or minimally-preprocessed 4D perfusion data (PWI or CTP), leveraging techniques such as channel-wise convolutions29,30, temporal convolutional networks31,32, and Transformers33 to model temporal dependencies. These advanced spatio-temporal models have achieved superior performance compared to perfusion map-based methods, demonstrating not only the feasibility but also the superiority of directly leveraging 4D perfusion data for lesion outcome prediction.

Functional outcome prediction

This task, in contrast, aims to estimate a patient’s long-term recovery, typically measured by the mRS at 90 days post-stroke (mRS90), using acute or sub-acute imaging modalities and/or associated clinical metadata. Due to the inherent complexity and non-uniform distribution of available datasets, this task is often framed as a binary classification problem by, for example, dichotomizing patients into “favorable” (mRS90 0–2) or “unfavorable” (mRS90 3–6) outcome groups34. Some deep learning models developed for this purpose have been trained solely on acute imaging data, including DWI35,36, NCCT22, and CTA37. These models have also outperformed traditional machine learning methods, such as logistic regression and random forest classifiers, further demonstrating the adaptability of deep learning in capturing complex patterns and managing the variability inherent in stroke outcome data.

Multimodal learning

As previously noted, stroke prognosis is influenced by a wide range of factors beyond imaging, including demographic, environmental, and treatment-related factors38. To account for this complexity and improve predictive accuracy, recent approaches have sought to develop multimodal deep learning models that integrate both imaging and clinical metadata for predicting either follow-up stroke lesions or functional outcomes. Most of those models rely on late fusion strategies, where imaging-derived features and clinical metadata are processed independently and combined only at the final stages of the model, typically via concatenation or summation. Notable examples include30,39 for lesion outcome prediction and40–44 for functional outcome prediction. While these approaches have been shown to outperform traditional unimodal baselines, their reliance on late fusion limits cross-modal interactions during feature extraction and processing, potentially restricting the learning of richer and more informative representations45. As a result, more advanced (intermediate) fusion strategies have recently emerged, allowing for earlier and more dynamic integration of imaging and clinical metadata. For example, Borsos et al.46 used affine transformations to combine CTP-based perfusion maps with clinical metadata, achieving better predictive performance than both deep learning models and traditional machine learning techniques (i.e., random forests and gradient-boosted classifiers) trained solely on clinical metadata. More recently, Amador et al.47,48 introduced cross-attention as an intermediate fusion strategy to predict follow-up stroke lesions and functional outcomes directly from acute 4D CTP and clinical metadata. Their approach outperformed unimodal models for lesion prediction47, demonstrating the value of providing clinical context to the image analysis task, and surpassed both unimodal models and late fusion strategies in functional outcome prediction48. These findings highlight the potential of multimodal models, especially those using intermediate fusion, to improve performance.

Multitask learning

Despite these advances, most existing outcome prediction methods remain limited to single-task learning, treating lesion and functional outcomes as separate problems and ignoring the interplay between tissue damage (location and size) and patient recovery. A promising solution that may enhance the predictions of both tasks is to jointly model both outcomes, a paradigm known as multitask learning, to improve model efficiency and enable a more comprehensive understanding of patient prognosis. Multitask learning is a machine learning paradigm where a single model is trained to perform multiple related tasks simultaneously, leveraging shared representations to improve data utilization, generalization, and computational efficiency49. The most common multitask architecture is hard parameter-sharing, where a shared feature extractor processes input data for all tasks of interest before task-specific branches generate individual predictions. This design encourages the learning of more generalizable patterns, reducing the risk of overfitting and improving overall model performance50. While multitask learning has been widely applied in computer vision51,52, natural language processing53, and medical image analysis54, its use in AIS remains limited. One exception is the work by Nishi et al.35, who proposed a U-Net-based multitask model for acute infarct segmentation and mRS90 prediction from pre-treatment DWI scans. Their model employed hard parameter-sharing, using a CNN encoder to extract spatial features, which were then processed by task-specific branches. The segmentation branch achieved a Dice score of 0.63, while the classification branch, using bottleneck features, reached an accuracy of 0.72. Expanding on this, Park et al.55 developed a similar U-Net-based multitask framework using DWI, improving acute infarct segmentation (Dice score: 0.73) but achieving only 0.69 accuracy for mRS90 prediction. To refine predictions, they used a separate machine learning model that combined initial mRS90 probabilities with clinical variables, increasing the accuracy to 0.73. However, treating imaging and clinical metadata separately limited the cross-modal interactions, thus restricting the benefits of a fully integrated multimodal approach. In contrast, Samak et al.22 introduced an autoencoder-based multitask model to predict stroke lesion outcomes and mRS90 from pre-treatment NCCT scans. Trained end-to-end, their model first generated a follow-up NCCT scan, which was then combined with the baseline scan for mRS90 classification, achieving 0.79 accuracy. More recently, Sun et al.56 and Jiang et al.57 proposed related multitask models for acute lesion segmentation and onset-time classification from multimodal MRI, using a shared encoder with task-specific branches, and introducing contrastive representation learning and diffusion–perfusion fusion strategies, respectively. Despite these advancements, most multitask studies have focused on acute infarct/lesion segmentation rather than follow-up lesion outcome prediction. Additionally, clinical metadata is typically not included in these analyses, while CTP-based multitask learning remains unexplored.

Contributions

In this work, we introduce CTPredict, the first fully multimodal, multitask deep learning model to jointly predict follow-up stroke lesions and functional outcomes using 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata. Building on our previous work47,48, which developed separate multimodal prediction models for each task, CTPredict unifies them within a single framework, leveraging shared feature representations to enhance efficiency and generalization. We demonstrate that CTPredict outperforms its single-task variants, underscoring the advantages of multitask learning for more accurate and comprehensive stroke outcome prediction.

Methods

In this work, we propose a novel unified multimodal, multitask deep learning model that simultaneously predicts follow-up stroke lesion outcomes and long-term functional outcomes (i.e., mRS90) using pre-treatment 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata. The framework addresses two core tasks: a voxel-wise classification that generates a binary mask labeling each voxel in the image as either “healthy” or “infarcted”, and an individual-level binary classification to predict whether the patient will have a favorable (mRS90 of 0–2) or unfavorable (mRS90 of 3–6) functional outcome at 90 days post-stroke. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the model comprises three main components: (1) Modality-specific encoders for extracting high-level feature representations from each input modality; (2) Multimodal fusion module that uses self- and cross-attention mechanisms to integrate intra- and inter-modality relationships; (3) Task-specific decoding branches for voxel-wise follow-up lesion segmentation (prediction) and binary mRS90 outcome prediction. The following subsections describe each component in detail.

Overview of the proposed multimodal, multitask model architecture for stroke lesion and functional outcome prediction. The architecture consists of three components: (1) Modality-specific encoders to extract high-level features from each input modality; (2) A multimodal fusion module to capture intra- and inter-modality dependencies; and (3) Task-specific decoding branches for voxel-wise infarct prediction and binary mRS90 class prediction.

Modality-specific encoders

Separate learnable encoding strategies are applied to process 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata, transforming each modality into optimized representations suitable for fusion in subsequent stages.

Imaging encoder

Each time point in the 4D CTP sequence is encoded into a d-dimensional embedding using a 3D CNN-encoder with shared weights across all time points33. This weight-sharing design enables efficient extraction of high-level spatial features while effectively reducing computational requirements. The CNN-encoder comprises four sequential blocks, each with two convolutional layers using ReLU activations, followed by a stride-2 convolutional layer for down-sampling/pooling. The encoded outputs of all time points are flattened and concatenated into a 2D matrix, where rows correspond to time points and columns represent the d-dimensional embedding space. Positional encodings58 are also added to preserve the temporal structure of the sequence, enabling the attention mechanisms in the fusion module to account for the relative positions of time points.

Clinical encoder

The clinical metadata is divided into categorical and continuous features, each processed using tailored encoding strategies. Categorical variables are encoded using embedding layers that map each discrete category to a dense, trainable vector representation, following the approach described in59. During training, these layers adaptively learn inter-category relationships, generating task-specific representations that enhance the relevance and utility of the encoded data60. Continuous features are processed through fully connected layers, producing dense embeddings. Note that the output dimensions of both the embedding and fully connected layers are set to d to match the size of the 4D CTP embeddings, ensuring compatibility with the mathematical operations of the multimodal fusion module. The embeddings for all clinical features are then concatenated into a single unified vector.

After encoding, a learnable class token (CLS)61 is added to the embedding sequences of both modalities. These tokens aggregate global contextual information and serve as representative summaries for the task-specific decoding branches. The augmented embeddings are then passed to the multimodal fusion module for further processing.

Multimodal fusion module

Building on our previous work described in48, the multimodal fusion module combines embeddings from both modalities using self-attention, cross-attention, and a feed-forward network to capture intra- and inter-modality dependencies. This process refines individual modality representations and integrates them into a unified embedding for downstream tasks.

Task-specific branches

Separate decoding branches process the fused representations to generate outputs tailored to each downstream task (i.e., lesion segmentation or functional outcome prediction).

Lesion outcome prediction branch

The CLS token from the 4D CTP data is reshaped into a three-dimensional array and passed through a 3D CNN-decoder. This decoder mirrors the encoder structure, except for the final block, which includes two convolutional layers followed by a Softmax activation to generate voxel-wise lesion probability maps. To help preserve localized high-frequency spatial information, skip connections link the encoder and decoder. This is achieved by concatenating the absolute differences between corresponding feature maps, emphasizing temporal changes while maintaining computational efficiency33. The resulting probabilistic maps are thresholded at 0.5 to produce a binary segmentation mask of the predicted follow-up lesion area.

Functional outcome prediction branc

The CLS tokens from the 4D CTP and clinical metadata branches are concatenated and then processed by a feed-forward network. This network comprises two fully connected layers, with a sigmoid activation in the final layer generating probability scores. Predictions above a threshold of 0.5 are classified as unfavorable functional outcomes (mRS90 3–6).

Experiments

The main goal of our experiments is to investigate and quantify the assumed advantages of using the proposed multitask prediction model over its single-task variants for predicting stroke lesion and functional outcomes. To this end, we implemented two additional models, each focusing on one of the tasks independently: the lesion-only model, which predicts voxel-wise follow-up stroke lesion outcomes, and the mRS90-only model, which predicts binary functional outcomes. Both single-task models are built using the same backbone architecture as the multitask model and have been validated in previous work47,48, where they achieved state-of-the-art performance in their respective tasks. This shared architecture ensures that any performance differences can be directly attributed to the multitask learning paradigm rather than architectural differences. As there are no existing multitask models (whether stroke-specific or general-purpose) that support this combination of data types (4D imaging and clinical metadata) for jointly predicting lesion and functional outcomes, our evaluation focuses specifically on comparing the proposed multitask model with these two single-task baselines. In total, three models were trained and evaluated using the same dataset and data splits of acute 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata from patients with acute ischemic stroke.

Data

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata from AIS patients with large vessel occlusions who underwent thrombectomy. Data were collected from two centers: 70 patients treated at the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf (Germany) between 2015 and 2019, and 41 patients treated at Foothills Medical Centre (Canada) between 2020 and 2024. The 4D CTP images were acquired at baseline (pre-treatment) with a spatial resolution of 512 512 S voxels (S ranging from 16 to 28), voxel spacing of 0.43–0.45 0.43–0.45 3–5 mm, and a temporal resolution of 1.8–2.8 seconds, capturing 24–47 time points. Follow-up NCCT or DWI scans, acquired within the first week after stroke, were also available for each patient. Lesion segmentations on these scans were delineated based on imaging patterns of hypoattenuation on NCCT or hyperintensity on DWI, and were generated semi-automatically using AnToNIa63 for the Hamburg cohort or manually for the Foothills cohort. In both cases, experienced radiologists and neurologists segmented the lesions, and the resulting binary masks served as the ground truth for the lesion outcome prediction task. The clinical metadata available for the patients of both centers included age, sex, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, smoking status, baseline NIHSS score, stroke laterality, and reperfusion status (measured using the modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction scale, or mTICI). Functional outcomes at 90 days (chronic stage) were evaluated using the mRS, with scores dichotomized for this analysis into favorable (mRS 0–2) and unfavorable (mRS 3–6) outcomes, as is standard practice in stroke research. These dichotomized labels served as the ground truth for the functional outcome prediction task. Pre-processing

Imaging data

Both acute CTP and follow-up images underwent preprocessing to ensure alignment and consistency. First, both datasets were skull-stripped using an intensity-based algorithm to generate binary brain tissue masks. The acute CTP scans were then processed with AnToNIa63, a perfusion analysis tool, to reduce motion artifacts and background noise. This included (1) motion correction via 3D rigid registration between the first and subsequent time points, (2) baseline intensity correction by subtracting the average of the first three time points (perfusion average) from the sequence, and (3) temporal resampling to a 1-second interval using B-spline approximation, which smoothed the temporal dimension and resulted in 54–151 time points per scan. Using the patient-individual perfusion average as a reference, the corresponding follow-up image and an NCCT brain atlas64 were subsequently registered using rigid and affine transformations, respectively, via NiftyReg65. Acute CTP scans were then resampled to a voxel spacing of 0.45 0.45 5.00 mm, and the inter-hemispheric fissure plane from the NCCT atlas was used to delineate the ipsilateral and contralateral components. The follow-up lesion segmentations were also resampled into the acute CTP image space. All images were masked to isolate the ipsilateral component and cropped to a dataset-wide 3D bounding box of 408 217 16. To maintain spatial consistency during the down-sampling stages of the deep learning model, the cropped images were zero-padded to 416 224 16. To standardize the sequence length, a 32-second window was extracted from each acute CTP scan by centering the window on the peak of each patient’s average intensity-time curve66. Finally, Z-score normalization was applied across all voxels and time points. Thus, the final output consisted of ipsilateral acute CTP scans and follow-up lesion segmentations, co-registered and standardized to a unified voxel size and image extents. The proposed method does not require any AIF selection or perfusion map calculation.

Clinical metadata

The clinical metadata was divided into two groups: categorical variables (sex, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, smoking status, recanalization status, and stroke laterality) and continuous variables (age and baseline NIHSS score). Among the 111 AIS patients in the dataset, 16 patients had one missing entry in their clinical data. Missing values in continuous variables were imputed using the mean of the corresponding group, while missing categorical variables were assigned to a new “missing” category. Additionally, all continuous variables were scaled to a 0–1 range to standardize their magnitude and ensure comparability across features.

Experimental setup

To ensure robust generalization, we adopted a 10-fold cross-validation strategy for training and testing. Stratified sampling was applied based on center, as well as lesion size and dichotomized mRS scores to preserve consistent class distributions across folds. Identical data partitions were used across all models to ensure fair comparisons.

Loss functions

The proposed multitask model was trained end-to-end using the multitask loss function (see Section 2.4), with balancing the contributions of and . This value was selected through a coarse grid search over in the range [0.2, 0.8] with a step size of 0.1. The single-task baseline models were also trained end-to-end, but used exclusively for the lesion-only model and for the mRS90-only model.

Optimization strategy

All models were trained using the Adam optimizer, a cosine learning rate scheduler (8 cycles, reducing the maximum learning rate by 25% per cycle), and a batch size of 1. The initial learning rate was selected by grid search, and early stopping determined the number of epochs: lesion-only (lr = 0.001, 101 epochs), mRS90-only (lr = 0.001, 93 epochs), and multitask (lr = 0.0005, 150 epochs).

Environment

All models were implemented in Python 3.9 using Keras with a TensorFlow 2.0 backend and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24GB).

Evaluation metrics

For the lesion outcome prediction task, evaluation metrics included the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) to measure overlap between predicted and ground truth segmentations, the average Hausdorff distance (HD, mm) to assess boundary alignment, and volume error (VE, ml) and absolute volume error (AVE, ml) to quantify differences in lesion volume. For the functional outcome prediction task, performance was assessed using accuracy and F1-score. These metrics aim to provide a comprehensive evaluation of task-specific performance. A paired sample t-test and McNemar’s test were employed to statistically compare the performance of the proposed and baseline models for the lesion and functional outcome prediction tasks, respectively, with significance set at .

Model performance

For the lesion outcome prediction task, the proposed multitask model consistently outperformed the lesion-only model across all metrics. Specifically, the multitask model achieved a DSC of 0.23 ± 0.04, a volume error of -7 ± 16 ml, an absolute volume error of 36 ± 5 ml, and a HD of 9 ± 3 mm. In comparison, the lesion-only model achieved a DSC of 0.21 ± 0.05, a volume error of -10 ± 27 ml, an absolute volume error of 38 ± 9 ml, and a HD of 10 ± 3 mm. The differences in DSC, volume error, and absolute volume error were statistically significant (). Even though both models underestimated the true follow-up lesion volumes, the multitask model demonstrated better overall performance, as further illustrated in Fig. 3, which highlights its improved accuracy in predicting stroke lesion outcomes. Similarly, for the functional outcome prediction task, the multitask model outperformed the mRS90-only model, achieving an accuracy of 0.77 ± 0.07 and F1-score of 0.76 ± 0.08, compared to the mRS90-only model’s accuracy of 0.73 ± 0.06 and F1-score of 0.72 ± 0.06, respectively, though these differences were not statistically significant. Given that baseline characteristics were largely comparable across centers (see Table 1), site-specific differences are unlikely to have substantially biased our predictive performance. Overall, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of the multitask learning framework in producing more accurate and robust predictions for both lesion and functional outcomes, showcasing the advantages of leveraging shared representations relevant to both related tasks.

Model size

As shown in Table 3, the multitask model required slightly more trainable parameters compared to the single-task models, primarily due to the inclusion of task-specific branches. While this increase in model size adds complexity, the observed improvements in both lesion and functional outcome prediction metrics demonstrate the effectiveness of the multitask design.

Discussion

The main finding of this work is that the proposed, novel multitask learning setup enables more accurate predictions of the follow-up stroke lesion and functional outcomes compared to single-task learning. This suggests that optimizing shared representations across related tasks allows the model to capture complex interdependencies between lesion characteristics and functional recovery that single-task approaches potentially fail to account for.

Although the absolute improvements over baseline may appear rather modest, they are consistent across tasks and reached statistical significance for several lesion outcome prediction metrics (e.g., DSC, volume error), suggesting robustness rather than chance effects. More importantly, this work should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept. The major contribution is not necessarily restricted to the observed quantitative gains but in demonstrating the feasibility of a single unified framework that can jointly predict lesion and functional outcomes using multimodal datasets. This innovative design avoids training two separate models in isolation and enables information about lesion evolution to inform long-term functional outcome prediction (and vice versa), laying a more coherent and clinically meaningful foundation for future work.

The performance advantages of the proposed CTPredict method over single-task models may be attributed to two main factors. First, the multitask learning framework enhances generalization through inductive information transfer, where knowledge gained from one task informs and improves the performance of the other. This approach, widely recognized for improving feature representations67, likely enabled CTPredict to better capture both shared and task-specific patterns that reflect the interdependencies between infarct progression and functional recovery. By jointly optimizing both tasks, multitask learning may also act as an implicit form of regularization, stabilizing training and improving generalization. These benefits are further amplified by the hard parameter-sharing architecture, which combines shared modality-specific encoders and a multimodal fusion module with task-specific heads. The shared layers likely extract generalized features relevant for both tasks, while the task-specific heads specialize in their respective outputs – for example, the lesion outcome prediction branch may focus more on spatial details, whereas the functional outcome prediction branch may focus more on global cues, such as the predicted lesion distribution and clinical metadata. This design helped reduce task interference, enabling the model to address the unique demands of each task more effectively and achieve superior performance across all metrics. Second, CTPredict leverages a multimodal architecture, integrating imaging and clinical metadata to provide context-aware predictions. Since infarct size alone does not fully explain functional impairment68, incorporating additional information, such as baseline NIHSS and co-existing medical conditions (e.g., hypertension, atrial fibrillation), facilitates the generation of more personalized and comprehensive predictions. Ultimately, the synergy between multitask learning and multimodal integration enhances CTPredict’s ability to improve final infarct prediction and to leverage this information to refine functional outcome predictions, resulting in a more robust and clinically relevant stroke outcome prediction model. Beyond clinical decision-making, this approach may also benefit in-silico studies by enabling more accurate simulations and evaluations of treatment strategies.

Despite these promising results, several areas require further investigation. First, while our model was trained on a multi-center database, the complexity of 4D CTP data, combined with a relatively small sample size, may limit both accuracy and generalizability. Thus, expanding to larger and more diverse datasets will be essential to validate robustness across different populations and strengthen confidence in the model’s clinical applicability. Second, our analyses were constrained to predicting functional outcomes using a dichotomized version of the mRS. While this provides a straightforward modeling setup, it reduces prediction granularity and ignores subtle recovery patterns. Incorporating ordinal regression techniques69 could better capture the inherent ranking structure of functional outcomes and provide a more nuanced alternative to binary grouping. Third, the interaction between 4D CTP and clinical metadata within our model remains underexplored. Since the model fuses these modalities through cross-attention, future studies could leverage attention visualization techniques47 to identify which clinical variables and time points the model prioritizes, improving interpretability. In parallel, asymmetry-aware approaches for acute lesion segmentation in NCCT70 have shown how domain-specific priors can regularize learning, motivating the adaption of similar strategies to enhance lesion outcome prediction. Finally, our loss function was optimized using a fixed weighting strategy, selected via grid search, which, while providing a straightforward and stable setup, required multiple training iterations and significant computational resources. A more efficient alternative would be to use adaptive loss weighting methods, where the contribution of each loss component is dynamically adjusted based on precision and recall metrics71. Similarly, instead of sampling all tasks at each step, adaptive task scheduling72 could be used to prioritize more challenging or uncertain tasks, accelerating convergence. Therefore, future work should focus on refining outcome prediction granularity, enhancing interpretability through attention visualization, incorporating domain knowledge to regularize training, and optimizing training efficiency with adaptive strategies.

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed CTPredict, the first multimodal, multitask deep learning model that integrates both spatio-temporal 4D CTP imaging and clinical metadata to simultaneously predict follow-up stroke lesions and functional outcomes in AIS patients, which had never been done before. By leveraging shared, optimized representations, this multitask approach improves predictive accuracy compared to the corresponding single-task models, reinforcing the value of a unified framework for more precise, personalized, data-driven stroke outcome predictions.
