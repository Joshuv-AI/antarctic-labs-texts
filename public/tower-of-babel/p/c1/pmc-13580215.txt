A glutamatergic S1-VPL-S1 corticothalamocortical loop amplifies mechanical hypersensitivity in neuropathic pain
Authors: Yujiao Hu, Hao Ding, Lijing Zou, Zhuo Wen, Hongming Wu, Tingting Ni, Lin He, Qi Gao, Han Cao, Junyu Zhou, Jiajun Chen, Baochun Jiang, Shuang Qiu, Lina Yu, Min Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.A recurrent glutamatergic S1HL-VPL-S1HL circuit amplifies cortical responses to subthreshold stimuli and promotes mechanical allodynia in neuropathic pain, whereas circuit inhibition alleviates allodynia.

1. Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a type of chronic pain caused by lesions or diseases affecting the somatosensory system. It manifests as spontaneous pain, hyperalgesia, and allodynia in the affected area. Neuropathic pain exerts multidimensional adverse effects on patients' physiological and psychological well-being, social participation, and overall quality of life.16 Therefore, further investigation into the modulation mechanisms of NP holds significant basic and clinical importance for effective treatments and interventions.

Corticothalamic descending pathways have been implicated to participate in pain and related emotional regulation. The activation of the circuits from the cortex to the ventral posterolateral nucleus (VPL), posterior thalamic nucleus (PO), and parafascicular nucleus of the thalamus has been shown to mediate allodynia.6,10,22,27 However, it remains unclear how the corticothalamic pathway processes descending cortical signals to regulate pain via its downstream outputs.

The cerebral cortex and thalamus are interconnected through sophisticated bidirectional neural circuits, which have been proposed to play a pivotal role in intra- and intercortical information transfer, integration, and sensation amplification.19,20 Specifically, the thalamus contains driver neurons receiving peripheral inputs and modulator neurons receiving cortical inputs, with the latter relaying signals back to the cortex, forming a corticothalamocortical loop.21 Whether and how this connection pattern contributes to pain modulation has yet to be determined.

The primary somatosensory cortex (S1) is a key cortical region for processing somatic sensation. It has extensive connections with the thalamus and other cortical areas.8 The hindlimb area of S1 (S1HL) specifically receives hindlimb sensory input. Studies show that pyramidal neurons and interneurons of S1 encode pain location, intensity, and quality. In pain models, S1 neurons exhibit a hyperactive state, and stimulating them can induce pain hypersensitivity.2,10,25 In addition, S1 neurons in bilateral hemispheres may collaboratively mediate pain perception via communication.9,26 Layer IV of S1 mainly receives thalamic input, and Layer V/VI send corticothalamic and other descending projections. These connections suggest that S1 may serve as a critical node within the bilateral corticothalamic connection. However, how S1 dynamically interacts with thalamic regions in pain circuits, the cell type connectivity mechanisms involved, and its precise role in pain signal processing and chronicity remain to be elucidated.

VPL serves as a crucial nucleus for sensory processing in the thalamus. Significantly increased spontaneous firing of glutamatergic neurons in the VPL was observed in spared nerve injury (SNI) mice.24 Previous studies primarily focused on the function of VPL as a relay nucleus in the peripheral sensory afferent pathway.11 However, the mechanism underlying its bidirectional connectivity with the cortex and its role in pain modulation remains unknown. As a unique intersection for both ascending sensory inputs and cortical descending feedback, the VPL may play a critical role in pain processing within the corticothalamic connection. In this study, we reveal that the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit constitutes a positive feedback amplifier, which potentiates cortical pain perception and drives hyperalgesia.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Animals

Male and female C57BL/6J mice aged 8 to 10 weeks (20-25 g) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory. Transgenic mice (CaMKIIa-Cre and Gad2-Cre) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). The mice breeding environment was under a light/dark cycle, with temperatures maintained at 23 to 25°C and humidity at 40%. All mice had free access to water and food. All experimental animal protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (Approval No.: ZJU20250405) and complied with the NIH Guidelines for Animal Welfare.

2.2. Spared nerve injury surgery

The neuropathic pain model was established using the SNI method.17 C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into SNI and sham groups. Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). The skin was cut parallel to the long axis of the hindlimb for about 1 to 2 cm, and the muscle was pierced with ophthalmic scissors. The muscle and connective tissue around the sciatic nerve were bluntly separated with ophthalmic forceps to expose the sciatic nerve bundle. The tibial nerve and the common peroneal nerve were ligated with 6-0 surgical thread and cut distally, leaving the sural nerve intact. The muscle and skin were sutured. The sham group underwent the same skin incision and muscle separation without damaging the sciatic nerve. Pain thresholds were measured a week after modeling to confirm success.

2.3. von Frey test

The von Frey filament test was used to measure mechanical pain thresholds, and the Dixon up-down method was used to calculate the 50% paw withdrawal threshold.1 Pain threshold tests were conducted during the daytime. Mice were acclimated to the environment for at least 30 minutes before testing. The tested area was the lateral midplantar region of the left hind paw, innervated by the sciatic nerve. The test began with a 0.16 g von Frey filament, and stimuli were applied sequentially until the response threshold was first crossed. In the absence of a paw withdrawal response, the stimulus intensity was increased; if a response occurred, a lower intensity was used in the subsequent trial. A total of 6 key data points were counted.

2.4. Assessment of spontaneous pain

To assess spontaneous pain behavior, each animal was placed individually in a chamber and videorecorded for 30 minutes. The recorded videos were subsequently played back in slow motion to count the number of licking/flinching/shaking/lifting of the ipsilateral hind paw. Movements associated with exploratory behavior, locomotion, body repositioning, or grooming were excluded. Scoring was as follows: each bout of licking was assigned 2 points, and each bout of flinching/shaking/lifting was assigned 1 point. The total cumulative score over the 30-minute recording period was used as the spontaneous pain score.

2.5. Stereotaxic injection and fiber implantation

Glass electrodes were pulled to the appropriate length based on the injection site and depth, and connected to a microinjector. Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). The mouse's head was fixed to the stereotaxic adapter using bilateral ear bars and incisor bars. The scalp was cut to expose the skull surface. Then, the skull was leveled before drilling holes at the injection site. Viruses were filled into the glass microelectrode for injection (40 nL/min). Stereotaxic coordinates were recorded as anteroposterior (AP), mediolateral (ML), and dorsoventral (DV) relative to bregma. The brain regions involved in this study include the following: S1HL (AP, −0.3 mm; ML, ±2.0 mm; DV, −1.45 mm), VPL (AP, −1.85 mm; ML, ±1.9 mm; DV, −3.9 mm), PO (AP, −1.85 mm; ML, ±1.25 mm; DV, −3.3 mm), secondary somatosensory cortex (S2) (AP, −1.25 mm; ML, ±4 mm; DV, −1.7 mm), and auditory cortex (ACx) (AP, −2 mm; ML, ±4.1 mm; DV, −1.65 mm). After injection, the microelectrode was left in place for 10 minutes before being slowly withdrawn. For mice with fiber implants, holes were drilled in the skull after virus injection, and skull screws were placed. The optical fiber was inserted at the following positions: S1HL (AP, −0.3 mm; ML, ±2.0 mm; DV, −0.8 mm), VPL (AP, −1.85 mm; ML, ±1.9 mm; DV, −3.75 mm). The skull surface was covered with tissue adhesive and dental adhesive resin cement. Mice were placed on a heating pad for recovery. For spinal cord stereotactic injection, the dorsal skin was incised along the midline, and the paravertebral muscles were dissected. The spine was then fixed by bilateral ear bars. The muscle and spinal dura mater covering the intervertebral space were removed to expose the spinal cord. The glass microelectrode filled with the virus was inserted 0.4 mm lateral to the midline of the spinal cord (the left dorsal horn) at a depth of 0.47 mm. The microelectrode was left in place for 10 minutes before withdrawal.

2.6. Viruses and drug delivery

The following viruses were obtained from BrainVTA (Wuhan, China), and the rabies virus (RV) system was obtained from Brain Case (Shenzhen, China). For anterograde tracing from the S1HL cortex, rAAV-hSyn-DIO-Synaptophysin-mCherry-WPRE-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL in CaMKIIa-Cre mice and Gad2-Cre mice. For retrograde tracing from VPL, CTB 555 or rAAV2/Retro-hSyn-mCherry-WPRE-pA (40 nL) was injected into the nuclei of wild-type mice. In the experiment of anterograde transsynaptic tracing in the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu pathway, scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL, and AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-EGFP-WPRE-pA (80 nL) was injected into the VPL; rAAV2/1-hSyn-FLP-WPRE-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL, and rAAV2/9-hSyn-fDIO-mCherry-WPRE-pA (80 nL) was injected into the VPL. For anterograde transsynaptic tracing from the lumbar spinal cord dorsal horn (DH) to thalamus, scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA (300 nL) was injected into the left lumbar DH, and AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-EGFP-WPRE-pA (80 nL) was injected into the right VPL; rAAV-CAG-mWGA-mCherry (300 nL) was injected into the left lumbar DH. For the tracing of the corticothalamocortical tertiary circuit, scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL, S2, and ACx. rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-Synaptophysin-mCherry-WPRE-pA was injected into VPL. For the second-order trans-synaptic anterograde tracing of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit, AAV-hSyn-ATLASSnCre with AAV-hSyn-HA-BACE and rAAV-hSyn-fDlO-mCherry cocktail (1:5:6 ratio, 150 nL overall) was injected into S1HL, and AAV-DIO-ATLASSnFlp with AAV-hSyn-HA-BACE cocktail (1:5 ratio, 80 nL overall) was injected into VPL. For retrograde trans-synaptic tracing from S1HL, rAAV-EF1a-DIO-mCherry-TVA and rAAV-EF1a-DIO-N2oG cocktail (1:1 ratio, 125 nL overall) were injected into the S1HL in CaMKIIa-Cre mice and Gad2-Cre mice. After 21 days, CVS-EnvA-ΔG-EGFP was injected at the same site.

For calcium signal fiber photometry recording in VPL, scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL, and rAAV-hSyn-DIO-GCamp8m-WPRE-pA (80 nL) was injected into VPL. Three weeks after viral expression, mice underwent SNI or sham surgery. For fiber photometry in S1HL, scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA and rAAV-CaMKIIa-GCamp6m-WPRE-pA cocktail (1:1 ratio, 125 nL overall) was injected into S1HL, rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM3Dq-EGFP-WPREs or AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM4Di-EYFP-WPRE-pA (80 nL) was injected into VPL. For the calcium signal recording of bilateral S1HL in response to bilateral hind paw stimuli, rAAV2/Retro-CaMKlla-Cre-mCherry with rAAV2/9-CaMKIIa-GCaMp6m-WPRE-pA cocktail (1:1 ratio, 125 nL overall) was injected into the left S1HL, and AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM4Di-EGFP-WPRE-pA with rAAV2/9-CaMKIIa-GCaMp6m-WPRE-pA cocktail (1:1 ratio, 125 nL overall) was injected into the right S1HL. After 3 weeks of viral expression, calcium signals were recorded under 2 conditions in the same cohort of mice: following saline administration (control condition) and following Clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) administration (5 mg/kg, 30 minutes before recording; chemogenetic activation or inhibition condition), with an interval of at least 3 days between the 2 recording sessions. Mice were habituated to the optical fiber (0.5 NA, Inper, Hangzhou, China) for 10 minutes in 3 consecutive days before recording. In the baseline condition and under chemical manipulation, mice received von Frey stimuli of identical force and intensity. Inper Signal and Inper Data Analysis software were used to record and analyze results. The calcium signal changes were calculated as ΔF/F = (F − F0)/F0. MATLAB software was employed to adjust curve plots and heatmap colors, coordinates, and other parameters.

Chemogenetic manipulation included activation and inhibition of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit. scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL, and rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM3Dq-EGFP-WPREs or AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM4Di-EGFP-WPRE-pA (80 nL) was injected into the VPL. AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-EGFP-WPRE-pA (80 nL) was injected into the VPL in control groups. After 4 weeks for viral expression, CNO (5 mg/kg) was administered 30 minutes before behavioral tests.

Optogenetic experiments included activation and inhibition of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu and the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit. For the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu manipulation, rAAV2/9-CaMKIIa-ChR2(H134R)-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-pA or rAAV2/9-CaMKIIa-eNpHR3.0-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL, and an optical fiber was implanted in the VPL to activate or inhibit the glutamatergic neuron terminals projecting from S1HL to VPL. The control group received AAV2/9-CaMKIIa-EGFP-WPREs injections in S1HL. For the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit manipulation, scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA (125 nL) was injected into S1HL, and rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-ChR2(H134R)-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-pA or rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-eNpHR3.0-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-pA (80 nL) was injected into the VPL, with an optical fiber implanted in S1HL to activate or inhibit the terminals of VPL neurons projecting back to S1HL. The control group received rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-EGFP-WPREs injections in the VPL. 473 nm blue light (power 5 mW, frequency 20 Hz, pulse width 10 ms) was used for optogenetic activation, and 589 nm yellow light (power 10 mW, constant) was used for optogenetic inhibition during the behavioral tests.

2.7. Behavioral tests

Experimenters of behavioral tests are blinded to treatment conditions. Mice were acclimated to the behavioral room for 1 hour before testing. The movements of mice were recorded by a camera. After the test, mice were returned to clean cages, and the field was wiped with 75% alcohol before the next mouse was tested. ANY-maze software was used to analyze the movement trajectories.

The open field test (OFT) was used to assess locomotor activity, anxiety, and exploratory behavior. The open field was a 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm white-bottomed box without a lid. The central area was defined as a 25 cm × 25 cm region in the center of the field. Mice were allowed to explore the open field for 10 minutes.

The elevated plus maze (EPM) test was used to assess anxiety levels. Mice were acclimated to the behavioral room for 1 hour before testing. The elevated plus maze consists of a central platform (6 cm × 6 cm), 2 open arms (30 cm × 6 cm), and 2 closed arms (30 cm × 6 cm × 20 cm), placed 100 cm above the ground. Mice were allowed to explore the maze for 5 minutes.

The real-time place preference (RTPP) test was used to assess real-time aversion or preference related to pain or pain relief. The test was conducted in a rectangular apparatus with 2 side chambers (25 cm × 25 cm × 30 cm). On the first day, mice were placed in the apparatus and allowed to explore freely for 15 minutes. On the second day, one side chamber was designated as the laser stimulation side, whereas the other side had no light stimulation. The test began by placing the mouse on the nonlaser side, and whenever the mouse entered the laser side, the optogenetic stimulation was triggered.

2.8. Immunohistochemistry and microimaging

After the experiment, mice anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) underwent perfusion by ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brain and spinal cord were removed and immersed in 4% PFA for 24 hours, followed by gradient sucrose dehydration in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions in the following 3 days. The brain was sectioned using Thermo NX50 cryostat (Waltham, MA, USA) into 40-μm-thick brain slices and was stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). For immunohistochemistry staining, the Fos staining was performed one week after SNI modeling. Brain sections were collected and washed 3 times with PBS. After that, sections were blocked in PBS containing 10% normal goat serum (NGS) and 0.3% Triton X-100 for an hour, followed by primary antibody incubation overnight at 4°C (1:10000, rabbit, Cat#226008, Synaptic Systems, Göttingen, Germany). Then, sections were washed 3 times with PBS and incubated with the secondary antibody (1:1000, Cat#ab150078, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at room temperature for 2 hours. After being washed 3 times with PBS, sections were stained with DAPI and covered with glass coverslips. All brain and spinal cord slice images were captured by the Olympus VS120 virtual slide scanning system (Tokyo, Japan).

2.9. RNAscope in situ hybridization

Anesthetized mice underwent perfusion by ice-cold diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC)-PBS and 4% DEPC-PFA. After DEPC-PFA fixation, DEPC-sucrose gradient dehydration and sectioning, 16-μm-thick brain slices were mounted on glass slides. The probes targeting Fos (Cat# 316921-C1), CaMKIIa (Cat# 445231-C2), Slc17a6 (Cat# 1170921-C2), Slc32a1 (Cat# 319191-C3), and Gad2 (Cat# 439371-C3) mRNA were provided by ACD Bio (Newark, CA, USA) (for detailed procedures, refer to the ACD Bio RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit V2 User Manual). After signal labeling, brain sections were stained with DAPI and imaged by the OLYMPUS VS120 system. For experiments of colocalization of S1HL neurons labeled by CTB or mCherry with glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons, immunofluorescence counterstaining was performed after completing the RNAscope procedure. The primary antibodies include CTB antibody (1:5000, rabbit, Cat# ab34992, Abcam) and mCherry antibody (1:500, rabbit, Cat# ab167453, Abcam). The remaining procedures were carried out as described above.

2.10. Statistical analysis

ImageJ Fiji (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) was used for neuronal count or density analysis in immunofluorescence and RNAscope in situ hybridization, as well as viral tracing projection intensity quantification. Inper Data Analysis (Hangzhou, China) and MATLAB R2022a (Natick, MA) were used for processing and statistical analysis of fiber photometry results. Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (Boston, MA). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using paired or unpaired 2-tailed t tests. Multigroup comparisons were analyzed via 2-way analysis of variance. Significance levels are denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ns, no significance.

3. Results

3.1. Activation and projection of S1HLGlu neurons to VPLGlu in spared nerve injury mice

To investigate the predominant neuronal types activated by pain stimuli in S1HL, left hind paw SNI or sham surgery was performed to induce neuropathic pain in mice (Fig. 1A). Immunofluorescence staining of Fos, the marker of neuron activation, was performed on day 7 and day 30 after surgery (Figs. 1B and C). Increased Fos-positive neurons were observed in bilateral S1HL of the SNI group compared with sham group (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1A-C, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). RNAscope in situ hybridization showed that 93.77% of Fos-positive neurons expressed CaMKIIa mRNA, whereas only 3.79% expressed Gad2 mRNA, indicating that glutamatergic neurons are the predominantly activated neuronal population in S1HL during neuropathic pain. To further investigate how S1HL regulates pain through downstream projections, rAAV-hSyn-DIO-Synaptophysin-mCherry was injected into the unilateral S1HL of CaMKIIa-Cre mice (Fig. 1F) or Gad2-Cre mice (Figs. S1D and S1E, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). The whole brain tracing results revealed that S1HL glutamatergic neurons (S1HLGlu) primarily project to thalamic VPL and PO nuclei (Fig. 1G). The GABAergic neurons rarely send descending projections to the thalamus (Fig. S1F, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). The synaptophysin fluorescence intensity ratio in VPL (82.22%) was higher than that in PO (66.08%) (Fig. 1H). In addition, synaptophysin expression was detected in contralateral S1HL of CaMKIIa-Cre mice, suggesting interhemispheric S1 communication as previous studies indicated (Figs. S1, G–I, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). Furthermore, retrograde tracing from VPL was performed to identify the upstream neuron types of S1HL (Fig. 1I). CTB-555 was injected into the VPL. Neuronal somata were traced in layer VI (L6) of S1HL, as well as other sensory cortices including the forelimb region of S1 (S1FL), trunk region of S1 (S1Tr), S2, ACx, etc. (Fig. 1J and Fig. S2A–C, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). The result of RNAscope in situ hybridization showed that most CTB-expressing neurons were colocalized with CaMKIIa-positive glutamatergic S1HL neurons (94.54%) (Figs. 1K and L), and VPL was composed almost entirely of vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (VGluT2)–positive glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 1M). These results suggest that S1HLGlu are the predominantly activated neuronal type and serve as the major neuronal population sending dense projections to the VPL glutamatergic neuron (VPLGlu).

3.2. Distinct populations of VPL neurons receive cortical and peripheral inputs

VPL is an important sensory relay nucleus that receives peripheral inputs from the spinothalamic tract and the medial lemniscus.12 Consistently, by injecting rAAV2/Retro-hSyn-mCherry into the right VPL, we retrogradely labeled neurons in the contralateral (left) lumbar spinal cord dorsal horn (DH) (Figs. 2A–C). Almost no labeled neuron was detected in the lumbar spinal cord after rAAV2/Retro-hSyn-mCherry injection into PO (Figs. S2D–F, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). Given that viral tracing results suggested that the VPL serves as a relay nucleus involved in both peripheral input and cortical feedback, we further investigated whether 2 distinct neuronal populations in the VPL separately received projections of the corticothalamic tract and spinothalamic tract. We employed 2 virus tracing strategies by injecting rAAV2/1-hSyn-Flpo into S1HL, rAAV2/9-hSyn-fDIO-mCherry and AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-EGFP cocktail into VPL, and scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre into left lumbar DH or injecting scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre into S1HL, AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-EGFP into VPL, and rAAV-CAG-mWGA-mCherry left lumbar DH (Figs. 2D and E). Results showed that these 2 neuronal populations showed minimal spatial overlap (Figs. 2F and G), indicating the potential differences in spatially specific distribution and function of these 2 groups of neurons.

3.3. The S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit is hyperactivated in spared nerve injury mice during peripheral stimulation

To investigate whether the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit participates in pain regulation, in vivo fiber photometry was performed to assess neuronal activity in response to von Frey stimulation. scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre was injected into S1HL, and rAAV-hSyn-FLEX-GCaMP8m into VPL (Figs. 3A and B). One week after SNI or sham surgery, von Frey stimulation was applied to the left hind paw during fiber photometry (Fig. 3C). Calcium signals in VPL neurons receiving S1HL projections showed significantly higher responses in SNI mice versus sham mice, indicated by significantly increased area under the curve (AUC) and peak compared with the sham group (Figs. 3D–F). Additionally, we simultaneously recorded bilateral VPL calcium signal responses to left paw stimulation in left-sided SNI model mice. Interestingly, we observed highly synchronized calcium activity in bilateral VPL nuclei, suggesting potential interconnection of S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit via interhemispheric communication structure (Fig. S3, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). These findings suggested that the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit is hyperactivated in the state of neuropathic pain during peripheral stimulation.

3.4. Activation or inhibition of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit facilitates or alleviates pain

After identifying the hyperactivation state of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit in the neuropathic pain state, we applied optogenetic and chemogenetic approaches to manipulate this pathway to investigate its function in pain regulation and associated behaviors. For optogenetic activation, rAAV2/9-CamkIIa-ChR2 was injected into the S1HL (Fig. 4A). For optogenetic inhibition, rAAV2/9-CamkIIa-eNpHR3.0 was injected into the S1HL (Fig. 4B). In both experiments, optical fibers were implanted above the VPL to manipulate the terminals of S1HLGlu neurons projecting to the VPL (Figs. 4C and D). Behavioral analysis using the von Frey test showed that optogenetic activation of the circuit significantly reduced the mechanical pain threshold of the left hind paw in both male and female mice, indicating the induction of mechanical hypersensitivity (Fig. 4E). In mice subjected to the SNI model, which exhibited a marked decrease in pain threshold on the operated side by day 7, 14, and 30 postsurgery, optogenetic inhibition of the circuit significantly alleviated this mechanical evoked pain hypersensitivity in acute and chronic phase (Fig. 4F). Meanwhile, optogenetic inhibition markedly attenuated the spontaneous pain-like behaviors in mice on day 30 post-SNI (Fig. 4G). For chemogenetic manipulation, we adopted a dual-virus Cre-dependent strategy. scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre was injected into the S1HL, combined with injection of either rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM3Dq for activation (Fig. 4H) or rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM4Di for inhibition (Fig. 4I) into the VPL. The downstream VPLGlu were subsequently activated or inhibited by injection of CNO (Figs. 4J and K). Correspondingly, chemogenetic activation of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit also resulted in mechanical hypersensitivity in mice of both genders (Fig. 4L), whereas its chemogenetic inhibition significantly alleviated mechanical hypersensitivity and spontaneous pain-like behaviors in the SNI model (Figs. 4M and N). Interestingly, inhibition of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit did not influence the baseline pain threshold (Figs. 4F and M). Tetanus toxin-mediated persistent chemical inhibition of synaptic transmission of the downstream VPLGlu also did not affect the pain threshold under the baseline condition but exerted significant analgesic effects during the late acute phase and chronic phase of pain (Fig. S4A–C, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). These findings suggest that the regulatory role of this circuit is relatively independent of peripheral nociceptive afferent pathways. Therefore, inhibiting the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit minimally affected nociceptive transmission under physiological conditions. However, its markedly heightened activity under neuropathic pain conditions mediates central sensitization, and activation of this circuit under physiological conditions mimics the state of pain hypersensitivity.

3.5. The S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit mainly projects back to glutamatergic neurons in S1HL

To further explore the downstream mechanisms by which S1HLGlu-VPLGlu regulates pain, we labeled the terminal fibers of projections of this neural circuit by injecting scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre into S1HL and rAAV-hSyn-DIO-Synaptophysin-mCherry into VPL (Fig. 5A). The result of whole-brain viral tracing revealed that S1HLGlu-VPLGlu predominantly projects back to cortical regions, mainly, the S1L and S2, rarely to other areas (Figs. 5B and C). The axonal terminals were distributed across layers of S1HL, with a primary distribution in layer IV (L4) (Fig. 5D, Figs. S8B and 8C, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). It is worth noting that this pattern of corticothalamocortical projections also exists in previously reported S2 and ACx corticothalamic pain modulation circuits and serves as their main downstream projection pathway (Fig. S8D–I, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545).6,27 Furthermore, to label the somata of recipient neurons in S1HL that receive the main returning projections from the S1HLGlu–VPLGlu circuit, we employed an anterograde transsynaptic label based on antibody-like sensors (ATLAS) viral tracing strategy to achieve sequential trans-synaptic anterograde tracing crossing 2 levels.18 AAV-hSyn-ATLASSnCre with the helper AAV-hSyn-HA-BACE was injected into S1HL, and AAV-DIO-ATLASSnFlp with the helper AAV-hSyn-HA-BACE was injected into VPL. Simultaneously, the rAAV-hSyn-fDIO-mCherry injected in S1HL was for labeling the third-order neuronal cell bodies (Fig. 5E). Prominently labeled neurons were observed across layers of S1HL (Fig. 5F). To identify whether both glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons in S1 receive projections from VPL, we applied a retrograde monosynaptic rabies virus (RV) tracing strategy with Cre-dependent helper viruses (rAAV-EF1a-DIO-mCherry-TVA and rAAV-EF1a-DIO-N2oG) injected into the S1HL of CaMKIIa-Cre and Gad2-Cre mice to facilitate retrograde monosynaptic spread of CVS-EnVA-ΔG-EGFP injected at the same site 21 days later (Figs. 5G and H). Dense neuronal somata were traced to the VPL as the upstream input for both types of neurons (Figs. 5I–L). Because both excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the cortex receive projections from the VPL, we further used RNAscope in situ hybridization to identify the neuronal types in S1HL that receive the main returning projections from the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu circuit (Fig. 5M). The result showed that 88.57% of mCherry-positive neurons expressed CaMKIIa mRNA, whereas only 8.09% expressed Gad2 mRNA, indicating the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit is predominantly a glutamatergic excitatory pathway (Fig. 5N).

3.6. Modulation of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu pathway bidirectionally alters S1HLGlu neuron responsivity

Because this glutamatergic circuit originates from the S1HLGlu and ultimately projects back to the S1HLGlu, we further investigated its specific effect on S1HLGlu responses to pain stimuli in this recurrent projection. scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre-pA and rAAV-CaMKIIa-GCamp6m-WPRE-pA cocktail were injected into S1HL with an optical fiber implanted above S1HL, and rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM3Dq was injected into VPL (Figs. 6A and B). Following adequate viral expression, saline or CNO was administered intraperitoneally at different time points, and calcium signal recordings in S1HLGlu under graded von Frey stimulations (0.02, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, and 0.6 g) were performed under both baseline and circuit-activated conditions (Figs. 6C–E). For general subthreshold stimuli (0.02, 0.04, and 0.07 g), the CNO group exhibited a significantly enhanced calcium signal response compared with the saline group, characterized by markedly increased AUC and moderately elevated peak. However, under higher intensities of pain stimulation (0.16, 0.4, and 0.6 g), no significant difference in calcium signal responses was observed between the CNO and saline group (Figs. 6F–I). Similar findings were obtained in female mice (Fig. S9, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545).

3.7. Activation or inhibition of the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit facilitates or alleviates pain

Next, we manipulated the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit to investigate its role in pain regulation. scAAV2/1-hSyn-Cre was injected into S1HL, rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-ChR2 or rAAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-eNpHR3.0 was injected into VPL, and optical fibers were implanted in S1HL (Figs. 8A–D). Similar to the results of S1HLGlu-VPLGlu manipulation, activation of S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu significantly reduced the baseline pain threshold, whereas its inhibition significantly alleviated the mechanical hyperalgesia and spontaneous pain behaviors of male and female SNI mice (Figs. 8E–G). Activation or inhibition of S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu mainly affects the pain perception without inducing or alleviating pain-related anxiety or aversion emotions (Fig. S11, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C545). Therefore, the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit primarily and specifically acts on pain modulation.

4. Discussion

This study revealed a pathological positive feedback glutamatergic loop from S1HL to VPL and back to S1HL in the SNI-induced NP model. Under NP conditions, activated S1HLGlu neurons specifically project to a subpopulation of VPLGlu neurons that project back to S1HLGlu to establish an excessively active bidirectional circuit. Activating this S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit exacerbates pain behaviors and amplifies S1HLGlu neuron-mediated hyperalgesia, whereas inhibiting the circuit alleviates pain and reduces the pathologically heightened responsiveness of S1HLGlu. These results indicate that this glutamatergic corticothalamocortical loop is specifically reinforced following NP injury, serving as a core central mechanism for driving allodynia.

Although the involvement of the corticothalamic circuits in pain modulation has been observed, their underlying downstream mechanisms have not been fully elucidated.4 Our investigation of its downstream neural circuits revealed that S1HLGlu-VPLGlu mainly projected back to the cortex, completing a positive feedback circuit that mediates pain hypersensitivity. Through the virus tracing, similar patterns were also observed in previously reported S2-VPL and ACx-VPL circuits, with their major downstream projections returning to the initiating cortex region (Figs. 5A–C).6,27 We detected that S1HLGlu L6 neurons send projections to VPLGlu, and the neuronal populations receiving return projections from VPL are mainly located in S1HL L4, with the most densely innervated nerve endings. Previous studies have reported that activating L6 neurons in S1 significantly increases spike frequency in the superficial layers (L2/3 and L4) of S1.29 Our study indicates the potential role played by the relay function of the thalamic circuit in this phenomenon.

Multiple approaches were applied in this study to confirm the role of this circuit in pain amplification. First, optogenetic and chemogenetic activation of this loop lowered baseline pain thresholds, whereas its inhibition alleviated mechanical allodynia in a nerve injury mouse model without affecting baseline pain thresholds in naive mice. This finding carries significant pathophysiological implications: under physiological conditions, this circuit may remain in a low-activity or suppressed state, thereby not interfering with baseline pain perception; however, under pathological conditions such as SNI, the loop becomes abnormally activated, initiating its amplification function. This amplifies originally subthreshold or mild nociceptive inputs into intense pain signals, resulting in hyperalgesia. Second, RNAscope in situ hybridization demonstrated that S1HLGlu-VPLGlu majorly returns to glutamatergic neurons within S1HL. Most importantly, in vivo fiber photometry recordings directly demonstrated that the activity of this loop synchronizes with the potentiation of subthreshold pain perception in S1HLGlu neurons during graded mechanical pain stimuli. This provides the most direct dynamic evidence for the “positive feedback amplifier” hypothesis. The operational mechanism of the loop may be as follows: peripheral nociceptive stimuli activate the spinal-thalamic ascending pathway, triggering initial responses in VPLGlu and S1HLGlu; subsequently, descending excitatory projections from S1HLGlu further activate VPLGlu, whose return projections again enhance S1HLGlu responses. This cyclical process allows weak nociceptive signals to oscillate and gain strength within the loop, ultimately leading to amplified pain perception in the cortex. This mechanism resembles the cortical-thalamic feedback gain control observed in auditory or visual systems such as the ACx-medial geniculate nucleus-ACx circuit13 and the visual cortex–pulvinar (Pul)–cortical circuit15 but is elucidated here for the first time in the pain regulation at the level of cell types and neural circuits. Therefore, the cortex–thalamus–cortex feedback mechanism may serve as a common mode in facilitating pain.

Currently, higher-order and lower-order neurons in the thalamus are primarily distinguished based on the nuclei. For example, neurons in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), medial geniculate nucleus (MGN), and ventral posterior nucleus (VP) are referred to as first-order neurons, whereas the Pul, PO, mediodorsal nucleus (MD), and lateroposterior nucleus (LP) are generally classified as higher-order thalamic nuclei.7 VPL is the primary thalamic nucleus that receives somatosensory pain input from the peripheral nervous system, whereas we notice that a subgroup of VPL neurons also receives extensive descending cortical feedback projections. Inhibition of these higher-order neurons produces little effect on normal peripheral sensory input. In addition, we observed that downstream projections from S1HLGlu-VPLGlu not only target the S1HL but also send relatively sparse projection to S2, which may also play a role in pain regulation. Therefore, our study reveals for the first time that the VPL may contain both first-order and higher-order neurons, indicating that the VPL serves as a unique information integration hub, participating in corticothalamic amplification of sensory information and thereby mediating central sensitization. This finding highlights the diversity and functional complexity of thalamic neurons. Although we distinguished these 2 neuronal populations based on information transmission and functional characteristics, we did not further define them by their molecular identity or electrophysiological characteristics. Nor did we investigate the direct interactions between them. Future studies could employ electrophysiology and Patch-seq techniques to characterize these 2 neuronal populations.

Previous studies have suggested that the POGlu-S1HLGlu circuit is also involved in pain regulation.28 In this study, anterograde tracing from S1HL indicated that the PO also receives corticothalamic feedback from S1, but unlike the VPL, few upstream neuronal cell bodies were traced in the lumbar spinal dorsal horn from PO. Based on these findings, the corticothalamic circuits from S1 to VPL and PO may represent 2 parallel pathways with different functions. The bidirectional connection between S1 and VPL is primarily responsible for transmitting peripheral information to the cortex and further amplifying it through positive feedback, with limited involvement in pain-related emotional regulation. The PO widely and diffusely projects to the sensory, motor, and limbic cortices, such as the cingulate cortex and insula,3,23 implicating its potential role in pain-related emotions and autonomic responses.

The known central pain modulation networks, such as the periaqueductal gray-rostral ventromedial medulla–dorsal horn descending inhibition and facilitation system, primarily focus on “top-down” modulation at the brainstem and spinal cord levels.5 The S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu pathway represents a higher-level regulatory system situated between the thalamus and cortex. This discovery provides a precise target for treating neuropathic pain. Traditional pharmacological or interventional approaches often target broad neurotransmitter systems such as opioids and GABAergic systems,14 accompanied by numerous side effects. This study demonstrates that specifically modulating this relatively discrete excitatory connection between S1HLGlu and VPLGlu can alleviate pathological pain hypersensitivity without affecting baseline physiological pain perception, exerting a protective function. Based on the excitatory glutamatergic nature of this pathway revealed in our study, low-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation and transcranial direct current stimulation can suppress cortical excitability, representing a promising noninvasive strategy to inhibit this pathway. Additionally, targeting AMPA/NMDA receptors or VGluT2 could disrupt nociceptive transmission. These mechanism-guided intervention strategies, spanning both pharmacological and neuromodulatory approaches, strengthen the logical bridge from fundamental discovery to clinical application.

This study observed that the bilateral S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuits both exhibited significant calcium signal responses to unilateral hind limb stimulation. Also, synaptic terminals were traced in the contralateral S1HL following synaptophysin injection into the ipsilateral S1HL. Such bilateral hemispheric interactions in pain perception and modulation have also been validated in previous studies. S2 glutamatergic neurons (S2Glu) respond to bilateral hind paw stimulation,6 and S2Glu project to the contralateral S2, mediating analgesic effects induced by pain stimulation on the opposite limb.9 Typically, the contralateral cortex responds to peripheral stimulation. However, bilateral cortical regions are interconnected with areas near the midsagittal plane exhibiting stronger and more dense mutual projections,26 which may underlie the synchronized bilateral responses recorded in our study.

The effects of this circuit were observed in both male and female mice, which enhances the scientific rigor of our findings and improves the translational relevance to human pain conditions that affect both sexes. Future research could validate the universality of this circuit in other pain models, such as inflammatory pain and cancer pain. Additionally, although the study focuses on glutamatergic excitatory connections, this circuit is likely to be regulated by local inhibitory interneurons or output from inhibitory nuclei. Previous studies have also reported that pain can induce gamma-band oscillations generated by parvalbumin-positive (PV) interneurons in S1, which participate in encoding pain intensity.25 The cortical neurons also send descending projections to the inhibitory thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN). Thus, the interaction and balance between the S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu circuit and GABAergic neurons in pain regulation remains to be explored. Moreover, the application of single-cell sequencing, synaptic proteomics, and imaging techniques is expected to elucidate the molecular and plasticity mechanisms underlying the hyperactivation of S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu in the NP state.

In summary, this study elucidates that S1HLGlu-VPLGlu-S1HLGlu is abnormally activated as a positive feedback amplifier under neuropathic pain conditions, driving the development of hyperalgesia. This finding provides critical theoretical groundwork for developing precise analgesic strategies targeting corticothalamocortical neural circuits.
