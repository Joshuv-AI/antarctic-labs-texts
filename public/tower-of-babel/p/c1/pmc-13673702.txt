DRP1 lysine 616 lactylation exacerbates cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury by activating the STING inflammatory pathway
Authors: Xiaoqi Ma, Dan Xin, Xianjia Li, Zhenhao Ying, Zifeng Li, Wenli Wang, Zhigang Mei, Jinwen Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemia-hypoxia-induced inflammation and glycolysis are linked to the severity of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (CIRI), but the mechanisms are unclear. Current research suggests that the inflammatory response of immune cells activated by STING is a key regulatory molecule in cellular inflammatory damage. However, the specific mechanisms underlying STING-mediated CIRI inflammatory responses remain unclear. This study found that STING expression was specifically elevated in microglia in the damaged side of the hippocampus in CIRI model mice, and this elevation was positively correlated with the severity of CIRI. Our previous research indicated that the dynamic process of mitochondrial fusion and fission is closely associated with CIRI. Building on this, we integrated glycolysis, mitochondrial fission, and the STING inflammatory pathway. Mechanistically, our data suggest that DRP1 K616 is a critical candidate site involved in DRP1 lactylation-associated regulation, which promotes STING pathway activation and contributes to the progression of CIRI. In conclusion, our findings offer substantial evidence that lactate-driven DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission facilitates the involvement of the STING inflammatory pathway in CIRI. These results suggest that modulating lactate metabolism may serve as a crucial upstream strategy for therapeutic intervention in CIRI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03093-7.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, cGAS-STING pathway, Microglia, Mitochondrial fission, DRP1, Lactylation

Introduction

CIRI is a complex pathological process that occurs when blood supply is restored to brain tissue after a period of ischemia. While reperfusion is essential for tissue survival, it paradoxically leads to further damage, primarily through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and metabolic reprogramming [1, 2]. Ischemia and reperfusion trigger the activation of microglia and infiltration of peripheral immune cells, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which exacerbate neuronal injury and compromise the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [3, 4]. In addition to inflammation, CIRI is associated with profound metabolic alterations. Ischemic conditions force neurons to shift from oxidative phosphorylation to anaerobic glycolysis to maintain ATP levels. However, during reperfusion, this metabolic switch persists abnormally, resulting in lactate accumulation, intracellular acidosis, and further oxidative damage [5]. Mitochondrial dynamics, including fusion and fission, are essential for maintaining mitochondrial function under stress. During CIRI, the balance between fusion proteins (e.g., MFN1/2, OPA1) and fission proteins (e.g., DRP1, FIS1) is disrupted, resulting in excessive mitochondrial fragmentation, loss of membrane potential, and initiation of mitophagy or cell death pathways [6]. Thus, understanding the crosstalk among inflammation, glycolytic reprogramming, oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial dynamics provides a comprehensive framework for developing novel therapeutic strategies against CIRI.

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway is a key component of innate immunity that senses cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and triggers inflammatory responses via interferon (IFN) production [7]. Recent studies have demonstrated that activation of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of CIRI [8, 9]. Given that cGAS specifically recognizes DNA, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released into the cytoplasm following mitochondrial damage has been identified as a critical upstream trigger of the cGAS–STING inflammatory pathway. In various disease, mitochondrial dysfunction-induced mtDNA leakage has been shown to activate the cGAS–STING signaling cascade and elicit downstream inflammatory responses [10–14]. However, the involvement of this pathway in CIRI remains poorly understood. Given these findings, targeting mitochondrial damage and subsequently intervening in the cGAS–STING pathway represents a potential therapeutic strategy for alleviating inflammation-induced brain damage after stroke. Elucidating the molecular connection between mitochondrial function and the cGAS-STING pathway could reveal new targets for CIRI intervention.

An imbalance in mitochondrial fission and fusion is key in CIRI [15]. Dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), a highly conserved dynamin family member, plays a crucial role in mitochondrial biogenesis and in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and integrity. It serves as the key regulator of mitochondrial fission [16]. Under normal conditions, DRP1 moves from the cytoplasm to the mitochondrial outer membrane, forming ring-like structures that facilitate mitochondrial fission via a GTP-dependent process. Stress conditions like oxidative stress and hypoxia increase DRP1 expression and its mitochondrial translocation, disrupting the balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion [17, 18]. Abundant experimental evidence has demonstrated that the activation of DRP1 and mitochondrial fission contributes to cerebral ischemic injury [19–22]. DRP1 is subjected to a variety of posttranscriptional modifications, including S-nitroylation, SUMOylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation, which can activate or inhibit fission activity [15, 23]. However, the impact of lactate and lactylation modification on DRP1 remains unexplored.

Lactic acid, a byproduct of glycolysis, accumulates significantly in damaged regions following cerebral ischemia and reperfusion, serving as a clinical marker for CIRI [24]. Traditionally regarded as detrimental, changes in metabolism and epigenetics are implicated in disease pathogenesis [25]. Lysine lactylation (Kla) is a post-translational modification induced by lactate. When lactate, whether endogenously produced or exogenously supplied, reaches a critical concentration within cells, acylation modifications such as Kla modulate protein activity and conformation through the covalent attachment of chemical groups or small molecules to the side chains of amino acids [24]. Kla plays a crucial role in the immune response mechanisms of macrophages [26]. However, research on the effects of Kla on CIRI remains limited, with most studies focusing primarily on histone Kla [27]. Investigating the impact of Kla on mitochondrial fission and fusion in microglia may provide a novel mechanism for mitigating inflammatory damage associated with CIRI.

This study explores the link between STING and CIRI progression, seeking therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway. Through bioinformatics analyses and the use of a mouse model of CIRI, we assessed the specific expression of STING in microglia. In vivo, STING expression was modulated via intracerebroventricular injection of adeno-associated virus to investigate its regulatory role in CIRI. In vitro, an oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R)-induced injury model of murine BV2 microglial cells was employed to examine the intrinsic connection between DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission and the activation of the STING inflammatory pathway. Notably, we investigated a previously uncharacterized post-translational modification, Kla, of DRP1, elucidating the primary modification site and its functional significance. Our findings suggest that aberrant lactate metabolism in microglia may promote STING pathway activation through DRP1 K616 lactylation, and that disruption of this process may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for reducing inflammation-related damage in CIRI. This approach may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for reducing inflammation-related damage in CIRI.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

In total, 100 C57BL/6JNifdc mice (8 weeks, weight 20–25 g) and 10 SD rats (8 weeks, weight 200–220 g) were purchased from the Hunan Slike Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (license No. SCXK, Xiang, 2019-0004). All mice were housed in the Hunan University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Laboratory Animal Center (license No. SCXK, Xiang, 2019‐0009) under temperatures of 20–25 °C, humidity of 50%–70%, 12‐h light/dark cycle, and good ventilation with free access to water and food. The mice were allowed to adapt to the new environment for 7 days before the experiment. All experimental protocols and animal‐related activities adhered to the National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Furthermore, the experimental protocol was approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethical Committee of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (No. LL2021030201).Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a randomization method. All outcome assessments and data analyses were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Chemical reagents

Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) was purchased from Sigma (C2759-100MG, Mitophagy agonist). Mdivi-1 was purchased from Sigma (M0199-5MG, Mitochondrial division inhibitor). RU.521 was purchased from Invitrogen (inh-ru521-2, the cGAS inhibitor). L-lactic acid was purchased from Solarbio (IL0540, Beijing, China). 2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) was bought from Solarbio (T8170, Wuhan, China).

CIRI model

The CIRI model refers to the methods previously used by our group [28]. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (oxygen delivered at 0.5 L/min with 3% isoflurane for induction and 1.5% for maintenance). Then, a small incision was made along the midline of the neck, and the muscle tissue was gently separated. The proximal end of the common carotid artery (CCA) and the distal end of the external carotid artery (ECA) were ligated. A suitable monofilament nylon suture (Beijing Cinontech Co. Ltd., China) was carefully inserted into the internal carotid artery (ICA) through a small hole opened on the CCA to occlude the blood flow. The monofilament nylon suture was then gradually withdrawn after occluding the designated duration for each experimental group, thereby permitting a 24-hour reperfusion of blood flow. In the sham group, the mice did not receive the monofilament nylon suture; however, identical surgical procedures were conducted as in the model group. During the surgical procedure, body temperature was maintained at 36.5–37.5 °C using an electric heating pad, and respiration and anesthetic depth were continuously monitored. All measurements were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Evaluation of cerebral infarct volume

The cerebral infarct volume was assessed by 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining as previously described. At 24 h after reperfusion, mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 0.9% normal saline to remove intravascular blood. The brains were rapidly removed and placed on ice for cooling, then sectioned into five coronal slices (2 mm thickness) using a brain matrix. Brain sections were incubated in 2% TTC solution prepared in 0.9% saline at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Viable tissue was stained red, whereas infarcted tissue remained pale white.

Images of stained sections were captured and analyzed using ImageJ software to quantify infarct volume. To minimize the influence of cerebral edema, infarct volume was corrected using the following formula: Corrected infarct volume (%) = {[total infarct volume − (contralateral hemisphere volume − ipsilateral hemisphere volume)] / contralateral hemisphere volume} × 100%. All measurements were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Observation of cerebral blood flow

Laser speckle imaging was used to monitor cerebral blood flow (CBF) in mice. The mice were positioned on a stereotaxic frame, and a cranial window (4 × 6 mm²) was carefully thinned on the right skull while preserving the integrity of the dura mater. CBF was recorded using a Moor FLPI-2 blood flow imaging system (Moor Instruments Ltd., Axminster, UK) at baseline (before MCAO), immediately after occlusion, and after 24 h of reperfusion.Successful induction of MCAO was defined as a reduction of CBF to less than 20% of baseline levels during occlusion. Mice that failed to reach this threshold, showed inadequate recovery of CBF after reperfusion, or died during surgery were excluded from further analysis. All measurements were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE)

The mice were anesthetized and perfused transcardially with normal saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. The brain samples were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Then, each sample was embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 4 μm coronally. The brain slices were subjected to HE according to standard protocols (Wuhan baiqiandu technology CO., LTD). Histopathological changes were subsequently observed under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Briefly, mouse brain tissue was postfixed in freshly precooled 4% paraformaldehyde (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.4) for 48 h, followed by gradient dehydration with 20%–30% sucrose. Brain tissue was processed for embedding and frozen sectioning using OCT. Sections were 30 μm thick coronal sections. To eliminate residual peroxidase, the sections were incubated with 3% H2O2 for half an hour. Sections were then blocked with 10% BSA and incubated with STING antibody (1:2000) at 4 °C overnight. Plaques were stained with ABC and DAB methods and counted at ×40 magnification.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Procure brain tissue from the hippocampal region of mice and BV2 cell culture supernatant, following the protocol provided with the kit. Conduct the assay and determine the absorbance at 450 nm utilizing an enzyme-linked reagent to quantify the expression levels of IFN-1, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and TGF-β. Subsequently, calculate the cytokine concentrations by employing a standard curve.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The potential morphological changes in mitochondria within BV2 cells were examined utilizing TEM. Initially, the cells underwent fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in a phosphate buffer for a duration of 4 h, followed by post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide in phosphate buffer for 2 h. The cells were then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series, with acetone serving as a substitute for ethanol. Subsequently, the cells were embedded in EPON 812 resin. Ultrathin sections, each 60 nm in thickness, were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Imaging was conducted using an HT7800 TEM (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted from mouse hippocampal tissue and BV2 cells using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The protein lysates were transferred to PVDF transfer membranes, sealed, and exposed to appropriate antibodies. Protein bands were detected using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analysed using Image J software.

Primary antibodies used for western blotting were β-actin (1:20,000, Proteintch, 66009-1-Ig), Cgas (1:1000, Proteintch, 29958-1-AP), STING (1:2000, Proteintch, 19851-1-AP), p65 (1:1000, Proteintch 66535-1-Ig), p-p65-S536 (1:1000, Proteintch, 82335-1-RR), DRP1 (1:4000, abcam, ab184247), Anti-L-Lactyl (1:1000,PTM BIO, PTM-1401RM), respectively. Goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:10,000, Proteintch, SA00001-1) and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP (1:10,000, Proteintch, B900210) were used as secondary antibodies.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using a kit designed for isolating 1 µg of RNA. Subsequently, the isolated RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) utilizing the TaKaRa reverse transcription kit. The expression levels of target mRNAs in BV2 cells were quantified following the protocol provided by the TaKaRa fluorescence quantitative kit (TB Green). The relative expression levels were determined using the 2^−△△CT method, and statistical analyses of the differences among groups were conducted using SPSS software.

Immunofluorescence staining

The morphology of mitochondria in BV2 cells was assessed utilizing the MitoTracker Red CMXRos probe (100 nmol/L, 30 min at 37 °C; Invitrogen, M7512). Imaging was conducted with a confocal laser scanning microscope (SP8; LEICA). Quantitative analysis of mitochondria was performed, and the proportion of cells exhibiting fragmented mitochondria, characterized as small and round, was calculated.

BV2 cells were cultured on coverslips and subjected to stimulation as previously described. A medium containing 100 nM MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Invitrogen, M7512) was introduced, and the cells were incubated for 20 min under standard culture conditions. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with goat serum for 60 min. Following this, the cells were incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and subsequently stained with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature. DAPI was employed to stain the cell nuclei, with cells being washed with PBS buffer between each procedural step. The stained cells were subsequently examined using confocal laser-scanning microscopy (Zeiss LSM 780, Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective. For immunofluorescence, primary antibodies utilized included anti-DRP1 (1:250, Abcam, ab184247) and anti-dsDNA (1:100, PROGEN, AC-30-10), alongside MitoSOX (5 mM, Invitrogen, M36008). The co-localization of mitochondria with DRP1 and dsDNA was quantified using ImageJ software. Mitochondrial network morphology parameters, such as mitochondrial length, branch junctions, and the number of mitochondria, were analyzed using Fiji software through binary and skeleton methods, in accordance with previously published literature [29].

Mitochondrial isolation

Mitochondrial isolation was conducted utilizing a Mitochondria Isolation Kit (ab110170, Abcam). Initially, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 5 mg/mL Reagent A and subjected to mechanical disruption using a Dounce Homogenizer to facilitate lysis, followed by incubation on ice for 10 min. The resulting homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min to separate the cytoplasmic fraction. The supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min to pellet the mitochondria. Finally, the mitochondrial pellet was gently resuspended in Reagent C and stored at − 80 °C in aliquots for subsequent analysis.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) constructs and stereotaxic injection

AAV vectors were used to induce STING overexpression or knockdown. For STING overexpression, an AAV vector encoding mouse Sting1 (AAV-STING; TOS1656-v4) under the control of the microglia-targeting hIBA1a promoter was constructed using the following expression cassette: pAAV [Exp]-hIBA1a> mSting1[NM_001289591.1]-GSG-P2A-mCherry-miR124T-WPRE3. The hIBA1a promoter and miR124T regulatory element were incorporated to restrict transgene expression predominantly to microglia and reduce unintended neuronal expression.

For STING knockdown, three AAV-based miR30-shRNA vectors targeting mouse Sting1 were generated. The targeting sequences were AGAGGTCACCGCTCCAAATAT for AAV-shSTING-1, GCATCAAGAATCGGGTTTATT for AAV-shSTING-2, and CAACATTCGATTCCGAGATAT for AAV-shSTING-3. AAV-shSTING-1 exhibited the greatest knockdown efficiency during preliminary screening and was therefore used in subsequent experiments. Corresponding AAV vectors carrying an empty expression cassette or a non-targeting shRNA sequence were used as the oe-NC and sh-NC controls, respectively. All vectors were obtained from Haixing Biosciences (Jiangsu, China). A total volume of 1 μL of the corresponding viral suspension was infused at a rate of 0.1 μL/min. After injection, the micropipette was left in place for 10–15 min to allow viral diffusion and minimize reflux along the injection tract.

For evaluation of microglial targeting, AAV5-hIBA1a-mCherry or AAV5-hIBA1a-EGFP reporter vectors were injected into the hippocampal CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus regions. C57BL/6J mice were anaesthetised with 2–5% isoflurane and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus (Digital Stereotaxic Instrument for Mice, RWD Life Science, China). After exposure of the skull, burr holes were drilled at the designated coordinates relative to bregma. The injection coordinates were anteroposterior − 2.00 mm, mediolateral + 1.00 mm, and dorsoventral − 2.00 mm for the dentate gyrus; anteroposterior − 2.00 mm, mediolateral + 1.50 mm, and dorsoventral − 1.40 mm for CA1; and anteroposterior − 2.00 mm, mediolateral + 2.50 mm, and dorsoventral − 2.20 mm for CA3. AAV was delivered using a glass micropipette with a tip diameter of approximately 20 μm, prepared using a P-2000 laser puller (Sutter Instrument, USA). Following AAV administration, mice were allowed to recover for 4 weeks to permit the establishment of stable AAV-mediated transgene expression. After efficient microglial transduction had been confirmed at the 4-week time point, CIRI modelling and all subsequent experiments were initiated.

Plasmids

The full-length mouse Dnm1l coding sequence (NM_001360007.2) was cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector to generate the wild-type DRP1 expression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-mDnm1l). The plasmid construct was verified by diagnostic restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing. Because BV2 microglial cells are of mouse origin, all site-directed mutagenesis experiments were designed based on the mouse DRP1/Dnm1l sequence. According to sequence alignment, the candidate lactylation-associated site K581 identified from rat proteomics data corresponds to K587 in mouse DRP1.

To functionally evaluate the contribution of candidate lysine residues, site-substitution mutants were generated at K587 and K616 by homologous recombination-based mutagenesis. Specifically, lysine residues at each site were replaced with glutamine, threonine, asparagine, or proline, generating DRP1 K587Q, K587T, K587N, K587P, K616Q, K616T, K616N, and K616P constructs. These mutants were used as site-substitution variants for functional assessment rather than definitive biochemical mimics of specific lactylation states. DNA fragments containing homologous terminal sequences were amplified from the DRP1 plasmid using the corresponding primers. Detailed primer sequences and plasmid information are provided in the Supplementary Materials. The plasmid design scheme and sequencing validation of lysine site substitutions are shown in Fig. 6B–C.

Cell culture and transfection

BV2 cells were purchased from the Abiowell Biotechnology Co (AW-CNM081, Hunan, China). and cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) was supplied with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Procell) for maintaining cells in a humid environment containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Before establishing OGD/R model, BV2 cells were treated with different concentrations of drugs for 2 h. Then the culture medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM, and cells were incubated in the incubator of 95% N2 and 5% CO2 for 3 h. After OGD treatment, BV2 cells were transferred back to the culture medium containing drugs and recovered under normoxic conditions for 6 h.

Basal medium containing 10% total FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin DMEM (Gibco, USA) was used to culture the cells. Plasmids, sgRNA, or empty vectors were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, no. L300008) for coimmunoblotting. The medium was completely replaced after 4–6 h of transfection and the cells were further incubated for 24 h.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

Protein A/Agarose beads (Sigma-Aldrich, P2545) were mixed with PBS to 50% and added to the protein sample, shaken at 4 °C for 10 min to remove nonspecific proteins. The supernatant’s protein concentration was standardized using BCA, and antibody was added as directed. The mixture was shaken at 4 °C overnight. Agarose beads were then added to capture the antigen-antibody complexes, which were shaken at 4 °C overnight or at room temperature for 2 h. After centrifugation, the complexes were washed three times with cold PBS. To release the antigen, antibody, and beads, 20–40 µl of 2× sample buffer was boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. The samples were centrifuged at 14,000 g for 30 s, and the supernatant was used for Western blotting.

Lactate measurement

To quantify lactate concentrations in the mouse hippocampus using an ELISA-based approach, employ the lactate assay kit (Sigma, MAK064). Adhere to the manufacturer’s protocol for the preparation of standards and samples in the sample diluent, establishing a series of six standard concentration gradients. Initially, introduce 50 µL of Detection A working solution to each well, followed by an incubation period of 1 h at 37 °C. After aspirating and washing the wells, add 100 µL of Detection B working solution and incubate for 45 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, aspirate and wash the wells again before adding 90 µL of substrate solution, and incubate at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. Conclude the process by adding 50 µL of stop solution to each well. Finally, utilize the PlateReader AF2000 (Eppendorf) to measure the optical density of each well at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Statistics

All data are presented as the mean ± SEM, and individual data points are shown where applicable. The sample size (n) represents the number of independent biological replicates. Technical replicates or multiple measurements obtained from the same biological sample were averaged before statistical analysis and were not treated as independent observations. Data distribution and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Brown-Forsythe tests, respectively, together with visual inspection of residual and quantile-quantile plots. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed paired or unpaired Student’s t-tests, depending on the experimental design. Welch’s correction was applied when variances were unequal. Comparisons involving multiple groups or experimental factors were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s or Šídák’s multiple-comparisons test, as appropriate. Repeated-measures ANOVA was used when multiple measurements were obtained from the same experimental subjects. When the assumptions of parametric testing were not satisfied, the corresponding nonparametric tests were used. All tests were two-sided, and multiplicity-adjusted P values were reported for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

STING pathway is upregulated in CIRI model

Using the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE61616) from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), we analysed the normal group rats (GSM1509422, GSM1509423, GSM15094224, GSM1509425, GSM1509426) and the model group rats (GSM1509427, GSM1509428, GSM1509429, GSM1509430, GSM1509431) to elucidate the direct relationship between the STING pathway and CIRI lesions. STING pathway-related molecules were significantly upregulated in CIRI brain tissue (e.g., STING, IRF3, TBK1, NF-κB), along with increased expression of various interferon-stimulated genes and inflammatory factors such as interleukins (Fig. 1A). Further GSEA enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in the GSE61616 dataset revealed that CIRI is enriched in Type I and Type II interferon responses and TNFα signalling pathways (Fig. 1B). As a key upstream activator regulating interferon and inflammatory responses, STING mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in the CIRI model group of the dataset (P < 0.001, Fig. 1C). To further investigate the impact of STING on the CIRI model, this study also constructed CIRI model mice using the ligation method. Immunohistochemical analysis and Western blot showed that STING protein expression in the hippocampus region of the injured side of the brain tissue in CIRI model mice was significantly increased compared to the uninjured side (Fig. 1D-G). Subsequently, this study also further observed the effect of reperfusion duration on STING expression. The results showed that as blood flow recovered after the ligature was removed, STING expression levels gradually increased within the first 8 h and reached a plateau between 1 and 2 h. However, as the duration of blood flow recovery increased, STING expression levels began to decrease from 16 h onwards, and by 48 h, expression levels were significantly reduced compared to the plateau phase (Fig. 1H-I). Subsequently, reperfusion time was consistently set to 2 h, after which the ligature was reinserted. STING is an inflammatory activation molecule that mediates innate immune responses. Studies have shown that STING is expressed to varying degrees in immune cells and tissue cells. However, its expression distribution in brain tissue, especially in CIRI, remains unclear. Therefore, this experiment further investigated the spatial distribution of STING expression after CIRI. The results showed that in CIRI model mice, the upregulation of STING in the hippocampal region of the ischemic side of the brain was primarily concentrated in microglia, with minimal expression in astrocytes, and little colocalisation with neuronal cells (Fig. 1J). These findings suggest that the upregulation of STING in hippocampal microglia may be positively correlated with the progression of CIRI.

STING regulates the ischemic hemisphere hippocampal damage in CIRI mice

To further clarify the role of STING in the CIRI mouse model, we established STING overexpression and knockdown models using AAV injection (Fig. 2A). In this study, AAV was administered via both intravenous injection (IV) and intra-cerebroventricular injection (ICV). ICV administration of the corresponding AAV significantly induced STING overexpression or knockdown at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2B-F). By contrast, IV administration of the corresponding AAV did not significantly affect STING expression at either the mRNA or protein level. This limited effect may be related to the protective function of the blood-brain barrier. Therefore, all subsequent AAV injections in this study were performed via ICV. Cerebral blood flow imaging and TTC staining further showed that STING overexpression reduced cerebral blood flow and increased infarct area in CIRI mice, whereas STING knockdown improved cerebral blood flow and reduced infarct size (Fig. 2G-J). HE staining showed that STING overexpression further exacerbated hippocampal damage in CIRI mice (Fig. 2K).

Microglia are resident immune cells in the central nervous system, playing a crucial role in maintaining brain homeostasis and clearing metabolic waste. However, in CIRI, microglia are rapidly activated and participate in the inflammatory cascade by releasing pro-inflammatory factors. Studies have shown that ischaemic stimulation can induce microglia to express various inflammation-related molecules, such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, thereby exacerbating neuronal damage [30]. Previous studies have reported that STING is associated with microglia polarisation and inflammatory responses [8]. Immunofluorescence results from this study indicate that overexpression of STING promotes M1-type polarisation of microglia in the hippocampus (Fig. 2L). Additionally, overexpression of STING also promotes the expression of inflammatory factors IFN-1, TNF-α, and IL-1β while reducing the expression of anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 and TGF-β (Fig. 2L). Overall, these data suggest that specific regulation of STING expression in the hippocampus can effectively intervene in the progression of CIRI. Combined with the conclusions from the first part, microglia may be the key cell type for intervening in CIRI. Therefore, further investigation into the activation mechanisms of the STING inflammatory pathway in microglia is crucial for CIRI.

Aberrant mitochondrial fission triggers STING signaling in OGD/R–induced microglia

To further investigate the intrinsic mechanisms underlying the activation of the STING pathway in microglia, we used an OGD/R BV2 cell model to simulate the role of microglia in CIRI. cGAS, a key upstream activator of STING, is a receptor that specifically recognises dsDNA. Therefore, mtDNA derived from mitochondria becomes the key substance for activating the cGAS-STING pathway. Electron microscopy revealed increased mitochondrial fragmentation and autophagosome formation in microglia following OGD/R (Fig. 3A). This indicates that OGD/R leads to increased mitochondrial fragmentation and mitochondrial autophagy in microglia. Following OGD/R, there was an increase in mitochondrial fragments (Fig. 3B, C, E), and mtDNA was released from mitochondria (Fig. 3B, D). This suggests that OGD/R leads to fragmentation and leakage of mtDNA into the cytoplasm, and the increased dsDNA in the cytoplasm may be the key to activating the STING pathway. Further investigation of the effects of mitochondrial fission and mitochondrial autophagy on the STING pathway was conducted. After using the mitochondrial autophagy inducer CCCP, there was no promotional effect on the cGAS-STING pathway after OGD/R (Fig. 3F-G). This suggests that mitochondrial autophagy may not be associated with mitochondrial fragmentation and increased cytoplasmic mtDNA. Subsequently, the mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 significantly inhibited STING pathway activation after OGD/R but had no significant effect on cGAS expression (Fig. 3H-I). This suggests that regulating mitochondrial fission may be a key factor influencing mitochondrial fragmentation and cytoplasmic mtDNA levels in microglia. Furthermore, the increase in cytoplasmic mtDNA caused by mitochondrial fission does not affect the function of the cGAS receptor. Subsequent fluorescence results further confirm that regulating mitochondrial fission can influence STING protein expression (Fig. 3J-K).

DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission induced mtDNA release activates STING signaling

To further investigate the specific mechanisms underlying the activation of the STING pathway and mitochondrial fission in microglia following OGD/R, we focused on the intrinsic connection between DRP1, a key molecule in mitochondrial fission, and the STING pathway. We established a BV2 stable cell line overexpressing DRP1, and Western blot and rt-PCR analysis confirmed the effectiveness of DRP1 overexpression (Fig. 4A-C). Further Western blot analysis revealed that in BV2 cells overexpressing DRP1, the STING pathway was further activated following OGD/R. Treatment with the cGAS inhibitor RU.521 effectively inhibited this activation (Fig. 4D-E). Fluorescence analysis also showed that in BV2 cells overexpressing DRP1, STING expression increased following OGD/R, and co-localisation between DRP1 and STING was enhanced (Fig. 4F).

Further analysis of the potential mechanisms by which DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission activates STING. We found that DRP1 overexpression further exacerbates OGD/R-induced increases in mitoSOX levels in BV2 cells (Fig. 4G-H). Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), formed during mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, are closely associated with mitochondrial morphology. Increased mitochondrial ROS levels lead to increased mitochondrial fission and fragmentation. DRP1 may be a key participant in this process. We then further examined the effect of DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission on mtDNA leakage. We found that DRP1 overexpression further increased OGD/R-induced mtDNA leakage (Fig. 4I-K). This aligns with the previous observation that DRP1 is involved in mitochondrial ROS and mitochondrial fission-induced mitochondrial fragmentation. Here, we draw the preliminary conclusion that DRP1 promotes mitochondrial fragmentation by influencing mitochondrial ROS and mitochondrial fission, leading to mtDNA leakage, which is then sensed and activated by the receptor cGAS to activate the STING pathway, thereby exacerbating OGD/R damage in BV2. Therefore, inhibiting the function and activity of DRP1 can directly intervene in the STING pathway and interfere with the BV2 and CIRI processes.

Lactate and the expression level of DRP1 Kla upregulated in the ipsilateral hippocampus of CIRI models

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are enzyme-driven chemical changes to proteins after synthesis, crucial for regulating their structure, activity, location, stability, and function. More than 650 types of protein modifications, such as the most well-known phosphorylation, acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, glycosylation, acylation, cysteine oxidation, SUMOylation, ADP‐ribosylation, neddylation, citrullination, carbamylation, and Kla, have been described to date (http://www.uniprot.org/docs/ptmlist.txt), and the inventory is still increasing [31, 32].

Lactic acid, a byproduct of glycolysis, plays a critical role in immune regulation, signaling pathways, and energy provision [33]. In 2019, Zhao Yingming’s research team introduced the concept of Kla as a novel post-translational modification. This modification involves the use of lysine and lactic acid residues as substrates for protein modification [34]. Kla represents a significant post-translational modification that can affect protein stability, interactions, and functionality. This modification can occur in both histone and non-histone proteins in vivo [26]. Research indicates that Kla is a crucial factor in macrophage polarization. During the polarization of macrophages into the M1 phenotype, lactate accumulates, and there is a marked increase in histone Kla in the later stages of this process [35]. Lactic acid builds up significantly in CIRI-damaged areas, but protein Kla research is lacking. DRP1 is known to activate the STING inflammatory pathway in microglia. Here, we examine how DRP1 Kla affects its function and its influence on the STING pathway.

To further evaluate metabolic remodeling after CIRI, we analyzed the expression profiles of genes involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism in the GSE61616 dataset. A large number of genes associated with glycolytic flux and pyruvate metabolism, such as Pdk4, Pdk1, Pkm, Ldhc, Glo1, Rps27a, Uba52, Maea, and Gid8, were upregulated in the MCAO group, whereas a small subset of genes, including Pdhx, Pdk2, Pdk3, Ldha, and Ldhb, were downregulated. These findings indicate that CIRI induces marked remodeling of glycolysis- and pyruvate metabolism-related gene programs rather than uniform activation of the entire pathway (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we measured lactate levels (Fig. 5B) and LDHA levels (Fig. 5C) in the brains of CIRI mice, indicating that lactate levels and LDHA levels in the ischemic side of the hippocampus were significantly increased after CIRI. This lactate accumulation induced by ischemia-reperfusion may affect protein Kla, consistent with recent reports indicating the presence of protein Kla modifications in CIRI models [36]. Subsequently, the expression level of Kla modification of DRP1 (DRP1-Kla) in the hippocampus of CIRI model mice was detected. The results showed that DRP1-Kla protein levels were significantly upregulated in the hippocampus of CIR model mice (Fig. 5E-F). The same detection was then performed in the CIRI model of rats, and the results were consistent with those of mice (Fig. 5G-H). Subsequently, DRP1-Kla protein levels were detected in BV2 cells and DRP1-overexpressing stable cell lines. It was found that DRP1-Kla protein levels were significantly increased after OGD/R, and further increased after DRP1 overexpression (Fig. 5I-J). In summary, CIRI exacerbates lactate accumulation in the hippocampus, and this lactate accumulation increases DRP1-Kla protein levels.

Mutation at the DRP1 K616 site attenuates STING signaling in microglia

To identify candidate DRP1 lysine residues potentially involved in lactylation-associated regulation, we reviewed published proteomics data [36], which suggested two possible lysine sites, K581 and K616, in rat DRP1. Sequence alignment further showed that the amino acid sequences surrounding DRP1 K581 and K616 are highly conserved among human, rat, and mouse (Fig. 6A). In the present study, all in vitro experiments were performed using BV2 microglial cells, which are of mouse origin. Therefore, mutagenesis and functional analyses were conducted based on the mouse DRP1 (Dnm1l, NM_001360007.2) sequence to ensure consistency with the experimental system. Sequence alignment analysis indicated that the residue corresponding to rat K581 aligns with K587 in mouse DRP1, whereas K616 is conserved without positional shift. To functionally assess the contribution of these candidate lysine residues, we generated a series of DRP1 site-substitution mutants. At the K581 site, lysine was substituted with glutamine, threonine, asparagine, or proline to generate DRP1 K581Q, K581T, K581N, and K581P, respectively (Fig. 6B). Similarly, at the K616 site, lysine was substituted with glutamine, threonine, asparagine, or proline to generate DRP1 K616Q, K616T, K616N, and K616P (Fig. 6C). These constructs were then transfected into BV2 cells.

The results showed that DRP1 K581Q, K581T, K581N, and K581P did not significantly alter the expression levels of DRP1 or STING compared with DRP1WT after OGD/R (Fig. 6D–E). Likewise, DRP1 K616Q, K616T, and K616N did not significantly affect DRP1 protein or STING pathway expression. In contrast, the DRP1 K616P substitution significantly reduced the expression of DRP1 protein and STING pathway after OGD/R (Fig. 6F-G). We further examined the effects of DRP1 site substitution on inflammatory factor expression and found that DRP1 K616P markedly suppressed the expression of inflammatory factors after OGD/R (Fig. 6H). Taken together, these results indicate that substitution at the DRP1 K616 site, particularly K616P, significantly affects DRP1-associated signaling and downstream inflammatory responses in BV2 cells under OGD/R conditions, supporting the functional importance of the K616 residue in DRP1 lactylation-associated regulation and its impact on the activation of the STING pathway.

DRP1-K616la regulates DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission

To investigate the effects of DRP1-K616la on mitochondrial fission, this section employed DRP1WT and DRP1 K616P plasmid transfection in BV2 cells for analysis. Current research indicates that when DRP1 expression increases and accumulates in mitochondria, DRP1 exerts a promoting effect on mitochondrial fission [37]. In vitro experiments with BV2 cells revealed that following OGD/R, DRP1 expression levels and co-localisation with mitochondria significantly increased. However, after transfecting with the DRP1 K616P plasmid, DRP1 co-localisation with mitochondria was significantly downregulated (Fig. 7A-B). Western blot results further indicated that OGD/R led to a significant upregulation of DRP1 expression in BV2 mitochondria. However, after transfecting the DRP1 K616P plasmid, DRP1 expression in mitochondria was significantly downregulated (Fig 7 C-E). Although there was a slight increase in DRP1 levels in the cytoplasm after OGD/R, transfection with the DRP1 K616P plasmid did not improve the increase in DRP1 in the cytoplasm. These results indicate that mutation of DRP1 K616P influencing DRP1 aggregation to mitochondria.

Previous research has demonstrated that the up-regulation of DRP1 phosphorylation at the serine 616 site exacerbates mitochondrial fission, resulting in the leakage of mtDNA into the cytoplasm [38]. This study investigates whether modulation of DRP1-K616la influences mtDNA leakage into the cytoplasm. Our findings indicate that mtDNA colocalization with mitochondria decreases following OGD/R, corroborating earlier findings. However, after transfection with the DRP1 K616P plasmid, there is a significant increase in mtDNA colocalization with mitochondria (Fig. 7F-G). Furthermore, the expression of representative cytoplasmic mtDNA (mt-co1, mt-cytb, and mt-nd2) is significantly reduced post-transfection with the DRP1 K616P plasmid (Fig. 7H). Moreover, activation of the STING signaling pathway was significantly inhibited after transfection with the DRP1 K616P plasmid (Fig. 7I). In conclusion, DRP1-K616la is integral to the regulation of mitochondrial fission, a process intimately linked to the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm. Additionally, DRP1-K616la may play a pivotal role in the activation of the STING signaling pathway. Therapeutic targeting of DRP1-K616la or direct modulation of lactate metabolism through pharmacological agents could potentially ameliorate mitochondrial damage in microglia and mitigate inflammatory responses.

Discussion

Following reperfusion after vascular recanalisation in ischemic stroke, the restoration of blood flow paradoxically exacerbates brain tissue damage, emerging as an independent risk factor threatening the prognosis and survival of stroke patients [39]. The core mechanism of this damage is the inflammatory cascade triggered by the activation of microglia. Microglia are resident macrophages in the central nervous system and are the first to be activated during CIRI. The pro-inflammatory activation of microglia is a key factor in CIRI [40]. In this study, we found that STING is specifically highly expressed in the hippocampus of CIRI model mice, thereby activating the STING inflammatory pathway and releasing multiple inflammatory factors. Mechanistically, we found that the activation of the STING pathway in OGD/R-treated BV2 cells is mediated by cytoplasmic mtDNA, which is released due to DRP1-induced mitochondrial fission and mtROS production. More importantly, the increase in lactate caused by CIRI further exacerbates DRP1-K616la. Intervention targeting DRP1 K616-associated lactylation modulates DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission and mtDNA release into the cytoplasm, thereby suppressing STING pathway activation. These findings provide new insights into the development of CIRI and a potential therapeutic target for managing this inflammatory injury.

Emerging evidence indicates that the aberrant activation of the STING signaling pathway within macrophages is pivotal in the initiation of inflammatory responses associated with various ischemic diseases [9, 41–43]. In this study, we provide the first clear demonstration that elevated STING expression in CIRI is predominantly localized in microglia. This observation aligns with previous research showing that STING is chiefly expressed in immune cells [44]. Our investigation into the role of the STING signaling pathway in CIRI reveals that STING overexpression exacerbates the condition, whereas STING knockdown ameliorates hippocampal tissue damage, improves cerebral blood flow, and reduces the expression of inflammatory factors in CIRI. These findings underscore the pivotal role of STING signaling pathway activation in mediating inflammatory damage in CIRI mouse models.

The changes of mitochondrial dynamics between fusion and fission play an important role in CIRI [6]. However, previous studies have primarily focused on mitochondrial dysfunction in neurons. This study is the first to investigate the impact of mitochondrial dynamics in BV2 cells on pro-inflammatory activation. In this study, it was demonstrated that mitochondrial fission in BV2 cells can activate the STING inflammatory pathway. By constructing a DRP1 overexpression stable cell line, it was found that BV2 cells exhibited increased release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm following OGD/R treatment, a process dependent on DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission. This aligns with previously reported findings that dsDNA can be sensed by cGAS, an upstream receptor of the STING pathway [45]. Our results also indicate that DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission increases the escape of mtDNA from fragmented mitochondria into the cytoplasm in BV2 cells, thereby activating the STING pathway. Targeting DRP1 function could serve as an effective intervention strategy for CIRI.

Lactic acid plays a role in regulating various brain functions, including cerebral blood flow [46], neurogenesis [47], cerebral microvascularisation [48], energy metabolism [49], neuronal activity [50, 51], and neuroprotection [52]. In fact, lactate can be used by the brain as an energy substrate and plays a protective role in the early stages of ischemic stroke [52]. However, while the increase in lactate caused by ischemia may initially have a neuroprotective effect on the brain, when lactate concentrations reach higher levels, it may also lead to lactic acidosis [52]. The accumulation of large amounts of lactate results in Kla, a novel post-translational modification that regulates protein function. As previously noted in this paper, DRP1 is a key gene mediating mitochondrial fission and activating the STING pathway. Therefore, understanding the role and mechanism of DRP1 Kla holds promise for providing a direction and target for CIRI treatment. In this study, we found increased lactate accumulation in the hippocampus of CIRI model mice, and Kla of DRP1 in the hippocampus was also significantly upregulated. Further analyses suggest that K616 is a key candidate lactylation-associated site of DRP1 that plays an important role in modulating STING pathway activation. Mechanistically, substitution at the DRP1 K616 site directly affects the subcellular localization of DRP1 between the cytoplasm and mitochondria, thereby influencing mitochondrial fission and regulating the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm, ultimately impacting STING signaling. These findings suggest that DRP1-K616la may have potential therapeutic benefits for CIRI. Therapeutic targeting of DRP1-K616la or direct modulation of lactate metabolism through pharmacological agents could potentially mitigate mitochondrial damage in microglia and reduce inflammatory responses.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations inherent in the current study. Although substitution at lysine 616 of DRP1 significantly affected DRP1-associated signaling and downstream functional events, the precise mechanism underlying these effects remains to be fully elucidated. In particular, the effects observed with the K616P mutant may partly reflect local structural changes in DRP1 rather than solely alterations in lactylation status. Nevertheless, our current results consistently support that the K616 residue of DRP1 is a functionally important candidate lactylation-associated site involved in regulating DRP1 function and STING pathway activation. Therefore, while our findings highlight the critical regulatory importance of K616, further studies are required to clarify the exact structural and biochemical mechanisms involved.

In addition, several other limitations should be noted. First, although the results from multiple experimental approaches were generally consistent, the sample size of some in vivo and molecular experiments was relatively limited, partly due to the high mortality, low successful modeling rate, and strict inclusion criteria of the MCAO/CIRI model, as well as constraints in experimental duration, laboratory capacity, and funding. Future studies with larger cohorts are required to further improve the robustness and reproducibility of these findings. Second, AAV vectors were used for in vivo STING manipulation in this study. Because AAV does not integrate into the host genome, its expression may theoretically be diluted in proliferating microglia after injury. Although our observation period was relatively short and the AAV system was designed to enhance microglia-associated expression while reducing off-target neuronal expression, future studies using lentiviral systems or genetic mouse models would provide stronger evidence for long-term STING regulation in proliferating microglial populations. Third, endogenous DRP1 was not depleted before the expression of exogenous DRP1WT or DRP1K616P constructs. Although all compared groups were maintained under the same endogenous DRP1 background and OGD/R condition, future studies using endogenous DRP1 knockdown or knockout followed by rescue with DRP1WT or K616-specific mutants will be necessary to more definitively clarify the site-specific regulatory role of DRP1 K616-associated lactylation. These future studies will help more comprehensively define the DRP1-K616la–mtDNA–STING axis in CIRI.

In summary, we have demonstrated that DRP1-K616la promotes DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission, leading to increased mtDNA in the cytoplasm, which promotes the STING inflammatory cascade. Our findings provide new insights into the pathogenesis and treatment of CIRI.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study uncovers a novel mechanism underlying CIRI, in which DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission in microglia promotes cytoplasmic translocation of mtDNA, thereby activating the STING inflammatory pathway. The lactate accumulation in CIRI induces site-specific DRP1 Kla at K616, enhancing mitochondrial fission, facilitating mtDNA release, and amplifying STING-dependent inflammatory responses. Collectively, these findings provide novel insights into CIRI pathogenesis and potential molecular targets for intervention.
