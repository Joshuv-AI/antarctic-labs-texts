The Time Has Come
Authors: David KC Cooper, Leonardo V Riella, Tatsuo Kawai, Jay A Fishman, Winfred W Williams, Nahel Elias, Joren C Madsen, Richard N Pierson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn vitro studies indicate that kidney transplantation from gene-edited pigs in which expression of all 3 of the known glycan xenoantigens has been deleted may be more challenging in nonhuman primates (NHPs) than it will be in human recipients. Furthermore, pig-to-human xenotransplantation offers several other advantages—(1) the patient can communicate with the surgical team; (2) recipient microbiological monitoring and environment will be clinical-grade; and (3) sophisticated graft monitoring and imaging techniques, (4) therapeutic interventions, eg, dialysis, plasmapheresis, and (5) intensive care can be deployed that are not easily available in NHP laboratory models. We suggest, therefore, that progress to develop safe, informative human clinical trials will be accelerated if pilot clinical cases are initiated. The selection of patients for kidney xenotransplantation can include those who are at high risk of dying imminently, for example, those experiencing increasing vascular access challenges with no realistic alternative therapy available, and those who have been accepted onto the waitlist for an allograft, but who are unlikely ever to receive one. Patients with an increased risk of dying include those with (1) age above 60 years, (2) blood groups O or B, and (3) diabetic nephropathy. UNOS data indicate that an average of 25 patients on the kidney waitlist in the United States die or are removed from the list every day (ie, >9000 each year). Given the improved xenograft survival observed in preclinical studies, we suggest that it is time to plan a small pilot clinical trial for healthy dialysis patients who understand the risks and potential benefits of kidney xenotransplantation.Key words: clinical trials, kidney, nonhuman primates, pig, gene edited, xenotransplantation

In the past 2 years, 2 clinical pig heart transplants were conducted on a “compassionate” basis in patients with end-stage heart failure for whom no alternative therapy was considered possible.1 These transplants involved hearts from pigs genetically engineered to lack expression of the 3 major glycan xenogeneic antigens against which humans have natural (preformed) antibodies (Table 1) and incorporating multiple human “protective” transgenes. Both patients lived for several weeks but died, most likely from antibody-mediated rejection, although this awaits confirmation in the second patient. The survival of life-supporting pig hearts in nonhuman primates (NHPs) has been limited, with, to our knowledge, no recipient surviving for >9 months.5–7 Failure to thrive, respiratory illness, primate viral infections, graft hypertrophy, and/or immunologic injury (such as antibody-mediated rejection), to which the demise of long-surviving NHP orthotopic heart recipients was ascribed,7 are difficult to manage in the preclinical laboratory, but likely could be more accurately evaluated and treated in patients.

In contrast, the results of the transplantation of gene-edited life-supporting pig kidneys in NHPs have improved considerably within the past few years,8 with an increasing number of recipients surviving for longer than a year.9–12 These advances are associated with the introduction of (1) improved gene-edited pigs as sources of kidneys13,14 and (2) novel agents that block the CD40/CD154 T cell co-stimulation pathway.15

Of particular importance has been the introduction of pigs in which expression of 3 known glycan antigens has been deleted by gene-editing—“triple-knockout (TKO) pigs” (Table 1).13 Some groups believe that organs from TKO pigs alone will be sufficiently resistant to the human immune response and that no additional gene edits are required. However, there is considerable evidence that the introduction into the TKO pig of human “protective” genes, eg, for complement- and coagulation-regulatory proteins, will provide added protection.12,14,16 We, therefore, advocate that TKO pigs with multiple human transgenes are preferred as sources of organs for clinical trials.

BARRIERS TO PIG ORGAN TRANSPLANTATION IN NHPS

Barriers to the long-term success of organ transplantation in NHPs remain that we hypothesize will not be faced by many human recipients. Whereas many humans do not have natural antibodies to TKO pig cells,17 all Old World NHPs have these antibodies (Fig. 1).18,19 TKO pig organ transplants in NHPs, therefore, can present challenges that may not be seen when xenotransplantation is initiated in human recipients. Indeed, survival of an NHP after the transplantation of a kidney from a pig in which expression of only 1 (important) glycan xenoantigen (galactose-α1,3-galacrose) has been deleted may be longer than after the transplantation of a kidney from a TKO pig.20

The mixed results that have been achieved in the TKO pig-to-NHP model are attributed to the observation that NHPs express Neu5Gc, and so do not make antibodies against this glycan antigen. Deletion of this antigen in the pig is believed to expose a “fourth antigen” (or “antigens”) against which virtually all NHPs have antibodies, but most humans do not. However, there may be other factors involved. For example, NHPs have much greater complement-dependent cytotoxicity against TKO pig cells than do humans (Fig. 2),21 which may not simply be attributed to the exposure of the “fourth” xenoantigen. Some NHPs have high complement-dependent cytotoxicity to double-carbohydrate knockout pig cells (in which Neu5Gc is still expressed) even though IgM and IgG antibody binding may be low.21

The success of pig kidney transplantation in NHPs remains inconsistent, presumably reflecting these barriers. The function of pig kidneys in NHPs has been demonstrated to be sufficient to maintain health25–28 although, in baboons, when both native kidneys have been removed, dehydration and hypovolemia often occur, requiring increased fluid administration.29 This has been hypothesized to be related to incompletely characterized incompatibilities between species in the function of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.26,27 Importantly, dehydration does not develop when one native baboon kidney (with the ureter ligated) remains in situ (K. Kinoshita et al, unpublished). Satisfactory pig kidney function is illustrated by the fact that numerous NHPs supported by a single pig kidney have remained healthy for >1 year,10–12 with one even surviving for almost 4 years. The problem of the rapid growth of the pig kidney after transplantation has been largely overcome either by (1) the use of miniature swine as sources of the organs11,12,28 or by (2) growth hormone receptor-knockout in the pig.30,31

In summary, TKO pig kidneys, with or without additional transgenes, provide life-supporting renal function in a substantial minority of NHPs for >1 year, and in vitro data predict that these kidneys are likely to fare better in human recipients Furthermore, clinical pig xenotransplantation offers several advantages relative to preclinical studies in cage-housed NHPs: (1) the patient will be able to communicate and cooperate with his/her caregivers in ways that NHPs cannot, (2) patients can be prescreened and treated for common latent infections, eg, cytomegalovirus (CMV), for which effective prophylactic regimens do not exist in NHPs, and (3) the modern hospital environment enables sophisticated diagnostic modalities (eg, invasive monitoring, imaging), therapeutic interventions (eg, dialysis, plasmapheresis), and intensive care that are not readily available in an animal laboratory. We predict that, for immunological, microbiological, and logistical reasons, human recipients should do significantly better after pig kidney transplantation than NHPs.

Specifically, preclinical data suggest that gene-edited pig kidney transplants in carefully selected, cross-match-negative human patients immunosuppressed with a CD40/CD154 co-stimulation pathway blockade-based regimen would likely function for a year or longer. To test this prediction, the question is which patients might first be offered xenotransplantation in an exploratory pilot clinical trial?

SELECTION OF PATIENTS FOR PIG KIDNEY TRANSPLANTATION

Two groups of patients might ethically be offered a pig kidney graft.

“High-risk” patients would be those who have no realistic alternative therapy available to them. This may be the case in one of the following scenarios.

Patients with 100% calculated panel reactive antibodies (cPRA) rendering access to a cross-match-negative human donor kidney improbable. While desensitization protocols and waitlist prioritization currently improve access for some of these patients, current regimens may increase morbidity. Current evidence suggests that many highly HLA-sensitized patients have no or low levels of anti-pig antibodies (reviewed in the study by Cooper et al32), though some experimental data question this conclusion.33

Patients who have lost 2 or more prior allografts to a recurrent kidney disease have predictably poor graft survival following a subsequent allograft. While it is unknown whether a human kidney disease will affect a pig xenograft, answering this question would be valuable to the field while offering a potential benefit both to the patient and the waitlist population competing for scarce allografts.

“Low-risk” patients are candidates listed for allotransplantation but who are very unlikely ever to be offered a suitable allograft. We suggest that the primary aim of a clinical trial in these patients would be to assess the safety and efficacy of pig kidney transplantation, either as a bridge or as destination therapy, in the absence of multiple or significant comorbidities. Overall, almost 50% of patients on the UNOS waitlist in the United States die or are withdrawn from the list within 5 years (because of general debility or increasing comorbidities) (Fig. 3).34 Early mortality and risk of accelerated decompensation among these patients can often be predicted at the time of listing based on (1) age >60 years, (2) ABO blood type O or B, and (3) diabetes as the cause of their end-stage renal disease.35 Exclusion criteria would include prohibitive surgical risk, for example, severely limited physiologic reserve, untreatable infection, probably unacceptable body mass index (BMI), and a history of noncompliance, alcohol or drug abuse, or significant psychiatric problems.

The immunological requirements for a first clinical trial that includes these patients would necessitate demonstrating no evidence of pretransplant serum anti-pig antibodies or complement-dependent cytotoxicity to donor-specific TKO pig cells to avoid the predictably increased risk of antibody-mediated graft injury in the setting of a “positive cross-match.”

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has indicated that candidates for the first clinical trial of pig kidney transplantation should have suffered loss of a previous allograft. This requirement, however, would marginally increase the risk of failure of the xenotransplant from both surgical and immunological perspectives,38,39 and so we would counsel that this guidance be reconsidered.

It is important that careful consideration be given to the potential benefit of a pig kidney transplant to the specific patient. Attention must be focused not on the impact of the transplant on the field of xenotransplantation research but rather on its potential to benefit the individual patient being assessed.

THE POTENTIAL FOR INFECTIOUS COMPLICATIONS

The topic of infection risk specific to xenotransplantation clinical trials has been addressed by many experts over many years.40,41 In brief, it is anticipated that if the organs are retrieved from pigs that are known to be free of potentially pathogenic exogenous microorganisms, for example, porcine cytomegalovirus or porcine circovirus, and recipients are appropriately screened and vaccinated, the majority of infectious complications are likely to be the same as those seen in recipients of human allografts. Infectious disease physicians have many years of experience in managing these conditions.

However, the risk of transmission to the recipient of porcine endogenous retroviruses (PERV, which are integrated into the genomes of most pig cells), will remain unknown until clinical xenotransplantation is undertaken. Nevertheless, if necessary, pigs are available in which PERV has been inactivated.42,43 Monitoring strategies, including metagenomics, both targeted and unbiased nucleic acid sequencing, can be applied to human xenograft recipients to augment standard microbiologic techniques.1,44

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

There is considerable patient support for the potential of clinical organ xenotransplantation45,46 as reflected by patients at a 2023 US National Kidney Foundation meeting.47 Many dialysis patients would choose to enroll in a pilot limited clinical trial of gene-edited pig kidney transplantation in which graft and patient survival are expected to exceed 12 months. During support by a pig kidney, patients on the waiting list for an allograft could remain eligible for a deceased human kidney. If graft rejection or other life-threatening complications develop, we would cautiously suggest that there is a possibility that the graft could be removed, all immunosuppressive therapy discontinued, and the patient returned to chronic dialysis. Nevertheless, there are clearly risks to the patient that need to be assessed realistically in the context of the circumstances of each candidate. Importantly, current evidence is that sensitization to pig xenoantigens (if it develops) would most probably not be detrimental to the success of a subsequent allotransplant (reviewed in the study by Cooper et al32).

Such pilot clinical studies would arguably be a source of more information that is more valuable to accurately assess and iteratively improve study components than can be gained simply by continuing studies in NHPs (although these would, of course, be required if new gene edits are introduced into donor pigs or novel immunosuppressive agents become available). Studies in brain-dead human subjects (decedents) may provide complementary data but are unlikely to demonstrate consistent survival of life-supporting grafts for 12 months, as requested by the FDA for IND-qualifying preclinical pig-to-NHP studies.48

The transplant community now has ~70 years of experience in clinical kidney allotransplantation to inform the management of patients with pig xenografts. In addition, the almost 40 years of research into xenotransplantation8 provides a strong foundation for clinical application well beyond that which was available to the pioneering kidney allotransplant surgeons. Nevertheless, despite our familiarity with allotransplantation, the problems faced in xenotransplantation are sufficiently different that we recommend that the initial clinical studies should only be undertaken by those groups with extensive experience in the pig-to-NHP kidney transplant model.

Given (1) the increasingly good results being obtained in pig-to-NHP kidney transplant models, (2) the immunological advantage of humans over NHPs as recipients, (3) the advantages of managing patients in a hospital setting, and (4) the pressing need, based on UNOS data indicating that an average of 25 patients on the kidney waitlist in the United States die or are removed from the list every day (ie, >9000 each year), we feel there is urgency, indeed an ethical imperative, to initiate pilot clinical studies of gene-edited pig kidney transplantation as soon as possible. The results of ethically robust, scientifically sound initial pilot efforts by those who have requisite expertise in the field will enable rational, evidence-based design of IND-qualifying clinical trials.

Two pig kidney-to-human xenotransplants have been performed recently. A recipient of a TKO kidney with multiple additional gene edits at the Massachusetts General Hospital died 52 days after transplantation due to a presumed cardiac event with stable, life-supporting renal xenograft function. A patient at New York University who received a ventricular assist device several days before a GalTKO renal xenograft underwent an explant of the dysfunctional graft 47 days later and subsequently died (details not yet reported). We have and will continue to gain new information from these pioneering studies that are invaluable to the design and conduct of each subsequent pilot clinical experiment, particularly regarding the risk/benefit estimates associated with clinical xenotransplantation, and the selection and management of patients for whom this therapeutic balance is justifiable.

However, studies in such “high-risk” patients will inevitably be influenced adversely by the presence of comorbidities in these patients and may not provide a true assessment of the potential benefits of xenotransplantation. We suggest, therefore, that careful consideration also be given to the initiation of a limited clinical trial in “low-risk” patients who may face a more favorable outcome with a pig kidney transplant compared to continued dialysis. This trial should be designed to rigorously assess whether the potential benefits of xenotransplantation, including improved quality of life and prolonged survival, can surpass the outcomes achieved with the current standard of dialysis.

Who, therefore, might be the best candidates for clinical xenotransplantation? One group includes elderly patients with diabetic nephropathy. In many regions of the country these patients are more likely to die or be removed from the waitlist than to receive a deceased human donor kidney, particularly those with disadvantageous blood groups or who are highly sensitized.37 Although it is impossible to determine exactly when death will occur, short survival may be predicted in such patients, and a case can be made for offering a pig kidney to them.

Given the pressing need for innovative solutions in organ transplantation and the promising preclinical data, it is imperative that we continue to advance xenotransplantation research and move decisively toward clinical trials, ensuring that this groundbreaking approach becomes a viable and life-saving option for patients who lack other meaningful alternatives.
