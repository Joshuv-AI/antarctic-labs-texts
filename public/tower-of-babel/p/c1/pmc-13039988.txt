Feedback neurons based on perovskite memristor with nickel single-atom engineered reduced graphene oxide cathode
Authors: Qing-Xiu Li, Hua-Xin Li, Tao Sun, Ming-Hao Jiang, Yan-Bing Leng, Zi-Yu Lv, You Zhou, Ye Zhou, Wai-Yeung Wong, Su-Ting Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The demand for bio-inspired neuromorphic systems drives research on artificial neuronal devices with dual excitatory/inhibitory capabilities. Although perovskite memristors show promise, interfacial barriers and rapid ion migration hinder modulation. This study addresses these challenges through atomic-scale cathode engineering, developing perovskite-based memristor (Au/Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3/ITO) with nickel single-atom modified reduced graphene oxide (Ni1-rGO) cathode. The Ni–O configuration of Ni1-rGO with a single Ni atom anchored on rGO, modulates the electronic properties of rGO, reducing Schottky barrier via energy band alignment for bipolar current symmetry and spatially confining iodide ion migration through a high diffusion energy barrier (2.912 eV), conferring 780 ms relaxation. The device achieves 1,000 distinct conductance states and emulates interlayer connections and bidirectional signal transmission in feedback neuronal networks. We demonstrate neuromorphic functionalities: unsupervised competitive learning exceeds 50% clustering accuracy, while cooperative learning solves NP-hard problem 6× faster than simulated annealing, establishing an atomic-engineered approach for high-efficiency neuromorphic hardware.

Introduction

Neuromorphic computing presents a transformative approach to overcoming the intrinsic speed and energy limitations of the traditional von Neumann architecture by emulating the computational strategies of biological neural systems, particularly in learning, recognition, optimization, and classification tasks1–3. Central to this field is the development of artificial neurons capable of precisely reproducing neural behavior to emulate the architecture and operational principles of mammalian brains4, such as temporal integration, graded excitation, inhibition control, spike generation, and competitive dynamics5,6. Within such systems, feedback neurons play a pivotal role. By integrating excitatory feedforward inputs with inhibitory feedback signals, they regulate membrane polarization, maintain homeostatic balance, and mediate essential functions including lateral inhibition and winner-take-all (WTA) decision making7–9. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the biological feedback neuron architecture implements the synaptic weight modulation and membrane polarization control from self-connected or adjacent neurons via spatiotemporal integration of excitatory (Iforward) and inhibitory (Iback) signals10. Furthermore, in a WTA network, multiple feedback neurons vie to become the winner, meaning only one neuron is activated or fires in response to a given input, while the others are suppressed14. This approach is advantageous compared to standard methods, as a single computational unit employing WTA dynamics can approximate multiple computational modules that use standard sigmoidal-type thresholding neurons15–17. For the hardware realization of feedback neurons, factors such as hardware complexity, energy efficiency, and reliability are critical evaluation criteria. Scalable volatile memristive devices with intrinsically tunable switching characteristics, ultrahigh ON/OFF ratios (>103), and high-density integration offer a promising pathway, as they can capture both deterministic and stochastic neuronal dynamics to build compact, function-dense computing elements4,6,18–21.

Nevertheless, the physical characteristics that dictate the dynamics in the volatile memristor differ significantly from the mechanisms that regulate biological feedback neurons: (1) In biological neurons, membrane potential regulation arises from an inherently capacitive electrochemical process that enables bidirectional charge modulation-supporting both excitatory (depolarization) and inhibitory (hyperpolarization) effects essential for neuronal computation22–24. This dynamic balance between excitation and inhibition not only supports integration of excitatory and inhibitory inputs but also stabilizes network activity, ensuring efficient information processing within a controlled dynamic range. Only through such balanced bidirectional dynamics can neuromorphic devices truly emulate the neuron’s capacity for signal processing. However, most two-terminal memristors exhibit unidirectional conduction owing to inherent energy level mismatch between switching materials and metallic electrodes. This asymmetrical interfacial barrier also generates localized electric-field hotspots during cyclic operation, accelerating fatigue-induced material phase segregation and irreversible interfacial degradation, thereby severely compromising device endurance (typically <103 cycles)5,25. (2) Feedback neurons that utilize gradual conductance switching to emulate membrane potential evolution are essential for high-efficiency neuromorphic computing. While analog switching a memristor typically yields a fast saturation and narrow dynamic range owing to the poorly modulated atomic-level properties such as the ion migrating distance, making it hard to implement multilevel switching2,21,26–28. (3) Biological neurons achieve precise temporal integration through ion channel gating coupled with membrane capacitance23,29, operating on a biophysical timescale that supports sustained potential accumulation and millisecond circuit synchronization30,31. In contrast, volatile perovskite-based memristors exhibit ultrafast ion relaxation (τ < 0.01 ms), over >10× faster than biological time constants, thereby creating a fundamental mismatch for neuronal leaky integration32–34. Current approaches that incorporate auxiliary capacitors to prolong charge retention introduce substantial system-level costs, increasing footprint by >40% and causing inter-pathway desynchronization due to RC latency23,29.

Therefore, modulating ion mobility and energy level alignment enables memristors to achieve symmetric bidirectional switching, tunable excitatory/inhibitory conductance, and long current relaxation for capacitor-free emulation of neuronal LIF dynamics (Fig. 1c)19,20,33,35,36. In this work, we proposed an approach to insert a nickel (Ni) single-atom engineered reduced graphene oxide (Ni1-rGO) as cathode in perovskite memristors (Au/Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3/ITO). The structure is characterized by atomically dispersed Ni species anchored via oxygen-mediated Ni–O and Ni–C bonding, enable two synergistic effects. First, precise engineering band alignment at the Ni1-rGO/perovskite interface confirmed by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and density functional theory (DFT), notably facilitates bidirectional charge transport and generates intrinsic excitation-inhibition modulation, allowing device-level emulation of neuronal depression and supporting key biological functions such as homeostatic balance and lateral inhibition. Second, 2D layered materials exhibit versatile chemistry, allowing incorporation of atoms, ions, or molecules. This property underpins their utility in battery electrodes and switching medium in memristive devices. In contrast to inert cathodes that block ion transport in memristors, Ni1-rGO composite cathodes features a high diffusion energy barrier (DEB) (DEB = 2.912 eV), enabling limited yet reversible ion intercalation/deintercalation. This controlled process introduces delayed kinetics that spatially restrict iodid migration pathways, while the regulated energy landscape promotes ion redistribution, promoting analog-type resistive switching behavior with extended relaxation times. As a result, our memristors exhibit analog switching to achieve 1000 tunable conductance states, highly balanced excitation-inhibition symmetricity (Mean Square Error, MSE is ~40) and long relaxation behavior (780 ms). We first show that employing a Ni1-rGO cathode enables perovskite volatile memristors to emulate capacitor-free neuronal dynamics through excitatory and inhibitory signals. Leveraging the intrinsic bidirectional excitation–inhibition dynamics of these memristive neurons, we construct a biologically inspired WTA network, demonstrating dual neuromorphic functionalities: unsupervised competitive learning achieves more than 50% accuracy in nonlinear clustering of different floral datasets, while cooperative learning configurations solve NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems like the travelling salesman problem with 6× faster convergence than conventional simulated annealing, enabled by modeling lateral inhibition of feedback neurons. These results establish Ni1-rGO engineered memristors as an ideal platform for compact, efficient, and biologically realistic neuromorphic systems.

Results

Coordination structure of Ni atoms in Ni1-rGO

To achieve symmetrical conductance modulation, well-aligned energy band alignment between MAPbI3/cathode to improve the charge transport is highly demanded. The exceptional promise of graphene-derived interfaces stems from their atomic-level electrostatic programmability which is capable to achieve sub-100 meV work function tunability via π-orbital-mediated charge transfer, uniquely suited for cathode material of perovskite memristors requiring low interfacial barrier37–39. Enhancing analog RS performance with prolonged relaxation requires integrating controlled slow kinetic mechanisms that spatially restrict iodide ion migration pathways through confined activation energy40. 2D layered structures demonstrate versatile host-guest interactionsby accommodating guest species ranging from atoms to complex molecules, enabling their use in energy storage electrodes and dynamic memristive devices2,41,42. Unlike traditional inert cathodes that inhibit ionic transport in memristors, graphene-derived cathodes facilitate selective, reversible ion intercalation and deintercalation. This regulated ionic activity modulates filamentary dynamics or interfacial energy barriers, promoting gradual RS transitions with extended temporal decay. Such characteristics are vital for emulating biological synaptic plasticity and enabling neuromorphic computing applications that demand bio-inspired signal decay properties43.

Current doping strategies, however, suffer from inherent atomic-structural ambiguity, manifested through chemically heterogeneous nucleation of metal clusters at graphene defect sites that create stochastic Schottky barrier distributions, pose a serious obstacle to clearly identify the precise structure at the atomic level and further establishing structural correlations with electrical properties44. As an emerging material, single atoms (SAs) materials (SAM) consist of isolated metal atoms of atomic size, which can be used to anchor on the graphene in a well-defined configuration for precisely modulating the electronic structure45,46.

The synthesis of Ni single atoms on reduced graphene oxide (rGO) is comprehensively illustrated in Fig. 2a and further explained in the Supporting Information. The oxygen-rich functional groups present in graphene oxide (GO) carriers provide numerous accessible sites for functional modifications and the loading of Ni SAs47. The atomic-scale synthesis of Ni SAs is achieved through adsorption-anchored strong electrostatic adsorption of charged Ni2+ cations in a GO mixed solution, followed by the pyrolytic reduction process under Ar atmosphere, resulting in Ni1-rGO, as depicted in Fig. 2a40. The microscopic morphologies and structures of Ni1-rGO and rGO were preliminarily characterized and compared using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images reveal that Ni1-rGO forms ultra-thin, wrinkled, and interlaced monolayer nanosheets, closely resembling the microstructure of rGO (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 3), and the selected area electron diffraction pattern of Ni1-rGO (inset in Fig. 2b) confirms that the sample primarily consists of layered rGO, indicating that the incorporation of Ni atoms does not compromise the inherent flexibility and structure of the rGO matrix48. To resolve atomic-level information about Ni atoms, aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) was employed, confirming the uniform dispersion of Ni atoms on rGO (marked with red circles in Fig. 2c). This advanced imaging technique revealed a homogeneous atomic dispersion of Ni atoms across the rGO substrate, with no evidence of Ni aggregates or clusters. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings further corroborated the compositional integrity, showing the presence of Ni, C, and O elements within the sample (Fig. 2d). Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) provided precise sample information, avoiding the limited identifiability of the HAADF signal49,50. The EELS spectrum in Fig. 2e shows a distinct Ni-L2,3 signal peak at 857.3 eV, highlighted by a blue circle. Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 4a) indicate that ID/IG ratio-corresponding to the D band at 1350 cm−1 and the G band at 1580 cm-1 of Ni1-rGO (0.928) is comparable to that of rGO (0.878), suggesting that the atomic-scale dispersion of Ni does not affect the structural integrity of the rGO lattice. Importantly, no characteristic peaks of Ni crystals were observed in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra (Supplementary Fig. 4b), reaffirming the compositional integrity. Quantitative elemental analysis via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) determined the Ni loading in Ni1-rGO to be 1.41%. The coordination environment of the Ni atomic sites was further elucidated using synchrotron-based X-ray absorption spectroscopy. Figure 2f presents the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra of the Ni K-edge in Ni1-rGO, alongside standard Ni foil and NiO for reference. The absorption edge energy of Ni1-rGO at 8350 eV is positioned between that of Ni foil (0) and NiO (+2), indicating that the average oxidation state of these Ni single atoms is between the metallic and fully oxidized states. This result suggests that charge transfer from Ni to rGO and adjacent O atoms depletes the free electrons of Ni, resulting in a positive charge on Ni. The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of the k2-weighted Fourier Transform (FT) Ni K-edge (Fig. 2g) further resolve the coordination environment of the Ni atom. The typical first shell peaks for standard reference Ni foil and NiO occur at 2.16 Å and 1.64 Å, corresponding to Ni–Ni and Ni–O interactions, respectively. In contrast, Ni1-rGO shows only one main peak at 1.62 Å, indicating Ni–O bonding. Complementary wavelet transform (WT) of EXAFS is crucial for distinguishing different ligand atoms. The wavelet transform contour map of Ni1-rGO in Fig.2h contains a single intensity maximum at k = 4.88 Å−1, corresponding to the Ni–O scattering paths, confirming the atomically dispersed Ni atoms in Ni1-rGO. Furthermore, quantitative EXAFS curve fitting analysis was performed to determine the structural parameters and chemical configuration of the Ni atoms. The fitting results (Fig. 2i and inset) indicate a coordination number of Ni of ~3.4 ± 0.1 and a Ni–O bond length of 1.61 Å (Supplementary Table 1). Combining these structural parameters and spectroscopic evidence, the atomic-scale structure of Ni1-rGO is depicted in Fig. 2j.

Design of perovskite memristor based on Ni1-rGO cathode

A pristine perovskite memristor with inert metal as cathode was first fabricated with the structure of Au/MAPbI3/ITO as control device. The current-voltage (I-V) curve of pristine perovskite memristor during the voltage scan (0→1→0→−1→0 V) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, revealing volatile RS behavior. For a positive polarity scan, the device is initially at a high resistance state (HRS, ~20 MΩ), switches to a low-resistance state (LRS) at 0.6 V, reaches a minimum resistance of ~10 kΩ at 1.0 V, and gradually decays back to the original HRS with the reverse scan. The I-V curve exhibits highly asymmetric hysteresis during reverse scanning, which originates from the different energy band alignments at the Au/MAPbI3 and MAPbI3/ITO interfaces. To investigate interfacial barrier effects on carrier transport at MAPbI3/Au junctions, in-situ Kelvin Probe Force Microscopy (KPFM) was performed to characterize potential shifts at the interface under application of different bias voltages51,52. The pristine perovskite memristor is in the HRS initially at lower bias voltages (+0.1 V (Supplementary Fig. 6a) and +2 V). In particular, there is a clear drop of the potential curve at the corresponding Au (+)/MAPbI3 and MAPbI3/Au (−) interfaces (Supplementary Fig. 6b bottom panel, denoted by yellow circles), implying there is a contact barrier at the interface that hinders carrier injection. Upon escalating the bias voltage beyond the critical voltage threshold of +5 V, electric field induced energy band bending decreases, leading to reduced carrier injection barrier. The device consequently transitions from an HRS to an LRS, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 6c (upper panel). Subsequently, once the bias voltage reverts to 0.1 V, the surface potential state reverts to the initial state (Supplementary Fig. 6d). These observations verify the effect of Schottky barriers on carrier transport at the MAPbI3/Au interface by measuring the change in contact potential difference.

In addition, in the pristine perovskite memristor, under a positive bias, MA+ vacancies (VMA−) and I− ions migrate toward the top electrode (TE), forming a p-type doped perovskite region, while MA+ and I⁻ vacancies (VI+) accumulate at the bottom electrode (BE), creating an n-type doped region. This ionic redistribution establishes an internal P–I–N structure that generates a built-in electric field opposing the external bias (Supplementary Fig. 7). After removal of the external electric field, the built-in internal electric field provides a spontaneously restored driving force that immediately dominates ionic motion. This compels ions and vacancies to migrate in opposite directions, rapidly accelerating ion redistribution and substantially increasing the migration rate.

To address the asymmetric conductance modulation and fast relaxation issue of the pristine perovskite memristor for its further emulation of feedback neuron, we design the sequentially deposited, vertical perovskite memristor with SAM Ni1-rGO film as the cathode material. The memristor device architecture, illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8a, features Au/Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3/ITO vertical structure. The cross-SEM (Supplementary Fig. 8b) reveals the MAPbI3 active layer with a uniform thickness of ~450 nm. In addition, to provide a clearer visualization of the device’s cross-sectional architecture, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images are included in Supplementary Fig. 9. Moreover, comprehensive characterization via SEM (Supplementary Fig. 10), combined with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Supplementary Fig. 11), XRD (Supplementary Fig. 12), and atomic force microscopy (AFM, Supplementary Fig. 13a), further confirm the spin-coated MAPbI3 films exhibit smooth surfaces, highly crystalline and pinhole-free properties. While cross-sectional SEM failed to resolve the Ni1-rGO surface morphology, AFM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 13b) reveals an atomic-scale thickness (6–10 nm) demonstrates the SAM material film achieves an ultrathin and uniform coverage, which provided critical support for carrier modulation. In contrast to existing memristive neurons, such as those based on VO25, NbO253, Ag-based54, or antiferroelectric devices55, which either lack inherent inhibition capabilities or rely on external circuitry for suppression, the proposed Ni1-rGO cathode offers intrinsic bidirectional excitation-inhibition dynamics. We anticipate that the Ni1-rGO cathode, with atomically dispersed Ni species anchored through oxygen-mediated coordination bonds, which is capable of modulating the ion mobility and interfacial barrier, will resolve the known issues of perovskite memristor from two perspectives: (1) The perovskite memristive device exhibits asymmetric conductance response under voltage bias, attributed to interfacial Schottky barriers arising from suboptimal contact quality and energy band offsets between the perovskite semiconductor and adjacent charge injection layers. These barriers induce imbalanced charge transport responses during positive and negative voltage scans56. Moreover, accumulated stress during multiple programming cycles aggravates material fatigue and interfacial degradation, significantly reducing the device durability. Precise engineering of band alignment at the perovskite/ Ni1-rGO interface is expected to be achieved since homogeneity of the distribution of Ni atoms on rGO ensures the efficient charge transfer between rGO and Ni atoms and subsequent notable shift in the Fermi level of rGO, enabling simultaneous excitatory/inhibitory signal processing with bipolar current rectification ratio enhancement47,57–60. (2) Dynamically creating a DEB with low ionic mobility to introduce a slow kinetic factor for limiting the motion of iodide ions. Two-dimensional layered materials exhibit unique structural properties that enable the incorporation of diverse foreign species (e.g., atoms, ions, and molecules)36. This capability has driven their innovative applications in energy storage and neuromorphic devices. In our memristor design, the Ni1-rGO cathode contrasts sharply with conventional inert cathodes. Unlike their ion-blocking counterparts, Ni1-rGO permits small amounts of ions penetration across the interface while enabling reversible ion intercalation/deintercalation. The dynamic ion migration mechanism regulates conductive filament formation/disruption and modulates interfacial energy barriers, thereby facilitating analog-type RS with gradual relaxation dynamics. During SET operations, iodide ion species migrate through the perovskite matrix and subsequently insert into the Ni1-rGO layer, while RESET processes trigger reversible deintercalation from layered Ni1-rGO, which enables capacitor-free emulation of neuronal integrate-and-leak dynamics through self-limiting ion intercalation thermodynamics.

Consistent with these mechanisms, Fig. 3a shows that the Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3 memristor exhibits pronounced DC I-V hysteresis, characteristic of volatile bipolar switching. In stark contrast, the pristine perovskite memristor exhibits memristive behavior with limited accessible conductance states and a rapid recovery of current to the initial state after pulse stimulation (Fig. 3b), corresponding to a relaxation timescale shorter than 0.1 ms. Such fast recovery suppresses stimulus-history-dependent state retention, making capacitor-free emulation of feedback neurons difficult. Moreover, the optimized device demonstrates robust analog switching endurance over 20 I-V cycles in the memristors with cycle-to-cycle variations <3% (coefficient of variation, CV; Supplementary Fig. 14a). Notably, during the first 20 forward sweeps (0 to 1 V, first 1st–20th), the device gradually transitions from the HRS to the LRS, reflecting an excitatory effect. In contrast, during the subsequent 20 reverse sweeps (0 to −1 V, last 21st–40th), a progressive current decay is observed, corresponding to a continuous transition from LRS back to HRS (Fig. 3c). Moreover, such analog RS can be reproducede in different devices regardless of the differences in batches with device-to-device coefficients of variation all less than 4% (Supplementary Fig. 14b, and 64 chip I-V curves are shown in Supplementary Fig. 15). The devices exhibited exceptional analog switching capabilities, achieving at least 1000 tunable conductance states with a conductance modulation range exceeding 3 orders of magnitude under both polarities, indicating their precise ability to emulate neuronal dynamics (Fig. 3d). Simultaneously, the Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3 memristor demonstrated a highly balanced excitatory-inhibitory symmetry (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 16), maintaining conductance enhancement and suppression over 7000 cycles. The symmetry is quantitatively characterized by a parameter of ~40 (Mean Squared Error), with detailed calculation schematics and formulas provided in Supplementary Fig. 17. Additionally, the memristors displayed long relaxation behavior, with relaxation times extending to ~780 ms (Fig. 3e, compare with Fig. 3d), indicating that the intercalation/deintercalation process dominates on the SET and RESET processes. Under stronger pulse intensities (e.g., large pulse width), higher induced currents were observed, requiring extended leakage durations for neuronal recovery - a phenomenon interpreted as adaptive restoration (Fig. 3f, and Supplementary Fig. 18). These mechanisms prove essential for precisely mimicking the LIF characteristics of biological feedback neurons. The detailed comparison of pristine perovskite, Ni1-rGO memristor and Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3 memristor in terms of relaxation time, condutance modulation range under pulse train, symmetricity of excitatory and inhibitory signal processing under pulse train with different pulse numbers and amplitude is depicted in the Supporting Information (Supplementary Fig. 19). In addition, the device exhibits excellent durability under excitatory-inhibitory pulse stimulation, as evidenced by the enhancement and suppression of conductance across 30 experimental sets totaling 6000 pulses (Fig. 3g, pulse, and detailed conductance modulation curves for each group are shown in Supplementary Fig. 20). From these data, it can also be concluded that following the application of inhibitory pulses, a greater number of subsequent pulses is required for the device to reach its original current state. The divergence underscores Ni1-rGO cathode to enhance bipolar modulation through synergistic energy band optimization and controlled ion migration dynamics. Resistive switching mechanism using Ni1-rGO cathode

The underlying RS mechanism is investigated to uncover the modulation of ion mobility and interfacial barrier via inserting the Ni1-rGO cathode. First, to verify the regulation of energy level alignment of MAPbI3 and cathode interface, theoretical DFT calculations as well as the UPS measurements of the memristors are collected. The influence of introducing atomic Ni on the electrical property of the rGO electrode was theoretically investigated by DFT calculation. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 21, the energy band structure derived from the rGO structural model (inset of Supplementary Fig. 21a) reveals a band gap of 0.45 eV at the Fermi level characterized by discontinuous energy band distribution near the Fermi level, which results in non-metallic behavior and is detrimental to conductivity. In contrast, Ni1-rGO exhibits a distinct shift of the Fermi level into the valence band (Fig. 4a), imparting metallic characteristics and thereby enhancing conductivity47. The calculated Fermi levels for rGO and Ni1-rGO are −2.806 eV and −3.250 eV, respectively, as depicted in Supplementary Table 2. Combined with the ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis) testing, the conduction band minimum and valence band maximum of MAPbI3 were measured at −3.70 eV and −5.28 eV, relative to the vacuum level, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 22). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 23, a clear Fermi-level mismatch of ~150 meV exists between rGO (−4.150 eV) and MAPbI3 (−4.30 eV), which gives rise to a significant Schottky barrier that impedes electron transport. In contrast, charge transfer between Ni and rGO modifies the local electronic structure, resulting in a downward shift of the Fermi level and an increase in the work function from 4.16 to 4.612 eV. This adjustment enables Ni1-rGO to achieve optimal band alignment with MAPbI3, thereby alleviating the interfacial barrier, reducing contact resistance, and ultimately improving charge transport. The layered energy-level configuration of the memristor under excitatory and inhibitory inputs further illustrates this advantage (Fig. 4e). The lower work function of Ni1-rGO electrodes effectively prevents interfacial Schottky barrier formation during charge transfer from MAPbI3 to Ni1-rGO, thereby significantly enhancing charge carrier transport.

Ni1-rGO with metallicity not only enables precise energy-level alignment with perovskite, but also suppresses rapid ion migration via a controllable I− intercalation/deintercalation mechanism, thereby imparting stable relaxation dynamics and reproducible memristive behavior. Importantly, this functionality primarily relies on interface-layer engineering rather than structural modifications of the perovskite. While material defects are essential for creating ion migration pathways, excessive defects facilitate deep ion penetration that impedes deintercalation, potentially causing irreversible LRS locking. In Ni1-rGO, Ni atoms anchored via surface oxygen groups on the rGO surface, preserving the underlying carbon skeleton structure of rGO, while intrinsic defects within the rGO matrix primarily serve as controlled ion migration pathways. This design strategically minimizes excessive defects while maintaining essential ion transport channels. As shown in Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 21b, the density of states (DOS) at the Fermi level is increased, enhancing the material’s metallic character. Moreover, Supplementary Fig. 24 illustrates that the d-orbitals profiles of isolated Ni atoms are concentrated near the Fermi level within the rGO matrix, further improving the electrical conductivity of the Ni1-rGO composite. This balanced architecture enables Ni1-rGO to ensure efficient charge transport and optimized ions migration pathways. Notably, the ion diffusion rate is governed by the DEB, which depends on the material’s structure, composition, and temperature. DEB represents the energy barrier for ions to move between lattice sites: a lower DEB promotes fast ion transport, whereas a higher DEB increases activation energy and slows diffusion. DFT calculations (Fig. 4f) indicate that for I− the DEB in rGO and Ni1-rGO is 2.106 eV (blue curve) and 2.912 eV (red curve), respectively. The higher DEB confines ion migration to the interface region, effectively suppressing excessive halide migration and deep ion accumulation, thus avoiding irreversible low-resistance locking. This interfacial confinement allows only limited and reversible I− intercalation/deintercalation, enabling gradual conductance modulation and pronounced relaxation behavior-critical for stable analog memristive operation. Thus, the Ni1-rGO cathode does not alter the intrinsic chemical properties of the perovskite; rather, it enhances device stability and ensures excellent reproducibility and durability through interface-mediated control of energy levels and ion migration pathways.

Under an external electric field, I− ions migrate according to the direction of the field. To overcome the high DEB of the SAM cathode, I− ions migrate to the cathode and are confined to localized regions, preventing excessive diffusion (Fig. 4g, left panel). Simultaneously, the application of the external electric field induces a built-in electric field within the perovskite, opposing the direction of the external field. When the external field is removed, the high DEB of the Ni1-rGO layer against I− ions prevents rapid ion resetting, ensuring only limited reversible intercalation/deintercalation of I− ions at the SAM cathode. This delayed migration of I− ions induces notable relaxation behavior in the device, while preventing significant structural deformation in the SAM cathode caused by rapid mass ion transport (Fig. 4g, left and right panels). This maintains sufficient conductivity in the material (evidenced by a significant increase in current magnitude), enabling stable memristive behavior with high device-to-device uniformity (Supplementary Figs. 14, and 15). Furthermore, this mechanism achieves tunable switching processes and triggers additional simulations of progressive resistance transitions with over 1000 tunable conductance states (Fig. 3g).

Neuronal characteristics of the Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3 memristor

In this work, we modeled biological neurons by constructing feedback neurons based on the leaky LIF mechanism. These neurons integrate incoming signals and generate spikes (i.e., action potentials) once the membrane potential reaches a threshold. Unlike simple linear summation, the dynamics of our feedback neuron model also incorporate inhibitory feedback from other neurons, enabling nonlinear integration and more faithfully capturing the complex behavior of biological neurons in processing input signals. To investigate the dynamic modulation and integration properties of Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3 memristor based neurons, we systematically modulated electrical pulse parameters (amplitude, width, and interval) to emulate LIF neuronal dynamics (Fig. 5a–c). First, increasing pulse amplitude from 0.8 to 1.2 V reduced the number of pulses required to reach the 0.1 mA firing threshold (Fig. 5a), as the enhanced electric field accelerated carrier migration and charge accumulation. Notably, 0.8 V pulses failed to trigger firing due to insufficient charge integration. Furthermore, an extended pulse duration improved charge injection efficiency and reduced integration time, attaining a faster threshold attainment (Fig. 5b). Correspondingly, stronger stimuli induced higher output currents, while neurons exhibited prolonged leakage dynamics (see Supplementary Fig. 19), suggesting adaptive recovery behavior. In addition, we conducted a comparative analysis of different firing threshold ranges in the feedback neuron system, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 25. The results show that the threshold is a key parameter: at the device level, it controls noise sensitivity and spike variability; at the network level, it affects neuronal selectivity and classification accuracy. A range of 0.1–0.2 mA minimizes variability while maximizing performance, leading us to select 0.1 mA for subsequent experiments. To probe the leakage dynamics during integration processes, we measured cumulative currents under variable-interval pulse stimulation (interval = 10, 50, 200 ms; Fig. 5c). Short intervals promoted sustained charge accumulation, requiring fewer pulses to reach the threshold, whereas prolonged intervals enabled I− ions migration, thereby increasing leakage and requiring additional pulses to offset charge losses. When the interval exceeded a critical duration (e.g., 200 ms), threshold emission becomes unachievable. Frequency-dependent current modulation was further characterized in memristor neurons (Supplementary Fig. 26). To evaluate operational stability, we analyzed firing patterns under varied stimulation parameters (Fig. 5d–f). Amplitude gradient tests (Fig. 5d) demonstrated that increasing voltage (0.5–1.5 V) significantly reduced and centralized the number of pulses required distribution for discharge triggering. Pulse width and interval effects were quantified in Fig. 5e, f, where extended pulses enhanced integration efficiency, while shorter intervals minimized leakage and improved discharge stability. We further evaluated the unsupervised learning compatibility of the device by examining its lateral inhibition characteristics. As shown in Figs. 3g and 5g, the device exhibits a robust inhibition function: its conductivity decreases significantly under negative pulse stimulation. In Fig. 3g, both inhibitory and excitatory responses are well-regulated, highlighting the reliability of the memristor’s inhibition behavior. Figure 5g illustrates this mechanism in detail, where the nonlinear excitatory activation (red trace) is modulated by negative pulses (blue trace). The negative pulses reduce conductance, enabling a WTA effect in which the most active neuron suppresses the firing of others. In contrast, positive pulses drive the excitatory responses, generating neuronal activation. Under strong lateral inhibition (Fig. 5h), neurons required enhanced excitatory input to trigger firing, demonstrating a competitive inhibition mechanism analogous to biological neural networks. The histogram in Fig. 5i, based on 500 trials across 5 devices, shows a narrow distribution of pulse counts (mean ≈ 5 pulses, CV = 5.64%). This indicates a stable intensity-frequency modulation, which is comparable to the behavior of biological neurons. This result demonstrates the device’s ability to maintain consistent firing patterns, making it well-suited for advanced neuromorphic computing applications, particularly in complex classification tasks that require high precision and reliability.

Memristive neurons for competitive and cooperative learning

Inspired by the self-organization and lateral inhibition phenomena of biological neural networks, we employ a Self-Organizing Map (SOM) framework that synergistically integrates competitive learning (CL) and cooperative learning (CoL) through biomimetic mechanisms61. Here, feedback neurons perform dual roles: in CL, they enforce inhibitory competition by reinforcing the winner neuron while suppressing rivals, refining feature selectivity. Simultaneously, in CoL, they enable excitatory collaboration by updating neighboring neuron weights to preserve spatial topology. This biomimetic interplay balances localized competition for precision and global cooperation for structural continuity, mirroring brain-like excitation-inhibition dynamics. As shown in Fig. 6a, b, the network comprises an input layer and a neuromorphic output layer with neurons arranged in crossbar array topology. Each cross-point establishes bidirectional feedback connections between input-output neurons, addressing three fundamental limitations of conventional neural networks: (1) Learning updates extend beyond WTA neurons62; (2) Weighted input features generate low-dimensional discrete representations in the competitive layer63; (3) Dynamic neighborhood interactions enable real-time feedback during training15. Specifically, the system operates in the following way: the input layer encodes the multidimensional morphological features (including sepal length, petal width, and receptacle size) of the three flowers into voltage pulses with fixed amplitude of 1.0 V and variable widths from 50 to 200 ms. Feature magnitudes correlate linearly with pulse width (e.g., 7 cm sepal length = 200 ms pulse; 3 cm = 50 ms). Discussion

In summary, we present a Ni single-atom engineered cathode for high-performance MAPbI3 perovskite memristors (Au/Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3/ITO). Through DFT calculations, we demonstrate that the introduction of Ni atoms modulates the electrostatic properties of rGO and achieves precise energy level alignment at the interface between the perovskite and the electrode, realizing a bipolar symmetric current. Concurrently, the Ni atom-anchored structure increases the DEB for I− ions, which realizes a tunable relaxation process through the restricted ion intercalation/deintercalation kinetics under an external electric field, while effectively suppressing the structural deformation. The results show that the Au/Ni1-rGO/MAPbI3/ITO memristor exhibits enhanced and inhibited bipolar current transfer properties, which are suitable for simulating interlayer connections and bidirectional data transfer in feedback neurons for neuromorphic computing and complex network architectures. Additionally, we have successfully implemented an unsupervised learning network based on self-organization and lateral inhibition of feedback neurons with memristor-baesd neurons. Through the dual excitatory-inhibitory capability of our memristor-based neurons, we have demonstrated their effectiveness in unsupervised competitive learning for data classification and cooperative learning for solving combinatorial optimization problems and provide an atomic-scale engineering approach for the creation of high-performance analog memristor.

Methods

Materials

N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, >99%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, >99%), nickel(II) acetylacetonate (C10H14NiO4, 95%), isopropanol (C3H8O, 98%), ethanol (C2H5OH, ≥99%), and lead(II) iodide (PbI2) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Diethylamine iodide (MAI) was purchased from Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd. All chemicals are used directly without further purification. ITO glass electrodes (6 Ω sq−1) were ordered from South China Science & Technology Co., Ltd.

Experimental details

The GO was prepared using the traditional Hummers method. 40 mg GO and 21.2 mg C10H14NiO4 were sonicated in 120 mL isopropanol solution for 1 h, and then magnetically stirred for 24 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 5500 × g three times, and the precipitate was collected and dried under vacuum. Finally, under an argon (Ar) atmosphere, 550 °C heat treatment was performed for 2 h, and natural annealing was done at room temperature to obtain Ni1-rGO. The rGO was prepared in the same method as Ni1-rGO, in the absence of C10H14NiO4.

Device fabrication

First, the pre-patterned ITO glass electrodes (2 cm × 2 cm) were ultrasonically cleaned using ultrapure water and absolute ethanol, respectively. Subsequently, dry the ITO electrodes was dried under flowing nitrogen gas and treated in a UV-ozone cleaner for 15 min. Next, the perovskite precursor solution was prepared following a literature-based method. Specifically, methylammonium iodide (MAI), lead (II) iodide (PbI2) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was dissolved at a 1:1:1 M ratio in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent, with continuous stirring using a stirrer until complete dissolution. The resulting solution was then filtered through a syringe filter to remove undissolved impurities, yielding a clear perovskite precursor solution. Afterwards, perovskite thin films were fabricated via one-step solution method. The precursor solution was spin-coated onto ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 30 s, during which 300 μL of chlorobenzene was added dropwise as the antisolvent to induce rapid crystallization and form dense MAPb3 films. Immediately after film formation, the samples were transferred to a hotplate for annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. To prepare the carbon-based electrode layer, 2 mg ml−1 of carbon material (Ni1-rGO or rGO) was dispersed into an isopropanol solution, followed by ultrasonication and ball milling to obtain a homogeneous slurry. Then, pray it onto the surface of the MAPbI3 perovskite film for 30 s and anneal at 100 °C for 20 min. Finally, 50 nm Au top electrodes were deposited through shadow mask via thermal-evaporation method. The device area of the memristor used for electrical testing and construction of LIF neurons was 500 μm × 500 μm. It is critical to note that all perovskite-related processing steps were performed in a nitrogen environment glovebox (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm).

Measurements

The microscopic morphology was examined using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM, JEOL F200, Japan) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HITACHI S-4800, Japan). The elemental distribution analyzer for materials by X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford). Aberration-corrected JEM-ARM300F (JOEL, Japan) was used to provide energy-dispersive EDS and HAADF-STEM characterization at an operating voltage of 300 kV. A BRUKER AXS D&Focus (Germany) diffractometer with Cu Kα1 radiation (wavelength = 1.54056 Å) was used to perform powder XRD patterns. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, KRATOS AXIS SUPRA+, England) fitted with an Al Kα anode was used to identify the surface chemical states; all spectra were correlated to C 1s peaks at 284.6 eV. The Raman spectra were obtained using a Renishaw inVia (England) confocal Raman spectrometer with a laser wavelength of 532 nm and a test range of 200 to 2000 cm−1. Memristor devices were dynamically characterized by the Agilent 2902 and a Keithley 4200-SCS Semiconductor Parameter Analyzer (Keithley, USA).

X-ray absorption fine spectroscopy measurements and analysis

The Ni K-edge XAFS spectra were collected at the BL11B beamline in the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. Data reduction, data analysis, and EXAFS fitting were applied through the Athena and Artemis software. The energy calibration of the sample was conducted through a standard Ni foil, which, as a reference, was simultaneously measured. For EXAFS modeling, the global amplitude EXAFS (CN, R, σ2, and ΔE0) were obtained by nonlinear fitting, with least-squares refinement, of the EXAFS equation to the Fourier-transformed data in R-space, using Artemis software, EXAFS of the Ni foil was fitted and the obtained amplitude reduction factor S02 value was set in the EXAFS analysis to determine the coordination numbers (CNs) in the scattering path in the sample. The Debye–Waller factors and delta Rs were obtained based on the guessing parameters and constrained for Ni–N. Wavelet transformation (WT) was also employed using the software package developed by Funke and Chukalina using a Morlet wavelet with κ = 10, σ = 1.

In situ KPFM measurements

In situ switching process of the perovskite memristor was probed by a B2902A precision source/measure unit in combination with a Fastscan (Bruker) AFM system. The memory cell was connected to the B2902A precision source/measure unit through the conductive Cu tape and was biased to 0.1, 1, 2 and 5 V constant voltage. All KPFM images were obtained in an electrical lift mode (AM-KPFM) using SCM-PIT-V2 tips (Bruker). Lift scan height and scan rate were 100 nm and 0.6 Hz, respectively.

Computational detail

All the calculations were performed in the framework of the DFT with the projector augmented plane-wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package. The generalized gradient approximation proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof was selected for the exchange-correlation potential. The long-range van der Waals interaction is described by the DFT-D2 approach. The cut-off energy for plane wave is set to 520 eV. The energy criterion was set to 10−5 eV in iterative solution of the Kohn–Sham equation. A vacuum layer of more than 15 Å was added perpendicular to the sheet to avoid artificial interaction between periodic images. The Brillouin zone integration was performed using a 2 × 2 × 1 k-mesh. All the structures were relaxed until the residual forces on the atoms had declined to less than 0.01 eV Å−1. DFT calculations of diffusion energy were performed by using the CP2K package mixed with Gaussian and plane-wave scheme and the Quickstep module. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange correlation functional, Goedecker–Teter–Hutter (GTH) pseudopotential, DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH/TZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH/TZVP-MOLOPT-GTH basis sets were used to describe the system. A plane-wave energy cut-off and relative cut-off of 400 Ry and 55 Ry have been employed, respectively. The energy convergence criterion was set to 10−6 Hartree. The DFT-D3(BJ) level correction for dispersion interactions was applied. Structural optimization was performed using the Limited Memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shannon (LBFGS) optimizer, until the maximum force was below 0.00045 Ry Bohr−1 (0.011 eV Å−1).
