Reporting a Novel Disease Causing Variant in PGAP3 Associated With Hyperphosphatasia and Intellectual Disability: A Case Report and Comprehensive Literature Review
Authors: Arash Salmaninejad, Mohammad Reza Seyedtaghia, Ali Hosseini Bereshneh, Nasrin Azizi, Reza Bayat, Somaye Esnaashari, Vahid Aminzadeh, Shahin Koohmanaee, Shahram Savad, Majid Mojarrad, Setila Dalili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA rare autosomal recessive disorder known as hyperphosphatasia with impaired intellectual development syndrome (HPMRS), also referred to as Mabry syndrome, is caused by a deficiency in glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI). Elevated blood alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels, cognitive impairment, and epileptic seizures are among its key features. These pathways are involved in the synthesis of GPI and the transfer of GPI anchor to the proteins, fatty acid remodeling, and transport of GPI‐anchored proteins (GPI‐APs).MethodsUsing exome sequencing (ES), the cause of hyperphosphatasia and ID of an 11‐year‐old girl from non‐consanguineous parents was solved, and the results confirmed by direct Sanger sequencing method.ResultsES identified a novel homozygous pathogenic variant, PGAP3 (NM_033419.5: c.202dupT, p.Cys68fs*2) that segregated within the family members. To the best of our knowledge at the time of writing this manuscript, this variant has not been reported in HPMRS in the literature.ConclusionOur findings indicate that the p.Cys68fs*2 variant may disrupt normal protein function and likely disrupt its interaction with its associated partner proteins, potentially leading to disruption of GPI biosynthesis. We present a novel pathogenic variant thus expanding the phenotypic and mutational spectrum of this extremely rare disorder. Elevated ALP assays and ES are valuable diagnostic tools for HPMRS. Additionally, a comprehensive literature review was conducted to expand the phenotypic and genotypic spectrum within the PGAP3 gene responsible for HPMRS.Keywords: glycosylphosphatidylinositol, hyperphosphatasia, intellectual disability, Mabry syndrome, PGAP3

Introduction

The PGAP3 gene, located on chromosome 17q12, consists of eight exons that encode a Golgi‐specific phospholipase responsible for glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) remodeling (Horn et al. 2014; Sakaguchi et al. 2018). GPI is a complex glycolipid synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and modified in the Golgi apparatus (Deracinois et al. 2015). GPI‐anchored proteins (GPI‐APs) are biosynthesized in the ER and remodeled in the Golgi apparatus through a pathway involving multiple enzymes (GPI‐APs), which are either in the Golgi apparatus or the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Kuki et al. 2013; Ng and Freeze 2015). These proteins play critical roles in neuronal development and require fatty acid remodeling by PGAP3 for proper integration into lipid rafts (Howard et al. 2014; Knaus et al. 2016; Abdel‐Hamid et al. 2018).

Hyperphosphatasia with mental retardation syndrome (HPMRS; OMIM #239300) also known as Mabry syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by defects in GPI‐anchor biosynthesis or remodeling. It was first described in 1970 in three siblings with elevated serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP), intellectual disability, and seizures (Mabry et al. 1970). Clinical features associated with HPMRS include severe intellectual disability (ID), variable neurological deficits such as hypotonia and seizures, persistently elevated ALP, and distinctive facial dysmorphism—characterized by hypertelorism, a broad nasal bridge and tip, long palpebral fissures, and a tented, thin upper lip—along with short distal phalanges (brachytelephalangy) (Horn et al. 2010; Krawitz et al. 2010). Additional documented congenital abnormalities include Hirschsprung disease, cleft palate, anorectal anomalies such as anteriorly displaced anus, and cardiac and urinary tract malformations (Marcelis et al. 2007; Horn et al. 2010; Maydan et al. 2011; Balobaid et al. 2018).

The six subtypes of HPMRS (HPMRS1‐6, OMIM PS239300) are associated with pathogenic variants in at least six genes involved in the glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor biosynthesis pathway. Pathogenic variants in known HPMRS‐associated genes have been identified in the majority of individuals with a clinical diagnosis of HPMRS, although a subset of patients remains without a confirmed molecular diagnosis. These genes primarily participate in the synthesis and maturation of the GPI anchor, including PIGV (OMIM #610274), which encodes alpha‐1,6‐mannosyltransferase II; PIGW (OMIM #610275), the inositol acyltransferase; PIGY (OMIM #610662), a GPI‐GlcNAc transferase; and PIGO (OMIM #614730), an EtNP transferase III. Other genes, such as PGAP2 (OMIM #614207) and PGAP3 (OMIM #615716), are involved in the post‐GPI attachment remodeling of the anchor. Notably, PGAP3 is the second most common genetic cause of HPMRS worldwide, following PIGV mutations, which are the most frequent (Krawitz et al. 2010; Horn et al. 2011, 2014; Thompson et al. 2012; Kinoshita 2014; Knaus et al. 2016). Most pathogenic variants in GPI‐anchor synthesis genes, such as PIGV, result in reduced surface expression of GPI‐anchored proteins (GPI‐APs). In contrast, pathogenic variants in PGAP2 or PGAP3, which are involved in lipid remodeling of the GPI anchor in the Golgi, lead to the production of GPI‐APs that are more susceptible to cleavage (Maeda et al. 2007; Knaus et al. 2016). Despite normal PGAP3 expression in cells, the phenotype caused by PGAP3 pathogenic variants may result from defective remodeling of the lipid structure of GPI‐anchored proteins (GPI‐APs), which prevents their localization to lipid rafts (Knaus et al. 2016).

HPMRS4 is classified as a congenital disorder of glycosylation (PGAP3‐CDG), as it results from defects in the biosynthesis of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors. As of the time of writing, including the present case, a total of 68 individuals—comprising 28 males and 40 females—have been diagnosed with PGAP3‐CDG (Jaeken 2011; Altassan et al. 2023; Mukai et al. 2024; Pan et al. 2024). PGAP3 pathogenic variants are associated with a wide range of clinical phenotypes, from severe syndromic presentations to milder cases with moderate intellectual disability. This variability underscores the evolving understanding of the condition, with newly identified cases continually contributing to scientific research (Nampoothiri et al. 2017; Messina et al. 2023). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the novel homozygous pathogenic variant c.202dupT (p.Cys68Leufs*2) in PGAP3. This novel variant was identified by exome sequencing, and its segregation was confirmed within the family.

Material and Methods

Ethical Compliance

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the University of Guilan's Research Ethics Committee (IR.GUMS.REC.1403.363). Due to patients' mental status and being under 18 years of age, parents of participants provided informed and voluntary consent for the publication of their information and images and signed a written consent form.

Sample Collection

With written informed consent, the proband and her family members were included in the research. Specialist physicians in neurology and medical genetics conducted the proband's clinical examination.

The patient's antenatal, natal, and postnatal medical histories were thoroughly obtained, with particular emphasis on developmental history, seizure classification, and any additional underlying medical conditions. A comprehensive neurological evaluation, a general checkup and urinary tests were performed following the construction of a pedigree. The patient's clinical images were uploaded to Fact2Gene web application (https://app.face2gene.com/), where the platform's top diagnostic suggestion was HPMRS, whose gestalt closely matched the presented case (Figure 1). This case was also examined on https://db.gestaltmatcher.org/, where the first rank of similarity for this case was HPMRS, with a calculated distance of 0.576 (Model version: v1.1.0; Gallery version: 27.01.2025). The patients' MRIs were analyzed using a Tim System 1.5 T eco machine (Siemens) equipped with a superconductive magnet and multiple pulse sequences at different imaging locations. The candidate variant was inherited from both carrier parents to the proband. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the peripheral blood leukocytes of the patient and her parents.

Exome Sequencing

In accordance with the Agilent SureSelect target enrichment kit preparation instructions (http://www.agilent.com), exome libraries were prepared and exome enrichment was performed on extracted DNA from three samples. Next, the libraries were sequenced using Illumina Hiseq 2000/2500 sequencer (https://www.illumina.com/), and data analysis was conducted using SAMtools after reads were mapped to the human assembly GRCh37 using Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner (BWA) (Li et al. 2009). PCR duplicates were identified and marked using Picard (DePristo et al. 2011), variants were called with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) (McKenna et al. 2010) and Variant Effect Predictor (VEP).

Variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 1% were filtered out. For interpretation of the variants data from in databases of genetic diseases including OMIM (http://omim.org), HGMD (https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php), ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), the database of the 1000 Genomes Project (http://www.1000genomes. Org/), gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), and Iranome (https://www.iranome.com/) were utilized. The effects of the pathogenic variant on protein function was predicted using PolyPhen 2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), SIFT (http://sift.jcvi.org) and CADD (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu/). Gene prioritization was performed using data derived from genes present in panels for epilepsy and intellectual disability, and data was filtered based on clinical features, including developmental delay and refractory seizure.

The proteins with which final impaired protein interacts were identified through the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/network/9606.ENSP00000300658). Finally, a novel homozygote variation (PGAP3, NM_033419.5:c.202dupT; p.Cys68fs*2) was identified as a potential genetic basis for the patient's condition.

Sanger Sequencing

Sanger sequencing was used to validate the variants to confirm that the exome analysis did not generate false positives. Primers for the Sanger sequencing were designed using the Primer3Plus web application. Next, the target DNA was amplified by PCR, purified with PCR purification kits, and sequenced using the Big Dye Terminator V3.1 Cycle Sequencing kit on an ABI genetic analyzer. Finally, the presence of the variant in the patient and her two unaffected carrier parents was confirmed.

Results

The patient was an 11‐year‐old Iranian girl (Figure 1) born to non‐consanguineous parents (from the same geographic region) following an uneventful prenatal history. At 5 months of age, she was hospitalized for hypotonia. Clinical features included low‐set ears, a thin tented upper lip, broad nasal bridge and tip, sagging cheek, short stature, severe ID, cerebral palsy, and megalocornea. Neurological findings other than hypotonia included refractory seizures and ataxia. Elevated serum ALP levels were noted. Urine organic acid analysis and urinalysis showed no evidence of metabolic disorders. Brain MRI revealed enlarged subarachnoid spaces.

This variant (NM_033419.5: c.202dupT, p.Cys68fs*2) met criteria for PVS1 (due to its protein‐truncating effect, very strong +8pts) and PM2 (absent in population databases, e.g., gnomAD, supporting +1pts) and was classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines (Richards et al. 2015). Additionally, PP1 (co‐segregation.data) along with PP4 (specificity of phenotypic features in the proband) could be applied to this variant. Inclusion of PP1 and PP4 upgraded the variant classification from likely pathogenic to pathogenic under ACMG recent update (Biesecker et al. 2024).

This protein is likely unable to interact with its associated partner proteins due to its truncated nature. According to STRING date base (https://string‐db.org/network/9606.ENSP00000300658), its associated partner proteins are include: PIGV, PGAP2, PGAP1, PIGW, PIGO, MIEN1, GRB7, TCAP, STARD3, and ERBB2.

Research and analysis of all PGAP3 pathogenic variants to date have revealed that missense mutations are the most common, accounting for 16 out of 42 variants (38.1%). Small insertions/deletions/duplications follow, comprising 11 variants (26.2%), while splicing/intronic/regulatory variants represent 6 cases (14.2%). Nonsense mutations are the least frequent, with 7 reported variants (16.7%).

Discussion

We identified a novel homozygous PGAP3 (c.202dupT, p.Cys68fs*2) in an 11‐year‐old Iranian female. This variant was detected in trans in the family. The variant is absent from population databases (gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, Iranome) and clinical databases (HGMD).

Three loss‐of‐function mutations in exon 2 of PGAP3 (c.203del (in twins), c.265C>T, and c.202dup) are each predicted to produce truncated protein products. Below is a comparative analysis of their clinical manifestations. All have hypotonia, DD in gross motor function and speech impairment, and some facial dysmorphism including broad nasal bridge and craniofacial anomalies. The c.203del variant presented vascular abnormalities (brain hemorrhages) and multi‐organ involvement (GI/cardiac). The c.265C>T showed accelerated somatic growth despite developmental delays. The c.202dup demonstrated severe neurological sequelae including cerebral palsy and treatment‐resistant seizures. Other features are summarized in Table 2.

Based on the phenotypes observed in Table 2, a strong genotype–phenotype correlation cannot be expected solely based on the truncating mutations. Moreover, considering that the three variants listed in the table are located in close proximity to each other, this observation can be extended to suggest that the site of the pathogenic variant alone does not determine the clinical presentation in this disease. These findings indicate that the phenotypic manifestations in patients with this syndrome are influenced by factors beyond the pathogenic variant type and its location within the protein domains. Nevertheless, the identification and characterization of more patients harboring identical pathogenic variants would be valuable for drawing more definitive conclusions.

Several genes, such as PIGV, PIGW, PIGY, PIGO, PGAP2, and PGAP3, have been linked to the pathogenicity of HPMRS (Ilkovski et al. 2015). Common clinical features of HPMRS include hypertelorism, cleft palate, broad nasal bridge, short nose, upslanted palpebral fissures, seizures, and long philtrum. Table 3 summarizes the clinical findings in the six HPMRS subtypes (Krawitz et al. 2012; Thompson et al. 2012; Howard et al. 2014; Knaus et al. 2016; Abdel‐Hamid et al. 2018; Balobaid et al. 2018; Sakaguchi et al. 2018; Abi Farraj et al. 2019; Doğan et al. 2019; Bezuidenhout et al. 2020; Da'as et al. 2020; Messina et al. 2023). While there is considerable phenotypic overlap, several notable differences are evident. For example, type 6 remains the only subtype where hypotonia has not been reported. Hearing impairment is present in subtypes 2, 3, and 4, and occasionally in type 1, while visual impairment occurs in types 3 and 6, and sometimes in type 4. Cardiac anomalies have not been reported in types 5 and 6, and gastrointestinal or anorectal malformations are absent in type 5. Regarding dysmorphic features, brachytelephalangy and hypoplastic nails are shared across subtypes 1, 2, and 6. However, broad nasal bridge has not been reported in types 3 and 6, and hypertelorism is absent in type 5, while variably present in types 2, 3, and 6. These distinctions reinforce the importance of careful phenotypic assessment, as subtle differences between subtypes may aid in prioritizing gene testing and improving diagnostic yield. Our case study of a patient presenting with features consistent with HPMRS, we identified pathogenic variants in PGAP3, a gene previously established as the genetic cause of HPMRS4. PGAP3 encodes a GPI‐specific phospholipase A2 enzyme involved in the fatty acid remodeling of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors. To our knowledge, this novel frameshift mutation in the PGAP3 gene has not been previously reported. This enzyme, which is primarily localized in the Golgi apparatus, catalyzes the first step of fatty acid remodeling by removing unsaturated fatty acids from GPI‐anchored proteins (GPI‐APs) (Pei et al. 2011; Krawitz et al. 2012).

In 2014, Howard et al. published the first report of HPMRS4, describing five individuals from three unrelated families who had seizures, ID, and distinctive facial features. Additionally, all patients exhibited elevated ALP levels (Howard et al. 2014). Furthermore, most individuals with PGAP3‐related HPMRS4 exhibit clinical features from birth or within the first few years of life. The typical symptoms, which can present from the first day of infancy to early childhood, are predominantly neurological and include ataxia, hypotonia, ID, significant developmental delay, and seizures with distinctive facial features (Altassan et al. 2023).

Research on the phenotypic spectrum of the largest PGAP3‐CDG cohort (n = 65) was conducted in 2023 by Altassan and colleagues. Global developmental delay (100%), motor delay (96%), cognitive delay (95%), facial dysmorphism (95%), speech delay (93%), hypotonia (90%), cleft palate/lip (62%), hypoplastic corpus callosum (54%), seizures (49%), behavioral abnormality (43%) and short stature (40%) were observed. Conversely, gastrointestinal abnormalities were less common (12%), highlighting phenotypic variability in PGAP3‐CDG (Altassan et al. 2023). The rarity of PGAP3‐CDG and limited studies may explain variability in symptom prevalence. Alternatively, the specific nature of mutations may contribute to this variability. Identifying a distinct genotype–phenotype correlation is challenging due to interacting variables. For example, no clear relationship between PGAP3 pathogenic variants and epilepsy subtypes (e.g., generalized or myoclonic seizures) has been identified. Patients with c.402dupC (p.Met135Hisfs*28) mutation have exhibited both generalized and myoclonic seizures. to date, no definitive genotype–phenotype correlate has been established (Abdel‐Hamid et al. 2018; Balobaid et al. 2018; Altassan et al. 2023).

Functional studies in CHO cells showed that mutant PGAP3 proteins have negligible or absent enzymatic activity, supporting impaired GPI‐anchor remodeling as a cause of HPMRS4 (Park et al. 2013). This provided evidence in favor of the theory that HPMRS4 may be brought on by impairment of GPI‐anchor remodeling. Zebrafish models, with 79.3% PGAP3 homology to humans, replicated HPMRS phenotypes: neurological deficits (neural tube defects, hyperactivity), dysmorphic features (head/ear abnormalities), and brain defects (tectum/cerebellum anomalies) (Da'as et al. 2020). Dose‐dependent brain abnormalities correlated with symptom variability, while reduced oligodendrocytes and shortened axons disrupted sensory responses and triggered seizures. These models confirm PGAP3's role in neurodevelopment and provide mechanistic insights into HPMRS pathophysiology.

Knockdown of PGAP3 in zebrafish results in distinct neurodevelopmental abnormalities that shed light on its role in early brain maturation. Genetically modified zebrafish exhibit increased burst movements during early development, a phenotype associated with altered neurotransmitter release and suggestive of epileptiform activity (Turrini et al. 2017; Burrows et al. 2020). These neural hyperactivities may contribute to oligodendrocyte injury, as excessive neurotransmitter release is known to impair oligodendrocyte survival and myelination (Káradóttir and Attwell 2007). Indeed, PGAP3‐deficient zebrafish display a reduction in oligodendrocyte number, diminished myelination in the tectal ventricles, and shortened ventral spinal cord axons at later embryonic stages, ultimately leading to impaired motor responses (Da'as et al. 2020). These findings are consistent with the physiological role of oligodendrocyte progenitors, which are generated in the subventricular zone and migrate to various regions of the developing brain and spinal cord, where they differentiate into myelinating cells (Richards et al. 2015; Bergles and Richardson 2016). Disruptions in these processes may underlie the hypotonia and seizures observed in HPMRS4 patients. Furthermore, reduced fetal movements—initiated by spontaneous motor neuron activity beginning around 6–7 weeks of gestation (McKenna et al. 2010; Fagard et al. 2018)—could also be linked to early motor circuit dysfunction in the absence of functional PGAP3. These findings suggest a critical role for PGAP3 in neuronal development and indicate a potential therapeutic window for interventions aimed at supporting axonal survival and promoting remyelination.

The subventricular zone of the brain generates oligodendrocytes progenitors, which migrate to various regions of the developing brain and spinal cord, where they differentiate into myelinating oligodendrocytes (Richards et al. 2015, Bergles and Richardson 2016). At around 5 weeks of gestation, the first synapses begin to form in the fetus's spinal cord. By 6–7 weeks, spontaneous motor neuron activity lead to first fetal movements (Medicine, I. o. and N. A. o. Sciences 1992; Fagard et al. 2018). Therefore, any disruptions in this pathway can affect the nervous system function. Knocked down zebrafish exhibit increased burst movements during early development, suggesting altered neurotransmitter release. Recent studies show that increased neural bursts, measured via brain and locomotor activity, can indicate epileptic seizures in larval zebrafish (Turrini et al. 2017, Burrows et al. 2020). It is hypothesized that excessive neurotransmitter release may harm oligodendrocytes, leading to impaired brain function (Káradóttir and Attwell 2007). Genetically modified zebrafish showed fewer oligodendrocytes, reduced myelination in the tectal ventricles of the brain, and shorter ventral spinal cord axons at later embryonic stages. These combined deficits result in reduced initiation of motor responses. These findings suggest that PGAP3 is important for early neuronal development, which may explain decreased fetal movements observed in affected pregnancies. Additionally, hypotonia and seizures are observed in HPMRS4 patients may be related to impaired development or function of motor neurons. This suggests a potential therapeutic window for intervention that support axonal survival and promote remyelination (Da'as et al. 2020).

Targeted gene panels, exome sequencing, or genome sequencing are often used to identify HPMRS. The presence of GPI‐APs on the surface of granulocytes may also be examined using flow cytometry (Balobaid et al. 2018). Furthermore, recent studies indicate that novel screening techniques such as cDNA sequencing and RNA sequencing can aid in identifying variants in GPI‐anchored protein genes (Knaus et al. 2016).

Preventing the recurrence of hereditary diseases requires comprehensive genetic counseling and family education. Parents are advised to consider preventative reproductive options such as preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) and prenatal diagnosis (PND). ALP levels are a distinctive clinical feature that, together with genetic testing for recurrent mutations, can expedite diagnosis and improve genetic counseling (Balobaid et al. 2018).

Frontiers in Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation Consortium (FCDGC) (https://fcdgc.rarediseasesnetwork.org/diseases‐studied/pgap3‐cdg) states that currently, HPMRS cannot be cured; management focuses on controlling its occurrence and alleviating certain symptoms. In some cases, daily pyridoxine (vitamin B6) supplementation has been associated with reduced seizures (Brasil et al. 2022). ALP is an essential enzyme that dephosphorylates pyridoxal 5‐phosphate to pyridoxal, facilitating vitamin B6 metabolism. In GPIBDs with deficient ALP activity, pyridoxal 5′‐phosphate cannot be efficiently dephosphorylated, which may affect the availability of vitamin B6 in the brain (Thompson et al. 2006). The research does not provide enough evidence to support the use of pyridoxine supplements in the management of uncontrollable seizures in GPIBDs (Kuki et al. 2013; Wilson et al. 2019; Bayat et al. 2022; Messina et al. 2023). A ketogenic diet has been associated with fewer seizures in certain GPIBD patients; however, the exact mechanism is still unknown and needs further study (Boyer et al. 2022).

Conclusion

In this report, we present for the first time a novel homozygous pathogenic variant in PGAP3 (c.202dupT; p.Cys68fs*2) identified in an 11‐year‐old girl with cerebral palsy, hypotonia, refractory seizures, severe intellectual disability, and dysmorphic features including low‐set ears and megalocornea. Elevated serum ALP levels served as a critical diagnostic clue, guiding the exome sequencing filtering strategy toward hyperphosphatasia with mental retardation syndrome (HPMRS). This study underscores the importance of considering HPMRS in children with unexplained neurodevelopmental delay and persistently elevated ALP. This case contributes to the expanding genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of PGAP3‐related HPMRS and is discussed in the context of a comprehensive literature review. Although limited by the absence of functional assays, the strength of this study lies in its clinical‐genetic correlation and detailed phenotype documentation.
