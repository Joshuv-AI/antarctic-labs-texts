Effectiveness of a structured physical therapist-led intervention compared to usual care in people with longstanding hip and groin pain referred to orthopaedic care in Sweden: protocol for a randomised controlled trial (the HIPSTER trial)
Authors: August Estberger, Joanne Kemp, Kristian Thorborg, Ioannis Kostogiannis, Anders Pålsson, Eva Ageberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeople with longstanding hip and groin pain (LHGP) are often referred to orthopaedic care. Physical therapist-led interventions are recommended in consensus statements as the first line of treatment, but it is unknown if structured interventions are more effective than usual care. The aim of this trial is to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured physical therapist-led treatment model (HIPSTER) compared with usual care on hip-related quality of life at 4 months for people with LHGP referred to orthopaedic care.Methods and analysisThis is a preregistered (clinicaltrials.gov, NCT05853640) study protocol for a double-blinded two-armed pragmatic randomised controlled trial. Patients with LHGP (n=122), referred to the Department of Orthopaedics at a university hospital in Sweden, will be randomised into the HIPSTER model or usual care. The HIPSTER model is a 16-week structured, individualised progressive treatment using exercise therapy and patient education. Usual care consists of a recommendation to contact a physical therapist in primary care. Both groups will undergo standard examinations and a surgical consultation at the Department of Orthopaedics. The primary outcome will be the mean group change in the International Hip Outcome Tool from baseline to 4 months, according to intention-to-treat principles. Secondary outcomes include patient-reported outcomes (such as perceived improvement, psychological factors and physical activity), physical impairment tests and radiographic measures. Additional time points will be 1, 2 and 5 years after baseline. Subgroups of patients will complete semistructured interviews and report additional data on psychosocial variables to provide more information on patient experience as well as determinants of adherence.Ethics and disseminationThe Swedish Ethical Review Authority approved this study (Dnr 202205023–01). The results of this study will be published, regardless of results, in scientific journals and as plain language summaries for participants.Trial registration numberNCT05853640.Keywords: Hip, Musculoskeletal disorders, REHABILITATION MEDICINE

Introduction

Non-arthritic longstanding hip and groin pain (LHGP) is a common problem in young to middle-aged physically active people and is associated with reduced quality of life, function, physical activity and participation.1 LHGP is likely multifactorial in origin,2 3 where intra-articular and extra-articular pathologies may co-exist.4,6 Intra-articular pathologies, such as femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) and hip dysplasia, have been linked with the development of osteoarthritis.7 8 Previous studies report no difference in patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) or physical impairments in people with LHGP with or without intra-articular pathologies, apart from less hip range of motion (ROM) in the intra-articular group.1 9 Both groups report significantly worse PROMs and physical function compared with healthy controls.1 9

Physical therapist-led interventions are recommended as first-line treatment for musculoskeletal disorders, including LHGP.10 11 Two international consensus statements11 12 and one clinical practice guideline13 have been published for physical therapist-led interventions for intra-articular hip pain. However, only a few pilot studies have compared the effectiveness of different physical therapist-led interventions for LHGP,14 reporting completeness of these interventions are poor or non-existent,15 and usual care by physical therapists in primary care does not fully adhere to clinical recommendations.16 As such, physical therapist-led interventions for LHGP are an often recommended but potentially underdeveloped treatment option.

People with LHGP often get referred to orthopaedic care for diagnosis and treatment. Surgical treatment options include osteoplasty and/or labral repair using hip arthroscopy for FAIS17 and open periacetabular osteotomy for hip dysplasia.18 However, previous studies have reported that only 50% of patients referred to the Department of Orthopaedics for LHGP are classified as having intra-articular pathologies that may be amendable to surgical intervention.1 19 Currently, patients with LHGP referred for orthopaedic consultation in tertiary care in Sweden often are put on waiting lists for several months prior to consultation, and no structured physical therapist-led intervention is provided during that time. A structured physical therapist-led intervention can potentially benefit patients with LHGP, and as both intra-articular and extra-articular pathologies present with similar physical impairments, an intervention can be based on identified impairments rather than structural diagnosis alone.

Studies report that people with LHGP have physical impairments, such as poorer hip muscle strength,9 20 21 worse dynamic balance9 20 and less hip ROM9 21 compared with healthy controls. Improvements in dynamic pelvic control,22 and hip abductor and adductor strength22 23 have been associated with improvements in self-reported symptoms in people with LHGP. While the literature on LHGP mainly focuses on physical factors, such as hip morphology and ROM,24 psychological factors seem to also play an important role.24,26 Symptom severity in people in FAIS is more closely related to mental health scores than severity of labral damage or morphology.27 Also, psychological factors, such as high pain catastrophising and low self-efficacy, are associated with poorer function25 28 and worse outcomes after arthroscopy.25 28 29 Interventions addressing both physical and psychological factors have been shown to improve outcomes in physical function and reduce pain intensity in individuals with chronic pain30 and low back pain31 compared with usual care. Patient education is considered a key component of quality musculoskeletal rehabilitation10 32 and can potentially affect psychosocial variables associated with poor outcomes.24 However, a recent systematic review showed that the reporting on the use of patient education strategies in physical therapist-led interventions for LHGP is generally poor.15 Despite evidence of the effectiveness of interventions encompassing both physical and psychological components in musculoskeletal care, this remains largely unexplored for people with LHGP.

Therefore, the aim of this trial is to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured physical therapist treatment model (HIPSTER) compared with usual care on hip-related quality of life at 4 months for people with LHGP referred to orthopaedic care.

The main hypothesis is that the HIPSTER model will be superior to usual care, beyond the minimally important clinical difference (MCID). Secondary aims include comparing between-group differences in patient-reported and objective outcomes as well as comparing physical impairment tests between patients and healthy controls in the short and long term (up to 5 years).

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a double-blinded two-armed superiority pragmatic randomised controlled trial, adhering to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement extension for pragmatic trials for reporting randomised trials (www.consort-statement.org).33 This trial was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Dnr 202205023–01) and was preregistered in Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05853640). This protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines34 (figure 1 and online supplemental file 2).

Study setting

Department of Orthopaedics (tertiary care), Skåne University Hospital and primary care centres in the southern part of Sweden.

Participants

Recruitment and eligibility

An orthopaedic surgeon (IK) in charge of hip arthroscopy at the Department of Orthopaedics at Skåne University Hospital will screen incoming referral notes and related medical history for all patients referred for LHGP. Eligible patients will be contacted through telephone by the study coordinator (AE), a PhD student and a physical therapist clinician with experience assessing and treating people with LHGP. The study coordinator will provide information about the study, and patients who express interest in participating will be asked further questions relating to inclusion and exclusion criteria (table 1). A plain language summary about the study will be sent via email if the patient is eligible. If the patient volunteers to participate in the study, consent will be collected through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a web-based application for data collection available through Lund University.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for the HIPSTER trial

After consent has been obtained, an experienced physical therapist at the Department of Orthopaedics, trained by clinical experts from the research team (AE and KT), will perform a clinical examination and screen patients according to inclusion and exclusion criteria (table 1). The number of referrals and patients screened will be recorded, as well as reasons for exclusion or for declining participation (reporting is voluntary for patients due to ethical legislation in Sweden).

Demographics

Participant demographics, including age, sex, height and weight, will be collected during data collection.

Patient and public partnership

People with LHGP undergoing a physical therapist-led intervention for the treatment of LHGP (n=3) were interviewed during the development of the study. The patients provided input on the overall design of the study and the experimental intervention, leveraging their expertise to ensure the applicability and usability of the education materials and exercise therapy. Clinically active physical therapists (n=2) not involved in the study also provided feedback on the structure and clarity of the experimental intervention and the overall usability of the treatment manual.

Randomisation

After baseline assessment, the participants will be randomised to either the HIPSTER model or to usual care, in a 1:1 ratio, using computer-generated random numbers and block randomisation. The script was generated and uploaded to REDCap, where the allocation sequence is hidden for the study coordinator randomising the participants.

Blinding

The physical therapist at the Department of Orthopaedics will screen and collect data prior to randomisation. The test leader conducting the data collection (AP) will perform baseline data collection prior to randomisation and will remain blinded to allocation at the subsequent data collection. The orthopaedic surgeon will be blinded to group allocation. Participants will be blinded through limited disclosure by being informed that two different care pathways for individuals with LHGP referred to orthopaedic care are being compared, which may include exercise and education. Participants will be unaware of the study hypothesis. The statistician performing data analysis will be blinded to group allocation.

Orthopaedic diagnostic pathway

All patients will undergo the standard orthopaedic diagnostic pathway as follows. The orthopaedic surgeon will order standardized radiological examinations (plain radiographs of the pelvis and hip, including anteroposterior and modified Dunn views) if recent appropriate imaging is not already available. Within 4 weeks of inclusion in the study, participants will receive a fluoroscopically guided diagnostic injection to the hip joint at the Department of Orthopaedics. The orthopaedic surgeon in charge of hip arthroscopy (IK) will administer the injection of anaesthetics (2 mL triamcinolone (Lederspan, 20 mg/mL), 4 mL ropivacaine (Narop 10 mg/mL) and cortisone (4 mL lidocaine (Xylocain 10 mg/mL)). Immediately prior to, and 10–20 min after injection of anaesthetics and cortisone, the study coordinator (AE) will perform the Flexion Adduction Internal Rotation test (FADIR)35 as well as measure hip internal rotation ROM. Hip internal rotation will be measured with the hip in 90° of flexion, using a digital inclinometer. Pain will be recorded using a numerical rating scale (NRS) from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable). After injection, patients will rate their change in pain during the FADIR test using a seven-point Likert scale (from much worse (1) to much better (7)).

After the primary endpoint, participants will undergo a clinical consultation with an orthopaedic surgeon (IK), according to clinical practice at Skåne University Hospital. This consultation consists of a patient interview; a clinical examination of the low back, pelvis, abdominal wall and hip and groin (active and passive ROM, isometric muscle tests and impingement tests); and interpretation of imaging. A patient is considered eligible for surgery if they have positive examination findings, such as a positive FADIR test and restricted hip ROM, imaging findings indicating abnormal morphology, such as an alpha angle above 60°, and a positive response to diagnostic injection.3 Patients who are eligible for surgery will be scheduled for arthroscopic surgery of the identified morphological issue or damaged tissue.

The orthopaedic diagnostic pathway in the context of the HIPSTER trial contains small deviations from usual clinical practice at Skåne University Hospital. The normal timing of the diagnostic injection varies by months in usual clinical practice (usually provided within 3–6 months after referral) and is normally provided after the clinical examination by the orthopaedic surgeon. To avoid the potential influence of the injection on the study, the injection will be performed within 1 month of inclusion. As a result, the order of injection and clinical examination by the orthopaedic surgeon has been switched in the trial.

HIPSTER model

Procedure

The HIPSTER model comprises a 16-week treatment package that includes patient education and structured, individualised and progressive exercise therapy (table 2) provided by physical therapists in primary care. Physical therapists will receive training in the intervention model by two researcher team members (AE and EA), including prerecorded presentations on LHGP assessment and treatment, as well as a 4-hour on-site workshop. All treating physical therapists will receive detailed treatment manuals and access to all patient-facing materials, such as education recordings and exercise videos.

During the intervention period, patients will receive 8–16 supervised treatment sessions with a physical therapist. Session frequency will be decided in shared decision-making between the treating physical therapist and the patient, though weekly visits are recommended during the first 4 weeks, then weekly or biweekly as deemed appropriate. In addition to the supervised sessions, the treating physical therapist will prescribe home- or gym-based exercises for the patient to complete on their own. While exercises and progressions are described in the treatment manual, the treating physical therapists may modify exercises and dosage depending on patient presentation (see online supplemental file 1 for the HIPSTER-model treatment manual, original version in Swedish, and translated to English).

Education

The education component of the HIPSTER model complements the exercise therapy by aiming to improve patient understanding of their condition and the rationale for the treatment, and improving adherence and investment in the exercise therapy intervention. The education will also target psychological factors such as pain catastrophising and self-efficacy.25 28

The key components of the education are (1) pain and pain management, that is, information on the nature of pain, its association with structural damage and influence of other lifestyle and psychosocial factors, changes with chronic pain conditions and pain monitoring strategies36; (2) rehabilitation, that is, information regarding the different phases of the treatment model and how the model progresses towards the patients’ desired functional activity; (3) patient expectations and goals, that is, information and discussion on the patient’s expectations of treatment and actual prognosis based on current evidence, each patient’s specific goals and how exercise therapy can help the patient achieve those goals37 and (4) physical activity, that is, information on the importance of adherence, any perceived physical or psychological barriers to adherence, the benefits of exercise on a systemic and local level (such as overall higher pain threshold and increased muscle strength and joint stability) and strategies to adapt exercise dosage based on symptom response and perceived exertion.

The patient education in the HIPSTER trial will be provided in two main formats: individualised face-to-face education provided by the treating physical therapists as part of the supervised sessions and as prewritten and recorded online materials available for participants. Treating physical therapists will receive guidance on addressing key components of education, as well as tailored information for the individual, provided in the intervention manual and training. As preferred by patients involved in the development of the study, as well as in a qualitative study on preferred education strategies,38 the online materials for patients will consist of texts and video presentations on the key components of the education for patients to return to as needed. Patients will receive a link to a webpage housing these presentations and will be asked not to share its content, to avoid spreading during active data collection. In the initial phases of the intervention, the treating physical therapist will focus on providing education, making sure key components have been covered. These components can then be revisited as needed during further supervised sessions. See online supplemental file 1 for details on education components.

Exercise therapy

The optimal dosage and method of delivering exercise therapy for people with LHGP is currently unknown. Consensus papers and clinical practice guidelines suggest a focus on improving identified physical impairments,39 intervention duration >3 months and targeting muscle strength deficits.11 40 Given that people with LHGP commonly present with altered movement strategies around the hip and pelvis,41 42 impairments to hip and trunk muscle strength and endurance20 and deficits in functional task performance,20 the exercise therapy in the present study will focus on three main components: (1) neuromuscular control of the hip and pelvis, (2) hip and trunk muscle strength and endurance and (3) functional task performance. Neuromuscular control is defined as the ability to produce controlled movement through coordinated muscle activity,43 limiting painful end-range movement of the hip and reducing joint loads.

The overall principles and treatment strategies of the exercise intervention are described in table 3. In summary, the exercise protocol is divided into three phases. Phase 1 will focus on establishing a tolerable exercise routine and hip and trunk muscle endurance with lower intensity and higher volume, as well as neuromuscular control in supported positions. Phase 2 will include increasing the intensity of hip and trunk exercises and increasing demands on neuromuscular control. Phase 3 will include combining neuromuscular control and hip strength into functional task performance, including compound lower extremity exercises and complex movements. Phases 1 and 2 will last approximately 2–4 weeks each, and phase 3 will last approximately 8–12 weeks with exercises performed three times per week. Shared decision-making between the treating physical therapist and the patient is used to create a structured and individualised programme, taking factors such as patient preference, pain response and functional progression into account. As such, progression through stages and exercises of the treatment model will vary from individual to individual.

Usual care

Usual care consists of a recommendation to the participant to contact a physical therapy clinic in primary care if no such contact is already established (table 2). The content, delivery, frequency and duration of any physical therapist-led intervention in the usual care group will be at the discretion of the provider, with no interference by the investigators. Physical therapists in Sweden are autonomous practitioners, and therefore, the provided treatment may vary considerably from therapist to therapist. Previous research on primary care management of LHGP indicates physical therapists do not fully adhere to clinical recommendations and that treatment is heterogeneous and may include manual therapy, exercise therapy, stretching, education and/or other modalities.16 As such, potential intervention prescribed within the usual care group is likely to be highly variable. To avoid contamination, the therapists involved in the HIPSTER model arm will not provide physical therapist-led interventions to the usual care group.

Outcomes

Data will be collected using PROMs, tests of physical impairments and radiographic imaging. All PROMs will be administered to participants by email using REDCap44 and objective measures will be collected on site at Lund University and Skåne University Hospital. Data collection time points will be at baseline and 4, 12, 24 and 60 months after baseline (table 4).

Outcomes in the HIPSTER study

Adherence and adverse events will be collected weekly in the HIPSTER group.

AP, anteroposterior; iHOT-33, International Hip Outcome Tool; LHGP, Longstanding hip and groin pain; ROM, range of motion.

Primary outcome and endpoint

The primary outcome and endpoint will be the mean between-group change between baseline and 4 months in hip-related quality of life, assessed using the International Hip Outcome Tool (iHOT-33) (table 4). The iHOT-33 is a valid and responsive PROM for young to middle-aged people with hip and groin pain45 and is recommended for research use in an international consensus statement.46 It comprises 33 questions across four domains: symptoms and functional limitations, sports and recreational physical activities, job-related concerns and social, emotional and lifestyle concerns. It is scored on a 0–100 scale, where 100 represents no symptoms, and 0 indicates severe symptoms. A minimal clinically important difference on the iHOT-33 has been reported to be 6–10 points in people with FAIS undergoing arthroscopy.45 47 The iHOT-33 has been translated into Swedish and has undergone cross-cultural validation.48

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes include patient-reported outcomes, tests of physical impairments, radiographic imaging and surgical interventions (table 4).

Patient-reported outcomes

Hip-related quality of life will be collected at 0, 4, 12, 24 and 60 months using the iHOT-33.

The patient acceptable symptom state will be collected at 4, 12, 24 and 60 months using a dichotomised question (‘Taking into account your hip and groin function and pain and how it affects your daily life, including your ability to participate in sport and social activities, do you consider that your current state is acceptable if it remained like that for the rest of your life?’).49

Treatment failure will be collected at 4 and 12 months using a dichotomised question: ‘Do you consider your current state so unsatisfactory that you think your treatment has failed?’50

Perceived symptom improvement will be collected at 4 and 12 months using a seven-point Global Rating Of Change scale, ranging from −3 (a lot worse) to +3 (a lot better).51

Patient desire and beliefs regarding surgical intervention will be collected at baseline, 4 and 12 months using two dichotomised questions: ‘Do you want to undergo surgery?” and “Do you believe surgery is needed for you to get better?’.

Physical activity and return to sport/exercise will be collected at baseline, 4 and 12 months using questions based on the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare physical activity screening.52 53

Pain catastrophising will be collected at baseline, 4 and 12 months using the Pain Catastrophising Scale, a valid and reliable PROM.54 55

Pain-related self-efficacy will be collected at baseline, 4 and 12 months using the short-form Pain Self-efficacy Questionnaire,56 a commonly used measure of self-efficacy in musculoskeletal pain,57 that has been validated in Swedish.58

Generic health-related quality of life will be collected at baseline, 4 and 12 months using EuroQol five dimensions (EQ5D), a valid and reliable PROM.59 It is scored from 0 to 1, where 1 corresponds to perfect health.

Employment status will be collected at baseline, 4 and 12 months using self-reported questions.

Other non-operative treatment will be collected at baseline, 4 and 12 months using self-reported questions.

Adherence and adverse events will be reported weekly by participants in the HIPSTER model, and the usual care group will report the number of visits and adherence to treatment at 4 months. Adherence will be reported as a percentage of exercise therapy completed, and adverse events will be reported using a Likert scale.

Physical impairment tests

Measures of physical impairments will be completed at the Movement and Reality Lab at Lund University, at baseline and 4 months. All participants will be asked to complete all PROMs prior to physical data collection, and all measurements will be completed by an experienced test leader (AP) blinded to group allocation.

Passive hip ROM will be measured with a digital inclinometer (Easy Angle (Meloq, Sweden)) in internal rotation at 90° and 0° of hip flexion,60 which has been reported as reliable.61 Three measurements will be taken in each direction, with the mean value serving as the outcome. Patients will be asked to rate their pain on an NRS from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable).

Isometric hip muscle force production will be measured with a belt-fixated handheld dynamometer (Mark-10 Series 3 (Mark-10 Corporation, Copiague, New York, USA)) for hip adduction, flexion and extension. Adduction will be tested as documented by Thorborg et al,62 63 and flexion and extension will be tested in a novel semiprone position that was tested by the authors in a separate study and found to have good reliability (manuscript submitted). The participant will be asked to conduct a maximal contraction for 5 s under strong verbal encouragement, and the peak force will be recorded. Three attempts with a 20-s rest period will be completed, and the maximal value will serve as the outcome. The participant will also be asked if the movement reproduced pain and, if so, asked to rate the pain on an NRS from 0 to 10.

Single-leg balance will be assessed using the Y-balance test.64

Hop performance will be measured by the single-leg hop for distance.65

Radiographic measures

Routine care in the orthopaedic diagnostic pathway at Skåne University Hospital includes standardised plain radiographs. The modified Dunn view will be used to quantify the alpha angle, and the anteroposterior pelvis view will be used to quantify the lateral centre-edge angle and the Tönnis grade, according to the methodology described by Clohisy et al.66 An experienced orthopaedic surgeon (IK) will analyse all projections. Modified Dunn of the affected hip and pelvic anteroposterior view will be collected at baseline, and the pelvic anteroposterior view will be repeated at 60 months.

Surgical intervention

Any hip surgery performed will be collected through medical records at 12, 24 and 60 months.

Nested studies

Participants in the main study, as well as healthy controls (n=60) matched by age, sex and activity level, will perform functional tasks, measured in three-dimensional kinematic data using a 16-camera video-based motion capture system (Qualisys AB, Gothenburg) at 100 Hz, and kinetic data using 4 AMTI 4060 force plates (AMTI, USA) at 1000 Hz. A set of 56 retroreflective markers will be attached to anatomical landmarks of the upper extremity, trunk, pelvis and lower extremities of the participant. Participants will perform three low-impact tasks (single-leg stance, double-leg deep squat and single-leg squat) and three high-impact tasks (Bulgarian split jump, side hop and single-leg hop for distance). Each functional task will be performed three times in a standardised fashion. Kinematic (peak angles) and kinetic (external moments normalised for bodyweight) data will be derived from the recordings for hip, knee, ankle, pelvis and trunk.

Smaller samples of patients will be asked to participate in two nested studies. Qualitative interviews will be conducted with patients (n=15 or until saturation) to explore their perceptions of living with, and seeking care for, LHGP. A subgroup of patients randomised into the experimental group (n=10) will also be recruited into a focus group discussion on the perceptions of the intervention. A subgroup of patients (n=20 in each arm) will be asked to answer PROMs relating to psychosocial factors of LHGP, such as perceived support, fear, motivation, self-efficacy and enablement. Results from nested studies will be presented in future exploratory articles; this protocol will therefore not cover this in-depth.

Data analysis and statistics

Data management

PROMs and physical data will be compiled to a pseudonymised database and stored on secure servers at Lund University, accessible only to the principal investigator (EA), the study coordinator (AE) and researchers involved in data analysis. Coding lists to identify individual participants will be stored separately from the database. The study coordinator (AE) will monitor data regularly and check for quality by range checks, and any irregularities will be discussed with the research team. Individual participants will be given access to their physical impairment data on request.

Sample size calculation

We calculated the sample size based on the primary outcome, iHOT-33, using SPSS V.28 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA) with alpha set at 0.05 and power at 0.8, employing a one-tailed analysis to detect a one-way difference, in line with the superiority design. Previous validation and intervention studies using the iHOT-33 have reported an SD of 16–21 points23 45 67 and an MCID of 6–10 points for people with FAIS undergoing hip arthroscopy.45 47 Standardised mean differences (SMD) of up to 0.66 have been reported for people with FAIS undergoing physical therapist-led interventions in a systematic review,40 which corresponds to the sample size requirements of n=60 (n=30 in each group). As the effect sizes and MCID for people with LHGP undergoing physical therapist-led interventions are currently unknown, we chose the more conservative SMD of 0.5 to reduce the risk of an underpowered study. Calculating sample sizes based on an MCID of 10 points and an SD of 20, corresponding to an SMD of 0.5, results in a sample size requirement of n=102 (n=51 per group). To account for a 20% drop-out rate, the total sample size needed for this study is n=122.

Data analysis

Data will be analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle. An independent t-test will be performed to compare group differences in iHOT-33 mean change from baseline to 4 months. A 10-point difference will be considered clinically relevant. In line with previous studies,67 68 the superiority margin was set to zero rather than corresponding to the clinically relevant difference.69 The statistical analyses will be completed by a statistician blinded to group allocation. Secondary outcomes, including PROMs and physical impairment tests, will be analysed using parametric and non-parametric tests as appropriate. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and proportions, and continuous variables as means and SDs or medians and IQRs after checking normality assumptions. We will also complete a per-protocol analysis on participants who completed the HIPSTER model with high or low adherence (≥70% or <70% of total exercise sessions completed, respectively), compared with usual care.

Secondary and exploratory analyses will be performed to examine long-term effectiveness, factors potentially influencing intervention effectiveness (eg, intra-articular pathology, response to diagnostic injection, pain catastrophising and self-efficacy) and the number of performed surgeries in the two treatment groups. Incremental cost-effectiveness will be calculated by using quality-adjusted life years from EQ5D and healthcare utilisation from medical records and trial data.

Trial status

This trial is currently ongoing, with the first participant enrolled in July 2023. Based on our previous cohort study on people with LHGP referred to orthopaedic care (n=81),1 recruitment was expected to take approximately 3–4 years. As of February 2026, 56 participants have been recruited, with an estimated end date of recruitment in December 2028.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (2022–05023-01). Individuals with LHGP were consulted during the development of the trial methodology and experimental intervention, and shared decision-making will be used in the experimental group. Informed consent will be provided by all participants, and contact information for the principal investigator and study coordinator will be present in written communication to the participants. The authors plan to publish the results of the trial, as described in this protocol and preregistration, regardless of study results, in peer-reviewed scientific journals. Participants in the trial will be offered a plain language summary of the results. Any significant changes to the trial will be reported in amendments to the trial registration.

Strengths and limitations

This study focuses on people with LHGP, a heterogeneous patient population. Including patients with both intra-articular and extra-articular sources of hip and groin pain can be considered both a strength and a limitation.

Similar PROMs and physical impairments have been reported in people with intra-articular and extra-articular LHGP,1 9 and even within intra-articular pathologies, the physical impairments are heterogeneous.70 Therefore, testing a structured and individualised treatment model that targets patient-reported and physical impairments rather than structural diagnosis is a reasonable and appealing approach. However, as the primary analysis is based on a heterogeneous population, specific treatment effects related to morphology and diagnosis may be overlooked. Secondary data analysis is therefore planned to explore diagnostic classification and pathology-specific responses to the intervention.

Physical therapist-led interventions are considered first-line treatment for musculoskeletal pain. In LHGP, few studies have compared the effectiveness of different physical therapist-led interventions,71 and thus far, interventions have been primarily based on a biomedical rationale.24 No studies to date have explored the potential benefit of including an education component focusing on the multifaceted factors related to outcomes in this patient group. Addressing psychosocial factors and beliefs and expectations of the patients can potentially optimise response to exercise therapy by improving adherence and understanding of their condition.11 24

Another strength of this study is the secondary data collection, including nested studies on patient perceptions of living with LHGP and psychosocial variables and their interaction with the different interventions. This study will also explore biomechanical variables in a large sample of patients with LHGP. While biomechanical differences between healthy controls and hip-related pathologies have been reported, primarily on gait, their relationship with symptoms and quality of life is underreported.72 Exploring psychosocial and mechanical variables in people with LHGP can potentially inform future research and interventions.

The pragmatic nature of the study, in combination with detailed reporting of the experimental intervention, will enhance future implementation into clinical practice. A clinical pathway where patients progress in a stepwise fashion from the least invasive intervention to the most has the potential to optimise resource utilisation within the healthcare system. Patients with LHGP may benefit from this structure by reduced diagnostic delays, improved outcomes and potentially avoidance of unnecessary surgical intervention.
