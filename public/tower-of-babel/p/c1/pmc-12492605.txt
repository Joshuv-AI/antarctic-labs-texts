Anxiety in young university students: the mediating role of sense of coherence and self-esteem
Authors: Henrique da-Silva-Domingues, Pedro Ángel Palomino-Moral, Rafael del-Pino-Casado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnxiety is a frequent mental health concern among university students, shaped by psychological, social, academic, and economic influences. While previous studies have linked anxiety to factors such as social support, family functioning, sense of coherence, and self-esteem, the mediating role of sense of coherence and self-esteem remains unclear. This study investigates these relationships, with particular attention to the potential mediating effects. The present study aimed to investigate the relationships between social support, family functioning, sense of coherence, self-esteem, and anxiety, with an emphasis on the mediating role of sense of coherence and self-esteem.MethodsThe study included 530 university students from public universities in Andalusia (Spain), selected through stratified multistage cluster sampling. Analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, t-tests, and path analysis.ResultsThe mean age of university students was 20.11 years. The majority of participants in the study were women (56.6%). More than 60% of university students considered themselves anxious. Anxiety was strongly and negatively associated with sense of coherence and self-esteem, and moderately negatively associated with social support and family functioning. Path analysis indicated that the model explained 69% of the variance in anxiety and 49% of self-esteem.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that social support and family functioning influence anxiety levels in university students, with sense of coherence and self-esteem acting as key mediators. Higher levels of both are linked to lower anxiety and better student well-being.Keywords: Anxiety, Students, College, Sense of coherence, Self concept, Social support, Family functioning

Background

Mental health has become a growing concern in the field of public health due to the high prevalence of problems caused by the imbalances of the mental well-being, such as stress, anxiety, or depression. Mental well-being is necessary to face life’s challenges and stressful situations and for the personal development of potentialities and qualities that include the ability to learn, the ability to work properly and also the ability to contribute to the improvement of one’s own community [1].

Young university students experience a stage of progressive transition to adult Life, during which skills related to personal and social competencies, decision-making or the adoption and consolidation of Lifestyles that are related to potential health benefits or risks in different aspects, such as diet, physical activity, sexual health and the consumption of alcohol and other drugs. The importance of this stage is also evident in the fact that most mental health problems have their onset between adolescence and 25 years of age [2].

The main determinants of the mental health of young university students include a wide variety of psychological, social, academic or economic factors that can significantly influence the appearance of mental health problems such as stress, anxiety or depression [3].

Entering university poses significant challenges for students’ mental health and well-being, such as adapting to new academic demands and distance from their support networks [4, 5]. These factors can generate emotional tensions that, although they decrease over time, do not return to the levels prior to entering university, making university students a population vulnerable to mental health problems [5]. This stage represents one of the main life transitions for students, which is not always successfully overcome by all young people [6].

The mental health of university students has been the subject of growing interest and research in recent decades; in a recent bibliometric analysis [5], the most frequent keywords Indexing mental health research over the past 45 years refers to common mental health challenges experienced by college students, such as depression, anxiety, and stress.

Anxiety is defined as a cognitive response and threats or dangers perceived by people [7]; This emotion can be a reaction to a specific situation (state anxiety) or more general and persistent (trait anxiety) resulting from adaptive maladjustment. The list of potential stressors is wide in the university population and encompasses factors such as workload and academic demands, lack of support from the university environment, academic expectations, economic situation, difficulties in accommodation and adaptation to the environment, social and affective relationships (isolation), social networks and uncertainty about the future [8–10]. In the university context, the project WHO World Mental Health Surveys International College Student [11] report showed that 35.5% of university students have experienced some form of mental disorder, one of the most common being generalized anxiety disorder. In addition, 20.4% of these cases report a significant impact on their academic, social and personal life [11]. In Line with these findings, an important study in Spain conducted by the Ministry of Universities, together with the Ministry of Health, reported that approximately 50% of students reported moderate or severe anxiety. In addition, 50.3% of students consulted with a health professional because of mental health problems, while 56.6% have perceived the need for psychological support due to recent emotional difficulties [12].

The appearance of anxiety in young university students is influenced by multiple factors. Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Stress Model [13] helps explain this phenomenon, as it describes how individuals evaluate and cope with stressful situations, considering both environmental demands and personal resources. In the context of anxiety, students with limited coping resources or lower perceived support may experience higher anxiety levels. Accordingly, previous studies have highlighted the role of social support [14], family functioning [15], sense of coherence (SOC) [16] and self-esteem [17] in modulating anxiety in this population. These variables were selected because they represent key personal and environmental resources that, according to the Transactional Stress Model, influence how young university students perceive and cope with stressful situations, and thus may modulate anxiety levels.

Previous research has extensively examined the interrelations between social support, SOC, self-esteem, family functioning, and anxiety in university students. Social support, whether from family, friends, or significant others, has consistently shown a positive association with SOC in different cultural contexts [18, 19]. Similarly, several studies confirm that higher levels of perceived social support are linked to higher self-esteem [20, 21]. The relationship between social support and anxiety has yielded mixed results: while some studies found no significant correlation [22], others reported an inverse association, with greater social support linked to lower anxiety levels [14].

Family functioning is another factor related to these variables. Poor family functioning or higher family stress has been associated with lower SOC [23] and lower self-esteem [24]. Likewise, studies in adolescents and university students have reported a negative relationship between family functioning and anxiety levels [15, 25].

SOC has also been directly related to self-esteem, with higher SOC linked to greater self-esteem in both men and women [26]. In turn, stronger SOC has been associated with lower anxiety symptoms [16, 27]. Self-esteem itself has an inverse relationship with anxiety: lower self-esteem is consistently associated with higher anxiety levels across different cultural and academic contexts [17, 28–30].

In this regard, studies in the university context have also highlighted the relevance of these psychosocial variables. Morales-Rodríguez et al. [31] reported associations between psychological well-being, anxiety, emotional intelligence, social skills, and self-concept, while Simón et al. [32] showed that self-efficacy and psychological well-being moderated the relationship between anxiety and academic engagement, reinforcing the importance of these factors among university students.

The available findings highlight the complex interactions between social support, family functioning, SOC, self-esteem, and anxiety in young university students, with important implications for their psychological well-being and academic performance. However, a gap in knowledge persists regarding the role of self-esteem and SOC as mediators in the relationship between social support, family functioning, and anxiety in college students. Existing research has not sufficiently examined how these constructs act as explanatory or amplification mechanisms in such relationships.

Clarifying the relationships between these variables can provide a better understanding of the factors influencing the mental health of this population. Previous research has demonstrated the mediating effect of the SOC in some relationships; In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, longitudinal research on Brazilian university students revealed that SOC mediated the link between social support and depression and between social support and stress [33]. It has also been observed that the mean SOC partially explains the relationship between revealed childhood trauma and levels of anxiety and depression [34].

Therefore, our work is based on the premise that SOC and self-esteem mediate the effects of social support and family functioning on anxiety, so that these factors have both direct and indirect effects on anxiety levels in university students (Fig. 1).

Conceptual model

Figure 1 presents the conceptual model of pathways proposed in this study. It posits that social support and family functioning, understood as contextual resources, influence university students’ anxiety both directly and indirectly. These effects are mediated by two psychological factors: SOC, which represents a global resource for approaching experiences in a comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful way, and self-esteem, which reflects personal evaluation. The model includes a sequential mediation, in which higher SOC contributes to the strengthening of self-esteem, and both resources are associated with lower levels of anxiety. In this way, the model integrates social and personal factors within a single framework to explain anxiety in university students.

The present study aimed to investigate the relationships between social support, family functioning, SOC, self-esteem, and anxiety, with an emphasis on the mediating role of SOC and self-esteem in these relationships.

Materials and methods

Design

Cross-sectional descriptive study.

Population, study setting, and sampling procedure

The study focused on undergraduate students from Andalusian public universities, who were enrolled during the 2021/2022 academic year.

A multistage cluster sampling was used in which the clusters corresponded to Andalusian public universities. These items were grouped according to their size, academic area, and course. The sample size was 361 participants (minimum), based on three fundamental criteria: (1) To calculate the mean of the measuring instruments with a margin of error of ± 1 unit, considering an estimated standard deviation of 6.0. (2) The sample size was adequate to detect a minimum correlation coefficient of 0.13, with a statistical power of 80% and a significance level of 5%. (3) This size also allowed detecting a minimum r² of 0.05 attributed to five independent variables, adjusted by two control variables with an increase in r² of 0.01, with a power of 80% and a significance level of 5%. Sample size calculations were performed using PASS 2021 (NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA).

The final sample consisted of 530 university students. Several factors were considered for the selection: the type of university (large and small), the branches of knowledge (Social and Legal Sciences, Engineering and Architecture, Arts and Humanities, Health Sciences, and Sciences), and the academic year (from 1 st to 4th grade).

The selection process was conducted in five phases:

Stratified random sampling: Given the variability in the number of students per university, universities were stratified into two groups: large (more than 15,000 students) and small (less than 15,000 students). The selection pool included all nine public universities in Andalusia, Spain. Two universities were randomly selected from each group: Seville (52.298 students) and Granada (43.926 students) as large universities, and Huelva (9.003 students) and Jaén (11.998 students) as small universities. The classification into large and small universities was based on the average number of enrolled students in all universities in the academic year prior to the study (14.934), considering universities with more students than this average as large and those with fewer as small.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To participate in the study, students were required to be enrolled in one of the selected class groups, agree to participate voluntarily, and be between 18 and 24 years of age. Students with visual impairments that made it difficult to read the questionnaires and those without sufficient understanding of the Spanish language were excluded.

Instrument validity and reliability

Independent variables

Family functioning

Evaluated through the APGAR Family questionnaire. The instrument consists of 5 questions with three answer options on a Likert scale (Almost never = 0, Sometimes = 1, Almost always = 2). Family functioning describe how family members interact, influencing group cohesion and stability through norms, roles, and affective relationships [35]. Example items include: “Are you satisfied with the help you receive from your family when you have a problem?” and “Do you discuss the problems you have at home with each other?” Scores can range from 0 to 10, and the score is considered a dichotomous variable (APGAR > 6 = functional, APGAR < 6 = dysfunctional) [36]. In the Spanish validation, the test-retest reliability of this instrument is 0.86, with a it presented a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.84 [37].

Social support

Evaluated through the Duke-UNC-11 questionnaire, adapted to the Spanish population [38, 39], is a self-administered instrument composed of 11 items that use a Likert-type response scale from 1 to 5. Social support is the availability of reliable relationships that provide emotional, instrumental, or informational assistance, and can come from natural sources, such as family and friends, or formal sources, such as health professionals [40]. Example items include: “Are you satisfied with the help you receive from your family when you have a problem?” and “Do you discuss the problems you have at home with each other?”. Scores range from 11 to 55 points, with a cut-off point of 32 or higher indicating normal social support. This instrument has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90 and test-retest correlations of 0.92 [38].

Mediating variables

Sense of coherence

Collected using the 13-item version of the Life Orientation Questionnaire (SOC-13) [41]. The items are measured by a Likert scale from 1 to 7, with a minimum score of 13 and a maximum of 91 points, with the score being proportional to the sense of coherence [42]. The SOC denotes people’s ability to understand, manage, and find meaning in life situations, especially in stressful contexts. This concept promotes effective stress coping and contributes significantly to mental and emotional well-being [43]. Example items include: “Do you have the feeling that you really don’t care about what happens around you?” and “Has it happened in the past that you were surprised by the behavior of people you thought you knew well?”. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the validations with Spanish samples was 0.76 [44] and 0.80 [45].

Self-esteem

Using the Rosenberg questionnaire that has 10 items, measured on a Likert scale from 1 to 4. The score ranges from 10 to 40 and a cut-off score of 30 points was used to categorize as high self-esteem [46, 47]. Self-esteem is defined as the value that a person has of themselves, which is influenced by significant social interactions from childhood and susceptible to variations throughout life [48, 49]. Example items include: “I feel that I am as valuable as other people” and “I generally tend to think that I am a failure”. Previous studies found internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient = 0.87 and 0.86) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.72 and 0.88) [50, 51].

Dependent variable

Anxiety

Using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI Trait) scale consisting of 20 items [52]. The instrument is composed of a 4-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 0 to 60 points. Example items include: “I feel like crying” and “I would like to be happy like others”. A score of 26 points or more was considered as a cut-off point for a medium-high anxiety category and a score of 33 or more as a very high level of anxiety. For men, a score of 20 points or more was considered for a medium-high anxiety category and a score of 26 or higher was considered a very high level of anxiety. The questionnaire has been used in more than 3,000 studies and has proven to be an effective and useful instrument for measuring anxiety [53]. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the Spanish validation was 0.89 [54].

Sociodemographic variables

By means of an “ad hoc” questionnaire, sex, age, and branch of study (Social Sciences and Law, Engineering and Architecture, Arts and Humanities, Health Sciences, Sciences) were collected.

Data collection

Data were collected during September and October 2021 in the selected class groups. Questionnaires and informed consent forms were administered in paper-and-pencil format during class sessions and supervised by the principal researcher, who provided the necessary instructions. The average completion time was approximately 18 min.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis of the quantitative variables was carried out by calculating measures of central tendency and dispersion. For qualitative variables, frequencies and percentages were calculated along with their corresponding confidence intervals (CI). The bivariate relationships between the independent variables (family functioning, social support, the mediating variables (sense of coherence and self-esteem), and the dependent variable (anxiety) were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (for quantitative variables). The differences between the sex of the participants were analyzed using Student’s t-test.

In order to contrast the hypotheses raised, a trajectory analysis was carried out. This technique was selected for its ability to simultaneously evaluate the study hypotheses and incorporate mediation effects [55], as well as to allow the joint estimation of the relationships between variables, reducing possible biases in these estimates. The adequacy of the model was assessed using several indicators: the p-value, the chi-square ratio over the degrees of freedom (X²/df), the mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) together with its confidence interval, the standardized mean square residual (SRMR) and the comparative fit index (CFI). These indices were chosen for their lower sensitivity to sample size, possible errors in model specification, and variations in parameter estimates [56]. The model was considered to show a good fit when the following criteria were met [55, 57]: p > 0.10, X²/df < 2, RMSEA < 0.08, SRMR < 0.08 and CFI > 0.95. The path analysis was performed using AMOS 18 with the maximum Likelihood estimation method, and mediation effects were assessed using bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples to calculate confidence intervals for the indirect effects. For all statistical tests, a significance level of 0.05 was set.

The analyses were performed with the SPSS Statistics 23 program, except for the CI of the frequency measurements, which was calculated with the EpiDat 4.2 and with AMOS 18 for the trajectory analyses.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Provincial Research Ethics Committee of Jaén (1192-N-21, CEI). Before participation, students were informed about the purpose and procedures of the research, emphasizing voluntariness and confidentiality. Participation was anonymous, carried no academic consequences, and all participants, except one who declined, signed the informed consent. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [58].

Trajectory analysis

The analysis of trajectories showed that the model explained 69% of the variance in anxiety (R² = 0.69) and 49% of self-esteem (R² = 0.49) (Fig. 2).

The model presented a good fit to the data, as indicated by the value of χ² = 0.093, p = 0.0761, df = 1 (degree of freedom), χ²/df = 0.093, RMSEA = 0.000 (95% CI [0.000, 0.078], p = 0.87), SRMR = 0.016, CFI = 1.000).

Our results observed significant direct relationships between the variables. Social support (β = 0.27) and family functioning (β = 0.24) were positively associated with SOC. In turn, the SOC presented a positive relationship with self-esteem (β = 0.60) and a strong negative relationship with anxiety (β = −0.47). Self-esteem also showed a direct negative association with anxiety (β = −0.40).

On the other hand, the direct relationship of social support on anxiety was weak (β = −0.06), which highlights the importance of mediated indirect effects. In this sense, social support could reduce anxiety levels through its influence on SOC and self-esteem.

As expected in the model, a positive association was specified between social support and family functioning (r = 0.51), reflecting the theoretical relationship between these factors.

Hypothesis 1 assumes that having a high level of social support is accompanied by lower anxiety, higher self-esteem, and high SOC. This hypothesis was confirmed as social support was negatively associated with anxiety (β = −0.295, standardized total effect) and positively associated with self-esteem (β = 0.10, direct effect) and SOC (β = 0.27, direct effect).

Hypothesis 2 assumes that a family functional dynamic is accompanied by higher self-esteem and a high level of SOC. This hypothesis was supported because family functioning was positively associated with self-esteem (β = 0.10, direct effect) and with SOC (β = 0.24, direct effect).

Hypothesis 3 posited that a high level of SOC is accompanied by lower anxiety and higher self-esteem. This hypothesis was supported as SOC was negatively associated with anxiety (β = −0.716, standardized total effect) and positively associated with self-esteem (β = 0.60, direct effect).

Hypothesis 4 assumed that the SOC mediated the relationship between social support and anxiety. This hypothesis was supported because anxiety was indirectly associated through SOC with social support (β = −0.13, p = 0.006, indirect effect).

Hypothesis 5 proposed that the SOC mediated the relationship between social support and self-esteem, on the one hand, and between family functioning and self-esteem, on the other. This hypothesis was supported as social support was associated with higher self-esteem through the SOC (β = 0.16, p = 0.006, indirect effect). Similarly, family functioning was associated with higher self-esteem through the SOC (β = 0.16, p = 0.002, indirect effect).

Hypothesis 6 predicted that self-esteem mediated the relationship between social support and anxiety, on the one hand, and between family functioning and anxiety, on the other. This hypothesis was supported as social support was associated with lower anxiety through self-esteem (β = −0.04, p = 0.006, indirect effect). Likewise, family functioning were associated with lower anxiety through self-esteem (β = −0.04, p = 0.006, indirect effect).

Hypothesis 7 predicted that self-esteem mediated the relationship between SOC and anxiety. This hypothesis was supported because SOC was associated with lower anxiety through self-esteem (β = −0.24, p = 0.008, indirect effect).

Discussion

In our study, the trajectories model provided a broad view of the problem, including key psychosocial variables and allowing the identification of direct and indirect relationships between them. The model explained a high percentage of the variance of anxiety (R² = 0.69), which demonstrates the robustness of the proposed theoretical model. The anxiety variable in university students was strongly and negatively associated with a sense of coherence and self-esteem and moderately associated with social support and family functioning. These associations allow us to understand how psychosocial, structural, and intermediate health resources [59] act as possible protective factors against anxiety.

Our findings support hypothesis 1 (A high level of social support is associated with lower anxiety, higher self-esteem, and high SOC) and in line with previous literature. Numerous studies have shown that perceived social support acts as a possible buffer against anxiety, providing emotional and practical resources that reduce the negative impact of adverse situations [14, 60]. In addition, it has been shown that feeling supported by other people improves the perception of personal worth and self-confidence, which translates into higher self-esteem [20, 61]. Social support is also associated with a higher SOC and this relationship could be explained because support networks provide structure, guidance and resources that favor a more positive and manageable interpretation of reality [19].

The second hypothesis of our research (A functional family is associated with higher self-esteem and a high level of SOC) was also confirmed by our results. Previous research highlights that the family environment is a key context for developing personal identity and resilience [24, 62]. Functional family relationships, characterized by communication, cohesion and support, provide emotional security that favors both a positive self-image and a sense of structured coherence, with direction and meaning in life [24, 43].

Our findings support hypothesis 3 (A high SOC level is associated with lower levels of anxiety and higher self-esteem). This result confirms that SOC acts as an essential psychological resource for coping with stress and preserving emotional well-being. Young university students with a high SOC tend to interpret their experiences as understandable, manageable and meaningful, which allows them to face adverse situations with lower levels of anxiety and maintain a more stable self-esteem [26, 27]. Although traditionally considered stable, SOC and self-esteem can change during adolescence and early adulthood. In university students, these resources are dynamic and may be strengthened or weakened by social and family factors [30, 44, 61].

Our findings support hypothesis 4 (SOC mediates the relationship between social support and anxiety). In this sense, our result could indicate that the impact of perceived social support on the decrease in anxiety levels could be explained, at least in part, by the strengthening of the SOC. In other words, perceived social support contributes to the development of a high SOC, which in turn reduces the anxiety levels of young university students. In existing Literature, no studies have specifically addressed the SOC mediation between the relationship of perceived social support and trait anxiety. However, a study conducted in 2021 with Chinese university students [63] identified that the SOC played a partial mediating role between the perceived social support and Social anxiety of university students. In this sense, the model of Lazarus and Folkman [13] highlights that the onset of anxiety in young university students is influenced by various factors and offers a solid framework for understanding how individuals manage stress. This model highlights the interaction between the demands of the environment and the available personal resources, with the SOC being a key resource in this process. Thus, according to this theory, university students with a higher perception of social support and with a high SOC could develop a more positive perception of their abilities to cope with difficult situations, which could allow them to manage anxiety better and maintain their psychological well-being [13].

Our results support hypothesis 5 (SOC mediates the relationship between social support and self-esteem, on the one hand, and between family functioning and self-esteem, on the other). This implies that our findings suggest that both a good level of perceived social support and a functional family dynamic strengthen the SOC, which in turn contributes to higher self-esteem in university students. In this way, the SOC could represent a key mechanism through which family and social environments influence the construction of self-esteem [43]. Although no previous studies have been identified that directly analyze the mediation of SOC between social support, family functioning and self-esteem, the available literature provides consistent evidence on its mediating role in similar contexts in the university population. In short, it has been observed that SOC mediates the relationship between academic stress and burnout [64], between stressors and overall well-being during the COVID-19 pandemic [65], as well as between collective self-esteem and emotional distress [66].

The results obtained in our research also confirm hypothesis 6 (Self-esteem mediates the relationship between social support and anxiety, on the one hand, and between family functioning and anxiety, on the other). Young university students who perceive a high level of social support and have a functional family dynamic tend to develop a more stable self-esteem, which could be linked to lower levels of anxiety. The mediation of the self-esteem variable has been corroborated in previous studies. On the one hand, in longitudinal studies with Chinese adolescents, it was observed that family dysfunction predicts significantly higher levels of anxiety, this relationship being partially mediated by self-esteem [67, 68]. On the other hand, the scientific evidence is not entirely uniform. In a study of high school adolescents, although it was found that the family environment positively predicts self-esteem, it did not mediate the relationship between the characteristics of the family environment in relation to social anxiety [69]. In the Turkish university population, self-esteem has been found to partially mediate the relationship between perceived social support and affective experiences, both positive and negative, supporting its role as a bridge between the social environment and overall emotional well-being [70].

Our findings supported hypothesis 7 (Self-esteem mediates the relationship between SOC and anxiety). In our model, the analyses show that a high SOC could contribute to higher self-esteem, and this, in turn, is associated with a possible decrease in anxiety levels in young university students. This model supports the interdependence between these constructs and their role in promoting the psychological well-being of young university students and highlights the relevance of intervening not only on contextual variables but also on internal resources. Although we have not found previous studies that directly analyze this mediation, the available literature on the bivariate relationships between these variables supports the hypothesis that both SOC and self-esteem are related to anxiety [17, 26, 30]. These studies suggest that self-esteem could act as a mediating factor in this relationship.

Finally, our findings suggest that social support is directly related to anxiety, albeit weakly, whereas family functioning does not show a direct effect. This pattern could reflect that social support provides an immediately accessible buffer against students’ emotional stress, while the influence of family functioning appears to operate indirectly through psychological resources such as SOC and self-esteem [15, 60].

The trajectory analyses carried out in our research reinforce the evidence that the relationship between personal (SOC, self-esteem) and social factors (social support and Family functioning) and the anxiety of young university students does not follow a direct and simple relationship, but is influenced by various factors [13, 41]. The importance of young people’s mental health has been highlighted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, as the pandemic has generated an increase in the prevalence of anxiety, depression and stress, especially in the young population [71, 72]. This phenomenon not only shows the direct consequences of the mental health emergency, but also the indirect repercussions linked to social isolation and alterations in daily routines, among others. A possible explanation for this could be due to the Increased use of digital technologies that has raised significant concerns about their impact on mental health, especially in young people. The excessive use of electronic devices and digital platforms has been associated with a significant increase in cases of anxiety, depression and other psychological conditions [73, 74]. In today’s digital society, young people are exposed for long periods to social media, which can intensify feelings of isolation and low self-esteem, negatively impacting their anxiety levels [74, 75].

Limitations

Our study presents the Limitation of the type of cross-sectional design that does not allow establishing causal relationships between the variables. However, this Limitation is partly mitigated by trajectory analysis, which allows hypotheses to be developed about the possible causal relationships and the direction of the effect between the variables studied. For future research, it would be pertinent to investigate the influence of SOC in relation to other variables linked to anxiety, such as academic anxiety. Additionally, it would be beneficial to expand the sample to other population groups, not just college students. In addition, it would be interesting to carry out longitudinal research that allows investigating these relationships between the variables. It should also be noted that the data were collected during the 2021/2022 academic year, a period still affected by the COVID-19 pandemic in Spain. Although the strictest restrictions had been lifted, universities maintained certain preventive measures, and students continued to experience residual academic, social, and psychological impacts of the pandemic. These contextual factors may have influenced the levels of the variables observed in the study, as well as the associations between them. Consequently, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings to post-pandemic conditions or other populations. Finally, a further limitation is that the equivalence of the path model across gender was not tested, so potential differences in the structural relationships between women and men cannot be fully determined.

Conclusion

Despite the aforementioned limitation, we can conclude that: (1) a high level of perceived social support is associated with lower levels of anxiety, higher self-esteem, and a high SOC; (2) a family functioning is positively associated with self-esteem and SOC; (3) A high level of SOC is associated with lower levels of anxiety and higher self-esteem; (4) SOC mediates the relationship between social support and anxiety; (5) both social support and family functioning affect self-esteem through SOC; (6) self-esteem mediates the relationships between social support and family functioning in anxiety levels and; (7) self-esteem mediates the relationship between SOC and anxiety levels in young university students.
