Background

Social drivers of health (SDOH), such as housing stability, food security and access to transportation, profoundly influence both healthcare access and health outcomes. In pregnancy, screening positively for SDOH domains correlates with poorer perinatal outcomes.1 In response to these findings, the American college of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists recommends screening for social and structural drivers of health at every routine prenatal visit, coupled with maximising referrals to social services.2

Despite the impact of SDOH on perinatal outcomes and recommendations to screen, research remains limited regarding effective implementation strategies to integrate SDOH screening into routine prenatal care. Healthcare systems face numerous challenges in systematically implementing SDOH screening, including a lack of standardisation of validated SDOH screening questions, workflow integration challenges, limited staff capacity and the need for reliable processes for managing positive screens. Recent research has also identified patient-centred implementation considerations, for example, implementing screening only when robust referral pathways are available, timing screening early in care (ideally during intake), ensuring screening approaches do not feel targeted based on patient identity, and making resources available proactively.3,5 This underscores the importance of implementation approaches that balance healthcare system priorities and resources with patient needs and expectations.

Subsequently, many prenatal practices struggle to screen patients for SDOH, with studies indicating that fewer than half are conducting screenings systematically.4 6 Having similar struggles, prior to December 2024, our institution also did not conduct SDOH screenings in a systematic way during prenatal care and failed to universally screen. This fragmented approach creates potential gaps in identifying and addressing social needs during pregnancy, a particularly vulnerable time when interventions to address SDOH could significantly impact maternal and infant outcomes.

Methods/design

This study is a multi-site, prospective formative implementation evaluation of a standardised SDOH screening and related follow-up protocol during prenatal care for an academic Obstetrics and Gynaecology (OB-GYN) department in North Carolina (NC), as part of an ongoing quality improvement (QI) initiative.7 This QI initiative developed out of a multidisciplinary workgroup within the department dedicated to improving the prenatal intake process. The goal of this QI initiative is: (1) All new prenatal patients are screened for seven targeted SDOH domains (eg, tobacco use, substance use, food insecurity, housing insecurity, transportation, depression and interpersonal violence (IPV)) before and/or during their first appointment and (2) All prenatal patients with positive screens are offered referral to social work staff and/or appropriate resources.

The decision to initiate implementation of SDOH screening and management with these two features as the core of our integration approach stems from important work that explored facilitators and barriers to SDOH screening and referral in prenatal care, as well as perspectives on optimal implementation approaches, with prenatal care providers and patients.5 8 Umstead et al and Tully et al found that both patients and providers believe screening should be implemented only when clinics have established protocols for addressing needs and can provide high-quality resource referrals.5 8 Their work also indicated that patients prefer screening to occur early in care, ideally during the intake process or initial prenatal visit, with resource information made available before the first appointment when possible.5 8

This study will employ a concurrent triangulation mixed-methods approach, integrating chart-abstracted patient data, staff surveys and staff and patient semi-structured interviews, to assess the implementation process and outcomes. Our approach is guided by the exploration, preparation, implementation and sustainment (EPIS) framework as our overarching structure.9 Within this EPIS structure, we will use the consolidated framework for implementation research (CFIR) and the implementation outcomes framework (IOF) as complementary frameworks to guide our evaluation of implementation determinants and outcomes.10 11

Study timeline

The total timeline of the study is estimated to be about 24 months, starting with the beginning of the preparation phase through early sustainment phase.

Preparation phase

The preparation phase will encompass the 12-month period preceding implementation (November 2023–November 2024). During this critical preparatory stage, the research team will focus on establishing foundational elements of the QI initiative. Key activities will include forming the implementation team, conducting meetings with clinical stakeholders and leadership, and developing an implementation blueprint. Concurrently, pre-implementation baseline clinical metrics will be established. Specifically, chart abstraction will be performed to identify baseline rates of SDOH screening and positive screen referrals across all identified domains. This baseline data collection period will be conducted from September through November 2024.

Implementation phase

The implementation phase will be defined as the first 9 months of implementation, spanning from December 2024 to September 2025. This phase represents the initial phase of SDOH screening rollout with a dedicated focus on the execution of the SDOH screening and management protocol, along with supporting implementation strategies. The implementation phase will be subdivided into a pilot phase from December 2024 to February 2025 and full implementation from March 2025 onward.

Sustainment phase

The sustainment phase is structured to cover months 9–12 following the start of implementation, specifically from October through December 2025. This phase is designed to assess the long-term viability of the SDOH screening protocols with scaled-back support from the implementation team.

Implementation setting and context

Implementation of SDOH screening into prenatal care will occur at the four primary prenatal clinic sites within the OB-GYN department of our academic medical system, including two general OB-GYN clinics and two maternal-fetal medicine (MFM) speciality clinics, distributed across urban and suburban locations. The entire medical system uses the EPIC electronic medical record (EMR) (Epic Systems, Verona, WI). The study population will include all new prenatal patients and the clinical staff in the included clinics.

Prior to our project’s implementation, universal screening in our prenatal care practices is typically limited to substance use, IPV and mood screening. Similar to other clinics in NC, for medicaid beneficiaries, we administer the paper NC medicaid pregnancy risk screening form, which includes SDOH screening questions about pregnancy intendedness, IPV, food insecurity, safe housing, tobacco and other substance use and mood.12 These forms are then faxed to the patient’s local health department to determine their eligibility for care coordination services during pregnancy.13 However, once faxed, the forms’ responses often become inaccessible to providers as they do not match the SDOH questions in our EMR and thus cannot be easily entered.

The prenatal clinical workflow of our clinics includes these standardised elements: (1) A new-to-nurse telephone visit for all patients seeking new prenatal care or self-referred transfers of care prior to their first prenatal appointment (if a referred transfer of prenatal care, no nurse-to-nurse call occurs and instead incoming record abstraction by nursing staff occurs) and (2) In-person initial prenatal visits where medical assistants (MAs)/nurses roomed patients and conduct initial screenings and history collection, followed by provider examination. The new-to-nurse EPIC template includes questions about IPV, tobacco use and substance use, but the questions and responses are difficult to find within EPIC and differ from the questions in the EPIC SDOH tab that are more widely used by other providers.

Standardised screening at the initial in-person prenatal visit includes depression screening with the Edinburgh postnatal depression scale (EPDS), as well as the SDOH questions on the pregnancy risk screening form if the patient uses NC medicaid.

Our organisational environment exhibits several key readiness components, including motivation, a supportive climate, capacity and compatibility.14 Before starting our work, staff feedback survey data collected by the prenatal intake process work group indicated an interest in improving SDOH screening during the prenatal intake process. Departmental leadership shows support for the initiative. An existing system-wide QI collaborative, called the women’s+ health improvement collaborative (W+HIC), provides an infrastructure for capacity building to support change. The already established prenatal intake process workflow, which includes administering other questionnaires, offers a foundation for integrating this additional screening. This combination of staff motivation, leadership support, system-level capacity for change and existing workflow compatibility places the department in a favourable position for successful implementation of the proposed SDOH screening and management protocol.

While a formal assessment of barriers and facilitators will occur as part of this evaluation (as outlined below), several anticipated barriers and facilitators were identified through meetings with departmental stakeholders and previously collected staff survey data from the prenatal intake process work group. These findings will be incorporated into our implementation blueprint and strategies. These barriers include: (1) Visit time constraints, (2) Lack of standardisation on whose role it is to screen for SDOH, (3) No standardised documentation practices for SDOH screening responses, (4) Limited guidance for providers on how to address positive responses to SDOH screening and (5) Underutilisation of existing EPIC SDOH functionality, including the SDOH tab, wheel, and/or myChart questionnaire. These gaps underscore the need for workflow integration, allowing us to maintain the efficiency of current processes while expanding our screening capabilities.

Implementation team

The implementation team consists of key stakeholders, including physician champions with experience in QI and implementation science, nursing leadership, social work representatives and EPIC specialists. Representatives from both general OB/GYN and MFM clinics serve on the team to ensure site-specific needs are addressed. Clinical leadership engagement occurs through meetings and communication with the W+HIC. The implementation team will meet weekly to prepare and create implementation blueprints, review progress, discuss adaptations needed and address implementation barriers identified through ongoing data collection and stakeholder feedback.

Participant recruitment, consent and study oversight

For surveys and interviews, participants will be recruited using a multi-faceted approach. To recruit prenatal patients for semi-structured interviews, we will use in-office flyers and handouts with QR codes to sign up for an interview and provide study personnel contact information. Clinical staff will be recruited for surveys and semi-structured interviews through internal communications and staff meetings. We will specifically prompt staff to take the survey at the time of the educational sessions about the SDOH screening initiative. Consent will be embedded into the staff survey, and verbal informed consent will be obtained from all participants undergoing semi-structured interviews. This study was reviewed and approved by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill’s Institutional Review Board (IRB #24-3104).

SDOH screening tool(s) and processes

Our screening and management protocol will use EPIC’s validated SDOH screening questionnaires with two key modifications to facilitate more specific perinatal screening.16 First, while EPIC allows for either the EPDS or patient health questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2)/PHQ-9, we will instruct clinics to use the EPDS given its more common use in perinatal spaces and its ability to pick up both anxiety and depression.17 Second, we will work to replace the standard national institute on drug abuse quick screen with the 5Ps substance use screener, which is currently part of the NC medicaid pregnancy risk screening form and demonstrates higher sensitivity in perinatal populations.18

We are also leveraging existing EMR and clinic workflows by implementing an automated pre-new-to-nurse visit myChart SDOH questionnaire and updating the new-to-nurse tele-visit templates so that they now will use the standardised questions found in the EPIC SDOH tab (table 1). Every new prenatal patient will be advised to sign up for myChart when they schedule the new-to-nurse phone visit. They will then get an email and/or text message request to complete an SDOH myChart questionnaire 1 week prior to their visit for the following SDOH domains: (1) Financial resource strain, (2) Food insecurity, (3) Housing/utilities, (4) Transportation needs, (5) Tobacco use and (6) Alcohol and substance use. During the new-to-nurse visit, nursing staff will ensure these questions are completed, and if not, they will be prompted to ask these questions during the call.

Mood and IPV will be screened when the patient presents in person for their new Obstetric visit. The MAs/nurses will ask these sensitive questions after the patient is roomed. The responses will be made available to the provider prior to seeing the patient. We decided to ask the screening questions for IPV in person based on patient and stakeholder feedback from Michigan’s MiPATH programme, which noted these questions should not be asked via telehealth because such visits may not provide sufficient privacy for patients to answer safely and appropriately.4 In addition, for both the IPV and mood questions, our team had concerns about ensuring that positive responses were addressed immediately by a responsible provider.

To ensure data consistency, staff will use identical question phrasing across all modalities that are aligned with the EPIC SDOH questionnaire. While some overlap exists between SDOH and traditional social history documentation, we will prioritise recording responses within the dedicated SDOH tab when applicable and will teach staff to do so in our educational sessions. This standardised documentation approach enhances both clinical efficiency during visits and facilitates implementation tracking and research analysis.

To ensure standardised management of positive SDOH screens, we will educate and instruct all staff, starting with nurses doing the new-to-nurse visits, to offer a referral to social work to those with a positive screen in any of the SDOH domains. If the referral is accepted, staff will then alert social work of the referral via EPIC chart routing. Social work will then be responsible for providing further needed support, referrals and/or connections to community resources. If someone does not desire a social work referral, staff can also offer a list of domain-specific resources sent via EPIC myChart. Staff will have access to these resource lists via pre-existing system EPIC SmartPhrases and staff will be instructed on how to find and add to their personal EPIC SmartPhrase lists during the educational sessions and in the educational materials.

Additionally, we will educate nurses conducting the new-to-nurse visits to use a standardised pre-populated EPIC SmartList in the new visit template to document positive screens and the outcome of offers of referral and/or resources. We will also educate providers to standardly review SDOH documentation, including whether positive screens were addressed, during the new prenatal visit and document any positive screens in the EPIC problem list tab, which is used by providers as part of our prenatal care workflow and communication. This tab will be used to ensure easier recognition of an SDOH positive screen and if it has been properly addressed across all prenatal providers and visits.

Outcome measures

Implementation outcomes

We will evaluate implementation outcomes as defined by Proctor et al’s IOF using a mixed-method approach.11 Adoption and fidelity will be measured through chart abstraction of 20% of all eligible new prenatal patients at each clinic site, with data securely entered and stored in REDCap@UNC to maintain confidentiality. Adoption will be measured by the proportion of eligible patients who receive screening across all seven of our targeted SDOH domains. Fidelity will be measured by adherence to the entire SDOH screening and management protocol, including SDOH screening of all domains, using correct SDOH questions, appropriate documentation of SDOH screening results in the EPIC SDOH tab, offer of referral to social work or resources for positive screens, and placement of social work referrals when accepted. We will also track instances where only partial adherence to protocol was accomplished, such as cases where only partial screening was performed, or the patient was screened but not with the recommended questionnaire tools.

Acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility will be evaluated using an anonymous 11-question Likert-type survey administered to clinical office staff and providers. The survey will inquire about the respondents’ staff roles and their respective clinic locations. This survey incorporates validated feasibility, acceptability and appropriateness measures and will be administered at two time points—during early implementation and in the sustainment phase during staff meetings and educational sessions about the SDOH screening.19 20

The survey will be offered twice to cross-sectionally assess staff attitude changes over time as the implementation plan is deployed and staff have more hands-on experience with it.20 If, during the implementation phase, any adaptations are made that would affect the relevance of questions asked on the survey, then the survey will be adapted while maintaining the validated portion of the questions. Surveys will be offered both online via Qualtrics and in paper format to maximise response rates. Acceptability and appropriateness of SDOH screening and referral during prenatal care will also be evaluated in greater depth through qualitative interviews of both office staff and patients.

Sustainability will be assessed by comparing screening rates and proper management of positive screens across different implementation time points and through qualitative interviews with staff members, where we will elicit feedback on what might be necessary for long-term sustainment.

Determinant outcomes

Implementation determinants will also be assessed through a mixed methods approach guided by the CFIR.10 Barriers and facilitators will be identified through qualitative interviews with clinic staff and patients, as well as through elicited feedback in staff, division and departmental leadership meetings that will be attended by implementation team members. Additionally, interviews will focus on more in-depth feedback regarding the perceived effectiveness of our chosen screening and management protocol, as well as implementation strategies.

The need for adaptations to the SDOH screening and management process or applied implementation strategies will be decided by the implementation team consensus through the evaluation of survey and semi-structured interview data and meetings with clinical stakeholders. If adaptations are instituted, they will be systematically documented throughout the study period, to better help inform sustainability and future scale-up into other prenatal clinic settings. To reflect the iterative and adaptive nature of the implementation process, survey and interview guides may also require modification to reflect changes to implementation, which will also be systematically documented and addressed in the analysis process as discussed below.

To ensure reliable data collection, study team members doing chart abstraction will complete a subset of patient charts together to form a consensus on the best abstraction methods to improve inter-rater reliability. All qualitative interviews will be conducted using semi-structured interview guides, with separate versions for staff and patients, and facilitated by trained study team members. Additionally, interviewers of patient participants will not have the potential to clinically interact with them during their prenatal care. For staff participants, interviewers will not hold a direct supervisory role to ensure unbiased feedback. Sample size

An a priori power calculation was not conducted, given this study’s formative implementation evaluation design, rather than effectiveness testing, and inability to control recruitment. However, for the qualitative interviews, we anticipate that we will need to perform up to 30 interviews (up to 15 from staff and up to 15 from patients) based on established qualitative research principles for achieving theoretical saturation within each group.21

Analysis plan

Chart-abstracted patient and implementation data analysis

The analysis of the study will include several components, starting with a descriptive analysis of the characteristics of patients served at each individual clinic site. The characteristics will include age, parity, race, ethnicity, insurance status, preferred language, gestational age at first prenatal visit and whether the patient was a transfer of care.

For clinical quality metrics, descriptive statistics will be used to quantify the number of SDOH screenings conducted pre-implementation, during and in the sustainment phase. We will also quantify the number of appropriate offers for referral to social work and/or for related resources. Each SDOH domain will be reported separately to also understand differences in screening among different domains. Additionally, we will describe the prevalence of positive screens in our population and provide detailed characteristics of those who screened positive. A multi-level logistic regression analysis will be performed to handle the nested data structure (multiple clinics) with dichotomous outcomes (screened Y/N and offered referral and/or resources Y/N). This analysis will compare data from pre-implementation, during implementation (analysed by month), and the sustainment phase. This longitudinal analysis will ensure understanding of improvements over time, as well as help analyse the effects of any implementation adaptations.

Survey data analysis

Survey data will be analysed both descriptively and visually, focusing on staff assessments of the implementation outcomes at two different measurement points. To account for the nested structure of the data (multiple clinics), multi-level regression analysis will be conducted with clinic as a random effect and staff role (nurse, physician, etc) as a fixed effect. Time point will be included as a between-groups factor to examine changes between measurement periods, with data treated as independent cross-sectional samples rather than repeated measures, given the anonymous nature of the survey administration. All the Likert-type, ordinal survey variables will be included in the models. If any survey questions require changing to reflect updated implementation strategies, such changes and implications for interpreting analyses will be noted, though the analysis will remain the same. All quantitative statistical analyses will be performed using StataNow V.18.5 BE software (Stata, College Station, Texas).

Semi-structured interviews qualitative analysis

A thematic analysis will be performed and guided by the CFIR to identify determinants influencing implementation and to understand feedback for improving the implementation strategies.10 To conduct this thematic analysis, a codebook will be created through team meetings and consensus building.22 The codebook will include both a priori and inductive codes guided by the CFIR.10 23 24 Once the codebook is created, 10% of interviews will be coded by all study team members who are participating in the coding process, and consensus will be formed on how to apply the codes to improve consistency and accuracy. The remaining interviews will then be coded by one study team member. Once interviews are coded, study team members will analyse the data and determine emerging themes through group consensus. The study team will then also form a consensus on whether any adaptation is needed to current implementation practices. Qualitative coding and analysis will be performed in Atlas.ti software.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not directly involved in the initial development of the research questions or design of this implementation evaluation study. However, our implementation approach was informed by prior research that explored patient perspectives on optimal implementation approaches for SDOH screening in prenatal care.3 5 8

For the current study, patients will be actively involved during the implementation and evaluation phases through semi-structured interviews. These interviews will elicit patient perspectives on our SDOH screening approach and management process. Patient feedback will be used to inform potential adaptations to the implementation strategies during the study period. Additionally, findings from patient interviews will be incorporated into our dissemination materials to ensure their perspectives are represented in the study results.

Ethics approval

This study involves human participants and was approved by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Institutional Review Board (ID#24-3104). Consent is obtained for survey and qualitative interview data. Chart abstracted data will become deidentified and does not require individual patient consent per the IRB.

Dissemination of results

Study results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. Findings will also be shared with staff in participating clinics throughout the process, as described in our implementation strategies, to encourage continued engagement and improvement.

Study’s current status

The study is currently in the implementation phase, having completed the preparation phase activities and the pilot phase (December 2024–February 2025).

Discussion

This study aims to bridge the gap between the recommendations for universal SDOH screening and management in prenatal care and its actual integration by evaluating strategies that can serve as a guide for other prenatal care clinics.

Our approach offers several key strengths that enhance its potential to positively impact both our organisation’s implementation process and other future efforts. By using a structured approach with established frameworks for the planning, evaluation and adaptation of implementation, we are enhancing the potential for reproducibility of our implementation process. Additionally, integrating the EPIC EMR infrastructure as part of our implementation strategy promotes the sustainability of the SDOH screening by embedding it within familiar workflows, which may decrease its burden on staff. EPIC is the most commonly used EMR in the US, allowing for the potential of our strategies to again be more easily applied to other health systems.25 By documenting adaptations, we hope to better hypothesise how this applied protocol and these implementation strategies may be adapted to different clinical contexts. In addition, our evaluation of both implementation determinants and outcomes will help identify which elements of our approach are most crucial for successful adoption and sustainability to help others anticipate possible challenges and proactively develop solutions. Lastly, standardised documentation within the EMR could support future research on the relationships between SDOH and interventions addressing SDOH with perinatal outcomes. It may also enable more effective resource allocation and care coordination.

While many implementation studies focus on provider and staff experiences as the end-users of implementation work in healthcare, our mixed methods design incorporates patient feedback through semi-structured interviews, adding to the strengths of our study design. By gathering insights from both patients and providers, we can better understand potential barriers to screening adoption and develop strategies to address them. Patient input is also particularly crucial for understanding how SDOH screening and management can be implemented in a way that is patient-centred, optimised to encourage patient participation, and is considered beneficial to patients.

While our study design aligns with many implementation science best practices, several study limitations exist. First, our protocol may result in lower screening rates for transfer patients, who do not receive the new-to-nurse call central to our screening approach. This may disproportionately affect MFM clinics, which have higher transfer patient volumes. We will monitor this during implementation and explore adaptations as needed. Second, while our multi-site approach within a single academic system may offer relevance for other academic medical centres and other similarly structured health systems, this study design may not capture all the challenges faced by smaller, resource-limited settings without available social work resources or those without similar EMR infrastructure and thus cannot be generalised to all prenatal care settings. However, we hope that our detailed documentation of implementation strategies and their outcomes will allow other clinics to select and adapt elements that will best fit their specific context.

Additionally, while our mixed-methods approach provides rich data, the relatively short sustainment phase (3 months) may not fully capture long-term sustained uptake of SDOH screening and management. Our focus on implementation outcomes rather than clinical outcomes and lack of randomisation means we cannot draw conclusions about the impact of SDOH screening and management on maternal health outcomes or make causal inferences about the effectiveness of our specific implementation strategies. Furthermore, our bundled implementation approach limits our ability to isolate the individual effects of specific implementation strategies, though our qualitative data will provide insights into which strategies stakeholders perceive as most valuable. Since we are not evaluating effectiveness, we are limited in analysing cost-effectiveness as one of our implementation outcomes.
