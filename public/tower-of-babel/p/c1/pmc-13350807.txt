A deep learning-based multimodal model with automated body composition analysis predicts prognosis in advanced clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Youwei Chi, Hanzheng Wang, Qingsong Zou, Fengze Sun, Chunhai Hu, Chu Liu, Qian Li, Bin Yang, Jitao Wu, Yongwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is an aggressive malignancy with a high risk of postoperative recurrence. Body composition has emerged as a prognostic marker, but its clinical use is hindered by manual measurement methods and poorly interpretable models. This study aimed to develop an automated, interpretable prediction model integrating deep learning-based body composition analysis to assess prognosis and explore underlying mechanisms in advanced ccRCC. This retrospective multicenter study included patients who underwent radical nephrectomy for advanced ccRCC. Body composition was quantified automatically from preoperative CT using a deep learning model (Comp2Comp). These metrics were integrated with clinicopathological features to build machine learning models for predicting 5-year survival and recurrence. Model performance was rigorously evaluated in an independent external cohort. To explore the biological basis of key imaging features, transcriptomic analysis and experimental validation were performed. Metrics for calibration and discrimination were used to compare the models. The multimodal model demonstrated robust predictive performance. The MLP model excelled in 5-year survival prediction (AUC 0.787 internal, 0.812 external), while the SVC model was optimal for recurrence (AUC 0.740 internal, 0.815 external). SHAP analysis identified visceral (VAT) and subcutaneous (SAT) adipose tissue as top predictive features. Subsequent transcriptomic analysis linked VAT to metabolic pathways and SAT to embryonic development programs. Protein expression of key associated genes was confirmed by immunohistochemistry. This study developed an automated multimodal prediction model by integrating deep learning–derived body composition metrics with clinicopathological features, demonstrating promising predictive performance in assessing survival and recurrence risks in advanced ccRCC, which was further supported by a preliminary external validation cohort. SHAP analysis identified visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue as key predictors. Transcriptomic analysis linked VAT to pathways of mature renal function and SAT to embryonic developmental programs, providing initial insights into potential biological correlates of these imaging biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46515-9.

Introduction

Despite advances in cancer treatment, renal cell carcinoma (RCC) remains one of the most common urinary malignancies1. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the dominant subtype, accounts for about 70% of cases2. Although surgical resection serves as the standard treatment for localized ccRCC, even following complete resection, up to 30% of patients still encounter local recurrence or distant metastasis during the follow-up period3. Beyond traditional staging and grading, growing evidence shows that body composition significantly influences ccRCC recurrence and prognosis4–6.

Recent advances in radiomics and multimodal AI have improved prognostic prediction in RCC by combining imaging features, clinicopathologic data, and machine learning6–10. However, many models rely on thousands of abstract radiomic features—limiting interpretability and clinical adoption11–13. The prognostic role of body composition—especially visceral (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT)—remains controversial: some studies link high VAT to worse outcomes, while others report neutral or even protective effects. These inconsistencies may stem from differences in measurement methods (e.g., BMI vs. CT-based quantification), patient selection, disease stage, or cancer-related cachexia. Established RCC nomograms achieve c-indices of 0.72–0.85 in external validation14,15 but depend heavily on postoperative pathology and omit standardized, CT-derived body composition metrics.

To address the gap between predictive performance and clinical interpretability, we developed an automated and biologically transparent multimodal model integrating CT-based body composition parameters—quantified using the Comp2Comp deep learning framework—with clinicopathologic data. Using SHAP analysis and transcriptomic validation, we probed the biological mechanisms underlying VAT/SAT-associated risk. The model’s robustness and generalizability were confirmed in an independent external cohort.

Methods

Ethics and registration

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital and the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Jinan Central Hospital, which waived the requirement for informed consent because of the retrospective study design. This study was a retrospective cohort study and was reported in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study protocol was not prepared, as this was a retrospective analysis of existing clinical data.

Patients and data

This retrospective study included patients who underwent surgery at two centers from January 2017 to December 2021 and were histopathologically confirmed to have advanced ccRCC, including 311 from Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital and 91 from Jinan Central Hospital.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) postoperative pathological diagnosis of advanced ccRCC; (2) underwent radical or partial nephrectomy; (3) available preoperative abdominal CT images; complete clinicopathological and follow-up data. Patients with incomplete follow-up (i.e., lost to follow-up before the study endpoint or with missing outcome ascertainment) were excluded to ensure data integrity.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) neoadjuvant therapy or history of other malignancies; (2) distant metastasis on preoperative imaging; (3) CT-surgery interval > 3 months; (4) perioperative death (survival ≤ 30 days); (5) failure of automatic segmentation due to poor CT image quality.

All included patients were followed for a minimum of 5 years after surgery or until death, with follow-up data completely ascertained for the 5-year endpoint. Patients with incomplete follow-up were excluded per the inclusion criteria.

In addition, this study obtained an independent cohort of ccRCC patients (n = 164) from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA). This cohort simultaneously possessed preoperative abdominal CT images and corresponding tumor transcriptome sequencing data, which could be used for subsequent mechanism exploration and analysis.

Outcomes

Two binary outcomes were defined: 5-year survival and postoperative recurrence. 5-year survival was defined as survival status 5 years after surgery (alive vs. deceased). Recurrence was defined as local, regional, or distant disease relapse confirmed by imaging (RECIST 1.1) and/or histopathology. To ensure consistency in endpoint adjudication, all recurrence events were independently reviewed by two oncologists. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Comp2Comp automated CT body composition analysis

This study used a deep learning model based on the open-source toolkit Comp2Comp (v0.1.0, https://github.com/StanfordMIMI/Comp2Comp) to automate quantitative body composition analysis. Developed by the Stanford University Medical Imaging and Machine Intelligence Laboratory (Stanford MIMI Lab), Comp2Comp has been trained on diverse medical image datasets and achieves stable segmentation performance at the L2-L4 vertebral levels (Dice coefficient > 92%). It enabled accurate, fully automated anatomical labeling of CT slices (Figure S1).

To validate segmentation accuracy in our cohort, 55 CT scans were randomly selected from the two centers and the TCIA cohort, and manually segmented by two radiologists blinded to clinical outcomes. Agreement between automated and manual measurements was excellent: ICC (2,1) values were 0.991 for SM, 0.997 for VAT, 0.993 for SAT, and 0.986 for IMAT. Bland-Altman analysis showed minimal bias, confirming the robustness of the automated segmentation pipeline (Figure S2).

In addition, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), lymphocyte-to-leukocyte ratio (LLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were calculated. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was defined as the albumin level (g/L) plus five times the absolute lymphocyte count. Furthermore, the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated by multiplying the absolute platelet count by the NLR16,17.

Feature selection

A total of 311 patients from Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital were randomly divided into an internal training set (n = 218) and an internal test set (n = 93) at a 7:3 ratio, while 91 patients from Jinan Central Hospital were used as an independent external validation set. For sensitivity analysis, we restricted the cohort to patients treated between 2018 and 2020, imputed missing data using multiple imputation, and repeated the 7:3 split and model development pipeline to compare performance with the primary analysis.

Feature selection was performed using a nested cross-validation framework. Five-fold outer cross-validation was used to evaluate model performance, and within each outer fold, five-fold inner cross-validation was employed for feature selection and hyperparameter optimization. Recursive feature elimination combined with cross-validation (RFECV) was conducted with a step size of 1, using AUC as the scoring metric. To ensure reproducibility, random seeds were fixed: outer StratifiedKFold seed = 42, inner StratifiedKFold seed = 24, with RFECV inheriting the inner seed. Features selected in more than 40% of the inner folds were retained. Based on clinical prior knowledge, age and gender were retained a priori in the feature set. Collinearity was assessed using VIF (< 10) and Spearman’s correlation (|r|<0.7) (Figure S3).

Model construction and validation.

Model development and hyperparameter tuning Six machine-learning models were built to predict 5-year survival and recurrence: Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Support Vector Classifier (SVC), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP).

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using grid search (for ≤ 4 parameters) or randomized search with 50 iterations. The complete hyperparameter search grids for all six models are provided in Supplementary Material to ensure full transparency and reproducibility. All searches employed 5-fold stratified cross-validation with ROC-AUC as the scoring metric. To address class imbalance (positive class: 31.3%), model-specific class weight parameters (e.g., class_weight, scale_pos_weight) were included in the search grids. Hyperparameter random search employed fold-specific seeds (42 + fold index) to balance randomness and reproducibility.

Model performance was evaluated on both internal and external validation sets. Metrics included the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), area under the precision-recall curve (PRC-AUC), sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), and Calibration Curve (CC), with AUC serving as the primary metric for model comparison. Furthermore, SHAP analysis was applied to the best-performing models to interpret the contribution of key features.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients treated between 2018 and 2020—a period during which clinical practices, imaging protocols, and follow-up procedures remained consistent at both centers, minimizing potential temporal biases. The model development pipeline (same 7:3 split, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning) was repeated on this subset.

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the TRIPOD guidelines.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using Metascape (https://metascape.org/), with p < 0.01 as the cutoff criterion. The top 10 GO items and top 20 KEGG pathways that meet this criterion were visualized using OmicShare (http://www.omicshare.com/) and microscopic letter (http://www.bioinformatics.com. cn)18–20.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, the t-test or rank-sum test was used to evaluate the differences between patients in the training set and the validation set. For categorical variables, the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test were used to assess the differences. Spearman’s correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the relationships between features. Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis were completed in R (3.8.3). The model construction was implemented using Python 3.9, with the main dependent libraries including scikit-learn (1.0.2), pandas (1.4.0), and NumPy (1.22.0).

Biological interpretability

Considering the significance of VAT and SAT in the prediction model during SHAP analysis, we categorized the TCIA cohort into high- and low-value groups based on their median values and carried out differential expression analysis (|log2FC| > 1, adjusted P-value < 0.05). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were then conducted on the obtained differentially expressed genes. Subsequently, the top two genes with the most significant results in the enrichment analysis were selected, and the differences in their protein expression were verified via immunohistochemistry using 6 pairs of independent tissue samples from our hospital.

Feature selection

Feature screening was performed using RFECV with 5-fold nested cross-validation. Features selected in ≥ 40% of outer folds were retained, with age and gender included a priori. Collinearity was assessed using VIF (< 10) and Spearman’s correlation (|r|<0.7). For 5-year survival prediction, 16 stable features were identified, with PNI, Urea/Cr, SII, and SAT selected in all folds. For recurrence prediction, 14 stable features were finalized, with PNI, SAT, Hgb, SM, IMAT, Urea/Cr, tumor size, and VAT selected in all folds. No severe multicollinearity was detected among selected features.

Threshold selection

To determine the optimal decision threshold when applying the model in clinical practice, we systematically evaluated the performance of the model under different thresholds on the test set and the external validation set (Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5). Given that missing a high-risk patient in advanced ccRCC could delay potentially life-saving intervention, we prioritized AUC along with recall in threshold selection. After comprehensively weighing recall, precision, F1-score, and clinical net benefit, we finally determined 0.35 as the unified decision threshold for predicting the 5-year survival rate and recurrence risk. We plotted the calibration curve and the DCA curve to evaluate the performance (Figures S4–5). The AUC and PRC values of the different models are presented (Fig. 2).

For recurrence prediction, the SVC model showed the best overall trade-off, with an AUC of 0.740 in the test set and 0.815 in the external validation set (Fig. 4). The F1-score was 0.578 (test) and 0.685 (external) at threshold 0.35. In addition, we compared the performance of different models when the threshold was 0.3 or 0.4 (Figure S6).

To assess model stability despite differences in F1-score, we examined the PRC, which is sensitive to class imbalance. The PRC-AUC remained stable between cohorts (test: 0.698; external: 0.703), indicating that the model’s underlying discriminative ability is robust, and the observed F1 variation reflects differences in class distribution rather than poor class-balance handling.

To assess the robustness of our primary findings, we repeated the analysis restricted to patients treated between 2018 and 2020 (n = 216), a period with consistent clinical practices. The MLP model for 5‑year survival maintained an AUC of 0.754, recall of 0.778, and F1‑score of 0.583 at the 0.35 threshold. The SVC model for recurrence achieved an AUC of 0.758, recall of 0.800, and F1‑score of 0.552. These metrics were consistent with those of the primary analysis (ΔAUC < 0.04), supporting the robustness of our conclusions.

Model interpretation and biological validation

To clarify the decision-making basis of the optimal models, we performed interpretability analysis using the SHAP framework (Fig. 5). In the MLP model for 5-year survival prediction, both SAT and VAT ranked among the top five features in importance in both the test set and the external validation set, and higher values of these features were associated with a lower predicted risk of death. In the SVC model for recurrence prediction, VAT also emerged as a key feature (ranked 2nd in the test set and 3rd in the validation set), and similarly, higher VAT levels were linked to a lower predicted risk of recurrence.

Based on these findings, we leveraged imaging data from the TCIA cohort and stratified patients into high- and low-level groups according to the median VAT and SAT values, followed by differential expression analysis (Fig. 6). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that VAT-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were primarily enriched in biological processes such as ion transmembrane transport, chemical synaptic signaling regulation, and mature renal epithelial development. In contrast, SAT-associated DEGs were significantly enriched in embryonic kidney development programs (e.g., ureteric bud morphogenesis, nephron morphogenesis) and the Wnt signaling pathway. These results suggest that VAT may influence prognosis by modulating mature cellular metabolism and signal transduction, whereas SAT might be related to the aberrant reactivation of embryonic developmental programs.

Finally, we validated the protein expression levels of the two most significantly differentially expressed genes—CALB1 (most significant in the VAT-based grouping) and HS3ST3A1 (most significant in the SAT-based grouping)—in tissue samples using immunohistochemistry (Figure S7).

We acknowledge that the TCIA cohort represents a potentially valuable resource for external validation. However, this cohort lacks several critical variables integral to our multimodal model—including hematological markers and tumor biomarkers—and therefore could not be used to validate the full model. Consequently, our use of TCIA was limited to transcriptomic exploration of VAT/SAT biology.

Discussion

This study developed an automated multimodal model that integrates deep - learning - derived body composition metrics with clinicopathological data to predict the prognosis of advanced ccRCC. The model exhibited promising performance and showed good generalizability. SHAP analysis identified VAT and SAT as the key predictors, and transcriptomic exploration revealed distinct biological pathways associated with each: mature renal epithelial function for VAT and embryonic developmental programs for SAT, which suggests potential biological correlates of adipose tissue distribution.

Previous studies have established the prognostic value of body composition, such as VAT and SAT, in RCC (e.g., Snyder et al.)4,6,21. However, such work has often relied on manual segmentation, which is subjective and labor-intensive, limiting reproducibility and clinical scalability. Furthermore, while advanced deep learning models (e.g., Zang et al.) can achieve high predictive performance12,22, their “end-to-end” black-box nature obscures the decision-making process, hindering clinical trust and biological interpretation. Our study bridges this gap through three innovations: (1) automated, reproducible body composition quantification via Comp2Comp; (2) a two-tiered interpretability framework that prioritizes clinically intuitive features—specifically, VAT and SAT—rather than pursuing marginal performance gains through high-dimensional variable inclusion. By coupling SHAP analysis with transcriptomic exploration of these key imaging phenotypes, we demonstrate that interpretability can be achieved without sacrificing biological insight or predictive validity; and (3) external validation supporting generalizability.

The apparent divergence in optimal algorithms across endpoints (MLP marginally better for survival, SVC for recurrence) likely reflects minor fluctuations attributable to model variance and endpoint-specific data structure rather than fundamental biological differences. The absolute differences were minimal (ΔAUC < 0.02) with overlapping confidence intervals. Importantly, the consistency of performance across algorithms underscores the robustness of the selected feature set rather than dependence on a specific classifier architecture.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, transcriptomic findings are correlational and require functional validation. Second, the link between imaging phenotypes and molecular processes remains to be causally established. Third, our focus on interpretability via clinically intuitive features (VAT, SAT) likely sacrificed some predictive ceiling achievable with high-dimensional radiomics—a deliberate trade-off justified by the clinical need for transparency. In advanced ccRCC, where treatment decisions carry substantial morbidity, an interpretable model linking predictions to visible anatomy may engender greater clinical trust than a marginally more accurate “black box.” Furthermore, as demonstrated in this study, interpretability enables downstream biological exploration—SHAP-identified features directly guided transcriptomic analysis—transforming the model into a hypothesis-generating platform. Fourth, the external validation cohort (n = 91) remains modest.

Several concrete pathways for future validation are warranted: prospective multi-center studies to confirm generalizability; clinical implementation studies to assess workflow integration, inference time, and failure handling protocols; functional experiments to establish causal roles of CALB1 and HS3ST3A1; and expanded mechanistic exploration using matched adipose and tumor tissue. Finally, implementation research should evaluate clinician acceptance and impact on treatment decisions.

In summary, this study provides an automated, interpretable multimodal model for advanced ccRCC prognosis, with external validation and novel biological insights into the prognostic associations of adipose tissue distribution.

Conclusion

In this study, the body composition of patients with advanced ccRCC after surgery was quantified using the deep learning-based automatic segmentation toolkit (Comp2Comp). By integrating this information with conventional clinicopathological features, a multimodal machine learning prediction model was constructed. This model can effectively evaluate the 5-year survival rate and recurrence risk of patients after radical surgery. It was validated in a multicenter external cohort, demonstrating promising discriminative ability and good generalization performance, as well as good generalization ability. This provides clinicians with an interpretable, individualized prognostic assessment tool based on objective imaging indicators.

In addition, through SHAP interpretability analysis, the core imaging features were identified. Furthermore, by utilizing transcriptomic data, the study identified distinct pathways associated with high and low fat distributions and different biological pathways such as “dysfunction of mature renal epithelial cells” and “reactivation of embryonic kidney development programs” respectively.

These findings provide initial insights into potential molecular correlates underlying the prognostic value of adipose tissue distribution in advanced ccRCC.
