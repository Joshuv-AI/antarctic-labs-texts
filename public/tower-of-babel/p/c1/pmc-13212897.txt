Nonlinear associations between the cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and glucose index and type 2 diabetes risk in Chinese prediabetic adults: a cohort study
Authors: Yuwei Xing, Yishan Yin, Qianqian Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to examine the association between the CHG (cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and glucose) index and future type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) risk in Chinese adults with prediabetes. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 14,192 adults with prediabetes, identified from 2010 to 2016 health examination records. Multivariable Cox models were used to assess associations, with restricted cubic splines exploring nonlinearity. During a mean 3.0‑year follow‑up, 1,748 participants (12.3%) developed diabetes. A nonlinear association was observed, with an inflection point at CHG = 5.22. Above this threshold, higher CHG levels were associated with a significantly lower diabetes risk (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.40–0.75). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant interaction with sex: higher CHG correlated with reduced risk in men but increased risk in women. In conclusion, the CHG index demonstrates a nonlinear, threshold-dependent association with T2DM risk that differs substantially by sex among Chinese prediabetic adults. This composite marker may help refine risk stratification, though sex-specific interpretation is essential. Prospective studies are needed to validate its predictive utility and elucidate underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46867-2.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, characterized by persistent high blood glucose due to impaired insulin secretion or action, represents a major global public health challenge1. The International Diabetes Federation estimated that 537 million adults had diabetes in 2021, a number projected to reach 783 million by 20452. As the prevalence increases, so does the burden of both microvascular (nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy) and macrovascular (cardiovascular disease) complications; these conditions substantially diminish the quality of life and generate considerable costs for healthcare systems globally3. Prediabetes, a transitional state affecting an even larger population, represents a critical and modifiable window for intervention to prevent progression to overt diabetes and its associated complications4.

Although fasting plasma glucose (FPG), oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) underpin routine diagnostic and risk stratification strategies, these established tools exhibit intrinsic limitations. Although convenient, HbA1c can be influenced by ethnicity, anemia, and other hematological conditions, which may compromise its accuracy in detecting early dysglycemia in diverse populations5. Although considered a diagnostic gold standard, the OGTT is cumbersome, poorly reproducible, and has low patient compliance, making it suboptimal for widespread screening6. Consequently, there is a pressing need for stable and accessible biomarkers that more comprehensively reflect the underlying pathophysiological processes that drive diabetes development. Therefore, composite indices derived from routine laboratory parameters have garnered considerable interest as practical risk assessment tools7.

The Cholesterol, High-density lipoprotein, and glucose (CHG) index, calculated as Ln[(total cholesterol × fasting plasma glucose) / (2 × HDL-C)], is a novel integrative marker designed to capture atherogenic lipid burden, reverse cholesterol transport capacity, and glycemic status8. This tripartite design theoretically provides a more comprehensive snapshot of glucolipid metabolic disorders central to insulin resistance and the pathogenesis of diabetes. Preliminary studies on related lipid ratios, such as the triglyceride-to-HDL-C ratio (Triglycerides/HDL-C), support the utility of combined metrics for predicting insulin resistance and diabetes risk9,10. However, despite this promise, the predictive performance, optimal thresholds, and clinical utility of the CHG index remain insufficiently investigated in the high-risk context of the Chinese prediabetic population. Genetic predisposition, lifestyle patterns, and distinct body composition can significantly influence biomarker efficacy, underscoring the need for population-specific validation11. A critical knowledge gap remains: no large-scale, longitudinal study has systematically evaluated the prospective association between the CHG index and incident diabetes in a Chinese prediabetic cohort12.

To fill this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort analysis to examine whether the baseline CHG index is associated with the subsequent onset of type 2 diabetes among a large community-based cohort of Chinese adults with prediabetes. Our specific objectives were to (1) quantify the strength and shape (linear or nonlinear) of this association, (2) identify potential threshold effects, and (3) examine the consistency of the association across key demographic subgroups such as those defined by sex and body mass index. These findings are expected to provide robust evidence on the applicability of the CHG index as a practical risk-stratification tool, thereby informing the development of tailored prevention and early management strategies for diabetes in China and similar populations.

Methods

Data sources

The data used in this study were obtained from the Dryad public repository (www.datadryad.org). Dryad is an open-access platform that facilitates the sharing, unrestricted reuse, and formal citation of research datasets. The original data package was downloaded, and the corresponding dataset was cited as follows: Chen et al., “Association of body mass index and age with incident diabetes among Chinese adults: a population-based cohort study”; Dryad Digital Repository: 10.1136/bmjopen-2018-02176813. Chen et al. deposited the original dataset in the Dryad repository. Under Dryad’s terms of service, conducting secondary analyses to explore alternative hypotheses is permitted, and does not violate the rights of the original investigators. This initial cohort study was approved by the Ruici Medical Group’s Institutional Review Board. Since this study represents a retrospective secondary analysis of anonymized data, it did not require additional ethical clearance. The secondary analysis was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations, and the reporting followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement14.

Study participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis using the Ruici Medical Group electronic registry. The baseline cohort comprised 685,277 Chinese adults aged ≥ 20 years who underwent at least two health examinations between 2010 and 2016 at 32 sites across 11 cities (including Shanghai, Beijing, Guangzhou and Shenzhen). The study protocol was approved by the Ruici Medical Group Institutional Review Board prior to the data collection. After applying the prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1), 671,085 individuals were excluded, yielding a final analytical cohort of 14,192 participants with prediabetes. This final cohort was used to assess the association between baseline CHG index and incident type 2 diabetes.

Study design and measurement of covariatet

A standardized questionnaire was administered to the participants at each visit. This questionnaire documented demographic characteristics (age and sex), lifestyle factors (tobacco and alcohol use), personal medical and medication history, and family history of chronic diseases, including diabetes. Trained staff collected anthropometric data (height and weight) and measured the blood pressure. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Blood pressure was measured using a conventional mercury sphygmomanometer. Tobacco and alcohol use was categorized as current, former, or never.

Fasting venous blood samples were collected at each visit following a fast of at least 10 h. Biochemical assays were performed using a fully automated Beckman 5800 analyzer. These assays included measurements of serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were quantified using the glucose oxidase method.

The baseline CHG index was used as primary exposure. Derived from clinical examinations and laboratory measurements, this variable was entered into the models as a continuous measure. The main outcome was new-onset type 2 diabetes, which was operationalized as the conversion from prediabetes to diabetes. This outcome was treated as a dichotomous endpoint in association analyses.

Definitions

The CHG index was calculated using the following formula: CHG = Ln{[TC (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)] / [2 × HDL-C (mg/dL)]}, where TC (total cholesterol), FBG (fasting blood glucose), and HDL-C (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) are expressed in mg/dL. For measurements originally recorded in mmol/L, values were converted to mg/dL using standard conversion factors (38.67 for TC and HDL-C, 18.02 for FBG) prior to calculation to ensure consistency with the original index definition. Incident diabetes during follow-up was defined as either a measured fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes15. Prediabetes was defined according to the 2022 American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria as a baseline FPG level between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L1.

Statistical analysis

We employed a six-stage analytical strategy to assess whether the baseline CHG index predicted incident diabetes in individuals with prediabetes.

Stage 1: Missing data handling. Multiple imputation was performed to create five datasets, thereby minimizing bias from incomplete records.

Stage 2: Baseline characteristics. Baseline participant characteristics were tabulated according to whether the participants developed diabetes. Continuous data are reported as mean ± SD or median (IQR) depending on the distribution, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and t‑tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. For calculation of the CHG index, biochemical measurements were converted to mg/dL: glucose (mmol/L × 18) and total cholesterol and HDL‑C (mmol/L × 38.67). The CHG index was then calculated as described above using these mg/dL values.

Stage 3: Model development. First, univariate screening was performed. Significant or clinically relevant variables were then included in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to generate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Stage 4: Primary association analysis. The CHG index was included in the analysis both as a continuous variable (per one standard deviation increase) and as a quintile group for comparison. Three nested Cox regression models were used for the association analysis: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, triglycerides, LDL-C, ALT, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, smoking, drinking, and family history of diabetes, based on Model 2.

Stage 5: Nonlinearity and sensitivity analysis. We evaluated the dose-response relationship and potential nonlinearity using restricted cubic splines. A piecewise Cox model was then fitted to identify the inflection point. The log-likelihood ratio test was used to compare the fit of the linear and piecewise models.

Stage 6: Subgroup and survival analysis. Subgroup analyses specified before the analysis were used to examine whether the associations were held across different subgroups and to identify effect modification, as assessed by interaction testing. In addition, unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves were drawn, and differences in event‑free survival across CHG quintiles were evaluated using the log-rank test.

Stage 7:The predictive performance of CHG index, TyG and TC/HDL was compared with ROC curve. To assess whether the CHG index offers incremental predictive value beyond established surrogates, we compared three Cox models: (1) a fully adjusted model including the CHG index plus all covariates from Model 2; (2) the TyG index alone, TyG was calculated as Ln(TG * FPG / 2); and (3) the TC/HDL ratio alone. Model 1 thus isolates the contribution of the CHG index beyond conventional risk factors.

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.2 (http://www.Rproject.org; The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and the Free Statistics software (version 2.3; Beijing FreeClinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China).

Results

Baseline characteristics of selected participants

The flow of participant selection is shown in Fig. 1. The final analytical cohort was comprised of 14,192 individuals. The cohort had a mean age of 51.3 ± 13.4 years, and 65.2% were male. During a mean follow-up of 3.0 ± 0.9 years, 1,748 individuals (12.3%) progressed from prediabetes to diabetes. The mean baseline CHG index was 5.3 ± 0.3, and the mean BMI was 24.9 ± 3.3 kg/m². The baseline characteristics stratified by diabetes incidence during follow-up are presented in Table 1. Compared to those who did not develop diabetes, individuals who progressed to diabetes were older and had significantly higher values for BMI, SBP, DBP, TC, TG, and CHG index. They also included a higher proportion of males, current smokers, current drinkers, and individuals with family history of diabetes. All these between-group differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001; Table 1).

Risk factors from univariate analysis

univariate Cox regression analyses identified several baseline factors significantly associated with an increased risk of progression from prediabetes to diabetes (all P < 0.05; see Supplementary Table S1). These factors included the CHG index, age, male sex, higher BMI, SBP, DBP, TG, lower HDL-C, AST, ALT, family history of diabetes, current smoking, and current alcohol use.

To examine the shape of this association in detail, we employed a restricted cubic spline model. This analysis revealed a statistically significant nonlinear relationship between the CHG index and incidence of type 2 diabetes (P for nonlinearity < 0.001; Fig. 2). Visual inspection of the spline curve suggested a pronounced decrease in diabetes risk beyond a CHG index value of 5.22 (Fig. 2; Table 3). This indicates that the association between CHG index and diabetes risk is nonlinear and cannot be adequately represented by a simple linear term.

Nonlinearity and threshold analyses

Restricted cubic spline modeling confirmed a significant association between the CHG index and diabetes risk ( overall P < 0.001), with evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity = 0.004). A piecewise Cox regression model identified an inflection point at a CHG index value of 5.22. The association differed significantly on either side of this threshold: below 5.22, the hazard ratio (HR) was 1.41 (95% CI 0.48–4.12), P = 0.528 (n = 5324); at or above 5.22, the HR was 0.55 (95% CI 0.40–0.75), P < 0.001 (n = 8868) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

A significant interaction by sex was observed (P for interaction < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among men, higher CHG index quintiles were associated with a lower risk of diabetes ( trend P < 0.001; trend HR 0.92 per quintile, 95% CI 0.87–0.96). Those in the highest quintile (Q5) had a 30% lower hazard than those in Q3 (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.83). In contrast, among women, a higher CHG index was associated with an elevated risk of diabetes (P for trend = 0.03; trend HR, 1.10 per quintile; 95% CI 1.01–1.19). No significant heterogeneity in the effect of age was observed (P for interaction = 0.54). The interaction by BMI category (< 25 vs. ≥25 kg/m2) was not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.121). However, the inverse association between the CHG index and diabetes risk was more pronounced among participants with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (P for trend = 0.007; trend HR 0.93 per quintile, 95% CI 0.88–0.98). In this subgroup, the HR for Q5 versus Q3 was 0.66 (95% CI 0.55–0.79). These subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution because of the exploratory nature of the analyses, multiple comparisons, and limited statistical power in some strata.

Predictive value of CHG index for incident of diabetes

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis revealed that the fully adjusted CHG index-based model yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.723 (95% CI: 0.711–0.734) for predicting incident of diabetes, compared to 0.621 (95% CI: 0.606–0.634) for the TyG index and 0.562 (95% CI: 0.548–0.576) for the TC/HDL ratio(Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

A retrospective analysis of 14,192 Chinese participants with prediabetes demonstrated a statistically significant nonlinear association between the CHG index measured at baseline and the subsequent development of T2DM. After a median follow-up of 3.0 years and rigorous adjustment for potential confounders, our analysis revealed a distinct inflection point at a CHG index of 5.22, indicating a threshold effect. Notably, the association exhibited pronounced sex-specific heterogeneity; a higher CHG index was associated with a lower risk of diabetes in men but an elevated risk in women. These findings suggest that CHG index may serve as a novel integrative marker for risk stratification in prediabetes. However, its interpretation must account for its nonlinear relationship with risk and the significant effect modification by sex. Furthermore, ROC analysis indicated that CHG index exhibited superior predictive ability for developing diabetes compared to TyG, TC/HDL alone, with statistical significance.

This study observed that in the prediabetic population, the cholesterol-high-density lipoprotein-glucose (CHG) index has an inverted V-shaped nonlinear association with the risk of type 2 diabetes, meaning that the risk decreases after exceeding a certain threshold. This phenomenon may be related to lipid metabolism exhaustion in the late stage of disease progression: when an individual is close to a diabetes diagnosis, severe hyperglycemia can lead to osmotic diuresis and calorie loss, causing involuntary weight loss and a decrease in total cholesterol (TC) consumption, and since the CHG index calculation formula includes TC, a “distorted decrease” in the calculated value occurs, creating the illusion of a negative correlation between risk and CHG16,17. Previous studies in the general population (including those with normal blood glucose) mostly reported a linear or continuously increasing relationship between CHG and diabetes risk after a threshold18, while this study focused on the high-risk subpopulation of prediabetes and was able to capture the late-stage characteristics before metabolic compensation collapse. This finding is consistent with the nonlinear turning point (5.28) of CHG reported by Li et al. in the Chinese population16 and the conclusion by Xiao et al. that CHG has the highest predictive value in the prediabetic population19, suggesting that the interpretation of the CHG index should be combined with the individual’s glucose metabolism status, and extremely high CHG values may not be a “low-risk” signal but rather a warning sign of metabolic exhaustion.

Our findings position the CHG index within the evolving landscape of the metabolic surrogate markers. Traditional indices, such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, primarily reflect isolated aspects of dysmetabolism20. The triglyceride glucose (TyG) index is susceptible to postprandial variability and adiposity, and triglycerides themselves fluctuate with dietary intake, which compromises its stability as a metabolic marker21. In contrast, the cholesterol-high-density lipoprotein-glucose (CHG) index integrates total cholesterol—a marker of lipotoxicity that reflects a relatively stable lipotoxic milieu—HDL-C as an indicator of anti-inflammatory capacity, and fasting glucose, enabling it to better capture chronic lipid-driven stress compared with triglyceride-based indices such as TyG21,22. Moreover, epidemiological evidence supports the superiority of cholesterol-glucose composite indices over TyG in cardiometabolic risk prediction22. These differences may arise from complementary underlying risk pathways reflected by CHG versus TyG23. However, the absence of assessments for apolipoprotein B (ApoB), HDL functionality, and hepatic fat content limits deeper mechanistic interpretation of the CHG index24, highlighting the need for further mechanistic studies to clarify its biological basis. Therefore, while direct comparisons are limited, our findings support the broader principle that composite indices integrating lipid and glucose parameters often possess superior predictive value for metabolic diseases compared with individual components alone25–28.

The biological plausibility of the CHG index is supported by its direct connection with the mechanisms of core type 2 diabetes. Each component maps onto a key dysfunctional pathway: elevated total cholesterol and low HDL-C are hallmarks of atherogenic dyslipidemia in insulin resistance29, while elevated fasting glucose marks hepatic insulin resistance and impaired pancreatic β-cell function30. These elements interact synergistically. Chronic hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia jointly fuel systemic inflammation and oxidative stress31, which in turn exacerbate insulin resistance and impair β-cell survival, creating a vicious cycle that accelerates disease progression32. By mathematically combining these three factors, the CHG index can quantitatively encapsulate the intensity of this deleterious cycle, providing a single numerical snapshot of metabolic risk15.

The opposing associations of the CHG index with diabetes risk in men (inverse) and women (positive) may reflect fundamental sex dimorphism in lipid metabolism. Endogenous estrogen promotes cardioprotective lipid profiles in women, with higher HDL-C and subcutaneous fat preserving insulin sensitivity. Longitudinal data link menopausal estradiol decline to increased visceral fat, adverse lipid changes, and rising insulin resistance33. Estrogen deficiency triggers proinflammatory adipose signatures (e.g., IL-6) correlating with lower HDL and poorer glycemic control34. Ovariectomy models confirm causality, with estrogen replacement reversing these metabolic disturbances35. In men, androgens increase hepatic lipase activity, reducing HDL-C and promoting smaller lipoprotein particles36. Thus, higher HDL-C (lower CHG) in men may reflect metabolic resilience, while rising CHG in women likely signals adverse postmenopausal metabolic transition increasing diabetes risk33,35,36. However, HDL-C concentration incompletely captures HDL functionality. studies show limited sex- or estradiol-driven differences in macrophage efflux, suggesting HDL-C changes may not directly reflect functional losses37. Extreme low CHG in men may indicate metabolic depletion (catabolism, weight loss) rather than true protection34,36.

The discovery of a significant nonlinear relationship with a clear inflection point is key. This suggests that the association between CHG index and diabetes risk is not constant across its range. The nonlinear association with a clear inflection point suggests heterogeneous physiology across CHG values. Below approximately 5.22, modest CHG variation showed no meaningful risk change; above this threshold, divergent patterns emerged. This may reflect late-stage metabolic transition in some individuals characterized by progressive β‑cell dysfunction, catabolic shift, and unintended weight loss—phenomena documented in diabetes pathogenesis studies38,39. Weight loss preceding diabetes onset, particularly in non-obese individuals, suggests declining mass may signal islet failure rather than metabolic improvement40. Severe hyperglycemia with glycosuria induces osmotic diuresis and caloric loss, potentially distorting composite indices incorporating cholesterol and anthropometrics41. Declining HDL cholesterol tracks with β‑cell functional decline42, while impaired α‑cell regulation amplifies glycemic excursions39. Thus, post-inflection risk reduction likely reflects altered physiology or reverse causation rather than true CHG benefit. Clinically, high CHG values warrant interpretation alongside weight trajectories, catabolic symptoms, and glycemic status. Longitudinal studies integrating repeated metabolic measures are needed to clarify these dynamics.

Strengths and limitations

The divergent associations observed between men and women constitute a key finding of this study. The robust inverse association in men contrasts with the positive association observed in women43. This sex disparity may be attributable to several factors. Fundamental biological differences in body composition, sex hormone profiles, and insulin resistance likely alter the physiological meaning and interplay of the index components44. For instance, the metabolic implications of HDL-C and total cholesterol may vary according to sex43. Furthermore, unmeasured or imperfectly measured confounders, such as sex-specific dietary patterns, physical activity, and body fat distribution, could contribute to this interaction. This finding underscores the necessity of evaluating metabolic risk markers separately by sex and challenges the application of uniform risk-prediction models44,45.

The strengths of our study include its large, multicenter cohort of well-characterized individuals with prediabetes, which enhances the generalizability and statistical power of our findings46. We employed a robust analytical strategy, including multiple imputations for missing data47, multivariable Cox models with extensive adjustment, and advanced methods (restricted cubic splines and piecewise regression) to rigorously characterize the nonlinearity and threshold effects.

This study had several limitations, which must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., detailed diet, physical activity, and genetic risk) may persist48. Second, the generalizability of our findings, particularly the specific inflection point and sex-specific risk patterns, requires validation in other ethnic groups and prospective settings49. Third, partial reliance on self-reported data for lifestyle factors is susceptible to recall bias50. Fourth, a “healthy survivor” effect may partly explain our findings—older adults with high HDL-C despite metabolic disturbances may represent a resilient phenotype where elevated CHG index reflects adaptive physiology rather than pathological risk. Fifth, reverse causality cannot be excluded: metabolic improvements from lifestyle changes or treatments may transiently lower CHG while reducing diabetes risk. Sixth, unavailability of sex hormones, body fat distribution indicators, and medication data (lipid-lowering) limited mechanistic validation and sensitivity analyses. Finally, the lack of serial fasting glucose measurements precluded characterization of glycemic trajectories and dynamic progression from prediabetes to diabetes. These limitations require careful interpretation and necessitate prospective studies that conduct comprehensive biomarker evaluations.

In conclusion, this Chinese prediabetic cohort reveals a nonlinear association between the CHG index and diabetes risk, with notable sex-specific differences. The index may serve as an early warning marker specifically for female patients, enabling more precise risk stratification. Given the retrospective design, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying hormonal mechanisms.
