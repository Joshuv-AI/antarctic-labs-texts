Patient perspectives and priorities in pleural infection: results of the MIST-3 feasibility trial qualitative substudy
Authors: Eihab O. Bedawi, Rebecca Burney, Sophie Harrad, Shakeeb H. Moosavi, Jack Seymour, Alison M. Condliffe, Emma Hedley, Catherine Henshall, Najib M. Rahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The question addressed by the studyThe optimal treatment for pleural infection is yet to be determined and recruitment to trials can be challenging. Research into patient priorities in pleural infection is lacking. This qualitative study aimed to help us understand the patient perspective regarding priorities of care, to identify patient-centred study outcomes, and to explore the understanding of the risks and benefits of randomisation to different pleural interventions.MethodsA prospective, multicentre, qualitative study using a semi-structured interview methodology explored key themes addressing the experiences of participation in a pleural infection randomised controlled trial (RCT) of combination intrapleural enzyme therapy versus surgery as well as priorities of care. Thematic analysis was conducted using the Framework method.ResultsThematic analysis identified five main themes: emotions, level of explanation and understanding, reaction to randomisation, influences on physical wellbeing, and overall experience. Pain was a significant feature due to both the infection and interventions. The overriding emotions described by patients were fear and anxiety. Participants demonstrated high levels of support for randomised allocation to intervention.The answer to the questionA pleural infection patient-reported outcome measure should be developed to assess pain, fatigue, anxiety and breathlessness, so that valid health-related quality of life data can be captured. Pain is an undertreated symptom of pleural infection and should be incorporated into treatment protocols. There was no clear patient preference between the interventions, and a surgical versus nonsurgical pleural infection RCT is acceptable to participants.

Introduction

Recent global epidemiological data have continued to demonstrate trends in rising incidence of pleural infection [1]. The treatment paradigm has seen a shift in recent years. Intrapleural fibrinolytic and enzyme therapy (IET) has been shown to reduce hospital stay and improve drainage, with a favourable safety profile reflected in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and real-world data [2–6], while the use of early video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has become more accessible, and has been shown to reduce hospital length of stay [7, 8]. Despite this, pleural infection continues to carry a significant 1-year mortality, reported as 12–21% [2, 9].

Thus far, outcomes for pleural infection trials have focussed on pleural effusion size, length of stay, mortality and requirement for additional therapy such as surgery [2, 9, 10]. However, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) do not feature as primary outcomes and rarely as secondary outcomes. Importantly, in a recent study of patients recovering from pleural infection, their physical functional ability and pain levels were directly associated with the treatment that they received [11]. Understanding the patient experience of pleural infection, including treatment in and beyond the hospital setting, could facilitate our delivery of personalised care and reduce the socioeconomic burden of disease. Correctly used PROMs can help ensure that we are finding treatments that produce meaningful results for patients [12]. There are no recognised PROMs validated in the pleural-infection patient group [13], which contributes to the paucity of comparable data for health-related quality of life (HRQoL) outcomes.

The UK-based third Multicenter Intrapleural Sepsis Trial (MIST)-3 study explored the feasibility of conducting an RCT to compare nonsurgical early IET to early VATS [14]. The primary outcome was feasibility to conduct a larger RCT. Conducting a trial comparing surgical versus nonsurgical interventions can present many challenges, such as increased early discontinuation due to poor recruitment [15], and a reluctance of patients to be randomly assigned to significantly different interventions [16]. Better understanding of the issues that might reduce recruitment is essential to the design of a successful and cost-effecive definitive trial.

Qualitative research provides a unique ability to explore the acceptability of different interventions [17] from the patient perspective, and to study the patient experience of hospitalisation related to pleural infections [12]. As a result, the MIST-3 qualitative substudy aimed to complement the quantitative dataset from the pilot study [14] (figure 1) and address these emergent questions. The main objectives were to understand the patient perspective regarding priorities of care, to identify patient-centred study outcomes, and to explore patient understanding of the risks and benefits of being randomised to standard care, early VATS or early IET. Our aim was to provide valuable insights into the patient experience that have been overlooked in pleural infection research so far.

Methods

A prospective, multicentre, exploratory qualitative study was conducted as a substudy to MIST-3 [14], which recruited between November 2019 and June 2021. Ethical approval was embedded within the wider MIST-3 study approval (National Health Service Research Ethics Committee and Health Research Authority reference number 19/EE/0174).

Access, recruitment and setting

The study was conducted at eight UK centres from November 2019 to July 2021. A systematic review by Hennink and Kaiser [18] showed that interview numbers of nine to 17 usually achieve data saturation. Therefore, we aimed to undertake up to 30 semi-structured interviews in 20 participants and 10 carers, in the expectation of reaching saturation of codes and themes with these numbers. Intermittent assessment for saturation was planned to be carried out every five interviews by a qualitative researcher independent to the study team (C. Henshall) to ensure coverage of demographic and trial arm. All participants were recruited as part of the main MIST-3 trial via a consecutive sampling method and simultaneously offered voluntary consent to participate in the qualitative interviews (figures 2 and 3, table 1). All participants received written and verbal information about the trial. Written consent was obtained by an authorised member of the delegation log.

Data collection

Due to the nature of qualitative research, the involvement of the researcher in data collection means that some bias will remain [19], and therefore characteristics of the interview team are detailed in supplementary table S1 for full transparency. Clinician–patient bias during the interview was limited by masking the participant to the interviewer's role. The interviewer was blinded to the participant's randomisation arm.

A semistructured interview approach [20] was used to collect qualitative data from participants as it allowed the researchers to ensure exploration of important themes, as well as providing opportunities for more responsive interviewing based on the data that emerged during the interview process. An interview topic guide (supplementary material) focused on approach to the trial, experience in the trial and experience of pleural infection. Interviews were audio recorded, de-identified, and transcribed by an external transcriber with a confidentiality agreement in place. Median interview duration was 22.5 min. An interview date 2 weeks post patient discharge from hospital was chosen to minimise the effect of recall bias, reduce appointment burden, and allow recovery time. Interviews were initially intended to be face-to-face; however, due to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic a protocol amendment introduced video conferencing or telephone interviews.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted utilising Gale et al.'s Framework method [21]. An a priori (deductive) framework informed the initial topic guide, ensuring key study questions were fully explored; subsequently, an open-code (inductive) approach was used during the initial coding. This approach enabled the addition of new codes and themes as they emerged. After the initial anonymised transcripts were read and re-read, they were double-coded by two researchers (E.O. Bedawi and S. Harrad), with a selection triple-coded (C. Henshall). An analytical framework was then developed and iteratively built upon until all transcripts had been analysed. The data were then summarised in a matrix to give a visual aid from which to interpret the findings. Figure 4 illustrates our data analysis process.

Reporting

We used the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines for this substudy to ensure rigorous reporting of data [19].

Theme 1: emotions

Feelings of being afraid and anxious were noted by most participants. For some, this stemmed from a fear of not being randomised to their perceived best option, as well as not getting better and/or requiring further interventions. For others, it resulted from uncertainty around the infection itself.

I felt really frightened, actually, because I didn't know what was happening (MI3-A-033)

Two-thirds of participants expressed feeling pleased about the premise of the MIST-3 trial and welcomed the opportunity to participate. One participant commented that they were a “believer that if you don't have to have surgery it is best” (MI3-A-010), while another randomised to the VATS arm was happy as they “felt like [they] needed surgery anyway” (MI3-A-028). In the standard-treatment arm, participant MI3-A-007 expressed relief at their randomisation, stating that they “would not have to go through too much discomfort again”. Many participants expressed confidence in the medical staff treating them, and that being part of the trial made them feel reassured that they were being treated by experts.

Pain was also a reported driver of emotional distress (theme 4), resulting in participants feeling scared and frightened because they were in so much pain. On discharge, anxiety continued for some patients, with one describing that he “did feel a bit nervy but not terrified” (MI3-A-002), and another feeling very anxious as they felt undersupported.

Theme 2: level of explanation/understanding

Most participants reported a good understanding of the randomisation process and recalled being spoken to by doctors and other researchers, as well as being given extensive information leaflets to read. Overall, most participants reported a thorough explanation of the different treatment arms, and the provision of reading material was particularly praised.

A real positive was being able to read all about what was going to happen and that it was explained really well (MI3-A-007)

While the standard and VATS treatment arms were well comprehended, two participants reported less clarity around the IET arm. Some commented on a lack of specific information on the pros and cons of each trial arm and one participant reported a lack of clear differentiation between the arms.

I don't know if I had the enzyme thing explained very well. I don't know if I would know now what that would have meant (MI3-E-001)

Participants commented that the level of understanding of the different treatment arms was impacted by the participants’ physical illness at the time as well as the impact of pain medications on their ability to fully comprehend information.

At the time, I was just in a bit of a fog, if I'm really truthful (MI3-A-002)

When it came to participants’ understanding of pleural infection, most reported an improved understanding at the time of interview compared to while they were in hospital. Confusion was stated to have arisen from the use of interchangeable words and the paronyms of medical terminology and it was felt by a fifth of participants that simplified patient-facing information using visual aids would have helped to resolve this issue somewhat.

There are too many words that look alike about what's going on in the lung (MI3-A-004)

Several participants stated that they felt ill-informed about their recovery process. This included uncertainty about the duration of their recovery, but also uncertainty about what they could do to aid their recovery. When participants had been informed that recovery would take months and that they could expect pain during this process, they reported feeling better prepared to manage their recovery.

Theme 3: reaction to randomisation

Most participants reported experiencing a positive reaction at the time of randomisation and were pleased with the intervention that they had been given; less than a fifth of participants expressed negative views of their allocated intervention. Some participants commented that they felt receiving a noncontrol intervention was positive, because of the view that standard care either had already not worked, or because the surgical or IET arms felt like more active treatment.

[I was] happy I wasn't in the control group because I knew that I'd be getting some form of positive intervention (MI3-A-001)

Preconceptions of surgery were mixed. Some felt that surgery would be more likely to deal with the problem at hand, with one participant stating that they “assumed it would be the best option” (MI3- A-001). Others said they felt scared of an operation and wanted to avoid it where possible. The need for surgery was often associated by participants with a realisation of how serious the pleural infection was.

Theme 4: influences on physical wellbeing

Prior to any treatment, the main symptom that participants experienced on account of their pleural infection was pain. This was described as “excruciating”, “agony”, and “11 on a scale of one to 10”. A further debilitating syndrome that was reported by the majority was general malaise, with fevers, aches, and fatigue.

I've never felt so ill in my life, if I'm honest (MI3-A-013)

Treatment-related pain was reported by participants in all study arms. While two participants described a relief from pain and release of pressure when the chest tube was inserted, the insertion and presence of the chest tube itself was “immensely uncomfortable” and “extremely painful”. All but one participant described significant pain from the IET. In some cases, this caused delay or discontinuation of treatment. In participants undergoing VATS there was a description of post-operative pain being both “sharp” and “tender”, but reports of pain were less prominent than in the IET arm, with one participant reporting that the pain felt better post-surgery.

A third of participants described shortness of breath either initially or during recovery, one participant finding this “particularly frightening”. Over half of participants described significant fatigue particularly during recovery: “my stamina has been…significantly reduced” (MI3-A-001); however, these symptoms improved throughout the post-discharge period.

Theme 5: overall experience

Most participants reported an overwhelmingly positive trial experience. The level of care offered by staff during the trial was reported as an important contributor to this positive outcome. Participants commended the communication, treatment, and compassion that they received.

I am moved by the amount of care I've been given. Not just the professionalism, that immense compassion (MI3-A-002)

Some participants commented on inconsistencies in information about their diagnosis and care which may have been due to being treated by both the usual ward team as well as the research team. One participant felt intimidated by the number of professionals involved, and another queried whether the research team and the medical treating team had differing opinions about what the best treatment was.

I wasn't sure if the two of them were on different sides… (MI3-A-033)

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge this is the first study to use a qualitative methodology to understand the patient experiences and priorities of care in pleural infection, delivering important clinical insights into pleural infection management and informing future trial design and PROMs.

Fear and anxiety were reported by most participants and stemmed from a realisation of the severity of their illness, pain and breathlessness, trepidation regarding the possible interventions, and the possibility of failure of that intervention. This was corroborated by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale scores reported in the main trial (mean score at day 1–2 post-randomisation 16.7 (95% CI 14.2–19.2), representing a severe score) [14]. It is impossible to completely detach these emotions from those which might be associated with participation in a clinical trial; however, understanding what alleviated these emotions can further support a future trial design that is acceptable to participants and minimises any trial-related anxiety. These emotions were mitigated by a strong sense of trust in medical professionals, and were heightened by inconsistencies between clinical teams, lack of information about the participants’ medical condition, and the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. This emphasises the importance of cohesion between the usual medical team and the research team, and the need to provide patients with regular updates.

It was evident that much of the patient journey was defined by the experience of significant pain. Pain was reported both as an initial symptom before diagnosis of pleural infection, and in direct relation to chest drain insertion, IET and surgery (supplementary material, theme codes 4.1 and 4.2). This is particularly interesting as among the few HRQoL measures used in studies for pleural effusions [13] only 58% (seven out of 12) address pain and out of these only the visual analogue scale (VAS)-pain has a minimal clinically important change defined in this patient group [22–26]. When examining the qualitative dataset, the most frequently experienced HRQoL determinants were pain, anxiety/fear, fatigue and breathlessness. These determinants were mapped against the content assessed by PROMs previously used in pleural disease studies (supplementary figure S1) to highlight any crossover between a HRQoL determinant that was important to the participant, and its assessment by the PROM. Only Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-pal (a multidirectional HRQoL measure specific to the palliative care population) [27] covered all four determinants.

Patients reported higher VAS-pain scores for chest drain insertion and IET versus VATS during the main study. This perhaps highlights improved protocols for post-operative analgesia in the surgical population compared to the other treatment arms. Ineffective pain relief was a recurrent theme and poses the question of whether the role of pain in pleural infection is underestimated; this could have an impact on guiding patient-centred outcomes, addressing effective analgesia, and assessing how pain impacts treatment compliance and subsequent treatment success in this patient cohort. It is noteworthy that the most common cause of noncompliance with treatment in the IET arm of the wider MIST-3 study was patient intolerance of the therapy. In contrast, participants in the VATS arm did report prolonged symptoms during recovery compared to those in the IETS arm, and this was reflected in the MIST-3 quality-of-life data, which demonstrated quicker recovery in the IET arm [14].

The overall participant experience of the trial was positive across all study arms. Both surgery and IET were deemed acceptable treatments despite some preconceptions around surgery (positive and negative), except for one participant who expressed a strong preference towards the VATS arm. This participant reported a lack of information about the other options, demonstrating the importance of consistent, unbiased information about all trial arms to optimise recruitment rates. The MIST-3 trial met its predefined feasibility criteria and achieved a 62% randomisation rate [14]; improvements in pain management and information about treatment arms is likely to further augment this, demonstrating favourable acceptability in future surgical versus nonsurgical pleural trials. This study has revealed important learning points with regards to how we can optimise informed recruitment to trials while patients are acutely unwell. The use of multiple formats and modes of information provision was found to be helpful; however, the benefits of this could be augmented using visual aids and simplified explanations.

The study had limitations. Due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and redirected resources, the initial recruitment target of 30 participants was not met; however, independent review analysis determined that saturation of data and themes had been achieved and that further interviews were not required. While data saturation was met, we acknowledge that including more interviews could have served to increase the representation of the sample by including people with a more diverse range of demographic variables. We did not perform an analysis of the patients who declined participation, which may have highlighted a selection bias. The voice of carers was underrepresented in the trial sample and further qualitative research can help to address this important source of information. This was a UK-based study with a largely white demographic, and it is important to consider that the transferability of our findings to other cultures could be limited. The use of different interviewers and interview modalities could have affected the consistency of data generated. However, a semi-structured interview approach and formal qualitative interview training helped mitigate this. Inclusion criteria stipulated a good level of understanding of the English language, and therefore the views of non-English-speaking participants were underrepresented.

In conclusion, a diagnosis of a pleural infection is associated with significant fear and anxiety and identifying the optimal upfront treatment can help alleviate this uncertainty. Pain is an undertreated symptom of pleural infection and its subsequent therapies. Pain management should be incorporated into treatment protocols for pleural infections and is an important outcome measure for future studies. The ideal pleural infection PROM should incorporate pain, fatigue, anxiety and breathlessness, and further studies are required to develop and validate such a PROM. This study highlighted the benefits of using a nested qualitative substudy within a larger scale feasibility study; this has provided evidence that a surgical versus nonsurgical pleural infection RCT is feasible and acceptable to participants, provided any learnings from this qualitative study are incorporated into the trial design.
