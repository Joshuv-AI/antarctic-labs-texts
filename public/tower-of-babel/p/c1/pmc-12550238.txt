Primed human pluripotent stem cell-derived blastocyst-like cell aggregates with partial lineage specification
Authors: Kaori Mutsuda-Zapater, Xiaopeng Wen, Satoshi Imamura, Shiho Terada, Kyoko Sawada, Kei Saito, Ken-ichiro Kamei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHuman blastoids, blastocyst-like structures assembled in vitro, hold great promise for decoding the earliest steps of human development. However, most existing protocols rely on naïve human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), which are chromosomally unstable and technically demanding.MethodsWe developed a method to generate human blastocyst-like cell aggregates directly from primed hPSCs using a thermoresponsive hydrogel. Generated blastocyst-like cell aggregates were evaluated for morphological features, lineage marker expression via immunocytochemistry, transcriptional profiles using single-cell RNA sequencing, and functional capacity through in vitro implantation assays.ResultsPrimed hPSC-derived blastocyst-like cell aggregates recapitulated key morphological features of human blastocysts, including cyst formation and spatial expression of epiblast, trophectoderm, and primitive endoderm markers. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that a subset of cells showed transcriptional profiles resembling epiblast-, trophectoderm-, and primitive endoderm-like cells although a substantial proportion remained undifferentiated. Functionally, blastocyst-like cell aggregates demonstrated in vitro implantation potential, trophoblast differentiation, and secretion of human chorionic gonadotropin.ConclusionsThis work introduces a more accessible platform for generating human blastocyst-like cell aggregates from primed hPSCs, broadening their utility for investigating early development events. Insights gained from blastocyst-like cell aggregates have the potential to advance the modelling of early-onset diseases, drive innovations in regenerative therapies, and contribute to the development of assisted reproductive technologies.Keywords: Blastocyst-like cell aggregate, Blastoid, Human pluripotent stem cells, Blastocyst, Hydrogel, Embryo model, Primed pluripotency

Introduction

Human blastoids, artificial structures that recapitulate key features of the blastocyst, have emerged as powerful in vitro models for studying early human embryogenesis [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. They provide a scalable and ethically acceptable alternative to donated embryos, unlike animal models, are free from species-specific differences inherent in animal models [[7], [8], [9]]. This enables mechanistic investigations of blastocyst formation, lineage specification, and implantation under precisely controlled laboratory conditions. However, a significant limitation of current generation protocols is their reliance on naïve human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) [3,6,10]. While naïve hPSCs closely resemble the preimplantation epiblast and possess a broad differentiation potential [11], their use is complicated by chromosomal instability and a dependence on feeder cells [[12], [13], [14], [15]].

In contrast, primed hPSCs offer greater stability and are easier to maintain, making them a more practical cell source [16,17]. Despite these advantages, their potential to efficiently form high-fidelity blastoids has remained largely underexplored. Given that the physical microenvironment strongly influences embryonic development [18], we hypothesized that recreating a supportive three-dimensional context might allow blastoid formation from primed hPSCs. Building on our earlier work with thermoresponsive hydrogels for uniform 3D aggregation [19], we explored whether this system could provide a more favorable niche for blastoid formation.

Here, we establish a hydrogel-based method for generating blastocyst-like cell aggregates directly from primed hPSCs and assess their developmental fidelity and functional potential through immunocytochemistry, single-cell RNA sequencing, and in vitro implantation assays. While not yet bona fide blastoids, these structures represent a novel platform to explore early human development, implantation dynamics, and reproductive biology.

Materials and methods

Culture of primed hPSCs

K1-OCT4-EGFP human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), expressing the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) driven by the octamer-binding transcription factor 4 promoter (OCT4; or POU domain, class 5, transcription factor 1 [POU5F1]), H1-OCT4-EGFP (WA01) and H9 (WA09) hESCs were used in this study. The K1-OCT4-EGFP hES cell line was obtained from Dr. Eihachiro Kawase [20], and WA01 and WA09 were obtained from WiCell Research Institute on April first 2022.

Prior to cell seeding, culture dishes were coated with 1.3 % v/v hESC-certified Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) in DMEM/F12 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), incubated 24 h at 4 °C and washed with fresh DMEM/F12. Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5 % v/v CO2 in mTeSR-1 medium (Stem Cell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada), supplemented with 10 μM Y27632 ROCK inhibitor (Med Chem Express, NJ, USA) (mTeSR-1 + Y) only for 48 h after each passage, to prevent apoptosis. Cells were passaged at 80 % confluency with TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) incubated for 3 min at 37 °C with 5 % v/v CO2.

Blastocyst-like cell aggregate generation

Once culture was stabilized (three passages from cell thawing), cells were dissociated with TrypLE Express and centrifuged at 150×g for 3 min. Supernatant was removed and pellet was resuspended in DMEM High glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10 % v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cell Culture Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan), 1 % v/v penicillin/streptomycin, 1 % w/v N-acetyl-l-cysteine (Cayman Chemical), and 10 μM Y27632 ROCK inhibitor (DMEM FBS + Y) for cell count. Cells were centrifuged again, and the cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM FBS + Y to achieve a desired cell concentration.

Meanwhile, the AggreWell 400 24-well plate (Stemcell Technologies, Vacouver, Canada) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, wells were rinsed with anti-adhesion rinsing solution (Stemcell Technologies), centrifuged at 150×g for 15 min, rinsed with DMEM/F12 and incubated at 37 °C until use.

Next, the DMEM/F12 was removed, and 1 mL of cell suspension (1.2 × 106 cells/well) was added to each well, and incubated at 37 °C with 5 % v/v CO2. After 24 h in the AggreWell, cell aggregates were collected in individual tubes and let to sink for 10 min. The supernatant was then removed leaving about 50 μL. The pellet was resuspended in 210 μL of a hydrogel solution (DMEM High glucose with 10 % w/v PNIPAAm-β-PEG hydrogel [Mebiol Inc., Hiratsuka, Japan]) at 4 °C, and the total volume (cell aggregate suspension and hydrogel) was transferred to an ultra-low attachment 24-well plate. 500 μL of DMEM FBS + Y was added to each well. Culture medium was changed daily, and the cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5 % v/v CO2 for 4 days.

To note, H1-OCT4-EGFP and H9 hESCs were cultured in the AggreWell for 48 h in mTeSR-1 + Y to compensate for their lower cell aggregation tendencies.

Blastocyst-like cell aggregate collection and quantification

Blastocyst-like cell aggregates were collected on day 4 since gel embedding by adding ice cold PBS with 0.04 %w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA) (PBS-BSA) to the wells. The plate was placed on ice for 5 min and the dissolved hydrogel containing the aggregates was collected in 15 mL tubes and centrifuged at 100×g for 3 min. PBS-BSA was added again to remove any excess hydrogel and centrifuged again. Finally, cell aggregates were resuspended in PBS-BSA to be counted and used for the following experiments.

The generation efficiency was calculated by manually counting >100 cell aggregates and identifying the blastocyst-like cell aggregates within them. We defined blastocyst-like cell aggregates as those aggregates with a size ranging from 50 μm to 300 μm in diameter and a cyst occupancy over 50 %. Aggregate size was calculated by the mean of the largest (d1) and smallest (d2) diameters, and cyst occupancy was established as the ratio of the cyst area (Acyst) and the aggregate total area (Aaggregate).

In vitro implantation assay for blastocyst-like cell agggregates

Collected blastocyst-like cell aggregates were subjected to an in vitro implantation assay to confirm their functionality. The assay was performed according to previously published protocols [2,3]. Briefly, collected blastocyst-like cell aggregates were transferred onto glass-bottom tissue culture plates (Iwaki, Japan) coated with Geltrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cultured in IVC1 medium (DMEM/F12, supplemented with 20 % v/v, 1 % v/v ITS-X supplement, 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.5 % v/v P/S, 1.25 mM N-acetyl-cysteine [Cayman Chemical], 8 μM β-estradiol [Cayman Chemical] and 200 ng/mL progesterone [Nacalai Tesque]) [21] with daily medium replacement at 37 °C with 5 % v/v CO2. On day 2, the culture medium was replaced with the IVC2 medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 30 % knockout serum replacement [KSR; ThermoFisher Scientific], 1 % v/v ITS-X supplement, 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.5 % v/v P/S, 25 μM N-acetyl-l-cysteine, 8 nM β-estradiol and 200 ng/mL progesterone) [22]. Blastocyst-like cell aggregates were cultured up to day 4.5 with daily medium replacement and collected for immunocytochemistry and qRT-PCR analysis. Medium on day 4.5 was also collected for chorionic gonadotropin-β (hCG) ELISA using the Human hCG ELISA kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or Human hCG (intact) ELISA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich).

Immunocytochemistry and image acquisition

Immunocytochemistry of free-floating blastocyst-like cell aggregates was performed to determine if cellular distribution within the blastocyst-like cell aggregates is comparable to that of blastocysts. Blastocyst-like cell aggregates were fixed with 4 % v/v paraformaldehyde in PBS for 60 min at 25 °C, permeabilized with 0.5 % v/v Triton X-100 (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA) in PBS for 16 h. Next, the cells were blocked at 4 °C for 16 h in 0.1 % Tween in PBS (PBST) containing 5 % v/v normal donkey serum (Wako) and 3 % w/v BSA. The aggregates were then incubated with primary antibodies in a blocking buffer at 4 °C for 16 h. Lastly, secondary antibodies and 300 nM 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Wako) were incubated in a blocking buffer at 25 °C for 2 h. The samples were washed with PBST between all steps. All the antibodies used in this study are listed in S1 Table.

Image acquisition

Image acquisition was conducted with the Andor Dragonfly 502 confocal microscopy system (Andor, Northern Ireland, UK) mounted on a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2-E inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The system featured a Zyla 4.2 Plus scientific complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (sCMOS) camera (Andor) driven by Fusion (Andor), with a CFI Plan apochromat lambda 10 × dry/0.45 NA (Nikon), CFI Super Fluor 20 × dry/0.75 NA (Nikon) It is complemented by a motorized XY stage, a stage piezo (Applied Scientific Instrumentation, MO, USA), and three lasers (405, 488, 561, and 637 nm). For H1-OCT4-EGFP and H9 hESC samples, images were acquired with UPLXAPO20X and UPLXAPO40X objectives and 405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm and 640 nm laser lines using Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope Olympus Fluoview FV3000 (Evident, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative RT-PCR

The absence of primitive streak markers was confirmed by Real-Time qPCR. Total RNA was isolated from cells using the ReliaPrep™ RNA Miniprep Systems (Promega, Wisconsin, United States) or the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), of which 100 ng were reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the PrimeScript RT kit (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan) or the SuperScript™ IV VILO™ Master Mix (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, US). The qRT-PCR reaction mixture was prepared with 20 ng cDNA and the reaction was conducted on the Applied Biosystems 7300 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Alternatively, HOT FIREPol EvaGreen qPCR Supermix 5x (Solis Biodyne, Tartu, Estonia) was used on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculating using the DDCt method, with GAPDH or ACTB as the housekeeping gene. All primers used in this study are listed on S2 Table.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (v4.4.1). All tests were conducted using data from at least three biologically independent replicates. One-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc Tukey's HSD test was conducted for normally distributed samples. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used as a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA for comparing multiple groups. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with FDR adjustment to control for multiple comparisons. For comparisons between two conditions, a Welch's Two Sample t-test was applied to account for potential differences in variances between the groups. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (p < 0.05). All p values are listed on S3-8 Table.

Sample preparation for single-cell RNA sequencing

For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), collected blastocyst-like cell aggregates treated with Accumax (Nacalai Tesque) for 25 min at 37 °C (pipetting every 5 min) and washed with ice cold PBS-BSA. The dissociated aggregates were passed through a 40 μm cell strainer, counted, and the appropriate number were then loaded into a chromium controller (10 × Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) for encapsulation, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3.1 [Dual Index]). Extracted RNA underwent quantification via qRT-PCR with the Collibri™ Library Quantification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The PCR parameters comprised an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, succeeded by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s and 60 °C for 45 s on a StepOnePlus system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). For each sample, a concentration of 1.8 pM 6 was used with the NextSeq 500 Mid Output Kit v2.5 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed with Illumina Next Seq 500 (Illumina) applying a pair-end (Read1:28 bp, Read2:90 cycles) sequencing approach aiming 20,000 reads per cell. Demultiplexing was achieved using Chromium barcodes and the CellRanger program (10 × Genomics; v6.1.1). Alignment and UMI counting were performed with CellRanger to map the reads to a customized Ensembl GRCh38 reference genome.

Data processing for single-cell RNA sequencing

To analyze gene expression at the single-cell level, the ICARUS web server (Interactive single Cell RNA-seq Analysis with R Shiny Using Seurat, v3) [23,24] was used. The uploaded datasets were subjected to batch effects reduction, and they were scaled and normalized using the following parameters: number of variable features = 3,000, normalization method = "LogNormalize", dimensionality reduction method = Harmony, and number of dimensions = 50. Doublets were identified and removed using the DoubletFinder package [25]. For data visualization, principal component analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were performed. The following parameters were applied for the nearest-neighbor graph construction: number of dimensions = 30, k.param = 20, n.trees = 50, clustering resolution = 0.3, and the Louvain clustering algorithm. UMAP was carried out with 30 dimensions, k-nearest neighbors = 20, and min.dist = 0.3.

Language optimization

To ensure clarity and grammatical accuracy, we used the language-editing service ChatGPT o3 and Gemini 2.5.

Results

Generation of human blastocyst-like cell aggregates from primed hPSCs

K1-OCT4-EGFP, H1-OCT4-EGFP and H9 hESCs were used in this study (Fig. 1 and S1–S3 Fig.). Primed hPSC aggregates were embedded in a thermo-responsive hydrogel [19] and induced for blastocyst-like aggregate formation by replacing the initial mTeSR-1 medium with DMEM supplemented with 10 % v/v FBS (Fig. 1a). K1-OCT4-EGFP and H1-OCT4-EGFP hESCs allowed real-time monitoring of both pluripotency and inner cell mass formation within blastocyst-like cell aggregates, given their EGFP driven by OCT4 promoter.

To assess the blastocyst-like cell aggregate formation efficiency, we established the following criteria (Fig. 1b).

For K1-OCT4-EGFP hESCs, cyst formation was first observed on day 3 after hydrogel embedding. By day 4, aggregates with a blastocyst-like morphology, characterized by a cystic cavity and a localized EGFP-expressing ICM (Fig. 1c), accounted for 57.3 ± 6.0 % of the total aggregate number (initial seeding density: 1000 cells/aggregate) (Fig. 1d and e). Lower seeding densities of 250 and 500 cells/aggregate resulted in no blastocyst-like cell aggregate generation (S1a Fig.). Moreover, H1-OCT4-EGFP and H9 hESCs exhibited significantly lower generation efficiencies, at 6.9 ± 2.7 % and 4.1 ± 2.1 % respectively (Fig. 1e). The resulting K1-OCT4-EGFP hESC blastocyst-like aggregates had an average diameter of 153.4 ± 26.7 μm, an average cyst diameter of 129.5 ± 24.7 μm and a cyst occupancy of 79.9 ± 11.1 %. In comparison, H1-OCT4-EGFP and H9 hESCs blastocyst-like aggregates measured 179.4 ± 25.2 μm and 169.7 ± 25.0 μm (Fig. 1f), with average cyst diameters of 145.7 ± 22.5 μm and 144.3 ± 24.8 μm (Fig. 1g), and cyst occupancies of 66.2 ± 8.5 % and 85.0 ± 6.8 %, respectively (Fig. 1h).

Primed hPSC blastocyst-like cell aggregates show similar cellular localization to natural blastocysts

To evaluate the spatial organization of lineage specification within the obtained blastocyst-like cell aggregates, we performed whole-mount immunocytochemistry and visualized key lineage markers associated with human blastocysts (Fig. 2a). The generated blastocyst-like cell aggregates derived from K1-OCT4-EGFP, H1-OCT4-EGFP and H9 hESCs showed a cystic morphology resembling that of natural blastocysts and displayed lineage-specific marker expression patterns consistent with epiblast (EPI), trophectoderm (TE) and primitive endoderm (PrE) (Fig. 2b). Specifically, OCT4, NANOG, and SOX2 labeled the EPI within the ICM-like region; CDX2 and GATA2 identified TE-like cells; and SOX17 and GATA6 labeled PrE-like cell populations (Fig. 2b–e, S2b-e, and S3b, c Fig., respectively). The spatial distribution of these lineages within the cystic structure closely resembled that of natural human blastocysts, supporting the developmental fidelity of the generated blastocyst-like cell aggregates.

Primed hPSC blastocyst-like cell aggregates show implantation capacity

Implantation capacity, a key indicator of blastocyst functionality was confirmed for our generated blastocyst-like cell aggregates by an in vitro implantation assay using dishes coated with Geltrex basement membrane matrix and in vitro culture media 1 (IVC1) [21] and 2 (IVC2) [22] consecutively. Generated blastocyst-like cell aggregates fully attached to the dishes on the seeding day. By day 4.5, some aggregates rapidly expanded into flattened structures containing 3D compact cell regions (Fig. 2f). In contrast, others developed only flattened, mono-layered structures (S1b Fig.). The formation of pro-amniotic-like cavities was confirmed by the appearance of a small lumen at the site of apical polarization, where F-actin and atypical Protein Kinase C (aPKC) were localized around the developing structure (Fig. 2g). Some of the TE-like cells differentiated into the invasive extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs), indicated by the expression of the matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2) and human leukocyte antigen-G (HLA-G). Chorionic gonadotropin-β (hCG-β) expression was localized surrounding the outer layer of the post-implanted blastocyst-like cell aggregate, indicating the successful differentiation of trophoblast cells to syncytiotrophoblasts (STBs). STBs expressing this key hormone, known to determine clinical pregnancy and for regulating trophoblast invasion [26], showed multinucleation (Fig. 2h–S2f and S3d Fig.). The secretion of hCG from post-implanted blastocyst-like cell aggregates was observed, whereas it was absent in free-floating (pre-implantation) blastocyst-like cell aggregates. (Fig. 2i–S2i and S3e Fig.). The markers of the primitive streak were not observed in post-implanted blastocyst-like cell aggregates (S1c, S2i, S3f Fig.).

Single-cell transcriptomic comparison of human blastocyst-like cell aggregates derived from primed hPSCs with natural human blastocysts

To further evaluate the cellular identities within the obtained blastocyst-like cell aggregates, we first performed an integrated transcriptome analysis, comparing day-4 primed hPSC blastocyst-like aggregates with human blastocysts at embryonic day (E) 5–7 [27] (Fig. 3a). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis revealed 11 distinct clusters based on gene expression profiles. The UMAP showed that the cellular distribution of blastocyst-like cell aggregates partially overlapped with that of natural human blastocysts.

We further confirmed the expression of key lineage markers for EPI (POU5F1), TE (GATA2), and PrE (GATA6) within the UMAP (Fig. 3b). POU5F1 was predominantly expressed in Cluster 1, both in natural blastocysts and blastocyst-like cell aggregates consistent with the EPI lineage. GATA2 was broadly expressed among cells from natural blastocysts but was less represented in blastocyst-like cell aggregates. GATA6 expression was observed in a relatively wide range of cells within blastocysts, although fewer GATA6-positive cells were found in blastocyst-like cell aggregates.

To characterize lineage identities, we analyzed the expression of multiple lineage markers using dot plots for each cluster (Fig. 3c). Based on these marker expression patterns, we assigned cell lineages to specific clusters. In addition to POU5F1, Cluster 1 contained smaller cell populations co-expressing SOX2, NANOG, and TDGF1, leading to its annotation as EPI. TE markers were broadly expressed across Clusters 3, 4, 5, and 6, leading to their designation as TE. Clusters 4 and 6 were labeled as polar TE due to the expression of additional characteristic markers. A clear PrE-specific cluster was not detected. However, Cluster 2 exhibited a mixed expression pattern of both EPI and PrE markers; therefore, we provisionally identified this cluster as EPI/PrE. Although additional clusters were present, they lacked definitive expression of established lineage markers used for blastocyst lineage identification and were consequently categorized as unidentified.

In terms of cluster composition, the proportion of EPI-like cells in the blastocyst-like cell aggregates (8.9 %) was comparable to that in the natural human blastocysts (5.8 %). TE-like cells in the blastocyst-like cell aggregates represented a total of 48.1 %, which included the 22.8 % were classified as polar TE. In the natural blastocysts, the total TE proportion was 87.1 % while polar TE represented 8.8 %. Lastly, the EPI/PrE cluster only represented a 3.8 % in the blastocyst-like cell aggregates and 0.3 % in natural blastocysts (Fig. 3d). Overall, our blastocyst-like cell aggregate dataset contained cellular populations resembling those in natural human blastocysts, along with undefined populations not found in them.

Discussion

We established a hydrogel-based method to generate blastocyst-like cell aggregates from primed hPSCs. While most previously published protocols rely on naïve or extended pluripotent stem cells for blastoid generation, our results demonstrate that primed hPSCs can also self-organize into structures with blastocyst-like morphology and partial lineage specification. Importantly, this was achieved across three independent primed hPSC lines, albeit with varying efficiency. H1-OCT4-EGFP and H9 hESCs exhibited lower self-aggregation capacity compared to K1-OCT4-EGFP hESCs necessitating line-specific modifications such as extending the aggregation period under maintenance conditions (mTeSR-1 medium [28] supplemented with the ROCK inhibitor Y27632) prior to differentiation induction. These adjustments improved blastocyst-like aggregate formation, yet overall efficiency remained limited (S2 and S3 Fig.), reflecting the intrinsic developmental constraints of primed hPSCs.

Several factors may explain why fully faithful blastoids were not obtained in this study. First, the possibility that the developmental potential of primed hPSCs may require additional time for efficient lineage specification. In particular, primed hPSCs may need to revert to a more plastic, naïve-like state during aggregation to fully recapitulate blastoid formation [29,30]. Although we did not assess intermediate phases of aggregate formation prior to blastocyst-like cell aggregate emergence, EPI-like cells within the resulting structures consistently displayed only transcriptional signatures of primed pluripotency. This contrasts with previous reports, which have suggested that reversion to a naïve-like state may be necessary for blastoid formation [31]. Secondly, primed hPSC cultures are inherently heterogeneous, often containing small populations of cells at earlier developmental stages [[32], [33], [34]], which may limit their ability to achieve full and accurate lineage specification within the time frame and conditions of our protocol. Lastly, the current hydrogel formulation likely requires further tuning. The physical and biochemical properties of the microenvironment are known to influence cell fate decisions [19,35,36]. Optimization of parameters such as stiffness and ligand presentation could improve lineage fidelity.

Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the H9 hESCs have previously been reported to exhibit low blastoid generation efficiency even from the naïve state, approximately 10 % [37], suggesting intrinsic line-specific limitations. Differences in metabolic profiles between primed and naïve cells, where primed cells primarily rely on glycolysis, whereas naïve cells utilize both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, could also contribute to differences in blastocyst-like cell aggregate formation efficiency. Moreover, differences in gene expression profiles, particularly among developmental signaling and pluripotency factors, have been reported to be significantly associated with distinct lineage biases in differentiation [38,39]. For instance, persistent “epigenetic memory” from the donor cell type, as well as variable epigenetic marks, have been linked to divergent differentiation capacities among iPSC lines [40]. Similarly, heterogeneity in the regulation of pluripotency genes and lineage-specific transcriptional programs can result in distinct signaling biases, particularly in pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, that influence lineage allocation and efficiency [41].

Although our method achieved high efficiency in generating primed hPSC aggregates with blastocyst-like morphology, scRNA-seq analysis revealed limited transcriptomic fidelity to natural human blastocysts. Approximately 57.7 % of the cells within the day-4 aggregates aligned with EPI, TE and PrE lineages, while a substantial proportion remained unassigned (Fig. 3d). EPI-like cells were found in similar proportions in both natural blastocysts and generated blastocyst-like cell aggregates, whereas TE-like cells were approximately twice as abundant in the natural blastocyst dataset. Moreover, PrE-like cells were limited, often co-expressing EPI markers, indicating impartial development of the ICM. This observation suggests that only a subset of the morphologically selected aggregates had fully differentiated into blastocyst-like cell aggregates by day 4, whereas the majority exhibited incomplete cell lineage specification. This discrepancy between blastoid morphology and transcriptional identity has also been emphasized in prior studies, showing that morphologically similar structures can have markedly different gene expression profiles, depending on the methods and cell lines used [4]. These findings collectively underscore the critical need to evaluate both morphological and molecular features when assessing the fidelity of blastoid models for human embryogenesis.

Despite its potential, our approach has notable limitations to be addressed in future studies. First, the number of hPSCs required to form blastocyst-like cell aggregates is significantly higher than the number of cells found in a natural human blastocyst, and it is also higher than the cell numbers used in naïve hPSC-based methods. This difference suggests a lower intrinsic efficiency of primed hPSCs for blastoid formation, likely reflecting their more restricted developmental potential. Second, while morphological cyst formation was highly efficient, transcriptomic fidelity to human blastocysts remained low, underscoring the need for further optimization of the protocol to improve lineage specification. Third, reliance on FBS introduces variability due to lot-to-lot differences, which can affect reproducibility. Although FBS contains factors essential for lineage induction, including BMP4 [42], the exact composition is undefined, and transitioning to chemically defined media would provide better control over the differentiation process. Identifying the critical factors within FBS that contribute to trophoblast [43] and other lineage inductions will be essential for refining culture conditions. This variability underscores the need to identify predictive markers of high-efficiency lines, or universal conditions that minimize inter-line differences, to establish more robust and generalizable methodologies.

Finally, although our study focused on pre-implantation-like stages, it remains unknown whether these blastocyst-like cell aggregates possess the capacity to model post-implantation development. Evaluating this capacity will be essential to determine the full utility of primed hPSC-derived blastocyst-like cell aggregates as platforms for studying human development. This uncertainty also invites ethical reflection on the conceptual boundaries between stem-cell-based embryo models and human embryos. As technologies advance, improved culture systems, tissue engineering, and genome editing may yield models that more closely recapitulate post-implantation structures, potentially narrowing the distinction between synthetic models and embryos. Current ISSCR guidelines [44], set boundaries for the ethical use of embryo-like models. However, a continuous dialogue and ethical review will be essential to keep research practices aligned with scientific progress and societal values.

Conclusions

In this study, we established a new method to generate human blastocyst-like cell aggregates from widely available primed hPSCs using a thermo-responsive hydrogel. These cell aggregates exhibited key morphological and lineage features of human blastocysts, including cyst formation and expression of EPI, TE, and PrE markers. However, single-cell RNA sequencing revealed incomplete lineage specification, with a substantial proportion of cells remaining undifferentiated. While not bona fide blastoids, our system provides a promising platform to explore the role of microenvironmental cues, culture duration, and intermediate cell states in blastoid formation. Although less efficient and transcriptionally faithful than naïve hPSC-derived blastoids, this scalable approach can be further refined to improve fidelity and expand application in reproductive research, disease modeling, and regenerative medicine.
