Phase-specific checkpoint modulation by organic and inorganic constituents of Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract in A549 lung cancer cells
Authors: Deniz Şumnulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Medicinal plant extracts are typically interpreted mainly through their organic phytochemical constituents; however, growing evidence suggests that inorganic components may also contribute to biological activity and cellular fate. In this study, we performed an integrated organic–inorganic mechanistic evaluation of the anticancer activity of Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract, focusing on its effects in A549 human lung carcinoma cells. Treatment concentrations were determined using MTT-based cytotoxicity assays. The phenolic composition of the methanolic extract was characterized by LC–MS/MS, while apoptosis induction and cell-cycle distribution were analyzed by flow cytometry. Inorganic anions were quantified by ion chromatography under conditions corresponding to the A549 IC50 value. LC–MS/MS profiling identified rutin, chlorogenic acid, and hesperidin as the major phenolic constituents. Exposure of A549 cells to the extract at its IC50 significantly reduced cell viability, whereas HEL299 cells retained high viability. Ion chromatography revealed the presence of fluoride, phosphate, and sulfate anions. Functional assays using certified NaF, Na2HPO4, and Na2SO4 standards showed that NaF significantly induced apoptosis and G0/G1 arrest, while sulfate caused S-phase depletion and G2/M accumulation. No significant effects were observed after phosphate treatment. These phase-specific responses suggest that Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract acts as a phytochemical–ionic system in which inorganic anions contribute to selective cell-cycle modulation in A549 cells. Introduction

Dysregulated cell-cycle progression is a defining hallmark of malignant transformation and remains a central target in anticancer drug development1. In normal cells, the transition from the quiescent G0/G1 phase into DNA synthesis is tightly regulated by cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and their endogenous inhibitors, most notably p21 and p27 of the Cip/Kip family2,3. Disruption of this regulatory network permits uncontrolled proliferation, genomic instability, and the development of therapeutic resistance in cancer cells. Accordingly, pharmacological enforcement of G0/G1 checkpoint arrest prior to S-phase entry is widely regarded as a rational and effective anticancer strategy.

Plants of the genus Berberis have attracted considerable attention owing to their rich content of isoquinoline alkaloids and polyphenols, particularly berberine4–6. berberine has been reported to exert antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects across a range of cancer models, including gastric carcinoma, neuroblastoma, oral squamous carcinoma, and breast cancer cells7–10. Nevertheless, its activity in A549 lung cancer cells remains controversial, with several studies reporting limited, inconsistent, or absent effects on cell-cycle arrest11. Moreover, increasing evidence indicates that berberine may induce genotoxic stress and DNA damage in non-malignant cells, raising important concerns regarding therapeutic selectivity and safety12–14.

Beyond organic phytochemicals, medicinal plant extracts also contain inorganic ions that can contribute to biological activity. Fluoride, phosphate, and sulfate are ions commonly found in the environment and in plants15,16. Interpreting medicinal plant extracts as phytochemical-ionic systems offers a powerful strategy to elucidate the variability in extract activity4. It has been shown in studies that rutin, chlorogenic acid, and hesperidin individually cause G0/G1 phase arrest in A549 cells7–9. By adding inorganic components present in plant extracts to these phenolics, it is possible to create a specific effect for both A549 and other cancer types with similar molecular mechanisms.

In this study, the leaf extract of Berberis thunbergii DC. was examined not only as a phenolic mixture but also as an integrated phytochemical-ionic system. By combining LC-MS/MS-based phenolic profiling, ion chromatography under biologically relevant A549 IC50 conditions, and functional validation using certified anion standards, we aimed to investigate the individual and collaborative contributions of organic and inorganic components to phase-specific, cancer-selective cell cycle modulation in A549 lung cancer cells. Additionally, to address the previously reported cytotoxic effects of berberine alone8,10, we aimed to examine the effects on healthy cells through parallel evaluation in HEL299 lung fibroblasts. Specifically, this study aims to provide information suggesting that inorganic ions embedded in a medicinal plant extract can function as phase-specific regulators of cell cycle control points, thereby defining the cancer-preventive activity of natural products beyond a purely phytochemical paradigm.

Materials and methods

Plant material and extract preparation

Fresh leaves of Berberis thunbergii DC. were collected from the Balkan Campus of Trakya University (Edirne, Türkiye) and authenticated by a taxonomist at the EDTU Herbarium, where a voucher specimen was deposited (EDTU-29958). The leaves were washed with ultrapure water, air-dried at room temperature, and homogenized. Methanolic extraction was performed under standardized conditions as previously described17. The resulting extracts were filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters and stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

Cell culture conditions

Human lung adenocarcinoma cells (A549; ATCC, CCL-185™) and healthy human lung fibroblasts (HEL299; ATCC, CCL-137™) were obtained from Trakya University Technology Research Development Application and Research Center Laboratory cell bank. Cells were maintained in a 1:1 mixture of DMEM/Ham’s F-12 (Diagnovum, D813-500ML; Germany), and EMEM (Multicell, 320-005CL, Canada) medium supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (Diagnovum, D204-500ML; Germany), 1% L-glutamine (Multicell, 609-065EL, Canada), and antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Multicell, 450-115EL; Canada). The pH value of the culture medium was determined to be 7.21 using a pH/Ion meter (Mettler Toledo Seven Compact; USA). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and routinely subcultured at 90–95% confluence.

Phenolic profiling by LC–MS/MS

Quantitative phenolic profiling of the methanolic leaf extract was conducted using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS; Agilent 1260 Infinity LC system, USA). Separation was achieved on a reverse-phase C18 column (Agilent, USA) under gradient elution conditions. Detection was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode using 44 authenticated phenolic standards (Sigma-Aldrich; USA). Calibration curves were generated for each compound, and concentrations were expressed as ng/mL of extract.

Ion chromatography

Ion chromatography was employed to comprehensively characterize the inorganic anion profile of the A549 IC50 application condition determined in A549 cells. Analyses were performed using a Metrohm 881 Compact Pro ion chromatography (Metrohm; Switzerland) system equipped with a Metrosep A Supp 7–250/4.0 anion column (Metrohm, Switzerland) and a conductivity detector.

The system was operated using 3.6 mM sodium carbonate as the mobile phase, with the column temperature maintained at 40 °C. Prior to analysis, the instrument was equilibrated for 90 min. Extract samples prepared at the IC50 concentration were diluted 10-fold with ultrapure water and analyzed in triplicate.

Ion chromatography analysis was performed using certified anion standards (Sigma-Aldrich TraceCert; Switzerland) including fluoride, chloride, nitrite, bromide, nitrate, phosphate, sulphate, chlorate, bromate, and chlorite, with identification and quantification based on retention times and external calibration. Quantification was performed using the instrument’s software, and concentrations were expressed relative to ultrapure water controls. The analytical procedure was conducted in accordance with EPA Method 300.1 guidelines.

Cytotoxicity and apoptosis analyses

To calculate the effective inhibition concentration 50 (IC50) from these treatments, 20 µl of MTT (Biomatik; A3338-5GM, USA) solution (0.5 mg of MTT powder dissolved in 1 mL DNase- RNase free water) was applied to all wells. Cells treated with MTT solution were incubated for 3 h in an incubator (Panasonic Incusafe; Japan) with 95% humidity and 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. At the end of the incubation, all the liquid phase on the cells was removed. 180 µl of di-methyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was applied to all wells for 20 min. Cell viabilities were calculated by reading the absorbance values at 570 nm wavelength in the spectrophotometer device (Thermo Scientific Multiskan GO; USA). Apoptosis was quantified using the Annexin V–FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience, E-CK-A211; USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by flow cytometric analysis using an Attune Acoustic Focusing Cytometer (Applied Biosystems, USA). Certified fluoride (SIGMA-ALDRICH TraceCert, 77365-100ML), phosphate (SIGMA-ALDRICH TraceCert, 3864-100ML), and sulphate (SIGMA-ALDRICH TraceCert, 90071-100ML) standards were applied to A549 and HEL299 cells at concentrations corresponding to those quantified in the A549 IC50 extract. All standards were obtained from the manufacturer’s data sheet, with fluoride: NaF, phosphate: Na2HPO4, and sulfate: Na2SO4 in the form of water and high purity. The pH was determined to be 6.78 for NaF, 7.8 for Na2HPO4, and 6.99 for Na2SO4.

Cell-cycle analysis

Cell-cycle distribution was assessed by propidium iodide/RNase staining. Following treatments with NaF, Na2HPO4, and Na2SO4, the A549 cells were trypsinized and collected into 1.5 mL collection tubes (Eppendorf; Germany). The tubes were centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and cells were fixed in 70% ethanol for 20 min and then incubated at 4 °C for four hours. Fixed cells were stained using FxCycle PI/RNase Staining Solution (Invitrogen, F10797; USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted to determine the percentage of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (without LC-MS/MS analysis). The IC50 value of A549 for Berberis thunbergii leaf extract application was calculated using CompuSyn 1.0 software. Statistical analyses for flow cytometer apoptosis and cell cycle assays were conducted using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics 22). After analyzing the homogeneity of data distributions between groups using Levene’s test, differences among the control group and the experimental groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Phenolic composition of Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract

LC–MS/MS analysis of the methanolic leaf extract of Berberis thunbergii DC. revealed rutin (28.76 µg/mL; 47.11 µM), chlorogenic acid (15.14 µg/mL; 42.73 µM), and hesperidin (10.14 µg/mL; 16.61 µM) as the dominant phenolic constituents. In addition, lower concentrations of quercetin (0.204 µg/mL; 0.66 µM), protocatechuic acid (0.092 µg/mL; 0.58 µM), salicylic acid (0.085 µg/mL; 0.58 µM), myricetin (0.032 µg/mL; 0.094 µM), and trace amounts of gallic acid (0.9 ng/mL; 5.29 nM) were detected (Fig. 1).

Cytotoxic selectivity of the extract in A549 and HEL299 cells

MTT assays demonstrated a concentration-dependent reduction in A549 cell viability following treatment with Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract (Fig. 2a). The IC50 dose of Berberis thunbergii DC. was calculated as 40.16 µg/mL for 24 h treatment (Fig. 2b).

Ion chromatography analysis of inorganic anions

Ion chromatography performed at the A549 IC50 condition revealed substantial enrichment of fluoride, phosphate, and sulphate ions in the extract compared with ultrapure water controls. Fluoride, phosphate, and sulphate concentrations in the A549 IC50 extract were quantified as 1.88 ± 0.01 ng/mL (44.77 µM), 1.98 ± 0.13 ng/mL (12.08 µM), and 32.05 ± 0.19 ng/mL (225.64 µM), respectively, whereas corresponding control values were 0.0016 ± 0.0003 ng/mL (0.038 µM), 0.0019 ± 0.0017 ng/mL (0.013 µM), and 0.014 ± 0.013 ng/mL (0.098 µM) (Fig. 3; Supplementary File 1).

Inorganic salts-specific apoptotic responses in A549 and HEL299 cells

Functional validation using certified anion standards demonstrated marked differences between malignant and healthy cells. In HEL299 cells, total apoptotic and necrotic cell percentages were 9.07 ± 0.13% for control, 11.49 ± 1.22% (P = 0.373) for NaF, 36.16 ± 1.25% (P = 0.000) for Na2HPO4, and 31.60 ± 2.96% (P = 0.000) for Na2SO4 treatments (Fig. 4a). In contrast, A549 cells exhibited a dramatic increase in apoptotic and necrotic populations following NaF exposure (90.68 ± 0.37%, P = 0.000), compared with control (7.51 ± 2.53%), Na2HPO4 (6.55 ± 1.74%, P = 0.871), and Na2SO4 (7.66 ± 0.15%, P = 0.999) treatments (Fig. 4b).

Phase-specific cell-cycle modulation by inorganic anions in A549 cells

Control cells displayed a balanced distribution across G0/G1 (45.37 ± 4.36%), S (20.26 ± 0.86%), and G2/M (33.36 ± 3.68%) phases. NaF treatment resulted in a marked accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase (60.48 ± 2.85%, P = 0.001), accompanied by reductions in S (23.11 ± 1.41%, P = 0.024) and G2/M (15.4 ± 1.33%, P = 0.000) fractions. Na2HPO4-treated cells closely resembled control profiles (G0/G1: 47.65 ± 3.21%, P = 0.801; S: 29.78 ± 0.59%, P = 0.000; G2/M: 21.59 ± 2.86%, P = 0.000). In contrast, Na2SO4 treatment induced a pronounced shift characterized by depletion of the S-phase population (7.88 ± 0.62%, P = 0.000) and substantial accumulation in G2/M (80.51 ± 0.95%, P = 0.000), with minimal G0/G1 representation (11.28 ± 0.5%).

Tukey’s post hoc analysis demonstrated that NaF-treated cells exhibited significantly higher G0/G1 accumulation than control and phosphate groups (P < 0.001), whereas Na2SO4-treated cells showed a significantly elevated G2/M fraction compared with all other groups (P < 0.001), confirming phase-specific checkpoint modulation rather than nonspecific cytotoxicity.

Discussion

This study advances the mechanistic reinterpretation of Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract, demonstrating that its anticancer activity arises not only from phenolic components but also from an integrated organic-inorganic system. While it has been reported that rutin, chlorogenic acid, and hesperidin individually cause G0/G1 arrest in A549 cells18–20, our data reveal that the inorganic anions embedded in the extract matrix function as phase-specific regulators of cell cycle progression.

In the study, it was shown that the application of NaF to A549 cell amounts led to an increase in the G0/G1 phases, and this increase was accompanied by the triggering of apoptosis. The transition from the G1 phase to the S phase is of extremely critical distinguishing importance between cancer cells and healthy cells. In healthy cells, as long as no growth signals reach the cell surface receptors, the retinoblastoma (RB) gene binds to E2F-1, inhibiting the dephosphorylation of HDAC, and the cells cannot transition from the G1 phase to the S phase21. In contrast, in many cancer cell lines, the RB gene is mutated, so it cannot bind E2F-1 regardless of whether there is an external signal or not, and thus HDAC is activated and the cell cycle continues22. However, in an in vitro study comparing the effects of sodium fluoride on TM3 mouse Leydig cells, the application of 2 ng/mL for 24, 48, and 72 h resulted in cell viability rates of 73.5%, 65.6%, and 62.3%, respectively, compared to the control group. Similarly, the application of 16 ng/mL NaF for the same durations resulted in cell viability rates of 65.7%, 54.3%, and 50.1% compared to the control group23. In response to the toxic effects of NaF on healthy cells, our study found that the application of Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract, containing a dose of 1.88 ± 0.01 ng/mL that caused accumulation in the G0/G1 phase and triggered apoptosis in the A549 cell line, resulted in a statistically significant reduction in cell viability in the A549 cell line, whereas no suppression was observed in the HEL299 cell line.

In contrast, following the application of Na2SO4, a decrease in the S-phase population and a significant increase in the G2/M phase were observed in the A549 cell line. However, it was shown that this application did not induce an apoptotic effect. In a study investigating the cytotoxic effects of various sulfate forms on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), it was observed that among the sulfate groups derived from red seaweed polysaccharides, sulfated K-carrageenan in the dose range of 0.005–0.1.005.1 mg/mL did not show significant cytotoxic effects compared to the control. However, it was noted that increasing concentrations above 0.1 mg/mL, especially at the application dose of 1 mg/mL, reduced HUVEC cell viability below 40%. Additionally, the study showed that the 0.005–1.005 mg/mL dose range of sulfated agarose did not produce a cytotoxic effect on the same cell line24.

The absence of similar effects after Na2HPO4 exposure further reinforces the specificity of these anion-derived phenotypes. Similarly, in a study investigating the in vitro cytotoxic effects of Na2HPO4 on EAhy926, Huvecs human endothelial cells, and bovine aortic endothelial GM-7373 cells, it was found that Na2HPO4 doses up to 1.4 mM did not show cytotoxic effects on these cell lines. However, at concentrations of 2.5 mM and above, a statistically significant dose-dependent reduction in cell viability was observed in all cell lines25.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the anticancer activity of Berberis thunbergii DC. leaf extract is derived not only from phenolic components but also from a coordinated phytochemical-inorganic component system. These effects were phase-specific, statistically robust, and distinctly selective for the A549 cell line, with minimal impact on healthy HEL299 lung fibroblasts. This situation explains why berberine alone is not a G0/G1 phase-specific inhibitor on the A549 cell line, as previously noted in a study11, and why analyzes limited to organic phytochemicals may overlook critical determinants of biological activity.
